Physician’s ethical perspectives on research involving terminally ill patients in Nigeria: A qualitative study
Authors: Ebere J. Achi, Onyinye H. Chime, Ifeyinwa U. Odoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch involving terminally ill patients presents unique ethical challenges due to vulnerability, fluctuating consent capacity, and risk of undue influence. In Nigeria, cultural norms, religious beliefs, and family dynamics further shape ethical engagement, but empirical evidence regarding physicians’ perspectives remains limited.ObjectivesExploring physicians’ ethical perspectives regarding the involvement of terminally ill patients in research by focusing on consent practices, safeguards, and culturally sensitive approaches to participation.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was conducted at two tertiary hospitals in Enugu State, Nigeria. Twenty-four purposively selected physicians involved in oncology and palliative care participated in in-depth and key informant interviews. Data were analyzed thematically using Braun and Clarke's framework.ResultsFour themes emerged: facilitators of consent, consent practices, preferred decision-makers, and ethical concerns. Physicians emphasized clear communication, empathy, and culturally sensitive support. Direct patient consent was prioritized, with surrogate consent and pediatric assent used when appropriate. Family involvement was central, although surrogate reliability required careful assessment. Ethical concerns focused on minimizing harm, ensuring equity, and integrating cultural and spiritual guidance.ConclusionIn general, physicians in this study supported the ethical inclusion of terminally ill patients in research provided rigorous safeguards are applied. They operationalize autonomy, beneficence, and justice within a relational framework that integrates family and spiritual guidance. These findings highlight culturally sensitive consent practices and provide practical guidance for ethics committees, researchers, and educators when promoting the ethical participation of vulnerable patients.

Background

Patients with terminal illnesses are a particularly vulnerable population in clinical research due to their progressive physical decline, cognitive or emotional impairment, and reliance on caregivers for daily functioning.1, 2, 3 These vulnerabilities increase their susceptibility to coercion or undue influence, heightening the ethical responsibility of researchers to protect participants while ensuring voluntary and informed engagement.3 However, the participation of terminally ill patients is essential for advancing medical knowledge, developing effective interventions, and improving quality of life, particularly in palliative care settings.4,5 In this study, terminally ill patients are defined as individuals with a life expectancy of 6 months or less, or those receiving palliative care due to progressive, incurable illness.6

Core ethical principles comprising autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, and respect for persons provide a guiding framework for conducting research with terminally ill patients.7 Respect for autonomy emphasizes that patients have the right to make decisions regarding their participation in research, regardless of prognosis, while beneficence and non-maleficence require the balancing of potential benefits against harm, particularly in populations with heightened vulnerability.2,8 Justice ensures equitable inclusion, preventing the unfair exclusion of terminally ill patients from research opportunities or exploitation due to their fragile condition.9

Obtaining valid informed consent is crucial for ethical research but presents practical challenges in terminally ill populations. Cognitive impairment, emotional distress, fatigue, and fluctuations in medical status can compromise the decision-making capacity, necessitating careful assessment and the use of surrogate decision-makers, where appropriate.3,10,11 For pediatric and adolescent participants, assent should complement parental or guardian consent in order to respect emerging autonomy while ensuring protection.11, 12, 13 Research conducted without robust consent mechanisms risks undermining patient rights and public trust, with potential long-term consequences for research participation in vulnerable groups.13,14

Evidence regarding research ethics in terminally ill populations is mainly derived from high-income countries.3,14 However, in Nigeria, cultural, religious, and systemic factors may shape patient expectations, family involvement, and clinician–patient interactions, necessitating context-specific understanding.15, 16, 17 Previous studies conducted in Nigeria and sub-Saharan Africa suggest that family involvement and spiritual guidance frequently shape healthcare and research decision-making, underscoring the importance of culturally sensitive ethical frameworks.18,19

Despite an increase in clinical research in Nigeria, there have been few empirical explorations of physicians’ perspectives on the ethical participation of terminally ill patients. Understanding these perspectives is critical for developing guidelines that balance patient protection with research advancement, ensure culturally appropriate engagement, and address context-specific challenges in informed consent, surrogate decision-making, and equitable inclusion.1,4,6

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The present study employed a qualitative descriptive design informed by a reflexive thematic analysis approach to explore the ethical perspectives and experiences of physicians regarding research involving terminally ill patients. The design was chosen to capture detailed, real-world insights into ethical decisions, consent practices, and safeguards by focusing on practical experience rather than theory.

The study was conducted at two tertiary hospitals in Enugu State, Nigeria: University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital (UNTH), Ituku-Ozalla, and Enugu State University Teaching Hospital (ESUTH), Parklane. These sites were selected because of their diverse clinical services, including active oncology departments across pediatrics, internal medicine, surgery, and obstetrics and gynecology, and their established engagement in clinical research. Inclusion of multiple specialties ensured that perspectives were represented from pediatric, adult, and geriatric terminally ill populations.

Study population and sampling

Participants were physicians with active involvement in terminally ill patient care, either clinically or through research participation. Eligible participants included consultants, senior residents, and junior residents with direct oncology or palliative care experience. Physicians without patient contact were excluded to ensure that practical insights were obtained into ethical decision-making.

A purposive sampling approach was employed to recruit participants with relevant expertise and experience in research involving terminally ill patients. Given the typical hierarchical structure in hospital environments, particular attention was paid to minimizing potential influences due to professional hierarchies or prior collegial relationships. Advance directives and prior documented patient preferences were considered where available to guide ethical enrollment and respect patient autonomy. The sampling framework was constructed from departmental lists, and potential participants were approached via phone calls and face-to-face invitations, facilitated by the chief residents of the selected departments. Among 28 eligible physicians approached, 24 gave their consent to participate. Reasons for non-participation included scheduling conflicts and unavailability. All participants were practicing physicians but their level of research involvement varied, where five had served as principal investigators and three as co-investigators, whereas the rest were primarily engaged in clinical care with occasional participation in research. Key informants were purposively selected because of their leadership roles in oncology and palliative care services, prior involvement in clinical research oversight, and extensive experience managing terminally ill patients. Participation was entirely voluntary, and physicians were informed that their decision to participate or decline would have no impacts on their professional roles or relationships within the institution. To further mitigate potential power dynamics, the informed consent procedures emphasized confidentiality and the independence of the research team from hospital management structures.

Data collection

Data were collected over a 2-month period in August and September 2021 through face-to-face interviews conducted in private clinical spaces or offices to ensure confidentiality and minimize disruption to clinical duties. All interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ consent and supplemented with field notes to capture non-verbal cues and contextual factors. The interviews used a semi-structured interview guide (Appendix 1), which was organized into thematic domains including facilitators of informed consent, consent practices and procedures, patient with surrogate decision-making, and integration of ethical principles into practice. Open-ended questions and probes encouraged participants to describe their experiences in depth, ensuring that rich and context-specific insights were obtained. The guide was pretested with two physicians who were not included in the final sample, and minor revisions were made to improve clarity and flow. The interview guide was developed from previous studies of informed consent, vulnerability, palliative care ethics, and relational decision-making in sub-Saharan Africa.

Interviews lasted between 35 and 60 min and were conducted primarily in English, but local languages were used when necessary, with translation and back-translation performed to ensure accuracy. Participants were assigned unique codes to maintain anonymity, i.e., in-depth interview = IDI, key informant interview = KII, followed by the participant number and specialty or clinical role; e.g., “Male, IDI/S3” denoted a male participant from specialty 3, and “Male, IDI/C2” denoted a participant from clinical role 2.

The research team comprised one senior physician (female) and two trained research assistants (male and female), who were all experienced in qualitative data collection. Reflexive notes were maintained throughout the data collection process to guide probe adjustments, interviewee–participant interactions, and emerging lines of inquiry. These reflections, as well as regular team debriefings, informed iterative refinement of the interview techniques and contributed to data quality improvement.

Data collection was guided by the principle of data saturation, which was defined as the point at which no new codes, themes, or subthemes emerged from successive interviews. Saturation was achieved after 20 in-depth interviews and four key informant interviews. Coding and preliminary thematic analysis were conducted concurrently with data collection to assess redundancy and the stability of emerging findings, and data collection was concluded when no novel insights were identified.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed inductively following Braun and Clarke's six-phase framework for thematic analysis20: familiarization with the data by repeatedly reading the transcripts and field notes; generation of initial codes from meaningful data segments; searching for candidate themes by grouping related codes; reviewing and refining themes to ensure internal coherence and distinction; defining and naming themes; and producing the final analytic narrative supported by representative quotations. NVivo software (Lumivero, version 14) facilitated the systematic organization of transcripts, storage of codes, retrieval of coded excerpts, and comparison of coding across interviews. An evolving codebook was developed containing code definitions, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and illustrative excerpts, and updated throughout the analytic process to support analytic consistency and transparency. Researchers first familiarized themselves with the data by repeatedly reading the verbatim transcripts and field notes to achieve immersion and gain an overall understanding of the participants' perspectives. Initial codes were then generated independently by multiple members of the research team from meaningful segments of the data, ensuring that coding remained grounded in the participants' accounts.

The coding process was reflexive and repeated, and involved regular comparisons and discussions among researchers to refine codes, resolve discrepancies, and enhance the analytical consistency. A codebook was developed and updated throughout the analysis process to ensure transparency and dependability. Coding was conducted iteratively by multiple researchers using the evolving codebook, with regular discussions to refine codes and organize them into subthemes and broader themes.

Themes were reviewed and refined to ensure internal coherence, consistency, and clear distinction between themes. Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection, thereby allowing emerging insights to inform subsequent interviews and enabling assessment of thematic saturation. Saturation was accepted when no new codes, categories, or themes emerged from successive interviews.

Several strategies consistent with qualitative research standards were employed to enhance rigor. Credibility was supported through prolonged engagement with the data, independent coding, and regular peer debriefing sessions among the research team to discuss coding differences and refine emerging themes. Reflexive memos were maintained throughout data collection and analysis to document the assumptions, positionality, and evolving interpretations of the researchers. Dependability and confirmability were strengthened through an audit trail documenting coding decisions, thematic revisions, analytic reflections, and development of the codebook. These strategies enhanced transparency, consistency, and trustworthiness throughout the analytic process.

Facilitators of consent, consent practices, decision-makers, and ethical concerns about consent for terminally ill patients

Theme 1: facilitators of consent

Physicians identified several interrelated factors that can facilitate informed consent among terminally ill patients, primarily by enhancing understanding, fostering trust, and reducing barriers to participation. Clear and simplified communication was described as a fundamental strategy. Participants emphasized the importance of presenting information in understandable terms, encouraging questions, and verifying patient understanding prior to enrolment, thereby strengthening the validity of consent:“We simplify explanations, encourage questions, and confirm patients' understanding, which helps ensure that their consent is truly informed before participation.” (Male, IDI/S3)

“We simplify explanations, encourage questions, and confirm patients' understanding, which helps ensure that their consent is truly informed before participation.” (Male, IDI/S3)

Relational factors, including empathy and emotional support, were highlighted as critical for facilitating consent. Establishing rapport and demonstrating genuine concern enhanced patient comfort and willingness to participate.“It is important to engage patients fully and show them empathy. When patients are engaged with empathy and treated with dignity, feeling truly heard and understood, consent becomes an expression of trust and is easy.” (Male, KII/C2)

“It is important to engage patients fully and show them empathy. When patients are engaged with empathy and treated with dignity, feeling truly heard and understood, consent becomes an expression of trust and is easy.” (Male, KII/C2)

Physicians also noted that incorporating spiritual or religious support where appropriate further promoted engagement.“By showing empathy and where necessary, involving the patient’s own pastor, or spiritual leader, patients feel more comfortable and supported, making them more open and willing to participate meaningfully in the consent process.” (Male, IDI/S4)

“By showing empathy and where necessary, involving the patient’s own pastor, or spiritual leader, patients feel more comfortable and supported, making them more open and willing to participate meaningfully in the consent process.” (Male, IDI/S4)

Participants also described practical support, such as providing transport reimbursement or refreshments, as important for reducing logistical barriers without being coercive:“Providing small practical support, such as transport reimbursement or refreshments, helps reduce participation barriers and makes it easier for patients to engage in the consent process.” (Female, IDI/S2)

“Providing small practical support, such as transport reimbursement or refreshments, helps reduce participation barriers and makes it easier for patients to engage in the consent process.” (Female, IDI/S2)

Theme 2: consent practices

Consent was described as a structured, adaptive, and iterative process tailored to the clinical condition and cognitive capacity of terminally ill patients. Direct patient consent was prioritized when possible rather than surrogate consent, and pediatric assent utilized as appropriate.“Patients should be actively involved in the consent process after counseling as the purpose of the research is to improve their care and advance better outcomes for others.” (Female, IDI/S6)

“Patients should be actively involved in the consent process after counseling as the purpose of the research is to improve their care and advance better outcomes for others.” (Female, IDI/S6)

Physicians highlighted preparatory engagement, reviewing patient concerns, and repeated assessment to ensure informed decision-making:“It is crucial to engage patients before the consent proper, exploring their concerns, deepening your understanding of their care, and revisiting discussions as needed. I’ve seen research with terminally ill patients do this. It identifies their problems and helps us manage them better.” (Male, IDI/S5)

“It is crucial to engage patients before the consent proper, exploring their concerns, deepening your understanding of their care, and revisiting discussions as needed. I’ve seen research with terminally ill patients do this. It identifies their problems and helps us manage them better.” (Male, IDI/S5)

Cultural and religious factors also influenced consent practices because patients frequently awaited guidance from family elders or spiritual leaders before making decisions. Participants reported that some families involved elders or religious leaders before decisions were finalized regarding participation in research:“In Nigeria, it is common for families to involve elders or religious leaders in decisions. Sometimes, we have to wait until the patient speaks with their spiritual leader or family elder before they give consent.” (Female, IDI/S4)

“In Nigeria, it is common for families to involve elders or religious leaders in decisions. Sometimes, we have to wait until the patient speaks with their spiritual leader or family elder before they give consent.” (Female, IDI/S4)

Physicians emphasized the crucial need for repeatedly assessing lucidity and the timing of consent:“Sometimes, the patients' conditions are poor. We often reassess the patient daily and wait for the day the patient appears most lucid before obtaining consent. This ensures that their participation is truly informed.” (Male, KII/C1)

“Sometimes, the patients' conditions are poor. We often reassess the patient daily and wait for the day the patient appears most lucid before obtaining consent. This ensures that their participation is truly informed.” (Male, KII/C1)

For pediatric populations, assent was obtained alongside parental consent, with married minors considered capable of independent consent:“For every child, we obtain assent, but when minors are married or have children themselves, they are considered capable of giving consent for participation in research.” (Female, IDI/S5)

“For every child, we obtain assent, but when minors are married or have children themselves, they are considered capable of giving consent for participation in research.” (Female, IDI/S5)

Clear communication about risks and benefits, as well as time for reflection, was reported as essential for ensuring voluntary participation:“I try to be very clear about the risks and benefits and give them time to reflect. This can run into days. It helps patients make informed decisions rather than feeling pressured.” (Female, IDI/S2)“Patients and families often worry that the research might not benefit them directly. However, discussing potential societal benefits also encouraged patients and families to consent voluntarily.” (Male, KII/C1)

Theme 3: preferred decision-makers

Decision-making was described as a relational process involving patients, families, and occasionally trusted non-family individuals. Family members were often central, particularly when patients lacked full decision-making capacities:“Most patients naturally rely on their families when making decisions, which makes sense given the long-standing relationship through years of close contact and interaction. In many cases, they play a central role, especially when patients cannot make decisions on their own.” (Female, IDI/S1)“Occasionally, a patient relies on a friend who has been involved in their care for many years, especially if they feel their family does not fully understand their wishes. In one case, a patient refused to sign consent until a close friend, who had accompanied them to every appointment, agreed it was okay.” (Male, IDI/S3)

Theme 4: ethical concerns

Ethical concerns are central to research involving terminally ill patients, focusing on minimizing harm, ensuring voluntary participation, and maintaining fairness. Physicians balanced potential benefits to society with the immediate welfare of patients by taking steps to prevent undue burden or coercion:“Conducting research with terminally ill patients will benefit society at large in subsequent management of other terminally ill patients. I believe it should be encouraged.” (Male, IDI/S8)“It is a good idea. It will go a long way in helping to see how to handle their issues and their management. Across the interviews, physicians explicitly articulated a clear moral stance regarding the permissibility of this research. They viewed clinical research involving terminally ill patients as ethically permissible, socially valuable, and necessary, provided that robust safeguards are actively enforced. Rather than viewing this population as too fragile to participate, physicians balanced potential societal benefits such as informing future treatment modalities, modifying drug dosages, and improving palliative protocols with an immediate moral obligation to prevent undue burden. Their support for the moral permissibility of these studies was found to be strictly conditional, where it requires confirming that motivation (e.g., transport reimbursement) does not cross into coercion, verifying that patient autonomy remains active through daily lucidity checks, and ensuring that local familial and spiritual support networks are respectfully integrated into the consent architecture.

Discussion

The physicians interviewed in this study described applying core ethical principles: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice, within a relational and sociocultural context, particularly in research involving terminally ill patients. Participants emphasized that ethical research participation is not applied in isolation but instead it is integrated with family involvement, spiritual support, and considerations related to the realities of terminal illness. Effective communication, empathy, and rapport building were consistently identified as central strategies that enhance patient understanding and facilitate informed decision-making, thereby supporting patient-centered practices highlighted in previous research.15,16,21,22

Physicians described providing clear, simplified explanations, creating opportunities for clarification, and engaging in supportive interactions to ensure that patients comprehended research procedures. Emotional and spiritual support, as well as the involvement of family members, was considered an essential component of ethical practice rather than an optional addition. These practices enhanced trust and reflected culturally grounded approaches reported in Nigeria and other sub-Saharan African settings where communal and spiritual considerations often shape healthcare decisions.

Modest practical support, including transport reimbursement or refreshments, was framed as a means of reducing barriers to participation without constituting undue influence. Some participants noted that explanations of potential benefits could inadvertently motivate participation, highlighting the delicate balance between encouraging engagement and maintaining voluntariness, thereby aligning with the nuanced ethical considerations required in vulnerable populations, where support may facilitate engagement while safeguarding autonomy.15,21

Consent was described as a dynamic and iterative process, which is particularly important given the fluctuating decision-making capacities of terminally ill patients. Physicians emphasized the need for repeatedly assessing understanding, careful documentation, and timing consent when patients were most lucid, reflecting ethical vigilance in palliative care settings. Direct patient consent was preferred, but surrogate consent and pediatric assent were accepted as necessary to respect emerging autonomy. These practices are consistent with the sociocultural context in Nigeria, where spouses, parents, grandparents, or spiritual leaders are often integral to healthcare and research decisions.15,21

Participants reported that trusted non-family advisors, such as close friends, occasionally influenced decisions when patients felt closer to them than family members. Physicians highlighted the need to assess surrogate reliability and intentions to prevent conflicts of interest. One participant described a scenario where a parent sought enrollment for perceived family benefit, requiring counseling to ensure alignment with the patient's wishes. Such examples highlight the relational nature of consent and the importance of balancing patient preferences with cultural and familial norms. Previous studies in sub-Saharan Africa have also demonstrated that healthcare and research decisions are embedded within family and community structures, where elders and spiritual leaders contribute to decision-making.22,23 This relational framework does not diminish autonomy but situates it within a collective context where patients, families, and clinicians negotiate ethically sound decisions.22,24 Similarly, in the broader African research context, empirical studies have emphasized the need to adapt informed consent processes to local cultural and communal environments.24,25 For example, empirical qualitative data compiled by Marsh26 in East Africa demonstrate that structured community engagement platforms serve as effective venues for sharing complex medical research information and engaging extended families in decision-making, illustrating the ethical value of collective involvement in consent processes.25 Similar research regarding informed consent dynamics in rural West African clinical environments has shown that while international consent standards are broadly affirmed, participants rely extensively on localized social networks, including family elders and peers, to interpret and validate consent information.23,26 These African studies closely resemble the findings obtained in our study, demonstrating that ethical consent in this environment cannot be fully understood through a Western individualistic lens alone.23,25,26 Instead, culturally responsive approaches that integrate family input, community engagement, and traditional decision structures are essential for safeguarding autonomy and equity in ethically sensitive research across sub-Saharan Africa.22,24,25

Ethical considerations were central to participants’ accounts. Respect for autonomy was enacted through efforts to ensure comprehension and voluntary participation, while beneficence and non-maleficence guided decisions to minimize additional burden or harm. Justice was evident in the emphasis on equitable inclusion, and resisting the exclusion of terminally ill patients based solely on prognosis. Physicians described the complex balancing act between respecting cultural and familial norms, motivating participation without coercion, and maintaining ethical integrity in research with vulnerable populations.

This study contributes to the literature on research ethics in low- and middle-income settings by highlighting how ethical practices are shaped by relational decision-making frameworks. In contrast to western countries where autonomy is often conceptualized as individualistic, our findings suggest that decision-making among participants frequently involved family structures and spiritual influences in this study.14,15 These findings highlight the importance of culturally-sensitive consent models that respect communal and spiritual influences while maintaining ethical standards. They also suggest areas for guideline development, such as structured capacity assessments, careful evaluation of surrogates, and formal recognition of family and spiritual support in decision-making.

From both clinical and research perspectives, our findings highlight the need for continuous ethics training to address complex consent situations, practical tools for assessing and documenting capacity, and culturally sensitive communication strategies. Applying these measures can help clinicians to manage ethical challenges while promoting patient-centered care.

The study had some limitations. Participants were recruited from two tertiary, research-active hospitals in Enugu State, and perspectives from other healthcare settings may differ. Reliance on self-reported practices introduced potential social desirability bias. Our study focused on physicians involved in research with terminally ill patients, which is a relatively small and specific group, so the eligible population was naturally limited, resulting in the small sample size. In addition, our study focused exclusively on physicians and did not capture patient or caregiver perspectives, which are critical for comprehensively understanding consent processes. Finally, data saturation was achieved but our findings are context specific and intended for analytical transferability rather than statistical generalization.

Physicians support the ethical inclusion of terminally ill patients in research when appropriate safeguards are implemented. Their practices reflect a balance between universal ethical principles and local sociocultural realities, incorporating clear communication, ongoing consent processes, relational decision-making, and integration of African communal and spiritual contexts. Strengthening regulatory guidance and clinical training to reflect these practices will enhance ethical research participation, and ensure the protection and appropriate inclusion of vulnerable populations.

Our findings suggest that facilitating informed consent among terminally ill patients requires a multidimensional and patient-centered approach that integrates effective communication, relational sensitivity, ethical safeguards, and context-specific adaptations. Physicians described consent as a dynamic process shaped by individual patient preferences but also by cultural, spiritual, and familial influences that frequently informed decision-making within the study context. These relational frameworks required careful mediation to ensure that voluntary participation, patient autonomy, and ethical standards were maintained. Across interviews, participants described ongoing efforts to balance the ethical principles of beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice, while ensuring that patient welfare remained central to research participation decisions.

Conclusion

This study obtained insights into how physicians at two tertiary hospitals in southeastern Nigeria navigated the ethical, cultural, and practical complexities of conducting research involving terminally ill patients. A key contribution of this study was demonstrating that decision-making was often relational rather than strictly individual, where patients frequently involved family members, grandparents, friends, or spiritual leaders in their choices. Consent was described as a dynamic, iterative process, where physicians carefully timed discussions to coincide with periods of patient lucidity, and repeatedly assessed understanding to ensure voluntary participation. Our study also clarified the ethical distinction between supportive motivation and undue inducement, demonstrating that modest support measures such as transport reimbursement, refreshments, or reassurance serve primarily to reduce barriers rather than coerce participation.

Direct patient consent remains the preferred approach when capacities are intact, whereas surrogate consent and pediatric assent are accepted when the decision-making capacities of patients are impaired. Physicians emphasized the importance of evaluating the reliability and intentions of surrogate decision-makers, particularly in situations where conflicts of interest might arise. They further described how family, friends, and spiritual leaders influenced consent, demonstrating that relational and cultural norms are integral to ethical practice in this context. This insight contributes novel evidence to the literature on consent for research involving terminally ill patients in low- and middle-income settings, showing that ethical principles are operationalized in ways that are both context sensitive and culturally grounded.

Our findings have important practical implications. Researchers are encouraged to follow iterative and context-sensitive consent processes, ensuring that patients have opportunities to reflect, ask questions, and engage trusted advisors, as appropriate. These strategies will enhance both the ethical integrity of research and patient-centered care, ensuring that vulnerable populations are appropriately included without compromising autonomy or protection.

Overall, the physicians in this study demonstrated that ethical research participation among terminally ill patients is achievable when universal ethical principles are balanced with sociocultural realities. Practices such as clear communication, repeated assessment of understanding, attention to cultural and spiritual contexts, and careful evaluation of surrogates allow autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice to be translated into practice.
