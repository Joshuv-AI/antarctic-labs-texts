Courage in Crisis: Latina Immigrant Survivors of IPV and the Quest for Help Amid Anti-Immigrant Sentiment
Authors: Veronica Valencia Gonzalez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examines how Latina immigrant survivors of intimate partner violence (IPV) understand and navigate California's sanctuary policies when deciding whether to seek formal help. Drawing on qualitative interviews with 12 immigrant IPV survivors and 14 service providers in Southern California, the findings reveal significant gaps in knowledge and trust surrounding sanctuary policies. Despite policies intended to limit cooperation with federal immigration enforcement, survivors remained uncertain about their protective value and feared exposure through identification requirements. Cultural norms, economic dependence, and social stigma further constrained help-seeking. The study highlights the need for clearer policy communication and community-based, culturally grounded support strategies.

Introduction

Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) is a pervasive social and public health issue in the United States. National estimates indicate that approximately one in three women and one in four men experience physical violence, sexual violence, or stalking by an intimate partner during their lifetime (Smith et al., 2018). While IPV cuts across social, racial, and economic lines, immigrant populations face distinct and compounded barriers to recognizing abuse and seeking help. As of recent estimates, more than 45 million foreign-born individuals reside in the United States, comprising roughly 14% of the total population, with Latina immigrants representing one of the largest immigrant groups (Budiman et al., 2020; Migration Policy Institute, 2023). Despite experiencing comparable or, in some contexts, elevated levels of IPV, immigrant survivors are consistently less likely to seek formal assistance, including contacting law enforcement or service providers, due to intersecting legal, economic, and social vulnerabilities (Erez et al., 2009; Reina et al., 2014).

During periods of heightened anti-immigrant rhetoric, such as that expressed by former President Donald J. Trump, many immigrants have reported fear of contacting law enforcement or other government agencies, worried that any interaction could lead to detention or deportation. For undocumented survivors of IPV, these fears are often intensified by uncertainty surrounding immigration enforcement practices and the perceived risks associated with visibility and disclosure. For instance, people such as Maria and Luisa (pseudonyms), two survivors of IPV who are undocumented, described how their documented status played a role in inhibiting them from involving authorities for the IPV they experienced at the hand of their romantic partner.

This paper explores how the immigration and documented statuses of IPV victims influence their decisions to seek help from law enforcement. Specifically, it examines the impact of California's sanctuary policies on undocumented and documented immigrant survivors of IPV. Sanctuary policies, such as California's Value Act (SB 54), aim to limit local and state resources from being used to assist federal immigration enforcement. The California Value Act, signed into law on October 5, 2017, prohibits state and local law enforcement officers from: 1) asking about immigration status, 2) arresting individuals solely based on deportation orders, 3) utilizing Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) or Border Patrol agents as interpreters, and 4) sharing personal information with ICE or Border Patrol. This climate shapes how sanctuary policies are interpreted and whether they provide the intended sense of protection for immigrant communities.

Guided by social constructionism and legal consciousness theory (Ewick & Silbey, 1998), this study examines how meaning is constructed around safety, law, and belonging among immigrant IPV survivors. Drawing on qualitative interviews with immigrant survivors and service providers in Southern California, this study examines the following research question: How do Latina immigrant survivors of IPV understand and navigate sanctuary policies in their decisions to seek help, and how do these interpretations reflect broader relationships between marginalized communities and the state?

Literature Review

This literature review situates the study at the intersection of intimate partner violence, immigration, and law. First, it examines how IPV is socially constructed and named, emphasizing how cultural norms and power relations shape survivors’ recognition of harm. Second, it reviews scholarship on barriers to help-seeking, with particular attention to immigrant survivors’ interactions with legal and social service institutions. Finally, the review considers how fear of deportation, legal precarity, and anti-immigrant policy environments influence survivors’ legal consciousness and decisions to seek formal assistance. Together, these bodies of literature provide the conceptual foundation for examining how Latina immigrant survivors interpret sanctuary policies and navigate help-seeking within a context of uncertainty and risk.

Naming IPV

Before IPV survivors can decide whether to seek help, they must first recognize that their situation warrants seeking help or redress (that is, formal or informal efforts to seek justice or assistance). This recognition is influenced by various factors, including cultural and social norms that shape the acceptability of violence, which can vary significantly across populations, countries, or regions (WHO, 2009). These norms impact how violence is viewed and experienced by victims and perpetrators, and they also influence what is deemed an appropriate response to violence and the suitable means of redress. However, norms and culture are not static; they evolve over time, as evidenced by changing perceptions of IPV in the United States. Recent analyses confirm that these shifts are ongoing, particularly in immigrant and refugee contexts where multiple cultural frameworks intersect (Sabri et al., 2025).

Social Constructionism and IPV

According to the theory of social constructionism, the development of understanding of the world is a collective process (Galbin, 2014). Social constructionists elucidate “the process by which people come to describe, explain, or otherwise account for the world in which they live” (Gergen, 1985, pp. 3–4). The definitions of IPV are constructed socially and reflect the interests of those in power (Baoumeister, 1997; Muehlenhard & Kimes, 1999). Those who define violent behaviors often exclude their own behaviors from these definitions, creating a biased understanding of what constitutes IPV. The process of broadening definitions of IPV, which historically affected women more frequently at the hands of male partners, has been challenging and ongoing.

Recent studies have expanded on this theory, showing how cultural context significantly influences the recognition and naming of IPV. For instance, Goodmark (2018) emphasizes that legal definitions of IPV often fail to capture the lived experiences of survivors, particularly those from marginalized communities. Similarly, Graca (2021) discusses how immigrant women's perceptions of IPV are shaped by their cultural backgrounds and the socio-legal environment in the host country. Building on these perspectives, Storer et al. (2021) illustrate how survivors use digital spaces to articulate harm in their own terms, reinforcing that naming and narration are themselves social acts shaped by context.

Dispute Pyramid

Borrowing from Miller and Sarat's (1981) pyramid of dispute, we can see that several steps are necessary before action is taken by the injured party. At the pyramid's base are unperceived injurious experiences, where IPV victims do not perceive the event as injurious. Naming is the next step, where the IPV victim perceives injury and labels it as injurious. Blaming involves identifying the party responsible for the injurious event, and claiming might involve the IPV victim asking for a remedy or confronting the perpetrator. For a victim of IPV to consider seeking redress, they must first conceive of the experience as injurious. The pyramid's narrowing shape demonstrates that a smaller proportion of unperceived injurious experiences move to the next level (Figure 1).

Recent research supports this model, showing that the progression from unperceived injury to claiming is fraught with barriers, especially for immigrant women. Vidales (2010) highlights that cultural stigma and fear of deportation often prevent Latina immigrants from naming their experiences as IPV. This finding is echoed by Messing et al. (2015), who found that immigrant women are less likely to report IPV due to fear of legal repercussions and mistrust of law enforcement. Together, these studies suggest that the movement from naming to claiming is shaped not only by individual perception but also by institutional trust and legal consciousness—central concerns of the present study.

Barriers to Seeking Help

There are numerous reasons why individuals who believe they have been wronged do not seek help or redress. Vulnerable and stigmatized populations, such as immigrant women, are less likely to progress up the dispute pyramid (Berrey & Nielsen, 2007; Calavita & Jenness, 2013). Factors such as class, race, and access to legal representation significantly impact the likelihood of seeking formal redress (Best & Andreasen, 1977; Curran, 1977; Mayhew, 1975). Lack of access to legal resources may explain the lower numbers of individuals from lower socio-economic or educational levels seeking redress (Sandefur, 2007). This phenomenon is also observed among IPV survivors, where feelings of entitlement, powerlessness, and past experiences influence whether individuals resort to legal means (Bumiller, 1988; Major & Kaiser, 2005; Sandefur, 2007). Additionally, Black (1971) found that existing relationships between parties reduce the likelihood of pursuing legal action compared to when parties are strangers.

Research by Raj and Silverman (2002) underscores the role of cultural norms in shaping help-seeking behaviors among immigrant women. Their study found that cultural beliefs about family unity and gender roles often deter women from seeking help for IPV. Furthermore, Vidales (2010) highlights the significant barrier posed by language, as many immigrant women are unable to access resources due to language barriers. Recent analyses (Nahar & Cronley, 2021) reaffirm that structural and logistical barriers, particularly economic precarity and transportation limits, continue to constrain immigrant women's capacity to act even after recognizing abuse. These insights highlight the importance of examining how sanctuary policies intersect with these enduring barriers.

Barriers for Immigrant IPV Survivors

While IPV affects all communities, undocumented immigrants represent a particularly vulnerable population due to their marginalization, limited alternatives, and additional barriers to accessing resources and services needed to escape violence (Adams & Campbell, 2012; Hass et al., 2000; Menjívar & Salcido, 2002). These barriers are compounded by cultural, religious, or familial pressures that may tolerate or normalize domestic violence among certain groups (Loke, 1996). These barriers are central to understanding how Latina immigrant survivors interpret sanctuary policies and assess whether seeking help is safe.

Fear of Deportation and Legal Repercussions

A significant barrier for immigrant survivors of IPV is the fear of legal consequences, including deportation and family separation. During periods of heightened anti-immigrant sentiment, such as the political climate under the Trump administration, these fears are exacerbated (Capps et al., 2018; López et al., 2017; Moynihan et al., 2008; Orloff et al., 1995; Pierce & Selee, 2017; Vidales, 2010; Yoshikawa et al., 2017). Policy analyses and empirical studies during this period document increased immigration enforcement activity, expanded interior enforcement priorities, and widespread fear among immigrant communities, including those in mixed-status families, contributing to reduced engagement with public institutions and service providers. This fear is particularly acute for undocumented immigrants who lack legal protection and fear that any interaction with law enforcement could lead to deportation.

Recent research underscores this barrier, showing that fear of deportation significantly deters Latina immigrant women from seeking help for IPV (Reina et al., 2014). Similarly, concerns about immigration status and potential deportation are primary reasons why immigrant women do not seek legal recourse or support services (Erez et al., 2009). Additionally, a study highlights that first-generation immigrant women in the U.S. face disproportionately high risks of IPV and related homicides, further emphasizing the severe impact of fear on help-seeking behaviors (Sabri et al., 2025).

Cultural and Linguistic Barriers

Cultural norms and values play a critical role in shaping the experiences of IPV survivors. In many immigrant communities, cultural beliefs about family unity and gender roles can discourage women from reporting abuse or seeking help. Language barriers also complicate access to information about rights and available services (Raj & Silverman, 2002).

Chand (2005) highlight how language barriers can prevent immigrant women from understanding their legal options and navigating the complex legal and social service systems. These barriers often leave immigrant women isolated and reliant on their abusers, further entrenching their vulnerability. Furthermore, a recent review on digital intervention for immigrant IPV survivors emphasizes the necessity of culturally and linguistically appropriate resources to effectively support these women in seeking help and accessing services (Sabri et al., 2025).

Economic Dependence

Economic dependence is another significant barrier for immigrant IPV survivors. Many immigrant women lack the financial resources to leave their abusers, as they may not have access to employment, social services, or financial assistance. This economic vulnerability is often exacerbated by their immigration status, which can limit their ability to work legally and access public benefits (Fussell, 2011; Galemba & Kuhn, 2021). A study by Abramsky et al. (2011) found that women's income is inversely related to their risk of IPV, indicating that economic independence plays a crucial role in mitigating IPV risks. This highlights the need for economic empowerment initiatives tailored to immigrant women to reduce their vulnerability to IPV.

Transportation Barriers

Transportation barriers significantly impact immigrant women experiencing IPV. Insufficient access to reliable transportation limits their ability to seek help, attend legal appointments, or access social services. A study found that transportation is often used as a means of control by abusers, further restricting the mobility and independence of IPV survivors (Nahar & Cronley, 2021). The study also suggests that improving transportation options could significantly aid IPV survivors in accessing necessary services and support.

Taken together, the literature demonstrates that immigrant survivors of intimate partner violence navigate help-seeking within a complex landscape shaped by cultural norms, legal precarity, economic dependency, and fear of state intervention. While prior research has documented the structural and interpersonal barriers that constrain immigrant survivors’ access to formal support, less is known about how sanctuary policies are understood, trusted, and experienced by those they are intended to protect. Existing scholarship offers limited insight into how immigrant survivors interpret the meaning and reliability of sanctuary policies in everyday decision-making about safety, disclosure, and engagement with legal institutions. To address this gap, the present study draws on qualitative interviews with immigrant IPV survivors and service providers in Southern California.

Methods

To explore the help-seeking behavior of undocumented survivors of IPV, this study employed qualitative in-depth semi-structured interviews. Interviews were chosen as the primary data collection method because IPV often occurs in private settings where others may not observe the behavior. Additionally, interviews allow for the exploration of perceptions, thoughts, and emotions experienced by those facing IPV, providing deeper insights into the thought processes of survivors that ultimately led them to seek formal help (Rubin & Rubin, 2012). The study received Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval from the University of California, Irvine (Protocol #12588).

Setting and Sampling

Interviews were conducted at an organization located in Southern California, which serves as a one-stop location where victims of IPV, sexual assault, child abuse, elder abuse, and dependent adult abuse can access referrals, resources, and services from a variety of community partners working within the organization. Among the services and resources available are victim advocacy, restraining order assistance, counseling, child support, emergency shelter referrals, immediate emergency financial assistance (gift cards, vouchers, cash), longer-term financial assistance (CalWorks, SNAP, and Medical), violence prevention educational programs, parenting programs, personal empowerment classes, and legal assistance.

When police officers are called to the scene of a domestic disturbance, they provide the apparent victim with a pamphlet containing information about the organization and available resources. Subsequently, victim advocates at the organization receive police incident reports for all domestic and family disturbances, including incidents of IPV. Victim advocates then contact the named victims in the incident reports to follow up and offer services and resources. Study recruitment occurred through this follow-up process and via flyers displayed in the organization's reception area. Participants recruited for the study were either seeking services (survivors of IPV) or providing services at the organization from late 2018 through 2019. Interviews took place in a private room at the organization and lasted approximately 60 to 90 min. Participants received a $25 gift card as compensation for their time. All participants were reminded that their decision to participate would have no impact on the services they received.

Given the sensitive nature of intimate partner violence, multiple steps were taken to prioritize participant safety throughout recruitment and data collection. Recruitment occurred only after a victim advocate had completed the organization's intake or initial interview and service needs had been identified. At that point, the advocate introduced the researcher, and potential participants were informed about the study and screened for eligibility. Eligible survivors who agreed to participate were interviewed on-site while they were awaiting referrals and services; survivors typically remained at the organization for several hours, which provided sufficient time to conduct interviews without interfering with service provision. This recruitment process also meant that survivors were already present at the organization without the abusive partner, and many arrived accompanied by a supportive friend or family member. Participants were explicitly informed that participation was voluntary, that they could decline or stop at any time without affecting services, and that the researcher was not affiliated with the organization or service providers but was a doctoral student at a local university.

Participants in this study consisted of two groups: individuals who identify as immigrant IPV survivors seeking assistance at the organization and individuals who work or volunteer assisting survivors of IPV. A total of 26 interviews were conducted; 12 were with individuals who self-identified as survivors of IPV and identified as immigrants to the United States. The remaining 14 interviews were with individuals who work or volunteer with survivors of IPV. Including both groups in the study provided a fuller perspective on the impact of sanctuary policies on the formal help-seeking behaviors of immigrant IPV survivors, helping to identify points of divergence and incongruence between the groups on the interview topics (Creswell, 2013).

Immigrant Survivors of IPV

The 12 survivors interviewed all identified as women. They ranged in age from 23 to 55 years of age. The women had migrated from Latin American countries including, Mexico (9), El Salvador (2), and Honduras (1). They lived in the United States for a range of 5 to 33 years. The survivors had 0 to 6 children. The average education level for survivors interviewed was less than high school.

IPV Resource Providers

Of the 14 individuals who work or volunteer with survivors of IPV who were interviewed, ten identified as women and four as men. They had various titles and interacted with survivors in varying capacities, including victim advocates, social workers, therapists, legal aids, law enforcement officers, and resource case managers. They ranged in age from 22 to 62 years of age. On average, they had been assisting survivors from 2 to 36 years.

Data Collection

The interviews were semi-structured, allowing for flexibility in exploring different areas of interest while ensuring that key topics were covered. The interview guide included questions about the participants’ experiences with IPV, their help-seeking behaviors, and their interactions with the organization and law enforcement. The guide also addressed the impact of sanctuary policies on their decision to seek help.

The interview guide included questions such as: “What brought you here today?”; “How did you find out about this place?”; “How long had you known about this place?”; “Did you know California is a sanctuary state?”; “How would you explain the sanctuary policy in your own words?”; “What do you think the sanctuary policy mean for people who may be undocumented or unauthorized?”; “Do sanctuary policies make people who may be undocumented or unauthorized feel safe or safer?”; and “Do you think the policy helps people seek help from law enforcement authorities for domestic violence, specifically those who are undocumented or unauthorized?” Follow-up probes were used to clarify responses and to explore how participants understood safety, risk, and institutional protection.

Interviews were digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim and translated by the researcher for consistency. To ensure confidentiality, all identifying information was removed from the transcripts.

Data Analysis

All interviews were audio-recorded and subsequently transcribed verbatim, capturing nuances such as pauses, sighs, and other non-verbal sounds that may convey additional meaning or emotions beyond the spoken words. These auditory details are crucial for understanding the full context of participants’ experiences (Kvale, 2007). To ensure confidentiality, the audio recordings were deleted immediately after transcription.

The transcriptions were analyzed using thematic analysis, a method for “identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns (themes) within the data” (Braun & Clarke, 2006, p. 79). Thematic analysis is a flexible approach that can be used across various epistemologies and research questions. The process of thematic analysis involved several key steps. Each transcript averaged 15–25 pages. Approximately 120–150 initial codes were generated and refined into 7 core themes using Atlas.ti qualitative-analysis software.

First, I familiarized myself with the data by reading and re-reading the transcripts to become intimately acquainted with the content. This initial phase involved taking preliminary notes and identifying potential codes. The immersion in the data allowed for a deep understanding of the participants’ narratives and contexts. Next, I generated initial codes systematically across the entire data set. This step involved organizing data into meaningful groups and was conducted both manually and using qualitative data analysis software, Atlas.ti. Each transcript was coded line-by-line to capture as much detail as possible. The goal was to ensure that all relevant information was identified and categorized.

Following the coding process, I searched for themes by collating codes into potential themes and gathering all data relevant to each theme. This phase required examining how codes combined to form overarching themes and sub-themes. The aim was to capture patterns in the data that addressed the research questions, thus providing a comprehensive understanding of the participants’ experiences. Themes were then reviewed and refined to ensure they accurately represented the data. This step included checking if themes worked in relation to the coded extracts and the entire data set. Some themes were merged, split, or discarded based on their relevance and coherence. Reviewing themes involved a recursive process of going back and forth between the data and the themes to refine the analysis further.

Once a satisfactory thematic map was developed, themes were defined and named. This involved identifying the essence of each theme and determining what aspect of the data each theme captured. Detailed analysis was written for each theme, considering the story each theme told about the data. This step ensured that the final themes were robust and provided meaningful insights into the research questions. The final step involved producing a detailed report of the analysis. This report included a narrative that integrated data extracts to illustrate the themes and provided a coherent account of the data in relation to the research questions (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The report aimed to present the findings in a clear and accessible manner, highlighting the key insights and implications of the study.

Trustworthiness and Rigor

To ensure analytic credibility, I engaged in reflexive journaling and maintained an audit trail documenting coding decisions and theme development. As the sole researcher, I maintained reflexive journals to document my thoughts, feelings, and potential biases throughout the research process. This practice helped mitigate the influence of personal biases on the analysis (Berger, 2015). Peer debriefing with a senior qualitative scholar familiar with IPV research further enhanced analytic consistency. Furthermore, thick description was provided by detailing the context, participants, and settings, allowing readers to understand the transferability of the findings to other contexts (Geertz, 1973). While all transcripts were coded by a single researcher, peer debriefing was used to review analytic decisions and theme development; nevertheless, the absence of a second independent coder represents a limitation of the study.

Results

Seven interrelated themes emerged from the interviews, reflecting the complex and often contradictory realities faced by both immigrant IPV survivors and service providers navigating California's sanctuary policies. These themes (knowledge gaps, fear of disclosure, cultural and economic barriers, help-seeking influences, blame assignment, legal consciousness, and emotional and social support) illustrate how policy awareness, structural constraints, and social context intersect to shape decisions about seeking formal assistance. Together, they reveal that the process of help-seeking is rarely linear; rather, it is shaped by shifting perceptions of safety, legality, and trust in institutions. These findings highlight how survivors’ lived experiences and service providers’ interpretations of policy converge and diverge in ways that influence both understanding and access to support.

There was a gap in the knowledge between the IPV survivors and IPV service providers interviewed concerning sanctuary policies. IPV service providers tended to have a more positive view and seemed to be more knowledgeable about sanctuary policies and their implications. However, there was a range in the knowledge of sanctuary policies, their application, and implications even within the IPV service providers. IPV Service Providers

All the 14 IPV service providers interviewed claimed they were somewhat aware of California's sanctuary policy and what it was supposed to accomplish. Moreover, all service providers stated that they knew that California had sanctuary policies in place. The respondents noted that their knowledge of sanctuary policy and its implications came from a variety of sources (in order of frequency mentioned), news sources (television, radio, and newspapers), job training, social media (Facebook and Twitter), and friends/family/co-workers. When asked to rate their confidence in providing accurate information to undocumented IPV survivors of sanctuary policy, most (9) stated they were at least somewhat comfortable providing information. However, when the question was framed by asking them to rate their ability to direct an undocumented IPV survivor to accurate information regarding sanctuary policies, most (12) service providers stated they felt very comfortable in their ability to do so. When probed to describe what the current sanctuary policy accomplished, answers varied among respondents. All respondents generally mentioned that the sanctuary policy limited their ability to directly inquire about an individual's documented status. All service providers also noted that the law prohibited state and local agencies from cooperating with ICE.

Service provider respondents mentioned that there had been a noticeable decline in services being provided to immigrant IPV survivors, especially undocumented immigrant survivors, starting with the run-up to the 2016 election, as the political rhetoric often turned to immigration. Although they also noted that the numbers of immigrant IPV survivors they assist have slowly been creeping up. However, victim advocates were quick to point out that the undocumented survivors they do see are very hesitant to reach out for additional assistance or disclose their immigration status. Moreover, victim advocates noted that not being able to ask about citizenship status has made the victim advocates’ task of service and resource referrals much more complicated since many of the services are only available to citizens or legal residents. As one service provider stated, You don't want to get people's hopes up and tell them that they can get a service [only offered to citizens] like access to legal aid or relocation assistance only to then have to tell them, oh sorry, you can't have it after all (Adela, victim advocate, interviewed November 2018).However, the policy has not precisely stopped law enforcement agents’ ability to collect this type of information. Police reports contain such information because although police officers are prohibited from directly asking about an individual's documented status, they do ask for identification from which they can infer legal status. It was not uncommon to see the police reports contain the words US Citizen or Permanent Resident on some reports, but the same box was noticeably empty on other reports. Although, there is no way to be certain that those reports with the missing information were undocumented, it is telling that the information was completed only in those cases where documented status was a non-issue. Victim advocates have also gotten around this by first mentioning which services require citizenship or legal residence and then asking survivors if they are interested in the services. However, there are some resources and services that due to the organizations’ policies and the limited supply, service providers are not allowed to disclose unless the IPV survivor specifically asks for them (e.g., diapers, children's clothes, formula, hotel vouchers, and gift cards). Although these items are available for anyone that requests them many people are not aware that they are available, meaning that those who need them do not necessarily get access to them because they don’t know to ask for them.

IPV Survivors

All 12 IPV survivors interviewed stated they had heard about California having a sanctuary policy. However, all admitted that they were at least somewhat unsure of what precisely the sanctuary policy was supposed to accomplish or what, if any, protections it offered undocumented individuals. When asked if the policy made them feel safer, most (10) respondents admitted that it had not made them feel much safer. As Flor, a respondent mentioned, I'm just not sure if the policy can protect us in reality. There are so many people that don't want us here, you see it more now. I don't know who to trust. You hear different things [about the policy], but then you see and hear that people are still getting detained and sent back. I don't know what to believe. (Flor, interview, October 2018)Another respondent, Claudia (40 years of age, interviewed November 2018), replied, I don't know, I just don't want to risk it. And would rather not find out if the policy does work. I just want to avoid encounters with the government as much as I can.

The most common source of information for IPV survivors was social media and media outlets (e.g., television and radio), followed by friends and family, and other sources (e.g., community meetings, religious organizations). Some respondents (5) were unsure when asked to rate their ability to find accurate information on sanctuary policies. Of those who believed they knew where to access information, three mentioned attorneys but noted they would be unable to afford to access these services. Other sources where respondents stated they could get reliable information on sanctuary policies were relatives/friends, community and religious organizations, and the internet. When asked to define what sanctuary policies explicitly mandated, the answers provided were generally vague. For example, one respondent stated, Well, I think they can't… supposed to deport you. But I don't believe that because you see in the news that they are deporting people. And they are picking people up, so I don't know if I believe it all (Cristina, interview November 2018). Cristina believed that since California had the sanctuary policy in place the law applied in California regardless of whether agents were local, state, or federal. It didn’t make sense that an agent could be in California and not follow the law of the state which made her view the law as powerless or ineffective. All respondents echoed this type of uncertainty of the actual power of the sanctuary policy and fear of interaction with law enforcement and other governmental agencies. The fear was described by Cristina (who was accompanied by her roommate), stating, [I'm] A little scared, but my friend came with me. She said I needed to come and get the restraining order so that [he] can stay away from me. She's also tired of hearing the fights, and she doesn't want to see me sad and hurt and scared and just not happy. It's just scary when you come to the police with no real papers. I don't know what can happen. […] But I trust my friend. (Cristina, Interview, November 2018)

Moreover, the respondents seemed to see an issue in the sanctuary policy as it stands. Although the policy prohibits law enforcement from asking about an individual's documented status, it is routine for law enforcement agents to ask for identification. As one respondent (Flor) said, The [sanctuary] policy means they [law enforcement] can't ask you if you are legal. But then they ask for your license, and then they can see because they are different, and they know. Oh, you don't have papers. Flor went on to say that she avoids interactions and places where she is required to show identification for fear that she will be questioned or reported to authorities.

Overcoming Barriers and Obstacles

Participants’ accounts of overcoming fear, shame, and dependency illustrate how interpersonal support networks (especially friends, family members, and children) served as catalysts for help-seeking. These narratives highlight that seeking help was rarely an individual act but rather a collective process shaped by relationships of care, responsibility, and moral obligation. Many of the IPV survivors interviewed did not seek help immediately after their partner's abusive behavior was acknowledged as problematic and labeled as IPV, often waiting years before doing so. It is interesting to note that the IPV survivors interviewed all mentioned other individuals as instrumental or the reason they finally decided to seek help at the organization These other individuals were often children, followed by other family members (siblings and mothers), friends, and other acquaintances (co-workers and roommates). Additionally, nearly all the women interviewed had been accompanied to the organization by a female friend or family member (including children). Furthermore, most (9) of the IPV survivors interviewed admitted that they had not been the ones that had contacted law enforcement for the incident for which they had subsequently been referred to the organization. In these cases, it had been a witness who had initiated law enforcement contact, these included children, family members, neighbors, roommates, or bystanders.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate how Latina immigrant survivors’ help-seeking decisions are shaped by a convergence of legal uncertainty, fear of deportation, economic dependence, and relational obligations to children and family members. While sanctuary policies are often understood as offering symbolic protection, participants’ accounts suggest that these policies do not consistently translate into feelings of safety or trust in law enforcement. Instead, survivors’ decisions to seek, or avoid, formal assistance reflect a broader legal consciousness informed by prior experiences, community narratives, and perceived risks associated with immigration enforcement. These findings highlight the gap between the intended protections of sanctuary policies and their lived meaning for immigrant survivors navigating intimate partner violence. The following section situates these findings within existing scholarship and theoretical frameworks to examine their broader implications.

Discussion

This discussion examines how the findings illuminate the gap between the intended protections of sanctuary policies and their lived meaning for Latina immigrant survivors of intimate partner violence. Drawing on legal consciousness theory and prior scholarship on immigrant help-seeking and state avoidance, the discussion situates survivors’ narratives within broader debates about law, safety, and institutional trust. In doing so, it highlights how legal status, fear of deportation, and relational obligations shape survivors’ interpretations of sanctuary policies and their decisions to engage with formal systems of support. The section concludes by considering the implications of these findings for policy, practice, and future research.

This study explored how sanctuary policies shape immigrant survivors’ help-seeking behaviors and perceptions of safety, legality, and belonging. Through interviews with immigrant IPV survivors and service providers in Southern California, the findings revealed gaps in policy understanding, persistent fears of deportation, and tensions between immediate safety and longer-term survival. As anticipated, there appeared to be a decrease in the number of immigrant clients accessing resources and services for IPV at the OCJFC following the 2016 election. This decline, attributed to the rise of anti-immigrant rhetoric, was expected yet feared by service providers (Capps et al., 2018; López et al., 2017; Pierce & Selee, 2017; Yoshikawa et al., 2017). However, providers noted that the number of immigrant IPV survivors had begun increasing again, attributing this rise to the adoption of state and local sanctuary policies (Cruz Nichols et al., 2018; Martínez-Schuldt & Martínez, 2021). Despite these policies, the interviews revealed a significant gap in knowledge about sanctuary policies between immigrant survivors of IPV and IPV service providers.

Policy Awareness and Legal Consciousness

Service providers believed that immigrant IPV survivors understood the implications of sanctuary policies, assuming these survivors were aware they would not be asked about their immigration status. However, survivors expressed confusion about the exact purpose of these policies and remained uncertain about their ability to protect them from detention and deportation. This finding aligns with prior research, which indicates that immigrant communities often have limited knowledge and understanding of legal protections available to them (Messing et al., 2015; Reina et al., 2014). More recent work similarly highlights that awareness alone does not translate into trust, particularly in contexts where survivors have experienced or witnessed immigration enforcement despite policy protections (Cruz Nichols et al., 2018; Martínez-Schuldt & Martínez, 2021).

Immigrant IPV survivors highlighted a discrepancy in the policy that disallows state and local law enforcement officers from asking about a person's immigration status while still requiring the presentation of identification documents. Survivors reported being asked to provide identification during their interactions with law enforcement, even when identified as the victim. Sometimes, the only valid form of identification was from their country of origin, which they felt revealed their undocumented status to law enforcement. These narratives underscore how legal consciousness operates within unequal power structures: survivors’ interpretations of policy were informed less by formal law than by everyday experiences of surveillance and exclusion (Ewick & Silbey, 1998; Solórzano, 2022).

Fear, Trust, and Identification Practices

For law enforcement and other IPV service providers, the everyday practice of asking for identification seemed inconsequential, bolstered by the view that sanctuary policies rendered it a trivial formality. Although California allows non-citizens and undocumented people to obtain a driver's license, differences between licenses of undocumented individuals and their documented counterparts could still indirectly reveal an individual's status. The requirements for obtaining an AB 60 license, proof of California residency, identity, and an affidavit regarding social security and presence in the U.S., posed additional barriers, as many survivors hesitated to interact with governmental agencies or situations requiring identification (García, 2009).

Participants’ accounts suggest that this identification process functions as an informal mechanism of social control, signaling who belongs and who is subject to scrutiny. Similar patterns have been observed in other immigrant contexts where bureaucratic procedures reproduce fear and constrain mobility (Abrego et al., 2017; Cruz Nichols et al., 2018; Hulley et al., 2023). This avoidance of governmental agencies contributed to the fact that most immigrant IPV survivors had not initiated contact with law enforcement for the IPV they experienced.

Cultural and Economic Realities

Applying Miller and Sarat's pyramid of dispute to immigrant IPV survivors is complex. Survivors often perceived injury only in cases of physical harm, struggled to assign blame for the violence, and were hesitant to make claims by calling law enforcement. Many attributed the violence to factors such as financial stress, alcohol consumption, or substance abuse, and some even blamed themselves for the violence. These findings are consistent with prior studies that highlight the complex dynamics of blame and help-seeking in IPV contexts (Bumiller, 1988; Crenshaw, 2013).

Even when blame was assigned to their partners, survivors were reluctant to contact law enforcement despite awareness of California's sanctuary policy. Previous research demonstrates that individuals from lower socio-economic backgrounds, lower education levels, non-whites, and those in relationships are less likely to seek legal redress (Black, 1976; Bumiller, 1988; Sandefur, 2007). Although contacting law enforcement is not the only way to seek redress, it was notably absent among the preferred methods for the interviewed immigrant IPV survivors. The findings also reinforce that economic precarity intersects with cultural norms of endurance and family unity, shaping survivors’ calculations of risk and responsibility. Economic dependence, gendered expectations, and intergenerational obligations collectively sustain decisions to remain silent or avoid formal systems (Galemba & Kuhn, 2021; Sabri & Campbell, 2024).

Community Support and Companionship

However, when law enforcement became involved—either by the victim or a third party—all women eventually sought resources from the organization. For those whose law enforcement involvement was involuntary, resources were often sought at the prompting of friends, family members, or acquaintances, who were trusted and seen as knowledgeable about resources and sanctuary policy. Immigrant IPV survivors were more likely to seek help when they perceived that someone other than themselves, such as their children, was in danger. Concerns about potential negative repercussions for their abusers, whether documented or undocumented, also influenced their decisions, as these repercussions could indirectly affect their children.

Many survivors hoped that the involvement of law enforcement would act as a wake-up call for their partners and lead to change. Surprisingly, some survivors who wanted to leave their partners still hoped their children could maintain relationships with their fathers. This reflects the survivors’ belief in their partners’ ability to parent and provide financially, separate from their abusive behavior towards themselves.

These findings highlight the relational dimension of help-seeking: decisions were rarely individual but embedded in moral economies of care and interdependence. Trust in peers, rather than in institutions, was the most reliable pathway to safety—a finding consistent with emerging literature emphasizing community-based and relational approaches to IPV intervention among immigrant populations (Hulley et al., 2023; Rudzinski et al., 2025).

In deciding whether to involve law enforcement or seek help, immigrant IPV survivors considered multiple factors, including finances, fear of detention or deportation, and their family's well-being. Many survivors were unsure if they could fulfill their family's financial needs without their partners. While IPV service providers viewed immediate safety from IPV as paramount, survivors were more concerned about detention or deportation and the inability to meet physiological needs in their home countries. Love needs were also complicated, as survivors often felt that their partners, despite their abusive behavior, genuinely loved them and were good parents.

These findings highlight the complex interplay of factors influencing immigrant IPV survivors’ help-seeking behaviors. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for developing more effective support systems and policies that address the unique challenges faced by immigrant IPV survivors. Future research should continue to explore these issues, particularly the impact of sanctuary policies on immigrant communities’ trust and interactions with law enforcement and social services.

Taken together, these findings underscore that sanctuary policies, while symbolically protective, cannot on their own resolve the deep structural and affective barriers that shape immigrant survivors’ access to safety. Policies that fail to address economic precarity, social isolation, and mistrust of law enforcement will continue to have limited reach. Future research should therefore explore how sanctuary frameworks can be expanded through culturally grounded, community-engaged models that rebuild trust while protecting survivors from both interpersonal and institutional harm.

Conclusion & Implications

This study set out to explore the role that sanctuary policies play in the decision of immigrant survivors of IPV to seek help for the IPV they experience. The interviews exposed some discrepancies between the immigrant women's knowledge about sanctuary policies and that of IPV service providers. Moreover, it appears that IPV service providers believed that immigrants were more knowledgeable of the policy than immigrant IPV survivors revealed in interviews. Furthermore, the interviews revealed that even when IPV survivors were aware of existing sanctuary policies, they felt unsure of the policy's ability to protect them from detention or deportation. These findings indicate a continuing gap between formal policy protections and the perceived safety of immigrant survivors.

Policy Implications

More information on the purpose, extent, and limitations of sanctuary policies should be disseminated to ensure that sanctuary policies are more fully understood by immigrant community members. Additionally, service providers and those interacting with the public may want to reconsider assumptions about people's knowledge about sanctuary policies and make the information more readily available to all they serve. Moreover, IPV service providers should be more aware of the vulnerability and exposure which immigrant IPV survivors feel when they are required to provide identification which the immigrant survivors viewed akin to disclosure of their immigration and/or undocumented status.

The fear felt by the immigrant IPV respondents is not unfounded. Currently California currently has implemented Sanctuary Policies to “protect” undocumented immigrants residing in that state by limiting cooperation between local and state law enforcement agencies and federal immigration agencies. However, at other times the state has implemented laws like California Proposition 187 which were detrimental to undocumented immigrants and prohibited them from using non-emergency health care, public education, and other services in the state. Furthermore, the California Values Act faced pushback from some communities in the state including communities in Orange County, CA where the study took place. The city of Los Alomitos (located in Orange County) was the first but not the last city to pushback against the policy vowing have local law enforcement to continue cooperating with federal immigration officials (Sanchez, 2018). Then came news in 2019 that ICE had requested the use of DMV Driver's License databases in three states to identify people suspected of being in the country illegally (Shahani, 2019). What is worse is that the three states identified (Utah, Washington, and Vermont) are states which allow undocumented immigrants to get drivers licenses. These examples reinforce immigrant survivors’ skepticism and suggest that even protective policies can feel uncertain in practice. In one hand these states are signaling that they are friendly towards undocumented immigrants but then using the information provided by these immigrants to assist ICE. If the goal is truly to assist immigrant IPV survivors, then sanctuary policies should be bolstered to ensure that immigrant community members are more fully protected and able to enjoy being a full member of the community where they reside without fear of detention or deportation.

Practice Implications

The study also aimed to understand better the process by which immigrant IPV survivors come to seek redress for the IPV they experience. Some of the immigrant IPV survivors mentioned other factors as responsible for the IPV. Interestingly, it seems that most IPV survivors did not choose to involve law enforcement. Instead, most IPV survivors found law enforcement being called upon by a third-party witness (family member, roommate, friend, co-worker). Although their immigration status did play a role in their hesitancy to call upon law enforcement for IPV, they experienced it was not the only reason they hesitated. Other reasons which prevented them from seeking assistance from law enforcement included wanting to keep family intact, finances, hoping the situation would improve, shame or embarrassment, and fear of negative impact for their partner which are very similar to the reasons given by non-immigrant IPV survivors (Overstreet & Quinn, 2013; Petersen et al., 2005; Storer et al., 2021). Moreover, some of the IPV survivors shifted the culpability away from the perpetrators and blamed factors outside of the perpetrator; these factors included financial stress, employment stress, alcohol (and other substances), and even themselves. If the blame is not being placed on the perpetrator, it is difficult to seek redress from the perpetrator since they are not seen at fault. This pattern highlights how survivors often internalize broader cultural expectations of endurance and self-blame, which can further delay help-seeking.

For practitioners working with immigrant IPV survivors—including social workers, advocates, case managers, counselors, and other service providers—these findings suggest several concrete practice recommendations. Practitioners should avoid assuming that survivors’ awareness of sanctuary policies translates into trust or perceived safety; instead, they should explicitly invite questions and provide clear, consistent explanations of what sanctuary policies do and do not protect. Practitioners should also minimize practices that survivors experience as exposing or surveilling, such as unnecessary requests for identification, and clearly explain when identification is required and how any information will be used. In addition, safety planning should explicitly account for survivors’ relational obligations, including concerns about children, partners’ immigration status, and ongoing contact with abusive partners. Finally, practitioners should recognize the role of trusted third parties (such as friends or family members who accompany survivors) and consider ways to ethically include these individuals in support and safety-planning processes when appropriate.

This study examined the help-seeking behavior of immigrant survivors of IPV during a particularly tense anti-immigrant former presidency in the US. The results indicate that there is still work to be done when it comes to informing the public about the exact nature and purpose of sanctuary policies. Furthermore, immigrant community members residing in locations with sanctuary policies need more reassurance of the policy's ability to protect them. The policy must find a way to bolster protections and ensure that any information collected will be safe beyond the current administration. Moreover, agencies and organizations aiding immigrant community members need to re-evaluate their policies and procedures to ensure that the people from immigrant backgrounds accessing these resources feel comfortable doing so. For example, the organization is in Orange County, CA, and falls under the California, still requires individuals entering the facility to show identification. However, during the interviews, it became apparent that the immigrant IPV survivors were afraid that showing their identification would out them as immigrants. History and their experiences demonstrate that the fear is not unfounded.

Research Implications

The study also found that most of those accessing resources at the organization came with a friend who offered emotional support to the immigrant IPV survivors. This demonstrates for educational campaigns to go beyond informing and educating IPV survivors but also providing information and education to the public on how to support those experiencing IPV. Furthermore, the immigrant IPV survivors describe their companion as knowledgeable of resources and sanctuary policies which made them more comfortable to go to the organization. Unfortunately, this study did not interview the companions of the immigrant IPV survivors. However, future research should explore the role of the companions of IPV survivors in their decision to seek formal and legal assistance for the IPV they experience. Additionally, given that most of the immigrant IPV survivors intended to continue some sort of contact with their romantic partner (IPV perpetrator), it would be helpful to provide the IPV survivors with resources and services that can help make such contact safe.

Strengths & Limitations

This study contributes valuable insight into immigrant survivors’ perspectives during a politically charged period while also acknowledging several limitations. A key methodological strength of the study is its use of in-depth qualitative interviews with both immigrant IPV survivors and IPV service providers, which allowed for triangulation across perspectives and a contextualized understanding of how sanctuary policies are interpreted and experienced in practice.

The period in which this study took place was unique, marked by overt anti-immigrant rhetoric from many in leadership positions, including the then U.S. President. During this period, federal policy shifts, expanded interior immigration enforcement, and public rhetoric contributed to widespread fear and avoidance of public institutions among immigrant communities (Capps et al., 2018; López et al., 2017; Pierce & Selee, 2017; Yoshikawa et al., 2017). The rise of sanctuary policies during this period were primarily a response to the anti-immigrant rhetoric espoused by President Trump. Although the 2020 election brought a new administration under President Joe Biden, there remains a loud anti-immigrant movement.

Additionally, while California and some Californian cities adopted sanctuary policies, other cities and agencies, including in Orange County, fought against these policies, vowing to continue working with ICE. The contradictory sanctuary policies within the county may have led respondents to doubt the effectiveness of sanctuary policies in their communities. The proximity of cities and counties with opposing stances on sanctuary policies added to the confusion and uncertainty.

Finally, the study recruited participants who had elected to go to the organization for additional services and resources, meaning these individuals had already overcome some fear and hesitation to interact with formal organizations. As such, the findings may not fully reflect the perspectives of those who continue to avoid formal support systems. Results may differ significantly for those who chose not to seek resources and services through the organization. In addition, all interviews were coded and analyzed by a single researcher; although reflexive journaling and peer debriefing were used to enhance analytic rigor, the absence of a second independent coder represents a methodological limitation.

Future Directions

Future research should continue to explore the impact of sanctuary policies on immigrant communities’ trust and interactions with law enforcement and social services. It should also investigate the role of companions in IPV survivors’ decisions to seek formal and legal assistance. Given the complexity of the factors influencing help-seeking behaviors among immigrant IPV survivors, developing more effective support systems and policies that address these unique challenges is crucial. Additionally, educational campaigns should extend to the public, providing information on how to support IPV survivors. Comparative studies across states with different policy environments could clarify how sanctuary policies influence perceptions of safety and access to services. Further studies should also examine how to make interactions with law enforcement and service providers safer for immigrant IPV survivors, ensuring they do not feel compelled to disclose their immigration status.

Building on these directions, future research would benefit from more clearly specified empirical designs. One qualitative study could involve in-depth interviews with companions—such as friends, family members, or roommates—who accompany immigrant IPV survivors to service organizations, focusing on how these individuals understand sanctuary policies and influence survivors’ help-seeking decisions. A complementary quantitative survey could examine immigrant survivors’ knowledge of sanctuary policies, trust in law enforcement, and willingness to seek help, comparing undocumented, mixed-status, and documented populations across sanctuary and non-sanctuary jurisdictions. Finally, a mixed-methods design combining interviews with service providers and survey data from survivors could assess how organizational practices, including identification requirements and information-sharing protocols, shape perceptions of safety, trust, and access to services.
