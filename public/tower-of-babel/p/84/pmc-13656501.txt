A systematic policy review of faculty development valuation in health professions education
Authors: Sol Roberts-Lieb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeContinuous professional development is essential for maintaining teaching excellence in health professions education. However, the extent to which faculty development is valued in high-stakes personnel decisions remains unclear. This study conducted a comprehensive systematic review of the seven core health professions to determine how participation in faculty development is codified and incentivized within advancement, promotion, and tenure (APT) policies.MethodA definitive study population (N = 3,101) was established using official accreditation directories for dentistry, medicine (MD/DO), nursing, pharmacy, physical therapy, and physician assistant programs in the United States. We employed a comprehensive, automated data harvesting pipeline to retrieve public policy documents via the Google Custom Search API. Following PRISMA guidelines, an automated content refinery filtered the dataset to 10,850 valid policy documents. Thematic analysis quantified the prevalence of faculty development terminology and requirements.ResultsText analysis of the final corpus identified 29,812 unique policy excerpts related to faculty development. The most dominant themes were Process/Requirement (41.5%) and General Terminology (41.4%), while Assessment requirements appeared in 13% of excerpts, and explicit Formal Structure requirements (e.g., Teaching Academies) appeared in only 4.1% of excerpts. A significant prevalence gap emerged between disciplines: Physician Assistant programs were the most likely to reference professional development (65.3%), whereas Physical Therapy programs were the least likely (11.0%). Pharmacy (28.6%) and Medicine (MD: 49.4%; DO: 32.3%) fell in the middle range.ConclusionsWhile universities frequently reference professional development as a general value, they rarely codify it as a requirement for tenure. A systemic Input/Output Paradox exists: institutions rigorously assess teaching effectiveness (outputs) but treat the developmental activities necessary to achieve it (inputs) as voluntary resources rather than professional standards. Background

The pedagogical and economic imperative

Universities invest substantial institutional resources in faculty development, recognizing that teaching effectiveness is a learned and continuously evolving skill rather than an innate ability. These activities—including training in active learning, curriculum design, assessment strategies, and educational leadership—are not merely supplemental but foundational to instructional quality. Participation in faculty development has been shown to improve educator self-efficacy, teaching practices, and learner outcomes, reinforcing its role as a critical component of academic excellence [1].

Beyond pedagogy, faculty development represents a significant institutional investment. Centers for teaching and learning, faculty academies, and structured development programs require sustained financial and organizational support. As such, the value assigned to faculty development within advancement systems has implications not only for individual faculty behavior but also for institutional return on investment.

The regulatory mandate

The importance of faculty development extends beyond institutional priorities to formal regulatory expectations. All major health professions accrediting bodies in the United States explicitly require institutions to provide faculty development opportunities, including the Liaison Committee on Medical Education (LCME), Commission on Osteopathic College Accreditation (COCA), Commission on Collegiate Nursing Education (CCNE), Accreditation Council for Pharmacy Education (ACPE), Commission on Accreditation in Physical Therapy Education (CAPTE), Commission on Dental Accreditation (CODA), and the Accreditation Review Commission on Education for the Physician Assistant (ARC-PA) [2–8]. Accreditation standards requiring faculty development

Taken together, these standards establish faculty development as a universal requirement across health professions education. However, accreditation standards focus primarily on the provision of development opportunities rather than the valuation of participation within advancement, promotion, and tenure systems. This creates a potential disconnect between regulatory expectations and institutional reward structures.

The incentive gap

Despite the widespread availability of faculty development programs, their utilization is shaped by the incentive structures embedded within advancement, promotion, and tenure systems. Faculty must make strategic decisions about how to allocate limited time and effort, prioritizing activities that are explicitly recognized and rewarded.

This tension reflects a longstanding challenge in higher education. Boyer’s reconceptualization of scholarship emphasized the importance of teaching as a scholarly activity, calling for its recognition within faculty reward systems [9]. However, subsequent research suggests that promotion criteria remain heavily weighted toward traditional research outputs, particularly bibliometric indicators [10].

Within this context, faculty development may be positioned as a voluntary resource rather than a professional requirement, creating ambiguity about its role in career advancement. This misalignment can produce conflicting signals, where faculty are encouraged to improve teaching but evaluated primarily on other forms of productivity. These tensions have also been associated with faculty retention, career satisfaction, and perceptions of institutional support within academic medicine [11].

Defining the construct

In health professions education, the term “professional development” is often used broadly and may include activities related to clinical practice, licensure maintenance, or continuing education. For the purposes of this study, faculty development is defined as activities explicitly designed to improve faculty members’ effectiveness in their roles as educators, scholars, and academic leaders, consistent with established definitions in the literature [1].

This distinction is critical. Many institutional policies conflate clinical continuing education with pedagogical development, obscuring the specific role of teaching-focused activities within advancement, promotion, and tenure systems. Accordingly, this study excludes references to clinical continuing education (e.g., CME, CE, board certification) and focuses exclusively on development activities related to teaching, curriculum design, assessment, and academic leadership.

Study objective

While accreditation standards mandate the provision of faculty development opportunities, it remains unclear whether participation in these activities is formally recognized or rewarded within advancement systems. As a result, institutional leaders and advancement committees lack a comprehensive understanding of how peer institutions operationalize and value faculty development within promotion and tenure processes.

This study addresses this gap by conducting a systematic policy review across the seven core health professions education disciplines: Dentistry, Medicine (MD and DO), Nursing, Pharmacy, Physical Therapy, and Physician Assistant.

The central research question guiding this study is:

How do universities incorporate faculty development participation within advancement, promotion, and tenure policies?

To address this question, the study pursues two specific aims:

Aim 1: To systematically identify and analyze APT policies to determine the prevalence and context of faculty development language.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a systematic policy review to examine how faculty development is incorporated into advancement, promotion, and tenure (APT) policies across seven core health professions education disciplines in the United States: Dentistry, Medicine (MD and DO), Nursing, Pharmacy, Physical Therapy, and Physician Assistant.

Because faculty development expectations are often embedded implicitly within institutional governance documents rather than articulated as discrete policy statements, a large-scale automated document analysis approach was necessary to capture both explicit requirements and implicit valuation. The review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, adapted for policy and governance document analysis.

Detailed technical implementation, including data harvesting scripts, processing workflows, and reproducibility documentation, is provided in the Online Supplementary Materials.

Study population

The study population consisted of all accredited U.S. programs within the seven targeted health professions (N = 3,101), identified directly from official accreditation directories as of December 2025.

Accrediting bodies included the Liaison Committee on Medical Education (MD), Commission on Osteopathic College Accreditation (DO), Commission on Collegiate Nursing Education (Nursing), Accreditation Review Commission on Education for the Physician Assistant (Physician Assistant), Commission on Accreditation in Physical Therapy Education (Physical Therapy), Accreditation Council for Pharmacy Education (Pharmacy), and Commission on Dental Accreditation (Dentistry).

Because advancement, promotion, and tenure policies are typically governed at the institutional level rather than the individual program level, all accredited programs were mapped to their parent institutions. This produced a definitive set of institutional search targets, ensuring comprehensive coverage across institutional types, geographic regions, and organizational structures.

Policy identification and retrieval

Publicly available APT-related policy documents were identified using an automated, multi-tiered web search strategy designed to capture both centralized (institution-wide) and decentralized (college- or unit-specific) policy structures.

Search queries were executed using the Google Custom Search API between November and December 2025. To improve retrieval consistency, all institutions were searched using a standardized tiered query structure combining institutional identifiers with predefined governance-related keywords. Retrieved records were logged and screened using a structured workflow that included duplicate detection, eligibility screening, and automated classification. Representative search strings and workflow details are provided in Supplementary Appendix A. A tiered query architecture was employed to systematically target three levels of institutional organization:

Search terms included combinations of institutional names with keywords such as “faculty handbook,” “promotion criteria,” “tenure policy,” and “advancement guidelines.”

To account for variation in document hosting and format, both downloadable files (e.g., PDF, Word documents) and policy text hosted directly on institutional web pages were included. When downloadable documents were not available, HTML content was programmatically extracted and processed.

Only publicly accessible documents were eligible for inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

For purposes of this study, governance language was defined as institutional language describing expectations, requirements, procedures, criteria, or standards related to faculty advancement, promotion, tenure, or reappointment. Policy documents were defined as formal institutional materials intended to guide or govern faculty evaluation processes, including faculty handbooks, promotion criteria documents, bylaws, and related governance resources.

Documents were included if they contained governance language related to faculty advancement (e.g., promotion, tenure, reappointment) and referenced faculty development or educator-focused professional development activities.

To distinguish pedagogical faculty development from clinical continuing education, documents referencing licensure maintenance, board certification, or continuing medical or professional education (CME/CE) were excluded. These exclusions were applied because the study focused specifically on educator development rather than maintenance of clinical competence.

Non-policy materials—including student handbooks, course catalogs, faculty curricula vitae, meeting minutes, and administrative agendas—were systematically excluded through automated filtering and manual validation.

To assess classification accuracy, a random 5% sample of both included and excluded documents was manually reviewed. This sampling threshold was selected as a pragmatic validation strategy for large-scale document analysis, balancing feasibility with sufficient coverage to identify systematic inclusion or exclusion errors.

Text extraction and thematic analysis

All eligible documents were converted to machine-readable text and analyzed using an automated text-mining approach. A predefined lexicon of educator-centric development terms (e.g., faculty development, pedagogy, teaching academy, instructional design, mentoring) was used to identify relevant excerpts within APT-related contexts.

The lexicon was developed through a multi-step process. First, foundational definitions of faculty development in health professions education informed the identification of core conceptual terms [1]. Second, terminology embedded within accreditation standards across the seven professions was reviewed to capture discipline-specific language. Third, a pilot review of policy documents from a subset of institutions was conducted to identify common lexical variants and contextual usage patterns. The keyword taxonomy was iteratively refined during screening to reduce false positives and minimize the risk of under-identification. Despite iterative refinement, terminology variability across institutions remains a potential source of under-identification, particularly when faculty development activities were described using institution-specific language not represented within the search lexicon.

For each identified term, surrounding text was extracted to allow assessment of how faculty development was operationalized within policy language. Extracted excerpts were categorized into four predefined thematic domains:

Validation and human oversight

Although document identification and initial text extraction were automated, all classification, thematic assignment, and synthesis steps involved human oversight.

Manual review of the 5% validation sample was used to verify the accuracy of inclusion criteria and keyword filtering. Discrepancies identified during this process informed iterative refinement of the lexicon and filtering rules.

In addition, AI-assisted summarization tools were used to support large-scale thematic aggregation; however, all outputs were reviewed and interpreted by the author to ensure consistency, contextual accuracy, and alignment with study objectives.

As a single-author study, structured validation procedures and full transparency of analytic methods were employed to mitigate the risk of interpretive bias.

Detailed technical specifications of the AI-assisted analysis workflow are provided in the Online Supplementary Materials.

Ethics

This study analyzed publicly available institutional policy documents and did not involve human subjects. As such, it meets criteria for exemption from Institutional Review Board (IRB) review under U.S. federal guidelines.

Results

Corpus identification and screening

The automated search process identified 16,574 documents across all targeted institutions. Following application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 10,850 documents were retained for analysis. The document identification, screening, and inclusion process is summarized in Fig. 1.

The final dataset represents a large cross-professional corpus of publicly available APT-related policy documents across U.S. health professions education.

Prevalence of faculty development language

Of the 3,101 accredited programs included in the study, 875 (28.2%) contained explicit references to faculty development within APT-related policy documents.

Substantial variation was observed across disciplines. Physician Assistant programs demonstrated the highest prevalence (65.3%), followed by Medicine (MD: 49.4%; DO: 32.3%). Prevalence of faculty development references across disciplines

General terminology

General references to faculty development were the most frequently observed form of inclusion, accounting for 41.4% of all identified excerpts.

These references typically appeared as broad, aspirational statements emphasizing continuous improvement, lifelong learning, or institutional commitment to teaching excellence. Faculty development was often framed as an expected component of professional identity, though not necessarily tied to specific advancement criteria.

Representative language included phrases such as “ongoing professional growth,” “commitment to teaching excellence,” and “participation in development activities,” without explicit requirements or evaluative consequences.

Process and requirement

References categorized within the Process/Requirement domain accounted for 41.5% of all excerpts, representing the most structured form of inclusion.

These statements described expectations for participation in faculty development as part of advancement processes, including requirements for documentation within promotion dossiers, evidence of engagement in teaching improvement activities, or participation in institutional development programs.

However, even within this domain, language frequently emphasized participation rather than enforcement, and requirements were often framed as recommendations or expectations rather than mandatory criteria.

Assessment criteria

Assessment-related references accounted for 13.0% of excerpts and represented instances in which faculty development activities were incorporated into evaluative frameworks.

These included consideration of participation in development programs as supporting evidence for teaching effectiveness, inclusion of development activities in teaching portfolios, or acknowledgment of pedagogical improvement efforts within performance reviews.

Notably, faculty development was rarely positioned as a primary evaluative criterion. Instead, it functioned as a supplementary indicator used to contextualize teaching performance rather than as a standalone requirement.

Formal structure

Formal structural references were the least prevalent domain, accounting for 4.1% of excerpts.

These references described institutional mechanisms supporting faculty development, such as teaching and learning centers, faculty academies, or structured development programs. While these structures provide access to development opportunities, they were infrequently linked directly to advancement requirements or evaluation processes.

Cross-domain synthesis

Across domains, a consistent pattern emerged in which faculty development was widely referenced but infrequently codified as a formal requirement within APT systems.

General Terminology and Process/Requirement domains emphasized participation and engagement, while Assessment Criteria and Formal Structure domains demonstrated limited integration into formal evaluation and promotion processes.

Synthesis of faculty development valuation

The combined findings reveal a structured but uneven pattern of valuation across APT systems. Faculty development is consistently positioned as an institutional expectation, yet its integration into formal advancement criteria remains limited.

Cross-professional variation

Variation across disciplines was a defining feature of the findings. Physician Assistant programs demonstrated the highest level of integration of faculty development within APT policies, including more explicit expectations for participation and documentation.

In contrast, Physical Therapy programs demonstrated the lowest prevalence despite accreditation standards that explicitly require regular participation in professional development activities. This discrepancy suggests a potential misalignment between accreditation expectations and institutional policy language.

Other disciplines, including Medicine and Pharmacy, demonstrated intermediate patterns, reflecting partial integration of faculty development within advancement frameworks.

Discussion

The input/output paradox in faculty evaluation

This study identifies a consistent pattern across health professions education in which teaching effectiveness is rigorously evaluated while the developmental activities that support it are inconsistently required or rewarded. This Input/Output Paradox reflects a structural misalignment within advancement, promotion, and tenure systems.

Across the dataset, faculty development was frequently referenced in general or procedural terms (82.9% combined across General Terminology and Process/Requirement domains), yet rarely embedded as a formal evaluative criterion (13.0%) or supported through explicit structural integration (4.1%). In effect, institutions emphasize the outcomes of teaching—such as learner performance, course evaluations, and instructional effectiveness—while leaving the processes that enable those outcomes under-specified.

This pattern aligns with broader theories of institutional behavior. Organizational structures often reflect symbolic alignment with external expectations while preserving internal flexibility in implementation. In this context, faculty development may function as a normative expectation without being consistently operationalized within formal reward systems.

Uneven valuation across disciplines

A second major finding is the substantial variation across health professions disciplines in how faculty development is represented within APT policies.

Physician Assistant programs demonstrated the highest prevalence of faculty development language (65.3%), suggesting a more explicit integration of development activities within advancement frameworks. In contrast, Physical Therapy programs demonstrated the lowest prevalence (11.0%), despite accreditation standards that explicitly require regular participation in professional development activities.

This discrepancy highlights a potential misalignment between external accreditation requirements and internal policy structures. While accreditation bodies establish expectations for faculty development, the translation of these expectations into institutional APT policies appears inconsistent across disciplines.

It is important to note that this study does not establish causal relationships between accreditation standards and institutional policy language. Rather, the findings highlight variation in how disciplines operationalize similar regulatory expectations within advancement systems.

Additionally, the present analysis did not differentiate between clinical and foundational science faculty roles, as such distinctions are inconsistently represented in institutional policy documents. Future research may explore whether expectations for faculty development differ across these roles.

Faculty development as rhetoric versus requirement

The findings suggest that faculty development occupies a dual role within institutional policy language: it is widely articulated as a value but less frequently enforced as a requirement.

Beyond faculty-level implications, these findings raise broader governance questions. If institutions invest substantial resources in faculty development while omitting meaningful recognition within advancement systems, a disconnect may emerge between organizational priorities and incentive structures. Such misalignment may limit participation, reduce perceived value, and complicate institutional efforts to promote teaching excellence. From a governance perspective, promotion policies serve not only as evaluative instruments but also as signals of institutional priorities. The limited integration of faculty development into formal advancement criteria suggests that many institutions continue to position teaching improvement as a cultural expectation rather than a codified professional responsibility.

General Terminology and Process/Requirement domains emphasize participation in development activities, often framed as part of professional identity or institutional culture. However, the relative absence of faculty development within Assessment Criteria and Formal Structure domains indicates limited integration into formal evaluation processes.

This distinction reflects a broader tension between aspirational and operational policy language. Institutions may promote faculty development as a shared value while maintaining flexibility in how it is interpreted and applied within advancement decisions.

The “Black Box” of teaching evaluation

The limited integration of faculty development into assessment criteria suggests the presence of a “black box” within teaching evaluation systems. Institutions assess teaching effectiveness using a range of outputs—such as student evaluations, peer observations, and learning outcomes—without consistently specifying the developmental processes expected to produce those outcomes.

This creates ambiguity for faculty, who are evaluated on teaching performance but receive limited guidance on how to systematically improve it. Without explicit linkage between development activities and evaluation criteria, faculty development may be perceived as optional rather than integral to advancement.

Incentives beyond promotion and tenure

This study focused specifically on APT policy language and did not systematically capture alternative incentive structures that may influence faculty engagement in development activities.

In practice, institutions may provide additional incentives such as stipends, teaching awards, workload adjustments, or recognition programs that operate outside formal promotion criteria. These mechanisms may play an important role in shaping faculty behavior but are not consistently documented within APT policies.

Future research may examine how these alternative incentive structures interact with formal advancement systems to influence participation in faculty development.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings.

First, the analysis was restricted to publicly accessible, web-hosted governance documents. Institutions that maintain advancement and tenure materials within password-protected intranet systems or internal platforms may be underrepresented. As a result, the dataset may overrepresent institutions with higher levels of policy transparency and may not fully capture internal evaluative practices that are not publicly codified.

Second, the automated document retrieval and classification process captured a heterogeneous set of governance-related materials, including formal APT policies as well as strategic plans and administrative documents that reference faculty development. While this broader corpus reflects institutional discourse and stated priorities rather than enacted practice, it may overestimate the prevalence of faculty development language.

Third, variation in disciplinary and institutional terminology presents an inherent limitation. Despite employing a multi-tiered search strategy and iterative lexicon refinement, differences in how faculty development is described may have led to under-identification of relevant content.

Fourth, this study is limited to U.S.-based health professions programs accredited by seven major accrediting bodies. The findings therefore reflect an American, accreditation-driven model of academic advancement and governance. Promotion and tenure systems in other national contexts may operate under different regulatory, cultural, or structural logics.

Fifth, institutions relying heavily on dynamic or JavaScript-driven web platforms may be underrepresented, as such content is less amenable to large-scale automated indexing.

Finally, as a single-author study, there is potential for interpretive bias in document classification and thematic synthesis. This risk was mitigated through structured validation procedures, including random sampling checks, iterative refinement of classification criteria, and full transparency of analytic methods.

Future directions

Future research may extend this analytic framework to international accrediting bodies to explore whether similar policy patterns emerge in non-U.S. contexts. Comparative analysis would help determine whether the Input/Output Paradox reflects a broader governance phenomenon or is specific to U.S. accreditation-driven systems.

Additional work may also examine variation across faculty roles, including differences between clinical and foundational science educators, as well as the role of informal and non-APT incentive structures in shaping engagement with faculty development.

Conclusion

Faculty development is widely recognized as essential to teaching excellence in health professions education; however, its role within advancement, promotion, and tenure systems remains inconsistently defined.

This study demonstrates that while institutions frequently reference faculty development as an expectation or value, it is rarely codified as a formal requirement or evaluative criterion. This creates a structural misalignment in which teaching effectiveness is rigorously assessed while the developmental activities that support it are not consistently required or rewarded.

The resulting Input/Output Paradox highlights a gap between institutional priorities and policy implementation. Faculty are expected to demonstrate high-quality teaching outcomes, yet the processes required to achieve those outcomes are often left implicit.

Addressing this misalignment does not necessarily require the creation of new development programs, but rather a clearer integration of existing activities into advancement frameworks. Explicit recognition of faculty development within promotion criteria may strengthen alignment between institutional expectations, faculty behavior, and educational outcomes.
