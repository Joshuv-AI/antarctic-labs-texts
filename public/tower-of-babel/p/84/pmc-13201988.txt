Safety and feasibility of laparoscopic pericardioperitoneal window
Authors: Samer Alhames, Mike Ghabally, Antoine Bayda, Elias Marta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent pericardial effusion remains a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge due to high recurrence rates. Laparoscopic pericardioperitoneal window represents an understudied method in the management of pericardial effusion and is a minimally invasive technique that allows continuous drainage, achieving symptomatic improvement with minimal recurrence.MethodsWe conducted a single-centre prospective study between 2022 and 2024, including patients with recurrent pericardial effusion requiring surgical intervention. A pericardioperitoneal window was created via laparoscopy for all patients. Clinical outcomes, complications and recurrence rates were assessed over a 12-month follow-up period.Results24 patients were included, and all patients tolerated the surgical procedure well. The surgery was technically successful in all patients, resulted in direct symptomatic improvement and none experienced postoperative complications. Postoperative drainage was not needed, and patients were discharged the next day. 12-month follow-up revealed no clinical or radiological signs of recurrence.ConclusionsLaparoscopic pericardioperitoneal window appears to be a safe, effective and durable alternative for patients with recurrent pericardial effusion. However, larger randomised controlled trials are required for further assessment of safety and long-term outcomes.Keywords: Cardiac Tamponade; Pericardial Effusion; Pericarditis, Constrictive

Introduction

Pericardial effusion refers to the pathological accumulation of fluid within the pericardial cavity.1 Recurrent pericardial effusion is defined as the reaccumulation of fluid in the pericardial sac and may arise from diverse aetiologies, including malignancy (10–25%), infections (15–30%), iatrogenic injury (15–20%) and connective tissue diseases (5–15%). Many cases remain idiopathic in developed countries, whereas tuberculosis is the dominant cause in developing countries (>60%), where tuberculosis is endemic.2

Pericardial effusion can progress to cardiac tamponade, a life-threatening condition requiring emergent intervention. Although several therapeutic options exist, management remains challenging due to high recurrence rates and the need to balance palliation with diagnostic yield. An effective approach should ensure complete and durable fluid evacuation with minimal discomfort and risk to the patient, while also providing sufficient histological, cytological and microbiological material for diagnostic evaluation.3

Multiple therapeutic approaches exist for drainage of the pericardial sac, including subxiphoid incision,4 pericardiocentesis with or without catheter drainage,5 subxiphoid pericardial window (pericardiostomy),6 surgical windows via thoracotomy or thoracoscopy7 and percutaneous balloon pericardiotomy,8 yet each carries limitations related to recurrence, invasiveness or restricted diagnostic yield. More recently, the pericardioperitoneal window (PPW) has emerged as a definitive treatment option, preventing reaccumulation of pericardial fluid by establishing communication between the pericardial and peritoneal cavities.9

In this case series, we present our experience with laparoscopic creation of a PPW, a technique first described by Ready et al.10 Although the procedure offers promising benefits, the published data remain extremely limited to small case series with heterogeneous techniques and short or inconsistent follow-up, particularly regarding diverse aetiologies beyond malignancy.

The present study represents the largest cohort to date, comprising 24 patients with malignant, tuberculous and idiopathic pericardial effusions, treated using a standardised laparoscopic approach with modern energy devices. Systematic 12-month follow-up provides more robust evidence regarding the safety, feasibility and long-term durability of this technique.

Methods

This single-centre study was performed prospectively between 2022 and 2024. The inclusion criteria included all patients presenting to our hospital with isolated pericardial effusion requiring a pericardial window for chronic or recurrent pericardial effusion or malignant pericardial effusion as palliative therapy.

Exclusion criteria included patients with haemodynamic instability, purulent pericardial effusion, the presence of concomitant pleural effusion or pulmonary pathology, cases in which abdominal laparoscopy was contraindicated, cases where general anaesthesia was contraindicated and cases of patient refusal. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients after explaining the surgical technique and the differences between other techniques.

The primary endpoint was to assess recurrence of clinically significant pericardial effusion requiring intervention within 12 months of the procedure. Secondary endpoints included technical success of the procedure, early postoperative outcomes including postoperative complications (bleeding, infection, arrhythmia, need for drainage, haemodynamic compromise and respiratory failure), length of hospital stay, symptomatic improvement and procedure-related mortality.

A detailed clinical examination was performed for all patients. Chest X-ray, chest CT scan, echocardiography and full laboratory workup, as well as pericardial fluid analysis and cytology tests, were performed routinely for all patients. In patients presenting with tamponade, given the very high risk of general anaesthesia and haemodynamic instability, pericardiocentesis using either the subxiphoid or apical approach was first performed. The surgical technique is explained below. Pericardial biopsy was performed routinely for all patients. Following surgery, all patients had regular outpatient 12-month follow-up. Follow-up included symptomatic assessment, full medical examination, chest X-ray and echocardiography to assess the presence of recurrence.

Technique

Patients were intubated via a single-lumen endotracheal tube and placed in the Fowler position (figure 1). Laparoscopic access was made at the supraumbilical level, 2 cm above the umbilicus. Low-pressure pneumoperitoneum was established, a 10 mm trocar was inserted at the same incision and two 5 mm trocars were inserted in the right and left midclavicular lines 3–5 cm beneath the costal margin (figure 1). Following adequate initial visualisation, a diagnostic abdominal laparoscopy was performed. An avascular region of the central pericardium in the area of maximal outbulging cardiac movement was punctured, and a PPW measuring 2 cm×3 cm was created using bipolar electrocautery or a sealing device, and the specimen was sent for histopathological examination (figure 2). On entry into the pericardium, immediate drainage of fluid was observed. Pericardioscopy was subsequently carried out to identify macroscopic abnormalities and to reunify the pericardial cavity in cases of pericardial adhesions. The trocars were removed under direct vision. No pericardial or peritoneal drains were placed, and the three ports were closed using an intradermal suture.

Results

24 patients (11 males and 13 females, with a male to female ratio of 1:1.18) underwent laparoscopic PPW during the period between 2022 and 2024 at our institute (figure 3, table 1). The mean age was 51.42 years (range: 27–72 years). 20 patients presented with specific signs and symptoms of recurrent pericardial effusion (exertional dyspnoea, followed by fatigue, was present in most patients) (83.34%), while four patients presented with tamponade (16.67%).

Patient characteristics

The aetiology of the pericardial effusion was malignant in 10 patients (41.67%): four patients were diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma (16.67%), four with breast cancer (16.67%), one with ovarian cancer (4.17%) and one with thyroid cancer (4.17%). Tuberculosis was the aetiology in patient number 3 (4.17%), in whom the diagnosis was made based on pericardial biopsy. However, there was no definitive diagnosis in 13 patients despite full workup (11 with recurrent pericardial effusion and two with tamponade), and the condition was considered idiopathic (54.17%). Pericardial fluid analysis revealed exudative lymphocytic fluid in all patients. Pericardial fluid cytology was positive in six patients (25%) (four patients with lung adenocarcinoma and two patients with breast cancer). Pericardial biopsy was obtained in all patients and was positive for malignancy in 10 patients and non-specific in 13 patients.

The procedure was technically successful and well tolerated in all patients. An incidental umbilical hernia was identified during laparoscopy in the last patient and was repaired after creation of the PPW without complications. The operative duration was 34±11 min (range: 24–45 min), and both vital signs and clinical symptoms improved markedly postoperatively. Patients were discharged 1 day after the procedure, except when hospitalisation was required for indications other than pericardial effusion. Postoperative analgesia consisted solely of paracetamol, and none of the patients required pericardial drainage or blood transfusion. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no procedure-related complications. During a 12-month follow-up period, no patient experienced recurrence of pericardial effusion. Four patients with underlying malignancy died during follow-up due to progression of their underlying malignancy; however, none demonstrated clinical or radiological evidence of pericardial effusion recurrence prior to death.

Discussion

The management of recurrent pericardial effusion has evolved through multiple techniques, each with inherent limitations, and no single procedure has emerged as clearly superior. Among the available options, the laparoscopic PPW offers a minimally invasive and effective solution. The absorptive capacity of the peritoneal cavity ensures continuous fluid clearance, even in malignancy-associated effusions.11

Compared with pericardiocentesis, whether performed with or without catheter drainage,5 or with subxiphoid pericardial window (pericardiostomy),6 the PPW provides superior visualisation, precise access to the whole pericardium and a dependent drainage route into the peritoneal cavity, thereby reducing the risk of recurrence.

In contrast to thoracoscopic approaches (via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery), the PPW avoids selective lung intubation (thereby shortening anaesthesia time), postoperative chest or abdominal tube placement, phrenic nerve injury (particularly in obese patients) and patient repositioning. These factors collectively reduce anaesthesia duration, decrease analgesic requirements and shorten hospitalisation, leading to a significant reduction in overall costs. The technique is also advantageous in patients with prior cardiac or thoracic surgery or pleural adhesions. Additionally, this approach allows concomitant intervention on abdominal pathology, such as umbilical hernia repair, cholecystectomy, or biopsy of the peritoneum or peritoneal lymph nodes.

The laparoscopic approach to PPW without the use of a shunt has been described in only five previous articles,910 12,14 involving a total of 28 patients. Although laparoscopic PPW was described more than two decades ago, its adoption has remained limited and it is less commonly performed, as it has not been highlighted extensively previously. This may be partly explained by the fact that pericardial window procedures are traditionally performed by cardiothoracic surgeons, whereas this technique requires laparoscopic expertise, which may have limited its broader dissemination.

In our series, which represents the largest cohort reported to date, the mean patient age was 51 years with a balanced sex distribution, compared with 64.7 years in previous reports. The primary indication for surgery was recurrent pericardial effusion, observed in the majority of patients (83.3%), while a smaller subset presented with tamponade (16.7%). By contrast, prior literature reported most cases presenting with tamponade (82.6%) and fewer with recurrent effusion (17.4%). The procedure was technically successful in all patients, with an average operative time of 38 min, closely matching the previously reported mean of 36.1 min. No intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred in our cohort, whereas earlier studies documented three complications, including one case of atrial fibrillation that resolved quickly with digoxin,10 one case of pneumonia14 and one myocardial infarction on the third postoperative day with subsequent death on the fifth day.14 Hospitalisation was brief, as all of our patients were discharged the following day and required only simple analgesia with paracetamol. At 12-month follow-up, no effusion recurrences were detected. These findings highlight the feasibility of laparoscopic PPW as a safe and well-tolerated intervention, offering durable drainage, rapid recovery and minimal perioperative morbidity.

Although general anaesthesia and laparoscopic expertise are essential, the advantages of this approach, including shorter operative time, reduced hospitalisation, lower costs and the opportunity for simultaneous evaluation and treatment of intra-abdominal pathology, support its broader adoption. A further benefit is the ability to perform endopericardioscopy, allowing direct visualisation and biopsy of intrapericardial lesions, as well as reunification of the pericardial cavity in cases of pericardial adhesions.

Potential complications include those associated with laparoscopic procedures in general, as well as specific risks such as cardiac injury and potentially life-threatening arrhythmias when the pericardium is opened and monopolar electrocautery is applied near the epicardium. Lowering intra-abdominal pressure has been recommended to reduce the risk of barotamponade.9 An additional rare but reported complication is herniation of intra-abdominal contents into the pericardial cavity, described in case reports following pericardioperitoneal communications,15 although it has not been reported after laparoscopic PPW. Technical challenges may also arise in patients with prior abdominal surgery or obesity,13 while thoracoscopic approaches may be preferred when concomitant thoracic pathology requires intervention.

The main limitation of our study is that it was not conducted as a randomised controlled trial which may introduce potential selection bias. A randomised design would be necessary to establish stronger evidence for adopting this procedure as a standard of care. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size limits the generalisability of our findings. Larger multicentre studies are therefore required to achieve a sufficiently representative cohort and to validate long-term outcomes.
