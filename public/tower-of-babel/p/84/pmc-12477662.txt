Ptgs2+ CPTC Function as a “Force‐Immune Axis” by Responding to Acupuncture and Mediating M2 Macrophage Activation for Anti‐Inflammatory Effects
Authors: Zhenwei Zhang, Haixiang Huang, Jianming Yue, Xudong Zhu, Yeping Shi, Bingbing Shen, Qianmei Zhu, Lu Mei, Qiusheng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent advancements have highlighted the imperative for cytological exploration of fascial tissues. Telocytes, specialized interstitial cells found in connective tissues such as fascia, have garnered significant attention. Our study explored the cellular landscape of the abdominal midline fascia (AMF), revealing a primary presence of Cd34+/Pdgfra+ telocytes (CPTCs) by single‐cell sequencing and cytomorphology. We identified six CPTC subpopulations with deep learning, notably Ptgs2+ CPTCs, which exhibited remarkable responsiveness to mechanical stimulus. Following the acupuncture stimulation at the special acupoints along the AMF in dysenteric rats, the proportion of Ptgs2+ CPTCs increased significantly, with these cells exhibiting strengthened cell adhesion with all CPTC subpopulations. Importantly, Ptgs2+ CPTCs activated M2 macrophages through the Il6–Il6st–Stat3 pathway, promoting anti‐inflammatory responses. For the first time, our findings established Ptgs2+ CPTCs as a key “force–immune axis” that linked mechanical stimulation and immune modulation, advancing our understanding of the cellular mechanisms behind acupuncture and its potential to modulate inflammation in immune‐related disorders.

Introduction

Acupuncture, a widely utilized traditional technique in medicine and therapeutic practice [1], has demonstrated efficacy across diverse medical scenarios, including anti‐inflammatory interventions [2], managing bacterial dysentery [3], and others [4]. Acupuncture needles typically penetrate through the subcutaneous fascia to depths ranging from approximately 3 to 4 mm [5, 6] beneath the rat skin. Over the years, extensive research has been conducted on the mechanisms of acupuncture, but the direct cellular responses at acupoints, particularly within the subcutaneous fascia, remain largely uncharted territory.

The fascial system, an intricate network of collagen‐rich connective tissues, forms a continuous matrix traversing the human body [7, 8]. As a structural bridge among muscles, bones, nerves, and internal organs [9], fascia plays pivotal roles in force transmission and wound repair [10, 11]. Subcutaneous fascia, a vital component of this intricate system, remains relatively understudied despite its significance in acupuncture. Research on subcutaneous fascia has predominantly focused on supporting the skin and regulating the extracellular matrix [12], leaving its precise cellular composition under discussion [13]. Additionally, the potential role of subcutaneous fascia in responding to acupuncture stimulus has mainly been overlooked [14]. According to traditional Chinese medicine, Guanyuan (CV4), Qihai (CV6), and Zhongwan (CV12) at AMF [15, 16, 17] are employed to treat diseases such as bacterial dysentery, diarrhea, and various other ailments [3, 18].

Telocytes (TCs), distinguished by their elongated shape and ultrastructural features under transmission electron microscopy (TEM), are ubiquitous interstitial cells marked by the expression of Cd34 and Pdgfra [19] in the fascia. Previous research has primarily focused on morphological identification [19, 20] of TCs within different organs [21, 22]. Although there have been some explorations and discussions regarding the presence of TCs within fascia [23], those investigations have primarily been confined to their morphological distribution.

To investigate the primary cellular components and their functions in fascia, we conducted single‐cell sequencing on rat AMF and corroborated our findings through detailed morphological analysis. We identified a subpopulation of CPTCs, specifically Ptgs2+ CPTCs, that responded to mechanical stimulation. To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying this response, we administered acupuncture, a form of mechanical stimulation, to dysenteric rats. We then conducted single‐cell sequencing and morphological analyses on the AMF from the dysentery‐affected and acupuncture‐treated groups. These studies aim to shed light on the cellular mechanisms of fascia in immune regulation and their potential therapeutic implications.

Experimental Animals and Ethical Approval

Fifteen female Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats, aged 8 weeks and weighing approximately 250 g, were procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. The experimental protocols involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanjing Agriculture University (Approval ID: NJAU. No20220427087) and conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Rats were housed in ventilated cages on racks with ad libitum feeding and water. The animal facility was maintained at roughly 25°C with a natural light–dark cycle.

Bacterial Strains and Growth Conditions

Salmonella typhimurium CVCC542 (S. typhimurium CVCC542) was obtained from the Joint International Research Laboratory of Animal Health and Food Safety. S. typhimurium was generally cultured in Luria‐Bertani (LB) medium with 50 μg/mL kanamycin at 37°C. Briefly, S. typhimurium was initially cultured overnight at 37°C in a 2 mL standing culture to enrich motile bacteria. Subsequently, the top 1 mL of the culture was transferred into 49 mL of prewarmed LB broth and incubated with shaking at 37°C for 5 h. The bacterial concentration was measured at 600 nm using a spectrophotometer (Eppendorf BioPhotometer Plus), and an appropriate dose was prepared in sterile 0.01 M phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH ~7.4).

Infection of Rats by S. typhimurium

Fifteen rats were randomly allocated into the mock group (n = 5) and the infection group (n = 10). S. typhimurium was diluted to 5 × 108 CFU/mL using 0.01 M PBS. Rats in the infection group received 5% NaHCO3 to neutralize gastric acid. Ten minutes thereafter, these rats were administered 400 μL of the diluted bacterial suspension. This oral gavage with the bacterial suspension was repeated every 24 h for three consecutive days. Rats in the mock group were fed as usual.

Acupuncture Treatment

Ten rats in the infection group were assigned to the dysentery group (n = 5) and the acupuncture group (n = 5) randomly. In the acupuncture group, the acupuncture needle (0.18 mm × 13 mm; Suzhou Acupuncture Goods Co. Ltd., China) was inserted into three designated acupoints (CV4, CV6, and CV12) on the AMF. CV4, CV6, and CV12 were located along the midline of the abdomen as follows: CV12 at the midpoint between the umbilicus and the xiphoid process, CV4 at 3/5 of the distance between the umbilicus and the pubic symphysis depression, and CV6 at the midpoint between the umbilicus and point CV4. These sessions continued for 8 days. For the dysentery group, rats underwent the same restraint procedure daily but did not receive acupuncture treatment. The acupuncture needle was inserted to a depth of approximately 6 mm. Following the operation protocol from the Shanghai Innovation Center of Traditional Chinese Medicine Health Service [24], the needles were evenly twisted approximately 360° at a rate of 60 times per minute for 10 to 20 s, manipulated every 5 min, and then withdrawn after 20 min.

Sampling

Rats from the three groups were euthanized following acupuncture treatment. This procedure adhered to the guiding principles of the IACUC and was conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Fascia samples for single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐Seq) analysis were collected with two replicates, and a third replicate was obtained under identical conditions as a backup, stored in Tissue Storage Solution. All subcutaneous fascia tissue samples were obtained from a specific anatomical region of the rat model, situated along the abdominal midline. This rectangular area extended from 1 cm before point CV12 to 1 cm after point CV4 and had a width of 2 cm.

The AMF and a portion of the colon were fixed in 4% Paraformaldehyde (PFA) for histology and immunofluorescence studies, while 0.25% glutaraldehyde (pH adjusted to 7.4) was used for TEM. Another portion of the colon, along with the liver, was fixed in 0.01 M PBS for colony‐counting experiments.

Colony Counting

To assess bacterial colonization, the collected tissues were homogenized to form a tissue slurry in PBS using a homogenizer. The slurry was plated onto MacConkey agar plates containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin and incubated at 37°C overnight. Colonies were counted to determine the colony‐forming units (CFUs) per gram of tissue. The CFUs between the dysentery and acupuncture groups were compared to evaluate the impact of acupuncture treatment on bacterial colonization.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining

First, clear the paraffin using two changes of xylene, each lasting 10 min. Then, hydrate the samples according to the standard operating protocol [25]. Stain the samples in hematoxylin solution for 2 min, rinse the slides for 5 min, stain the samples in working eosin solution for 30 s, and proceed to dehydrate the samples. Finally, add a drop of neutral resin over each slide's tissue and a coverslip. Examine the slides using a microscope (OLYMPUS BX53).

TUNEL Staining

Tissue sections were subjected to TUNEL staining according to the instructions. Deparaffinize and rehydrate sections, then pipet proteinase K solution for 30 min at 37°C. For the positive control, sections were exposed to DNase I reaction solution and incubated for 30 min at 37°C. Cover the sections with TdT reaction buffer and Streptavidin‐TRITC labeling solution successively and stain cell nuclei with DAPI. Finally, the stained samples were visualized with a fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS BX53).

Extraction and Culture of CPTCs

TCs were extracted from the rat AMF. Tissue samples were collected and processed following established protocols [26]. The AMF was dissected and digested with a dissociation solution composed of collagenase type 4 and type 1, dissolved in DMEM/F12 medium containing 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS). The resulting cell suspension was first filtered through a 100 μm mesh and then through a 40 μm mesh to remove tissue debris. To stop the enzymatic reaction, the suspension was washed with PBS. The resulting cell suspension was plated in T75 flasks for 2 h. Then, slowly adherent cells left on the flask surface were transferred to new culture flasks and cultured.

Isolated CPTCs were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium, supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cells were incubated in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity. Immunofluorescence staining was performed to confirm the purity of the CPTC population. Cell slides were positioned in the wells of 12‐well plates before seeding. Samples were harvested at 72 h by adding 1 mL of 4% PFA solution to each well and then fixed at 4°C for 24 h before any assays were conducted.

Transfection and Transwell Co‐Culture

To overexpress Il6, the rat Il6 gene was cloned into the pCaggs‐HA vector containing an HA tag for detection. The plasmid was verified by sequencing to ensure the correct insertion of the Il6 gene.

For transfection, Telocytes were seeded into the upper chambers of transwells overnight. The next day, the cells were transfected with the pCaggs‐HA‐Il6 plasmid using Lipofectamine LTX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the protocol.

Twenty‐four hours post‐transfection, the upper chambers of the transwells were transferred to new 12‐well plates. M2 macrophages, differentiated from THP‐1 with PMA and IL4, were seeded in the lower chamber of the transwells, allowing for indirect co‐culture. The co‐culture was maintained for 36 h in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Then, samples and cellular supernatants were harvested for analysis.

ELISA

The levels of Il6 were measured using a rat Il6 ELISA kit according to the instructions. Briefly, six different gradient standards and samples were added to a 96‐well plate pre‐coated with capture antibody and incubated. After washing, a biotinylated detection antibody was added, followed by streptavidin‐HRP. The wells were then incubated with a TMB substrate solution. The reaction was terminated by adding stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Il6 concentrations were determined using a standard curve.

RT‐PCR

Total RNA was extracted from M2 macrophages using TRIzol reagent and reverse‐transcribed into cDNA using a Reverse Transcription kit. Quantitative PCR was performed using specific primers for IL10 and STAT3, with GAPDH as the internal control. The reactions were run on a Real‐Time PCR System (Bio‐Rad CFX Connect) using SYBR Green Master Mix. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Western Blot

M2 Macrophages were collected and lysed in SDS‐PAGE protein loading buffer containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors. 20 μg protein was resolved by SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% BSA and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against phospho‐STAT3 and GAPDH, followed by incubation with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence, and images were acquired under different exposure times (Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc) to ensure specific detection of p‐STAT3. Group comparisons were performed using two‐tailed unpaired t tests; Welch's correction was applied when appropriate. Data are presented as mean ± SEM.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Deparaffinize and rehydrate the tissue sections. Subsequently, perform Heat‐Induced Epitope Retrieval (HIER) by incubating the sections in a 10 mm sodium citrate buffer, pH 6.0, at a sub‐boiling temperature for 10 min. After blocking, the specimens are incubated at 4°C for 12 to 16 h with a solution containing the primary antibody. The specimens are then incubated with a series of secondary antibody dilutions. After staining cell nuclei with DAPI, the image was captured by confocal microscopy (Carl Zeiss, LSM900).

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

The tissues from the fascia were initially fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in 0.2 M PBS at 4°C for at least 48 h to preserve cellular ultrastructure. Post‐fixation was performed using 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h at 4°C. The tissues were then subjected to a graded ethanol dehydration series (75%, 85%, 95%, and 100%), with each step lasting 10 min.

After dehydration, the samples were infiltrated and embedded in epoxy resin to ensure structural integrity. Ultrathin sections, approximately 50 nm in thickness, were cut and carefully mounted onto copper grids. The sections were stained sequentially with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The prepared sections were examined using a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope (TEM) operated at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

Tissue Dissociation and Cell Purification for scRNA

The AMF tissues were processed under sterile conditions to prepare single‐cell suspensions for single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq). Tissues were rinsed in 10 mL of ice‐cold 1× DPBS to remove blood and adipose residues, then minced into small fragments on ice using sterile instruments.

The AMF was digested with a dissociation solution, composed of the collagenase type 4 and type 1, dissolved in DMEM/F12 medium containing 10% FBS. The tissue fragments were digested at 37°C while shaking at 50 rpm for 40 min, with cells collected every 20 min to optimize both yield and viability.

Cell suspensions were sequentially filtered using nylon strainers (100 μm and then 40 μm pore sizes) to remove debris. Any remaining red blood cells were lysed using a 1× Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution, followed by washing with 1× DPBS containing 2% FBS. Viable cells were identified through 0.4% Trypan Blue staining using a Countess II Automated Cell Counter. To enhance viability, dead cells were removed using the Dead Cell Removal kit. Only cell suspensions with a viability rate exceeding 95% were retained for subsequent processing.

The prepared single‐cell suspension was used for constructing 10× Genomics libraries and subjected to downstream steps, including droplet‐based 3′ sequencing.

10× Library Preparation and Sequencing

Single cells were encapsulated with gel beads carrying unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and cell‐specific barcodes within Gel Beads‐in‐Emulsion (GEM) droplets using the Chromium Single Cell Controller (10× Genomics). Cells within the droplets were lysed to release RNA, which then hybridized with the oligonucleotides on the beads. Reverse transcription was performed to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA), tagging each transcript with both a UMI and a cell barcode.

Subsequently, cDNA underwent second‐strand synthesis, adaptor ligation, and whole‐transcriptome amplification to prepare material for sequencing library construction. These libraries were optimized to enrich the 3′ ends of mRNA, ensuring the inclusion of cell‐specific barcodes and UMIs in the sequencing data.

Library preparation followed the manufacturer's recommended protocols (Chromium Single Cell 3′ v3.1). Quantification was performed using both the High‐Sensitivity DNA Chip on a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent) and the Qubit High‐Sensitivity DNA Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform with paired‐end 150 bp reads to achieve high‐depth single‐cell.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

scRNA Data Analysis

The sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger v6.0.1 with default parameters. FASTQ files were aligned to the rat genome (mRatBN7.2.) using the STAR algorithm. Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs) were quantified, and non‐cell‐associated barcodes were filtered to produce gene‐barcode matrices for each sample.

The resulting matrices were imported into Seurat v4.3.0 in R for quality control and analysis. Quality filtering excluded cells with low UMI counts, low gene expression, or high mitochondrial transcript percentages (> 7%). Post‐filtered data were subjected to normalization, harmony, and dimensionality reduction (UMAP and t‐SNE). Erythrocytes and doublets were eliminated to ensure data integrity. Principal component analysis was optimized for resolution, and the final objects were saved as RData files for downstream analysis without altering raw counts.

Correlation Analysis

For correlation analyses, we first calculated the average expression of CPTCs with the Average Expression function from the R Seurat (4.4.0) package [27]. The correlation coefficients were then computed with the cor.test function from the R stats package. To account for multiple testing, p‐values were adjusted with the p.adjust function [28].

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Gene and Genome (KEGG) Analysis

Gene identifiers were converted to Entrez IDs and Symbols using the biomaRt [29] package in R. Mappings for gene symbols and GO terms were retrieved using the org.Rn.eg.db package, while Entrez IDs were matched to KEGG terms using clusterProfiler [30, 31]. Background genes were identified using the FindMarkers function in Seurat, and differentially expressed genes (adjusted p < 0.05) were selected for analysis. GO and KEGG term enrichment were assessed using the gseGO and gseKEGG functions in clusterProfiler, with a p‐value threshold of 0.05.

CellCall Analysis

Cell communication analysis was conducted using the R package CellCall [32]. Following the creation of the analysis object and the computation of communication scores using the functions CreateObject_fromSeurat and transCommuProfile, we enriched KEGG terms associated with signaling pathways in cell communication by employing the getHyperPathway function. Pathways were selected based on a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and a normalized enrichment score (NES) greater than 0.

pySCENIC Analysis

Single‐Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering (pySCENIC) analysis was performed on each CPTC subpopulation to infer regulatory network activity. Using the single‐cell RNA sequencing data, gene regulatory networks were constructed, and transcription factor–target interactions were identified. Regulon activity was quantified using the AUCell algorithm, generating Regulon Specificity Scores (RSS) for each subpopulation.

CytoTRACE

R package CytoTRACE v0.3.3 was applied to calculate the CytoTRACE scores for CPTC populations [33]. CytoTRACE scores range from 0 to 1, while higher scores indicate higher stemness (less differentiation) and vice versa.

Deep Learning‐Based Pipeline for Single‐Cell Data Analysis

A deep learning pipeline was designed for the analysis of single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data, incorporating preprocessing, training, and interpretation steps via R and Python platforms.

Data Preparation

Single‐cell datasets were divided based on experimental conditions and cell types using the Seurat (4.4.0) package in R.4.3.0. The processed gene expression matrices were exported in a standardized CSV format, ensuring unique column identifiers for seamless integration across subsets in downstream analyses.

Neural Network Architecture

A feedforward neural network (FNN) was employed for single‐cell classification. It consists of an input layer corresponding to gene features, several hidden layers, and an output layer representing distinct cell types or experimental groups. Model configurations, including activation functions, optimizer selection, and network size, were adjusted systematically.

Hyperparameter Optimization

The optimization algorithms were optimized through grid search, such as learning rates and the number of neurons per layer. Data splitting into training and validation sets (90%: 10% ratio) was utilized to ensure robust performance.

Feature Importance Assessment

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were used to interpret gene contributions to model predictions. The SHAP DeepExplainer module was applied to generate quantitative importance scores for each gene, identifying those most relevant to classification outcomes.

Outputs and Validation

Classification accuracy and feature importance rankings were recorded. Models achieving over 99% accuracy were retained for further biological interpretation, and all outputs were structured to facilitate reproducibility.

Results

CPTCs Are the Primary Component in Rat Abdominal Midline Fascia (AMF)

To obtain a global view of the subcutaneous fascia, scRNA‐Seq analyses using the 10X Chromium System were conducted on the samples obtained from rats in the mock group (Figure 1A). The sampling regions are AMF of rats in Figure 1B. Unsupervised clustering partitioned 11 116 cells into 22 different clusters. We combined some clusters and identified six major cell types (Figure 1C) based on specifically expressed or highly enriched genes (Figure S1A). CPTCs accounted for the proportion at 27.8%, which were the most numerous cell type in the fascia, followed by macrophages at 26.6%, myofibroblasts at 24%, endothelial cells at 13.1%, lymphocytes at 7.9%, and other cells at 0.6% (Figure 1C–E). Furthermore, CPTCs were often observed to cluster in the superficial fascia (Figure 1F) and the retinacula (Figure 1G) by IF. Correspondingly, our TEM observations revealed that CPTCs were always aligned in clusters, oriented parallelly within the subcutaneous fascia (Figure 1H). Some are located within tissue microchannels, the gaps between collagen fiber bundles in the connective tissue (Figure 1I). CPTCs exhibited elongated cellular extensions with secretion of extracellular vesicles (Figure 1J).

Reclustering these CPTCs using unsupervised clustering methods yielded six distinct subpopulations (Figure 2A). To explore the gene expression profiles underlying the cellular heterogeneity of CPTCs, we identified subpopulation‐specific markers (Figure 2B, Table sup 2‐1), designating the CPTC subgroups as Mrgprg+ CPTCs, Ptgs2+ CPTCs, Igfbp3+ CPTCs, Ccn5+ CPTCs, Ifi2712b+ CPTCs, and Abca8a+ CPTCs. Correlation analysis was performed among these subgroups (Figure 2C) to understand their relationships further.

To investigate the functional relevance of these subpopulations and elucidate their biological roles, GO enrichment analysis [30, 31] was conducted on genes that showed significant expression in each subpopulation. Each subpopulation exhibited distinct primary functions (sup 2‐2). Particularly, Ptgs2+ CPTCs demonstrated remarkable capabilities in responding to mechanical stimulus (Figure 2D). Ptgs2+ CPTCs were observed clustered parallelly in the superficial fascia by IF (Figure 2E).

pySCENIC analysis [34] was performed to calculate regulon‐specific scores (RSS) for transcription factors (TFs) across each CPTC subpopulation (sup 2‐3). Subsequently, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was applied to TFs with high RSS in Ptgs2+ CPTCs, revealing significant enrichment in pathways associated with the “response to stimulus” (Figure 2F).

We explored the interrelations among the different CPTC subpopulations using CellCall [32]. The primary recipients of ligand signals from CPTC subpopulations were Abca8a + TCs and Igfbp3+ TCs (Figure 2G1). Pathway activity analysis revealed that their intercellular signaling was enriched mainly in the cellular junctions, encompassing focal adhesion and adherens junctions (Figure 2G2, sup 2‐4).

Monocle [35, 36, 37, 38] reconstructed a trajectory leading from Igfbp3+ TCs to Mrgprg+ CPTCs (Figure 2H), with Ptgs2+ CPTCs occupying an intermediate position along the trajectory (Figure 2I). To explore genes with similar kinetic trends, we clustered genes into three sets based on their pseudo temporal expression patterns (Figure 2J): (1) genes with increasing expression over differentiation time, (2) genes with decreasing expression over differentiation time, and (3) genes demonstrating an initial upregulation followed by subsequent downregulation during differentiation. Intriguingly, genes highly expressed in Ptgs2+ CPTCs (sup 2‐5) significantly overlapped with those in the third cluster. Functional annotation of the overlapping gene set revealed their enrichment in pathways related to the cellular response to mechanical stimulus (Figure 2J).

We identified Ptgs2+ CPTCs, one of the subpopulations of CPTCs, that exhibit significant potential in responding to mechanical stimulus.

Ptgs2+ CPTCs as Mechanosensitive Sentinels: Responding to Acupuncture and Enhancing Cell Adhesion With All CPTC Subpopulations

Acupuncture, a form of mechanical stimulation, typically penetrates the subcutaneous fascia. The acupoint along the abdominal midline is frequently employed to treat dysenteric enteritis. To investigate the role of Ptgs2+ CPTCs within the AMF in response to mechanical stimulus, we administered acupuncture to the AMF of dysenteric rats.

We successfully established a dysentery model in rats through intragastric administration of Salmonella and subsequently administered acupuncture therapy at CV4, CV6, and CV12 along AMF. After treatment, we analyzed the subcutaneous fascia along the abdominal midline (Figure 3A). Salmonella colonization occurred in the colon and liver, along with significant weight loss in rats. Conversely, acupuncture intervention effectively reduced the number of Salmonella colonies in both the colon and liver (Figure 3B1) and alleviated weight loss (Figure 3B2). To further substantiate the efficacy of acupuncture, TUNEL and HE staining were conducted on colonic tissues from rats in the mock, dysentery, and acupuncture groups, respectively. Following Salmonella gavage, compared with rats in the mock group, the rats in the dysentery group exhibited colonic mucosal epithelial cell necrosis, epithelial detachment, and congestion. Conversely, acupuncture treatment significantly ameliorated cell necrosis and epithelial congestion within the colonic mucosal epithelium (Figure 3C).

Under TEM, as dysentery progressed, the quantity of CPTCs within the tissue microchannels (gaps between collagen fiber bundles) of the AMF markedly diminished, with no detectable intercellular connections (Figure 3D). Following acupuncture therapy, CPTCs exhibited longer telopodes, accompanied by a notable increase in the cellular connection among homotypic cells (Figure 3D), akin to those in the mock group.

To further validate the Ptgs2+ CPTCs in responding to mechanical stimulus and facilitating intercellular connectivity after acupuncture, CPTCs were isolated from the fascia single‐cell sequencing data of the fascia in both dysentery and acupuncture groups. Subsequently, we performed dimensionality reduction and reclustered these CPTCs using unsupervised clustering methods (Figure S2A,B). In alignment with the mock group, CPTCs from the dysentery and acupuncture groups were clustered into six distinct subpopulations. Notably, following acupuncture treatment, there was a marked increase in the proportion of Ptgs2+ CPTCs (Figure 3E). CytoTRACE analysis indicated that Ptgs2+ CPTCs exhibit the lowest differentiation status and strongest stem‐like characteristics among all CPTC subpopulations after acupuncture (Figure S2C1,C2).

Deep learning techniques were applied to single‐cell RNA data from the dysentery and acupuncture groups. It included an input layer for gene features, multiple hidden layers, and an output layer representing dysentery and acupuncture groups (Figure S2D–G). By interpreting the resulting models (sup 3‐1), we identified acupuncture‐related gene sets specific to CPTCs (sup 3‐2) and analyzed their expression differences between the two groups using the FindMarkers function (sup 3‐3). GSEA with GO enrichment for BP (p < 0.05) revealed that the pathway “response to mechanical stimulus” had the highest NES (Figures 3F1, S2H). The same GO analysis of marker genes from six CPTC subpopulations revealed that Ptgs2+ CPTCs exhibited the highest NES and significance in the “response to mechanical stimulus” pathway following acupuncture (Figures 3F2, sup 3‐4). These findings highlighted Ptgs2+ CPTCs as the principal subpopulation that responds to acupuncture stimulus.

Cellular signaling analysis among CPTC subpopulations revealed significant shifts in communication patterns after acupuncture. During dysentery, Ccn5+ CPTCs and Ifi27l2b+ CPTCs were the primary recipients of ligand signals, whereas Ptgs2+ CPTCs became the primary recipients after acupuncture. Pathway activity analysis highlighted enrichment in the pathway about cell adhesion following acupuncture (Figures 3G1,G2; sup 3‐5 and sup 3‐6), suggesting that acupuncture reconfigures cell–cell interactions among CPTCs, with Ptgs2+ CPTCs serving as central hubs.

We employed NicheNet [39] to identify the primary differential ligands and corresponding target genes involved in cellular communication in dysentery and acupuncture groups (Figure S3A–C). GO enrichment analysis of these activated target genes (Figure S3D) revealed their critical role in enhancing Ptgs2+ CPTCs' responsiveness to acupuncture stimulus.

To explore the role of Ptgs2+ CPTCs in regulating downstream cellular interactions in response to mechanical stimuli and signals from CPTC subpopulations after acupuncture, we focused on macrophages—key anti‐inflammatory cells and the second most abundant subpopulation in the AMF.

Ptpr and Itgam expression identified myeloid cells, and macrophages were distinguished by their Cd68+Itgax‐ expression (Figure S4A,B). Dimensionality reduction and reclustering of macrophages from rat AMF single‐cell data (Figure 4A) revealed M1, M2, and other subsets based on Cd86 and Cd163 expression (Figures S4C, sup 4‐1).

Comparative analysis of the macrophage population across the three groups revealed increased M1 macrophages in dysenteric rats, whereas acupuncture significantly elevated the proportion of M2 macrophages (Figure 4B). TEM analysis revealed that M2 macrophages extended longer and more pseudopodia after acupuncture (Figure 4C). Monocle trajectory analysis showed enhanced M1 to M2 transitions with increased branching after acupuncture (Figure 4D1,D2). Acupuncture‐related gene sets specific to M1 and M2 macrophages, identified through deep learning (sup 4‐2, sup 4‐3), were analyzed using GSEA. The GSEA highlighted the activation of the Il17, TNF, and NF‐κB pathways in M1 and M2 subsets (Figure S4D). Significantly, acupuncture stimulated M2 macrophages to secrete the anti‐inflammatory cytokine Il10 (Figure 4E).

To investigate the interrelations among the CPTCs and macrophages, we conducted CellCall analysis to compare Ptgs2+ CPTCs from the acupuncture group with Ccn5+ and Igfbp8+ CPTCs from the dysentery group, along with their corresponding M2 macrophages (Figure 4F1,F2). This analysis revealed that, among all interconnection signals, Il6 was a uniquely expressed signaling molecule in Ptgs2+ CPTCs, detected exclusively in the acupuncture group and absent in all other cell types and conditions, including dysenteric rats (Figures 4H1,H2, S5A,B). IF further confirmed that Il6 expression was specific to Ptgs2+ CPTCs in the acupuncture group (Figure 4G). Subsequently, Ptgs2+ CPTCs transmitted Il6 to M2 macrophages via Il6st, activating Stat3, which was inactive in dysenteric rats. To discern whether acupuncture‐induced cytokine expression reflects a localized or systemic response, we quantified serum levels of Il6 and Il10 via ELISA (Figure S4E1–E2). These measurements effectively ruled out the possibility that the observed cytokine alterations arose from a systemic inflammatory response, such as that elicited by bacterial infection.

Through the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, we delineated interactions among Il6, Il6st, Il10, and Stat3 (Figure 4J). CPTCs overexpressed Il6 were co‐cultured with M2 macrophages in vitro (Figure 4I). IF revealed a pronounced increase in Il6 secretion following overexpression (Figure 4K), which was further substantiated by the elevated Il6 levels detected in the cell supernatant via ELISA (Figure S4F). Furthermore, qPCR analysis of the co‐cultured M2 macrophages revealed that Il6‐overexpressing CPTCs significantly upregulated the expression of Stat3 and Il10 in M2 macrophages (Figure 4L). Western blot analysis showed a significant increase in Stat3 phosphorylation at Tyr705 in M2 macrophages co‐cultured with Il6‐overexpressing Ptgs2+ CPTCs (p = 0.0033, two‐tailed Welch' t test), whereas the total Stat3 protein level remained unchanged (p = 0.0632; Figure S4G1–G3).

In conclusion, Ptgs2+ CPTCs act as a pivotal bridge, connecting acupuncture stimulation to the regulation of M2 macrophages via the Il6–Il6st–Stat3 axis, thereby modulating anti‐inflammatory responses.

Discussion

CPTCs Constituted the Predominant Cell Population in Rat AMF

Acknowledging the limited evidence elucidating the functional and cellular composition of the subcutaneous fascia, we conducted a single‐cell analysis of cells along the AMF in rats to achieve a global view. Our findings revealed that CPTCs were the predominant component of the AMF, followed by macrophages and myofibroblasts, thereby advancing our understanding of the cellular diversity in fascia. Evidence suggests that CPTCs exhibit heterogeneity across different anatomical locations [21, 40, 41, 42], a phenomenon we also observed in the subcutaneous fascia. Specifically, our analysis identified six distinct CPTC subpopulations, resulting in a comprehensive cellular atlas of CPTCs in the rat AMF. This atlas provides a critical framework for delineating the functional roles of CPTC subpopulations. It fills a key gap in our understanding of subcutaneous fascia, offering new opportunities for clinical and therapeutic exploration.

Ptgs2+ CPTCs Is a Stimulus–Response Hub in Rat AMF

Research on CPTCs has predominantly concentrated on their role in establishing cellular connections and facilitating intercellular communication over long distances [19, 43, 44, 45, 46]. While some studies have highlighted the responsiveness of uterine TCs to laser stimulation [47], the broader understanding of TC responses to physical stimulus remains underexplored. In this study, we identified a distinct subset of CPTCs, Ptgs2+ CPTCs, which exhibit a marked mechanosensitive property in the rat AMF. Furthermore, we substantiated this potential by employing various analytical approaches, including GO enrichment, TF RSS activity analysis, and trajectory analysis.

Acupuncture is a distinctive therapeutic method that treats diseases through mechanical stimulation [5]. Following acupuncture treatment in dysenteric rat AMF, Ptgs2+ CPTCs demonstrated a pronounced responsiveness, manifested through the activation of pathways “response to mechanical stimulus,” augmented cellular communication, proliferation of the cell population, and elongation of telopodes. Moreover, following mechanical stimulation by acupuncture, Ptgs2+ CPTCs establish enhanced cell adhesion with all CPTC subpopulations. This provides a more comprehensive understanding of the enhanced connectivity of CPTCs [48, 49] and helps explain the cellular response induced by acupuncture.

Moreover, Ptgs2+ CPTCs were found to exhibit the highest CytoTRACE scores among all CPTC subpopulations after acupuncture, indicating a lower differentiation status and stronger stem‐like potential. These findings suggest that Ptgs2+ CPTCs represent a mechanoresponsive subpopulation with intrinsic plasticity and self‐renewal capacity. Consistent with this, we observed a marked increase in the proportion of Ptgs2+ CPTCs after acupuncture. We interpret this expansion because of their inherent ability to respond to mechanical stimulation through self‐activation and proliferation.

Ptgs2+ TCs Act as “Force‐Immune Axis” Bridging Acupuncture Stimulation to M2 Macrophage Anti‐Inflammation

Macrophages are the second most numerous cells in rat AMF. Macrophages display diverse functional responses to environmental cues [50], with M1 macrophages secreting pro‐inflammatory cytokines, while M2 macrophages release anti‐inflammatory mediators, including interleukin‐10 (Il10) [51, 52]. Il6 is a pivotal regulator of macrophage activation, with its deficiency resulting in heightened apoptosis and aggravated inflammation [50]. It selectively drives the activation of M2 macrophages [53], thereby fostering anti‐inflammatory and immunomodulatory responses [52]. Furthermore, Il6 stimulates the production of Il10 [54], and Stat3 plays a crucial role in amplifying the anti‐inflammatory effects of Il10 [55, 56].

Our study concluded that acupuncture increased the proportion of M2 macrophages and activated their anti‐inflammatory response. This is consistent with previous findings that acupuncture stimulation activates immune responses [2, 55, 56, 57, 58] and differentiates macrophages [59, 60, 61]. However, the specific pathway connecting mechanical stimulation to immune regulation is an undiscovered “black box,” which is also called “meridian.” Several hypotheses have suggested that meridians might be equivalent to blood vessels [62], mast cells [63], or nerves [64]. However, these hypotheses are fundamentally flawed. They resemble a “fragmented bridge,” incapable of linking both shores simultaneously. Either they fail to respond to acupuncture stimulation without causing pain, or they do respond but lack the downstream regulatory functions essential for a holistic physiological effect.

Fortunately, we identified Ptgs2+ CPTCs, which act as a “force‐immune axis” bridging acupuncture stimulation to M2 anti‐inflammation. On the one hand, Ptgs2+ CPTCs exhibit a robust response to acupuncture stimulation. More importantly, they play a crucial role in modulating the anti‐inflammatory response of M2. By secreting Il6, Ptgs2+ CPTCs activate the transcriptional activity of Stat3 in M2, ultimately prompting M2 to secrete Il10, thereby exerting anti‐inflammatory effects. Notably, Il6 was exclusively expressed in Ptgs2+ CPTCs in response to acupuncture, with no Il6 detected in any other cell subsets. We calculated the differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways of several other cell populations before and after acupuncture to rule out the possibility that they directly responded to acupuncture (sup 5‐1–5‐7).

Several studies have reported that mechanical stretching of various cell types can induce Il6 transcription and secretion [65, 66, 67, 68]. In our study, we observed that Ptgs2+ CPTCs will secrete Il6 after acupuncture. Given Ptgs2+ CPTCs close anatomical association with collagen fibers in the fascia, and the fact that acupuncture manipulation—such as lifting, thrusting, or twirling—can physically displace and stretch collagen fibers, it is plausible that Ptgs2+ CPTCs are mechanically stimulated via collagen‐mediated tension, ultimately leading to Il6 upregulation. This mechanism may represent an early event in the immune‐regulatory cascade triggered by acupuncture. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the precise signaling mechanisms involved.

Previous studies have shown that acupuncture can significantly reduce the expression level of Il6 in serum and activate macrophages [59]. Interestingly, our findings suggest that acupuncture stimulation leads to a local increase in Il6 expression by Ptgs2+ CPTCs in the fascia. Spatially restricted Il6 signaling can exert distinct immunoregulatory roles, where localized Il6 promotes regenerative or anti‐inflammatory phenotypes, while systemic Il6 is associated with pro‐inflammatory states [69, 70]. We believe Ptgs2+ CPTCs will act in a paracrine fashion to activate neighboring macrophages and promote M2 polarization via the Il6–Il6st–Stat3. In contrast, reduced bacterial load and inflammation after acupuncture may contribute to the downregulation of systemic Il6, reflecting the resolution of systemic inflammation. Future studies employing genetic or pharmacological inhibition of Il6 signaling will be essential to establish a causal relationship.

In conclusion, our study identifies Ptgs2+ CPTCs as critical mediators bridging mechanical stimulation and immune regulation in acupuncture. We provide new insights into the cellular mechanisms underlying acupuncture's therapeutic effects by demonstrating their role in enhancing M2 macrophage anti‐inflammatory responses through the Il6‐Il6st‐Stat3 pathway. This work not only reinforces the relevance of acupuncture in modern medicine but also highlights Ptgs2+ CPTCs as potential therapeutic targets for modulating inflammation and immune responses. Linking traditional healing practices to molecular mechanisms opens new avenues for acupuncture‐based therapies in immune regulation.

Summary
