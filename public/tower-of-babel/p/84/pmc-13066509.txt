A unified EfficientSwinB-based framework for medicinal plant recognition and safflower germplasm classification
Authors: Phuong Thuy Khuat, Hoang Thien Van
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe accurate recognition of medicinal plants and fine-grained crop genetic resources is essential for pharmacology, precision agriculture, and biodiversity conservation. However, leaf-based plant recognition remains challenging due to complex backgrounds, illumination variations, and subtle inter-class differences. To address these challenges, this study investigates plant image recognition at two levels of granularity. For general medicinal plant recognition under unconstrained outdoor conditions, we propose EfficientSwinB-SE, an enhanced Swin Transformer–based model that incorporates structured pruning and a squeeze-and-excitation module to improve efficiency and discriminative capability. For fine-grained classification of safflower germplasm varieties under controlled laboratory conditions, we further develop OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE, a task-specific extension optimized for distinguishing closely related varieties. Experimental results demonstrate that EfficientSwinB-SE consistently outperforms SwinB and other state-of-the-art models, such as VGG11_bn, MobileNetV2, DenseNet, EfficientNetB4, ViT, ConvNeXt, and DeiT, in medicinal plant recognition, achieving accuracies of 99.75% on the Indian Medicinal Plant dataset and 97.70% on the Indonesian Medicinal Plant dataset. Meanwhile, OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE achieves superior performance in fine-grained safflower germplasm classification with an accuracy of 91.63%. These findings confirm the effectiveness of the proposed framework for multi-level plant recognition, with practical applications in medicinal plant identification, germplasm management, and intelligent agricultural systems.Keywords: Deep learning, Medicinal plant, Pruning, Squeeze and excitation, Swin transformer, Vision transformerSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Medicinal plants play a crucial role in human health and disease treatment. Herbs, spices, and medicinal plants provide a wide range of health benefits, including antioxidant activity, anticancer potential, enhancement of mental and cognitive functions, diabetes control, pain relief, anti-inflammatory and antitumor effects, glucose- and cholesterol-lowering properties, modulation of mood and cognition, and treatment of respiratory diseases1–4. Given their importance to human health, there is an urgent need for accurate and efficient classification of medicinal plants, particularly due to their high biodiversity and the pronounced morphological similarities among species. Accurate classification is essential not only for the discovery, breeding, and cultivation of medicinal plants but also for biodiversity conservation.

Traditional techniques for medicinal plant classification require extensive expertise and experience from biologists and herbalists, as medicinal plants exhibit high diversity at both genus and species levels, and many different species share similar shapes and vein patterns. These manual identification processes are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and costly. In recent years, to reduce time and cost while improving classification accuracy and efficiency, automated deep learning–based approaches have been increasingly introduced for medicinal plant classification. Among these approaches, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have proven to be one of the most effective architectures, particularly for image-based plant recognition tasks. CNNs are capable of automatically learning and extracting discriminative features from images across various environments5–7. However, CNN-based models typically require large-scale image datasets; otherwise, they are prone to overfitting. In addition, training deep CNNs is computationally expensive. To address these limitations, transfer learning has been widely adopted as an effective solution. This approach leverages pre-trained models to solve new classification tasks with limited data, thereby reducing overfitting and computational cost. Numerous studies have demonstrated that transfer learning significantly improves the performance of CNNs in medicinal plant classification tasks12,13.

More recently, Vision Transformer (ViT) models8 have been introduced for image recognition tasks by employing global self-attention mechanisms. ViT has shown promising performance in plant classification, particularly in controlled environments. In our previous work35, we proposed OLSF-ViT, which integrates an optimized Gabor filter with ViT to enhance fine-grained classification of safflower varieties under laboratory conditions. Although effective for distinguishing subtle intra-species differences, ViT-based models remain less robust in outdoor scenarios. This limitation arises because ViT primarily relies on global attention and lacks an explicit hierarchical structure, making it less capable of modeling local leaf details such as vein patterns, edges, and texture variations in complex backgrounds and under varying illumination. To address these challenges, the Swin Transformer (SwinB)9 introduces a hierarchical architecture with shifted-window self-attention, enabling efficient modeling of both local structures and global contextual information. This design makes SwinB particularly suitable for outdoor leaf images, where fine local features and large-scale contextual cues must be jointly exploited. However, despite its strong representational capability, the original SwinB architecture does not explicitly model channel-wise feature importance. Its attention mechanism primarily focuses on spatial relationships within windows, while the contribution of individual feature channels is learned implicitly. As a result, SwinB may underutilize discriminative channel information that is critical for plant recognition, especially in the presence of background noise and subtle inter-class variations. In parallel, recent studies have shown that model efficiency and generalization can be improved by incorporating pruning strategies25,26 and channel-attention mechanisms such as Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) blocks27,30. Pruning removes redundant or low-importance weights, reducing computational cost and improving generalization, while SE blocks explicitly learn channel-wise dependencies by adaptively reweighting informative feature channels. However, existing works typically apply these techniques independently or focus on CNN-based architectures, and their integration into hierarchical transformer models for plant recognition remains largely underexplored.

Motivated by these observations, this paper proposes EfficientSwinB-SE, an enhanced Swin Transformer framework for medicinal plant recognition under unconstrained outdoor conditions. Two targeted architectural refinements are introduced. First, a pruning strategy based on the Pruning of Least Important Weights (PLIW) criterion is applied to the Conv2D layer in the linear embedding stage. This design choice preserves the structural integrity of the Swin Transformer while reducing redundant computations at the feature embedding level. Second, a Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) module is integrated into the classifier head to explicitly model channel-wise feature importance, thereby compensating for SwinB’s lack of explicit channel attention and enhancing discriminative feature learning. Furthermore, recognizing that fine-grained germplasm classification poses a fundamentally different challenge from species-level recognition, we extend EfficientSwinB-SE to develop OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE for safflower germplasm identification under controlled laboratory conditions. In this setting, varieties exhibit extremely subtle morphological differences, requiring greater sensitivity to local structural patterns and channel-level feature variations. By inheriting the pruning-aware embedding design and SE-based channel attention, OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE provides a task-specific architecture optimized for fine-grained intra-species discrimination. The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows:

Proposal of EfficientSwinB-SE, a refined Swin Transformer architecture that enhances channel-wise feature learning via squeeze-and-excitation attention and improves computational efficiency through embedding-level pruning, thereby addressing the absence of explicit channel modeling and parameter redundancy in the original SwinB for medicinal plant recognition.

Proposal of EfficientSwinB-SE, a refined Swin Transformer architecture that enhances channel-wise feature learning via squeeze-and-excitation attention and improves computational efficiency through embedding-level pruning, thereby addressing the absence of explicit channel modeling and parameter redundancy in the original SwinB for medicinal plant recognition.

Development of OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE, a task-specific extension tailored for fine-grained safflower germplasm classification, enabling reliable discrimination of closely related varieties under controlled laboratory conditions.

Related works

In the classification of plants, especially medicinal plants, CNNs and transfer learning with CNNs have demonstrated the capability to automatically and accurately classify leaf images. Akter and Hosen5 utilized CNNs to categorize 10 medicinal plants in Bangladesh, attaining an accuracy of 71.3% with 34,123 training images and 3,570 testing images. In 2020, Zin et al.10 achieved an accuracy of 99.00% using a CNN model with extensive parameter tuning and data augmentation for classifying 12 medicinal plant species in Malaysia. Musa et al.7(2021) showed that CNNs performed effectively by correctly classifying six types of medicinal plant leaves with an accuracy of 95.58%. In 2023, Ang and Linsangan11 introduced a hybrid approach for plant classification by combining Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) with CNN, achieving 95% accuracy on five herb datasets from the Philippines. Although CNNs offer strong classification performance, they require large amounts of training data to avoid overfitting. This requirement limits their flexibility when applied to smaller or imbalanced datasets.

To address this issue, transfer learning with pre-trained CNN models enables more efficient training on smaller medicinal plant datasets. Kaya et al. (2019)12 applied four pre-trained models (DF-VGG16/LDA, CNN-RNN, DF-AlexNet/LDA, and FT-VGG16) to four public leaf datasets (Flavia, Swedish Leaf, UCI Leaf, and PlantVillage), achieving accuracies of 99%, 98.8%, 96.2%, and 98.8%, respectively. Roopashree and Anitha (2021)13 proposed the DeepHerb model based on Xception and an Artificial Neural Network (ANN), which achieved 97.5% accuracy on an Indian herbal dataset containing 2,515 images across 40 species. Similarly, TS and Prabalakshmi14 combined Canny edge detection with the InceptionV3 architecture to classify plants, achieving 97.32% accuracy on the Ayur Bharat dataset. In 2022, Valdez et al.15 utilized MobileNetV3 to classify ten plant species with an accuracy of 97.43%. Pushpa and Rani (2023)16 introduced Ayur-PlantNet for the Indian Ayurvedic dataset (6,000 images, 40 species), attaining 92.27% accuracy with a parameter-efficient architecture. Sinha et al. (2024)17 employed DenseNet121 to classify 30 Ayurvedic leaf types, achieving 98.97% accuracy and outperforming VGG16, MobileNetV2, and InceptionV3. Sharma and Vardhan18 utilized Sobel edge detection in conjunction with a multi-task joint learning network (MTJNet) to classify Indian medicinal leaves, achieving an accuracy of 99.71%. Finally, Musyaffa et al. (2024)19 introduced the Indonesian Medicinal Plant dataset and applied ConvNeXt, obtaining 92.5% accuracy. These studies demonstrate that leveraging pre-trained feature extractors through transfer learning significantly improves classification accuracy on medicinal plant datasets. In addition, Wu et al. [43] proposed a taxonomic loss function that explicitly encodes hierarchical relationships among family, genus, and species labels, consistently outperforming cross-entropy loss on the PlantCLEF 2015 and 2017 benchmarks. In particular, SENet-154 trained with taxonomic loss achieved notable accuracy gains across all taxonomic levels on the large-scale PlantCLEF 2017 dataset. Dalvi et al.38 addressed plant species identification by proposing a multi-attribute framework that combines botanical taxonomy with deep learning–based transfer learning. Their study evaluated several CNN architectures, including InceptionResNetV2, MobileNetV2, InceptionV3, and VGG16, on a dataset of 12,000 images using both leaf-based and non-leaf attributes. While the models achieved near-perfect accuracy for leaf-based recognition, their performance degraded when relying on other plant characteristics, highlighting the importance of discriminative structural features in plant identification.

Even though CNNs and transfer learning are effective at learning local features, they struggle with modeling long-range dependencies and often rely heavily on large datasets. This limitation reduces their effectiveness in complex outdoor conditions. Vision Transformers (ViTs) address this problem by using self-attention mechanisms to capture global relationships. Picek et al. (2022)20 applied ViT-Large/16 to the PlantCLEF 2017 dataset, achieving an accuracy of 91.15%, and to the ExpertLifeCLEF 2018 dataset, achieving an accuracy of 83.54%. In 2023, Van Hieu et al.21 proposed the PlantKViT model, which combines ViT and k-nearest neighbors (KNN) for forest tree classification and achieved 93.0% accuracy on the DanangForestPlant dataset. In 2024, Patel22 demonstrated that ViT achieved an accuracy of 95.20%, outperforming models such as VGG16, MobileNetV2, and DenseNet169 in automatic seedling classification. In 2025, H. T. Van et al.35 proposed the OLSF-ViT method for safflower germplasm differentiation and leaf species classification. Although this model performed effectively on laboratory data, it was less effective on outdoor datasets due to ViT’s reliance on global attention, which limits its ability to capture local features and hierarchical information. In addition, ViTs require high computational cost for high-resolution images because of their global attention mechanism. The Swin Transformer9 addresses these limitations by adopting a hierarchical, window-based self-attention mechanism, which significantly reduces computational complexity. In 2024, Pinasthika et al.23 introduced the SparseSwin model, achieving accuracies of 87.26%, 97.43%, and 85.35% on ImageNet100, CIFAR10, and CIFAR100, respectively. In the same year, Liu et al.24 proposed an improved version of the SwinB model for strawberry ripeness classification, reporting 93.9% AP50 and 84.1% AP75 on a large-scale dataset.

In parallel, model optimization techniques have also been widely explored. Pruning is a common approach to reducing model complexity and accelerating inference without incurring significant accuracy degradation. Fountsop et al.25 applied unstructured pruning to LeNet5, VGG16, and AlexNet, achieving up to a 38× reduction in model size with only a minor loss in accuracy. In 2022, Chen et al.26 introduced CACPNET, which combines ResNet-18 with unstructured pruning, achieving 99.7% accuracy on the PlantVillage dataset while reducing the model size by 57.85%. In addition, attention-based modules, such as Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) blocks27, have been proposed to enhance channel-wise feature learning and improve classification performance. For instance, Chen et al.28 proposed SE-MobileNet, which achieved 99.33% accuracy in rice disease detection. SECNN29 achieved 99.12% accuracy for chili leaf disease classification when data augmentation was applied. More recently, Swapno et al.30 proposed ViT-SENet-Tom for tomato fruit classification, achieving a validation accuracy of 93.87%.

These studies indicate that although Vision Transformers achieve strong performance on laboratory datasets, their heavy reliance on global attention limits generalization in outdoor leaf classification, where local structures and background variations are critical. In contrast, Swin Transformers, with their hierarchical architecture and window-based self-attention, are better suited to modeling the complex spatial variations present in unconstrained outdoor images. In parallel, optimization strategies such as pruning and squeeze-and-excitation (SE) attention have been shown to improve efficiency and discriminative power in CNN- and ViT-based models. However, these techniques have not yet been systematically integrated into Swin Transformer architectures for plant recognition tasks. Moreover, existing Swin-based studies primarily focus on species-level classification and do not address fine-grained germplasm recognition under controlled conditions. These gaps motivate our work. We propose EfficientSwinB-SE to enhance SwinB with embedding-level pruning and explicit channel-wise attention for robust medicinal plant recognition in outdoor environments, and further develop OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE as a task-specific extension for fine-grained safflower germplasm classification under laboratory settings.

Datasets

The Indian Medicinal Plant dataset (DIMPSAR)33 was collected from various botanical gardens in and around Mysuru, Karnataka, and Kasaragod, Kerala, India. These images were captured using various mobile phones with varying resolutions and specifications (Fig. 1a). The Indonesian Medicinal Plant dataset19 was independently gathered using the Google Images search engine and then checked with a list from the Medicinal Plant Maintenance Installation at the Baturaja Health Research and Development Center under the Indonesian Ministry of Health. The JNUSafflower dataset (Fig. 1c)35 was collected by biologists from the Department of Plant Resources and Environment, Jeju National University, Jeju, Republic of Korea, and contains 43 safflower varieties. Proposed methods

Building on our previous work35, which proposed OLSF-ViT for fine-grained safflower variety recognition under laboratory conditions, this study extends the investigation to a broader plant recognition setting encompassing both outdoor medicinal plant species and fine-grained germplasm classification. While OLSF-ViT achieved strong performance on controlled datasets, its reliance on global attention limited its generalization to outdoor leaf images. To address this limitation, we adopt the Swin Transformer9 as the backbone architecture due to its hierarchical structure and shifted-window self-attention mechanism, which jointly model global contextual information and local leaf structures under complex backgrounds and varying illumination conditions. Accordingly, we investigate plant image recognition at two levels of granularity. For general medicinal plant recognition in unconstrained outdoor environments, we propose EfficientSwinB-SE, which enhances SwinB by introducing embedding-level pruning and explicit channel-wise feature learning. Specifically, redundant weights in the Conv2D embedding layer are removed using the PLIW criterion to improve computational efficiency and reduce parameter redundancy, while a squeeze-and-excitation (SE) module is integrated into the classifier head to recalibrate channel responses and emphasize discriminative structural features. For the more challenging task of fine-grained safflower germplasm classification under controlled laboratory conditions, we further develop OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE, a task-specific extension optimized for distinguishing closely related varieties. Together, these designs enable robust and efficient plant recognition across varying visual complexities and classification granularities. SwinB transform

The architecture of the SwinB Transformer (Fig. 2)9 consists of four main stages. First, the 224 × 224 × 3 RGB input image is divided into non-overlapping 4 × 4 patches, each treated as a token with 4 × 4 × 3 = 48 raw features (patch partition). A learnable linear embedding then projects each 48-dimensional patch vector into a C-dimensional feature space, yielding an initial sequence of tokens. In Stage 1, the sequence passes through two Swin Transformer blocks, followed by Patch Merging, which reduces the spatial resolution (e.g., 56 × 56 → 28 × 28) while doubling the channel dimension. Stages 2, 3, and 4 repeat this pattern with 2, 18, and 2 Swin Transformer blocks, respectively, each followed by Patch Merging (except after Stage 4). After four stages, the feature map is globally average pooled and fed into a fully connected layer to produce the final class prediction for medicinal plant classification. Within each stage, Swin Transformer blocks come in pairs: one with window-based multi-head self-attention (W-MSA) and one with shifted-window self-attention (SW-MSA). W-MSA captures local context inside each window, while SW-MSA enables information flow across windows. Their alternation balances the ability to model both local and global token relationships with computational efficiency.

PLIW algorithm

In this section, we present the Patch-Level Importance Weight (PLIW) pruning algorithm for EfficientSwinB-SE, which targets low-importance Conv2D weights in the linear embedding module. This approach simplifies the model while maintaining high classification accuracy (Fig. 2). The Conv2D layer in this stage converts raw image patches into token embeddings but often contains redundant weights. Therefore, we use L1-unstructured pruning, which eliminates weights with the smallest absolute values31, to reduce this redundancy. This creates sparsity in the early stage, where unnecessary parameters are most common. Inspired by recent findings on the benefits of pruning in initial layers32, our approach produces a leaner embedding layer that improves both robustness and accuracy in medicinal leaf classification. EfficientSwinB-SE for medicinal plant classification

The proposed EfficientSwinB-SE model enhances the Swin Transformer-Base (SwinB) by integrating embedding-level structured pruning and explicit channel-wise attention to improve both efficiency and discriminative capability for medicinal plant classification. The model is initialized with ImageNet-1 K pre-trained weights and fine-tuned on medicinal plant datasets collected under outdoor conditions. The overall processing pipeline is summarized as follows:

Patch Embedding with Structured Pruning: Input plant images are partitioned into non-overlapping patches and embedded using a Conv2D-based linear embedding layer. To improve efficiency and reduce redundancy, embedding-level structured pruning based on the PLIW criterion is applied, eliminating filters with low L1 importance.

Unlike the original SwinB architecture, which lacks explicit channel-wise importance learning and retains substantial redundancy in its embedding stage, EfficientSwinB-SE jointly addresses these limitations through structured pruning and SE-based channel recalibration. Embedding-level pruning reduces unnecessary parameters and sharpens early feature representations, while the SE block enables the model to adaptively emphasize informative channels associated with leaf morphology and venation patterns. This complementary design leads to a more compact, discriminative, and robust model for medicinal plant classification, particularly in challenging outdoor environments with background clutter and illumination variations.

OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE for fine-grained safflower germplasm classification

Safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.) is an important medicinal crop with well-established pharmacological value, and accurate discrimination of its germplasm varieties is essential for genetic resource management, breeding programs, and biodiversity conservation39,40. Nevertheless, fine-grained variety classification remains challenging due to subtle inter-variety differences, which are primarily reflected in leaf vein structures and fine texture patterns.

Unlike outdoor medicinal plant datasets, the safflower leaf images used in this study are captured under controlled laboratory conditions, where background clutter and illumination variations are largely eliminated. Consequently, the most discriminative cues are concentrated in fine-scale venation characteristics. In our previous work35, we demonstrated that the Optimal Leaf Structure Feature (OLSF) effectively enhances vein-related structures and improves safflower variety recognition. OLSF extracts oriented local structural responses by convolving the input image with a bank of directionally tuned Gabor filters and selecting the most informative representations, thereby enhancing leaf venation while preserving the overall texture and shape. Building on this idea, we propose OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE, which integrates OLSF-based vein enhancement with the proposed EfficientSwinB-SE backbone for fine-grained safflower germplasm classification. Specifically, OLSF35 is applied as a preprocessing step to emphasize oriented venation patterns and suppress homogeneous regions. The enhanced images are then fed into EfficientSwinB-SE, which leverages hierarchical shifted-window self-attention to model multi-scale structural relationships, embedding-level pruning to improve efficiency, and SE-based channel recalibration to highlight discriminative features before classification. By combining biologically meaningful vein enhancement with hierarchical transformer-based feature learning, OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE effectively exploits the characteristics of laboratory-acquired safflower leaf images and provides a robust solution for fine-grained germplasm discrimination.

Experimental results

The objective of these experiments was to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed EfficientSwinB-SE method compared with other state-of-the-art approaches such as VGG11_bn36, MobileNetV215, DenseNet37, EfficientNetB434, ConvNeXt41, DeiT42, ViT8, and SwinB9 for leaf classification in both outdoor and laboratory settings. Therefore, we used our previously published laboratory-collected JNUSafflower dataset for variety classification, the Indian Medicinal Plant and Indonesian Medicinal Plant datasets for outdoor species-level classification. All images were resized directly to 224 × 224 pixels and were normalized using the standard ImageNet mean and standard deviation. Each dataset was randomly split into 80% training and 20% testing sets using a fixed random seed (42), with stratified sampling to preserve the original class distributions. On the 80% training set, 5-fold stratified cross-validation was performed to assess model stability and to support the training process. In each cross-validation fold, four folds were used for training and one fold was used as a validation set for monitoring training convergence and applying early stopping. Early stopping was applied only during the cross-validation stage. Validation accuracy from the held-out fold was monitored, with a patience of 5 epochs and a minimum improvement threshold (min delta) of 0.001.

Training was terminated if the validation accuracy did not improve by at least 0.001 for five consecutive epochs. After compxleting cross-validation, each model was trained from scratch on the entire 80% training set using the same training configuration and a fixed number of training epochs (50 epochs). The trained model was then evaluated once on the independent 20% test set to obtain the final reported results. Details of the training setup are listed in Table 2.

Effectiveness of EfficientSwinB-SE for medicinal plant and leaf variety classification

In this experiment, we evaluated EfficientSwinB-SE on the same datasets used in Experiment 1 to compare its performance with that of SwinB. The experiment consisted of two phases: (1) pruning less important weights in the Conv2D embedding layer of SwinB, and (2) integrating an SE block into the classifier head. SwinB was first initialized with ImageNet-1 K pretrained weights and fine-tuned on each dataset, following the same settings as in Experiment 1. Pruning was then applied to these fine-tuned models using the PLIW algorithm, with sparsity ratios ranging from 10% to 50% in increments of 10%. Figure 7 illustrates that pruning removes a substantial number of low-importance weights. The weight histogram shows a sharp spike at zero after pruning, indicating that many parameters have been eliminated. The remaining weights are still evenly spread around zero, which shows that pruning doesn’t change the overall statistics of the parameters. In other words, pruning effectively eliminates redundant weights from the Conv2D layer while preserving the model’s primary representational capacity. Table 7 shows the test accuracies of the pruned SwinB models on all datasets. The results indicate that SwinB achieves the highest accuracy at a pruning ratio of 10%. In contrast, higher pruning levels consistently degrade performance across all datasets. Based on these findings, a pruning ratio of 10% was selected for the subsequent step, which involved integrating an SE block into the classifier head.

Discussion

The experimental results demonstrate that EfficientSwinB-SE substantially advances the state of the art in medicinal plant classification and fine-grained germplasm recognition. It achieves the highest accuracy and F1-scores among all evaluated deep learning models. On the Indian Medicinal Plant dataset, accuracy improves from 97.89% with the original SwinB to 99.75% with EfficientSwinB-SE. On the Indonesian Medicinal Plant dataset, accuracy increases from 94.15% to 96.15%. The most pronounced improvement is observed on the safflower (OLSF) dataset, where accuracy rises from 78.37% (SwinB) to 91.63% (EfficientSwinB-SE). These gains are primarily attributed to the combined effects of structured pruning and SE integration. Pruning removes redundant Conv2D weights in the embedding layer, reducing model complexity and focusing learning on informative features. The SE module recalibrates channel-wise responses, emphasizing discriminative morphological patterns such as leaf venation and serrated margins. This synergy enables the model to capture subtle intra-class differences while maintaining efficiency and generalization capability. Compared to prior work, EfficientSwinB-SE consistently outperforms existing methods. For instance, Ayur-PlantNet16 achieved 92.27% on the Indian Medicinal Plant dataset, while ConvNeXt19 reached 92.50% on the Indonesian Medicinal Plant dataset (Table 15). For fine-grained safflower germplasm classification, OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE (91.63%) surpasses OLSF-ViT35(89.65%), demonstrating its ability to discriminate closely related varieties without relying on additional handcrafted feature extraction.

These results highlight that targeted pruning and channel-wise attention not only produce leaner models but also enhance the learning of biologically meaningful leaf features, leading to more reliable classification. Beyond medicinal plants, EfficientSwinB-SE shows strong potential for plant germplasm identification, thereby supporting biodiversity conservation, crop improvement, and pharmaceutical research. The proposed approach can also be extended to smart agriculture applications, such as automated leaf-based monitoring and precision crop management. Nevertheless, further validation across diverse plant species, imaging conditions, and real-world outdoor scenarios is necessary to fully establish the generalizability and robustness of the proposed framework.

Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive framework for multi-level plant recognition, addressing both general medicinal plant classification under unconstrained outdoor conditions and fine-grained safflower germplasm identification under controlled laboratory settings. We propose EfficientSwinB-SE, an enhanced Swin Transformer model that integrates embedding-level pruning based on the PLIW criterion and a squeeze-and-excitation (SE) block to emphasize discriminative channel-wise features. For fine-grained safflower classification, we further develop OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE, which combines Optimal Leaf Structure Feature (OLSF) enhancement with the EfficientSwinB-SE backbone to capture subtle venation and texture patterns. Experiments conducted on the Indian Medicinal Plant, Indonesian Medicinal Plant, and JNUSafflower datasets demonstrate that EfficientSwinB-SE consistently outperforms SwinB, ViT, and other state-of-the-art CNN and transformer models in terms of both accuracy and robustness. OLSF-EfficientSwinB-SE achieves superior performance in distinguishing closely related safflower varieties, highlighting the effectiveness of combining biologically meaningful preprocessing with hierarchical transformer-based modeling, pruning, and channel-wise attention mechanisms. Beyond medicinal plant recognition, the proposed framework has strong potential applications in germplasm management, biodiversity conservation, smart agriculture, and other plant-related research domains. Future work will extend this approach to additional plant species and imaging conditions and explore its integration into automated crop monitoring, disease detection, and precision farming systems.
