Epigenetic potentiation of 5-fluorouracil by HDAC inhibitor quisinostat enhances antitumor effects in colorectal cancer
Authors: Gizem Calibasi-Kocal, Hasan Kurter, Eylem Doga Nartas, Ender Berat Ellidokuz, Yasemin Basbinar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHistone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors have emerged as promising epigenetic therapeutics in cancer treatment; however, their clinical efficacy in solid tumors remains limited. Quisinostat is a potent pan-HDAC inhibitor with demonstrated anticancer activity in various malignancies. This study evaluates the therapeutic potential of Quisinostat, in combination with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), in colorectal cancer (CRC) cell lines.MethodsThe effects of Quisinostat and 5-FU, as monotherapies and in combination, were investigated in HCT-116 and HT-29 CRC cell lines. H3K27 acetylation levels were assessed by immunofluorescence to evaluate epigenetic modulation. Cell viability, IC50 shifts, and proliferation were measured using resazurin assays and Ki-67 staining. Apoptosis and mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) were analyzed via Hoechst 33342/PI dual staining and JC-1 assays. Migration and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) phenotypes were evaluated through wound healing assays and E-cadherin/N-cadherin immunostaining.ResultsQuisinostat significantly increased H3K27 acetylation and enhanced the cytotoxic effect of 5-FU by lowering its IC50 in both cell lines. The combination treatment markedly suppressed proliferation, as evidenced by reduced Ki-67 expression. Quisinostat in combination with 5-FU significantly enhanced ΔΨm loss and apoptotic cell death. Furthermore, the combination treatment inhibited cell migration and reversed the EMT phenotype by increasing E-cadherin and decreasing N-cadherin expression.ConclusionQuisinostat enhances the antitumor efficacy of 5-FU in CRC cells by promoting histone acetylation, augmenting cytotoxic and apoptotic responses, and reversing EMT-associated migratory phenotypes. These findings support the potential of Quisinostat as an effective adjunct to 5-FU-based chemotherapy in CRC.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Histone deacetylase, Histone deacetylase inhibitor, Apoptosis, Metastasis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global Health challenge and representing 10.0% of cancer incidence and 9.4% of cancer-related mortality worldwide, ranking it third in incidence and second in mortality among all malignancies [1, 2]. Despite advances in screening and multimodal therapy, the five-year survival for metastatic CRC remains below 15%, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that overcome resistance to standard chemotherapy.

Epigenetic dysregulation is now recognized as a key driver of CRC pathogenesis and progression. Heritable, reversible modifications such as DNA methylation, histone post-translational modifications, and non-coding RNA mediated gene regulation alter chromatin states without changing the underlying DNA sequence [3]. Aberrant methylation of tumor suppressor gene promoters and dysregulated histone modification patterns are detected early in the adenoma–carcinoma sequence and serve as diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive biomarkers in CRC [4].

Among these markers, post-translational modifications of histones which are the key regulators of gene expression, including acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination, can either activate or repress gene expression, depending on how they alter chromatin structure [5]. As components of co-repressor complexes, Histone deacetylases (HDACs) remove acetyl groups from ε-amino lysine residues on histone tails, promoting chromatin compaction and transcriptional repression, and regulate a wide range of essential biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and development, through their interactions with various transcription factors and transcription co-regulators [6]. The human HDAC family comprises Class I (HDAC1, 2, 3, 8), Class IIa (HDAC4, 5, 7, 9), Class IIb (HDAC6, 10), Class III (sirtuins), and Class IV (HDAC11) enzymes [6]. Overexpression of Class I HDACs, particularly HDAC1, HDAC2 and HDAC8, correlates with poor prognosis in CRC, where they repress differentiation-associated genes and facilitate epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [7, 8].

Pharmacological HDAC inhibitors (HDACi), are epigenetic regulatory chemotherapeutics used as primary treatment models in different types of cancer. These inhibitors prevent histone deacetylation and cause increased acetylation. Thus, they cause the relaxation of chromatin structures and allow the increase in the expression levels of genes that have suppressive effects on cancer cells [9]. HDACi have shown promise by inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in cancer cells. They mediate these effects through cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) and cyclins, essential cell cycle regulators [10]. Studies highlight that HDAC inhibitors can restore p21 expression, causing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis through the intrinsic pathway involving poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) and caspase activation [11].

Quisinostat (JNJ-26481585) is a second-generation hydroxamate-based HDACi with high potency against Class I/II enzymes and favorable pharmacodynamic properties in preclinical solid and hematologic malignancy models particularly in T-cell lymphoma and myeloid leukemia [12–15].

While Quisinostat monotherapy elicits robust histone H3 hyperacetylation and growth inhibition in various cancer cell lines [16], its effects in CRC, particularly in combination with 5-FU, remain underexplored. In this study, we assess the impact of Quisinostat alone and combined with 5-FU on proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and EMT in HCT-116 and HT-29 CRC cell lines, providing mechanistic insight into its potential to enhance 5-FU efficacy.

Material & methods

Cell culture

Human colorectal carcinoma cell lines HCT-116 (ATCC® CCL-247™) and HT-29 (ATCC® HTB-38™) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Cegrogen Biotech, Germany) supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cegrogen Biotech, Germany) and 1% (v/v) penicillin (100 U/mL)–streptomycin (100 µg/mL) (Cegrogen Biotech, Germany). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and were passaged every 2–3 days upon reaching ~ 80% confluence.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was quantified using the Resazurin assay (Cayman Chemical Company, USA) to evaluate cytotoxicity, determine the IC25 and IC50 values of 5-FU, and identify the highest non-cytotoxic concentration of Quisinostat.

To determine the IC25 and IC50 values, HCT-116 and HT-29 cells were treated for 48 h with serial dilutions of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU; Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), ranging from 0.69 to 178 µM for HCT-116 cells and from 1 to 729 µM for HT-29 cells. The highest non-cytotoxic concentration of Quisinostat was determined through preliminary dose–response experiments in which HCT-116 and HT-29 cells were treated for 48 h with serial dilutions of Quisinostat (Qui; MedChemExpress, USA) ranging from 5 to 640 nM.

After 48 h incubation with drugs, 10 µL resazurin reagent (0.01 mg/mL; Cayman Chemical, USA) was added to each well, and plates were incubated an additional 2 h under standard culture conditions. Fluorescence was measured on a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with excitation at 560 nm and emission at 590 nm. Relative cell viability (%) was calculated as [17]:

IC25 and IC50 values for 5-FU, as well as the non-cytotoxic concentration of Quisinostat, were determined from dose–response curves generated using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.4.2. software). All treatments were performed in quadruplicate wells for each condition and repeated in three independent experiments.

In the experimental design, it was aimed to evaluate the potentiating effect of Quisinostat without introducing direct cytotoxicity that could confound the interpretation of combination outcomes. Therefore, the highest concentration of Quisinostat that did not cause a statistically significant reduction in cell viability compared with untreated controls, as determined by preliminary dose–response experiments, was defined as the non-cytotoxic concentration and used as a fixed dose in combination assays. This strategy allowed us to specifically assess Quisinostat’s role as an epigenetic modulator sensitizing cells to 5-FU, rather than as a direct cytotoxic agent.Combination treatments employed 5-FU at its IC25 and IC50 concentrations, each co-administered with Qui at a fixed concentration, which is the highest non-cytotoxic concentration on cells (Treatment groups: IC25, IC25 + Qui, IC50, IC50 + Qui, and untreated control).

The interaction between Quisinostat and 5-fluorouracil 5-FU was assessed using the Bliss independence model in the Synergy Finder web platform (Institute for Molecular Medicine Finland; https://synergyfinder.org, accessed August 24, 2025) [18]. This tool enables quantitative evaluation of whether the observed combination effect deviates from the expected additive outcome. In this context, synergy refers to a combined effect that is greater than predicted based on the activity of each drug alone, whereas antagonism denotes a reduction of the effect compared to additivity [19]. For analysis, experimental data consisting of percentage inhibition values were organized with a fixed Quisinostat concentration and serial dilutions of 5-FU. The Bliss score (ΔBliss) was calculated as the difference between the observed and expected effects. Positive ΔBliss values indicate synergistic interactions, and negative values denote antagonism. Where applicable, ΔBliss outcomes are presented as mean ± SD.

Determination of the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨM)

Mitochondrial dysfunction plays a key role in the initiation of apoptosis and is often considered a central component of the apoptotic pathway. A critical event in this process is the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, which leads to dissipation of the transmembrane potential (Δψm), release of pro-apoptotic factors, and impairment of oxidative phosphorylation. In many models of apoptosis, the loss of Δψm occurs at an early stage, serving as a sensitive indicator of mitochondrial-mediated apoptotic signaling [20, 21]. Therefore, mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), an early indicator of intrinsic apoptotic signaling reflecting loss of mitochondrial integrity under cytotoxic stress, was measured using the tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) (Abcam, UK), a cationic dye that accumulates in energized mitochondria. At low mitochondrial membrane potential, JC-1 is predominantly a monomer that yields green fluorescence with emission of 530 ± 15 nm. At high mitochondrial membrane potential, the dye aggregates yielding a red to orange colored emission (590 ± 17.5 nm). Therefore, a decrease in the aggregate fluorescent count is indicative of depolarization due to apoptosis whereas an increase is indicative of hyperpolarization due to healthy cells.

Cells were exposed to 5-FU at its IC25 and IC50 concentrations administered as monotherapy or in combination with Quisinostat (IC25 + Qui; IC50 + Qui), in addition to a treatment arm receiving Quisinostat alone. Following drug treatment, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with 10 µM JC-1 dye for 15 min at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator, protected from light. After staining, fluorescence was recorded on a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Ex. 485/Em. 530 for monomer and Ex. 535/Em. 590 for aggregates. Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was quantified as the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity. A high red/green (aggregate/monomer) ratio reflects mitochondrial polarization (healthy cells), whereas a reduced ratio indicates ΔΨm depolarization characteristic of apoptotic cells. Additionally for visualisation, cells were imaged under a fluorescence microscope at excitation/emission of 540/570 (for JC-1 aggregate) and 485/535 (for JC-1 monomer), respectively. Each treatment was carried out in four parallel wells for each condition and the experiments were independently repeated three times.

Hoescht 33342/propidium iodide dual staining

Hoechst 33,342/Propidium Iodide (PI) dual staining is a widely used fluorescence-based method to evaluate cell membrane integrity, nuclear morphology and cell viability in apoptosis and necrosis studies. Following 48 h treatment with 5-FU, Quisinostat, or their combination, the cells were treated with a mixed solution comprising 1 µg/mL Hoechst 33342 and 5 µg/mL PI, and then they were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS and imaged under a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, USA) at excitation/emission of 405/561nm. Hoechst 33342 binds to double-stranded DNA and stains blue the nuclei of both live and dead cells. PI penetrates cells with compromised plasma membranes (typically late apoptotic or necrotic cells) and emits red fluorescence. Treatments were conducted in four replicate wells for each condition and reproduced in three separate independent experiments. Quantification was performed in five random fields per wells.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to evaluate the effects of alone and combined applications of Quisinostat and 5-FU on histone protein acetylation (H3K27-Ac), proliferation index (Ki-67) and metastatic phenotype (E-cadherin, N-cadherin) properties in colorectal cancer cells. Cells were treated with Quisinostat and/or 5-FU at IC25 and IC50 concentrations (monotherapy and combination arms) for 48 h. Following treatment, wells were gently rinsed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 20 min at room temperature. Permeabilization was performed with 0,1% Triton X-100 solution in 1X PBS for 5 min, followed by washing with PBS. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating cells in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were prepared in blocking buffer and applied overnight at 4 °C as follows: E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, #14472S, USA) (1:400 dilution), N-cadherin (Proteintech, #22018-1-AP, USA) (1:450 dilution), H3K27ac (Cell Signaling Technology, #8173S, USA) (1:250 Dilution), Ki-67 (Abcam, #ab15580, UK) (1:1000 dilution). The next day, cells were washed three times with cold PBS (5 min each) and incubated with secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor-594 goat anti-rabbit (Cell Signaling, #8889S, USA) and Alexa Fluor-488 goat anti-mouse (Cell Signaling, #4408S, USA) (both at 1:1 000 in blocking buffer) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After secondary antibody incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 dye (1 µg/mL in PBS) for 5 min at room temperature. After the staining protocol, the cells were washed in PBS, and fluorescent images were acquired on five random fields per well using a confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany) under identical settings for all conditions. Quantification of mean fluorescence intensity were analyzed with ImageJ Software (Version 1.54 g, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) [22]. Each treatment was conducted in four replicate wells for each condition and reproduced in three independent experiments.

Wound-healing assay

To assess cell migratory capacity, wound-scratch assays were performed on confluent monolayers of HCT-116 and HT-29 cells. A uniform linear scratch was generated in the monolayer using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Cells were incubated for 48 h in medium supplemented with 5-FU at either its IC25 or IC50 concentration—administered alone or in combination with Quisinostat (5-FU IC25 + Qui; 5-FU IC50 + Qui)—and in a separate arm treated with Quisinostat monotherapy. Treatments were conducted in four replicate wells for each condition and reproduced in three separate independent experiments. Images of the wound area were captured immediately after scratching (0 h) and at 48 h using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Zeiss, Germany). Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated three times (biological replicates), with each condition tested in quadruplicate wells (technical replicates) per experiment. All data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.4.2. software) (California, USA). Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between the two experimental groups were performed with the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test, which was selected based on normality that indicated the data did not conform to a normal distribution. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance is indicated in the figures as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Quisinostat amplifies H3K27 acetylation both as a single agent and in combination with 5-FU

Quisinostat (Qui), a potent pan-HDAC inhibitor, is known to enhance histone acetylation and modulate epigenetic regulation in cancer cells. To investigate the epigenetic impact of Qui treatment, H3K27 acetylation (H3K27-Ac) levels were assessed via immunofluorescence staining in HCT-116 and HT-29 colorectal cancer cell lines following treatment with Qui alone (vs. control) and in combination with 5-FU (vs. 5-FU monotherapy).

In HCT-116 cells, H3K27-Ac fluorescence intensity was significantly elevated following Qui treatment compared to the control group (**p < 0.01, Fig. 1B). Conversely, treatment with 5-FU at IC25 resulted in a marked reduction in H3K27-Ac levels (****p < 0.0001), while IC50 treatment led to a slight, non-significant decrease (p = 0.1011). Notably, co-treatment with IC25 + Qui significantly increased H3K27-Ac intensity relative to IC25 alone (***p < 0.001). Similarly, the IC50 + Qui combination significantly enhanced H3K27-Ac levels compared to IC50 alone (****p < 0.0001).

Quisinostat enhances H3K27 acetylation alone and in combination with 5-FU in colorectal cancer cells.(A) Representative immunofluorescence images showing H3K27 acetylation (H3K27-Ac; red) and nuclear staining with DAPI (blue) in HCT-116 and HT-29 cells following treatment with Quisinostat (Qui), 5-FU at IC25 or IC50 doses, or combinations of 5-FU (IC25 or IC50) with Qui. Scale bars = 20 μm. (B–C) Quantification of H3K27-Ac fluorescence intensity in HCT-116 (B) and HT-29 (C) cells. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

In HT-29 cells, Qui treatment also significantly increased H3K27-Ac levels compared to the control (*p < 0.05, Fig. 1 C). While IC25 treatment with 5-FU did not significantly alter H3K27-Ac levels (P = 0.4155), the IC50 concentration led to a substantial reduction (****p < 0.0001). Consistent with the results in HCT-116 cells, the combinations IC25 + Qui and IC50 + Qui significantly elevated H3K27-Ac levels compared to IC25 and IC50 monotherapy, respectively (****p < 0.0001).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Quisinostat robustly increases H3K27-Ac both as a monotherapy and in combination with 5-FU, providing a potential mechanistic basis for the enhanced antitumor effects observed with combination treatment.

Quisinostat augments 5-FU efficacy by increasing cytotoxicity and decreasing cell proliferation

To evaluate the cytotoxic potential of Quisinostat and determine appropriate concentrations for combination treatment, HCT-116 and HT-29 cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of Qui (5–640 nM) for 48 h. Cell viability was measured using the resazurin assay. Qui demonstrated a dose-dependent decrease in cell viability in both cell Lines. The highest concentrations that did not significantly affect viability were determined to be 20 nM ± 3.99 for HCT-116 (Fig. 2 A) and 10 nM ± 5.47 for HT-29 (Fig. 2 C), and these concentrations were used for subsequent combination experiments.

Next, the IC50 values of 5-FU were determined to be 84 ± 6.75 µM for HCT-116 and 237.7 ± 7.6 µM for HT-29 cells. Co-treatment with the highest non-cytotoxic concentrations of Qui significantly enhanced the cytotoxic effect of 5-FU: in HCT-116 cells, the IC50 of 5-FU was reduced to 19.4 ± 1.07 µM (Fig. 2B), and in HT-29 cells, it was lowered to 27.6 ± 1.85 µM (Fig. 2D).

The interaction between Quisinostat and 5-FU was assessed using the Bliss independence model analysis, which revealed a dose-dependent interaction between Quisinostat and 5-FU in HCT116 cells at a fixed concentration of 20 nM Quisinostat. At lower 5-FU concentrations (0.69–11.125 µM), ΔBliss values were consistently negative (–0.130 ± 0.022 at 0.69 µM, − 0.094 ± 0.007 at 1.39 µM, − 0.171 ± 0.003 at 2.78 µM, − 0.134 ± 0.010 at 5.56 µM, and − 0.016 ± 0.023 at 11.125 µM), indicating an antagonistic interaction. In contrast, higher 5-FU concentrations resulted in positive ΔBliss values (0.099 ± 0.024 at 22.25 µM, 0.117 ± 0.025 at 44.5 µM, 0.087 ± 0.024 at 89 µM, and 0.093 ± 0.024 at 178 µM), suggesting a synergistic effect and enhanced therapeutic efficacy when Quisinostat was combined with 5-FU at doses ≥ 22.25 µM.

In HT29 cells, Bliss independence analysis at a fixed Quisinostat concentration of 10 nM demonstrated positive ΔBliss values across all 5-FU concentrations tested (1–729 µM). Mean ΔBliss values were 0.08 ± 0.07 at 1 µM, 0.08 ± 0.07 at 3 µM, 0.14 ± 0.09 at 9 µM, 0.16 ± 0.03 at 27 µM, 0.13 ± 0.03 at 81 µM, 0.12 ± 0.03 at 243 µM, and 0.10 ± 0.03 at 729 µM. These findings indicate a robust synergistic interaction across the full range of 5-FU concentrations tested, highlighting Quisinostat’s capacity to consistently potentiate the chemotherapeutic efficacy of 5-FU in HT29 cells. Combination assays were performed at IC25 and IC50 concentrations of 5-FU, representing biologically relevant dose levels that allow assessment of potential sensitizing effects of Quisinostat without confounding toxicity.

To investigate the impact on proliferation, immunofluorescence staining for Ki-67 was performed (Fig. 2E). In HCT-116 cells, Qui monotherapy significantly reduced the percentage of Ki-67 stained cells from 70% ± 10.89 to 15% ± 6.53 compared to control (***p < 0.001, Fig. 2 F). Combination treatment with IC25 + Qui further reduced Ki-67 stained cells to from 70% ± 5.67 to 30% ± 5.59 (***p = 0.001), and IC50 + Qui decreased from 63% ± 8.07 to 44% ± 9.21 (****p < 0.0001) when compared to their respective monotherapies.

In HT-29 cells, Qui reduced Ki-67 stained cells from 65% ± 10.23 to 26% ± 5.86 compared to control (****p < 0.0001, Fig. 2G). However, IC25 + Qui reduced Ki-67 stained cells from 83% ± 18.52 to 56% ± 12.11 (****p < 0.0001), and IC50 + Qui reduced it from 65% ± 9.09 to 52% ± 6.54 (***p < 0.001), when compared to their respective monotherapies.

These findings highlight the ability of Quisinostat, particularly in combination with 5-FU, to suppress proliferation in CRC cells.

Combination of quisinostat and 5-FU boosts apoptotic response in colorectal cancer

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) serves as a key indicator of mitochondrial health, and its loss is a hallmark of mitochondria-mediated apoptosis. To examine this parameter, a fluorescence-based assay was employed (Fig. 3 A). According to the assay principle, a reduction in red fluorescence (mitochondrial aggregates) and an increase in green fluorescence (cytoplasmic monomers) indicate mitochondrial depolarization.

In HCT-116 cells, Qui treatment alone did not significantly alter ΔΨm compared to the control (2.30 ± 0.56 vs. 2.81 ± 0.54; p = 0.1508; Fig. 3B). However, co-treatment with 5-FU significantly decreased ΔΨm in both IC25 + Qui and IC50 + Qui groups compared to their respective monotherapies (*p < 0.05), indicating enhanced mitochondrial depolarization.

Quisinostat enhances 5-FU-induced mitochondrial membrane depolarization and apoptotic cell death in colorectal cancer cells.(A) Representative confocal images of HCT-116 and HT-29 cells stained with JC-1 after 48 h treatment with Quisinostat (Qui), 5-FU (IC25 or IC50), or their combinations. Red fluorescence indicates polarized (healthy) mitochondria, while green fluorescence reflects depolarized mitochondria associated with apoptosis. Scale bar = 20 μm. (B, C) Quantification of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) disruption in HCT-116 (B) and HT-29 (C) cells using a VarioSkan™ Lux plate reader, indicating significant MMP loss upon combined treatment. (D) Hoechst 33342/PI double staining of HCT-116 and HT-29 cells following 48 h treatment. Light blue nuclei represent viable cells, bright blue nuclei indicate chromatin condensation (apoptosis), and red PI staining identifies apoptotic/necrotic cells. Scale bar = 50 μm. (E, F) Quantitative analysis of apoptotic cell death based on Hoechst 33342/PI staining in HCT-116 (E) and HT-29 (F) cells. ImageJ software was used to calculate the percentage of apoptotic cells. Data represent mean ± SD of three independent experiments. Statistical significance was denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

In HT-29 cells, a similar pattern was observed. Qui alone had no significant effect on ΔΨm (11.18 ± 2.83 vs. 8.74 ± 3.56; p = 0.2286) compared to control group, while combination treatments significantly reduced ΔΨm in both IC25 + Qui (2.81 ± 0.73 vs. 4.93 ± 0.55) and IC50 + Qui groups (3.37 ± 1.16 vs. 7.41 ± 2.1) (*p < 0.05; Fig. 3 C) compared to their respective monotherapies. These findings suggest that Qui enhances 5-FU-induced mitochondrial dysfunction.

To assess apoptotic cell death, Hoechst 33342/PI dual staining was performed (Fig. 3D). PI selectively stains cells with compromised membrane integrity, while Hoechst 33342 highlights chromatin condensation and fragmentation.

In HCT-116 cells (Fig. 3E), Qui monotherapy did not significantly increase apoptosis compared to control (6.1% ± 1.13 vs. 5.4% ± 2.1; p = 0.1510). IC25 also had no significant effect (4.64% ± 2.4; p = 0.1328), but IC50 significantly increased apoptosis to 16% ± 3.35 (****p < 0.0001). Co-treatment with Qui further enhanced apoptosis: IC25 + Qui increased it to 9% ± 4.07 compared to IC25 alone (****p < 0.0001), and IC50 + Qui raised apoptosis to 22% ± 3.6 versus 16% ± 3.35 with IC50 alone (***p < 0.001).

In HT-29 cells (Fig. 3 F), Qui monotherapy did not significantly alter apoptosis (17.2% ± 3.4 vs. 28.7% ± 14.1; P = 0.4651). In contrast, 5-FU at IC25 and IC50 induced apoptosis rates of 30% ± 9.34 and 29% ± 5.8, respectively (**p < 0.01). Co-treatment with Qui significantly amplified apoptosis: IC25 + Qui reached 47% ± 11.25 (**p < 0.01), and IC50 + Qui increased it to 43% ± 7.99 compared to their respective monotherapies (***p < 0.001).

These results indicate that while Qui alone does not significantly induce apoptosis, its combination with 5-FU markedly enhances the apoptotic response in CRC cells, likely via mitochondrial depolarization.

Combination of quisinostat and 5-FU inhibits cell migration and reverses EMT

To assess the impact of Quisinostat (Qui), 5-FU, and their combination on cell migration, wound healing assays were performed on CRC cell lines (Figs. 4 A and 4 C).

Migration was assessed by measuring wound closure at 0 h and 48 h. Scale bars = 200 μm. (B, D) Quantitative analysis of migration rates in HCT-116 (B) and HT-29 (D) cells. (E) Immunofluorescence staining of epithelial marker E-cadherin (green) and mesenchymal marker N-cadherin (red) in HCT-116 and HT-29 cells following drug treatments. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (blue). Scale bars = 20 μm. (F, G) Quantification of E-cadherin and N-cadherin fluorescence intensity in HCT-116 (F) and HT-29 (G) cells. Data represent mean ± SD of at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance was denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

In HCT-116 cells (Fig. 4B), Qui monotherapy significantly reduced wound closure to 36.1% ± 6.59, compared to 45% ± 6.61 in the control group (*p < 0.05), indicating an anti-migratory effect. Combination treatments further enhanced this inhibition: migration was reduced from 43.2% ± 4.14 (IC25) to 35.2% ± 6.06 (IC25 + Qui) (**p < 0.01), and from 43% ± 8.28 (IC50) to 28.5% ± 5.03 (IC50 + Qui) (***p < 0.001).

In HT-29 cells (Fig. 4D), Qui monotherapy reduced wound closure to 22% ± 3.85, compared to 28% ± 7.12 in the control group, although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.4206). However, combination treatments significantly suppressed migration compared to 5-FU alone: IC25 + Qui reduced the rate from 29.2% ± 5.75 to 19.1% ± 3.86 (**p < 0.01), and IC50 + Qui reduced it from 22.7% ± 4.02 to 12.6% ± 3.19 (**p < 0.01).

These results suggest that Quisinostat, particularly in combination with 5-FU, significantly impairs the migratory capacity of CRC cells, supporting its potential anti-metastatic activity.

To evaluate the effects of treatment on EMT, immunofluorescence staining was used to assess the expression of E-cadherin (epithelial marker) and N-cadherin (mesenchymal marker) (Fig. 4E), and fluorescence intensities were quantitatively analyzed (Figs. 4 F–G).

In HCT-116 cells, Quisinostat monotherapy significantly increased E-cadherin fluorescence intensity from 5.39 ± 0.50 (control) to 12.04 ± 1.04 (***p < 0.001, Fig. 4 F). This effect was enhanced in combination treatments: IC25 + Qui increased intensity to 8.27 ± 0.60 (****p < 0.0001) versus IC25 alone (5.44 ± 0.53), and IC50 + Qui increased it to 4.23 ± 0.52 (**p < 0.01) versus IC50 alone (3.41 ± 0.97).

Quisinostat alone also increased N-cadherin intensity to 11.82 ± 0.85 compared to 7.78 ± 0.64 in the control group (***p < 0.001). However, when combined with 5-FU, Qui markedly reduced N-cadherin expression. IC25 + Qui decreased fluorescence to 8.09 ± 1.62 from 10.36 ± 1.13 (IC25 alone; **p < 0.01), and IC50 + Qui reduced it to 8.90 ± 0.84 from 12.17 ± 2.00 (IC50 alone; ****p < 0.0001).

In HT-29 cells (Fig. 4G), Quisinostat significantly increased E-cadherin intensity to 13.99 ± 2.70 compared to 6.56 ± 0.95 in the control group (****p < 0.0001). Combination treatment further enhanced this effect: IC25 + Qui yielded 10.09 ± 1.03 vs. 6.89 ± 0.96 in IC25 alone (****p < 0.0001), and IC50 + Qui yielded 13.77 ± 2.10 vs. 6.17 ± 1.25 in IC50 alone (****p < 0.0001).

Contrary to HCT-116 results, Quisinostat monotherapy in HT-29 cells significantly reduced N-cadherin fluorescence intensity to 11.69 ± 0.44 from 15.06 ± 2.85 in the control group (*p < 0.05). This reduction was more pronounced in combination groups: IC25 + Qui lowered it to 7.52 ± 0.64 from 12.28 ± 2.03 (****p < 0.0001), and IC50 + Qui reduced it to 9.97 ± 1.79 from 13.70 ± 1.27 (****p < 0.0001).

Collectively, these results indicate that Quisinostat promotes epithelial characteristics (increased E-cadherin) and suppresses mesenchymal traits (reduced N-cadherin), suggesting a reversal of the EMT phenotype. This may underlie the compound’s observed anti-migratory and anti-metastatic effects in CRC cells.

Discussion

Histone acetylation, regulated by histone deacetylases (HDACs) and histone acetyltransferases (HATs), plays a critical role in controlling chromatin structure, gene transcription, cell survival, and apoptosis. Aberrant HDAC activity is frequently observed in various cancers, including CRC, making HDACs attractive targets for anticancer therapy [23]. HDAC inhibitors (HDACi) modulate the acetylation landscape, leading to the suppression of tumor cell proliferation, induction of apoptosis, and inhibition of metastasis. Several HDAC inhibitors, such as vorinostat, romidepsin, belinostat, and panobinostat, have been FDA-approved for the treatment of hematologic malignancies, particularly cutaneous T-cell lymphoma [24, 25]. However, the limited clinical success of HDACi in solid tumors has prompted research into combination therapies to enhance therapeutic responses.

Several studies have reported that class I HDAC isoforms (HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3) are significantly upregulated in colorectal tumors compared to normal tissue, and their elevated expression correlates with increased tumor cell proliferation and dedifferentiation [26–28]. These isoforms play key roles in tumorigenesis, metastasis, and patient prognosis, underscoring their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in CRC.

To address the potential overlap with prior studies combining HDAC inhibitors, we highlight the distinct advantages of quisinostat in this context. Unlike first-generation pan-HDAC inhibitors such as vorinostat, which exhibits broader and less selective inhibition across HDAC isoforms [29], quisinostat is a second-generation HDAC inhibitor with superior potency and a unique selectivity profile, targeting HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC4, HDAC10, and HDAC11 with > 30-fold selectivity over other isoforms like HDAC3, HDAC5, HDAC8, and HDAC9 [12, 13, 30]. Compared to CUDC-907, a dual HDAC/PI3K inhibitor [31], quisinostat’s focused HDAC inhibition avoids off-target PI3K pathway modulation, potentially reducing unintended effects. Mechanistically, quisinostat induces of G0/G1 cell cycle arrest through the PI3K/AKT/p21 pathway and apoptosis via the JNK/c-Jun/caspase-3/PARP pathway [32]. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests quisinostat’s ability to induce autophagy, which may provide a complementary mechanism to enhance 5-FU response in CRC, as well as resistance, distinguishing it from vorinostat’s primarily apoptosis-driven effects [32–34]. Given the recognized importance of HDAC inhibition in CRC, these unique properties position Quisinostat as a promising candidate for optimizing HDAC inhibitor–5-FU combinations in CRC treatment by enhancing 5-FU responses.

In this study, we demonstrate that Quisinostat (Qui), a potent pan-HDAC inhibitor, exerts pronounced epigenetic and anticancer effects in CRC cell lines HCT-116 and HT-29, both as a single agent and in combination with 5-FU. By targeting multiple cancer hallmarks, including epigenetic dysregulation, uncontrolled proliferation, apoptotic resistance, and metastasis, Qui shows considerable promise as a combinatorial therapeutic agent in CRC.

Our results reveal that Quisinostat significantly increases H3K27-Ac levels, aligning with its activity against class I and II HDACs. This effect was further amplified when Qui was combined with 5-FU, suggesting that HDAC inhibition enhances chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activation, potentially sensitizing cells to chemotherapy. Restoration of H3K27-Ac levels following 5-FU-induced reduction—particularly at sub-IC50 concentrations—points to a role for epigenetic reprogramming in therapeutic synergy.

Quisinostat also enhanced the cytotoxicity of 5-FU, as evidenced by a substantial reduction in IC50 values in both cell Lines. This aligns with prior studies showing that HDACi sensitize tumor cells to DNA-damaging agents by altering chromatin structure and inhibiting DNA repair. Consistent with these observations, Bliss independence analysis further demonstrated that Quisinostat modulates 5-FU responses in a cell line– and dose-dependent manner, displaying antagonism at lower but synergism at higher concentrations in HCT116 cells, while exerting a robust synergistic effect across all tested doses in HT29 cells. Additionally, Ki-67 proliferation assays showed that Quisinostat alone significantly reduced cell proliferation, and that its combination with 5-FU exerted a stronger anti-proliferative effect than either agent alone. Interestingly, in HT29 cells we observed a paradoxical increase in Ki-67 positivity at low-dose 5-FU. Such paradoxical proliferative response has also been reported in previous study, suggest that 5-FU may not fully suppress proliferation but instead promote the expansion of resistant subpopulations or cells with stem-like features. Indeed, prolonged 5-FU treatment has been shown to upregulate stemness-associated genes (KLF4, SOX2, OCT4, C-MYC, CD44, CD133) in HT29 cells, thereby enhancing survival and proliferative potential [35]. Consistent with this, flow cytometry analyses in the same study confirmed a modest increase in proliferation under 5-FU conditions. In our experiments, this paradoxical effect in Ki-67 positivity was effectively suppressed when Quisinostat was combined with 5-FU, indicating that epigenetic modulation may help counteract adaptive resistance mechanisms.

Previous studies corroborate our findings, demonstrating that Quisinostat reduces cell viability and proliferation in diverse tumor models, including non-small cell lung cancer, tongue squamous cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, urothelial carcinoma, glioblastoma, and melanoma [32, 36–41].

In combination settings, Quisinostat has shown synergistic effects with various targeted therapies. For example, in hepatocellular carcinoma, co-treatment with sorafenib led to enhanced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [32]. In urothelial carcinoma, combinations with cisplatin or talazoparib produced synergistic reductions in viability and allowed for dose minimization [40]. In melanoma, combination with the CDK inhibitor flavopiridol enhanced cytotoxicity in resistant tumors [41]. These findings highlight Quisinostat’s potential to improve therapeutic response by modulating key cellular pathways.

Apoptosis, a regulated form of cell death, is often impaired in cancer, allowing for unchecked tumor growth [42]. In our combination studies, Quisinostat alone did not significantly induce apoptosis or mitochondrial depolarization, which is consistent with the viability assay results. This is Likely attributable to the use of a non-cytotoxic concentration of Quisinostat in the combination experiments. However, when combined with 5-FU, a significant increase in apoptotic cell death and a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) were observed, suggesting that Quisinostat enhances 5-FU-induced mitochondrial apoptosis. Hoechst 33,342/PI dual staining confirmed this result, indicating increased late-stage apoptosis and necrosis upon co-treatment.

Considering the limitation of Hoechst 33342/PI staining in distinguishing apoptosis from necrosis, in our study this limitation was addressed by performing mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) analysis. Loss of ΔΨm is recognized as an early and critical indicator of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, as it reflects increased mitochondrial outer membrane permeability, disruption of the proton gradient, and subsequent release of cytochrome c and activation of caspases, ultimately driving the cell into programmed death [43]. In our experiments, the significant reduction of ΔΨm observed with the Quisinostat and 5-FU combination supports the notion that this treatment induces apoptosis via the mitochondrial pathway and reinforces the apoptotic process. Consistent with our findings, Bao et al. showed that Quisinostat induces mitochondrial depolarization and upregulates pro-apoptotic proteins in lung cancer cells [36]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, Quisinostat activated mitochondrial apoptosis via caspase activation and downregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 and Mcl-1 [44]. Additionally, Quisinostat has been shown to increase ROS production and activate p53 signaling, leading to apoptosis via upregulation of p21 and caspase-3 [36, 37, 44]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, it activates the JNK/c-Jun/caspase-3 axis to induce apoptosis [32]. In urothelial carcinoma and synovial sarcoma, Quisinostat in combination with cisplatin or proteasome inhibitors led to enhanced apoptosis, particularly in resistant cells, by activating intrinsic pathways and pro-apoptotic factors [40, 45]. Our findings further support the role of Quisinostat as an apoptosis sensitizer when combined with conventional chemotherapies.

Cell migration plays a critical role in tumor metastasis and therapy resistance. Wound Healing assays in our study revealed that Quisinostat, particularly in combination with 5-FU, significantly inhibited migration in CRC cells. Similar anti-migratory effects of Quisinostat have been reported in tongue squamous cell carcinoma [37]. The wound-healing assay used in this study provides valuable insight into the migratory capacity of CRC cells; however, the contribution of reduced proliferation cannot be fully excluded. Since Quisinostat exerts a pronounced anti-proliferative effect, part of the reduced wound closure observed may also be attributable to its growth-inhibitory properties in addition to migration suppression. More refined approaches, such as transwell migration under proliferation-controlled conditions, would therefore be helpful in providing more specific information regarding cell motility.

Metastasis is accompanied by EMT, a process that endows epithelial cancer cells with mesenchymal traits, including increased motility and invasiveness [46]. EMT is marked by downregulation of E-cadherin and upregulation of N-cadherin and vimentin and is often driven by epigenetic reprogramming [47]. HDAC inhibitors are known to reverse EMT by modulating these markers in various cancers [48–51].

Although studies on Quisinostat’s role in EMT regulation are limited, evidence suggests it can revert EMT and restore drug sensitivity in resistant lung cancer models. Quisinostat pretreatment re-sensitized crizotinib-resistant ALK-rearranged lung cancer cells to new-generation ALK inhibitors by reversing EMT [52]. Similarly, it restored EGFR inhibitor sensitivity by increasing E-cadherin and decreasing ZEB1 expression [53].

In our study, Quisinostat treatment induced a phenotypic shift toward epithelial characteristics in CRC cells, marked by increased E-cadherin and decreased N-cadherin expression. These effects were most pronounced in the combination groups, suggesting that Quisinostat can counteract 5-FU-induced or intrinsic EMT. Interestingly, Quisinostat alone increased N-cadherin in HCT-116 cells, but this was reversed in the presence of 5-FU, indicating a context-dependent modulation of EMT pathways.

The observed increase in H3K27-Ac may provide a mechanistic basis for the enhanced apoptosis and EMT reversal identified in our study. H3K27-Ac is a well-established marker of active enhancers and promoters that facilitates chromatin relaxation and transcriptional activation of tumor-suppressive and pro-apoptotic genes, including regulators of apoptosis (e.g., caspases, BCL-2 family) and EMT (e.g., E-cadherin) programs [54, 55]. Although these results remain correlative, they support the notion that Quisinostat-induced acetylation may restore epithelial identity and sensitize cells to apoptosis. Building on this mechanistic insight, our study provides a proof-of-concept framework demonstrating that Quisinostat can modulate 5-FU response at both the cellular and epigenetic levels, as reflected by alterations in H3K27-Ac, proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and EMT readouts.

While these findings strengthen the translational relevance of Quisinostat as a potential therapeutic partner for 5-FU, the mechanistic depth of the present work remains somewhat constrained. To address this caveat, we have acknowledged this limitation and emphasized that future studies should incorporate advanced mechanistic approaches, including molecular pathway validation, quantitative apoptosis and EMT assays, and omics-based profiling. Integration of these strategies will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the downstream pathways involved and help translate the proof-of-concept findings reported here into robust preclinical evidence, ideally through patient-derived organoid models or animal studies.

Conclusion

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that Quisinostat enhances the therapeutic efficacy of 5-FU in CRC cells by modulating epigenetic marks, sensitizing cells to apoptosis, suppressing proliferation, and reversing EMT-associated migratory phenotypes. These results support the potential of Quisinostat as a multifaceted anticancer agent, particularly in combination regimens aimed at improving therapeutic response and clinical outcomes in CRC.
