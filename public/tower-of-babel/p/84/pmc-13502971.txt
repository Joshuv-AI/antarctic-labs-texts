Human Development as a Structural Determinant of WHO FCTC Implementation: A Cross-national Ecological Study
Authors: Rohini Ruhil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The World Health Organization Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC) serves as a critical instrument for regulating tobacco use globally. However, the effectiveness of its implementation varies considerably across nations, and understanding this variance is essential for public health progress. The Objective of the study was to identify an association between the “Human Development Index (HDI) Score 2020” and “WHO FCTC Implementation Score 2020” of 167 countries. This study utilized an ecological study design. Aggregated data related to “HDI Score 2020” was taken from the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) Human Development Report 2020, and the aggregate data related to “WHO FCTC Implementation Score 2020” was taken from “The Global Tobacco Control Progress Hub.” It was analyzed using a simple linear regression model and a fitted line plot to visualize the data. A significant positive correlation was observed (Spearman’s ρ = 0.37, P < 0.001). Kruskal–Wallis testing confirmed significant differences across HDI groups (H = 28.15, P < 0.0001). In adjusted regression analysis controlling for WHO region, FCTC implementation remained significantly associated with HDI (β = 95.12, 95% CI: 62.40–127.84, P < 0.001; Adjusted R² = 0.35). Consequently, countries that scored higher on the Human Development Index (HDI) typically also demonstrated better implementation of the WHO FCTC guidelines. To conclude, nations with greater levels of human development were generally more successful in enforcing tobacco control measures. This research thereby advocates for a multidisciplinary approach to tobacco control that considers the interconnected facets of human development.

INTRODUCTION

The GBD 2019 study by He et al.[1] presented comprehensive estimates of the health effects of tobacco across global, regional, and national levels. In 2019 alone, over 8.7 million deaths and 230 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) were attributed to tobacco use. Most of these burdens were linked to cardiovascular diseases, chronic respiratory conditions, and various cancers.[1] The World Health Organization Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC) is a globally acknowledged treaty designed to counter the health risks associated with tobacco consumption.[2] As the first global health treaty negotiated under the auspices of the WHO, the FCTC has given a new legal dimension to international health cooperation. The WHO FCTC came out as a multilateral treaty ratified by 183 national parties, which legally binds its member nations to exert tobacco control measures.[2] Global statistics reveal both persistent challenges and some signs of progress. According to the World Health Organization, overall global tobacco use has been declining in adults since 2000, with further reductions projected by 2030.[3] However, disparities persist by region and demographic. The effectiveness of policy implementation in a country is often intricately tied to its development status, as measured by indicators such as the Human Development Index (HDI). The Human Development Index (HDI), is an annual statistical measure that gauges a nation’s status based on life expectancy, educational attainment, and per capita income.[4] Thus, this study aims to identify an association between the “HDI Score 2020” and the “WHO FCTC implementation Score 2020” of 167 countries.

This study adopts a conceptual framing inspired by Amartya Sen’s capability approach, as articulated in Development as Freedom, wherein development is understood as the expansion of substantive human freedoms. Within this framework, freedom from tobacco use and exposure can be interpreted as both a health outcome and a capability, as tobacco dependence constrains individual agency, imposes health risks, and limits socioeconomic opportunities.[5]

This study utilized an ecological design to identify an association between the “HDI Score 2020” and the “WHO FCTC Implementation Score 2020” of 167 countries.

Sampling method

The dataset uses census sampling (complete enumeration) rather than probabilistic or non-probabilistic sampling. It includes 167 countries, which represent nearly the full set of sovereign states for which both the Human Development Index (HDI) 2020 and WHO FCTC implementation scores are available.

Adjusted Model (WITH Region Controls)

In the adjusted model, FCTC implementation remained positively associated with HDI (β = 95.12, 95% CI: 62.40–127.84, P < 0.001). The model explained a substantial proportion of variance (R² = 0.38; Adjusted R² = 0.35).

The inclusion of regional fixed effects substantially improved model fit (Adjusted R² = 0.35), indicating that geopolitical and institutional contexts play a major role in shaping development outcomes.

Therefore, the nations with higher HDI scores generally had better implementation scores of FCTC guidelines. In other words, the countries with higher human development were more likely to implement tobacco control measures effectively. Given the cross-sectional ecological design, causal inference cannot be established.

DISCUSSION

Thus, this is the first ecological research to study the correlation between the HDI Score and the WHO FCTC Implementation Score. Most published research on the WHO FCTC describes the level of compliance or adherence to the FCTC guidelines.[78910] In Europe, TCS monitoring shows that countries advancing comprehensive packages score higher and sustain gains, paralleling our finding that implementation strength is higher for very high and high HDI groups.[11]

While the study indicates a positive correlation between the HDI Score and the WHO FCTC Implementation Score, theoretical frameworks provide various explanations for this relationship. Countries with higher HDI scores have better resources[11] that they can allocate toward public health measures, including tobacco control. Further, nations with higher HDI scores may have a greater political will and a more robust policy diffusion mechanism, thereby adopting and implementing international treaties more effectively.[12] Moreover, in countries with higher HDI scores, the administrative machinery is often more robust, benefiting from systems that are transparent, accountable, and efficient.[13]

Conversely, successful tobacco control implementation itself improves health outcomes and thereby boosts HDI. Improved tobacco control may itself enhance HDI through reduced mortality, increased productivity, and improved household welfare, indicating potential bidirectional causality. Beyond direct health effects, tobacco control may also influence the education and income dimensions of HDI. Reduced household expenditure on tobacco can improve financial stability and enable greater investment in nutrition and education, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.[14] At the macroeconomic level, tobacco-related productivity losses—arising from morbidity and premature mortality—are substantial, and their reduction may contribute to improved economic performance and national income indicators.[15]

This topic necessitates further research to understand the intricacies of the correlation, especially across different cultural and economic contexts. Such research could contribute to the discourse on global public health governance and tobacco control.

This research has certain limitations. Ecological fallacy occurs when conclusions about individual cases are improperly drawn from group-level data. Findings should not be interpreted at the individual level; high HDI countries do not imply that individuals with higher socioeconomic status are driving FCTC implementation. Policy adoption occurs at institutional and political levels. Additionally, the study may contain confounding bias that was not accounted for in its design. Residual confounding may arise from governance quality, tobacco industry interference, and civil society capacity. These factors are likely to bias estimates upward, as countries with stronger governance may both implement FCTC more effectively and achieve higher HDI.

The strengths of the study is that dataset includes 167 countries, spanning all WHO regions and a wide spectrum of socioeconomic and development contexts. This near-global coverage minimizes sampling bias and enhances the external validity of the findings. Also, by linking development status (HDI) to tobacco control implementation, the study identifies structural determinants of policy strength, thus expanding beyond purely behavioral approaches.

Data availability statement

Data for this study were obtained from publicly available sources, including the United Nations Development Programme Human Development Report 2020 dataset and the Global Tobacco Control Progress Hub (Tableau Public dashboard; accessed 18 March 2026). As the Tableau dashboard is dynamically updated and does not provide a fixed downloadable dataset, the authors extracted, cleaned, and harmonized a static analytical dataset at the time of access.

The compiled dataset, along with details of data processing and variable construction, is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, HDI is a significant predictor of FCTC implementation strength, though additional factors (regional, political, economic, etc.) also influence variation. Freedom from tobacco is inextricably linked with broader developmental objectives. Thus, policymakers, researchers, and civil society should adopt a more nuanced, interdisciplinary approach to tobacco control.

Ethical considerations

The study had no direct involvement with human subjects. The study is a secondary analysis of data available in the public domain. All information sources that helped complete this study have been acknowledged rightly in the references.

What this study adds

The majority of existing research on the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) focuses on the degree of compliance or adherence to its guidelines. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this research is the first ecological study to explore the relationship between Human Development Index (HDI) scores and WHO FCTC Implementation Scores at a Global level.
