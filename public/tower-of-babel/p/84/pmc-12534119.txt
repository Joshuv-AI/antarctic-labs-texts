Marjolin ulcer in a 63-year-old with hidradenitis suppurativa
Authors: Meghan Kanou, Ahmad Alamari, Ivan V Litvinov
Published: 2025

A 63-year-old woman with a 20-year history of hidradenitis suppurativa, classified as Hurley Stage III (a disease-specific staging system); a 12 pack-year smoking history; pyoderma gangrenosum; and diabetes presented with worsening pain and unrelated lower gastrointestinal bleeding. She had previously been treated with ustekinumab, secukinumab, colchicine, and multiple systemic and topical antibiotic regimens. Examination showed extensive scarring, irregular ulcerations, and friable verrucous nodules on the buttocks (Figure 1). These features raised concern for invasive cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, specifically Marjolin ulcer, and 5 biopsies taken from lesion edges confirmed this diagnosis (p16, the human papillomavirus surrogate marker, was negative). She was then treated with cemiplimab and radiotherapy, which were discontinued owing to severe drug eruption and lack of clinical response, respectively. The patient was referred to palliative care.

Extensive scarring and sinus tracts of the buttocks of a 63-year-old woman with advanced hidradenitis suppurativa (Hurley Stage III: extensive disease with widespread involvement, scarring, and interconnected sinus tracts). In addition, irregular, jagged ulcerations (black arrows) and friable verrucous nodules (white arrows) were present. These morphologic features, particularly the irregular borders, spontaneous bleeding, and friability with easy bleeding, raised concern for malignant transformation to Marjolin ulcer, an aggressive subtype of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma with distinct pathogenesis and prognosis.

Hidradenitis suppurativa is a chronic, relapsing disease characterized by painful nodules, abscesses, sinus tracts, and scarring. Lesions most often occur in intertriginous areas such as the axillae, groin, and buttocks, and may drain purulent material. Marjolin ulcer is a rare but aggressive transformation of chronic inflammatory or scarred skin, seen in about 4.6% of people with long-standing hidradenitis suppurativa, and must be distinguished from other cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas.1 It represents a different and aggressive subtype with distinct pathogenesis and a worse prognosis than ultraviolet radiation–driven cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma. Fabbrocini and colleagues describe the immunocompromised cutaneous district as chronic inflammation, lymphatic stasis, and immune dysfunction that foster malignancy in damaged skin.2 In hidradenitis suppurativa, impaired lymphatic drainage and repeated injury create a tumour-promoting environment.

Marjolin ulcer can also arise in burns, venous ulcers, or chronic wounds,2 and signs include persistent ulcers with jagged or friable borders, spontaneous bleeding, and verrucous nodules. Given the high mortality rate associated with Marjolin ulcer (21% to 60%),2 when suspicious lesions are noted, taking a biopsy and conducting a regional lymph node examination is critical. Early diagnosis is key to successful management.
