Efficacy and Safety of Variable-Dose Versus Continuous-Dose Abrocitinib Treatment in Patients with Moderate-to-Severe Atopic Dermatitis: A Pooled Analysis
Authors: Jonathan I Silverberg, Eric L Simpson, Saleem A Farooqui, Gary Chan, Pinaki Biswas, Erman Güler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSigns and symptoms of atopic dermatitis (AD) can fluctuate over time, necessitating a flexible dosing treatment approach for long-term management. This study evaluated the long-term efficacy and safety of variable-dose abrocitinib treatment in patients with moderate-to-severe AD compared with continuous abrocitinib 200-mg dosing.MethodsPatients from phase 3 trials in the JADE clinical program were divided into a continuous abrocitinib 200-mg dose cohort or a variable-dose cohort that received abrocitinib 100 mg and 200 mg at different phases of the trial(s).ResultsAt week 48 of abrocitinib treatment, 82% and 69% attained 75% improvement from baseline in the Eczema Area and Severity Index in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg (n = 941) and variable-dose (n = 90) cohorts, respectively, while 67% and 44% achieved a ≥ 4-point improvement from baseline in the Peak Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (PP-NRS4) response. In the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg and variable-dose cohorts, respectively, 23% and 13% achieved complete skin clearance per the Investigator’s Global Assessment score of 0, and 44% and 21% achieved a PP-NRS of 0/1 (profound itch control). Similar trends were observed for patient-reported outcomes. Incidence rates for serious adverse events—those leading to discontinuation, deaths, and serious infections—were numerically higher in the variable-dose cohort but numerically lower for malignancies (excluding nonmelanoma skin cancer, major adverse cardiovascular events, venous thromboembolism, thrombocytopenia, lymphopenia, and retinal detachment compared with the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort.ConclusionThus, sustained long-term efficacy was observed with both continuous abrocitinib 200 mg and variable-dose abrocitinib, with no new safety signals. Introduction

The signs and symptoms of atopic dermatitis (AD)—including itch, skin lesions, and pain—are dynamic, with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations that may occur independently of each other and vary over the course of the disease [1–4]. In clinical practice, standard-of-care treatment in most patients with moderate or severe AD failed to maintain persistent lesional clearance; among these patients, in general, skin pain improvements were sustained while itch and severity of excoriations fluctuated over time [1, 2, 5–7]. This heterogeneous longitudinal nature of AD often requires individualized and flexible disease management [2, 3].

The goals of AD therapy include a reduction in the signs and symptoms of AD—such as itch, skin lesions, and sleep difficulties—and the establishment of long-term disease control to improve a patient’s quality of life (QoL) [3, 8]. Flares (defined as periods of acute worsening of disease) are common in AD and can sometimes be predictable, such as with changes of seasons or a patient’s lapse in skin care; however, they often cannot be predicted or attributed to a specific exposure [9]. AD management plans should be patient-centered and minimize environmental risk factors that are likely to cause flares [3, 10]. Additionally, a personalized treatment plan that balances the individual’s risk–benefit profile and uses the lowest effective dose to achieve disease control is required [11–14].

Abrocitinib is an oral, once-daily, Janus kinase 1-selective inhibitor approved for patients with moderate-to-severe AD at doses of 200 mg and 100 mg [11–15]. In clinical practice, variable-dose treatment with abrocitinib is available. In Canada, the USA, the UK, and Europe, local product information states that the abrocitinib dose may be decreased or increased based on efficacy and tolerability and depending on individual patient characteristics [11–14]. In the USA, the UK, and Europe, guidelines recommend the lowest effective dose of abrocitinib to maintain control of symptoms, the severity of which may vary over time [11, 13, 14]. As treatment with abrocitinib 200 mg has been associated with higher rates of adverse events (AEs) and an increased risk of thrombocytopenia, lymphopenia, and hyperlipidemia compared with the lower 100-mg dose [16–18], the product labels in Canada, the USA, the UK, and Europe recommend a dose reduction to abrocitinib 100 mg in patients who achieve symptom control with the 200-mg dose [11–14]. A 50-mg abrocitinib dose is also available. Although the efficacy and safety of this dose was not formally assessed in clinical trials, it is intended for exposure adjustment in selected populations, including in patients with renal impairment or those with concomitant use of medications that interact with abrocitinib; therefore, its efficacy and safety are expected to be similar to those of higher doses [11–14].

The efficacy of variable-dose abrocitinib up to 1 year of treatment was assessed in the phase 3 JADE REGIMEN trial (NCT03627767). Patients enrolled in JADE REGIMEN who responded to open-label abrocitinib 200 mg for 12 weeks were randomly allocated to either abrocitinib (100 or 200 mg) or placebo during a maintenance period of up to 40 weeks. Patients who experienced a protocol-defined flare during maintenance received rescue therapy with abrocitinib 200 mg and topical corticosteroids (TCS) for 12 weeks. The results of JADE REGIMEN showed that abrocitinib 100 mg was efficacious in maintaining clinical response in patients who initially attained response with abrocitinib 200 mg; in addition, abrocitinib 200 mg was efficacious in recapturing response in patients who experienced disease flare with abrocitinib 100 mg [19]. JADE EXTEND (NCT03422822) was a phase 3, open-label extension trial (ongoing at the time of this analysis, completed December 2025) that enrolled eligible patients from qualifying trials in the JADE clinical program (including JADE REGIMEN) and in which patients receive abrocitinib 200 mg or 100 mg, with or without TCS [20]. Patients who received abrocitinib 200 mg in the open-label phase of REGIMEN, followed by abrocitinib 100 mg in the maintenance phase and abrocitinib 200 mg if they experienced a flare, were assigned to receive abrocitinib 100 mg upon entry into JADE EXTEND (i.e., treatment corresponding with the dose allocated in the randomized maintenance period of JADE REGIMEN). This group of patients (n = 90) was exposed to different doses of abrocitinib over variable time frames and constituted a long-term variable-dose population.

This post hoc analysis aimed to evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of variable-dose abrocitinib in patients with moderate-to-severe AD using data from JADE REGIMEN and an interim analysis of data from JADE EXTEND (as of September 25, 2021, data cutoff date) compared with patients who received continuous abrocitinib 200 mg treatment.

Methods

Patients

This analysis includes adolescent (aged 12 to < 18 years) and adult (aged > 18 years) patients who enrolled into qualifying trials in the JADE clinical program and met the individual trial inclusion/exclusion criteria, prior to entering the long-term extension trial—JADE EXTEND. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria for each trial were published previously [16, 17, 19–23].

Two patient populations were analyzed. The variable-dose cohort included patients from JADE REGIMEN who (1) met protocol-defined response criteria during the 12-week abrocitinib 200 mg induction phase, (2) were randomly allocated to receive abrocitinib 100 mg for up to 40 weeks in the randomized maintenance phase, (3) experienced a flare and received rescue treatment with abrocitinib 200 mg plus TCS for 12 weeks, and (4) subsequently entered JADE EXTEND, in which they were allocated to receive abrocitinib 100 mg with or without TCS (Fig. 1). The continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort included patients who were randomly allocated to receive placebo or abrocitinib 200 mg, as monotherapy or in combination with medicated topical therapy, in qualifying trials JADE MONO-1 (NCT03349060) [16], JADE MONO-2 (NCT03575871) [17], JADE COMPARE (NCT03720470) [21], JADE TEEN (NCT03796676) [22], or JADE DARE (NCT04345367) [23] and subsequently entered JADE EXTEND, in which they were allocated to receive abrocitinib 200 mg with or without TCS. Patients who were randomly allocated to the placebo cohort in the qualifying trials received their first abrocitinib dose in JADE EXTEND. Within the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort, for patients who were randomly assigned to abrocitinib 200 mg in the qualifying trials and continued to receive the same dose in JADE EXTEND, day 1 of treatment was defined as the first dose of abrocitinib received by the patient in the relevant qualifying study; for those who received placebo in the qualifying trials and were randomly allocated to receive abrocitinib 200 mg in JADE EXTEND, day 1 was defined as day 1 of abrocitinib treatment in JADE EXTEND. For patients in the variable-dose cohort, day 1 was also defined as day 1 of abrocitinib treatment in JADE EXTEND. For all patients, the baseline value was the last pre-dose observation taken from the qualifying trial.

Assessments

For the variable-dose cohort, efficacy responses were evaluated over the first 48 weeks of abrocitinib 100 mg treatment in JADE EXTEND, and safety was evaluated from the first exposure to abrocitinib in JADE REGIMEN (i.e., the first 200-mg dose in the induction period) through week 48 of JADE EXTEND (Fig. S1). For the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort, efficacy responses and safety were evaluated over the first 48 weeks of abrocitinib 200 mg treatment from day 1.

The proportions of patients who achieved the following response criteria were evaluated: ≥ 50, ≥ 75, ≥ 90%, and 100% improvement from baseline in the Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI-50, EASI-75, EASI-90, and EASI-100, respectively); a score of 0 on the Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA 0); a ≥ 4-point improvement from baseline in the Peak Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (PP-NRS4; PP-NRS used with permission from Regeneron Pharmaceuticals Inc., and Sanofi); a PP-NRS score of < 2 (PP-NRS 0/1 [profound itch control]); a score of < 2 on the Dermatology Life Quality Index or Children’s Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI 0/1 or CDLQI 0/1; indicating no effect of AD on a patient’s QoL); a ≥ 4-point improvement from baseline in the DLQI/CDLQI; least-squares mean (LSM) absolute values for DLQI/CDLQI; a Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure (POEM) score of 0 to 2; a > 4-point improvement from baseline in POEM; and LSM absolute values for POEM.

Safety data were reported as incidence rates (IRs) of serious AEs (SAEs), severe AEs, AEs leading to discontinuation, and AEs of special interest. AEs of special interest included serious infections, malignancies (nonmelanoma skin cancer [NMSC] or malignancies excluding NMSC), cardiovascular events including major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and venous thromboembolism (VTE), deaths, and changes in laboratory measures. Malignancies, cardiovascular events, and opportunistic infections were adjudicated by independent committees based on prespecified criteria.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. All endpoints were analyzed based on all observed data without any imputation for missing values. The estimates and confidence intervals (CIs) for differences were calculated based on the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel weighted average difference for each stratification factor (study, baseline disease severity, and age category [adults or adolescents]) using the normal approximation of binomial proportions. LSM absolute values for DLQI or CDLQI and POEM at each time point were estimated using mixed models for repeated measures containing fixed factors of treatment, week, treatment-by-week interaction, study, baseline disease severity, age category (adults or adolescents), and unstructured covariance matrix or compound symmetry covariance matrix. IRs (number of unique patients with events per 100 patient-years [PY]) and 95% CIs were calculated for SAEs, AEs leading to discontinuation, and AEs of special interest. Exposure time was defined as the duration from the first abrocitinib dose up to the day of the first event for patients who experienced events or up to the end of the risk period for patients who did not experience events. The risk period was defined as the time from the first abrocitinib dose to one of the following: last dose plus 28 days, death, or data cutoff date (for JADE EXTEND only), whichever occurred first. Exact 95% CIs were calculated for IRs based on the assumption that the actual count followed a Poisson distribution.

Ethics Statement

The protocols were reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards or ethics committees of the participating institutions. The studies were conducted in compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments and all International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All local regulatory requirements were followed. Informed consent was obtained from all participants of the individual studies included in this analysis.

Results

Of these, 264 patients (28.1%) in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort and 26 patients (28.9%) in the variable-dose cohort discontinued for various reasons but were included in the analysis, as they received ≥ 1 dose of abrocitinib in JADE EXTEND. Patient disposition is provided in Table S1. Disease characteristics at baseline, prior to any abrocitinib dose, were generally comparable between the two cohorts; slightly higher median EASI values and percentage of body surface area were observed in the variable-dose cohort (Table 1).

Efficacy Response

In both the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg and variable-dose cohorts, most patients attained EASI-50 or EASI-75 at all assessed time points through week 48 of abrocitinib treatment (Fig. 2). While patients in the variable-dose cohort previously received abrocitinib for variable durations in the JADE REGIMEN trial, data from patients in the continuous 200 mg cohort were reported from the first day of abrocitinib treatment; therefore, the response profile of the continuous 200 mg cohort includes a “ramp-up” phase where the response rate substantially increased between 2 and 12 weeks of treatment before reaching a plateau; this is artifactual and occurred because, for some patients in this cohort, the first day of abrocitinib dosing only occurred in EXTEND. At week 48 of abrocitinib treatment, the proportions of responders were numerically higher with continuous abrocitinib 200 mg than with variable-dose treatment, with overlapping 95% CIs (EASI-50, 94% [95% CI 92–96%] vs 87% [79–95%]; EASI-75, 82% [79–86%] vs 69% [58–79%]). Substantial proportions of patients achieved a PP-NRS4 response in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg and variable-dose cohorts at all assessed time points through week 48 (Fig. 2). Response rates were greater with continuous abrocitinib 200 mg than with variable-dose abrocitinib at week 48 of abrocitinib treatment; 67% (95% CI 63–71%) of patients in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort achieved PP-NRS4 compared with 44% (32–55%) in the variable-dose cohort.

High-Threshold Efficacy Response

The proportions of patients who attained EASI-90, EASI-100, and IGA 0 (high-threshold skin improvement or skin clearance) or PP-NRS 0/1 (profound itch control) increased from week 2 through week 48 of abrocitinib treatment in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort but were sustained up to week 48 of abrocitinib treatment in the variable-dose cohort (Fig. 4). At week 48, response rates were higher with continuous abrocitinib 200 mg than with variable-dose abrocitinib, with non-overlapping 95% CIs (EASI-90: 57% [95% CI 53–61%] vs 39% [27–50%]; PP-NRS 0/1, i.e., profound itch control: 44% [40–48%] vs 21% [12–31%]), and were numerically higher, with overlapping 95% CIs (EASI-100: 21% [18–25%] vs 11% [4–19%]; IGA 0: 23% [19–26%] vs 13% [5–21%]).

Quality of Life Improvements

Substantial proportions of adults and adolescents in both cohorts had a ≥ 4-point improvement in DLQI/CDLQI scores at all assessed time points through week 48 of abrocitinib treatment (Fig. 3). At week 48, proportions of responders were numerically greater in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort compared with the variable-dose cohort, with non-overlapping 95% CIs for DLQI (≥ 4-point improvement in DLQI: 90% [87–93%] vs 73% [62–84%]) and overlapping 95% CIs for CDLQI (≥ 4-point improvement in CDLQI: 83% [76–89%] vs 67% [29–100%]).

Similar trends were observed for CDLQI 0/1 and DLQI 0/1 responses during treatment with abrocitinib (Fig. 3). LSM absolute CDLQI and DLQI scores were numerically lower (better) at week 12 of abrocitinib treatment in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort than in the variable-dose cohort, and these observations were sustained through week 48; 95% CIs were large in the variable-dose cohort (Fig. S3).

Safety

The median (quartile [Q] 1, Q3) duration of abrocitinib exposure was 561.0 (304.0, 783.0) days in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort. Of the 941 patients in this cohort, 378 (40.2%) had ≥ 96 weeks of exposure. In the variable-dose cohort, the median (Q1, Q3) duration of abrocitinib exposure was 1161.5 (587.0, 1251.0) days. Of the 90 patients in this cohort, 66 (73.3%) had ≥ 96 weeks of cumulative exposure.

In the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort, the IRs (95% CI) per 100 PY for SAEs (6.57 [5.30–8.05]; n = 93) and AEs leading to discontinuation (6.96 [5.67–8.46]; n = 101) were numerically higher than in the variable-dose cohort (SAEs: 3.81 [1.53–7.85], n = 7; AEs leading to discontinuation: 4.77 [2.18–9.06], n = 9); CIs were widely overlapping (Table 2). IRs (95% CI) for severe AEs were numerically lower in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort (5.69 [4.51–7.08]; n = 80) than in the variable-dose cohort (9.92 [5.78–15.89]; n = 17); however, CIs were also widely overlapping. There were 3 (0.3%) deaths among patients in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort; 2 of these deaths (previously reported [18, 24]) were considered unrelated to the study drug by the investigators: a 69-year-old Black or African American woman who died due to COVID-19 and a 42-year-old white man who died due to cardiac failure. Another patient death (also previously reported [24]) occurred in a 75-year-old man due to septic shock, which was deemed related to the study drug. No deaths occurred in the variable-dose cohort.

The IRs (95% CI) per 100 PY for AEs of special interest—including serious infections, malignancies, cardiovascular events, and laboratory changes—were generally comparable between the two cohorts, with widely overlapping CIs (Table S2). IRs were numerically higher in the variable-dose cohort than in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort for serious infections (3.23 [95% CI 1.18–7.02] vs 2.61 [1.85–3.58]), all herpes zoster infections (5.06 [2.31–9.60] vs 4.99 [3.89–6.30]), and opportunistic herpes zoster infections (1.62 [0.33–4.73] vs 1.17 [0.68–1.88]). There were no events of tuberculosis in the variable-dose cohort and one case in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort. IRs for malignancies (excluding NMSC), NMSC, MACE, VTE, thrombocytopenia, lymphopenia, and retinal detachment were numerically higher in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort than in the variable-dose cohort (CIs were widely overlapping); no cases were observed in the variable-dose cohort.

Discussion

In this pooled analysis of data obtained from qualifying trials in the JADE clinical program and the long-term JADE EXTEND trial, substantial proportions of patients with moderate-to-severe AD achieved long-term clinically meaningful, and even stringent high-threshold, efficacy and QoL outcomes with either variable-dose abrocitinib or continuous abrocitinib 200 mg dosing; there were no new or unexpected safety signals.

Higher proportions of patients in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort achieved clinically relevant and high-threshold efficacy and QoL outcomes compared with the variable-dose cohort, indicating that treatment with continuous abrocitinib 200 mg once daily was the more effective regimen. This is consistent with findings from short- and long-term JADE clinical trials, which demonstrated that abrocitinib 200 mg was more effective than abrocitinib 100 mg in patients with moderate-to-severe AD; however, both doses significantly improved signs and symptoms of AD when compared with placebo [16, 17, 20–22]. Conversely, higher proportions of the variable dose cohort achieved clinically relevant efficacy and QoL outcomes compared with findings from previous JADE clinical trials reporting on the efficacy and QoL outcomes in patients receiving abrocitinib 100 mg [25]; variable dosing likely performed better than 100 mg continuous as a result of a combination of tighter disease control during flares and greater total cumulative dose over the study period. A similar trend was observed during the maintenance phase of JADE REGIMEN; higher rates of maintenance response occurred with abrocitinib 200 mg following an initial induction phase with the same dose compared with patients who received the same 200-mg induction dose and stepped down treatment to abrocitinib 100 mg or placebo for maintenance [19]. In short-term JADE clinical trials, faster response times were observed with abrocitinib 200 mg compared with abrocitinib 100 mg. In this study, the increase in response rate among patients in the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort, who were not pre-treated, is consistent with findings from short-term JADE clinical trials, which showed a rapid onset of clinically meaningful improvements in itch (as early as day 1 after the first dose of abrocitinib) [22, 23, 25, 26] and skin signs of AD (as early as week 2) [16, 17, 21–23].

Regarding safety data, previous findings from the JADE clinical program [16, 17, 21, 22, 27] and results from the variable-dose pool from the JADE REGIMEN trial [19] have showed higher AE occurrence with abrocitinib 200 mg than with 100 mg. In this study, rates of AEs were generally comparable between the two cohorts, with a numerically lower risk in the variable-dose cohort. While safety findings may be consistent with those previously reported for abrocitinib, conclusions need to be interpreted with caution due to the small number of patients in the variable-dose population included in this study. The safety profile for SAEs (i.e., AEs leading to study discontinuation and deaths) in patients who received continuous abrocitinib 200 mg and variable-dose abrocitinib in this analysis was comparable to that found in a larger integrated safety analysis of patients who received consistent abrocitinib 200 mg or variable-dose treatment in JADE REGIMEN and JADE EXTEND [24].

In a clinical setting, flexible abrocitinib dosing could allow healthcare providers to tailor treatment intensity and exposure to optimize the benefit-risk profile for individual patients based on their treatment response and individual risk profile. The variable-dose cohort represents a selected subset of patients who experienced a flare during the initial step-down in JADE REGIMEN, potentially indicating a more aggressive disease profile compared with that of the overall patient population. After patients received abrocitinib 200 mg with TCS rescue therapy for 12 weeks, the abrocitinib dose was reduced once more to 100 mg in JADE EXTEND. A substantial proportion of these patients maintained long-term clinically meaningful improvements in itch, signs of AD, and QoL for up to 48 weeks on abrocitinib 100 mg once daily and had overall lower rates of AEs compared with the continuous abrocitinib 200 mg cohort. If permitted in locally approved dosing recommendations, a starting dose of abrocitinib 200 mg may be considered for patients with a high burden of symptoms to achieve rapid treatment response for itch reduction, improvement in skin signs of AD, and improved QoL; however, the higher rates of AEs with abrocitinib 200 mg compared with 100 mg may limit its use for long-term maintenance, especially in patients with high comorbidity risk. For such patients, long-term maintenance of disease control may be achieved with abrocitinib 100 mg while minimizing the risk of AEs.

To our knowledge, this was the first analysis to explore multiple treatment escalations and de-escalations with a Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor, although these findings are consistent with those from previous studies in patients with AD treated with other JAK inhibitors. For example, in an analysis of a phase 3 trial of baricitinib, patients who experienced regression after down-titration from baricitinib 4 mg or 2 mg to 2 mg or 1 mg regained disease control upon reintroduction of baricitinib at the original dose [28]. In a phase 2b trial with another JAK inhibitor, upadacitinib, patients who experienced symptom recurrence after switching from upadacitinib 7.5 mg, 15 mg, or 30 mg to placebo were able to achieve clinically meaningful response on re-treatment [29].

The observations reported here indicate that continuous abrocitinib 200 mg treatment is the most effective dosing strategy for achieving long-term AD disease control for patients with moderate-to-severe AD. The findings also support variable-dose abrocitinib as an effective treatment strategy, which may optimize risk–benefit for the majority of patients, with occasional up-titration of dose followed by step-down treatment for maintenance. A tailored, structured treatment plan that considers longitudinal disease patterns and individual risk of AEs can achieve robust endpoints and may be a more appropriate strategy than a one-size-fits-all regimen. The availability of abrocitinib at multiple dosages across different regions can facilitate this individualized approach.

This analysis was limited by its post hoc nature. Further studies evaluating the effect of consistent versus variable abrocitinib dosing are warranted. The variable-dose cohort had a relatively small sample size compared with the consistent dose cohort; discontinuations further reduced the size of both cohorts over time. Conclusions therefore need to be interpreted with caution due to the difference in sample size between the two cohorts and the relatively small sample size of the variable-dose cohort. Patients in the variable-dose cohort had already received between 24 and 64 weeks of variable-dose abrocitinib (200 mg during 12-week induction, 100 mg during 40-week maintenance, and 200 mg plus topical therapy as 12-week rescue treatment) in JADE REGIMEN before entering JADE EXTEND. Therefore, a landmark analysis of efficacy at week 48 in JADE EXTEND represents > 48 weeks of abrocitinib exposure. Patients in JADE EXTEND were not assessed for flares and could have discontinued the trial for reasons other than lack of efficacy; we could not analyze flaring patterns in individual patients in this analysis. The use of topical medicated therapy varied across the qualifying trials in the JADE clinical program—some required it, some permitted it, and some prohibited it. Finally, without imputation for missing data, bias may have been introduced, potentially overestimating efficacy and underestimating safety risks if patients discontinued due to lack of response or AEs.

Conclusion

Ultimately, both continuous abrocitinib 200 mg and variable-dose abrocitinib were associated with long-term efficacy in patients with moderate-to-severe AD. Continuous abrocitinib 200 mg was the more effective dosing strategy, while a variable dosing strategy was more effective than continuous abrocitinib 100 mg with numerically lower safety event rates compared with continuous abrocitinib 200 mg. Selected patients may be able to achieve long-term clinically meaningful, and even high-threshold, responses, while minimizing AE risks with a variable-dose regimen adapting the dose to disease dynamics.
