CAR-T and TIL therapies in solid tumors: barriers, clinical lessons, and convergent solutions
Authors: Duc-Hiep Bach, Van T. Hoang, Pham Thi Viet Huong, Dinh Duy Nguyen, Thanh Liem Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The approval of lifileucel in 2024 marked an important milestone in oncology as the first cellular therapy authorized for a solid tumor. This milestone stands in sharp contrast to the success of CAR-T cells in hematologic malignancies, where six products have been licensed, and highlights the central challenge that solid tumors remain largely unconquered. At the mechanistic core lies a three-stage framework describing the major barriers encountered by therapeutic T cells in solid tumors, a series of escalating barriers that any therapeutic T cell must overcome to achieve durable tumor control: (1) Access: overcoming stromal and vascular barriers that restrict T-cell infiltration into tumors, (2) Recognition: identifying malignant cells in the setting of antigen heterogeneity and immune evasion, and (3) Persistence: maintaining T-cell function within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Historically, CAR-T and TIL therapies were viewed in competition, each occupying distinct niches. The field is increasingly adopting a convergent paradigm in which both platforms address a common challenge: overcoming the biological barriers that limit durable responses in solid tumors through complementary engineering and biological strategies. We review the biological obstacles, emerging convergence strategies, and translational frameworks including biomarker-guided patient selection that define this new area. Therapeutic selection may increasingly be guided by a tumor’s dominant biological barriers rather than by platform classification alone.

Introduction

Cellular immunotherapies now occupy two strikingly different positions in oncology. Among the most clinically advanced adoptive T-cell platforms are chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, which employs genetically engineered T cells to recognize predefined tumor-associated antigens, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy, which expands naturally occurring tumor-reactive T cells isolated directly from patient tumors. Engineered chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells have produced durable remissions in selected hematologic malignancies and established the feasibility of long-term disease control in certain patient populations [1–3]. In contrast, the first regulatory validation of an adoptive T-cell therapy in a solid tumor, TIL therapy in melanoma arrived only recently [4]. Together, these developments highlight both the therapeutic potential and the remaining challenges of adoptive cellular therapies: although the capacity of T cells to eradicate cancer has been demonstrated, achieving consistent efficacy in epithelial solid tumors remains a major challenge.

The field’s thinking has evolved accordingly. The first era of cellular therapy was shaped by a competitive paradigm that treated platforms as distinct and disease-segmented: CAR-T for blood cancers [1, 2], TIL for melanoma and select immunogenic tumors [5, 6]. That framing was useful for establishing clinical footholds, but it may overlook a common challenge revealed by the limited translation to epithelial cancers: both platforms face similar biological barriers within the solid tumor microenvironment. The field is increasingly adopting a convergent paradigm in which the focus has shifted from “CAR-T versus TIL therapy” to identifying complementary engineering and biological strategies that address the major barriers limiting cellular therapy efficacy in solid tumors.

To organize the major barriers to effective cellular therapy in solid tumors, we propose a mechanistic framework termed the T-cell gauntlet, which describes the sequential barriers that therapeutic T cells must overcome to achieve durable tumor control. The framework comprises three sequential stages. Access: T cells must overcome aberrant vasculature, chemokine mismatches, and dense stromal barriers that restrict intratumoral infiltration and distribution [7–9]. Recognition: once inside, T cells face antigenic heterogeneity, lineage plasticity, and depending on the platform variable MHC expression that confounds accurate, sustained tumor recognition [10, 11]. Persistence and function: even after successful tumor recognition, metabolic stress, suppressive myeloid networks, and checkpoint signaling within the tumor microenvironment can impair T-cell function and persistence. Failures observed clinically limited trafficking, early antigen escape, or short-lived response can be mapped to attrition at one or more of these stages [12–14].

This framework has several practical implications. It explains why TIL therapy with its native polyclonality and tumor-imprinted homing can achieve meaningful activity in inflamed, antigen-diverse tumors, yet still falter without sufficient access or persistence support [15–17]. It likewise clarifies why CAR-T cells, whose programmable potency is unmatched in the circulation and marrow, require multi-antigen logic, enhanced trafficking, and resistance to intratumoral suppression to translate that potency to epithelial sites [18–20]. Most importantly, it points to shared solutions that cut across platforms, including chemokine receptor engineering and matrix remodeling to improve access; dual, tandem, and logic-gated receptors to sustain recognition in the face of heterogeneity; and gene editing, cytokine armoring, and rational combinations to preserve function. In a convergent paradigm, these are not CAR-specific or TIL-specific strategies; they are modular interventions applied according to the dominant stage(s) of failure in a given tumor [21–23].

This Review adopts that convergence mindset and pursues four goals. First, we define the biological architecture of the T-cell gauntlet and synthesize the strongest mechanistic evidence for each barrier. Second, we analyze the clinical experience of TIL and CAR-T in epithelial cancers through this mechanistic framework, asking not only whether responses occurred but also where in the sequence of access, recognition, and persistence attrition was most likely. Third, we summarize a set of convergent therapeutic strategies such as armored constructs, logic-gated and multi-specific targeting, regional delivery, and rational combinations with checkpoint blockade, virotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted agents and link each tool to its mechanistic target within the gauntlet. Fourth, we propose a biomarker-guided decision framework and translational roadmap that move the conversation from platform allegiance to tumor-centric assignment: therapies selected and sequenced based on a tumor’s gauntlet profile, defined as its measurable pattern of access, recognition, and persistence barriers.

Two design principles run throughout. Mechanism-to-translation continuity: each mechanistic subsection ends with a concise translational note that turns biology into trial design route and lesion selection for access, adaptive/multi-antigen schemas for recognition, and integrated longitudinal readouts (serial biopsies, spatial omics, advanced imaging, circulating markers) for persistence and microenvironment remodeling. To enhance practical utility, the figures and tables include an anchor schematic of the T-cell gauntlet and its therapeutic solutions; a side-by-side comparison of platform-specific failure modes and corresponding interventions; a clinical landscape summary organized by route and barrier; and a two-axis decision grid (tumor antigenicity × T-cell infiltration) that generates testable, quadrant-specific hypotheses.

Hematologic successes are summarized briefly because the biological and clinical challenges associated with epithelial solid tumors are fundamentally distinct. Achieving consistent efficacy in epithelial cancers will likely require integrated strategies that promote tumor infiltration, sustain recognition across evolving targets, and preserve T-cell fitness under metabolic and immunologic pressure. The objective of this convergent paradigm is to develop personalized cellular therapies that can be deployed more effectively and safely, with treatment selection guided by the dominant biological barriers present within individual tumors rather than by platform classification alone. The sections that follow provide a mechanistic framework, clinical synthesis, and translational considerations for advancing cellular therapy in solid tumors.

The t-cell gauntlet in solid tumors

While established frameworks like the Cancer-Immunity Cycle (CIC) provide a comprehensive map of the anti-tumor immune response from antigen release to tumor cell elimination, the unique challenges of adoptive cell therapy in solid tumors concentrate within the effector phase. The CIC’s final three steps: T-cell infiltration into tumors, recognition of malignant cells, and execution of cytotoxic function map directly onto the sequential hurdles of our “T-cell gauntlet” [24, 25]. In this framing, this framework provides a high-resolution, T-cell–centric perspective on steps 5–7 of the CIC, focusing on the decisive juncture where an activated therapeutic T cell must traverse physical barriers (Access), maintain accurate target engagement in the face of heterogeneity (Recognition), and resist attrition under suppressive pressures (Persistence) [26]. By positioning this framework as a refinement rather than a replacement of the CIC, we acknowledge the foundational framework while sharpening attention on the phase where adoptive cell therapies most often succeed or fail [27].

access: overcoming physical barriers

The first challenge is physical access. Tumor vasculature is often tortuous, poorly perfused, and characterized by high interstitial pressure. Endothelial cells can even express anti-adhesive or pro-apoptotic cues that block T-cell entry. To counter this, strategies such as vascular normalization with anti-VEGF or low-dose radiotherapy can transiently restore adhesion molecule expression and create a “window” for infiltration [28, 29].

Beyond the vasculature, the extracellular matrix (ECM) rich in collagen, hyaluronan, and fibroblast-derived proteins creates a dense, fibrotic barrier. Enzymes like heparanase or hyaluronidase, as well as approaches targeting fibroblast activation protein (FAP), are being developed to soften and remodel this matrix [8, 30–32].

Chemokine mismatches add another layer of exclusion. Many solid tumors attract myeloid cells through CXCL1/2 or CCL2, while producing little of the CXCL9/10 signals that recruit effector T cells. Engineering CAR-T cells to express receptors such as CXCR2 or CCR2 can re-tune migration to match these chemokine gradients [33, 34]. Finally, regional delivery into cerebrospinal fluid, pleura, peritoneum, or hepatic artery can bypass systemic obstacles altogether and achieve locally concentrated access [35, 36]. TIL products sometimes retain tumor-imprinted homing features, giving them a natural advantage in certain inflamed lesions [37]. Translational note: Early-phase trials should prospectively select lesions and routes of administration with access in mind. Endpoints should include direct measures of infiltration paired biopsies, spatial mapping of infiltrates, and imaging of excluded-to-inflamed transitions so that access is evaluated not as background biology but as a therapeutic outcome [38].

Recognition: identifying malignant cells

Once inside, T cells must reliably identify malignant cells. Solid tumors rarely present uniform targets. Antigen density can vary among subclones, and immune pressure can drive antigen loss or lineage plasticity. For TILs, downregulation of MHC class I molecules (e.g., via B2M mutations) impairs antigen presentation. For CAR-T, which rely on high-density surface proteins, single-antigen designs are highly vulnerable to escape [10, 11, 39]. Here, the contrast between platforms is most evident. TIL therapy provides a biologically distinct approach: polyclonal breadth that naturally buffers against antigen loss [15]. CAR-T cells rely on engineered solutions: tandem or bicistronic CARs to broaden recognition, logic-gated systems to improve specificity, and adapter CARs to flexibly retarget as the antigenic landscape evolves. Affinity tuning further balances potency and safety, while TCR-based approaches extend recognition to intracellular targets. Engineered TILs selected for dominant neoantigen reactivity represent a hybrid path, bringing greater precision into polyclonal repertoires [19, 20].

Clinical implication: Clinical trial design must reflect antigenic diversity. Instead of restricting eligibility to single antigens, trials should incorporate antigen panels and adaptive protocols that allow retargeting upon escape. Real-time monitoring through serial biopsies, spatial proteomics, and ctDNA analysis can guide when to escalate from single-antigen to multi-antigen or logic-based constructs [40].

Persistence and function: overcoming immunosuppressive barriers

Even when access and recognition are achieved, the tumor microenvironment imposes multiple immunosuppressive pressures on therapeutic T cells. Chronic stimulation induces exhaustion through inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3 [12]. Suppressive myeloid cells release TGF-β, IL-10, and arginase, while metabolic stresses, hypoxia, acidity, and adenosine accumulation undermine T-cell fitness [13, 14].

Chronic stimulation in the TME drives a dysfunctional program broadly termed “exhaustion.” However, exhaustion is not a binary state but a progressive differentiation trajectory. A critical distinction exists between progenitor-exhausted (TPEX) cells, defined by expression of TCF1 and self-renewal capacity, and terminally exhausted (TEX) cells, characterized by irreversible loss of function and resistance to rescue [41–44]. TPEX cells are the primary reservoir that responds to checkpoint blockade, whereas TEX cells remain largely refractory. This bifurcation, orchestrated by transcriptional regulators such as TOX and the NR4A family, underscores why simply prolonging T-cell survival is insufficient: effective persistence strategies must preserve or expand the progenitor-exhausted pool [45–47]. Exhaustion also differs from senescence, a distinct dysfunction involving permanent cell-cycle arrest and pro-inflammatory secretory features. Linking these molecular states to therapeutic strategy provides important translational insights: “young TIL” protocols aim to maintain less-differentiated states at harvest; CAR constructs are being rewired to limit tonic signaling and preserve TPEX-like features; and rational combinations with PD-1 blockade preferentially reinvigorate progenitor subsets. Thus, persistence-focused interventions are most impactful when explicitly designed to defend functional, self-renewing pools rather than merely delaying attrition.

Solutions fall into two main categories.

Cell-intrinsic reprogramming focuses on enhancing T-cell function and fitness. Gene editing can knock out PD-1 or rewire it into a stimulatory receptor [23]. Dominant-negative TGF-β receptors and adenosine-resistant constructs counter major suppressive pathways. Cytokine armoring with IL-12, IL-18, or IL-7 can support effector tone, while metabolic engineering enhances resilience under adverse microenvironmental conditions [48, 49]. Manufacturing protocols that preserve memory-like states, together with costimulatory backbones favoring long-term persistence, further improve durability [50].

Extrinsic support and microenvironment remodeling focus on improving the tumor microenvironment. Checkpoint blockade can relieve inhibitory signaling on both infused and endogenous T cells. Oncolytic viruses, radiotherapy, and selected targeted agents can upregulate antigen and HLA, promoting the transition of immune-excluded tumors toward inflamed tumor microenvironments [51]. Strategies that target myeloid populations, such as CSF1R or CCR2 blockade, can reprogram suppressive stroma to permit T-cell activity [52].

TIL therapy provides tumor-imprinted resilience but often requires checkpoint blockade to maintain activity. CAR-T cells provide engineered functionality but depend on both intrinsic reprogramming and extrinsic remodeling for persistence in epithelial tumors.

Clinical implication: Persistence should be treated as a defined endpoint in trials. Longitudinal biopsies, clonotype tracking, and integration of spatial omics and imaging can capture exhaustion trajectories and functional fitness. Linking these measurements to ctDNA clearance and durability of response will sharpen the field’s understanding of what persistence truly requires.

Taken together, the three stages of the T-cell gauntlet including access, recognition, and persistence are sequential and interdependent. Failure at any stage truncates therapeutic impact. Access without recognition produces bystander infiltration; recognition without persistence yields transient responses; persistence without specificity risks toxicity. Effective strategies will therefore require solutions that are stacked and tailored according to each tumor’s measurable gauntlet profile. Clinical evidence in solid tumors

The clinical landscape has evolved rapidly with several important advances since 2024. The FDA approval of afamitresgene autoleucel (afami-cel), a TCR-T therapy targeting MAGE-A4 in synovial sarcoma, established the second regulatory milestone for adoptive cell therapy in solid tumors [53]. In parallel, next-generation TIL products such as OBX-115 (engineered to express membrane-bound IL-15, eliminating reliance on high-dose IL-2) have reported promising early data, illustrating how biologic breadth can be combined with engineered approaches to improve persistence [54]. For CAR-T therapy, satri-cel (targeting CLDN18.2) delivered positive pivotal trial results in gastric and gastroesophageal cancers, marking the most advanced CAR program in epithelial malignancies to date [55, 56]. In pediatric diffuse midline glioma, intracerebroventricular delivery of GD2-CARs produced durable regressions and neurological recovery, offering compelling proof-of-concept for overcoming the access barrier via regional administration [57, 58]. Meanwhile, dual-target CARs (e.g., EGFR/IL13Rα2) in glioblastoma demonstrated early signals of activity at ASCO 2025, illustrating strategies that explicitly address antigen heterogeneity [59]. Together, these findings suggest that durable responses in solid tumors are more likely when therapeutic strategies are matched to the dominant biological barriers limiting efficacy, including regional delivery to improve access, logic- and dual-target designs to address antigen heterogeneity, and cytokine armoring or IL-2 alternatives to support persistence.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy: proof of concept and extension beyond melanoma

The US accelerated approval of lifileucel for post-immune checkpoint inhibitor (post-ICI) advanced melanoma (Feb 16, 2024), the first cellular therapy approved for a solid tumor, established the clinical feasibility of one-time autologous TIL therapy with durable responses in an ICI-refractory population [5, 60]. Pivotal and pooled updates consistently show ~ 30–40% objective response rates (ORR) with durable remissions in a subset; a randomized Dutch trial previously demonstrated superiority of TIL over ipilimumab in advanced melanoma, confirming platform efficacy in a controlled setting [61]. These data collectively support the therapeutic rationale for TIL therapy polyclonal, intratumoral reactivity that can reset exhausted immunity after lymphodepletion.

Signals now extend to other epithelial malignancies. In metastatic NSCLC progressing on PD-1 blockade, a phase I single-arm study (n = 20) combining lymphodepletion, TIL therapy, IL-2, and maintenance nivolumab reported confirmed responses in 3/13 evaluable patients (including ongoing CRs), broad tumor shrinkage in most patients, and persistent neoantigen-reactive clones evidence that TIL therapy can provide broader antigen recognition in settings where prior ICI therapy was ineffective [62].

In recurrent or metastatic cervical cancer (a disease typically driven by HPV infection), the innovaTIL-04 (C-145-04) trial of LN-145 demonstrated clinically meaningful responses in heavily pretreated patients, which supported FDA Breakthrough Therapy designation. Early combination cohorts with PD-1 blockade showed further improvements in activity, suggesting enhanced persistence and synergy. These studies also highlighted practical considerations such as lesion selection for harvest, surgical coordination, and peri–IL-2 management that are critical for trial design [63].

In early head and neck squamous cell carcinoma experience, Cohort 2 A of the Phase II IOV‑COM‑202 trial (NCT03645928) evaluating LN‑145 plus pembrolizumab in ICI‑naive advanced disease produced clinically meaningful response rates, with manageable safety and encouraging durability signals reinforcing that polyclonal recognition may be amplified by checkpoint blockade in an inflamed tumor microenvironment [64].

Clinical implication: Feasibility rises when procurement targets immunologically accessible lesions; responses track with polyclonal breadth; and durability improves with rational persistence support (lymphodepletion, low-dose IL-2, PD-(L)1 combinations). Trial logistics (harvestable site, timing) and correlative plans (serial blood/biopsies for clonotype tracking) are not ancillary they are determinants of success [6, 62].

CAR-T therapy: selective signals, engineering lessons, and the persistence gap

CAR-T experience in solid tumors is heterogeneous across targets and routes, but several representative studies illustrate the major barriers limiting efficacy and how engineering strategies may address them.

Access: Locoregional delivery can partially solve the first major barrier to efficacy. In malignant pleural mesothelioma, intrapleural mesothelin CAR-T followed by pembrolizumab was safe, achieved peripheral persistence > 100 days in ~ 40% of patients, and yielded favorable overall survival outcomes with metabolic responses observed in a subset of patients, an access-focused strategy that reduced sequestration and enabled functional activity at lower doses [65, 66].

Recognition: Antigen choice and heterogeneity remain central. In glioblastoma, EGFRvIII-CAR-T showed on-target trafficking but rapid antigen loss, a representative example highlighting the impact of antigen heterogeneity as a major barrier to therapeutic efficacy [67]. Conversely, IL-13Rα2-CAR-T delivered intracranially produced substantial tumor regressions in an index patient without high-grade toxicity [35, 67], highlighting how target selection plus route can transiently overcome recognition and access but still leave persistence at risk.

Pediatric diffuse midline gliomas (H3K27M+) have emerged as a setting where a well-matched antigen (GD2) and repeated intracranial dosing yielded radiographic regressions and neurological improvements in early trials [68], suggesting that precise target biology plus engineered delivery can crest both the access and recognition hurdles while the durability question (third hurdle) is addressed by re-infusion and CNS-directed delivery.

Safety/persistence: On-target/off-tumor toxicity remains a boundary condition for CARs in solid tissues (e.g., historical HER2 experience), underscoring why affinity tuning, multi-antigen logic, and regional delivery are necessary to improve persistence while maintaining an acceptable safety profile [69].

Clinical implication: If the lesion is compartmentalized (pleura, ventricles), favor regional delivery to maximize access. For heterogeneous antigens, consider multi-specific or logic-gated CARs or shift the indication to one with a validated, selectively expressed target (e.g., GD2 in DMG, CLDN18.2 in gastric cancer) [70], where early-phase data show higher early response rates. Regardless, plan prospectively for persistence: checkpoint co-therapy, cytokine armoring, and scheduled re-infusions are design levers, not afterthoughts.

Comparative insights from CAR-T and TIL therapies within the T-cell framework

Access (Physical barriers to tumor infiltration): Surgical procurement for TIL doubles as a biomarker event (an “access audit”); lesions that are harvestable are often those that are subsequently infiltrable. For CAR-T, the strongest clinical evidence for improving access comes from regional administration (pleura; CNS), which shortens the path to tumor and reduces first-pass loss [62, 65, 68].

Recognition (Identification of malignant cells): This concept is supported by observations that neoantigen-reactive TIL clonotypes not only persist in peripheral blood following infusion in NSCLC [62], but also that loss of such clonotypes and disappearance of targeted subclonal neoantigens correlate with disease progression [71], suggesting that polyclonal breadth provides resilience against antigenic heterogeneity. CAR-T offers engineered precision but must explicitly solve heterogeneity (dual/triple CARs, synNotch gating, or sequential targets) to avoid the EGFRvIII experience of selective escape [19, 67].

Persistence (Overcoming immunosuppressive barriers): Both platforms benefit from lymphodepletion and, in many contexts, PD-(L)1 modulation [65, 72]. For CAR-T therapy, persistence often hinges on (i) mitigating tonic signaling/exhaustion (costimulatory domain choice; transcriptional rewiring), (ii) microenvironment conditioning (oncolytics, radiotherapy), and (iii) lower-affinity binders to reduce off-tumor engagement principles derived from early solid-tumor studies [50, 73].

Indication selection and biomarkers: toward rational deployment

A pragmatic reading of today’s evidence suggests: (i) Inflamed, antigen-heterogeneous tumors (melanoma; subsets of HPV-driven cancers; some NSCLC) are natural TIL terrain biologic breadth plus checkpoint synergy can produce durable benefit; (ii) Tumors with a discrete, selectively expressed surface target and a permissive route (CLDN18.2 + gastric cancer; GD2 + DMG; mesothelin+ pleural disease) are where CAR-T’s engineering can shine, especially with regional delivery; and (iii) cross-platform biomarkers (lesion accessibility; spatial antigen maps; T-cell fitness; soluble target shedding) should be prospectively embedded into phase I/II designs [5, 62, 65, 68, 70, 74, 75].

Shared therapeutic strategies across platforms

Successful solid-tumor therapy is likely to require a coordinated set of interventions that address the major barriers to effective T-cell activity in solid tumors: entry, accurate recognition, and durable function. The strategies below are presented not as platform-specific strategies but as modular components that can be applied to CAR-T or TIL therapies according to a tumor’s measured barrier profile.

Armoring and intrinsic rewiring to enhance persistence, access, and recognition

Armoring strategies alter the cell’s intrinsic circuitry to resist suppression and maintain fitness. Checkpoint pathway rewiring includes deletion of inhibitory receptors (for example, PD-1 knockout) or their conversion into costimulatory inputs (e.g., PD-1:CD28 switch receptors) so that exhaustion signals are translated into activation. Dominant-negative receptors (such as TGF-βR2) insulate against stromal cytokines, while edits that blunt adenosine signaling or restore antigen presentation support sustained synapse formation [23, 76, 77].

Cytokine armoring provides local help at the tumor site. Inducible IL-12 or IL-18 can amplify effector programs, recruit and organize endogenous immunity, and counteract myeloid suppression; IL-7/CCL19 motifs foster lymphoid-like niches within tumors [78–80]. Because constitutive payloads risk systemic toxicity and tonic activation, next-generation circuits couple cytokine release to antigen encounter or microenvironmental cues, and incorporate feedback shut-offs.

Metabolic rewiring targets the suppressive metabolic environment of the TME. Programs that enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative capacity, buffer hypoxia and acidity, or limit adenosine/A2A signaling help maintain cytolytic function under nutrient stress. At the product level, preserving stem-like and memory-rich phenotypes during manufacturing shorter culture, selective enrichment, and appropriate co-stimulatory backbones creates a cell intrinsically capable of persistence [81, 82].

Clinical implication: Armoring should be conditional wherever possible (antigen- or microenvironment-triggered) and paired with explicit control elements (suicide switches, small-molecule ON/OFF systems). Early trials should embed pharmacodynamic readouts serial biopsies for pathway engagement; circulating markers of cytokine exposure; and longitudinal clonotype tracking to confirm that armoring improves function without driving undue inflammation.

Logic and multi-specificity for enhanced recognition and specificity

Heterogeneous antigen expression is the canonical failure mode in solids; logic and multi-specificity aim to broaden coverage without sacrificing precision. Bicistronic or tandem receptors extend recognition across two or more antigens; OR logic raises sensitivity in mosaics, whereas AND logic restricts activation to cells co-expressing safer antigen combinations [19, 83]. SynNotch circuits add contextual control, whereby recognition of a priming antigen induces expression of a second tumor-restricted receptor, thereby localizing activity to the tumor environment. Adapter or “universal” CAR-T cells introduce a programmable interface in which soluble binders direct the same cell to different antigens over time, enabling rapid retargeting as the tumor evolves [84–86]. Affinity/avidity tuning remains an important determinant of therapeutic selectivity: lower-affinity binders can discriminate high-density tumor from low-density normal tissue; multimeric architectures raise avidity selectively in the tumor milieu [73].

These constructs are equally relevant to TIL: selected and expanded TIL pools can be augmented with defined clones against dominant neoantigens, or edited to add homing receptors and checkpoint rewires, achieving a hybrid of polyclonal breadth and engineered precision [87].

Clinical implication: Logic design should be pre-specified by spatial co-expression maps rather than by single-antigen IHC. Protocols benefit from adaptive clauses (e.g., switching adapters or escalating from single- to multi-antigen logic at biochemical evidence of escape). Off-tumor risk management (affinity ceilings, NOT gates) and comparability plans for complex products should be agreed with regulators in advance [88].

Regional and focused delivery to improve access and safety

Direct administration to tumor-adjacent compartments increases the fraction of infused cells that reach the target while lowering systemic exposure. In the CNS, ventricular or parenchymal reservoirs facilitate repeated dosing and pharmacodynamic sampling; in pleural and peritoneal disease, intracompartmental delivery achieves high local concentrations [35, 65, 89]; in hepatic metastases, arterial infusion can saturate vascular beds inaccessible to systemic cells [90, 91]. Regional approaches also create control knobs dose per infusion, interval, and location that are unavailable systemically and can expand the therapeutic window.

The same principle applies to TIL: careful selection of harvest sites and surgical pathways that preserve access for future biopsies and, if feasible, local delivery or oncolytic priming can align procurement with treatment logistics.

Clinical implication: Trials should treat delivery as an intervention, not a detail. Device placement, imaging guidance, and schedules for repeat dosing need to be protocolized from the outset. Safety monitoring must be compartment-specific (neurologic, pleural, peritoneal) and paired with on-treatment tissue sampling to confirm improved intratumoral burden and distribution [35, 68].

Engineered TIL and fitness-focused manufacturing

TIL therapy retains native polyclonality and tumor-imprinted homing but is associated with longer manufacturing times, product heterogeneity, and IL-2-related toxicity. “Young TIL” protocols and closed-system, short-duration expansions preserve less-differentiated states and reduce vein-to-vein time [92]. Gene edits can remove dominant inhibitory programs (e.g., PD-1), render TIL resistant to TGF-β, or add chemokine receptors to better match intratumoral gradients [79, 93]. Selection strategies enrich for high-value clonotypes neoantigen-reactive, stem-like subsets that can be tracked longitudinally as a surrogate of product quality [94].

Adjunctive changes to support biology rather than replace it IL-2 alternatives or modified dosing, peri-infusion checkpoint modulation, and peri-operative oncolytic or radiation priming can increase early engraftment while keeping systemic toxicity in check [95].

Clinical implication: Engineered TIL programs should prospectively define which edits or selections are being tested and how success will be measured (clonotype persistence, intratumoral density, functional assays). Logistics harvestable lesion choice, surgical coordination, and fallback plans if procurement fails must be integrated into eligibility and scheduling to avoid attrition.

Rational combinations and microenvironment remodeling

Combination strategies can remodel an immunosuppressive microenvironment to better support T-cell function. Checkpoint blockade remains the most generalizable partner: peri- or post-infusion PD-(L)1, and in selected contexts LAG-3 or TIGIT, can relieve extrinsic inhibition of both infused and endogenous T cells [11, 96, 97]. Oncolytic viruses and radiotherapy provide antigenic and inflammatory primers increasing MHC and target expression, releasing danger signals, and reshaping myeloid composition often improving both access and recognition [98, 99]. Targeted agents can be used functionally rather than solely cytotoxically: brief kinase inhibition to upregulate antigens, anti-angiogenic schedules to open vascular windows, or myeloid-directed therapies (CSF1R, CCR2/CCL2, TGF-β axis) to reduce immune exclusion within the tumor microenvironment [100–104].

The central design principle is sequence and timing. Priming interventions that alter vasculature or antigen display should precede or bracket cell infusion; checkpoint support is most informative when paired with on-treatment tissue and blood to confirm pathway engagement; and re-infusions or adapters can be scheduled in response to early molecular signs of antigenic drift.

Clinical implication: Combination trials should declare a mechanistic intent (improve access, stabilize recognition, sustain persistence), select endpoints that match that intent (spatial conversion, antigen upregulation, exhaustion reversal), and incorporate safety-net controls (dose holds, switch-off circuits) to manage overlapping toxicities.

Taken together, three principles may improve the likelihood of therapeutic success. First, design with conditionality and control inducible payloads, logic gates, and built-in safety switches to concentrate activity at the intended site while minimizing activity in non-target tissues. Second, align interventions to the dominant biological barrier identified in that tumor regional delivery when access is limiting, logic/multi-specificity when heterogeneity dominates, and armoring plus remodeling when persistence fails. Third, plan for adaptation: pre-authorize retargeting, re-infusion, or escalation of circuit complexity based on real-time molecular readouts. In practice, the convergence framework shifts the focus from platform selection to implementation strategy; what matters is selecting, sequencing, and controlling the right modules to address a tumor’s specific biological barriers.

Biomarkers and the therapeutic decision framework

The central aim of biomarker development in cellular therapy for solid tumors is not to rank platforms, but to match a product and its auxiliary interventions to the specific barriers a tumor presents. We operationalize this with a two-axis framework that is simple enough to use prospectively and rich enough to drive adaptive decisions: one axis captures tumor antigenicity (the abundance and distribution of actionable targets), the other captures T-cell infiltration (the degree and pattern of immune access). The intersection defines a tumor’s biological barrier profile and guides platform choice, delivery route, and the need for adjuncts that improve access, recognition, or persistence.

Rationale for integrating antigenicity and infiltration

Solid tumors fail therapy for two broad reasons. Some are poorly accessible: vessels are abnormal, chemokines are mismatched, and matrix is dense, yielding an immune-excluded architecture. Others are accessible but not reliably recognized: targets are sparse, heterogeneous, or rapidly evolving; MHC presentation may be impaired [8, 9, 101, 105]. These failure modes map naturally to orthogonal measurements. Infiltration integrates vascular/stromal barriers and chemokine alignment; antigenicity integrates target density, breadth, and homogeneity (for CAR-based approaches) or the state of antigen presentation (for TCR-based approaches and TIL) [11, 106]. Because both axes are measurable at baseline and on-treatment, the framework supports initial assignment and dynamic repositioning as tumors evolve under pressure [107, 108].

These evolving barrier profiles likely reflect cumulative influences from tumor evolution, host immunity, prior therapies, and other long-term biological factors that are not fully captured by single cross-sectional assessments. Longitudinal monitoring may therefore provide a more accurate representation of therapeutic opportunities and emerging resistance mechanisms.

Importantly, tumor barrier profiles should not be viewed as static biological states. Consequently, longitudinal biomarker assessment and repeated tumor profiling may provide more informative guidance than single cross-sectional measurements, enabling adaptive reassignment of therapeutic strategies as barrier profiles change.

Prospective cohort-linked incident-tumor biobank approaches may further help connect prediagnostic exposures, tumor immune phenotypes, and later therapeutic responsiveness [109].

A limitation of the current two-axis model (antigenicity × infiltration) is that it does not fully capture the functional characteristics of immune infiltration. Tumors with high T-cell density may remain unresponsive if infiltrates are dominated by suppressive subsets such as Tregs or MDSCs, or if resident T cells are predominantly terminally exhausted. To address this, we propose that infiltration be considered as a composite score integrating both quantity and functional polarity. Alternatively, a third axis representing “immune microenvironment phenotype” (suppressive versus permissive) could be incorporated. Practical surrogates include the CD8:Treg ratio, the abundance of suppressive myeloid cells, or spatial transcriptomic signatures of immunosuppression [110–113]. This refinement highlights the deep interdependence of the biological barriers: an immunosuppressive milieu simultaneously restricts access, distorts recognition, and accelerates loss of persistence. By embedding functional state into the framework, decision-making can move beyond simple infiltration status to distinguishing between functionally effective and immunosuppressed immune infiltrates, enabling more precise classification and therapeutic alignment.

Measuring T-cell infiltration with reproducible methods

Infiltration should be quantified with assays that preserve spatial context and are practical in early-phase trials. Multiplex immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescence can enumerate CD8, CD4, FOXP3, and myeloid subsets, while defining geometry (inflamed versus excluded) across tumor–stroma interfaces [38, 104, 105, 114]. Spatial transcriptomics or curated gene signatures (for example, interferon-γ–responsive chemokines, adhesion molecules) complement protein data and are useful when tissue is scarce [115, 116]. Imaging adds a macroscopic view: diffusion-weighted or dynamic-contrast MRI can reflect perfusion and stroma, and emerging immuno-PET probes report on activated cytotoxic cells [117–119]. Whatever the mix, studies should prespecify a small, standardized panel, lock down pre-analytical handling, and analyze lesions rather than patients when disease is multifocal, because access is often site-specific [120]. On-treatment biopsies ideally at two to four weeks are essential to detect conversion from excluded to inflamed architecture, a primary success criterion for access-focused interventions.

Measuring antigenicity and its homogeneity

Antigenicity is not a single number. For CAR-based strategies it includes the absolute density of the candidate surface target, which can be quantified by flow cytometry on viable tumor digests to report molecules per cell and the fraction of positive cells, and increasingly by spatial proteomic methods that map co-expression and heterogeneity across tumor regions [88, 121–123]. In addition, the overall fraction of cells expressing the target and the evenness of that expression within and across lesions are critical to predicting therapeutic durability.

For TCR-dependent approaches (TIL therapy, TCR-T), antigenicity encompasses neoantigen load, clonality, HLA status, and the integrity of antigen-processing machinery [11, 106, 124]. Multiplex protein assays and spatial RNA profiling can provide high-resolution co-expression maps, guiding logic-gated or multi-specific designs [11]. Circulating biomarkers such as soluble antigen or tumor-derived DNA also offer non-invasive readouts of target dynamics under therapy [125, 126].

Because heterogeneity is a dominant driver of escape, reports should include a simple dispersion metric (for example, a coefficient of variation across regions of interest) to distinguish homogeneous from mosaic targets. When lesions disagree, decisions should be made at the level of the dominant clinical driver (threatening lesion) and revisited as new sites emerge [120].

Quadrant assignment and adaptive treatment planning

The two axes define four practical states [127]. Hot–heterogeneous tumors those with brisk infiltration and diversified antigenic landscapes invite polyclonal solutions and minimal access intervention. Hot–homogeneous tumors good infiltration with uniform, high-density targets favor engineered precision with guardrails for safety. Cold–heterogeneous tumors require microenvironmental remodeling to improve tumor accessibility, after which a polyclonal product can exploit breadth. Cold–homogeneous tumors present substantial therapeutic challenges and require both improved tumor accessibility and robust logic-enabled engineering to prevent off-tumor effects as density rises [84, 88, 101, 104], 128– [121]. Assignment is made at baseline from a short, prespecified panel; movement between quadrants is expected and may reflect therapeutic response (for example, cold to hot after a priming maneuver). Trial protocols should allow adaptation when movement reveals new needs, for instance, adding a second target when a hot tumor’s antigen map fragments under pressure.

Therapeutic alignment by quadrant

In hot–heterogeneous disease, TIL therapy aligns naturally with the measured biology. The product’s polyclonal nature buffers against subclonal loss, and activity can be extended with checkpoint support. Harvest strategy matters: selecting lesions that mirror the dominant antigenic terrain preserves therapeutic relevance, and early on-treatment sampling can help confirm intratumoral engagement of diverse antigen-specific clonotypes. CAR-T-based options can still be considered when a stable, high-value target exists, but multi-antigen or adapter systems are preferable to single-target designs [38, 61, 84, 130–132].

In hot–homogeneous disease, engineered receptors can exploit the uniform target landscape. Affinity-tuned CARs reduce off-tumor engagement, and logic gating adds a safety layer when the target has low-level expression in healthy tissue. Persistence remains a risk; embedding anti-exhaustion programming, scheduling checkpoint partners, and planning for re-infusions or adapter changes should be part of the initial design rather than a salvage concept [84, 121, 130, 133, 134].

Cold–heterogeneous tumors require preparatory work before any cellular product can succeed. Vascular normalization, stromal softening, or oncolytic/radiation priming can convert geometry and chemokine tone from excluded to inflamed, while also increasing antigen presentation [98, 99, 101]. Once conversion is documented, TIL therapy or TCR-based approaches can leverage polyclonal breadth; if conversion plateaus, regional delivery can localize cells to permissive niches [65]. A key biomarker of success is architectural: a measurable rise in intratumoral T-cell density, not merely peripheral expansion [38, 105].

Cold–homogeneous tumors present simultaneous access, recognition, and persistence barriers. Here, access interventions and focused delivery reduce first-pass loss, while multi-antigen, armored constructs counter suppression and limit escape [65, 135]. Logic gates (AND/NOT) can enforce specificity when the target exists at low density on critical normal tissues, and safety switches provide a mechanism to discontinue therapy if local inflammation threatens organ function [38, 67, 84, 136]. Because these tumors may transition to an inflamed state during treatment, on-treatment assays should watch for rising infiltration and, paradoxically, for new heterogeneity that may necessitate retargeting.

Endpoints and analytics matched to intent

Endpoints should reflect the barrier being addressed. Access-first studies should co-primary measure spatial conversion (excluded to inflamed) or absolute change in intratumoral T-cell density, alongside safety [9, 38, 105, 117]. Recognition-first studies should track target engagement and evolution: quantitative antigen density, co-expression maps, and early circulating tumor DNA kinetics as a functional readout of cytoreduction across clones [88, 121, 122, 125, 137]. Persistence-first studies should emphasize longitudinal pharmacodynamics: transgene or clonotype tracking in blood and tissue, exhaustion-state trajectories, and functional imaging when available [2, 119, 138–140]. Conventional oncologic outcomes (objective response, duration, progression-free survival) remain essential but should be interpreted in the context of success or failure of the targeted biological barrier, because a negative response rate may still provide mechanistic insight if the intended biological barrier has been modified [141].

Minimal panel and reporting standard for tumor barrier profiling

To enable broad adoption, the decision framework should rely on a minimal, interoperable assay set rather than bespoke platforms [114, 117]. At baseline and at a single early on-treatment time point, we advocate: (i) spatial enumeration of CD8+ T cells with an assessment of exclusion versus infiltration; (ii) quantification of target antigen(s) with co-expression mapping where multiple antigens are under consideration; (iii) a compact interferon-γ/chemokine signature to reflect trafficking tone; (iv) assessment of HLA status and antigen-processing integrity when TCR-based strategies are planned; and (v) circulating tumor DNA to measure early molecular response [11, 88, 104, 105, 116, 120, 121, 124, 137, 142]. Reports should state the quadrant assignment, list the measurements that drove it, and specify any pre-authorized adaptations (for example, conditions that trigger addition of a second target, switch to regional dosing, or escalation of checkpoint support) [107]. When disease is multifocal, the report should be lesion-specific and highlight discordance.

Translational integration of the framework into trial design

Embedding this framework requires modest but deliberate changes. Eligibility should be defined by axis-specific thresholds rather than single biomarkers. Randomization can be stratified by quadrant to avoid imbalances that mask biology. Adaptive clauses should be written into the protocol to allow retargeting, re-infusion, or delivery-route changes upon prespecified biomarker events, with independent review to maintain rigor. Correlative budgets should prioritize the early on-treatment biopsy, as this is where most barrier-specific insights are gained. Finally, publications should report longitudinal changes in barrier status alongside clinical outcomes; doing so will facilitate cross-study comparisons and support deployment of therapies based on underlying biological mechanisms rather than platform classification.

In summary, this biomarker-guided framework provides a practical approach for matching therapeutic strategies to measurable barriers in solid tumors, aligning therapy selection and sequencing with barriers related to access, recognition, and persistence, and enabling a standardized decision-making process across clinical trials and practice.

Safety and the therapeutic window: engineering strategies for risk management

The therapeutic window for cellular therapies is narrower in solid tumors than in hematologic malignancies. Anatomy magnifies risk edema in the brain or pleura can be decisive and targets are often shared with essential normal tissues. Consequently, the toxicity profile shifts: systemic cytokine release syndrome (CRS) can still occur, but the dominant liabilities are on-target/off-tumor injury and compartment-specific inflammatory syndromes such as cerebral edema or pleuritis [65, 69, 143–145]. The goal is not to eliminate inflammation, but to confine it to the tumor and ensure it remains controllable, reversible, and anatomically tolerable.

Platform-specific liabilities. For CAR-T cell therapy, on-target/off-tumor injury is the central hazard. This risk is mitigated by receptor engineering lower-affinity binders that spare low-density normal tissues, and logic circuits (AND/NOT designs) that demand multi-antigen confirmation before activation [61, 69, 121, 146]. For TIL therapy, toxicity stems primarily from regimen intensity: lymphodepletion and high-dose IL-2 drive myelosuppression and capillary leak [61]. Modernization is reducing this burden: fitness-preserving manufacturing yields more potent cells that need less cytokine support, and novel IL-2 alternatives maintain engraftment with a wider safety margin.

Three strategies that improve the therapeutic window: (1) Localization: Regional delivery (intracranial, intrapleural, hepatic) raises intralesional exposure while minimizing systemic spillover [35, 65, 90, 145]; (2) Specificity control: Affinity tuning, logic gating, and adapter systems enforce higher thresholds for activation, limiting engagement of normal tissue [121, 130, 146]; (3) Controllability: Suicide switches, inducible payloads, adjustable regimens, and pharmacologic ON/OFF circuits provide failsafe to de-escalate or terminate activity if toxicity emerges [76, 147–149].

Translational note: Safety in solid tumors should be treated as a design parameter, not just a management plan. Protocols that embed localization, enforce specificity, and build in controllability can expand the therapeutic window and accelerate learning. When safety is engineered upstream [144], adoptive T-cell therapies may be deployed earlier in appropriately selected settings, and systematic evaluation of both successful and unsuccessful clinical outcomes can help refine future therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion: biomarker-guided cellular therapy in solid tumors

Cellular therapies have demonstrated that durable cancer control can be achieved in selected clinical settings [1]. What remains unproven especially in common epithelial malignancies is whether this can be done reliably and at scale. The central challenge is overcoming three interconnected barriers: access, recognition, and persistence that explain both successes and failures. The field is increasingly adopting a convergent framework that integrates complementary strategies from both CAR-T and TIL therapies. In this framework, the complementary strengths of each platform become clear: TILs, with polyclonal breadth and tumor-imprinted homing, are ideally suited for antigen-heterogeneous, inflamed tumors; CAR-Ts, with programmable precision and logic gating, excel when targets are homogeneous or can be made so by engineering and delivery. The path forward lies in matching these tools to the barriers each tumor presents.

Future directions for convergent cellular therapy

Near-term priorities (1–3 years): Barrier-focused clinical trials

Progress will depend on redesigning early-phase studies around barrier-specific endpoints that redefine success as measurable improvement in the biological barriers limiting efficacy, rather than tumor shrinkage alone: (1) Access-first trials: Convert immune-excluded lesions into inflamed ones; primary endpoints include intratumoral T-cell burden and architecture change at prespecified time points; (2) Recognition-first programs: Begin with co-expression maps and pre-authorize adaptive retargeting (adapter swaps, sequential CARs) upon molecular evidence of antigenic drift; (3) Persistence-first designs: Embed longitudinal tissue sampling, clonotype tracking, and exhaustion trajectories, paired with checkpoint or myeloid-modulating partners; (4) Cross-cutting principles: Utilize regional delivery wherever anatomy allows; make armoring conditional and reversible; and engineer safety upstream via route, dose fractionation, and receptor logic.

Mid-term priorities (3–5 years): Platform convergence and biomarker-guided treatment assignment

Engineered TILs that preserve fitness and resist suppressive cues, and multi-antigen, logic-gated CARs that balance breadth with specificity, will move from feasibility to comparative testing. Biomarker-guided assignment will replace single-antigen eligibility: hot/heterogeneous tumors toward TIL-dominant strategies; hot/homogeneous toward CAR-dominant strategies; cold tumors first primed for access, then assigned to the best-matched platform. Regional reservoirs (ventricular, pleural, peritoneal) will evolve into multicenter standards, enabling repeated, dose-controlled infusions with built-in pharmacodynamic sampling [35, 145]. Manufacturing will shorten, and default cell states will shift toward memory-rich phenotypes with tuned co-stimulation to prevent tonic signaling.

Long-term goals (5–10 years): Improved controllability and broader accessibility

The distinction between “CAR-T” and “TIL” will blur. CAR architectures will routinely incorporate multi-antigen logic, inducible payloads, and safety switches; TILs will integrate edits to improve homing, resist suppression, and minimize reliance on toxic systemic cytokines. Universal (allogeneic) and in situ–programmed cells will expand access where vein-to-vein delays are lethal or infrastructure limited. A major challenge is expected to shift from biology to logistics: global deployment will require shorter, automated manufacturing pipelines and decentralized production nodes.

Redefining progress

In solid tumors, progress should be judged not only by response rate but also by measurable improvements in access, recognition, and persistence. A trial that converts an immune-excluded lesion into an inflamed lesion may achieve its mechanistic objective even if tumor shrinkage is delayed; that success should trigger the next step in sequence. Conversely, a transient response without durable persistence may indicate limitations in the therapeutic sequence and suggest the need for microenvironment conditioning, co-stimulatory optimization, or combination therapies. To accelerate collective learning, trials should publish baseline and early on-treatment assessments of access, recognition, and persistence-related biomarkers (infiltration geometry, antigen density and evenness, early pharmacodynamics of persistence), the barrier-specific endpoints, and any preauthorized adaptations triggered.

Safety and equity as design constraints

The therapeutic window will widen when localization and controllability are treated as primary design variables. Inducible armoring, affinity ceilings, NOT gates, and regional dosing are safety features and efficacy enablers, allowing higher intralesional exposure with reduced systemic cost. At the same time, equity is not optional: standardized, rapid manufacturing; point-of-care or regional production; and viable reimbursement models are prerequisites for relevance. Broad implementation will require approaches that can be deployed beyond specialized centers and integrated into routine clinical practice.

Key future studies

Three study designs could help evaluate this framework: (1) A randomized, biomarker-stratified study assigning platform by tumor barrier profile (hot/heterogeneous vs. hot/homogeneous), with adaptive retargeting built in, to prove matching tool to problem outperforms single-antigen eligibility; (2) A regional delivery study with pharmacodynamic gates requiring tissue confirmation of access before dose escalation or partner addition to quantify how much route control widens the therapeutic window; (3) A head-to-head of conditional versus constitutive armoring, using identical backbones and prespecified tissue endpoints, to settle whether inducibility separates efficacy from systemic inflammation in humans.

Future perspectives

Engineered TILs may be particularly suitable for inflamed, antigen-heterogeneous tumors, whereas multi-antigen, logic-gated CAR-T approaches may be advantageous when targets are relatively homogeneous or regional delivery is feasible. Key challenges over the next five years may increasingly relate to manufacturing capacity and treatment accessibility. The first reliably successful solid tumor programs will be sequence-aware by design: priming for access, locking in recognition breadth, then defending persistence.

Future outlook

A key lesson from a decade of adoptive T-cell therapy is that therapeutic efficacy depends on matching treatment strategies to the dominant biological barriers present within individual tumors. The convergent paradigm focuses on identifying the dominant barriers limiting efficacy and deploying modular solutions to address them. When that becomes routine, treatment selection may increasingly be guided by tumor biological profiles rather than by platform classification alone. Oncologists will prescribe a therapy because a lesion is cold and homogeneous or hot and heterogeneous not because it is “CAR-T” or “TIL.” In this future, therapies are chosen according to a tumor’s biological barrier profile, adapted as that profile evolves, and delivered safely because their activity is local, conditional, and controllable. Such an approach may support broader and more consistent clinical implementation.

Boxes

Box 1 | Lessons from hematology: calibration, not blueprint

Experience in B-cell malignancies established that living drugs can be curative when three favorable conditions coincide: a uniform, dispensable target (for example, CD19 or BCMA) with high antigen density; an anatomically permissive milieu where cells circulate with minimal physical barriers; and product designs that achieve durable persistence without prohibitive toxicity. These conditions rarely hold in epithelial cancers. Solid tumors fragment targets across subclones, downregulate antigen presentation, and impose stromal, vascular, and metabolic obstacles that limit intratumoral accumulation and fitness. What transfers from hematology is the principle not the playbook: precise antigen engagement, memory-rich products, and rational control of activation remain essential, but they must be embedded within strategies that secure access and defend function inside hostile tissue. Hematologic success should therefore be treated as calibration of what T cells can achieve, while acknowledging that solid tumors require additional modules and sequence-aware deployment.

Box 2 | Manufacturing contrasts and convergence: from bespoke to scalable

TIL and CAR-T manufacturing begin from different inputs and impose different constraints. TIL programs rely on surgical harvest, outgrowth from tumor fragments, and rapid expansion steps that can select for tumor-experienced repertoires but introduce variability in yield, timing, and phenotype. Regimen intensity (lymphodepletion and IL-2) historically compensated for these uncertainties. CAR-T programs begin with peripheral blood, enabling predictable yields and standardized ex vivo engineering, but must add homing and breadth to overcome solid-tumor barriers. Vein-to-vein time has narrowed for both yet remains decisive for patients with fast-moving disease.

Convergence is underway. “Young TIL” protocols and closed-system expansions preserve stem-like states and shorten timelines; gene edits (for example, PD-1 or TGF-β resistance) and added homing receptors reduce reliance on toxic systemic support. CAR-T manufacture increasingly prioritizes short culture, memory bias, switchable payloads, and logic circuits with prespecified control. Across platforms, quality is shifting from bulk cell numbers toward fitness metrics (phenotype, transcriptional state, mitochondrial health) and function under stress (hypoxia, low glucose, suppressive cytokines). Scalability will depend on modular, GMP-compatible unit operations; explicit comparability frameworks for complex edits; and point-of-care or regional production networks that reduce logistics as a barrier to care. The shared destination is standardized, controllable products tuned for the gauntlet they will face.

Box 3 | Regulation, economics, and equity: making convergence a clinical reality

For the convergent paradigm to move from framework to practice, the field must solve three linked problems regulatory science, economic viability, and equitable access. Regulators should require concise design-intent dossiers for modular therapies that specify how each element (logic gates, inducible payloads, adapters) addresses access, recognition, or persistence, alongside explicit control plans (suicide switch, dose–response limits, adapter ceilings). Comparability should emphasize fitness and function under stress rather than surface phenotype; release testing should standardize memory state, metabolic resilience, and response to inhibitory cues, and multicenter guidance should cover regional delivery devices, compartment-specific safety monitoring, and tissue pharmacodynamics. These guardrails enable sustainable economics: shortened, automated, regional or point-of-care manufacturing to shrink costs and vein-to-vein time, with reimbursement models that recognize front-loaded expense and durable benefit. Equity then follows from design trial networks that include community sites, streamlined apheresis and follow-up, and real-world registries that track safety and barrier movement, not response alone underpinned by international quality-by-design standards and rapid sharing of rare toxicities.
