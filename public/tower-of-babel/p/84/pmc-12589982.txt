Diet, nutrition, and healthy aging: Are miRNAs the link? A narrative review
Authors: Beatriz B Elliott, Xavier Vazquez, Sharon A Ross, Elizabeth M McNeill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroRNAs are a class of small, single-stranded, noncoding RNA molecules that regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level. Cellular and circulating microRNA expression alterations have been observed in non-pathological aging and age-related diseases. microRNAs have been proposed to regulate aging signaling pathways, including cell death and senescence, oxidative stress, DNA damage, nutrient-sensing, and other metabolic processes. MicroRNAs may provide a molecular mechanism whereby diet can regulate gene expression, affecting aging phenotypes and lifespan. Herein, we review the recent evidence for diet in modulating the expression of microRNAs to influence the aging process. Current challenges and approaches to studying microRNAs and their function in the context of diet and aging research are highlighted in this review. Diet-mediated regulation of microRNA in aging is an emerging area of study, and future research incorporating functional analyses of dietary-responsive microRNAs will be necessary to clarify their actions in the aging process.Keywords: Small RNA, Caloric restriction, Intermittent fasting, Extracellular vesicle, Epigenetics, Microbiome

Introduction

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small, single-stranded, noncoding RNA molecules comprising 18–25 nucleotides, synthesized through a tightly regulated sequential process. These molecules function in post-transcriptional gene regulation, modulating protein expression by binding to a complementary region of target mRNAs, which can lead to changes in cellular function and phenotype. This binding, typically mediated by the miRNA-induced silencing complex (miRNARISC), can be imperfect and results in either mRNA degradation or translational repression [1,2]. Through this mechanism, miRNAs influence a wide range of biological processes, including development, stress responses, and aging, by fine-tuning the expression of genes involved in these pathways. miRNAs were initially identified as regulators of developmental timing in Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) [3,4]. Since then, research has demonstrated that these short noncoding RNAs are conserved across many species due to sequence similarity [5]. The significance of miRNAs is underscored by their estimated involvement in regulating approximately 60% of protein-coding genes in humans [6].

Given their regulatory roles, miRNAs may provide a mechanistic link between diet, gene expression, and aging phenotypes. Several nutrients have been shown to modulate miRNA expression in vitro [[7], [8], [9], [10]], affecting key cellular processes such as apoptosis, inflammation, lipid metabolism, and cell migration. While the mechanisms underlying diet-induced miRNA regulation remain incompletely understood, they likely involve transcriptional control and epigenetic modulation. Collectively, the evidence suggests that diet can shape physiological phenotypes through miRNA regulation.

miRNAs have been detected in a wide range of biological fluids—including blood, urine, milk, saliva, and cerebrospinal fluid, indicating their role in both intercellular and extracellular communication [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Extracellular miRNAs, in particular, show promise as disease biomarkers [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. These circulating miRNAs are transported through various mechanisms, including cell membrane-derived vesicles (e.g., exosomes and microvesicles), as well as in association with proteins, lipoproteins, and ribonucleoprotein complexes [20]. Evidence suggests that some of these transport pathways involve selective cellular export processes, which may be linked to disease mechanisms [21,22]. The notable stability of extracellular miRNAs is partly attributed to their short length and their association with protective carriers that shield them from circulating ribonucleases [23,24]. For instance, the Ago2 protein provides stability to plasma miRNAs [23]. Much like hormones, miRNAs facilitate communication between distant cells and influence various physiological processes, including processes related to aging.

Each miRNA can regulate multiple mRNA targets, and conversely, individual mRNAs may be regulated by several miRNAs. This multiplicity underscores their extensive regulatory capacity [25]. miRNAs are implicated in critical cellular processes including proliferation, cell cycle control, apoptosis, differentiation, and stem cell maintenance [26,27]. Their expression varies across tissues and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in age-related conditions such as diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and dementia [[28], [29], [30]]. These age-related changes in miRNA expression have spurred interest in their potential use as biomarkers of aging and regulators of aging-related pathways [[31], [32], [33]]. Notably, in the aging brain, miRNAs are involved in regulating genes related to synaptic plasticity, inflammation, neuroprotection, mitochondrial function, and lipid metabolism [[34], [35], [36]].

Beyond the central nervous system, recent research has turned toward the gut microbiome, a diverse ecosystem of microorganisms critical to host immunity and metabolic function. Microbiome composition has been linked to cognitive health, disease resistance, and lower obesity risk [37,38]. As a key modulator of the microbiome, diet can drive microbial changes associated with aging and longevity [38]. Emerging evidence also suggests that diet-derived exogenous miRNAs, or ‘xenomirs,’ may influence host metabolism and gut microbiota composition [39]. While intriguing, these findings remain preliminary and are not the primary focus of this review. Instead, we will be focusing on the overall role of diet. Here, dietary interventions are being defined as purposeful changes in dietary intake, either at the level of overall patterns or specific components, compared to a control diet or group to explore health outcomes. For example, this might involve altering the components or timing of meals in one group, while the control group maintains their usual diet, to evaluate differences in aging-related measures.

Various dietary regimes can lead to changes in miRNA expression when compared to a control diet. These miRNAs are proposed to modulate gut barrier function and intestinal health [38]. For instance, Tarallo et al. observed that vegetarian and vegan diets were associated with the upregulation of miR-435-3p, a metabolism-related miRNA, in stool samples, alongside corresponding shifts in gut microbial taxa [40]. This finding raises the possibility that vegan and vegetarian diets could influence gut microbiome-associated miRNA expression relevant to aging.

This review explores the role of miRNA in aging and how it is influenced by diet. This topic will be discussed in various biological systems, including tissue, blood, the gut microbiome, and extracellular miRNAs. It summarizes current findings and suggests future research directions on miRNAs in relation to aging and diet-related health outcomes.

Search results were restricted to English‑language articles published within the last ten years. We included both animal and human studies to capture mechanistic insights and clinical relevance. To remain focused, we selected studies that specifically assessed how diet can modulate miRNA expression during aging, with particular emphasis on extracellular vesicle–associated miRNAs and gut microbiome–derived miRNAs. We excluded reviews, in vitro–only experiments, non‑dietary interventions, and studies lacking adequate methodological detail. Full‑text access was obtained via Iowa State University library resources. Finally, we hand‑searched reference lists of all included articles to ensure comprehensive coverage of recent and significant findings. This narrative review synthesizes current evidence without adhering to the formal protocol of a systematic review.

Caloric intervention

This section will focus on energy-related dietary strategies that involve quantitative changes in energy intake, often without altering the types of foods consumed. Dietary restriction (DR), which includes various forms of reduced food intake, has been shown to increase lifespan and prevent age-related diseases in organisms ranging from yeast to mice and may also be an intervention to slow the rate of aging in humans [41]. A well-studied form of DR is caloric restriction (CR), defined as reduced caloric intake without malnutrition. Therefore, it is not surprising that studies have also examined the ability of CR to modulate miRNA to influence aging phenotypes [42,43]. Caloric restriction

CR has been shown to extend both mean and maximal lifespan across multiple species [44,45]. A variety of biological mechanisms are implicated in this lifespan extension, including epigenetic and transcriptional modifications. Notably, CR influences gene expression through mechanisms such as chromatin remodeling, altered mRNA transcription and processing, and the regulation of mRNA translation via miRNAs [46,47]. CR-induced changes in miRNA expression profiles have been linked to numerous aging-associated signaling pathways (Fig. 2) [48,49]. Investigations in model organisms, including Caenorhabditis elegans, rodents, and rhesus monkeys, support the role of CR-responsive miRNAs in modulating key physiological processes relevant to aging [[50], [51], [52]]. Several CR-responsive miRNAs are implicated in age-related processes and pathologies, including apoptosis, cellular senescence, oxidative stress, DNA damage response, nutrient sensing, and metabolic regulation [43,46]. For instance, RNA sequencing analysis of skeletal muscle from young and old rhesus monkeys revealed differential expression of multiple miRNAs with age. miRNAs such as miR-451, miR-144, miR-18a, and miR-15a were upregulated in aged muscle, whereas miR-181a and miR-181b were downregulated [52]. These miRNAs are predicted to be involved in muscle development and inflammation-related disorders. Interestingly, CR mitigated these age-related changes by restoring miR-181b levels and attenuating the increase in miR-451 and miR-144, suggesting a protective role of CR in preserving muscle integrity through miRNA-mediated mechanisms. Additional studies support an anti-inflammatory role for miR-181 within other inflammatory biological models, which reduces expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-8 [53,54]. Although these studies do not include a CR component, they provide mechanistic insight into the anti-inflammatory potential of miR-181. Given that CR is widely believed to exert its beneficial effects, in part, through anti-inflammatory pathways, these findings help reinforce the plausibility that miR-181 may contribute to CR’s ability to attenuate inflammation in aging and would be an interesting area for further research.

Profiling circulating miRNAs in response to CR offers a non-invasive approach to identifying biomarkers of aging-associated phenotypes. Using small RNA sequencing, Dhahbi et al.

analyzed serum from young, aged, and CR-treated aged mice, identifying 48 miRNAs whose age-related expression was reversed by CR [55]. Predicted targets of these miRNAs included genes involved in biosynthesis, apoptosis, and Wnt signaling [56], highlighting a potential role for CR-induced miRNA changes in promoting healthy aging. While numerous miRNAs have been found to be altered by CR in aging models, functional validation of miRNA function remains essential. Determining the molecular mechanisms by which CR alters miRNA expression will be critical for advancing this field. For example, levels of the endoribonuclease

Dicer, required for miRNA processing, are reduced in aged mice and worms but restored with CR, suggesting a regulatory mechanism by which CR modulates miRNA biogenesis [33,57].

Sheng et al. examined the effects of CR on cardiac aging in mice initiated at different life stages [58]. Researchers found that while CR reversed age-associated cardiac phenotypes such as hypertrophy and fibrosis in middle-aged and old mice, it unexpectedly worsened these phenotypes in young mice. CR also modulated miRNAs involved in fibrosis and inflammation, including upregulation of miR-146a-5p and miR-146b-5p in older mice, suggesting age-specific effects of CR on cardiac health mediated by miRNA regulation. Bioinformatic predictions further suggested that CR-regulated miRNAs target key aging-related genes, including AMPK and FOXO, which work together to protect the aging heart by reducing oxidative stress and promoting cellular resilience, with differential effects across life stages [58].

CR also mitigates age-related metabolic dysfunction, including insulin resistance. In subcutaneous white adipose tissue of male mice, Corrales et al. found that CR prevented the age-related downregulation of miRNAs, including miR-29a-3p, -30a-5p, -103-3p, -107-3p, and 126a-3p, which impairs insulin signaling [59]. These findings suggest that CR maintains youthful miRNA expression profiles in adipose tissue, preserving insulin sensitivity.

In the liver, Ashiqueali et al. observed that CR downregulated miR-34a-5p in both wild-type and Ames Dwarf mice, a long-lived model, correlating with improved lipid metabolism gene expression [60]. Genes such as Sirt1, Pparg, Acc, and Cpt1α were upregulated, highlighting the role of CR and miRNAs in modulating metabolic homeostasis.

Long-term CR further supports miRNA regulation in the context of aging. Using microarray analysis, Makwana et al. found that 30% CR over two years in female mice upregulated miR-125a-5p in the liver and downregulated its targets (Stat3, Casp2, Stard13), genes implicated in aging-related pathways, including insulin resistance, cell death, and inflammatory response [46]. Interestingly, Stat3, Casp2, and Stard13 were significantly downregulated, while miR-125a-5p was upregulated with age in the liver when comparing young and old mice. This evidence is indicative of a possible mechanism by which CR mediates aging symptoms, specifically through miR-125a-5p. In contrast, deep sequencing of plasma miRNAs comparing control-diet and CR diets revealed that CR reduced plasma miR-125a-5p levels in rhesus monkeys, where it positively correlated with insulin sensitivity [61]. This divergence may reflect differences in systemic signaling pathways, such as those mediated by extracellular vesicles (EVs), rather than direct tissue-specific regulation. Moreover, inherent species-specific variations in metabolic rate and insulin signaling may further influence how CR modulates insulin sensitivity in mice versus rhesus monkeys. These system- and species-specific differences emphasize the need for context-aware interpretations in miRNA studies.

In addition to examining the effects of CR alone, research has also investigated how CR modulates the effects of a high-fat/obesogenic diet. In rats previously fed a high-fat diet,

CR reversed the expression of pro-inflammatory miR-19b and upregulated the anti-inflammatory miR-181a, which targets Tnf mRNA [62]. This post-transcriptional regulation highlights a mechanism by which CR ameliorates inflammation and metabolic impairment. In a human study, a 28-day CR intervention in overweight men upregulated serum levels of skeletal muscle-specific miRNAs (myomiRs), including miR-133a and miR-133b [63]. A composite myomiR score correlated with reduced whole-body protein synthesis, suggesting that these circulating miRNAs may serve as noninvasive markers of skeletal muscle adaptation and aging.

Proteostasis, the process of maintaining the proper balance of proteins, which is critical for longevity, may also be modulated by CR via miRNA signaling. Zhang et al. identified miR-122 as the most upregulated miRNA in the liver and liver mitochondria of mice after 12 weeks of CR [64]. Overexpression of miR-122 enhanced mitochondrial protein translation and upregulated

UPRmt effectors like HSP60 and ClpP. Knockdown experiments confirmed miR-122’s role in CR-induced mitochondrial proteostasis, although the contribution of cytosolic miRNAs remains to be explored.

CR has also been studied with a focus on identifying patterns in extracellular miRNAs. EVs are small, membrane-bound nanoparticles that originate from recipient cells and can be found in most body fluids. An important function of EVs is their ability to communicate with other cells via their inner cargo. EVs carry cargo such as cytokines, mRNA fragments, non-coding RNAs, miRNAs, and proteins [65]. EVs are engulfed by target cells through processes like receptor-mediated endocytosis, depending on the type of recipient cell. Once taken in, one important mechanism is that EVs release their contents, which can be used by the target cell to influence physiological processes [65]. As introduced earlier in this review, there is evidence that suggests CR increases longevity through changes in miRNA expression.

Understanding the role of EVs and their bioavailability prompts the question of whether CR can affect miRNA expression within EVs, potentially contributing to the anti-aging effects of CR.

A study conducted on young (7-month), old (22-month), and old-CR rats investigated how a 40% CR affects the expression of miRNAs in serum exosomes, a subtype of EVs, and the role that specific miRNAs (miR-500-3p and miR-770-3p) play in aging using qPCR [66]. It was found that miR-500-3p and miR-770-3p were upregulated in old rats and were downregulated in the old-CR rats. Analysis of their associated pathways revealed involvement in Wnt signaling, cytokine-mediated inflammation, and other key signaling processes. These findings suggest that CR may alleviate physiological symptoms commonly associated with aging, such as inflammation and metabolic dysfunction, through miRNA-mediated pathways, potentially contributing to increased longevity. Further research is needed to assess whether the downregulation of age-related exosomal miRNAs, specifically miR-500-3p and miR-770-3p, increases longevity.

EV miRNA expression has also been evaluated in age-related renal disease. Renal interstitial fibrosis is a condition found in an aging kidney and is caused by epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT). MiR-21 is a miRNA found in EV’s that has been associated with the onset of age-related renal fibrosis. In a study done by Liu et al., the expression of EV miR-21 levels was assessed in young (3 months old), old (24 months old), and old-CR rats who were on a 30% CR diet [67]. It was found that miR-21 was significantly downregulated in the CR rats and significantly upregulated in rats that had high amounts of glucose in their bloodstream. The researchers then assessed specific proteins (PPAR α and HIF-1α) associated with renal fibrosis to measure their relationship with miR-21. PPARα is a protein that has been linked to delaying the onset of EMT while HIF-1α promotes it [67]. PPARα delays the onset of EMT by silencing HIF1α, and these researchers found that HIF-1α had higher concentrations while PPARα was suppressed in aging rat kidneys. In contrast, in old-CR rats, levels of HIF-1α and miR-21 were decreased while levels of PPARα were increased significantly when compared to the old rats [67]. While these findings are promising, further mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether the observed changes in EV miRNA expression directly contribute to the antiaging effects of CR.

These findings clarify how CR influences the expression of EV miRNAs associated with aging, highlighting their potential as targets for interventions that promote healthy aging. While the current evidence underscores a complex relationship between CR, miRNA regulation, and age-related physiology, further research is needed to elucidate the specific molecular pathways involved and to determine how these mechanisms operate across different tissues and biological systems. Such insights could inform the development of CR-mimetic strategies for human aging.

Intermittent fasting

Given the challenges in maintaining long-term CR, such as reduced adherence and potential nutritional deficiencies, alternative approaches like IF have gained attention. Many feeding protocols (e.g., fasting length and periodicity).

have been utilized to mimic CR’s benefits on aging and lifespan without its austerity and continue as a topic of research interest. Protocols for IF can include periodic fasting; time-restricted eating (TRE), where the daily feeding window is reduced, to say, within 6 h; and alternate-day fasting (ADF), which involves cycling between fasting on one day and then eating the next day [68,69]. Honjoh and colleagues have shown that ADF compared to ad libitum feeding, extends the lifespan of C. elegans and have also explored molecular mechanisms for their observations, including changes in gene expression [70]. This same laboratory has characterized the involvement of miRNA in fasting-induced longevity in C. elegans [41]. IF through ADF upregulated the expression of the miRNA-induced silencing complex (miRISC) components, such as Argonaute and the miRNA-processing enzyme Drosha/drsh-1. Experimental knockdown or knock-out of miRISC components also reduced the observed IF-induced longevity. Moreover, they reported that the expression levels of several miRNAs changed after 48 h of fasting starting on day 2 of the adult stage C. elegans [41]. These results suggest the importance of the miRNA synthesis machinery in fasting-induced changes in gene expression and IF-induced longevity in this model. Due to the well-established evolutionary conservation of miRNA processing genes between nematodes and mammals [41], these findings may offer translational insights into human health span modulation.

Studies on IF have also been conducted on humans. In a pilot study, Saini et al. found that a 4week TRE regimen in older overweight adults significantly altered the expression of 14 circulating miRNAs (hsa-miR-623, -4303, -7162-3p, -411-5p, -5682, and -4513 were upregulated, while hsa-4649-5p, -2467-3p, -543, -301a-3p, -3132, -19a-5p, -495-3p, and -47613p were downregulated) [71]. These changes were associated with pathways involved in autophagy, insulin signaling, and mTOR regulation, suggesting that IF may influence aging-related molecular pathways through miRNA modulation [71]. This pilot study provides evidence that a short-term TRE regimen can alter circulating miRNA profiles in older overweight adults and potentially influence cell growth and survival pathways, contributing to TRE's health benefits, including weight loss and improved metabolic health.

Another study investigated how IF regimens can modulate energy metabolism. For example, a fed-fast-refed protocol in mice was used as a tool to discover the importance of miRNA regulation of metabolic and energetic signaling through SIRT1 and PGC1-α during the fast-fed transition [72]. The authors found that hepatic regulatory fasting/fed miRNAs were dysregulated in aging, but more research is needed.

From a practical point of view, adherence to IF is potentially more promising than CR in the human setting, suggesting that additional studies are warranted to understand the mechanisms of miRNA-mediated IF-induced longevity.

Diet overview

Different diets have been studied to better understand the molecular mechanisms related to lifespan extension and aging. Both interventional and observational studies have been conducted to link types of diet, aging, and the role of miRNAs. This section will discuss studies carried out in both mouse models and humans.

Extracellular miRNAs have been studied in the context of diet in observational studies. A study investigating how lifestyle factors, including diet (established through the food frequency questionnaire), influence circulating miRNA expression in healthy adults found interesting results. High consumption of vegetables, fruits, and whole grains, compared to low consumption, was associated with increased levels of miR-let-7a and miR-328, implicated in anti-inflammatory pathways, and decreased levels of pro-inflammatory miR-21 [73]. The researchers observed a sex-specific increase in miR-155 expression in aging females, potentially influencing autoimmune-related pathways. Results from this study highlight the importance of diet in aging-related miRNA expression and other biological factors, including sex. These sex-specific differences in miRNA expression underscore the need for personalized approaches in aging research and nutrition science, advancing the field toward more targeted and equitable interventions.

Building on these findings, an additional population-based study has explored how specific diets, such as vegetarianism, influence circulating miRNA profiles associated with aging and immune function. In the Adventist Health Study-2 (AHS-2), which includes a longlived population of Adventists in California, Liu et al. identified differentially expressed circulating miRNAs between vegetarians and non-vegetarians. These miRNAs were associated with immune regulation, cytokine signaling, cell growth, and age-related diseases such as cardiovascular and neurodegenerative conditions [74,75]. One example of a relevant upregulated miRNA was miR-29a, which has been previously associated with aging-related disease, including its downregulation in individuals with Alzheimer’s disease. These findings suggest that a vegetarian diet may modulate aging-associated miRNAs, offering molecular insight into the health span benefits observed in this population.

In contrast to observational studies in humans, experimental dietary interventions in animal models provide mechanistic insights into how specific diets, such as high-fat intake, modulate miRNA expression across the lifespan. Cho et al. investigated the impact of a high-fat diet on adipose tissue-derived EV miRNAs in male mice [76]. Animals were fed either a standard control diet or a 60% high-fat diet for 10–12 weeks (young cohort) or 50–61 weeks (middle-aged cohort). The study revealed that middle-aged mice maintained an elevated weight and adiposity regardless of diet, while young mice fed a high-fat diet developed metabolic traits similar to those of middle-aged mice. In the control diet group, 131 differentially expressed miRNAs were identified in middle-aged mice compared to young mice, including miR-21, miR-148a, and miR-29a, miRNAs previously linked to cancer, neuropsychological disorders, and reproductive diseases. In the high-fat diet group, 55 DE-miRNAs were found in middle-aged mice, with many associated with suppressed insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) activity, a key regulator of metabolic homeostasis. Future research should explore whether dietary interventions or targeted miRNA interventions can mitigate these effects, potentially offering therapeutic strategies to address obesity-related aging complications.

An interesting comparison of multiple dietary interventions was performed to characterize lifespan-related genes and pathways at different molecular layers, including coding, noncoding, and epigenetics [77]. The study analyzed liver samples from midlife male C57BL/6 J mice subjected to six lifespan-altering interventions from early life, including CR, low-fat diet, dietary restriction, high-fat diet, high-fat diet with exercise, and Western diet. RNA-seq profiling revealed that dietary interventions modulated liver miRNA expression, identifying lifespan-associated miRNAs targeting chromatin remodelers like Chd1 [77]. Lifespan-extending diets, including CR, low-fat diet, and dietary restriction, were associated with repression of specific miRNA-chromatin remodeler interactions. The authors suggest that this repression may minimize dysregulated transcription induced by aging and lifespan-shortening diets.

The Walnuts and Healthy Aging (WAHA) study evaluated the effects of a year-long walnuts-supplemented diet in older adults [78]. The intervention group received walnuts comprising 15% of daily energy intake. Blood-derived circulating miRNAs were screened using a miRNome PCR panel, revealing 20 differentially expressed miRNAs. Two, hsa-miR-32-5p and hsa-miR-29b-3p, were validated by qPCR. Bioinformatics analysis implicated hsa-miR-32-5p in transcriptional regulation, cell proliferation, and neural development, while hsa-miR-29b-3p was linked to integrin signaling, Alzheimer’s disease, apoptosis, and inflammatory pathways.

Research on dietary interventions has primarily focused on extracellular miRNAs, which play a crucial role in intercellular communication and influence diet-related aging effects. Future studies should clarify the transport mechanisms of these miRNAs, whether they are associated with EVs, lipoproteins, or ribonucleoprotein complexes. For EV-specific research, including exosomes, functional assays involving cells from young animals exposed to EVs from older individuals can help evaluate changes in inflammatory, senescence, or metabolic markers. Current mouse studies indicate that liver and adipose tissue are promising targets for validating extracellular miRNA functions. Further insights can be gained with miRNA mimics or inhibitors (e.g., antagomiRs) to investigate downstream targets and signaling pathways.

Nutrient intake

Several nutrients have been found to modulate the expression of miRNAs in vitro [10]. The findings suggest that nutrient intake regulates miRNAs that are involved in a host of biological functions, such as apoptosis, inflammation, lipid metabolism, and cell migration. The mechanism of this nutrient regulation of miRNA expression is currently unknown but may include transcription factor regulation, epigenetic modulation, and other actions. The findings suggest that nutrients and their metabolites may modify phenotype by regulating miRNA expression, but functional activity is needed to verify these possibilities. This section will discuss the current knowledge on how nutrient intake, particularly of macronutrients and micronutrients, may be modulating health and aging through miRNAs.

Macronutrients

Research on macronutrients and their relationship to aging, particularly through the lens of miRNA modulation, remains limited, though emerging studies are beginning to shed light on this interaction. Aging is associated with increased adiposity and a heightened risk of metabolic disorders [79]. A recent retrospective study examined how dietary fatty acids (DFAs) influence miRNA expression in the context of metabolic dysfunction [80]. Human trials have shown that omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) modulate miRNA profiles linked to lipid metabolism and inflammation. Complementary animal studies demonstrated that diets high in saturated fatty acids (SFAs) upregulate pro-inflammatory miRNAs, while PUFA-rich diets downregulate them [81].

Further investigations have explored how different types of DFAs, saturated, unsaturated (UFAs), and their combinations, affect miRNA expression, with many of the identified targets implicated in lipid metabolism [82,83]. For instance, miR-96 is upregulated in hepatocytes treated with palmitate and in the livers of mice fed a high-fat diet, where it plays a role in insulin signaling and glucose metabolism [81]. Similarly, unsaturated fatty acids have been shown to promote hepatic steatosis via upregulation of miR-21, which regulates the PTEN signaling pathway. This pattern was observed in both UFA-treated hepatocytes and the livers of rats on high-fat diets and is consistent with elevated miR-21 expression in liver biopsies from obese human patients with steatosis [82].

Given the diversity of models and methodologies used in these studies, further research is needed to clarify the therapeutic potential of specific macronutrients in aging-related metabolic health. For example, PUFAs, found in fish and plant sources, have been shown to extend lifespan and reduce reactive oxygen species production in Drosophila melanogaster [83]. miRNAs involved in metabolic and oxidative stress pathways also exhibit differential expression in aging models [42,43,84], suggesting a promising avenue for future studies to investigate how polyunsaturated fats influence longevity and miRNA regulation across species.

Micronutrients

Micronutrients are crucial for maintaining physiological function and cellular health, especially as we age. Recent studies indicate that these nutrients can impact aging-related processes by modulating microRNA (miRNA) expression, revealing important molecular mechanisms. This section examines how specific micronutrients affect miRNA profiles and what this means for muscle function, cognitive health, and disease risk.

To explore the relevance of circulating exosome-derived miRNAs in age-related disease, Boccardi et al. analyzed vitamin E isoforms, cytokines, and miRNA profiles in older adults with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) compared to healthy controls (HC) [85]. AD patients exhibited lower α-tocopherol levels and altered expression of IL-8, INF-α2, G-CSF, IL-17, GM-CSF, and IL-3. miRNA profiling revealed reduced levels of miR-29b, miR-21, miR-132, miR-9, and miR-122 in AD patients. These miRNAs are associated with pathways involved in inflammation, atherosclerosis, and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Notably, miR-122 levels correlated positively with pro-inflammatory cytokines elevated in the AD group.

These findings underscore the potential of nutrients such as vitamin E, to influence molecular mechanisms underlying age-related diseases. Future research should explore whether targeted nutritional interventions can modulate miRNA expression to reduce inflammation and improve cognitive outcomes in aging populations. Longitudinal studies and controlled trials incorporating dietary assessments and miRNA profiling may help clarify the therapeutic potential of diet in neurodegenerative disease prevention and management.

Nutritional adequacy, defined as sufficient intake of essential nutrients, including the adequacy of the B vitamin folate, contributes to molecular pathways involved in successful aging. Several mechanisms of action for folate in health, aging, and disease have been characterized and include one-carbon metabolism and methylation reactions [86,87], but other activities are emerging, which include the actions of miRNA. Folate and folate status has been shown to alter miRNA profiles, and miRNAs have also been found to influence the activity of folate-dependent pathways [88]. miRNAs are also being investigated for their utility as biomarkers in the diagnosis or prognosis of (age-related) conditions associated with folate status. For example, serum miR-21 has been proposed as a biomarker for adenomatous polyps and colorectal cancer [89]. Beckett et al. further demonstrated that circulating miR-21 levels are associated with folate status, indicating that folate may modulate miR-21 expression. This connection supports the potential role of miRNAs like miR-21 as biomarkers of both disease risk and nutrient-responsive biological effects, particularly in aging-related conditions involving miRNA modulation [90].

Another nutrient involved in methylation reactions, as a methyl donor, is choline which has been studied in conjunction with folate in some model systems to explore disease and miRNA relationships. Choline has been implicated in aging, including neuronal function, and in fact, a lifelong regimen of choline at four times the recommended daily intake (RDI) improved cognitive deficits and reduced activated microglia in female AD mice [91]. Further research is needed to investigate how choline affects miRNAs in aging, particularly regarding cognitive decline, neuroinflammation, and cellular senescence. Longitudinal studies or clinical trials evaluating the impact of choline supplementation on miRNA expression in aging populations would also be valuable.

Zinc is an essential trace element in many essential activities, including growth and development, protein and DNA synthesis, neurosensory functions, cell-mediated immunity, thyroid, and bone metabolism. These functions are particularly relevant in the context of aging, as older adults often experience a decline in zinc absorption efficiency alongside marginal dietary intake [92]. In fact, moderate deficiency of Zn has been observed in older subjects in several populations [93]. This combination can contribute to the suboptimal zinc status in aging populations, as evidenced by moderate zinc deficiency observed in older individuals.

Although the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of moderate zinc deficiency remain incompletely understood, emerging evidence suggests that miRNAs may play a mediating role.

One study has identified specific miRNAs that respond to Zn intake, revealing a molecular effect that Zn deficiency has. Ryu and colleagues reported that serum miRNAs were among the zinc-responsive circulating biomarkers observed by dietary zinc restriction and repletion studies in men 21–35 years of age [94]. Such findings suggest that miRNAs may be biomarkers of nutrient status in addition to their function in regulating gene translation.

Vitamin D is essential in maintaining circulating calcium homeostasis and bone health. In addition, vitamin D influences several biological effects, including other pathways often disrupted in aging, such as inflammatory pathways. Many of the actions of vitamin D work through the vitamin D receptor (VDR)-vitamin D response elements (VDRE) to regulate gene expression [95]. The molecular mechanisms of vitamin D in this regulatory context may also involve miRNAs.

In an exploratory study by Ferrero et al., investigators analyzed plasma samples from 120 healthy individuals with different diets (vegan, vegetarian, and omnivorous) to examine associations between daily nutrient intake, including vitamin D, and circulating miRNA expression [96]. Daily vitamin D intake was found to correlate with the expression of several circulating miRNAs, including negatively correlated miR-1277-5p and miR-144-3p. The in-silico analysis of gene targets for these miRNAs revealed functional enrichment in pathways related to steroid hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, specifically including estrogen and androgen signaling [96]. Modulation of these hormone-related pathways by vitamin D–associated miRNAs may have implications for endocrine function, and age-related hormonal changes, highlighting a potential mechanistic link between nutrient intake and hormonal regulation in healthy aging.

Beckett and colleagues examined the relationship between vitamin D intake (using food frequency and supplement questionnaires) and circulating let-7b expression by qPCR in 200 older adult participants. Correlations between vitamin D intake and circulating levels of let-7a-5p and let-7b-5p, known tumor suppressors [97], were observed. The correlation with let-7a-5p and let-7b-5p was related to specific polymorphisms at the vitamin D receptor (VDR) coding gene. This study highlights the importance of exploring the influence of underlying genotypic variance in miRNA expression studies and understanding individual responses to vitamin D [97]. miRNA expression has also been evaluated in the context of vitamin D status in a cohort of older adult postmenopausal women aged 50–80 years [98]. In this study, participants were classified into two groups based on bone mineral density (BMD): a control group with normal BMD (T-score ≥ −1.0) and an osteoporotic group (T-score ≤ −2.5). Participants were then randomized to receive either calcium/vitamin D supplementation or a placebo. The study investigated associations among vitamin D levels, stress hormones, and circulating miRNAs. Notably, miR-148a was significantly upregulated and miR-122-5p significantly downregulated in the osteoporotic group compared to controls. Both miRNAs showed significant correlations with vitamin D status: miR148a was negatively correlated, while miR-122-5p was positively correlated with serum vitamin D levels. [98]. These findings suggest that altered expression of miR-148a and miR-122-5p may reflect both bone health status and vitamin D levels, supporting their potential utility as biomarkers in osteoporosis risk assessment among aging women.

Given age-related changes in nutrient absorption and metabolism, further research is needed to determine whether circulating miRNAs can reliably reflect micronutrient status, such as zinc, in older adults, potentially serving as early biomarkers of deficiency. Additional nutrients like vitamin E, resistant starch, medium-chain triglycerides, vitamin K2, and selenium have all been implicated in aging and warrant future focus [[99], [100], [101], [102], [103]]. Longitudinal and controlled studies incorporating dietary assessments and miRNA profiling may clarify whether targeted nutritional interventions can reduce inflammation, improve cognitive outcomes, and help prevent neurodegenerative disease. Ultimately, elucidating the molecular functions of nutrients may advance personalized nutrition strategies for healthy aging, with miRNAs as both biomarkers and potential targets for dietary modulation.

The microbiome

The relationship between health, the microbiome, and miRNAs is a topic of great interest. However, studies on the influence of diet on miRNA expression and activity within the gut microbiome are limited, especially in the aging context [[104], [105], [106]]. This section includes evidence that elucidates the potential role of diet in aging through miRNAs. For example, a one-year consumption of a Mediterranean diet intervention reduced frailty and inflammation by modulating gut microbiota in older adults, compared to age-matched individuals who maintained their usual eating habits without dietary intervention. This was linked to lower levels of inflammatory markers (hsCRP, IL-17) and higher levels of anti-inflammatory markers (adiponectin, sGP130) [104]. These results indicate that a Mediterranean diet can positively influence the gut microbiome, alleviating aging-related symptoms by ameliorating inflammatory pathways. Although authors did not profile miRNA expression, given the known crosstalk between microbial metabolites and miRNA-regulated pathways involved in inflammation and aging, further insight into miRNA expression may elucidate a potential mechanism by which the Mediterranean diet can benefit the gut microbiota.

Some studies have evaluated miRNA expression in this context. For example, a study explored the interplay between diet quality, defined as the overall alignment of an individual's dietary intake with health-promoting principles, and gut microbiota composition, miRNA expression, and the risk of cognitive impairment among middle-aged and older adult individuals [105]. Researchers assessed diet quality and cognitive function and analyzed gut microbiota and miRNA. A higher risk of mild cognitive impairment was significantly associated with a more pro-inflammatory diet. Results showed elevated levels of hsa-let-7g-5p, hsa-miR-107, and hsamiR-186-3p in the serum of individuals with mild cognitive impairment [105]. Additionally, elevated miRNAs showed correlations with an increased abundance of Proteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria. Results from this study suggest that better diet quality, such as a less proinflammatory diet, is associated with a reduced risk of mild cognitive impairment and is potentially mediated by favorable gut microbiota composition and miRNA expression.

Li and colleagues investigated similar questions by examining the relationship between dietary fatty acid intake, cognitive function, gut microbiota, and circulating miRNAs in overweight and obese middle-aged and older adults [106]. Participants were grouped by BMI and assessed for diet, cognitive performance, and biological markers. Overweight and obese individuals had significant cognitive decline compared to the normal weight individuals. The higher intake of α-linolenic acid (C18:3n-3), an omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA), was positively correlated with cognitive decline, while the intake of γ-linolenic acid (C18:3n-6), an omega-6 PUFA, was negatively associated with cognitive decline [106]. hsa-miR-142-5p was elevated in both overweight and obese groups and negatively correlated with cognitive function in the overweight group [106]. In the obese group, this miRNA was also linked to altered gut microbiota, including increased Fusobacteria and Proteobacteria. These findings underscore the importance of diet in cognitive function and suggest that targeting dietary fat intake, miRNA expression, and/or gut microbiota could be a potential strategy for preventing cognitive decline in overweight and obese populations.

miRNA expression and the gut microbiome have also been studied in the context of periodic fasting. Fifty-one healthy adults were placed on five consecutive days of supervised fasting following Buchinger fasting guidelines [107]. Researchers assessed pre- and post-fasting blood samples for gene expression analysis, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) content, and stool samples for microbiota composition. Metabolic results included a significant increase in βhydroxybutyrate (BHB) levels, which the authors discussed as being indicative of enhanced ketogenesis, and upregulation of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase isoform 4 (PDK4), which suggests the favoring of fat utilization, a process that increases in aging [107]. miRNA profiling revealed elevated levels of miR-let-7b-5p, which is associated with metabolic regulation, and reduced levels of miR-34a-5p, a miRNA linked to aging, mitochondrial function, and cellular senescence. The downregulation of miR-34a-5p may reflect improved mitochondrial health and reduced age-related transcriptional activity. Additionally, Christensenella, a gut microbe previously associated with longevity, was found to increase post-fasting and showed an inverse correlation with age and body mass index. A significant correlation was also observed between SIRT1, a key regulator of aging and mitochondrial function (and a known target of miR-34a-5p), and the abundance of Prevotella and Lactobacillus in the gut microbiota [107]. These findings suggest that short-term periodic fasting may induce beneficial metabolic, molecular, and microbial changes that support healthy aging. Further research is warranted to explore the mechanistic links between miR-34a-5p, SIRT1, and gut microbiota composition in aging populations.

Although most recent studies are exploring the role of diet on miRNAs in the microbiome with a focus on aging-related disease [104,108], further research is needed to evaluate the influence of different types of diet, such as a high-fat or Mediterranean diet, on miRNA-microbiome interactions related to aging in a healthy population. Beyond screening for differentially expressed miRNAs in relation to diet using profiling methods, these studies should include evaluation of miRNA interactions, including expression target analysis and functional analysis, along with functional studies confirming the interaction between differentially expressed miRNAs and their targets.

Summary and conclusion

This review underscores the growing body of evidence linking diet to changes in miRNA expression as an emerging mechanism that may influence mRNA translation, cellular aging, and overall longevity. These miRNA-driven changes have the potential to shape pathways involved in healthy aging and the development of age-related diseases.

We’ve highlighted key studies demonstrating how diet modulates miRNA expression across various biological systems and experimental models. While the evidence supporting the role of diet in modulating miRNA expression and influencing aging is growing, particularly in response to CR, a consistent set of miRNAs and their networks has yet to be firmly established, indicating the need for deeper investigation.

CR has shown strong effects on miRNA regulation in aging, yet its long-term feasibility in humans remains limited. This opens the door to alternative strategies, such as diet modifications, targeted nutrient intake, or pharmacological approaches that might mimic the beneficial effects of CR on miRNA pathways.

We also explore the interplay between macro- and micronutrient intake, miRNA expression, and aging. Despite encouraging progress, many fundamental questions remain due to the challenges that accompany human studies. Due to advances in the field, we have new tools that will allow us to further inspect those questions, as outlined in Table 1, along with potential research strategies to address them.
