HIV-Stigmatizing Beliefs and Attitudes as a Barrier to Early PrEP Care Continuum Engagement Among People Who Inject Drugs
Authors: Suzan M. Walters, Hyungrok Do, Jessica Jaiswal, Mehrdad Khezri, Roman Ivasiy, Samuel R. Friedman, Danielle C. Ompad, Omar El Shahawy, Sahanah Lim, John A. Schneider, Alida Bouris, Ricky N. Bluthenthal, Valerie A. Earnshaw, Jimi Huh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a highly effective prevention strategy, yet awareness, knowledge, and willingness to use it among people who inject drugs (PWID) remains inadequate despite widespread eligibility. Stigma, particularly HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes, may be a key barrier to engagement at early stages of the PrEP care continuum. We examine how HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes affect PrEP awareness, knowledge, and willingness among PWID. We surveyed 262 HIV-negative PWID in Los Angeles and Denver (2021–2023) and used structural equation modeling to examine associations between HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes (11-item validated scale with α = 0.899 and 1-factor structure) and three early PrEP outcomes: awareness, knowledge, and willingness, while controlling for race/ethnicity, gender, housing status, and conducted sub-analyses on willingness to use long-acting injectable PrEP. HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes were significantly associated with lower PrEP awareness (β − 0.212, p < 0.001) and less accurate knowledge (β − 0.179, p = 0.006). Accurate knowledge was associated with greater willingness to use PrEP (β 0.175, p = 0.027). Black, Indigenous, and Other Persons of Color (BIPOC) participants reported higher HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes than non-Hispanic White participants (β 0.196, p = 0.003). Over half (56%) of participants were willing to take daily oral PrEP once informed, and many were interested in long-acting injectable PrEP. HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes are associated with lower PrEP care continuum engagement among PWID, particularly through limiting awareness and understanding of PrEP. BIPOC participants reported higher levels of stigmatizing attitudes, suggesting that broader structural and intersectional stigma may shape PrEP engagement, consistent with prior research. Interventions to increase PrEP uptake should address both individual- and structural-level stigma and consider leveraging peer networks and community supports to foster resilience and improve equitable access to HIV prevention tools.

Introduction

Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) is an effective HIV prevention method for people who inject drugs (PWID) [1], yet PrEP uptake among PWID remains remarkably low. Studies estimate that 84–95% of PWID meet the United States (U.S.) Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC) indications for PrEP [2, 3], but studies reported only 1–2% of PWID take PrEP [4–6]. Multiple studies have demonstrated low awareness of PrEP among PWID [2, 3, 5–9]. This low awareness essentially halts PWID at the first step of the care continuum, which includes progressive stages of awareness, knowledge, willingness, uptake, and adherence [10]. In addition, when PWID report being aware of PrEP, they often have inaccurate information about what PrEP is, presenting further challenges for PWID to progress from knowledge to willingness, critical steps at the beginning of the care continuum needed for uptake and persistence [8]. However, when provided with correct information about PrEP, many (e.g., 53%–63%) PWID reported being willing to take it [4, 5, 11]. Consistent with this pattern, a systematic review of the PrEP care cascade among PWID documented substantial drop-off across cascade stages, with relatively high willingness but very low PrEP uptake (0–3%), highlighting early-stage gaps as key targets for intervention [12]. Given that HIV prevention among PWID remains a critical public health priority, due to their disproportionate burden of HIV, improving PrEP access and uptake in this population is central to achieving the goals of the U.S. Ending the HIV Epidemic initiative [13].

Stigma is a critical barrier to PrEP care continuum engagement among PWID [8]. Stigma is a social process that results in social devaluation and discrediting, functions at multiple levels of the social-ecological system, and is shaped by multiple intersecting social positions that are tied to systems of oppression and/or privilege [8, 14, 15]. As a social process that allows some groups of people and institutions with more power to stigmatize others [15], stigma is a mechanism that upholds systems of oppression in society by “keeping people down, in and/or away” [16]. A number of studies have found that PWID encounter drug use stigma at multiple social-ecological levels, including in healthcare settings [17–19] and within larger community contexts [20–23]. For example, research has shown that clinicians are less likely to prescribe PrEP to PWID compared to other key populations [24, 25]. Among PWID, experiencing stigma from others (i.e., enacted stigma) can translate to healthcare avoidance [26] and has been associated with increased injection [27, 28] and sexual behaviors [29] that heighten vulnerability to HIV. Increased probability of HIV transmission can also, in part, be attributed to reduced access to protective resources resulting from structural stigma, such as policies that create vulnerabilities to HIV, including laws that criminalize needle and syringe exchange programs [30, 31]. Although previous work has focused on PWID’s experiences of drug use stigma, greater focus on HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes are needed to understand how stigma undermines engagement across the early PrEP care continuum, including PrEP awareness, willingness, and initiation.

Research on substance use stigma among PWID provides important context for this study. Substance use stigma is well documented and has been linked to healthcare avoidance, treatment disengagement, and adverse health outcomes among people who use drugs [17, 22, 30]. However, PWID may both experience stigma related to drug use and simultaneously endorse stigmatizing attitudes toward people living with HIV. While substance use stigma positions PWID as stigma targets, HIV stigma represents a distinct process in which PWID may function as stigma holders, particularly when they are HIV-negative. Intersectional stigma frameworks emphasize how multiple stigma systems can co-occur within individuals and communities [22, 30, 32]. Examining HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes among PWID therefore extends the existing literature by clarifying how HIV stigma operates within this population, separately from drug use stigma, and how it may undermine early engagement in the PrEP care continuum.

HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes can act as a barrier to engaging in the PrEP care continuum [33–35] for a range of key populations, including women [35, 36], African Americans [37], and sexual minority men [34]. Although few quantitative studies have examined the role of HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes on PrEP use among PWID, qualitative studies suggest that HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes play an important role [8, 38]. Among PWID, stigma can take the form of prejudice (emotions), stereotypes (beliefs), and discrimination (behaviors) about HIV and towards people living with HIV [39]. These stigma mechanisms can create barriers to HIV prevention for PWID. For example, HIV prejudice can affect social ties as well as resources and information (e.g., learning (or not) about PrEP), while stereotypes can influence health behaviors like PrEP uptake [40]. To date, relatively little research has been conducted exploring HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes as a pathway to PrEP care continuum outcomes among PWID. In this study, we operationalize HIV stigma as endorsement of stigmatizing attitudes toward HIV and people living with HIV (prejudice/stereotypes), rather than experienced (lived) stigma as a stigma target.

This study examines how stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes toward HIV, particularly stereotypes and prejudice, among PWID are associated with the first three stages of the PrEP care continuum: awareness, knowledge, and willingness. These early stages are foundational precursors to PrEP uptake and subsequent adherence, as individuals must first become aware of PrEP, understand its purpose and benefits, and be willing to consider its use before initiating PrEP and engaging in sustained adherence behaviors [8]. We hypothesize that higher levels of HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes will be associated with lower PrEP awareness, knowledge, and willingness. Moreover, we controlled for the effects of race/ethnicity, gender, and housing status, demographic proxies for broader systems of inequality such as racism, sexism, and status discrimination [41, 42]. Housing instability is especially relevant for PWID, as it compounds exposure to stigma, limits access to healthcare, and increases vulnerability to HIV and other adverse health outcomes [43]. These intersecting structural barriers may uniquely shape stigma experiences and, in turn, hinder engagement at critical early stages of the PrEP continuum for specific sub-groups of PWID [30]. Finally, because the HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes scale was adapted from a version validated in other populations, we also assessed its internal consistency and factor structure to ensure its appropriateness for PWID. By identifying how HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes are influenced by (or associated with) these social identities, this study seeks to inform the design of more equitable and targeted interventions to improve early PrEP engagement.

Methods

Study Design, Participants and Procedures

Data for this analysis were drawn from a larger longitudinal cohort study funded by the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA), titled “Cannabis Use and Health Outcomes Among Opioid-Using People Who Inject Drugs” (R01 DA046049). The parent study employed identical eligibility criteria and recruitment procedures as described above. Participants were recruited through community-based and peer outreach at syringe service programs (SSPs), medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD) clinics, shower and laundry facilities for unhoused individuals, and other public venues commonly frequented by PWID [44] in Los Angeles, CA (three sites) and Denver, CO.

Participants completed a 45-minute computer-assisted personal interview administered via the Questionnaire Design System (NOVA Research, Bethesda, MD), which included 351 survey items previously described in detail [44]. A subsample of 262 participants was additionally administered a supplemental stigma and PrEP-related survey module, introduced during the study period, which included validated measures of HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes, PrEP Knowledge, PrEP Awareness, PrEP Willingness, and Long-Acting Injectable PrEP Interest to assess the role of HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes and related attitudes in early engagement with the PrEP care continuum. Selection into this analytic subsample was based solely on module availability and timing of enrollment, rather than participant characteristics.

Data on willingness to use long-acting injectable PrEP were obtained from a subset of 141 participants who completed the 6-month follow-up visit, at which the long-acting injectable PrEP module was administered. Inclusion in this analytic subsample was therefore determined by study retention and completion of the 6-month assessment, rather than by participant characteristics or recruitment setting.

Eligibility criteria included: (1) age 18 years or older, (2) self-reported HIV-negative status, (3) any opioid use (e.g., prescription opioids, heroin, or fentanyl), and (4) self-reported injection drug use within the past 30 days, verified by visual inspection of injection stigmata (“track marks”) [45]. Individuals currently using PrEP were not excluded due to the low prevalence of use in this population; only 6 participants reported PrEP use. Participants had the opportunity to complete the survey at baseline, 3-month, and 6-month follow-up visits, conducted between April 2021 and June 2023. Each participant received $15 USD for completing the supplemental stigma survey at each wave they took it. For this analysis, we used the baseline data for the main findings presented in the text. Supplementary data on willingness to use long-acting injectable PrEP from the 6-month follow-up are included in Appendix A.

Measures

Sociodemographic covariates collected at baseline included gender (0 = Male, 1 = Female), housing status (0 = Unhoused, 1 = Housed), and race/ethnicity. Due to small cell sizes, we categorized race and ethnicity for the multivariable analyses as Black, Indigenous, and Other People of Color (BIPOC) and Non-Hispanic White (0 = Non-Hispanic White [NHW], 1 = BIPOC).

Endorsed HIV stigmatizing attitudes were measured at baseline using an 11-item scale adapted from Visser et al. [46] to assess stigmatizing attitudes toward people living with HIV, including stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination (for example, moral judgment/blame and interpersonal distancing). Items were drawn from the Visser “personal stigma” scale and presented as statements about people with HIV. Respondents were asked to rate their level of agreement on a 5-point scale with higher scores indicating greater levels of stigma (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Respondents were asked to respond to statements such as “People with HIV should be ashamed of themselves,” “I would not like to be friends with someone with HIV,” and “I think less of someone because they have HIV.” The internal consistency of the scale was high in both the original (α = 0.87) [46] and current study (α = 0.899). Although this scale has been used in prior community-based research, it has been less commonly applied among PWID, and its strong reliability in this sample supports its applicability in this population.

PrEP awareness collected at baseline was assessed through a five-point Likert scale question that asked, “How familiar are you with PrEP?” The response options were (a) “I’d never heard of it before today”; (b) “I have heard about it, but I don’t know what it is”; (c) “I know a little about it”; (d) “I know a fair amount about it”; and (e) “I know a lot about it”, with the lower numerical value corresponding to less awareness.

PrEP knowledge collected at baseline was assessed with 8 items. The questions were “PrEP is an emergency medication that you take right after you have been exposed to HIV,” “PrEP can be given to HIV positive people,” “PrEP also protects against STDs, like gonorrhea and syphilis,” “PrEP also protects against other blood borne diseases such as hepatitis C virus (HCV),” “Most people who take PrEP experience side effects,” “An HIV test is required before PrEP can be started,” “PrEP works best when taken every day,” and “PrEP is a drug that could be useful for people who inject drugs.” The response options included a Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree [47]. Responses were recoded into a dichotomous variable such that correct responses = 1 and all other responses = 0. Cronbach’s alpha for 8 items were 0.855. A composite score was then created by summing the number of correct responses, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 8.

PrEP willingness was assessed at baseline with the item: “Would you be willing to take a once-a-day pill, every day to lower your risk of becoming HIV infected by 90%?” Response options were 1 = Yes, 0 = No, 7 = Don’t Know, 8 = Refuse, 9 = Not Applicable. For analysis, responses were dichotomized (1 = Yes, 0 = No); 7, 8, and 9 were treated as missing [5].

Long Acting Injectable PrEP data was collected at the 6-month follow up survey (n = 141) [48]. Participants were given the following prompt “Scientists are also working to make a different kind of PrEP that would not require taking a pill. Instead, it would involve getting an injection or shot every month or perhaps only every three months. For these next questions, this will be referred to as ‘long-acting injectable’ PrEP. We are assuming that this method of PrEP is just as effective as daily PrEP if taken as prescribed.” We then asked three questions about injectable PrEP. The questions were (a) “How likely is it that you would consider long-acting injectable PrEP (1 injection every one to three months) if it was $75 a month?”; (b) “How likely is it that you would consider long-acting injectable PrEP (1 injection every one to three months) if it was free?”; and (c) “How likely is it that you would consider taking long-acting injectable PrEP (1 injection every one to three months) if it had mild side effects for the first 1–2 weeks following the first shot ONLY such as nausea, cramping and vomiting?” These items were analyzed independently; no composite variable was created, and they were not included in the SEM.

Analysis

We explored the effect of stigma on PrEP awareness, knowledge, and willingness collected in the baseline survey. We limited the analysis to participants who completed the survey and answered questions related to HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes and PrEP at baseline. We first examined descriptive statistics related to socio-demographic characteristics, HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes, and PrEP.

We then conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to fit a single factor measurement model with 11 HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes items. The fit indices for the CFA model were adequate (CFI=0.908, TLI=0.885, RMSEA=0.114). We ran CFA only for the HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes items because this construct is a multidimensional latent scale with a published factor structure [46]; PrEP awareness, knowledge, and willingness were single-item observed measures. Subsequently, we developed a structural equation model (SEM) to examine the association between the HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes latent factor and three observed PrEP-related outcome variables collected at baseline (PrEP awareness, PrEP knowledge, and PrEP willingness), controlling for the effects of sociodemographic covariates, housing, gender, race and ethnicity on HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes. A sub analysis (n = 141) examining willingness to use long-acting injectable PrEP was also conducted. Missing data were handled in Mplus under the MAR assumption. Missingness on latent factor indicators and endogenous outcomes was addressed by WLSMV using pairwise present data, while missingness on exogenous covariates (e.g., gender, displaced, race/ethnicity) resulted in listwise deletion. All reported test statistics (z) and associated p-values were derived from Wald tests as implemented in Mplus. All analyses were conducted in Mplus v8.5 [49].

Overall, 27.5% of participants reported never having heard of PrEP, and an additional 17.6% reported having heard of PrEP but not knowing what it is, indicating limited baseline awareness. Only 21.4% reported knowing a fair amount or a lot about PrEP (Table 1). The mean PrEP knowledge score was 3.14 (SD = 2.70), based on the sum of correct responses across eight items, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge.

At the 6-month follow-up (n = 141), 63.2% of participants reported being likely or very likely to consider long-acting injectable PrEP if it were free, and 27.0% reported being likely or very likely to consider it at a cost of $75 per month. Willingness also decreased when mild side effects were introduced, though 34.1% still reported being likely or very likely to consider long-acting injectable PrEP (Table 2; Appendix A).

The final SEM (Fig. 1; Table 3) showed a good fit (CFI = 0.934, TLI = 0.921, RMSEA = 0.052). This improvement was obtained by including correlated residuals for the three PrEP outcomes (awareness, knowledge, willingness) and by adding a residual correlation between HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes items 2 and 5. The standardized factor loadings for the HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes CFA model ranged from 0.408 to 0.894, which were comparable to prior research [50, 51]. Gender (z = − 1.547, p = 0.122) and housing status (z = 0.355, p = 0.722) were not related to HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes latent factor. Only race/ethnicity was associated with the HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes latent factor; BIPOC (vs. non-Hispanic White) was positively associated with HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes (β = 0.196, z = − 2.921, p = 0.003). Greater endorsement of HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes was associated with lower likelihood of correct PrEP knowledge (β=− 0.179, z = − 2.762, p = 0.006) and the lower levels of awareness with PrEP (β=− 0.212, z = − 3.671, p<0.001), but not with willingness (β=− 0.098, z = − 1.135, p = 0.256). The likelihood of willingness to try PrEP was positively correlated with having correct PrEP knowledge (ρ = 0.175, z = 2.218, p = 0.027) but not with the level of awareness with PrEP (ρ = 0.135, z = 1.719, p = 0.086).

Discussion

In this study, we examined how HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes relate to early-stage PrEP care continuum outcomes among PWID, specifically focusing on awareness, knowledge, and willingness. Using structural equation modeling, we found that higher levels of HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes were associated with lower awareness and less accurate knowledge of PrEP, controlling for race/ethnicity, gender and housing status. These findings align with a systematic review of the PrEP care cascade among PWID, which documented substantial attrition across cascade stages and emphasized early-stage engagement (awareness and knowledge) as critical leverage points for improving PrEP uptake [12]. While previous research has linked HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes to lower PrEP initiation, particularly among women [35–37], few have evaluated these associations among PWID using a latent factor approach or have modeled willingness to use PrEP while simultaneously accounting for awareness and knowledge. Our findings suggest that the HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency and factor structure among PWID in our sample, supporting its use in this population. While we adapted the scale from a previously version validated among the general population and pregnant women living with HIV [46], future work should further validate its psychometric properties in this context.

HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes were associated with lower PrEP awareness and less accurate knowledge in this study, echoing prior research that highlights how stigma can limit engagement with HIV prevention [42, 52]. Our past qualitative work also identified HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes among PWID as an obstacle to PrEP awareness and knowledge [8], which our current findings corroborate. Stigma often stems from deeply ingrained societal biases, such as those codified in laws and institutional practices, and may cause PWID to avoid HIV-related conversations or services to distance themselves from a stigmatized identity [16]. This avoidance can reduce exposure to accurate information and hinder PrEP uptake. Limited healthcare access further compounds this issue: many PWID face structural barriers including financial hardship, lack of insurance, and provider discrimination, which reduce access to PrEP-related education. Historical mistreatment and mistrust of healthcare systems may also contribute to disengagement from HIV prevention [53–55]. However, HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes were not significantly related to willingness to take PrEP once participants were informed about it, suggesting that willingness may be shaped by other factors, such as individual risk perception or perceived benefits. Addressing stigma at both individual and structural levels is thus essential to improving early engagement in the PrEP care continuum. Future research should continue identifying how stigma operates within PWID communities and explores strategies to reduce its impact.

Berger introduced the concept of intersectional stigma to describe how overlapping systems of oppression, including racism, drug use stigma, and HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes, jointly shape health behaviors and access to care [56]. In this study, we measured HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes using the Visser scale [46], which captures endorsement of stigmatizing beliefs rather than lived experiences of stigma; however, PWID, particularly BIPOC individuals, often experience multiple, intersecting stigmas related to drug use, healthcare engagement, and structural racism [30]. These overlapping stigmas may contribute to healthcare avoidance and reduced access to HIV prevention information, helping to explain persistent racial and ethnic disparities in HIV burden among PWID [42, 57–59]. Internalized stigma, defined as the internal adoption of negative societal beliefs, often develops within broader structural and interpersonal contexts of enacted and anticipated stigma. Enacted, anticipated, and internalized stigma are intertwined and mutually reinforcing processes operating across levels [22, 40]. Internalized stigma may further shape how PWID perceive their HIV risk and engage with HIV prevention services, including PrEP. Future studies should examine how internalized, anticipated, and enacted stigma interact to influence engagement across the PrEP care continuum. Prior qualitative work has identified stigma at the intersection of gender, sexuality, and HIV as a key barrier to PrEP engagement, particularly among women who use drugs [8, 60]. This analysis found that BIPOC PWID reported higher levels of HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes, suggesting an interplay between systems of oppression colliding to influence health outcomes differently for sub-groups of PWID [61]. Past research further has indicates that individual behaviors, including PrEP care continuum engagement, are shaped by historical and structural processes that constrain access to information, resources, and healthcare [42, 62]. Beyond PrEP engagement, intersectional stigma may contribute to mental health challenges [32], social isolation and overdose [63, 64], reduce quality of life, and avoidance of other essential health services among PWID. Because we did not measure stigma related to other identities, intersectional stigma remains a limitation, and future research should incorporate measures across multiple identities to better capture its broader health impacts.

Future research should examine how structural factors, such as laws, policies, and institutional practices, can reinforce stigma and contribute to health inequities among PWID. Policies can be stigmatizing in their effects when they criminalize drug use, restrict harm reduction services, or otherwise marginalize people who use drugs [65]. These structural barriers are compounded by competing priorities, as many PWID must focus on meeting basic needs such as food, shelter, and treatment for existing health problems, which can limit engagement with HIV prevention. Policies that promote the rights and wellbeing of PWID, racially and ethnically marginalized communities [66, 67], and people living with HIV [68] may help to reduce discrimination and increase access to PrEP. Given ongoing debates around harm reduction and needle exchange programs, advocacy for such policies should be a key component of efforts to address HIV and intersectional stigma and improve PrEP care continuum outcomes among PWID.

Additionally, future work should focus on intersectional stigma, as there is an interplay between systems of oppression [69]. Future research should also explore strengths and empowerment factors in conjunction with intersectional stigma experiences [70], as emerging evidence suggests that positive racial and ethnic identity and attitudes may support better outcomes in HIV care [71]. For example, research might explore how social networks, community-based organizations, and other neighborhood factors can serve as social supports and means for resilience [6, 7, 72].

This study has several limitations: First, this study was cross-sectional which can limit the ability to draw causal inferences due to the lack of temporal sequencing, susceptibility to confounding, and the potential for reverse causality. Second, due to sample size limitations, we also could not examine relationship between HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes with all racial/ethnic subgroups. Third, while we assessed stigma using a validated scale, future work should expand validation efforts in diverse PWID populations. Fourth, as the parent study required opioid use as an inclusion criterion, individuals who exclusively used stimulants (for example, methamphetamine or cocaine) without reported opioid use were not eligible, which may limit generalizability. Fifth, because many participants were recruited from SSPs and MOUD clinics, which are designed to provide non-stigmatizing and HIV prevention-oriented services, our findings may underestimate HIV-related stigma among PWID not engaged in harm reduction or treatment settings. Sixth, although we controlled for key covariates, we were not able to control for all potential confounding factors, including incarceration history. Studies show that PWID have high rates of incarceration and criminal legal system involvement. Future research should examine the joint influence of incarceration and HIV stigma on PrEP awareness and other PrEP care cascade outcomes. Finally, the study was conducted in two U.S. cities, Los Angeles and Denver, limiting generalizability to other settings with different social or healthcare environments.

Conclusion

This study contributes to a growing body of evidence suggesting that HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes remain a potent barrier to early PrEP care continuum engagement among PWID. While many PWID meet CDC criteria for PrEP eligibility, some may be hindered from initiating PrEP due lack of awareness or stigma-related concerns. Thus, addressing HIV-stigmatizing beliefs and attitudes should be a priority in public health strategies aimed at increasing PrEP uptake among PWID. Addressing these challenges could be an effective if a multifaceted approach that includes stigma reduction interventions, policy advocacy, and the promotion of resilience and empowerment within PWID communities. Future research should focus on structural stigmas and the development of inclusive policies that facilitate access to health resources while safeguarding the rights of PWID and other marginalized groups. Exploring the role of social supports and community-based organizations may reveal new strategies for enhancing PrEP care continuum outcomes. Prior work with PWID suggests leveraging social networks to increase PrEP uptake [73], and the WHO recommends utilizing social network approaches with key populations [74]. While some aspects of network-based interventions, such as information-sharing and enhancing motivation, may align with the Transtheoretical Model, other mechanisms like peer pressure or normative influence likely fall outside its scope and may require alternative or complementary theoretical frameworks. Working with PWID who are willing to take PrEP and/or are already taking PrEP could be one way to influence people’s networks. The current findings underscore the importance of such efforts and highlight the necessity for comprehensive strategies to overcome the barriers of HIV and intersectional stigmas for improving PrEP uptake among PWID.
