A complete tear of Achilles tendon caused by a kite string — A case report
Authors: Anil Kumar Kotteda, Mehar Dhillon, Akshay Shreegan, Prasoon Kumar, Ankit Dadra, Sandeep Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKite flying in India and other South-East Asian countries is a significant cultural activity, especially during festive seasons. There is an increasing use of non-biodegradable kite strings like the Chinese manja has caused a rise in injuries not just to kite flyers but also to pedestrians and motorcyclists, sometimes even causing life threatening injuries.CaseWe present a rare case of a 27-year-old male patient who was injured by a kite string while crossing the road. He sustained a lacerated wound and a completely torn tendo achilles on the right side. Surgical debridement and end-to-end Krackow repair of the tendon was done using non-absorbable suture successfully. Functional outcome of the patient at 12 months follow-up was excellent.ConclusionThis case highlights the rare but serious risk of Achilles tendon injury caused by kite strings. It emphasizes the need for public awareness, safety regulations, and enforcement regarding the use of hazardous kite strings. Early diagnosis and prompt surgical management are essential for optimal outcomes in such injuries.Keywords: Kite-string injury, Achilles tendon rupture, Chinese manja, Pedestrian trauma, Penetrating lower limb injury, Festive season hazard

Introduction

Kite flying is one of the most popular activities during festive seasons in India and is even enjoyed as a popular sport in countries like Pakistan, Bangladesh, China, Brazil and Japan [1]. Originally a cotton thread “Manja” was used for kite flying as a hobby but due to its transition to a competitive sport, a special kind of monofilament material called “Chinese Manja” replaced the original thread to destroy the opponent's kite [2]. This thread is a cotton or a nylon thread which is coated with powdered glass, metal dust or chemicals, which are non-biodegradable and potentially lethal [2]. A spectrum of kite string injuries in the human body involving the head, neck and upper limb have been documented, affecting not only the participants of this sport but also bystanders and pedestrians [3]. Injuries to the lower limb especially leading to complete tear of the tendo-achilles are extremely rare [4]. This case report highlights one of the rare yet significant patterns of injury leading to complete tear of Achilles tendon by a kite string. We present a 12 month follow up of a 27-year-old male with kite string injury leading to complete tear of the Achilles tendon while crossing the road. Considering the rare mode of injury as well as mechanism that lead to the injury we report the case presentation to educate readers regarding the hazardous use of kite strings during festive seasons especially in South-East Asia that can become lethal for a casual bystander crossing the road.

Case report

A 27-year-old male presented to our advanced trauma center with complaints of pain, swelling and a lacerated wound at the distal part of right heel with another lacerated wound over the left anterior aspect of the ankle, following an injury by the kite string while he was walking on the road. On further questioning regarding the mechanism of injury, it was understood that while the patient was crossing the road during the kite flying season in India, a kite string got entangled around his feet in a figure of eight pattern and then the string got pulled by a two wheel during which the patient suffered his concomitant lower-limb injuries. He was resuscitated according to the ATLS protocol; thorough wash was given to the wound on the posterior aspect of right leg and anterior aspect of the left ankle. Examining the wounds, there was 3 cm lacerated transverse cut on the skin over tendo-achilles (Fig. 1) with discontinuity of the tendon and a palpable clinical defect and the laceration over the left ankle was found to be superficial with no tendon cuts. The patient was unable to actively plantarflex his right ankle and complained of pain on passive movement of the ankle, while movement over the left ankle was normal. He complained of pain on pressure over the margins of tendo-achilles tendon. Thompson test was found to be positive [5]. On passive movement of the ankle, the defect in the tendon could be easily felt. Based on symptoms and signs, a clinical diagnosis of complete tendo-achilles tear was made and temporary splintage was provided as and patient was shifted to the operating room with the plan for repair.

Treatment and follow up

The patient underwent primary debridement and repair of the torn tendon over the right ankle and primary closure of the lacerated wound over the left ankle under general anesthesia in prone position. Skin incision was extended curving the lacerated wound on one end to identify the torn ends (Fig. 2a). Both the ends were identified, the margins of the tendon were found to be friable and freshened. Krackow sutures were taken in both the ends and an end to end repair of the tendon was performed (Fig. 2b) with the non-absorbable suture (Ethibond). No fractures were observed. A strong end to end repair, continuity of the tendon was achieved and was confirmed on table by performing passive ankle movements. Thorough wound wash was given, wound closure done and splintage was provided using below knee slab in plantar flexion.

a: Skin incision extended and the cut ends identified.

b: End to end repair of the torn tendon.

Immediate postoperative wound care was explained to the patient and was discharged with below knee slab in plantar flexion. He was followed up at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 4 months and 6 months. Two weeks post-surgery, sutures were removed over both ankles and slab were changed to plantigrade. After 6 weeks follow up, patient complained of no pain, his wounds had completely healed (Fig. 3a & b) and physiotherapy was started [6]. The American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score initially at 8 weeks was 77 points (Fair) with mild pain and restriction of movement. The clinical outcomes were assessed at 4 months again according to AOFAS was found to be “excellent” with 100% score [7]. At 6 months follow up, patient is able to do active plantar flexion without any pain (Fig. 4a, b & c), comparable to the uninjured side and has no limitation in the activity of his daily living. Repeat follow was performed at 1 year since injury and patient had no complaints.

After a close follow, up of 6 months patient continued to achieve complete range of motion at ankle joint (Fig. 4a, b & c) and was able to carry out his daily activities without any pain or restriction.

Discussion

The festival of kite flying is usually enjoyed on rooftops of buildings and due to the growing fierce competitive nature of the sport, the strings of polypropylene are coated with metal dust or glass pieces to strengthen their kites [2]. These new range strings are strong and not only resist breakage but also aid in damaging the opponents kite on entanglement. This monofilament is made by melting and mixing certain polymers that extrude via tiny holes and then are spun into spools of various thickness. It is non-biodegradable and very thin, thus cannot be seen properly by the naked eye [3]. Cut injuries because of “Chinese Manja” or “Killer Manja” occur when the taut string, which is not visible during the day light, slices into the skin or underlying tissue and causes severe injury, sometimes even leading to fatal hemorrhage if major vessels are involved. The injuries not only affect the kite flyers but also pedestrians, bystanders and also animals [4], [8]. The razor-sharp string can get entangled around the neck or other parts of the body and is difficult to break. The most common injuries sustained by kite flyers are linear abrasions and deep incised wounds to the hand especially the zone two [4], [9]. The neck and the face are the most common sites amongst the bystanders [10]. Prajapati et al. reported an analysis of 104 patients over 4 years suffering from kite injuries in which the most injuries occurred while driving or amongst pedestrians, with the neck being the most commonly involved. Thus, injury to the Achilles tendon is found to be extremely rare [11], [12]. A study evaluating 30 cases managed in a tertiary hospital reported maximum injuries over the hand, neck and face. Another study reported the tendon injuries caused by kite string elaborated on primary upper limb injuries with lower limb regarded as uncommon. The clean-cut nature of the injuries reported favorable outcomes however, adequate wound irrigation was required. No bony injury was reported and all the injuries took place in overcrowded urban areas [12]. The coating of the string with glass powder from fused bulbs and tubes with metallic powder and chemicals is found to be the reason for such severe injuries [12]. A study reporting 20 cases of Kite injury depicted maximum injuries to the deep tissues involving muscles and even reported decreased chance of fatal injuries amongst people wearing protective gear like neck scarves or helmet during kite flying season [13]. Our case report depicts an unusual mechanism of injury in which the thread became entangled around the ankles of a pedestrian and ultimately pulled by a two wheeler in a figure of eight pattern leading to bilateral ankle injuries with right (TA cut) more severe than left side (lacerated wound over anterior ankle).

Achilles tendon is the strongest tendon in the body and is important for not only plantar flexion of the ankle but also for a normal gait, thus warranting early management [14]. This injury presents clinically with lacerated wound, pain, swelling, with a palpable defect along the length of the tendon and inability to do plantar flexion [15]. Signs include a positive Thompson test [5] and it can be confirmed radiologically using an Ultrasound or a MRI scan. Tendo achilles is commonly injured during trauma or activities involving eccentric contraction of the tendon, however, complete lacerations caused by external penetrating trauma like the kite string is extremely rare. This mechanism of injury is rarely observed in our orthopaedic practise and it presents a unique challenge regarding prevention, surgical management and follow up care.

The management of such injuries apart from the routine repair, require additional gentle irrigation with 4–5 l of normal saline for prevention of infection and thorough assessment for neurovascular status is imperative [16]. The wound should be carefully and copiously irrigated in the operating room as the string contains various small non-biodegradable particles that can be embedded into the deeper tissues as managed in our reported case [9]. Bagaria et al. reported a case of delayed repair of the tendon due to kite string injury leading to delayed recovery [16].

Our patient presented to us with all these signs and symptoms and had clearly visualised the torn tendon ends through the wound. We did not use any imaging modality to confirm the diagnosis. Surgical repair is the treatment of choice in complete tendo achilles rupture and is widely supported by the current literature especially in young, active patients [17]. It provides higher strength, reduced chances of re-rupture, and also better long term results compared to the non-operative management. The wound was copiously washed and we used the technique of Krackow suture, tying the torn ends together which is one of the standard surgical options. The paratenon over the tendon was meticulously closed so that there are decreased chances of adhesion formation and also provides smooth gliding of the tendon. Early mobilisation and rehabilitation was ensured to prevent stiffness and promote healing [18].

The pattern of kite string injuries is underreported in India [1]. A study reviewing kite injuries amongst pediatric age group reported an alarming mortality of 9.5% [19]. They have razor like quality, making it possible to cut through even deeper tough tissues. The mode and mechanism of injury of this peculiar case shows that even causal outdoor activity like walking or crossing a road can be dangerous in areas where kite flying activities are occurring. Emergency physician should know that these trivial looking injuries can be associated with significant lethal injuries.

Beyond the orthopaedic perspective, this report aims to provide attention to the significant hazard posed by the manja thread or unregulated kite flying. It is banned in multiple Indian states but the enforcement is not consistent. It should be regulated at designated locations during festivals, away from the densely populated traffic prone areas, with proper discarding of the strings. The people on road during such festivals should be advised to wear protective gear in the form of scarves, helmets and gloves. In conclusion, Achilles tendon injuries due to kite string are relatively rare injuries. Early recognition of the condition and operative management gives better results.
