AI-assisted label-free single-particle analysis of milk-derived extracellular vesicles enabled by nanotweezers
Authors: Ikjun Hong, Abayomi E Opadele, Guodong Zhu, Yuankai Huo, Justus C Ndukaife
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMilk-derived extracellular vesicles (mEVs) are promising drug carriers because they can survive the digestive tract, be engineered to avoid immune rejection, and deliver therapeutic cargo to the body. Fully realizing this potential requires precise, single-particle measurements of size, composition, and purity, yet current analytical tools are often slow, require chemical labels. Here we present an electrohydrodynamic nanotweezer platform that rapidly traps thousands of mEVs in parallel, enables label-free interferometric scattering imaging, and uses artificial intelligence for automated analysis. The device employs a thin gold film patterned with a 15 µm hole array that generates radially-converging AC-driven fluid flows to hold vesicles in place in parallel within seconds. By briefly releasing them, we track their Brownian motion to calculate their size from diffusion behavior and estimate refractive index from their interferometric contrast signals to assess sample purity and heterogeneity. This fast, non-perturbative workflow combines scalable trapping, real-time label-free imaging, and automated analysis, offering a powerful approach to studying mEVs and accelerate their use as therapeutic delivery vehicles.Subject terms: Engineering, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

The ability to stably trap nanoscale particles in solution against randomizing Brownian motion is foundational to progress in diverse disciplines such as biology, colloidal chemistry, and biophysics. Over the past decades, a range of techniques has been developed to achieve stable trapping of nanoscale objects in solution. These include plasmonic nanotweezers1–5, which concentrate light beyond the diffraction limit to create strong optical forces, photonic crystal cavity-based nanotweezers6,7, resonant dielectric traps8, and optothermal tweezers9,10. More recently, optical nanotweezers have evolved significantly to offer improvements in trapping speed, throughput, and applicability to biologically relevant particles11–13.

Among recent advances is the use of geometry-induced electrohydrodynamic trapping, which enables rapid parallel trapping and deterministic placement of single nanoscale extracellular vesicles (EVs) at the nanogap hotspots of plasmonic antennas11,13. In parallel, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning are being explored to enhance the performance of optical nanotweezers and improve trapping stability with low illumination powers14–16.

Emerging optical nanotweezer platforms present substantial opportunities to address fundamental biological questions and drive translational applications. A particularly promising area is the study and therapeutic use of EVs, which are nanoscale biomaterials that mediate intercellular communication17–19. EVs are inherently biocompatible and can be engineered to deliver therapeutic cargo with high specificity to target cells or tissues. To fully realize their clinical potential, however, it is critical to identify scalable sources of EVs, characterize their heterogeneity, and accurately determine their size, as particle size directly influences drug-loading capacity and dosing requirements20,21.

Effective therapeutic use of EVs demands single-particle level analysis to resolve size, surface markers, and cargo composition. This need creates significant opportunities for the development of next-generation nanotweezer platforms for characterizing EV heterogeneity. Integration with artificial intelligence further enhances trapping efficiency and analytical performance, enabling more precise characterization of EVs and accelerating their translation into clinical applications.

In this work, we present a step forward: a nanotweezer-based platform that enables the parallel trapping of EVs within seconds and integrates AI-assisted analysis to track and characterize the motion, size, and refractive index of individual EVs in a label-free manner. Furthermore, our nanotweezer approach allows to sort EVs based on size, thus providing the capability to seamlessly select EVs from sub-10 nm contaminating particles such as small proteins and extracellular RNA not associated with EVs. Our platform is applied specifically to milk-derived EVs (mEVs), which represent a promising source of EVs due to their scalability. One dairy cow can produce ~10,800 kg of milk annually, yielding over 10²¹ vesicles—and their resilience to enzymatic degradation and ability to traverse gastrointestinal barriers make them attractive for EV-based therapeutics22.

To achieve parallel trapping, we induce alternating current (AC) electro-osmotic flows to direct and localize EVs at predefined positions in a microfluidic chip. These trapped particles are then imaged using interferometric scattering microscopy23–26, which leverages the interference between incident and scattered light to detect nanoscale particles without fluorescent labeling, while they are held in the traps.

To automate particle detection and tracking, we integrate a deep learning-based image segmentation approach. Inspired by recent advances in medical imaging, we adopt a U-Net architecture27, which is a convolutional neural network with an encoder-decoder structure and skip connections, to robustly identify nanoscale particles in microscopy images, even with limited training data. After segmentation, particle centroids are tracked across consecutive frames to construct trajectories, from which diffusion coefficients are extracted. This AI-enabled pipeline greatly enhances the efficiency, speed, and scalability of particle tracking under complex imaging conditions.

Together, this integrated platform combining optical nanotweezers, interferometric imaging, and AI-based analysis offers a powerful new tool for high-throughput trapping and label-free EV characterization, with potential to advance both fundamental EV research and its application in therapeutic development.

Results

A protocol for high-purity isolation of milk extracellular vesicles

We first begin by developing a protocol for isolating EVs from bovine milk. The efficient and reliable isolation of milk-derived EVs is essential for both mechanistic studies and therapeutic applications28,29. However, the isolation of high-fidelity extracellular vesicles from milk has been challenging due to the abundance of casein micelles in the whey fractions. Our adapted and streamlined protocol (Fig. 1A) combines mild acidification (acetic acid) with sequential centrifugation to yield EV fractions that will be free of protein contaminants, particularly caseins, while retaining surface markers like CD63 and preserving native zeta potential30. Treating whole ultra-pasteurized milk with acetic acid (AA) reduced the pH from ~6.5 to ~4.6 (Fig. 1B, P < 0.0001) and induced casein isoelectric precipitation, while clearing the whey fractions29,31. Our approach minimized protein contamination prior to ultracentrifugation, consistent with previously described acid-precipitation protocols for milk EV enrichment31. Furthermore, our approach aligns well with literature reports describing acid-based removal of casein micelles to facilitate EV purification. To validate EV purification, we conducted capillary western blotting of 100,000 × g EV pellets (Fig. 1D), and it revealed a robust CD63 signal, indicating successful EV recovery. In AA-treated samples, casein bands were markedly diminished compared to untreated controls, and cytosolic markers such as β-actin and GAPDH were virtually undetectable. These results demonstrate that AA-based isolation efficiently eliminates major milk protein contaminants without compromising EV integrity. The acid treatment also enhances EV purity, though it requires caution with respect to potential effects on surface proteins. Notably, while prior studies have raised concerns that harsh acid exposure may degrade EV surface proteins, our capillary western analysis shows that CD63 remains intact following treatment. This finding underscores the mildness of our AA protocol and supports its suitability for downstream functional studies. We further assessed the surface charge of AA-purified EVs (Fig. 1C). Zeta potential analysis revealed a slight but statistically significant shift toward a more negative charge (–18 mV in untreated vs. –24 mV in AA-treated samples). The preservation of consistent surface charge profiles indicates that AA treatment does not substantially alter EV membrane properties, consistent with previous reports showing that chemical pretreatments maintain vesicle charge characteristics. Collectively, these results suggest that acetic acid–based pH shifting provides a gentle pretreatment strategy for milk, improving EV purity without compromising structural integrity or surface marker expression. This conclusion is further supported by the characteristic cup-shaped morphology observed in TEM images of the purified mEV samples (Fig. 1E). By refining existing approaches, our purification strategy expands the toolkit available to the milk-EV community.

Trapping, imaging, and AI-assisted tracking of extracellular vesicles

Subsequently, the purified milk EVs were introduced into the microfluidic chip containing the trapping sites, as shown in Fig. 2. The EV trapping is achieved by inducing an electro-osmotic flow in the presence of an applied AC electric field. The trapping sites comprise a gold film with arrays of micro holes. The diameter of the micro holes is 18 μm with a lattice spacing of 2 μm. The application of an AC voltage normal to the structured gold film results in an AC electric field having both normal and tangential components. The tangential component of the AC field acts on the electrical double layer charge induced at the interface between the gold film and the adjoining fluid medium, inducing AC electro-osmotic flows. The AC electro-osmotic flow acts radially inward to localize the EVs at the center of the gold film, as indicated in Fig. 2B and Supplementary information 1. Next, we implemented interferometric imaging to image the trapped EVs without any fluorescent labels. Details of the interferometric imaging are provided in Supplementary information 2. The trapped mEVs were visualized in real time using interferometric scattering microscopy, and the recordings captured mEV motion over several seconds. To improve the visibility of mEVs for computational analysis, raw video frames were processed using background subtraction, yielding enhanced contrast images with clearer mEV images against the trapping structures, as shown in Fig. 2C. The background image was generated by averaging the frames captured after the particle was released from the trapping site. Each individual frame was then divided by this average image to obtain the background-corrected result. These background-corrected images served as input data for a supervised deep learning segmentation pipeline, where annotated binary masks were used to train a U-Net convolutional neural network to detect and segment individual EVs (Fig. 2D, E and Supplementary information 6). The U-Net model included a contracting path for feature extraction and an expansive path for spatial localization, incorporating convolutional layers, max-pooling, up-convolution, and skip connections to retain resolution and contextual information (Fig. 2E). In this structure, a series of convolutional layers and continuous down-sampling layers (Encoder) are used to extract high-frequency features with a large receptive field. Then, up-sampling of the extracted deep features produces a pixel-level semantic prediction (Decoder). The skip connections between the encoder and decoder at different levels alleviate the loss of spatial information, thus enabling the recovery of high-resolution features. Once trained, the model was used to predict segmentation masks from new image frames, accurately identifying EVs within the trapping regions, as shown in Fig. 1F (left panel). The resulting binary masks were then used for single-particle tracking across consecutive frames, enabling the extraction of spatiotemporal trajectories and quantification of mEV dynamics, such as diffusion and trapping efficiency, as shown in Fig. 2F.

Correlation of Interferometric Contrast with Particle Size and Refractive Index

To correlate particle size and refractive index with the image contrast of the trapped particle, we monitored the Brownian dynamics after temporarily releasing the particles by turning OFF the applied AC voltage. We also quantified the image contrast when the particles were trapped, as shown in Fig. 3A. For the trapping and imaging experiments, a 520 nm laser beam was used for label-free interferometric scattering imaging, while a 3 kHz sinusoidal waveform was applied to induce trapping. When the AC field was active, particles were confined within the central regions of the gold film, while they undergo Brownian diffusion when the AC voltage is turned OFF. The MSD analysis used to quantify the degree of confinement during the trapping phase is described in Supplementary Information 3. A representative movie clip from the recorded videos showing the experimental procedure, with six particles (a-f) clearly detected, as shown in Supporting Video 1 and Fig. 3B. The trajectories of these particles were extracted over time using a U-Net model, showing random Brownian motion during the period the AC voltage was turned OFF, as described in Fig. 3B and in Supporting Video 2. The transition from confined to released particle motion is quantified through MSD analysis, as described in Supplementary Information 3. Here, the contrast for a 300 nm polystyrene particle (Fluoro-Max R300, Thermo Fisher Scientific) is first investigated as a reference particle with known refractive index to estimate the refractive index of other trapped milk-derived vesicles and particles. To further quantify contrast, the radial intensity profiles of 300 nm PS particle were extracted and plotted, as shown in Supplementary Information 4 and the Methods section.

Based on this reference, we simulated the scattering cross-section using FDTD, taking into account a collection angle of ~129°, corresponding to a 1.2 numerical aperture, as the detail described in Supplementary information 5. The scattering cross-sections for particles of different sizes, under the same experimental parameters, were then applied to generate contrast (C=β2σscat+2βσscat) versus particle diameter while varying the refractive index, as shown by the different colored lines in Fig. 3D. Next, the contrast of milk-derived particles in the interferometric scattering images during the period when the voltage was turned ON (i.e., the trapping period) was quantified. A line crossing the center of each particle was defined, and the standard deviation of the image intensity along that line was calculated, as described in Fig. 3C and the Methods section. Using the AI-assisted trajectory information, the mean squared displacement (MSD) as a function of lag time was then calculated. The trajectories of six representative particles (a-f) are described in Fig. 3B and Supplementary Information 7.

The quantified size from Brownian dynamics and the contrast information of each particle are mapped in Fig. 3D. The mapping of twelve investigated particles is shown in Fig. 3D and provides the refractive index information for each. The distribution of refractive index ranges from 1.38 to 1.50. The error bars represent three times the standard deviation (σ) derived from the linear fitting procedure used to extract diffusion coefficients. Specifically, the slope m of the MSD curves was obtained using least-squares regression, and its standard deviation was calculated from the residual variance of the fit. This standard deviation reflects the statistical uncertainty of the MSD analysis arising from finite trajectory length, thermal fluctuations, and localization noise. The uncertainty in the diffusion coefficient was obtained from this fitting uncertainty. Error bars correspond to multiples of this propagated standard deviation. This mapping result also implies that some particles with a refractive index higher than 1.5 are likely milk-derived residue particles, such as protein aggregates. We compared the NTA results with the size histogram of individually trapped extracellular vesicles obtained using the AI-assisted nanotweezer platform (blue bars), overlaid with the corresponding NTA-derived size concentration profile (red curve), as shown in Fig. 3E. The nanotweezer measurements yield a discrete particle count distribution, while NTA provides ensemble concentration data (right y-axis, ×10⁸ mL⁻¹). The substantial population of nanotweezer-detected vesicles falls within the 150-250 nm size range, aligning with the dominant NTA intensity maxima. Subsequently, the ensemble-averaged NTA size distribution depicts multiple resolved subpopulations, with local maxima centered at approximately 43.5 nm, 157.5 nm, and 219.5 nm, indicative of a heterogeneous vesicle population. The effective size window interrogated by the nanotweezer platform overlaps primarily with the mid-to-large vesicle fractions. The data represent the mean of three independent measurements (n = 3), with corresponding averages and standard errors reported.

Frequency-controlled size sorting of trapped particles

Size-sorting experiments were carried out by varying the applied A.C. field frequency between 10 and 30 kHz. At 10 kHz, milk-derived EVs were stably confined within the trapping region. When the frequency was increased to 20 kHz, smaller particles began to escape from the traps, as shown in Fig. 4 and Supporting Video 3. A further increase to 30 kHz caused additional particles that had remained at 20 kHz to also be released. At this point, the population that remained localized was predominantly composed of larger EVs.

Sorting experiment of multiple EVs by varying the applied frequency.

The a.c. frequency was initially set to 10 kHz to enable trapping of EV of various sizes. As the a.c. frequency is increased to 20 kHz and 30 kHz, smaller EVs are expelled from the trap. This demonstrates that size-based sorting can be achieved by changing the a.c. frequency.

This frequency-dependent sorting arises because the particle–surface interaction force that stabilizes out-of-plane confinement is strongest at lower frequencies and progressively weakens as the frequency increases. At higher frequencies, the electrical double layer surrounding the particle cannot be fully polarized in response to the rapidly oscillating field, thereby reducing the effective trapping force. As a result, smaller EVs, along with free proteins and extracellular RNA fragments that diffuse more readily, are preferentially ejected as the A.C. frequency is raised. To release smaller particles from the trap, the AC field frequency is increased. However, even after release, the EVs remain subject to the applied AC field at the higher frequency and therefore continue to experience background AC electro-osmotic (ACEO) flow. As a result, the particles do not undergo free Brownian diffusion. This prevents reliable estimation of particle size from their Brownian motion during release. To address this issue, and provide data on the particle release for varying AC frequency, we conducted size-based sorting experiments using polymer bead particles with known diameters, specifically 100, 300, and 500 nm, to validate the sorting capability of our platform, as detailed in Supplementary information 8 and Supporting Video 4.

Discussion

We developed an integrated platform that combines electrohydrodynamic trapping, interferometric scattering microscopy, and AI-assisted tracking for rapid, label-free analysis of milk-derived extracellular vesicles (mEVs). The system enables parallel trapping of EVs within seconds, high-sensitivity imaging, and automated single-particle sizing, yielding distributions consistent with the inherent heterogeneity of milk EVs. This non-perturbative workflow preserves vesicle structure and dynamics while also allowing simultaneous assessment of additional properties such as refractive index, paving the way to assess the purity of EV preparations. Together, these capabilities establish a scalable and versatile approach for advancing both fundamental studies and translational applications of EVs.

Methods

Milk collection and sample processing

A 2% grade A reduced-fat bovine milk was commercially pooled from a local grocery store. To minimize substantial degradation and alteration of pH, milk samples were processed within 4 h of procurement. Prior to isolation of extracellular vesicles, the fresh milk samples were centrifuged at 2000 x g for 30 mins at 4 °C for the removal of cream and large particulates. The resulting supernatant of the defatted milk was carefully collected with a pipette to avoid contamination with sedimented pellets into separate 50 ml conical tubes. For the removal of residual fat globules and casein clumps in the milk supernatant, A grade 1 150 mm Whatman filter (Cytivia, 18123982) was used to pre-filter the milk samples before differential ultracentrifugation.

Isoelectric precipitation of casein

To precipitate casein and enrich the milk whey fraction, defatted milk was first warmed to 37 °C for 10 minutes as previously described. 6 N of Acetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, 102762795) was then added to the milk at a volume ratio of 1:100 (acetic acid:milk), followed by incubation for 5 minutes at room temperature. The pH of the milk sample was validated to be at 4.6 following acid-treatment. Milk samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The supernatant (designated whey) was collected, and the casein pellet was discarded. Whey samples were then filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane filter (Millipore, 0000430715) to eliminate residual debris.

Differential Ultracentrifugation and EV purification

The designated whey was subjected to successive differential ultracentrifugation for EV isolation. Briefly, samples were ultracentrifuged at 12,000 × g (12 K), 35,000 × g (35 K), 70,000 × g (70 K) and 100,000 × g (100 K) for 90 minutes each at 4 °C using a Ti45 rotor (Beckman Coulter, Optima XPN-80 Ultracentrifuge). The pellets of 12 K and 35k were discarded, while that of 70 K & 100 K were kept for further analysis. Nevertheless, the EV-containing pellet from 100 K was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and a washing step was performed by a second ultracentrifugation at 100 K for 60 minutes at 4 °C using a TLA-110 rotor (Beckman Coulter, Optima MAX-XP Ultracentrifuge). The final EV pellet was resuspended in 500 µL PBS after discarding the supernatant. To remove residual protein aggregates or precipitates, the EV suspension was clarified by a final centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as purified milk-derived EVs (mEVs). The isolated mEVs were stored in 4 °C for immediate downstream analysis or kept at −20 °C in multiple 1.5 ml microcentrifuges (VMR, 87003-294) for a short time. For iSCAT imaging, mEVs were further suspended in filtered D.I water at 1:2000 dilution following particle characterization by NTA.

Nano Tracking Particle Analysis

The particle size distribution and concentration of isolated mEVs were characterized using the NanoSight NS300 system (Malvern Instruments, Malvern), equipped with a 405 nm laser module and a temperature-controlled sample chamber. Prior to analysis, EV samples were diluted in filtered 1X PBS to a final sample concentration of 1:2000 dilution and gently suspended by repeated aspiration through a micropipette to minimize vesicle clustering. Approximately 1 mL of diluted sample was introduced into the NTA chamber using a syringe-driven pump, and videos were captured at a frame rate of 30 frames per second. Instrument parameters were set as follows: camera level 7, detection threshold 10, and flow rate 50. All measurements were conducted at 25 °C. Data were processed and analyzed using the NanoSight NTA software version 3.2. NTA data was re-plotted with Origin Pro 2021.

Zeta potential measurements of mEVs

The surface charge (zeta potential) of milk-derived EVs was measured using the ZetaView® PMX 120 instrument (Particle Metrix) equipped with a laser-based tracking system. EV samples were diluted in filtered 1× PBS to an appropriate concentration (typically yielding 100–300 particles/frame) and temperature set at 25 °C. Samples were homogenized by gentle pipetting before measurement to minimize vesicle aggregation. Measurements were acquired in triplicate across 10 standard positions within the cell chamber. Instrument settings were configured as follows: sensitivity set to 85, shutter speed of 70, and a frame rate of 30 frames per second. All data acquisition and analysis were performed using ZetaView software (version 4.4), and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation from triplicate measurements. Zeta potential measurement was re-plotted using GraphPad Prism 5.

Capillary Western immunoblotting (ProteinJess system)

Following previously established protocol32,33, Capillary-based Western blotting was performed using the automated ProteinSimple Wes System according to the manufacturer’s protocol. EV protein lysates or whole-tissue extracts were first diluted with 0.1× Sample Buffer. Subsequently, four parts of the diluted sample were mixed with one part of 5× Fluorescent Master Mix, which contains 5× sample buffer, 5× fluorescent standards, and 200 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). The mixture was denatured at 95 °C for 5 minutes prior to loading. The fluorescent standard, comprising three internal fluorescent proteins, served as a migration reference to normalize protein separation distances across individual capillaries, as the molecular weight ladder is applied only to the first capillary. After denaturation, prepared samples, blocking reagent, diluted primary antibodies (1:1000 for anti-CD63 [BIO-RAD, MCA2042GA], 1:1000 for Casein [Thermofisher Scientific, BS-0813R], and 1:2000 for both GAPDH [CST, D16H11] and β-actin [CST, #4970]), HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and chemiluminescent substrate were loaded into designated wells of the assay plate along with a biotinylated molecular weight ladder. Protein separation and immunodetection were performed automatically within the Jess capillary system, which integrates electrophoresis, antibody incubation, and detection into a single streamlined workflow. Data were analyzed using the Compass software (ProteinSimple, version 6.3.0).

Transmission electron microscopy

Milk-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) were visualized by transmission electron microscopy following negative staining using a previously established protocol with slight modification34. Briefly, Ultrathin Carbon Type-A 400-mesh copper grids (Tedpella, 01822) were pre-treated with plasma for 10 seconds using a glow discharger (PELCO). A volume of <20 μL of EV suspension (1:5000) was deposited onto a paraffin film, and the grid was injected into the EV solution and incubated for 1 minutes at room temperature to allow adsorption. The grid was briefly washed in filtered D.I water while the excess solution was dropped on a wick paper. For conventional negative staining, grids were incubated using a solution of <20ul of 2% uranyl acetate (Tedpella, 19481) for 1 minute. Subsequently, the grid was allowed to dry at room temperature without washing without any buffer. Samples were imaged using a Tecnai TG2 Osiris S/TEM system (FEI, USA) at 200 kV. Images were acquired using a 4 K CETA TEM CCD camera (Olympus).

Device fabrication

The fabrication process starts with a SiO₂ substrate. An initial 10-second descum step is carried out, after which NR9 photoresist is spin-coated onto the surface. The coated substrate is then baked at 150 °C for 60 seconds to solidify the resist. UV exposure at 408 nm is performed for 7 seconds using a chromium mask, followed by a post-exposure bake at 100 °C for 180 seconds. Development in MF-319 for 6 seconds reveals the pattern, and a subsequent 10-second descum step results in NR9 pillar structures on the substrate. A thin 10 nm chromium adhesion layer is deposited first, followed by gold deposition through electron beam evaporation. In the final step, the sample is sonicated in acetone for 8 minutes to lift off the remaining resist, yielding the completed chip.

Quantification of contrast

To evaluate the scattering contrast caused by the particles, the variation in intensity at the particle’s center, which is quantified as the standard deviation (σariy disk), is measured. Separately, the background variation without any particles present (σbackground) is determined. The effective particle-induced contrast is then calculated by taking the cube root of the difference between these two standard deviations, expressed mathematically as (σariy disk- σbackground)1/3.

Tracking method

Particle tracking analysis was conducted using the open-source Python package Trackpy (soft-matter/trackpy: Trackpy v0.5.0)35, which employs a built-in algorithm to detect the fluorescent positions of trapped particles in each frame.

U-Net-based particle tracking

The U-Net architecture is illustrated in Fig. 1E. It has the encoder part (left side) that contracts the image to the latent space and the decoder part (right side) that reconstructs the image from the latent space. The encoder part consists of a series of repeated 3×3 convolutions, each followed by a rectified linear unit (ReLU) and a 2 × 2 max pooling operation for down-sampling. At each downsampling step, the number of feature channels is doubled. The decoder part consists of a series of 2 × 2 upsampling convolutions, which halve the feature channels, and two 3 × 3 convolutions, followed by ReLU. Additionally, the concatenation with the correspondingly cropped feature map between encoder and decoder at different levels compensates for the loss of a pixel border for every convolution. In total, the architecture has 23 convolution networks. The network is implemented in PyTorch and is trained with Google T100, with microscopy particle trapping images and corresponding mask images. Then, we used the Trackpy package35 in Python to construct the trajectories of each particle and analyze the size of particles to calibrate the contrast information of the particle.
