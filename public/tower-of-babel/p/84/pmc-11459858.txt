Comparative analysis of functional outcomes in arthroscopic repair for traumatic and non-traumatic rotator cuff injuries
Authors: Aixin Liu, Mingxing Wang, Baorui Zhang, Tong Lai, Gongyi Wu, Shilin Liu, Tao Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of the etiology of rotator cuff injury (RCI) on outcomes after rotator cuff repair remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the postoperative outcomes of patients with RCIs of different etiologies and identify the risk factors affecting prognosis.MethodsThis study included 73 patients with RCI who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. The patients were categorized into either a traumatic group or a non-traumatic group based on their history of trauma. Preoperative and postoperative assessments included shoulder range of motion, muscle strength, and physical examination findings specific to the shoulder for both groups. Clinical differences between arthroscopic repair of traumatic and non-traumatic RCIs were evaluated using univariate analysis. Logistic regression analysis determined independent risk factors for rotator cuff repair prognosis.ResultsAmong the 73 patients, 31 were in the traumatic group and 42 in the non-traumatic group, with a minimum postoperative follow-up of 12 months and a mean follow-up of 13.8 months. The duration of the disease was significantly longer in the non-traumatic group compared with the traumatic group (P < 0.001). The mean tear area was more significant in the traumatic group than in the non-traumatic group (P = 0.003), and the preoperative pain level and functional scores were better in the non-traumatic group compared with the traumatic group. Postoperatively, there were no differences between the two groups regarding scores, joint mobility, strength, and complications. At 12 months postoperatively, multivariate regression analysis indicated that full-layer tear (OR = 5.106, 95% CI: 1.137–22.927, P = 0.033), fat infiltration (OR = 6.020, 95% CI: 1.113–32.554, P = 0.037), and tear area (OR = 6.038, 95% CI: 2.122–17.177, P < 0.001) significantly affected the University of California at Los Angeles (UCLA) score.ConclusionCompared with non-traumatic RCI, traumatic RCI presents with more pronounced pain and impaired joint function preoperatively yet demonstrates comparable postoperative clinical outcomes. Full-layer tears, fat infiltration, and large tear areas are unfavorable factors affecting rotator cuff repair, and postoperative rehabilitation management of these patients should be emphasized.Keywords: Traumatic, Non-traumatic, Shoulder cuff repair, Prognostic factors

Background

Rotator cuff injuries (RCIs) are a leading cause of chronic pain and limited range of motion (ROM) in individuals with shoulder dysfunction in clinical settings [1, 2]. These injuries can be categorized as either traumatic or non-traumatic, resulting in substantial burdens on patients, society, and healthcare systems [3]. Traumatic tears commonly occur in younger individuals due to a fall or traumata to an abducted and externally rotated arm, while non-traumatic or chronic tears are believed to arise from age-related tendon degeneration, progressing from tendinopathy to partial layer and full-layer tears [4–6]. Surgical rotator cuff repair has been indicated to enhance shoulder discomfort and function, with the most significant functional outcomes associated with repairs that remain intact [7, 8]. Currently, the most common repair methods used are arthroscopic rotator cuff repair and acromioplasty [9]. Previous studies have indicated controversy regarding the outcomes of RCIs in different etiologies [10, 11]. Braune et al. [10] investigated the postoperative results of traumatic and non-traumatic RCIs, finding that patients with traumatic injuries experienced lower postoperative visual analog scale (VAS) scores, higher Constant–Murley scores, and improved ROM.

In contrast, Tan et al. [11] compared 1,300 patients with RCIs and found that patients with traumatic RCIs exhibited a higher rate of postoperative tears and shoulder-limited motor function. At the 2-year follow-up, Cederqvist et al. [12] observed that non-surgical and surgical RCI therapies improved pain and function equally. In addition to etiology effects, tear size, degree of chronicity, tendon involvement, and fat infiltration of the rotator cuff muscles have been identified as significant predictors of functional outcome and completeness of tendon healing [13–16].

This study aimed to analyze and compare the shoulder functional outcomes following arthroscopic rotator cuff repair in patients with traumatic and non-traumatic RCIs and to identify potential prognostic factors that may influence post-surgery clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

The imaging data and medical records of 97 patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair at Lu’an Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, affiliated with Anhui University of Chinese Medicine, from March 2021 to March 2023, were retrospectively analyzed. Our institutional review board approved the study and waived the requirement for informed consent due to its retrospective nature.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age above 18 years with a minimum follow-up duration of 12 months; (2) preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was used to diagnose RCI; (3) underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair for the first time.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) comorbid shoulder osteoarthritis, shoulder instability or fracture; (2) a history of shoulder surgery on the same side. (Fig. 1).

Study variables

Clinical information was collected by chart review, including gender, age, duration of symptoms, hand habituation, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking status, fat infiltration, and tear area. Most of these variables were associated with rotator cuff tendon healing. The shoulder symptoms studied included shoulder joint pain, restricted shoulder joint mobility, and decreased muscle strength. Preoperative and postoperative functional scores included the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score of shoulder surgeons, the Constant–Murley score of shoulder function, the simple shoulder test (SST) score of simple shoulder function examination, the University of California at Los Angeles (UCLA) shoulder joint scoring system, and the VAS score. The final results were recorded at the 12-month follow-up. Additionally, an orthopedic specialist performed a physical examination and measured shoulder ROM and strength in forward flexion (FF), external rotation (ER), and internal rotation (IR) at both time points.

Radiological assessments

All participants in the study experienced MRI evidence of the affected shoulder. All patients underwent anterior thoracic, posterior dorsal, and supraspinatus outlet radiography and MRI. We used high-resolution MRI (Philips, 3.0 T) to assess fatty degeneration and tendon integrity, with imaging including coronal, sagittal, and transected views (Fig. 2). The degree of fat infiltration was determined according to the Goutallier classification, and postoperative tendon integrity was graded according to the Sugaya classification [17]. Sugaya types I–III were considered tendon healing, whereas types IV and V were considered re-tears (Fig. 3). Neer subtyping of the acromion was determined according to its morphology (straight acromion, curved acromion, and hooked acromion). A limited sequence MRI was performed within a few days after surgery to ensure the efficacy of the surgical repair. MRI was performed again at each follow-up time point, and tendon healing results at the 12-month follow-up were recorded and included in statistical analyses. Two specialized orthopedic surgeons assessed all imaging results in a blinded manner.

Study cohorts

We categorized patients into two groups based on the cause of their RCIs: traumatic or non-traumatic groups. For the individual medical examination, the classification depended on the following four criteria: history, analysis of the accident, analysis of the post-accident (or pre-accident) complaints, and MRI results. First, to be classified in the traumatic group, a patient needed to report a specific injury associated with the onset of shoulder symptoms, including traffic injuries, sports injuries, and accidental falls, and an appropriate mechanism of injury. The patient was classified in the non-traumatic group if these criteria were not achieved. Second, we excluded degenerative (non-traumatic) lesions that existed before the traumatic mechanism, and the patient confirmed the requirement that the affected shoulder was previously asymptomatic [18]. Finally, in the early stages of traumatic RCIs, MRI imaging revealed a specific muscle edema condition.

Additionally, infraspinatus and subscapularis tears, Hill–Sachs injuries, and large tubercle fractures were more likely to be detected in patients reporting traumatic. In contrast, supraspinatus tendon degeneration, calcific tendinitis of the supraspinatus tendon, articular degeneration, and subacromial fat pad deficiency were more easily confirmed in the non-traumatic group [19]. We compared preoperative and postoperative measurements at the 12-month follow-up between the two groups and within each group. We also analyzed changes in values before and after surgery within and across the groups.

Surgery and rehabilitation protocol

The surgeries were conducted by a highly experienced senior orthopedic surgeon specializing in arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. Patients underwent surgery in the beach chair position under general anesthesia and brachial plexus nerve block. The repair technique, which could be single-row, double-row, or suture bridge, was determined based on the type of tear. After the rotator cuff repair, additional procedures such as acromioplasty, biceps tenodesis, or tendon fixation may be performed. In the first 0–6 weeks post-surgery, patients wore a shoulder external fixation brace and performed pendulum exercises. From 7 to 12 weeks, the brace was removed, shoulder ROM was increased, muscle strengthening began, and daily life movement training was initiated. After 12 weeks postoperatively, in addition to continuing the previous training, patients used tables, doors, elastic bands, dumbbells, and isokinetic muscle tester for strength exercises in all directions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (Version 24.0; IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). The distribution of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were analyzed using analysis of variance, while categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-square test. Nonparametric variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. A univariate analysis was performed on the clinical and demographic data of patients with traumatic and non-traumatic RCIs. According to the UCLA [20], patients were categorized into two groups based on their postoperative recovery status at the 12-month follow-up: patients with excellent recovery (UCLA score ≥ 29) and patients with poor recovery (UCLA score < 29). Subsequently, a univariate analysis was conducted on the clinical and demographic characteristics of both recovery groups. Lastly, a multivariate regression analysis was performed to ascertain the potential risk factors in patients who experienced postoperative recovery after surgery.

Results

Patient demographics and preoperative characteristics

A total of 73 patients were included in the final analysis: 31 in the traumatic group and 42 in the non-traumatic group. The mean age at the time of rotator cuff repair was 58.81 ± 6.31 years in the traumatic group and 60.19 ± 6.83 years in the non-traumatic group. The minimum postoperative follow-up was 12 months, with a mean follow-up of 13.8 months.

The mean duration of the disease was 5.48 ± 4.34 months in the traumatic group and 13.43 ± 6.76 months in the non-traumatic group (P < 0.001). The mean tear area was 3.68 ± 0.87 cm² in the traumatic group and 3.12 ± 0.57 cm² in the non-traumatic group (P = 0.003). The mean operative duration was 103.23 ± 15.33 min in the traumatic group and 93.45 ± 14.60 min in the non-traumatic group (P = 0.007). Preoperative physiotherapy was received by 6 patients (19.4%) in the traumatic group and 21 patients (50.0%) in the non-traumatic group (P = 0.007). In the traumatic group, 15 patients (48.4%) underwent intraoperative acromioplasty, and 30 (71.4%) underwent intraoperative acromioplasty in the non-traumatic group (P = 0.045). There were no statistical differences between the traumatic and the non-traumatic groups regarding the side of the affected limb, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, number of smokers, and repair techniques (Table 1).

Preoperative imaging evaluation

Preoperative MRI revealed the following distribution of Neer types in the traumatic group: 5 patients with Neer type I (16.1%), 16 patients with Neer type II (51.6%), and 10 patients with Neer type III (32.3%). In the non-traumatic group, there were 4 patients with Neer type I (9.5%), 12 patients with Neer type II (28.6%), and 26 patients with Neer type III (61.9%) (P = 0.043). There was no statistical difference between the two groups regarding Goutallier grade (Table 1) [21]. In the involved tendons, injury to two tendons was the most common: 18 patients (58.1%) in the traumatic group and 22 patients (52.4%) in the non-traumatic group. There were no statistical differences between the two groups regarding tendon involvement and tear patterns (Table 2).

Functional outcomes

Preoperatively, pain and functional scores were better in the non-traumatic group than in the traumatic group, as measured by VAS score (P = 0.019), ASES score (P < 0.001), Constant–Murley score (P = 0.005), SST score (P = 0.019), and UCLA score (P = 0.004) (Fig. 4). There was no difference in the scores between the two groups postoperatively, with VAS score (P = 0.745), ASES score (P = 0.624), Constant–Murley score (P = 0.471), SST score (P = 0.403), and UCLA score (P = 0.522). Preoperatively, FF and ER were better in the non-traumatic group compared with the traumatic group (P = 0.023; P = 0.005), and the difference was statistically non-significant postoperatively (P = 0.738; P = 0.833). FF strength was better in the preoperative non-traumatic group compared with the traumatic group (P = 0.041), but there was no statistically significant difference postoperatively (P = 0.804). Besides, there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups regarding postoperative joint stiffness and re-tear rates (Table 3).

Factors influencing postoperative UCLA scores

At 12 months postoperatively, patients in the poor recovery group exhibited a longer duration of illness (P = 0.045), a higher number of full-layer tears (P = 0.034), greater depth of fat infiltration (P = 0.011), several subscapular tendon injuries (P = 0.029), and larger tear areas (P = 0.005) compared to those in the excellent recovery group (Table 4). Subsequently, we established a logistic regression model using UCLA score ≥ 29 and UCLA score < 29 as the dependent variable. The results indicated that full-layer tear (OR = 5.106, 95% CI: 1.137–22.927, P = 0.033), fat infiltration (OR = 6.020, 95% CI: 1.113–32.554, P = 0.037), and tear area (OR = 6.038, 95% CI: 2.122–17.177, P < 0.001) were independent risk factors for the postoperative prognosis of rotator cuff repair (Table 5).

Optimal cutoff thresholds

Analysis of ROCs revealed optimal cutoff thresholds for tear area and could distinguish between excellent and poor recovery groups (Fig. 5). We found that the optimal tear area threshold was 3.735 (AUC, 0.829; 95% CI: 0.721–0.938), with a sensitivity of 70.6%, and a specificity of 85.7%.

Discussion

This study examined the clinical outcomes of arthroscopic rotator cuff repair in patients with traumatic and non-traumatic RCIs. The results indicated that both groups achieved comparable positive clinical outcomes despite patients in the traumatic group presenting with larger preoperative tear areas, higher pain scores, and poorer joint function compared with those in the non-traumatic group. Moreover, patients with excellent postoperative recovery exhibited shorter preoperative courses, lower rates of full-layer tears, fat infiltration, subscapularis tendon injuries, and smaller tear areas. Multivariable logistic regression analysis indicated that full-layer tear, fat infiltration, and tear area were significant predictors of postoperative prognosis of rotator cuff repair.

The prevailing explanation for RCIs in the current study is the exogenous impingement (traumatic) and endogenous degeneration (non-traumatic) theory [22, 23]. Non-traumatic etiologies usually present with a chronic course, which may be due to the longer asymptomatic period of non-traumatic injuries [24]. This is consistent with our findings. Previous studies [15, 25, 26] have demonstrated an association among fat infiltration, muscle atrophy, and postoperative functional outcome after rotator cuff repair, with more severe fat infiltration and muscle atrophy leading to worse postoperative functional recovery. Although we did not find a significant difference in the degree of fat infiltration between the two groups, this result aligned with the well-known pattern of progressive fat infiltration over several years [13].

Traumatic RCIs frequently exhibit more extensive tearing and increased pain in the initial stages. Consistent with prior research findings [11], the mean tear area in the traumatic group of our study was notably larger at 3.68 ± 0.87 cm² compared with the non-traumatic group (P = 0.003). Furthermore, acromioplasty was performed significantly more frequently in the non-traumatic group compared with the traumatic group, suggesting a greater need for acromioplasty in non-traumatic RCIs compared with traumatic injuries. Our study revealed that the traumatic group exhibited lower preoperative shoulder ROM and strength compared with the non-traumatic group, potentially due to higher preoperative VAS scores and larger tear areas in the traumatic group. Notably, both groups of patients exhibited similar postoperative functional outcomes. In addition, there was no significant difference between the two groups regarding the incidence of joint stiffness and re-tear. We hypothesized that the traumatic group would exhibit superior clinical outcomes due to increased reparative capacity. However, our findings contradict previous reports [10, 27], possibly because of the comparable age distribution between patient groups in our study and the absence of significant muscle degeneration in the non-traumatic group.

To determine the factors influencing the success of rotator cuff repair, we analyzed predictive variables such as gender, age, tear size, duration, and fat infiltration. This study used the UCLA score as the clinical prognostic outcome, a scoring system that objectively and comprehensively evaluates patient clinical outcomes in five dimensions. Many studies examining the prognosis of RCIs have primarily emphasized the quality of tendon repair over functional recovery [28–30]. Nevertheless, functional outcomes hold greater clinical significance, indicating successful rotator cuff restoration more than tendon healing status. Our analysis suggests that tear size and fat infiltration are the most influential predictors impacting functional outcomes following rotator cuff repair, as consistently reported in the study [31, 32]. Significantly, our study identified full-layer tears as a pertinent predictor of postoperative outcomes in rotator cuff repair, a factor that has been underreported in the existing research. For these patients, the timing of postoperative rehabilitation is critical, and consideration must be given to allowing sufficient time for tendon healing when implementing shoulder mobility and strength exercise [33]. Since re-tear is a common problem after rotator cuff repair, active sports and strengthening after rotator cuff repair are often limited by the biology of tendon healing, and appropriate timing of rehabilitation is critical for any consistent intervention to maximize clinical prognostic outcomes [34]. Meanwhile, we noted that certain prognostic factors, including disease duration, history of trauma, duration of surgery, preoperative physiotherapy, and subscapularis tendon injuries, have not been adequately addressed in prior studies. While the influence of these factors on functional outcomes may be deemed less significant compared to other variables, such as tear size or fat infiltration, the lack of comprehensive prognostic studies necessitates the identification of variables that could impact outcomes and merit further investigation in future research.

Regarding the discrepancy between the findings of the study and existing scholarly research, it is posited that the relative homogeneity of the studied population, characterized by small variances in baseline characteristics between traumatic and non-traumatic cohorts, may have hindered the identification of prognostic factors. This necessitates greater diversity in outcomes. Additionally, our institution’s rigorous selection criteria in surgical decision-making may have influenced the observed discrepancies. For example, patients with severe underlying disease or a Goutallier classification of more than two are excluded from arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. In contrast, other research studies have employed less strict eligibility criteria, potentially resulting in outcome variability.

This study has several limitations. First, all patients were operated on in the same hospital and under the same surgeon, which may reduce the bias of the data but may also skew the distribution of the data. Second, this was a retrospective cohort study, whereas prospective studies have more convincing results. Lastly, the follow-up period was relatively short, and long-term follow-up is needed to assess the differences between surgical repair of traumatic and non-traumatic RCIs and their prognostic factors.

Conclusion

Traumatic patients have higher preoperative pain levels and poorer joint function. Nevertheless, their clinical outcomes after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair are comparable to those of non-traumatic patients. Meanwhile, full-layer tear, fat infiltration, and tear area are essential predictors of postoperative clinical function. Therefore, attention should be paid to the postoperative rehabilitation management of such patients, and individualized rehabilitation programs should be provided.
