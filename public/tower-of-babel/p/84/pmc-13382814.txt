Curcumin-releasing oxidized konjac/GelMA hydrogel for ROS-mediated suppression of postoperative prostate cancer recurrence and metastasis
Authors: Liang He, Chenke Wei, Yukai Guo, Xinting Yang, Quan Lin, Wan Wang, Chao Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postoperative recurrence remains a major challenge in prostate cancer (PCa) treatment, and insufficient drug concentration at the tumor site represents a critical barrier to effectively suppressing tumor regrowth and lymph node metastasis. To address these limitations, we developed a photo-crosslinkable oxidized konjac glucomannan/gelatin-methacrylate hydrogel (OG@Cur) for localized and sustained delivery of curcumin (Cur) during the postoperative period. This hydrogel forms a porous and adhesive network that enables prolonged curcumin release during biodegradation while simultaneously facilitating hemostasis. Cur released from the hydrogel maintained a high local concentration at the surgical site, thereby enhancing antitumor activity through the induction of intracellular reactive oxygen species accumulation. In a postoperative PCa recurrence model, this localized and sustained delivery strategy significantly suppressed tumor regrowth and reduced lymph node metastasis, while exhibiting good biocompatibility. Overall, this hydrogel system, which continuously releases curcumin, provides an effective localized therapeutic approach for preventing postoperative prostate cancer recurrence and may offer advantages over conventional systemic therapies.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most prevalent malignancies among men worldwide, ranking as the second most frequently diagnosed cancer and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality (Raychaudhuri et al., 2025). Although progress has been made in early diagnosis and radical prostatectomy, a significant number of patients continue to develop local recurrence or distant metastasis after surgery. About one-third of patients develop biochemical recurrence (BCR) after radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy, increasing to ∼50% in high-risk cases (Mazzone et al., 2025). BCR of PCa remains a major challenge, driven by residual microscopic disease and disseminated tumor cells resistant to conventional therapy (Rizzo et al., 2025). Current adjuvant therapies, including androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, have contributed to improved outcomes in selected patient populations; however, these approaches are often associated with systemic toxicity, drug resistance, and limited long-term efficacy (Burgess et al., 2021). Importantly, insufficient drug accumulation at the tumor or surgical site has emerged as a critical barrier, making it difficult to effectively eliminate residual tumor cells and prevent tumor regrowth and lymph node metastasis. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop safer and more effective strategies to suppress PCa postoperative recurrence, progression, and metastasis.

Natural compounds have attracted increasing attention as complementary or alternative therapeutic agents. One of the most studied natural compounds, curcumin (Cur), a polyphenolic compound derived from the rhizome of Curcuma longa, has been extensively studied due to its pleiotropic pharmacological activities (Ahmad et al., 2024). Cur showed anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiproliferative effects. Moreover, preclinical evidence has demonstrated its capacity to inhibit multiple mechanisms involved in PCa growth and metastasis (Einafshar et al., 2024). In addition, increasing evidence suggests that Cur can exert antitumor effects by inducing intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby promoting cancer cell death (Hao et al., 2023).Furthermore, Cur is widely recognized for its good biocompatibility and low toxicity, which making it an attractive candidate for long-term or adjuvant use in cancer patients (El-Saadony et al., 2023). However, the clinical application of curcumin is limited by its poor solubility in water, rapid systemic metabolism, and low bioavailability (Einafshar et al., 2024).These limitations reduce its therapeutic efficacy when administered by conventional routes, underscoring the necessity of innovative delivery systems.

Hydrogel-based drug delivery platforms have emerged as a promising solution to overcome the shortcomings of conventional small-molecule administration (Mikhail et al., 2023). In situ hydrogel platforms, with their ability for local delivery, controlled release, and sustained supply, hold great potential as alternatives to minimize systemic side effects while enhancing direct antitumor efficacy of anticancer drugs (Feng et al., 2023). Hydrogels are particularly advantageous for poorly water-soluble drugs, such as Cur, which tend to aggregate, form large particles, and sediment in aqueous solutions, making direct administration ineffective. In contrast, Cur can be uniformly dispersed in hydrogel precursor solutions without noticeable aggregation, enabling efficient loading and subsequent controlled release (Han et al., 2022). For postoperative cancer therapy, hydrogels offer distinct advantages: (i) they can be implanted or injected directly into the surgical cavity, thereby achieving high local drug concentrations while minimizing systemic exposure; (ii) their physicochemical properties can be tailored to control drug release kinetics, ensuring prolonged therapeutic activity; and (iii) their soft, tissue-like properties facilitate integration with the surrounding microenvironment, reducing the risk of adverse immune reactions. Recent advances in hydrogel technology have enabled the encapsulation of various chemotherapeutic agents, nucleic acids, and bioactive compounds, highlighting their potential in cancer therapy.

Konjac glucomannan (KGM) is a natural polysaccharide primarily found in the tubers of the konjac plant. KGM possesses excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and gelation ability. Nevertheless, its high viscosity, even at low concentrations, poses a considerable limitation to its applications. Oxidizing KGM to produce OKGM can effectively enhance its water solubility and reduce viscosity (Lin et al., 2024). Gelatin is a natural polymer derived from the hydrolysis of collagen, whose structure closely resembles that of native extracellular matrix. However, its poor mechanical strength and limited adhesiveness restrict its broader biomedical applications. To overcome these limitations, gelatin was chemically modified and functionally functionalized using methacrylic anhydride, yielding methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) rich in carbon–carbon double bonds. GelMA not only retains excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and water absorption, but also exhibits significantly improved mechanical properties and adhesiveness, making it a versatile platform for biomedical applications (Eskandarinia et al., 2024). Carboxymethyl chitosan (CMC), a water-soluble and biocompatible chitosan derivative, has been widely employed as hydrogel for localized drug delivery and controlled release (Liu et al., 2025).

In this study, to overcome the difficulty of suppressing postoperative PCa regrowth and lymph node metastasis caused by insufficient drug concentration at the surgical site, we developed a photo-crosslinkable dual-network hydrogel (OG@Cur) for localized and sustained delivery of Cur. This system forms a porous and adhesive network with favorable biocompatibility, injectability, hemostatic capability, and tunable degradation behavior, enabling efficient loading and prolonged local release of poorly water-soluble Cur. By maintaining a high local Cur concentration in the postoperative tumor bed, OG@Cur is expected to eliminate residual tumor cells and suppress recurrence and metastasis through ROS-related antitumor activity. Therefore, this hydrogel system provides a multifunctional perioperative strategy for intraoperative hemostasis and postoperative PCa control. Materials and methods

Materials

CMC and methacrylic anhydride (MA) were provided by Aladdin Reagent Co. (Shanghai, China). Konjac glucomannan (KGM) and curcumin (Cur) were obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Sodium periodate (NaIO4, ∼99%) and gelatin from porcine skin were ordered from Sigma-Aldrich. Lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) was provided by Yuanye Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Deionized water was made by using a Milli-QA10 filtration system (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). RM-1 PCa cell line is reserved by our laboratory (Wang et al., 2023a). High-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (HG-DMEM) was purchased from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Procell, Wuhan, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). Clear 6-well and 96-well tissue culture polystyrene (TCP) plates were purchased from Corning Costar Co. (Cambridge, MA, USA). The apoptosis detection kit and the Live/Dead cell staining kit were purchased from Servicebio Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Cell and animal experiments

The mouse PCa cell lines RM-1 was obtained from ATCC and preserved in our laboratory (He et al., 2025). RM-1 is a murine prostate cancer cell line originally derived from C57BL/6 mice. RM-1 cells were cultured in HG-DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. All experimental animals were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The animal study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Basic Medical Sciences, Jilin University, and all procedures were performed in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Synthesis of oxidized konjac glucomannan (OKGM)

OKGM was synthesized mainly as reported (Pan et al., 2025). Briefly, 2.0 g of KGM was dissolved in 200 mL of deionized water under stirring. Subsequently, 0.6 g of NaIO4 was added, and the mixture was stirred for 12 h under dark conditions. Then, 4 mL of ethylene glycol was added to the above solution to terminate the reaction. The mixture was stirred for 2 h, and then dialyzed in deionized water for 3 days at room temperature, followed by freezing and lyophilizing.

Synthesis of gelatin methacrylate (GelMA)

GelMA was synthesized mainly according to a previously reported method (Khoshmaram et al., 2024). Briefly, 10.0 g of gelatin was dissolved in 100.0 mL of PBS and stirred until fully dissolved. Then, add 1 mL of MA to the gelatin solution and continue stirring for 3 h to ensure complete reaction. Subsequently, the reaction solution was diluted with a large volume of PBS. Finally, the above solution was dialyzed in deionized water for 7 days, followed by freezing and lyophilizing.

Preparation of hydrogels

Slowly add curcumin to a 2% CMC solution to achieve a final Cur concentration of 10 mg/mL. Continuously stir and sonicate until the curcumin is uniformly dispersed in the solution with no visible large particle precipitation. Subsequently, sequentially add 2% GelMA and 0.5% photoinitiator (LAP), stirring for 20 min to ensure thorough mixing. Then the above mixture was uniformly blended with a 2% OKGM solution at a 1:1 volume ratio. The mixture was poured into molds and photopolymerized under a 405 nm UV lamp, forming OG@Cur hydrogels within 0.5–3 min of UV irradiation. For in vivo experiments, the content of curcumin loading in the OG@Cur hydrogel can be controlled approximately 10, 20, 30, 40 mg/Kg as an in-vivo dose.

In vitro degradation of hydrogels

To evaluate the degradation rate of the hydrogels, we immersed the hydrogels in PBS at 37 °C. The hydrogels were removed after a specified period of time, then freeze-dried. The degradation performance of hydrogels was evaluated based on their residual mass percentage. The weights of the initial hydrogels were recorded as m0. The weights at different points were measured and recorded as m. Thus, the residual rate of the hydrogels is calculated as formula 1:(1)Residual Weight%=mm0×100%

In vitro release of drug

Curcumin powder of varying weights was dissolved in a PBS-ethanol solution (v/v = 3:2) to prepare a series of curcumin solutions with a concentration gradient. Then, the absorbance of each solution was measured using Ultraviolet-visible Spectrophotometer to obtain the working curve of curcumin concentration versus absorbance.

To measure the amount of released curcumin, the hydrogels loaded with curcumin were immersed in a PBS-ethanol solution (v/v = 3:2) at 37 °C. Then, 5.0 mL of the solution from each group was sampled at the designed time points to measure its absorbance until reaching its plateau as an indication of complete release of curcumin. The concentrations of curcumin could be obtained according to the standard curve, and the cumulative release rate of curcumin was calculated.

Adhesion of hydrogels

The adhesion properties of hydrogels were measured through shear tensile tests. Before the experiment, substrates of various materials were selected, such as glass, wood, aluminum sheets, polytetrafluoroethylene sheets, and pig skin, to evaluate the adhesion performance of the hydrogels on different substrates. During testing, the hydrogels were placed between two identical substrates and tested using a universal testing machine equipped with a 100 N sensor. The contact area between the hydrogels and the substrates, as well as the maximum load during the shear process, was accurately recorded. The adhesion strength of hydrogels was defined as the maximum load during the shear process divided by the contact area.

In vivo degradation of OG and OG@Cur

Female BALB/c mice (average body weight ∼ 20 g) were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation. 0.1 mL of either OG or OG@Cur was implanted subcutaneously on the dorsal side. The incision was then closed with interrupted sutures. To prevent infection, each mouse received a single intramuscular injection of 8 U Penicillin Sodium dissolved in 0.9% saline. At predetermined time points (days 1, 3, 5, and 7), the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the gel tissues were dissected, weighted and photographed.

Continuously, Free Cur, extraction of OG and different timepoint (2 and 7 days) OG@Cur were added into the wells. After 24 h of incubation, the culture medium in each well was removed and replaced with fresh DMEM containing 10% CCK-8 solution. Live/Dead cell assay

For the Live/Dead viability assay and morphological analysis, RM-1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well. After 24 h of culture, the medium was discarded and replaced with fresh medium containing extracts of OG or OG@Cur (2- or 7-day) and free Cur. Then, RM-1 Cells were co-cultured for another 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with PBS and stained with a Calcein-AM/PI reagent mixture (Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Finally, the cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (IX73, Olympus, Japan).

Flow cytometry apoptosis analysis

To determine the apoptosis caused by Cur, we performed the flow cytometry analysis. 3 × 105 RM-1 cells in DMEM culture medium were seeded on 12-well plate. After 24 h cultured, Free Cur, extraction of OG and different time period OG@Cur were added into the wells. After another 24 h incubation, cells were harvested followed by Annexin V-FITC/PI staining through Annexin V-FITC/PI Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Then, the cells were subjected to flow cytometry (FACSymphony AS, BD, US).

Intracellular ROS detection

To evaluate intracellular ROS generation in RM-1 cells after different treatments, a DCFH-DA fluorescent probe assay was performed. RM-1 cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. Then, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing free Cur, extract of OG, and extracts of OG@Cur collected at different time points (2 and 7 days). After another 24 h of incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with DCFH-DA probe solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China) in serum-free DMEM at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to remove excess probe. Finally, intracellular green fluorescence intensity was observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope. The fluorescence intensity reflected the intracellular ROS level. Finally, semi-quantitative analysis of ROS fluorescence was performed using ImageJ.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) assay

The ΔΨm of RM-1 cells was evaluated using a JC-1 staining kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China). RM-1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. After attachment, the cells were treated with free Cur, extract of OG, and extracts of OG@Cur collected at different time points (2 and 7 days) for another 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with JC-1 staining working solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark according to the manufacturer's instructions. After staining, the cells were washed 3 times with JC-1 staining buffer. The fluorescence images were then captured under a fluorescence microscope. In healthy cells with high ΔΨm, JC-1 formed aggregates and emitted red fluorescence, whereas in damaged cells with decreased ΔΨm, JC-1 remained in monomeric form and emitted green fluorescence. Therefore, the ratio of red to green fluorescence was used to evaluate changes in ΔΨm.

Wound healing assay

RM-1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and cultured until reaching over 90% density. Then a uniform scratch was created across the cell layer by using a 200 μL pipette tip. The wells were then washed 3 times with PBS to remove detached cells. Cells were treated with extraction of OG and different timepoint (2 and 7 days) OG@Cur serum-free medium. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 h, 12 h, and 24 h using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). The wound closure rate was quantified using ImageJ software by calculating the percentage of wound area reduction relative to the initial wound area.

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size, Corning, USA). For the migration assay, 1 × 105 RM-1 cells in 200 μL serum-free DMEM were seeded into the upper chamber, and 600 μL complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 12 h of incubation to allow cell adhesion, the medium in the upper chamber was replaced with 200 μL fresh serum-free DMEM containing free Cur, OG extract, or OG@Cur at different time points (2 and 7 days). Following an additional 24 h incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. Then, Cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, and washed with PBS. Representative images were captured under inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan), and the number of migrated/invaded cells was quantified in at least five random fields per well using ImageJ software.

In vivo postoperative PCa recurrence inhibition

To evaluate the anti-recurrence and anti-metastasis effects of OG@Cur, an in vivo postoperative PCa recurrence model was established. Five weeks male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into 4 groups with each group consisting of 5 mice: Control, OG, Free Cur, and OG@Cur. 1× 106 RM-1 PCa cells suspended in saline were subcutaneously injected into the right chest to establish the PCa xenografted model. Then, the size of tumor and body weight of experiment mice was measured for every single day. Tumor size was calculated by using Formula 3 (L: length; W: Width). When the average tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, all experiment mice underwent tumor resection.

Before surgery, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. Then, 90% of the tumors were excised through operation. In the OG and OG@Cur groups, 0.1 mL of the corresponding hydrogel was implanted at the tumor site. While the content of curcumin loaded within the OG@Cur hydrogel is equivalent to an in-vivo dose of 40 mg/kg, based on the average body weight of the mice. In the Free Cur group, 1 mg of curcumin dispersed in saline was evenly applied to the tumor wound surface. For the Control group, an equal volume of saline was applied. The wound was then closed with interrupted sutures. After surgery, a single intramuscular injection of 8 U Penicillin Sodium in 0.9% saline for each mouse was administered to prevent infections. Mice were monitored daily for body weight and tumor growth. The experiment was terminated when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3, at which point five of the experiment mice were euthanized. JC-1 staining of freshly isolated tissue-derived cells

Fresh tumor tissues (100–200 mg) were minced into approximately 1 mm3 fragments and transferred into grinding tubes. Each tube was supplemented with 1 mL of filtered tissue dissociation solution (Servicebio, G4154-50 T) and two 3-mm stainless steel beads (Servicebio, G0103-200G). Tissue dissociation was performed using a tissue grinder (SWE-FP PLUS, Servicebio) at 25 Hz for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting cell suspension was collected and filtered through a 200-mesh cell strainer to remove tissue debris. The cells were then washed three times with PBS and resuspended in 1 mL of complete culture medium containing fetal bovine serum and phenol red.

For mitochondrial membrane potential analysis, 1 mL of JC-1 working solution (Servicebio, G1515-100 T) was added to the cell suspension, and the cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, protected from light. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. The ratio of JC-1 aggregates (red fluorescence) to monomers (green fluorescence) was used to evaluate the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm).

For the detection of mitochondrial membrane potential in frozen tumor sections, fresh tumor tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and cryosectioned into 10-μm-thick sections. Before staining, the sections were washed twice with PBS to remove residual OCT compound. The sections were then incubated with 1 mL of JC-1 staining working solution (Servicebio, G1515-100 T) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, the sections were gently washed twice with PBS to remove excess dye and imaged using fluorescence microscope.

Detection of intracellular ROS in freshly isolated tissue-derived cells

Fresh tumor tissues (100–200 mg) were minced into approximately 1 mm3 fragments and transferred into grinding tubes. Each tube was supplemented with 1 mL of filtered tissue dissociation solution (Servicebio, G4154-50 T) and two 3-mm stainless steel beads (Servicebio, G0103-200G). Tissue dissociation was performed using a tissue grinder (Servicebio, SWE-FP PLUS) at 25 Hz for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting cell suspension was collected and filtered through a 200-mesh cell strainer to remove tissue debris. The cells were then washed three times with PBS and resuspended in 1 mL of 1× DHE staining working solution (Servicebio, G1746-100 T). The cell suspension was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, the cells were washed two to three times with PBS and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry to determine intracellular ROS levels.

For the detection of ROS in frozen tumor sections, fresh tumor tissues were embedded in OCT compound and cryosectioned into 10-μm-thick sections. Before staining, the sections were washed twice with PBS to remove residual OCT compound. The sections were incubated with 1 mL of 1× DHE staining working solution (Servicebio, G1746-100 T) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, the sections were gently washed with PBS to remove excess dye and imaged using fluorescence microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the NcmSpin Rapid RNA Extraction Kit (M5106, NCM Biotech) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the ExonScript All-in-One RT SuperMix with dsDNase kit (A505–01, Exongen). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using UltraStart Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (A411, Exongen) on a LineGene 9600 Plus Real-Time PCR System (BIOER). GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

Histology analysis

After the in vivo treatment experiment, the mice were sacrificed, and tumor tissues along with TDLNs and major organs were collected and fixed in 4% (w/v) PFA for 24 h. The samples then underwent a series of treatments, including dehydration, clearing, wax infiltration, and embedding. Paraffin sections approximately 5 μm thick were subsequently prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The H&E-stained sections of major organs, TDLNs and tumor tissues were then observed under a microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti, Optical Apparatus Co., Ardmore, PA, USA).

Paraffin-embedded tumor tissue sections, each approximately 5 μm thick, were prepared for immunohistochemical analysis of Ki-67 and TUNEL. In addition, paraffin-embedded TDLN sections were employed to immunohistochemical staining for pan-cytokeratin (PanCK) to assess lymph node metastasis. The activities of Ki-67, TUNEL, and PanCK were measured using the following method: (1) antigen retrieval, (2) serum blocking, (3) primary antibody addition, and (4) secondary antibody addition. For the evaluation of Ki-67 and TUNEL expression, the number of positive cells was counted per 100 cells within a single field of view. For PanCK staining, representative images were captured under identical microscope settings, and the positive staining area was quantified using ImageJ software. This process was repeated across five different fields, and the results were subsequently calculated and analyzed.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.4.3, GraphPad Software, USA). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc tests.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of OG@Cur

Considering the high risk of postoperative recurrence and metastasis in PCa and the difficulty of eradicating residual tumor cells at the surgical site, we developed a photo-crosslinked curcumin-loaded hydrogel system (OG@Cur) for localized and sustained postoperative therapy (Shore et al., 2024). Benefiting from the photo-crosslinking process, the hydrogel could rapidly form a stable and porous network at the lesion site, which favored high drug loading and prolonged local release of Cur. This design aimed at maintain an effective local drug concentration during the early postoperative period, thereby improving the elimination of residual tumor cells and suppressing subsequent tumor regrowth and lymph node metastasis. In addition to its therapeutic potential, the adhesive property of the hydrogel may also facilitate wound management during the perioperative period.

To construct OG@Cur, Cur was first uniformly dispersed in the CMC/GelMA precursor solution, followed by mixing with OKGM and subsequent photo-crosslinking to form hydrogel network. We then systematically characterized the physicochemical properties of the resulting OG@Cur system (Fig. 2). As shown in Fig. 2A, OG@Cur exhibited favorable photo-crosslinking properties, enabling its rapid transition from a liquid to a stable solid state under UV irradiation, with gelation occurring within 0.5–3 min depending on the formulation and irradiation conditions. This rapid gelation behavior is particularly advantageous for intraoperative application, allowing the hydrogel to quickly conform to irregular surgical cavities and achieve effective local retention. The SEM images revealed a highly porous and interconnected microstructure in OG@Cur (Fig. 2B), which is favorable for drug encapsulation and sustained release. The UV–Vis spectra confirmed the successful encapsulation of Cur into the OG network (Fig. 2C). By using UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy, OG@Cur hydrogels exhibited characteristic Cur peak at 432 nm, indicating successful Cur incorporation into the hydrogel network. Then, the rheological behavior of the OG@Cur hydrogel was characterized by measuring the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) over a strain range of 0.1–1000% using a rotational rheometer. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, the G′ and G″ curves intersect at a strain of 136%, which represents the critical strain point of the hydrogel. Below this critical point, G′ consistently exceeds G″ and remains nearly constant, indicating that the hydrogel maintains a stable gel-like state. After the strain surpasses the critical value, G″ becomes greater than G′, suggesting collapse of the gel network and transition to sol-like state. OG@Cur exhibiting slightly altered storage modulus compared with blank OG (Fig. 2D), suggesting that Cur loading may influence the crosslinking density. and the incorporation of Cur slightly enhanced the mechanical strength of OG@Cur. The XPS spectrum demonstrated elemental signals corresponding to Cur, validating its presence (Fig. 2F). The XPS spectrum confirmed the presence of C, N, and O elements. Subsequently, we evaluated the in vitro degradation behavior of OG@Cur together with the release profile of Cur. As shown in Fig. 2J, OG@Cur degraded steadily and was completely degraded within 12 days. Owing to the intrinsic biodegradability of KGM and gelatin, the hydrogel underwent gradual enzymatic and hydrolytic degradation (Yang et al., 2025), while its degradation kinetics could be tuned by adjusting the composition and crosslinking density. Importantly, the porous crosslinked structure of OG@Cur favored efficient Cur loading and sustained release during degradation (Fig. 2K), which is essential for maintaining prolonged local drug exposure up to 12 days at the postoperative tumor site and for improving the suppression of residual tumor cells, recurrence, and metastasis.

To evaluate the adhesion capability of OG@Cur, it was first evaluated on various materials, including glass, wood, pigskin, aluminum, and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) (Fig. S2A). OG@Cur exhibited strong adhesion on all tested materials, demonstrating excellent interfacial affinity. This enhanced adhesion is attributed to the multiple hydroxyl groups of Cur, which introduce additional interaction sites within the hydrogel network and promote the formation of abundant hydrogen bonds, thereby strengthening the interfacial interactions between OG@Cur and the substrate. Meanwhile, OG@Cur exhibited higher adhesive strength than OG (Fig. S2B). This is because the polyhydroxyl structure of Cur introduces additional interaction sites within the hydrogel, promoting the formation of more hydrogen bonds and thereby enhancing the interfacial interactions between OG@Cur and the underlying substrates (Fu et al., 2024). The enhanced adhesion can be attributed to interactions among OKGM, GelMA, and Cur, which improve interfacial contact and tissue affinity (Yuan et al., 2025).

Collectively, these findings confirmed that the porous and adhesive hydrogel structure of OG@Cur provides a stable and chemically compatible platform for efficient Cur loading and sustained release, thereby supporting prolonged local drug retention and its potential for further in vivo postoperative therapy.

OG@Cur was also effective in reducing the bleeding length in the liver resection model, while simultaneously shortening the bleeding time like blank OG. These results indicate that OG@Cur system can efficiently promote hemostasis, which show potential for intraoperative application. The remarkable hemostatic capability of both OG and OG@Cur can be attributed to two synergistic features. First, OG formed via photo-crosslinking have a loose highly porous internal network. This porous network not only enables efficient Cur loading but also enhances hemostasis by facilitating blood absorption and clot formation (Lim et al., 2024). Second, the strong adhesiveness of OG ensures firm attachment to the wound area, thereby reducing blood leakage and improving local sealing (Yang et al., 2024). Collectively, the combination of porous structure and strong tissue adhesion enable OG@Cur with excellent hemostatic efficacy, supporting it potential application of in vivo intraoperative hemostasis.

Effective hemostasis at the surgical site is closely associated with local retention and degradation behavior. Therefore, we next evaluated the in vivo biodegradation of OG@Cur (Fig. S3). As shown in the photos, the initial OG@Cur hydrogel maintained its shape with a yellow appearance after implantation (Fig. S3A). Over time, OG@Cur gradually decreased in volume and colour intensity, indicating continuous degradation and sustained Cur release. Quantitative analysis of the remaining weight confirmed a time-dependent mass loss profile, in which approximately 80% of OG and OG@Cur degraded within 7 days (Fig. S3B).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that OG@Cur exhibits a controllable and steady biodegradation process. This sustained releasing, gradual degradation, together with sustained Cur release, preserves local pharmacological exposure in the early PCa postoperative window. Combined with OG@Cur strong hemostatic performance, it shows excellent potential for intraoperative utility and postoperative prevention of PCa therapeutics.

OG@Cur effectively suppresses postoperative prostate tumor regrowth in vivo

An RM-1 prostate cancer recurrence model was established in mice to assess the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of OG@Cur against postoperative recurrence and metastasis (Wang et al., 2023b). Upon reaching a size of ∼100 mm3, 90% of the tumor was removed under operation, and the resection site was embedded with OG, OG@Cur, or free Cur. As depicted in tumor growth curves (Fig. 7B), the Ctrl and OG groups showed rapid PCa progression, while free Cur slightly delayed PCa growth. Compared with free Cur group, OG@Cur exhibited significant suppression of tumor growth (p<0.001). Tumor weight at the endpoint of the study also confirmed that OG@Cur exhibits the most efficacy of anti-tumor ability (Fig. 7D). The controlled release behavior of OG@Cur at the PCa site enables progressive curcumin release to achieve therapeutic efficacy. Beyond improving local drug retention, our additional in vitro findings further suggest that sustained curcumin delivery continuously modulates intracellular oxidative stress. Compared with free curcumin, OG@Cur induced higher ROS accumulation accompanied by more pronounced mitochondrial membrane depolarization, indicating persistent mitochondrial dysfunction (Figs. These changes were associated with enhanced apoptosis, reduced proliferative activity, and modulation of oxidative stress-related genes, including decreased Ki67 and PCNA expression together with altered HMOX1 levels (Fig. S9). Importantly, these mechanistic findings are consistent with the increased Caspase-3 expression, enhanced TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells, and reduced Ki-67 staining observed in vivo, further supporting that sustained local curcumin delivery translates into durable antitumor activity.

Overall, the integration of local delivery and prolonged retention contributes to enhanced therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects, highlighting its potential as a promising strategy for PCa therapy.

To further verify lymphatic metastasis, TDLNs were subjected to PanCK immunohistochemical staining. Collectively, the Cur released from OG@Cur could effectively inhibit the PCa recurrence with metastasis. Cur is confirmed to suppress epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), then reduce cytoskeletal remodeling, migration, and invasion of PCa (Xie et al., 2024). More importantly, OG@Cur provides sustained and relatively high local Cur exposure at the postoperative lesion site, which is critical for eliminating residual tumor cells and limiting subsequent metastatic spread. Therefore, OG@Cur achieves simultaneous inhibition of local recurrence and metastatic spread in PCa through sustained Cur exposure.

The present study demonstrates a multifunctional hydrogel platform capable of integrating intraoperative hemostasis with sustained local anti-tumor therapy. From clinical perspective, such strategy may be beneficial for PCa patients undergoing surgical intervention, where postoperative residual tumor cells and local recurrence remain important challenges. Compared with conventional systemic administration, localized hydrogel-mediated drug delivery may enhance drug retention at the surgical site while reducing systemic exposure. Therefore, the OG@Cur hydrogel platform represents a promising perioperative therapeutic approach that combines wound management with localized tumor suppression.

Despite the promising therapeutic potential of OG@Cur, some limitations should be discussed. First, although the OG@Cur system exhibited significant anti-tumor efficacy, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying PCa suppression were not fully elucidated. Future studies may provide deeper mechanistic insights. Second, the current strategy primarily focuses on local tumor control and postoperative recurrence prevention, while its therapeutic potential against established distant metastatic disease remains to be investigated. Nevertheless, this work provides a promising strategy for integrating intraoperative hemostasis with postoperative localized anti-tumor therapy for PCa management.

Conclusion

In this work, we established photo-crosslinkable OG@Cur system. The porous/adhesive architecture of OG@Cur supports bleeding control capability. Then, the sustained releasing of Cur showed significant suppression of PCa recurrence and inhibition of metastasis toward TDLN with satisfied biosafety. In addition, OG@Cur treatment increased intracellular ROS levels and decreased ΔΨm in PCa cells, suggesting a possible mechanism underlying its antitumor effect. These results demonstrate that OG@Cur is a potential dual-functional local therapeutic platform for intra- and postoperative treatment of PCa, enabling intraoperative hemostasis and postoperative, site-specific sustained delivery of Cur to inhibit PCa recurrence and metastasis. Future work should focus on improving properties of this sustained releasing photo-crosslinkable hydrogel to better support intraoperative interventions and postoperative localized therapy for various cancers.
