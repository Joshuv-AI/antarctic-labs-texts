Moral foundations and attitudes towards supervised consumption services: a regional moderation study
Authors: Robert P Dryden, Em M Pijl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch shows consistent links between supervised consumption services (SCS) and positive health outcomes for people who use drugs, as well as reductions in the economic burdens associated with illicit substance use. However, SCS often face considerable opposition and resistance from political figures and local community members. This opposition to SCS appears to be driven, in part, by perceived violations of individuals’ moral foundations.PurposeWe sought to interrogate a recently published and unexpected finding indicating that Manitobans’ had the highest levels of support for SCS among the Prairie Provinces, despite being the only Prairie Province without a currently operating SCS. The Prairie Provinces refer to Alberta, Manitoba, and Saskatchewan, and comprise a region of Western Canada characterized by grasslands and lowlands. We hypothesized that differences in the predictive strength of moral foundations on attitudes towards SCS between residents of provinces with (Alberta and Saskatchewan) and without (Manitoba) an operating SCS might help to clarify this phenomenon.MethodWe recruited a sample of 2116 adult participants from the three Prairie Provinces in Canada through online research panels (Manitoba, n = 716; Saskatchewan, n = 700; and Alberta, n = 700). We tested Province × Moral Foundations interactions through a series of moderated multiple logistic regression models.ResultsWe found consistent interaction effects indicating that binding moral foundations (authority, loyalty, purity) were stronger predictors of opposition towards SCS for residents of Manitoba compared to residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan.ConclusionsThese findings help to clarify how Manitobans’ slightly lower levels of binding moral foundations may have translated into relatively large differences in support for SCS compared to individuals living in Alberta and Saskatchewan. This study extends past research in being the first, to date, to show that associations between moral foundations and attitudes towards SCS may depend on regional factors such as proximity to an extant SCS. Our results point to the strategic value of framing arguments that appeal to individuals’ binding moral foundations for overcoming opposition to SCS, especially the first SCS to be introduced in a province, state, or region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12954-026-01500-3.Keywords: Moral foundations theory, Harm reduction, Survey, Supervised consumption services, Public attitudes

Moral foundations and attitudes towards supervised consumption services: a regional moderation study

Fatal overdoses are the fourth leading cause of accidental death worldwide [1]. In 2019, approximately 128,000 deaths were attributed to drug overdose, with almost 70% of these deaths due to opioid use [2]. Globally, drug-related mortality rates rose more than 30% over the past 30 years [3]. In 2020, the total economic costs of opioid use disorder were estimated to be 1.5 trillion dollars in the United States alone [4]. In 2023, there were approximately 7000 fatal overdoses, 5500 hospitalizations, 24,500 emergency department visits, and 36,000 emergency medical service responses related to opioid use in Canada [5]. This represents over seven billion dollars in annual economic costs on the healthcare and the criminal justice system, and in terms of lost productivity [6].

Supervised consumption services (SCS) are reliably associated with reductions in harmful outcomes for people who use drugs and the economic burdens imposed by drug use [7, 8]. SCS are designed to provide safe spaces for people who use drugs to consume substances purchased off-site under the supervision of health care professionals [9]. Research suggests that SCS are a cost-effective way to decrease mortality from substance use, increase access to addiction treatment and medical care, reduce rates of viral transmission, and decrease substance use and syringe litter in public [10–16]. Studies show that SCS may reduce rates of fatal overdose by up to 35% and increase use of detoxification treatments by up to 33% [17, 18]. For people who use drugs, use of SCS is linked to increases in life expectancy of more than 30 years [19]. Among approximately 200 SCS worldwide, only one fatal overdose has been recorded [12, 20, 21].1

Despite the large body of evidence demonstrating benefits to communities and individuals who use these services, SCS remain a controversial issue [22–25]. Establishing an SCS often requires overcoming strong public opposition, particularly from members of the community in which the prospective SCS would be located [26]. SCS advocates working with local and provincial governments often fail to overcome this opposition, thus perpetuating the social, economic, and public health crises caused by illicit drug use [16, 26–28]. Drug policies pertaining to implementation of SCS and other harm reduction services are thus an inherently political issue, and by extension a moral issue [24, 25, 29–31]. In recognition of this reality, a growing body of research demonstrates that individuals’ moral foundations are strong and reliable predictors of attitudes towards SCS and other harm reduction services [32–36]. A recent special issue focused on moralities and drug policy helps to illustrate the relevance and contemporary nature of these avenues of research [31]. For example, one article within this special issue demonstrated the efficacy of morality-based arguments over strictly empirical arguments for swaying public opinion in favor of harm reduction treatments, particularly among individuals who identify as politically conservative [36].

Moral foundations theory (MFT) is a social psychological theory that posits that people’s moral concerns do not originate from conscious cognition [37], but rather from the extent to which they value and prioritize six moral foundations: care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, purity/contamination, and liberty/oppression [38–40].2 Briefly, the care foundation aligns with values of treating others with compassion and preserving human dignity; fairness pertains to matters such as social justice and individual rights; loyalty focuses on group cohesion and cooperation; authority emphasizes respect for laws and those in positions of leadership; purity prioritizes cleanliness and health [41]. Simply put, MFT posits that an individual’s specific levels of the six moral foundations determine their perception of what is a moral violation, which in turn triggers strong emotions like anger, contempt and disgust [42].

MFT is a valuable framework for explaining morality-based differences across the political spectrum [39, 43]. Individuals who identify politically as ‘left-wing’ such as United States Democrats and Canadian Liberals tend to support government spending for social programs and prioritize the care and fairness foundations. In contrast, people who identify as ‘right-wing’ such as United States Republicans and Canadian Conservatives tend to oppose social programs for marginalized populations, and place relatively greater emphasis on the loyalty, authority, and purity foundations [39]. This distinction maps onto the categories of individualizing (care, fairness) and binding (loyalty, authority, purity) moral foundations [44–46], and can be used to explain, in part, why left-wing policies typically support SCS and why right-wing policies often oppose SCS.

Harm reduction is defined as being “grounded in justice and human rights” and focusing on “positive change and on working with people without judgement, coercion, discrimination” [47, pg. 28]. This definition explicitly evokes both of the individualizing moral foundations, and it thus follows that these foundations positively predict support for SCS and other harm reduction services [32, 34–36, 48]. In contrast, North American backlash and opposition to harm reduction is predominantly voiced by conservative politicians and stakeholders and appears to be rooted in perceived violations of the binding moral foundations [32].

McKenna [22] analyzed all testimony and lawmaking discourse relevant to opposition to harm reduction services in the United States from 2023 onward. Two major themes emerged: harm to the community, and ideological disapproval. Harm to the community arguments were underpinned by the moral foundations of purity (dirty neighbourhoods), authority (increase in crime), and loyalty (favoring individuals who use drugs over those that do not). Similarly, the theme of ideological disapproval mapped onto a perceived violation of authority such that government policies were facilitating the use of illegal drugs and thus subverting laws and the criminal justice system. Consistent with this analysis, research shows that binding moral foundations predict negative attitudes and opposition to SCS and other harm reduction services [32, 34–36, 49].

A recent study by Pijl et al. [34] examined moral foundations and attitudes towards SCS in the Canadian Prairie Provinces. One of the most notable and unexpected findings of their research was that Manitobans were the most supportive of the three provinces, despite being the only province without an operating SCS. Pijl et al. [34] speculated that closer proximity to an SCS might lead to greater frequency of negative direct [50, 51], and indirect exposure via local news media [23, 52, 53], to SCS. For example, studies show that news reports related to SCS can feature dehumanizing language towards people who use these services [52], present SCS as a threat to youth [53], and manufacture an impression of public majority opposition [23]. These negative experiences, in turn, might alter the relationships between individuals’ moral foundations and their attitudes towards SCS. As such, we sought to further interrogate this finding through a series of follow-up moderation analyses. Our purpose for conducting these tests was to determine if the relationships between moral foundations and attitudes towards SCS differed between provinces with, and without, a currently operating SCS.

We reasoned that associations between moral foundations and attitudes towards SCS may be moderated by proximity to an SCS in one or more of the following ways: (1) closer proximity increases the negative association of binding moral foundations by increasing the frequency of exposure to perceived violations of binding moral foundations; (2) closer proximity weakens the negative relationship of binding moral foundations such that they are ‘overridden’ by negative direct and indirect exposure to SCS, without the need for individuals to evoke or consider their own morals; (3) closer proximity increases the positive association of individualizing moral foundations as a boomerang effect for those who prioritize these foundations and thus deliberately reject or ignore negative exposure; or (4) closer proximity decreases the positive relationship of individualizing moral foundations such that negative first- and second-hand SCS experiences conflict with these moral foundations.

Study purpose and hypotheses

The purpose of our study was to examine the possible moderating effect(s) of proximity to an operating SCS, defined as provinces that have (close proximity), compared with those that do not have (far proximity), an operating SCS. As this is an exploratory follow-up study designed to interrogate an unexpected and important finding, we tested the following non-directional hypotheses: H1: individualizing moral foundations will interact with participants’ province of residence (does or does not have a currently operating SCS) to predict support for SCS in general, near one’s home, near one’s work, and the belief that SCS provide a necessary service; and, H2: binding moral foundations will interact with participant’s province of residence (does or does not have a currently operating SCS) to predict these four variables. In other words, we hypothesized that the associations between individualizing and binding moral foundations and support for SCS will be significantly different for residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan compared to residents of Manitoba. We did not specify a direction for this difference.

Methods

Participants and procedure

We recruited participants through online research panels (Prairie Research Associates [PRA] in Manitoba [54], and Leger’s Panel [55] in Alberta and Saskatchewan). PRA distributed email invitations to a random selection of approximately 5000 members of its Manitoba Research Collective. To meet a representative sample quota of younger men, which are typically underrepresented in online surveys, a non-probability sampling approach was used and all these eligible panelists were sent an invitation to participate. The response rate was 14% with a survey completion rate of 79%. Leger’s panel followed similar recruitment procedures, and concluded data collection once a matching sample size for residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan was obtained. The final sample consisted of 700 adult (over 18 years of age) respondents from each of Manitoba (n = 716), Saskatchewan (n = 700) and Alberta (n = 700). Participants were excluded if they were below 18 years of age or were not residents of a Prairie Province. Surveys were completed between September 27, 2022 and October 24, 2022. The error rate (based on a 95% confidence interval) would be ± 3.7% per province and ± 2.1% overall.

Moral foundations

Participants completed the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ-30 [39, 56]). This is a 30-item self-report measure that assesses the extent to which individuals prioritize five moral domains: care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and purity/contamination [56]. The instrument consists of two 15-item (plus two attention-check questions) subscales, each of which is scored on a 6-point Likert scale. The first subscale assesses the relevance respondents ascribe to each foundation in response to the following prompt “When you decide whether something is right or wrong, to what extent are the following considerations relevant to your thinking?” The second subscale requires respondents to indicate the degree to which they agree or disagree with a range of moral statements [38, 56, 57].

Prior research reports Cronbach’s alpha values for the five moral foundation subscales ranging from poor to good based on standard conventions (care/harm = 0.61; fairness/cheating = 0.59; loyalty/betrayal = 0.61; authority/subversion = 0.61; purity/contamination = 0.76; [58, 59]. However, in keeping with theoretical considerations [39] and past research [32, 60, 61], combining the care/harm and fairness/cheating subscales into an individualizing moral foundations scale, and the loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and purity/contamination subscales into a binding moral foundations scale greatly improved psychometric reliability (individualizing moral foundations Cronbach’s α = 0.79; binding moral foundations Cronbach’s α = 0.88). We standardized individualizing and binding moral foundations prior to analysis to enhance interpretability of results.

Currently operating SCS in province

We derived a new variable based on participants’ province-of-residence and whether there was an SCS currently operating in that province. Participants in Manitoba were coded as 0 = no currently operating SCS in province, and participants in Saskatchewan and Alberta were coded as 1 = currently operating SCS in province.

Dependent variables

We examined four dependent variables. These were attitudes towards SCS (a) in general, (b) near one’s home, and (c) near one’s place of work; and beliefs about whether SCS provide a necessary service. Participants reported their general attitudes towards SCS in response to the following prompt “in general, how supportive are you of supervised consumption sites for people who use drugs?” (1 = strongly oppose; 5 = strongly support). Participants communicated their attitudes to the SCS-proximity variables through the prompt(s) “Would you support a supervised consumption site in your neighbourhood (near where you live)/near where you work?” (1 = strongly oppose; 6 = strongly support). We measured beliefs that SCS were necessary through the survey item “supervised consumption sites provide a necessary service to people who use drugs” (1 = strongly disagree; 6 = strongly agree.)

We transformed these variables from their original scales into binary variables by recoding responses indicating opposition and those indicating support into (0 = oppose, 1 = support). For the general attitudes variable we removed 184 responses of ‘I don’t know,’ and treated them as missing data.

Covariates

We included five sociodemographic covariates as predictor variables in all regression analyses: (1) sex [biological sex at birth; 1 = male, 2 = female]; (2) age [in years]; (3) population centre size in which participants lived [1 < 1000 and far from a city, 2 = < 1000 and close to a city, 3 = 1000–14,999, 4 = 15,000–29 999, 5 = 30,000–49,999, 6 = 50,000–99,999, 7 ≥ 100,000, 8 = I don’t know (coded as missing values, n = 6)]; (4) highest level of education completed [1 = less than high school, 2 = high school, 3 = trade school diploma or certificate, 4 = bachelor’s degree, 5 = graduate degree); and (5) household income [1 = $5,000 to $19,999, 2 = $20,000 to $49,999, 3 = $50,000 to $79,999, 4 = $80,000 to $149,999, 5 = $150,000 or higher, 6 = I prefer not to answer (coded as missing values, n = 131)].

Statistical analyses

We first analyzed correlations between all study variables. We then used moderated multiple logistic regression models to test our hypotheses (please see supplemental materials for further details on moderated multiple logistic regression). We included a Moral Foundations × Province interaction term as a predictor in the regression equation(s). We probed all significant interactions with follow-up analyses estimating the predictive strength of moral foundations for residents of Prairie Provinces with and without an SCS, known as ‘simple-slope’ analyses [62]. We performed all analyses with IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0.0. We used Hayes’ PROCESS macro for SPSS to analyze our logistic regression models [63].

Results

Missing data and statistical assumption checks

Among incomplete responses, binding moral foundations had the highest number of missing entries (n = 10), which represented 0.5% of the total sample. This excludes responses of “I don’t know” or “I prefer not to answer” that were coded as missing for the general attitudes towards SCS, populations centre size, and income variables. Given the low number of missing entries, we followed the recommendations of Meyers et al. [62] by using listwise deletion where applicable.

The statistical assumptions underpinning logistic regressions analyses are as follows: (1) independence of observations; (2) sufficient sample size (minimum of 30 cases per independent variable in the equation); (3) lack of multicollinearity among predictor variables; (4) linearity between continuous predictor variables and the log odds of the outcome variables; and (5) a lack of outliers. We confirmed that the first two assumptions were met based on methodological design (N > 240, each participant only measured once). We assessed multicollinearity through a series of preliminary regression models examining the variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance levels between the predictor variables. No VIFs exceeded 5.0 and no tolerance levels were below 0.1, indicating this assumption was met. We identified 32 potential multivariate outliers according to their Mahalanobis distance, a metric of multivariate distance between two points such that higher scores reflect greater likelihood of outlier status. We decided to retain these 32 cases on the grounds that they constituted a small proportion of the total sample (1.52%), and due to the lack of theoretical justification for such exclusion.3 We checked the assumption of linearity through visual inspection of scatterplots between continuous predictor variables and the log-odds of the dependent variables. We found no evidence of deviation from linear relationships [62].

Correlations (Table 1)

Consistent with past research [34], participants’ individualizing moral foundations showed positive associations with support for SCS in general, near one’s home, near one’s work, and the belief that SCS provide a necessary service (r range = 0.24 to 0.31, all ps < 0.001). In contrast, Prairie Province residents’ binding moral foundations had negative associations with these variables (r range = − 0.12 to − 0.17, all ps < 0.001). Other salient associations include positive relationships between education and all support for SCS variables (r range = 0.12 to 0.14, all ps < 0.001), and correlations indicating that residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan had lower levels of individualizing moral foundations (r = − 0.13, p < 0.001) and higher levels of binding moral foundations compared to residents of Manitoba (r = 0.09, p < 0.001).

We tested four separate moderated multiple logistic regression models examining interactions between binding moral foundations and whether there was a currently operating SCS in participants’ province on the four focal outcome variables. For general support for SCS, the model showed a significant Binding × Province interaction [OR1.56, p = 0.001, CIs 1.20 to 2.02]. Follow-up simple slope analyses showed that binding moral foundations were stronger negative predictors of general support for Manitobans [OR 0.49, p < 0.001, CIs 0.39 to 0.61] compared to residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan [OR 0.75, p < 0.001, CIs 0.66 to 0.86]. This means that for Manitobans, every 1 SD increase in binding moral foundations predicted a 51% decrease in the odds of support for SCS in general. For residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan, this was only a 25% decrease.

Regarding support for SCS near one’s home, the model showed a significant Binding × Province interaction [OR1.75, p < 0.001, CIs 1.41 to 2.16]. Follow-up simple slope analyses showed that binding moral foundations were stronger negative predictors of support near home for Manitobans [OR 0.52, p < 0.001, CIs 0.43 to 0.63] compared to residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan [OR 0.91, p = 0.098, CIs 0.81 to 1.02]. This means that for Manitobans, every 1 SD increase in binding moral foundations predicted a 48% decrease in the odds of support for SCS near one’s home. For residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan, this was only a 9% decrease that was not statistically significant.

Regarding support for SCS near one’s work, the model showed a significant Binding × Province interaction [OR 1.64, p < 0.001, CIs 1.32 to 2.03]. Follow-up simple slope analyses showed that binding moral foundations were stronger negative predictors of general support for Manitobans [OR 0.54, p < 0.001, CIs 0.45 to 0.66] compared to residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan [OR 0.89, p < 0.001, CIs 0.79 to 1.00]. This means that for Manitobans, every 1 SD increase in binding moral foundations predicted a 46% decrease in the odds of support for SCS near one’s work. For residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan, this was only an 11% decrease that was not statistically significant.

For beliefs that SCS provide a necessary service, the model showed a significant Binding × Province interaction [OR 1.37, p = 0.001, CIs 1.07 to 1.74]. Follow-up simple slope analyses showed that binding moral foundations were stronger negative predictors of general support for Manitobans [OR 0.61, p < 0.001, CIs 0.49 to 0.75] compared to residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan [OR 0.83, p < 0.001, CIs 0.73 to 0.94]. This means that for Manitobans, every 1 SD increase in binding moral foundations predicted a 39% decrease in the odds of believing that SCS provide a necessary service. For residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan, this was only a 17% decrease.

Discussion

Establishing an SCS requires overcoming considerable opposition and resistance from political figures and local community members [26, 27]. This opposition to SCS appears to be driven, in part, by perceived violations of individuals’ moral foundations [32, 33, 35]. This study sought to interrogate the unexpected finding of Pijl et al. [34] indicating that Manitobans’ had the highest levels of support for SCS among the Prairie Provinces, despite being the only Prairie Province without a currently operating SCS.

To further explore the mechanisms underpinning this unexpected finding, we reasoned that relationships between individuals’ moral foundations and their attitudes towards SCS may depend on whether participants resided in provinces with (Alberta and Saskatchewan) or without an operating SCS. For those living in a province without an operating SCS, concerns regarding SCS implementation may be more ‘hypothetical’ and thus be more sensitive to individuals’ specific moral foundations compared to the experience-driven concerns of individuals living in near proximity to an SCS. As such, we examined if the relationships between Prairie Province residents’ individualizing and binding foundations and their attitudes towards SCS were moderated by whether or not there was an operating SCS in their province.

Contrary to our hypotheses, we found no evidence to suggest that the positive relationships between individualizing moral foundations and attitudes towards SCS were moderated by proximity to an existing SCS. Across the Prairie Provinces, there was no difference in the magnitude of positive association between individualizing moral foundations and support for SCS in general, near one’s home, near one’s work, and the belief that SCS provide a necessary service.

However, we found consistent evidence indicating that binding foundations were stronger negative predictors of these attitudes for Manitobans’ compared to residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan. More than 70% of Manitobans who reported low levels of binding moral foundations were predicted to support the establishment of an SCS near their homes, and near their workplace. This is in stark contrast to Manitobans who reported high levels of binding moral foundations. These individuals were predicted to show levels of support aligned with residents of Alberta and Saskatchewan, with rates of less than 55%. As such, these moderation effects help to clarify how Manitobans’ slightly lower levels of binding moral foundations may have translated into larger differences in support for SCS relative to individuals living in Alberta and Saskatchewan.

For those living in provinces with one or more currently operating SCS, it is possible that negative exposure to these SCS, either through direct experience or news media, may underpin the decrease in negative relationships between binding moral foundations and attitudes for SCS observed in this study [52, 53]. Individuals may not need to consider or perceive how SCS violate foundations of loyalty, authority, and/or purity if personal negative experiences or sensationalist news stories and articles can instead be used to justify their negative attitudes towards SCS. For example, Michaud et al. [53] demonstrated that Canadian news media in provinces that implement harm reduction services are more likely to instigate ‘moral panics’, often based on the false claim that these services will increase drug use in youth. Similarly, Habib et al. [52] provide details on dehumanizing language commonly used in news media to describe people who use drugs. The potential impact of such negative news exposure relates to principles of cognitive psychology and the availability heuristic; the greater ease with which negative examples of SCS come to mind may obviate the need to draw on personal morals [36].

From a harm reduction policy standpoint, these findings point to the efficacy of moral reframing strategies for overcoming public opposition to SCS, particularly in provinces, states, and territories that do not currently have an operating SCS. Moral reframing refers to tailoring arguments according to individuals’ specific moral foundations, with a focus on the foundations most likely to encourage opposition and resistance to one’s goals [64]. Logically, if the negative predictive effects of binding moral foundations are particularly strong in regions without an operating SCS, then framing arguments to mitigate the SCS-related concerns associated with these foundations may be particularly effective in said regions. Some harm reduction advocates argue in favor of taking an ostensibly amoral and strictly evidence-based position when trying to advance SCS and other harm reduction services [22]. However, given that harm reduction is an inherently political, and thus moral issue, taking an amoral stance in one’s arguments may be perceived as disingenuous [25], and strictly evidence-based arguments have been shown to be more susceptible to dismissal based on confirmation bias and motivated reasoning by opposed parties [24, 36].

Instead, utilization of explicitly moral arguments in favor of SCS tailored to concerns arising from perceived violations of the binding moral foundations may be more effective for persuading those who hold ambivalent or negative attitudes [33, 35, 49]. For example, highlighting how an SCS will reduce public drug use and drug litter would likely appeal to individuals who prioritize the moral foundation of purity. Similarly, framing arguments in terms of distance between SCS and communal areas (e.g., schools, parks) may appeal to individuals’ foundation of loyalty, such that SCS are not seen as favoring people who use drugs over those who do not. Finally, addressing safety and crime concerns by including provisions for security staff and a continued local police presence would likely appease individuals who oppose SCS due to authority concerns. Of course, these arguments must also be accompanied by measures to fulfill these promises, such as funding for maintenance and clean-up crews, appropriate siting of SCS away from schools and community centres, and hiring of security staff.

Limitations and future directions

We did not collect any data on participants’ postal code or neighborhood beyond province-of-residence and population centre size, or their frequency of direct or indirect negative exposure to SCS. As such, it is unclear to what extent our moderation findings were driven by proximity to an SCS, or if there are other factors distinguishing the relative strength of binding moral foundations between residents of Manitoba and the rest of the Prairie Provinces. However, it should be noted that 66% of Alberta residents, and 49% of Saskatchewan residents in our sample reported living in a large urban centre with a population > 100,000. This suggests that a meaningful proportion of respondents in these provinces were living in cities with an operating SCS.

Our survey had a response rate of only 14%. This is another study limitation, as low response rates may introduce bias such that individuals with strong attitudes either in support or in opposition to SCS may have been more likely to participate in the survey study. Overall, the majority of participants expressed support for SCS across all outcomes, with general attitudes and the belief that SCS are necessary receiving the highest levels of support (65% and 73%, respectively). This suggests that our sample may be positively biased towards SCS relative to the general population. However, the survey completion rate was satisfactory (79%) with little missing data, which suggests that the focal SCS variables were not the direct cause of non-response [65]. As a final limitation, we did not collect data on the liberty/oppression moral foundation due to a lack of psychometric validation at the time of data collection [40, 46]. This is a notable omission as this moral foundation may be highly relevant to individuals’ beliefs about personal freedoms related to drug use [30].

This study provides several avenues for further research. Replicating and extending this study by including a more accurate measure of proximity to an existing SCS such as postal code, and recruiting a nationally representative Canadian sample across all provinces with and without a currently operating SCS would help to clarify if proximity is truly the mechanism underpinning the pattern of moderation found in this study. Future studies examining relationships between moral foundations and attitudes towards SCS would also benefit from including the liberty/oppression foundation.

Additionally, despite a recent uptick in survey-based framing experiments situated within harm reduction, no such study to date has experimentally manipulated arguments according to the individualizing vs. binding moral foundation distinction. For example, Ragsdale and Elliott [35] manipulated variables such as gender and proximity of a methadone clinic from one’s home in their experimental vignettes, and Ruiz Flores López, Sánchez Hiza [36] combined elements of individualizing (care) with binding (loyalty, authority) moral foundations in their vignette manipulations. Future experimental moral-reframing studies could develop experimental stimuli according to strictly individualizing foundation arguments and binding foundation arguments and examine if their persuasiveness was moderated by participants’ moral foundations and/or proximity to an existing SCS.

Conclusion

This is the first study to date demonstrating that the strength of relationships between individuals’ binding moral foundations and attitudes towards SCS may depend upon proximity to SCS. Although further research is needed to rule out or identify other mechanisms that may underpin our findings, this is an important first step towards understanding how the predictive strength of moral foundations may depend on regional, political, and/or historical context.

From an applied standpoint, the current study highlights how moral reframing techniques may be particularly effective for establishing the first SCS in a region. A growing body of research in moral reframing shows promise for overcoming obstacles toward SCS and other harm reduction services [24, 33, 35, 49]. It is incumbent upon harm reduction advocates, policy-makers, and municipal, regional, and national governments to use this evidence to improve their strategies for implementing SCS [25]. For residents of regions without an SCS, morality-based concerns may be more salient due to a lack of direct exposure and/or experience. Thus, preempting said concerns with a compassionate understanding and acknowledgment of perceived threats to loyalty, authority, and purity may be an effective means to consensus-building around SCS and other harm reduction services.
