Switching from Rituximab to Eculizumab in US Patients with Neuromyelitis Optica Spectrum Disorder: Impact on Hospitalizations
Authors: Justin Lee, Adrian Kielhorn, Sami Fam, Emily Riser, Eoin P Flanagan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNeuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), a rare autoimmune disease, affects the optic nerves and spinal cord. Rituximab is often used off-label to treat NMOSD; however, comparator-controlled trial data are limited. Eculizumab is approved for treatment of anti-aquaporin-4 antibody-positive (AQP4-Ab+) NMOSD. Outcomes data for patients who switch from rituximab to eculizumab are limited.MethodsThis retrospective study used claims data from the IQVIA PharMetrics® Plus database (1/1/2015–3/31/2022). Patients aged ≥ 18 years with NMOSD treated with rituximab were identified and split into two groups: patients with an eculizumab claim after rituximab (switch group, n = 20) and patients without an eculizumab claim (control group, n = 525). Hospitalization rates and duration were assessed 6 months before and 6 months after the switch date (switch group) or reference date (1-year post-rituximab initiation; control group).ResultsThe percentage of patients hospitalized in the switch group decreased after transitioning to eculizumab (45.0% vs. 10.0%; p = 0.034) corresponding to a decreased mean number of hospitalizations per patient from 1.1 to 0.1 (p = 0.005). The percentage of patients hospitalized in the control group was similar before and after the reference date (10.1% vs. 9.3%; p = 0.755) corresponding to a mean number of hospitalizations per patient of 0.160 and 0.156 (p = 0.922), respectively. In the switch group, median (interquartile range [IQR]) hospitalization duration decreased from 8.5 (5.0–11.0) days before switching to eculizumab to 2.5 (2.2–2.8) days after switching (p < 0.001). Median (IQR) hospitalization duration in the control group was 4.0 (2.0–9.0) days before and 4.0 (2.0–6.0) days after the reference date.ConclusionAlthough the claims-based analysis and small patient numbers limit generalizability, we observed a reduction in hospitalizations number and length of stay in a cohort of patients with NMOSD who switched from rituximab to eculizumab.Keywords: Eculizumab, Healthcare resource utilization, Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, Rare disease, Rituximab

Introduction

Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is a rare, chronic, autoimmune disease of the central nervous system (CNS) [1]. Inflammation of the CNS primarily affects the optic nerves and spinal cord in patients with NMOSD causing optic neuritis and transverse myelitis [1]. Patients commonly experience repeated, unpredictable relapses, often leading to irreversible neurological disability including vision loss and paralysis [1, 2]. Approximately 80% of patients have autoantibodies against the water channel aquaporin-4 (AQP4) and are considered anti-AQP4 antibody-positive (AQP4-Ab+) [1, 3–5]. Binding of anti-AQP4 antibodies to AQP4 channels in astrocyte endfeet membranes triggers the complement cascade leading to astrocyte destruction and neuronal injury characteristic of the disease [1, 5]. The presence of these antibodies is a diagnostic marker of AQP4-Ab+ NMOSD [1, 3–5].

The main goals of treatment for NMOSD include preventing disease relapse, reducing relapse-related symptoms, and maintaining a stable condition [6]. Patients who experience relapses may require hospital/inpatient care, use more healthcare resources, accrue higher costs than patients without relapses, and are at risk of accruing permanent disability such as blindness, paralysis, and increased mortality [1, 7, 8]. Acute relapses are typically treated with intravenous high-dose glucocorticoids for 3–5 days, and patients who do not respond sufficiently can be treated with plasmapheresis [1, 3, 8, 9]. In two studies, the average length of stay was approximately 2 weeks for patients with NMOSD admitted to the hospital; specifically, one study reported a length of stay of 12.5 days (median 1.5; range 1–90) and another reported 14.6 days (95% confidence interval [CI] 13.9–15.2]) [8, 10]. The length of stay for patients hospitalized with NMOSD varies by disease severity and may range from 5 days for patients with less severe symptoms (Expanded Disability Status Scale [EDSS] score ≤ 4.0) to 90 days for patients with more severe symptoms (EDSS score 8.0–9.5) [10].

Therapeutic options for relapse prevention in patients with NMOSD vary by mechanism and target [6]. Newer biologic therapies, including the terminal complement component 5 inhibitors eculizumab and ravulizumab, the anti-CD19 B cell-depleting antibody inebilizumab, and the anti-interleukin-6 receptor antibody satralizumab, have been approved for the treatment of AQP4-Ab+ NMOSD [6, 11–14]. Immunosuppressive therapies such as the DNA synthesis inhibitors azathioprine and mycophenolate mofetil are not approved for NMOSD but have been shown to effectively reduce relapse rates [3, 15]. Additionally, rituximab—a B cell-targeting antibody that ultimately reduces antibody production, including pathogenic AQP4 autoantibodies—can be used to alleviate NMOSD symptoms [6, 16]. Rituximab is largely used off-label as a first-line treatment or maintenance therapy for NMOSD to help prevent relapses [3, 6, 17], and it is approved for the treatment of NMOSD in Japan but not in the USA or Europe [3].

The recent approval of rituximab in Japan was based on a small randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial (RIN-1; UMIN000013453) [3, 18]. Two separate retrospective cohort studies of patients in the German Neuromyelitis Optica Study (NEMOS) also showed the mean (95% CI) annualized relapse rate (ARR) of 0.46 (0.34–0.61) and 0.5 (0.4–0.64) for patients receiving rituximab [19, 20]. A meta-analysis of patients with NMOSD treated with rituximab found that although the ARR decreased after rituximab treatment, there was wide variation in the degree of reduction in ARR across studies, ranging from 0.37 up to 2.56 [21]. The safety profile of rituximab is generally acceptable, with most adverse events (AEs) being mild in severity and related to infusion; however, the increased risk of hypogammaglobulinemia and infection with prolonged treatment is an emerging concern [3, 22–25].

Eculizumab, a complement inhibitor approved for treatment of AQP4-Ab+ NMOSD, reduced the risk of relapse in patients with AQP4-Ab+ NMOSD in the phase 3 PREVENT trial and its open-label extension (OLE) [11, 26, 27]. In the PREVENT trial, the adjudicated ARR was 0.02 for patients receiving eculizumab (n = 96) and 0.35 for patients in the placebo group (n = 47) [27]. The adjudicated ARR for patients who received eculizumab across PREVENT and its subsequent OLE (n = 137) was 0.025 [26]. Ravulizumab is a newer complement inhibitor with an extended half-life compared with eculizumab, requiring less frequent administration [14, 28]. In the phase 3 CHAMPION-NMOSD trial, ravulizumab treatment resulted in a 98.6% relapse risk reduction and an adjusted ARR of 0.000 (n = 58; 95% upper confidence limit 0.044) in patients with AQP4-Ab+ NMOSD, and these results are consistent with findings observed after eculizumab treatment [28]. Both eculizumab and ravulizumab are well tolerated, with most AEs being mild in severity [27, 28]. Inhibition of the terminal complement pathway can leave patients vulnerable to infection from encapsulated bacteria; however, with existing risk mitigation strategies (e.g., meningococcal vaccination or antibiotic prophylaxis), very few cases of meningococcal infections have been reported among patients with AQP4-Ab+ NMOSD receiving eculizumab or ravulizumab in a clinical trial or real-world setting [26–30].

Data regarding outcomes among patients with NMOSD who switch from rituximab treatment to eculizumab are limited. A subgroup analysis from the PREVENT trial demonstrated that prior rituximab treatment, with the last dose at least 3 months prior to eculizumab initiation, did not impact the efficacy or safety of eculizumab; however, this analysis was for a subgroup in a clinical trial setting [26, 31]. In the current analysis, we used retrospective claims data to investigate the impact of switching from rituximab to eculizumab on the number and duration of hospitalizations to evaluate healthcare resource utilization for adult patients with NMOSD in the USA.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

A retrospective study using a pretest–posttest control group design was conducted using data from the IQVIA PharMetrics® Plus database between January 1, 2015 and March 31, 2022. This database contains real-world medical and pharmacy claims data for commercially insured patients in the USA [32]. Patients aged ≥ 18 years with NMOSD were identified using International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes. Patients were included in this analysis if they had at least one diagnosis code for NMO (ICD-10 G36.0) in the acute care hospital/inpatient setting or at least two diagnosis codes for NMO at least 30 days apart in the ambulatory/outpatient setting. Only patients with at least one rituximab claim were included. Patients were categorized on the basis of treatment patterns (Fig. 1). The switch group included patients who had at least one rituximab claim with a subsequent eculizumab claim during the analysis period. The control group included patients with at least one rituximab claim but without a subsequent eculizumab claim during the analysis period to help detect and account for the influence of external factors, such as the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on hospitalization rates.

The first eculizumab claim was defined as the switch date for the switch group, and 1 year after rituximab initiation was defined as the reference date for the control group. Patients were required to have continuous enrollment from 6 months before the switch/reference day to 6 months after.

Outcomes

The primary objective of this study was to compare the number of hospitalization claims and duration of hospital stays before and after the switch from rituximab to eculizumab in the switch group. Outcomes were assessed from 6 months before to 6 months after the switch/reference date and included the all-cause hospitalization claims rate identified by a confinement number and total number and median duration of hospitalizations. Hospitalization was defined as an admission with an overnight stay; visits associated with drug administration (e.g., infusions) were not considered a hospitalization. Hospitalization was categorized as “related to NMOSD” if the principal diagnosis for hospitalization was listed as NMOSD based on ICD-10 G36.0 and was categorized as “non-NMOSD-related” if a non-NMOSD ICD-10 code was used for the principal diagnosis.

Hospitalization expenditure per patient was also evaluated in the 6 months before to 6 months after the switch/reference date. Costs represent actual fees paid to the insurance company for services rendered by the provider and do not include patient co-pays or other deductibles.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were summarized and included age at NMOSD diagnosis date, sex, days from rituximab initiation to switch/reference date, and days between last rituximab and first eculizumab treatment. A chi-squared test was performed to derive the p value for the comparison of the number of patients hospitalized in the 6 months before versus the 6 months after the switch/reference date. Relative risk of hospitalization was calculated as the ratio of postperiod risk to preperiod risk. A two-tailed t test was performed to derive the p value for the comparison of the number of hospitalizations and the hospitalization expenditure per patient before versus after the switch/reference date. Welch’s t test, which accounts for unequal variances between groups, was used to calculate the p values for differences in the duration of hospitalization before versus after the switch/reference date. Time to first all-cause hospitalization was evaluated in the switch group with a Kaplan–Meier curve. A Cox proportional hazards model was used to estimate the hazard ratio (HR), and the log-rank test was used to compare the survival distributions before versus after the switch dates. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were made. Data were analyzed using Instant Health Data platform and Python/R Jupyter Notebooks.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and is consistent with Good Clinical Practice and applicable regulatory requirements. The retrospective claims data were de-identified according to regulations. Federal guidance on Public Welfare and Protection of Human Subjects was followed. This study was determined to be exempt from an institutional review board (IRB) and informed consent was not required. This study was funded by Alexion, AstraZeneca Rare Disease, which paid the license fee to access the IQVIA PharMetrics® Plus database. Study design development, analysis, and interpretation were conducted collaboratively with input from all clinical/academic and study sponsor coauthors. The study sponsor performed the initial analysis using prespecified criteria applied to de-identified claims with prespecified methods and reproducible scripts, and full results were shared with all authors.

Results

Participants

A total of 4194 adult patients met the inclusion criteria based on NMOSD diagnosis code; 988 of these patients had at least one rituximab claim. Overall, 20 patients in the switch group and 525 in the control group fulfilled all criteria and were included in this analysis (Fig. 2). Baseline patient attributes were similar across both groups (Table 1). Among patients in the switch group, the mean (SD) time from the last rituximab infusion to the first eculizumab infusion was 180.9 (85.30) days. In the switch group, patients received a median (interquartile range [IQR]) of 0.5 (0.0–1.0) doses of rituximab in the 6 months prior to switching and a median (IQR) of 15.0 (13.8–15.0) doses of eculizumab in the 6 months after the switch. In the control group, patients received a median (IQR) of 1.0 (1.0–2.0) and 1.0 (0.0–2.0) doses of rituximab in the 6 months before and after the reference date, respectively.

All-Cause Hospitalizations

In the switch group, nine (45.0%) patients were hospitalized during rituximab treatment prior to switching to eculizumab whereas only two (10.0%) patients were hospitalized after the switch (p = 0.034) (Fig. 3a), representing a relative risk of hospitalization of 0.22 (95% CI 0.05–0.09). The total number of hospitalizations decreased from 22 (1.1 hospitalizations per patient) during rituximab treatment to two (0.1 hospitalizations per patient) after the switch to eculizumab (p = 0.005) (Fig. 3b). Before switching to eculizumab, the majority of hospitalizations were related to NMOSD (17/22, 77.3%). After switching to eculizumab, two (10.0%) patients experienced a hospitalization; both hospitalizations were short in duration (2–3 days). Of the patients who were hospitalized after switching, one patient had discontinued eculizumab approximately 7.5 weeks before the hospitalization event, and a claim for COVID lab test existed in between the discontinuation and hospitalization. This patient resumed eculizumab approximately 3 weeks after discharge (Fig. 4). The second patient’s hospitalization was likely due to a surgical procedure based on claims made during this period, and this patient resumed eculizumab treatment approximately 10.5 weeks after discharge.

Prior to the reference date, 53 (10.1%) patients in the control group were hospitalized whereas 49 (9.3%) patients were hospitalized after the reference date (p = 0.755) (Fig. 3a), corresponding to a relative risk of 0.92 (95% CI 0.64–1.34). The total number of hospitalizations in the control group was 84 (0.16 hospitalizations per patient) before the reference date and 82 (0.16 hospitalizations per patient) after the reference date (p = 0.922) (Fig. 3b). Before the reference date, 13 of 84 (15.5%) hospitalizations in the control group were related to NMOSD whereas 71 of 84 (84.5%) were for other non-NMOSD-related reasons (e.g., infection, respiratory failure, epilepsy). After the reference date, 10 of 82 (12.2%) hospitalizations were related to NMOSD and 72 of 82 (87.8%) were for non-NMOSD-related reasons.

In the switch group, patients spent a combined total of 200 days in the hospital in the 6 months prior to switching to eculizumab (Fig. 3c). In the 6 months after switching to eculizumab, patients in the switch group spent a combined total of 5 days in the hospital, corresponding to a 98% decrease. In contrast, the combined total number of days spent in the hospital before and after the reference date for the control group were 554 and 481 days, respectively (13% decrease; Fig. 3c).

For the switch group, the median (IQR) duration of hospitalization decreased from 8.5 (5.0–11.0) days before switching to 2.5 (2.2–2.8) days after switching to eculizumab (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5). For the control group, the median (IQR) duration of hospitalization remained stable at 4.0 (2.0–9.0) days before the reference date and 4.0 (2.0–6.0) days after the reference date (p = 0.479). The time to first hospitalization in the 6 months after switching to eculizumab was significantly prolonged compared with the 6 months prior to switching (HR 0.184; 95% CI 0.040–0.850) (Fig. 6).

The hospitalization expenditure per patient decreased from $24,981 on rituximab to $7082 after switching to eculizumab (p = 0.075). For the control group, the hospitalization expenditure per patient was $3903 before the reference date and $3687 after the reference date (p = 0.873).

Discussion

The results from this study provide evidence that in a cohort of US patients with NMOSD switching treatment from rituximab to eculizumab is associated with a decrease in healthcare resource utilization. These findings are consistent with the known clinical benefit of eculizumab as a treatment for the prevention of relapse in NMOSD [26, 27]. Patients with NMOSD who switched from rituximab to eculizumab had a substantial reduction in the number of hospitalizations and duration of hospitalization in the 6 months after switching compared with the 6 months prior to switching, whereas the hospitalization rates and duration remained relatively stable in patients who continued on rituximab over the same analysis period.

To our knowledge, this is the first study assessing healthcare resource utilization outcomes in patients with NMOSD who switched from rituximab to eculizumab. Although a subgroup analysis of the PREVENT trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of eculizumab in patients with AQP4-Ab+ NMOSD who were previously treated with rituximab, the focus of that study was on relapse risk [26, 27, 31]. Our current study focused on hospitalizations and included a control group of patients who remained on rituximab to evaluate impacts of these therapies on healthcare resource utilization. The stable rate of hospitalization in this control group suggests the benefits observed in the switch group were the result of eculizumab treatment and not external factors. Controlling for external factors was important in this study as the study period spans years when hospitalizations declined as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic [33]. Our analysis used a pretest–posttest control group design to reduce confounding: participants in the switch group served as their own control, which inherently adjusts for fixed, time-invariant factors; and the contemporaneous control group helped distinguish treatment-related changes from background temporal trends. Nonetheless, we could not fully adjust for baseline severity, AQP4 serostatus, or reasons for switching, and time-varying confounders around the switch may persist. We therefore interpret the observed effects with appropriate caution, acknowledging the potential for residual confounding and selection bias despite these design-based safeguards.

After the switch to eculizumab, only two patients in the switch group were hospitalized. Although the primary diagnosis in the hospitalization claim is listed as NMOSD, the claims data indicate other reasons may be involved. One patient discontinued eculizumab prior to hospitalization with a COVID lab test in the intervening period, and concurrent claims indicate the second patient was likely hospitalized for a surgical procedure. Aside from the periods around their hospitalization, both patients appear to have received eculizumab as recommended (e.g., approximately every 2 weeks), with regular treatment resuming within 11 weeks after discharge. Claims for clinical intervention consistent with an NMOSD relapse were not detected in either patient. This suggests these hospitalization events were likely a consequence of other factors and not due to a relapse. Among all patients in the switch group, most hospitalizations prior to starting eculizumab were related to NMOSD. Although we did not investigate relapses directly, prior data indicate that approximately one-third of NMOSD relapses are treated by an inpatient/hospital admission [8]; therefore, the reduction in hospitalizations after switching may also indicate a reduction in relapses among the patients in this group.

These results also suggest switching from rituximab to eculizumab can lead to a reduction in hospitalization costs associated with NMOSD. The total hospitalization expenditure decreased, and patients experienced a reduction in healthcare resource utilization after switching to eculizumab.

Because eculizumab is an approved and effective treatment for patients with AQP4-Ab+ NMOSD [11, 26, 27], patients and their care providers may consider transitioning from rituximab to eculizumab. Rituximab is largely used off-label, and although rituximab reduces the ARR for patients with NMOSD, the extent of relapse reduction is variable [3, 17, 19–21]. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests prolonged treatment with rituximab may increase the risk of hypogammaglobulinemia and infection [21, 24, 25]. The majority of AEs among patients treated with eculizumab in the PREVENT trial or its OLE were mild [26, 27]; across indications, the cumulative rates of meningococcal infection in patients treated with eculizumab are low despite the increased risk of infection from encapsulated bacteria with complement inhibition [30]. Ravulizumab, another terminal complement inhibitor, is also a highly effective treatment for patients with AQP4-Ab+ NMOSD [28]. Although the timing of the approval of ravulizumab in the USA (March 2024) [14] precluded its inclusion in this analysis, future studies could examine if patients experience a similar benefit by switching from rituximab to ravulizumab.

This analysis had several limitations that are common to claims data studies. Because insurance claims are generally used for billing, details from patient medical records were unavailable. Therefore, it was not possible to determine information such as specific criteria used for NMOSD diagnosis [2, 4] to control for any differences in patients or exclude patients who were potentially misclassified. The lack of AQP4 antibody status, disease severity, complete meningococcal vaccine history, reason for switching treatments, and full details of the reason for each hospitalization further limit conclusions that can be drawn. The IQVIA PharMetrics Plus® database includes approximately 30% of the US patient population and therefore does not represent the entire US population affected by NMOSD and may not reflect non-US populations, limiting the generalizability of our findings.

This study was also limited by the low number of patients in the switch group. This is likely a result of few patients meeting the continuous enrollment inclusion criteria, due in part to the short period of time eculizumab has been on the market as a treatment for NMOSD (since 2019) [34]; further studies in larger populations and with longer follow-up are warranted.

This study could overestimate the relapse rate in patients treated with rituximab before the initiation of eculizumab, as the average number of days between the last rituximab dose and the first dose of eculizumab was 180.85 days, which is approximately 6 months. Rituximab is typically dosed every 6 months, suggesting patients who experienced a relapse prior to switching may have been inadequately protected against relapses with rituximab [3]. Finally, evaluation of hospitalization expenditure in this study only captures one component of the full economic impact of switching from rituximab to eculizumab, and interpretation of the findings may be impacted by between group differences in the expenditures in the 6 months prior to the switch/reference date. The total economic burden of rare diseases, such as NMOSD, includes direct medical costs (e.g., cost of therapies) and indirect costs (e.g., lost productivity and nonmedical costs) [35]. Future studies could include evaluation of additional direct and indirect costs associated with switching from rituximab to eculizumab and explore factors contributing to costs before/after switching.

Conclusion

In this retrospective claims analysis, patients with NMOSD who transitioned from rituximab to eculizumab had a significant decrease in the rate of hospitalization and duration compared with patients who remain on rituximab treatment. These results suggest that for some patients switching from rituximab to eculizumab has a substantial clinical benefit, which may be attributed to the demonstrated efficacy of eculizumab in relapse prevention and that switching can lead to a reduction in utilization of certain healthcare resources.
