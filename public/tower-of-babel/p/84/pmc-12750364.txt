E-Cigarette Use Among Older Adults with Chronic Spinal Cord Injury: Relationship With History of Cigarette Smoking
Authors: James S Krause, Clara E Dismuke-Greer, Melinda Jarnecke, Brielle D Grant
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify self-reported electronic cigarette use and its associations with demographic, spinal cord injury (SCI), socioeconomic, and cigarette smoking history variables among adults with SCI.DesignCross-sectional self-report study.SettingMedical University.ParticipantsPatients (N=1253) with traumatic SCI who averaged 56.7 years of age and 24.1 years since onset.InterventionsNot applicable.Main Outcome MeasuresSelf-reported e-cigarette use, cigarette smoking history.Results16.3% had used e-cigarettes and 4.9% were current users. Both current cigarette smoking (odds ratio [OR], 4.20; confidence interval [CI], 2.45-7.18) and smoking history (OR, 3.55; CI, 2.25-5.60) were related to a history of vaping, as was having some college, but no degree compared with those with no education beyond high school (OR, 1.89; CI, 1.15-3.13). Non-Hispanic Black participants reported lower odds of vaping history than non-Hispanic whites (OR, 0.55; CI, 0.31-0.96). Compared with those under 40, each of 3 age groups reported lower odds of a history of vaping. Odds of current e-cigarette use were higher among those with a smoking history (OR, 5.71; CI, 2.62-12.42); and lower for those between the ages of 51-60 (OR, 0.37, CI, 0.14-0.96) and those 60 and older (OR, 0.11, CI, 0.03-0.38), compared with those under the age of 40.ConclusionsE-cigarette was most strongly related to age and smoking history. It is important to evaluate e-cigarette use and their potential consequences and implement intervention strategies.KEYWORDS: Cigarette smoking, Electronic nicotine delivery systems, Rehabilitation, Risk-taking, Spinal cord injuries, Vaping

Cigarette smoking in the United States (US) has declined considerably in recent decades, with adults who identify as current smokers down from 20.9% in 20051 to 11.2% in 2021.2 This decline is significant given the well-established health consequences of traditional cigarette smoking. These risks include cardiovascular disease, pulmonary disease, lung cancer, gastrointestinal disorders among many others.3,4 Conversely, vaping, or the use of electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) has increased in popularity since its introduction in the US in 2007.5, 6, 7 Data from the National Health Interview Survey revealed that in 2023,8 roughly 6.5% of adults in the US were current e-cigarette users,9 with e-cigarette usage highest among those aged 21-24 (15.5%) followed by 25-34 (12.6%), 18-20 (10.3%), 35-49 (7.3%), 50-64 (3.3%) and 65 and older (0.9%).

Although initially thought of as less harmful than traditional cigarettes and a tool for smoking cessation, the possible risks e-cigarettes may have on users are not fully known. Whether from cigarettes or e-cigarettes, nicotine presents harmful effects on cardiovascular and respiratory health.10,11 As e-cigarettes have varying amounts of nicotine, carcinogens, and toxins, and are rapidly evolving over time, the long-term effects remain poorly understood.12 Preliminary findings suggest that having ever used e-cigarettes was associated with obstructive lung function impairment,13 including pulmonary disease, and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.11,14,15 Any health complications from e-cigarettes may be magnified among special populations who are most vulnerable to decline by virtue of an existing condition.

Respiratory issues are one of the most common comorbidities among those with spinal cord injury (SCI), with pneumonia being especially common.16 Persons with SCI are at elevated risk for secondary health conditions and early mortality, with cigarette smoking being an important contributor to mortality.17, 18, 19, 20 In 2016, 15.5% of the US population were current cigarette smokers.1 A study specific to SCI found that in the same time period, more than double (35.3%) were current smokers.21

There are numerous documented negative health effects on smoking after SCI, including increased risk of hospitalization.22 For instance, Garshick et al18 found that current smokers had an increase in mortality of 4% per cigarette smoked compared to those who never smoked. Research has also found that adults with a disability are more likely to use e-cigarettes (8.6%) than those without disabilities (4.8%).23 In a study among those with chronic pain, 10.5% of participants reported a history of e-cigarette use and 3.8% reported current use.24 Although studies of smoking and SCI are limited, the literature is nearly completely void of research surrounding e-cigarette use and SCI.

Our purpose was to assess the percentage of participants with SCI who use e-cigarettes and factors related to e-cigarette utilization, particularly history of cigarette smoking. Additionally, we evaluated the relationships of demographic, SCI, socioeconomic, and Veteran status with e-cigarette use. The findings offer insights into the extent of e-cigarette use among those with SCI and its relationship with cigarette smoking. Although we do not investigate the relationships with other outcomes, findings are particularly relevant because people with SCI are at greater risk of multiple secondary health conditions, including compromised respiratory and pulmonary function.18, 19, 20

Methods

Participants and data sources

Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to initiating the study. Participants (n=1253) were adults with traumatic SCI with noncomplete recovery of at least 1 year duration and each participated in earlier stages of a longitudinal study focused more broadly on health outcomes. They were initially identified from 3 sources: (1) a regional specialty hospital in the southeastern US which began in 1997-1998; (2) a second cohort enrolled in 2007-2010 from the same specialty hospital and (3) a cohort from a state-based surveillance system in the southeastern US enrolled in 2011-2017. There were 1253 participants. Those with missing data were eliminated from the analyses.

Data collection procedures

Prospective participants were sent a recruitment letter via mail. The letter included all elements of informed consent and a unique weblink to complete the self-report assessment (SRA) online. If not completed online within a month, a paper form of the SRA was mailed. Data were recorded by phone for those who had difficulty completing the materials independently. Additional follow-up mailings and phone calls were used to promote response. Completion and return of the materials demonstrated implied consent, and a waiver of signed consent was obtained from the IRB. All responses were received between September 2021 and December 2023, and participants received $50 in remuneration for their time.

Measures

The SRA included items on demographic characteristics (age, race-ethnicity, sex), SCI characteristics (injury severity, years since onset), e-cigarette use, and cigarette smoking (description of the wording appears in appendix 1). Injury severity was assessed using a combination of ambulatory status and neurologic level, with all ambulatory participants grouped together, regardless of level, and the remaining nonambulatory participants grouped into 3 additional categories: C1-C4, C5-C8, noncervical). We used items from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System to assess smoking and e-cigarette use.25

For cigarette smoking, participants were asked a dichotomous yes/no questions of “Have you smoked at least 100 cigarettes in your entire life?” and “Do you smoke right now?” with response options of not at all, some days, and every day. These questions directly follow a question “Have you smoked 100 cigarettes in your entire life?”25 These items appeared in a separate section before items on e-cigarette use.

For e-cigarette use, participants were asked a dichotomous yes/no question of “Have you ever used an e-cigarette or other electronic ‘vaping’ product, even just one time, in your entire life?” For those who indicated yes, frequency of use was measured by “Do you now use e-cigarettes or other electronic ‘vaping’ products every day, some days, or not at all?”

Data analysis

Stata Version 16.1a was used for all statistical analyses. First, the chi-square test was used to examine differences in vaping history (yes/no) and current vaping (yes/no) by age, years since injury, SCI injury severity, race/ethnicity, military Veteran status, education, household income, smoking history and current smoking. We then estimated multivariable models (including multiple predictors simultaneously) of binary indicators of vaping history (yes/no), and current vaping (yes/no), using logistic model estimation methods. We included age, years since injury, SCI injury severity, race/ethnicity, military Veteran status, education, and household income as predictors, along with binary indicators of smoking history (yes/no), and current smoking (yes/no). Odds ratios, as well as 95% confidence intervals, are reported.

Results

Descriptives

Participants were predominantly men (70.5%) and non-Hispanic White (70.6%) (table 1). The average age at the time of the study was 56.7 years. The average age of participants at onset of their SCI was 32.6 years, with an average of just over 24 years having passed since SCI onset. In terms of injury severity, 40.8% were ambulatory. The next highest group comprised of nonambulatory noncervical (31.2%), nonambulatory C5-C8 (20.2%), and nonambulatory C1-C4 (7.8%). Nearly 40% had no more than a high school education whereas 27% had completed some college and 33.3% had completed a 4-year degree. Just over 11% were military veterans. 36% reported having ever smoked on a regular basis and 13.7% reported being current smokers. Just over 16% endorsed having ever used e-cigarettes, with 4.9% having current e-cigarette use.

History of e-cigarette use

The results of logistic regression showed chronologic age was strongly related to having used e-cigarettes (table 4). Compared with those under the age of 40, the odds of e-cigarette use decreased with each aging interval, with the lowest odds ratio (0.15; CI, 0.08-0.29) among those 60 and older. Non-Hispanic Blacks reported lower odds of a history of e-cigarette use compared with non-Hispanic whites (OR, 0.55; CI, 0.31-0.96). Those with some college education reported higher odds of history of e-cigarette use compared with those with no more than a high school certificate (OR, 1.89; CI, 1.15-3.13). Those with a history of cigarette smoking had a 3.55 (CI, 2.25-5.60) greater odds of a history of e-cigarette use compared with those who have never smoked, and current smokers had a 4.19 (CI, 2.45-7.18) greater odds of a history of e-cigarette use. No other factors were significant.

Current e-cigarette use

The results of logistic regression showed current age was significantly related to the odds of e-cigarette use, as those in the age group 50-59 (OR, 0.37, CI, 0.14-0.96) and those 60 and older reported lower odds of current e-cigarette use compared to those under the age of 40 (OR, 0.11, CI, 0.03-0.38) based on logistic regression (table 5) . Those in their 40s were not significantly different than those younger than 40 years of age. A history of smoking was strongly related to current e-cigarette use, as those who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime had a 5.71 (OR, 2.62-12.42) greater odds of being a current user of e-cigarette usage or compared with those who had not smoked 100 cigarettes in their lifetime. However, current cigarette smoking was unrelated to current e-cigarette usage, nor were any of the other predictors.

Discussion

The unique contribution of this study was the focus on vaping/e-cigarette use among people with SCI and its relationships with a wide array of variables including demographic, SCI, socioeconomic, and smoking history. Furthermore, there were even more significant variables of having used e-cigarettes, even if not a current user, and this included a history of both previous and current cigarette smoking and younger age. The lower percentage of e-cigarette use among non-Hispanic Black participants was also notable.

The findings are important in that the portion of people with SCI that have used e- cigarettes, given the elevated risk of respiratory considerations, is of concern. The detrimental effects of cigarette smoking on people with SCI has been well-established, including the increased likelihood for hospitalization and a 4% increase in mortality.18 Until more is known about the consequences of vaping, the potential negative effects of vaping on the respiratory system must be considered. The percentage of vaping observed in this study is lower than that reported in the general population, although direct comparisons must be interpreted with caution.

Consistent with our findings, it is well documented that both current previous cigarette users are more likely to use e-cigarettes because of a variety of factors, possibly including smoking cessation attempts.26, 27, 28 Similarly, younger age is a well-established risk factor for e-cigarette use aligning with the findings from our study.8,28 The finding that non-Hispanic Black adults had lower odds of e-cigarette use when compared to non-Hispanic White adults is consistent with existing literature among the general population.8,29 Potential explanations for these differences include variations in attitude and beliefs, social norms, and media/advertising influence across racial/ethnic groups.30

There were some findings that were somewhat surprising, sometimes because of the absence of statistical significance. For instance, we were unable to identify any relationship between injury severity, as measured by neurologic level and ambulatory status, with vaping. Previous research with smoking suggested that individuals with high-level injuries who are unable smoke independently were less likely to smoke.31 It is also rather surprising that socioeconomic factors were not related to risk of e-cigarette use, except that those with some college were actually at higher risk. We have no basis for explaining this finding, as research on cigarette smoking has demonstrated an inverse relationship between socioeconomic status and smoking.32 However, the relationship between socioeconomic status and e-cigarette usage in adults is not well understood. Friedman and Horn33 found that, unlike conventional cigarette usage, higher income and at least a year of college education do not act as protective factors against e-cigarette use. The CDC study also reported higher e-cigarette usage among those with lower income, less education, and lack of health inusrance.9 Another study among those with a smoking history found that non-Hispanic White individuals were more likely to use e-cigarettes in the context of quitting traditional cigarettes when compared to lower income and racial/ethnic minority smokers.30 This suggests that e-cigarette use patterns may be driven by different social, cultural, and behavioral factors than traditional smoking.

Implications for rehabilitation and public health professionals

The findings unfortunately draw attention to another health-related behavior where people with SCI are participating in activities that could lead to increased risk of adverse outcomes. In this case, just as with higher usage of cigarettes historically compared with the general population21,31 and contrary to the respiratory issues related to SCI, people with SCI are now reporting e-cigarette use. Rehabilitation professionals should discuss e-cigarette use during the initial rehabilitation hospitalization and e-cigarette use should be monitored during outpatient visits. Public health workers should also attend to the potential risks of e- cigarettes to health after SCI, as well as with people with other types of physical disability. Although e-cigarette cessation research is still emerging, traditional cigarette smoking cessation methods are often recommended. These include behavioral therapy to target addictive behaviors and triggers, used alongside pharmacotherapy to treat nicotine dependence.34 E-cigarette-specific digital approaches, such as text message and social media-based interventions, have also demonstrated effectiveness in promoting cessation, particularly among young adults.35,36 Rehabilitation and public health professionals should stay informed about emerging cessation strategies to better support individuals with SCI in reducing e-cigarette use.

Study limitations

Data were collected by self-report, including cigarette and e-cigarette use. There is always a risk of inaccuracies using self-report because of errors in recall or other factors including social desirability. Second, the cohort was comprised of individuals largely well removed in time from their initial SCI (an average of 24.1y since injury) and was relatively old for an SCI cohort (56.7y of age). Therefore, the findings apply most directly to those in that age group. It would be expected that percentages would be higher among younger individuals, just as we found higher percentages within the current study with those under the age of 40. Third, our findings are restricted to general use of e-cigarettes and does not address quantity of use, nor does it address the relationship with SCI outcomes. Lastly, participants were identified in 2 geographic areas of the country (Midwest and southeastern US). The portion of people using cigarettes may vary geographically.

Future research

Additional research is needed to identify e-cigarette use among those prior to SCI and the extent to which e-cigarette use continues after SCI onset. Ultimately, we need to identify the effect of e-cigarette use on the health, well-being, and longevity of those with SCI and develop strategies to prevent e-cigarette use among those with SCI. This would include both rehabilitation and public health professionals.

Conclusions

Among study participants, 16.3% reported using e-cigarettes at least once and 4.9% reported current use of e-cigarettes. Race-ethnicity (Non-Hispanic Black) and older age (above 40) were protective of e-cigarette use. Younger age and cigarette smoking were risk factors related to a greater use of e-cigarettes. Use of e-cigarette has unknown consequences for people with SCI. The findings suggest the need to routinely assess e-cigarette use, educate people on the potential consequences based on the general literature, and intervene when appropriate. Prevention strategies should be developed both during inpatient rehabilitation and upon outpatient follow-up.
