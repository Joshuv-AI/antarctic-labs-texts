Evaluation of the modified critical fusion frequency test apparatus
Authors: Akihiko Sugino, Hiroki Nomoto, Chota Matsumoto, Tomoyasu Kayazawa, Fumi Tanabe, Shunji Kusaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo compare the critical fusion frequency (CFF) thresholds between the newly modified device and the current device.MethodsSixty eyes from 60 normal participants and 60 eyes from 60 patients with ophthalmic diseases were included in this study. All participants underwent the CFF test with the new and current devices. The relationships between age and the CFF values were analyzed in normal participants. The CFF values measured with the new and current devices were compared for all participants.ResultsSignificant correlations were found between the CFF values measured with the new and current devices in normal participants (r = 0.92, p < 0.001) and disease patients (r = 0.99, p < 0.001). Although the mean CFF value of the new device was approximately 1 dB lower than that of the current device (p < 0.01) in normal eyes, no difference was found in eyes with ocular diseases (p = 0.261).ConclusionThe CFF values measured with the new device are equivalent to those of the current device. Our results show that the new device is a competent successor to the current device.

Introduction

The human visual system is highly sensitive to both temporal and spatial changes. The temporal pathway, which is primarily mediated by the magnocellular system, specializes in detecting rapid luminance changes and motion, whereas the spatial pathway, which is associated with the parvocellular system, processes fine spatial details and color [1–3]. Damage to these pathways can selectively impair visual function, depending on the nature and location of the pathology. Optic neuropathies, including optic neuritis and glaucoma, often preferentially affect the temporal pathway in their early stages, leading to deficits in motion detection and temporal processing without necessarily affecting spatial acuity [4–8].

Critical fusion frequency (CFF) threshold measurements offer a noninvasive and objective means of evaluating temporal visual function. By determining the highest frequency at which a flickering light is perceived as continuous [9], CFF testing assesses the temporal resolution of the visual system. Previous studies have demonstrated that optic neuropathies can lead to significant reductions in CFF thresholds, even in the absence of overt spatial vision loss. Moreover, CFF measurements remain valuable during the recovery phase, providing a sensitive indicator of residual temporal dysfunction that may persist even after apparent improvements in spatial visual function [10–12].

CFF measurement values vary across devices due to differences in luminance and flicker presentation methods inherent to each system [12]. Advances in technology have led to the development of more refined devices, such as the one introduced in this paper, which utilize light-emitting diode (LED) to deliver precise and stable light flickers for CFF testing. However, the introduction of new devices raises concerns regarding the compatibility of the results obtained using new devices with those obtained using earlier devices. Ensuring consistency and comparability across different generations of CFF measurement systems is essential for longitudinal studies and clinical applications. The aim of this study was to compare the CFF thresholds obtained using the newly developed device and the previous-generation device in normal and diseased eyes and to assess the clinical applicability of the new system.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This cross-sectional study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kindai University (No. 31–243) and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. After an oral explanation of the study’s nature and potential consequences, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and consent was also obtained from the guardians of patients under the age of 18.

We recruited study participants who visited the outpatient clinic at the Department of Ophthalmology, Kindai University Hospital, between January 1 st and February 28th, 2020. Healthy volunteers were included on the basis of the following criteria: best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) better than 1.0, no history of ocular disease or ocular surgery (except for cataract surgery), and no history of systemic or intracranial disease affecting visual function. All healthy volunteers underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, including slit‒lamp biomicroscopy, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement, and fundus evaluation using either fundus photography or optical coherence tomography (OCT), to exclude retinal disease. Patients with ocular diseases included those diagnosed with neuro-ophthalmological disorders (optic neuritis, traumatic optic neuropathy and ischemic optic neuropathy), retinitis pigmentosa, diabetic retinopathy, branch retinal vein occlusion and glaucoma. All of these patients underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, as performed for the healthy volunteers, and received additional visual field testing. For patients with neuroophthalmological disorders, the diagnostic evaluation included magnetic resonance imaging of the brain and optic nerves.

CFF test apparatus and CFF thresholds

CFF is defined as the highest frequency at which an intermittently presented light stimulus is still perceived as flickering; beyond this point, the stimulus appears fused. In Japan, the Kindai Type II CFF test apparatus (Type II; Yagami Co., Ltd., Japan) (Fig. 1) has been widely used for measuring CFF. The device uses white light as the illumination source and a servo motor to drive the rotation of a center disk, which is segmented into light and dark areas at a 1:1 ratio. The rotation of this disk in front of the light source produces a rectangular-wave modulation of luminance, enabling the measurement of CFF thresholds. The newly developed device, the Kindai Type III CFF test apparatus (Type III; Towa Co., Ltd., Japan) (Fig. 2), employs an LED light source, and CFF is measured by generating flickering stimuli through LED modulation. For both the Type II and Type III devices, the test conditions are standardized as follows: the target size subtends a visual angle of 2 degrees, the background luminance is set at 20 apostilb, the target luminance at 1000 apostilb, and the distance between the target and the eye is fixed at 300 mm. In this study, CFF thresholds were determined using both the ascending and descending methods, and the mean of three measurements was used for analysis. CFF measurements using both the Type II and Type III apparatuses were conducted on the same day for all participants. Analysis

Age-related changes in CFF were examined by evaluating the slope of the regression line for each device in normal eyes. For both normal and diseased eyes, differences in CFF measurements between the two devices were analyzed using a paired t-test. The relationship between the measurements was assessed by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient, and the agreement between the two devices was further evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis. All the statistical analyses were conducted with Microsoft Excel 2013 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Results

Sixty eyes from 60 healthy participants (range: 15–82 y.o. median: 44.5 y.o., female = 47) and sixty eyes from 60 patients with ocular diseases (range: 15–89 y.o. median: 64.0 y.o., female = 35) were included. Among the sixty patients with diseased eyes, forty-five had neuroophthalmological disorders(thirty eyes with optic neuritis, eleven eyes with intracranial or orbital tumor, three eyes with ischemic optic neuropathy and one eye with traumatic optic neuropathy), ten had retinal diseases (five eyes with retinitis pigmentosa, three eyes with diabetic retinopathy and two eyes with branch retinal vein occlusion), and five had glaucoma.

In normal eyes, CFF decreased with age at a rate of –0.12 Hz/year when the Type II device was used and –0.076 Hz/year when the Type III device was used (Fig. 3). The mean CFF values measured with the Type II and Type III devices were 45.8 ± 5.0 Hz and 44.4 ± 4.0 Hz, respectively, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.01). The CFF thresholds corresponding to two standard deviations below the mean were 35.8 Hz for the Type II device and 36.4 Hz for the Type III device. The Type II and Type III devices showed a strong correlation in the CFF measurements (r = 0.92, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). Discussion

The development of the Type III device was prompted in part by the discontinuation of servo motor components used in the Type II model. In addition to adopting an LED light source, the Type III incorporates several modern features, including a digital display and the capability to export results directly into electronic health records. These improvements reflect a design that is better suited to contemporary clinical environments and technological standards. In the present study, we compared CFF measurements obtained with a newly developed Type III device and the conventional Type II device in both normal and diseased eyes. In normal eyes, the Type II device yielded slightly higher CFF values than did the Type III device (p < 0.01); however, no significant difference was detected between the two devices in the diseased group (p = 0.261). Significant correlations between the measurements from the two devices were found in both groups.

CFF values have been shown to decline with age [13, 14], and this decline has been suggested to be related to age-associated reductions in prefrontal cortex function [15]. The age-related decline in CFF was more gradual with the Type III device (–0.076 Hz/year) than with the Type II device (–0.12 Hz/year). Iwagaki et al. [16] reported an age-related decline slope of –0.077 Hz/year for the Type II device, which was nearly identical to the slope observed for the Type III device in the present study. The steeper age-related decline observed with the Type II device may reflect its tendency to produce higher CFF measurements in younger participants (Fig. 5A, 5B). This difference may partially account for the tendency of CFF values measured with the Type III device to be approximately 1 Hz lower than those obtained with the Type II device. One possible factor is the difference in the light sources, particularly in terms of their spectral distributions and the temporal characteristics of their rectangular waveforms, which may affect the results of visual function testing [17, 18]. This is especially relevant in the high-frequency range, where the rising edge of the rectangular waveform is typically less steep in the LED-based Type III device than in the Type II device. Nevertheless, we consider a 1 Hz difference within the normal range is unlikely to be clinically significant. When the lower limit of normal was defined as the mean minus two standard deviations (−2SD), a previous report indicated a cutoff value of 35 Hz [16]. In the present study, similar results were observed, with a lower limit of 35.8 Hz for the Type II device and 36.4 Hz for the Type III device. Previous studies have reported decreased CFF values in patients with neuroophthalmological disorders, retinal disease, and glaucoma. Among these, CFF testing is regarded as especially valuable for the clinical evaluation of visual function in patients with neuroophthalmological disorders. This is because CFF thresholds can reflect both the extent of optic nerve dysfunction and improvements following treatment [11, 12, 19–21]. CFF values greater than 35 Hz are classified as normal, whereas those less than 25 Hz are classified as abnormal [22]. Values ranging from 25 to 35 Hz fall into an indeterminate range and may require further clinical interpretation. In the present study, most cases with reduced CFF values (less than 35 Hz) were associated with neuroophthalmological disorders. In the combined disease group, the CFF measurements obtained with the Type II and Type III devices were significantly correlated (r = 0.99, p < 0.001), and no significant difference was detected between the two devices (p = 0.261). However, another important point to consider is the appropriateness of using 35 Hz as the lower limit of normal for all age groups. As CFF values are also affected by aging, we calculated the age-stratified lower limits of normal by dividing the normal subjects into three age groups (< 40, 40–59, and ≥ 60 years). The lower limits were 39.9 Hz, 35.4 Hz and 35.1 Hz for Type II, and 39.8 Hz, 36.0 Hz and 35.5 Hz for Type III, in order of increasing age group. These values were comparable to the age-stratified –2SD thresholds calculated from the results reported by Iwagaki A, et al. [16]. If the lower limit of normal is uniformly set at 35 Hz, it may lead to underestimation in younger individuals when age-related decline is considered. Further studies are needed to clarify this issue.

This study had several limitations. First, the disease group was predominantly composed of patients with neuroophthalmological disorders. This limitation may be acceptable, as CFF testing is used primarily in actual clinical practice for the evaluation of neuroophthalmological disorders. Furthermore, the cases of retinitis pigmentosa, diabetic retinopathy, branch retinal vein occlusion and glaucoma included in this study largely involved patients with preserved central visual fields and relatively good visual acuity. In the future, it will be important to evaluate CFF in more advanced cases of these diseases as well. Second, the lack of longitudinal data limits the ability to evaluate how CFF changes over time or in response to treatment. Future studies incorporating follow-up assessments will be essential to clarify these longitudinal effects. Third, although the mean of three repeated CFF measurements was used in this study to minimize variability, assessing the degree of fluctuation inherent to each device remains important for evaluating their precision and reproducibility.

In conclusion, the CFF values measured with the newly developed Type III device in eyes with ocular diseases were significantly correlated with those measured with the previous-generation Type II device, and the values were nearly equivalent. These results indicate that the Type III device has sufficient capability to be used as a clinically applicable successor to Type II devices for evaluating visual function.
