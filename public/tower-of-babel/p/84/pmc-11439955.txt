Nanoparticles as a novel key driver for the isolation and detection of circulating tumour cells
Authors: Shahab Bashir, Muhammad Amn Zia, Madiha Shoukat, Imdad Kaleem, Shahid Bashir
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Circulating tumour cells (CTCs), derived from primary tumours, play a pivotal role in cancer metastasis by migrating into the peripheral bloodstream. These cells are paramount in clinical research, serving as early diagnostic markers for metastatic cancer. Analysing CTC counts and their biomarker characteristics can provide invaluable insights into tumour identification, profiling, and metastatic capabilities. However, the rarity and diverse nature of CTCs in the bloodstream present significant challenges to their isolation and detection, especially in the initial stages of metastasis. Recent advancements in nanotechnology have led to the development of innovative CTC separation and detection methods. This review focuses on applying nanoparticles, nanomaterials, and microfluidic platforms to simplify the isolation and detection of CTCs. The infusion of nanotechnology in this field marks a crucial turning point, enabling the necessary progress to advance CTC research.

Introduction

Isolating and finding Circulating Tumour Cells (CTCs) are essential steps in cancer research, which is constantly changing, so we can learn more about it and control it better1. CTCs are cancer cells shed from the primary tumour and enter the bloodstream. They are signs of metastasis and can be used as biomarkers to detect cancer early, predict how healthy treatment will work, and monitor the disease. However, because CTCs are rare and unique, they present challenges requiring innovative solutions to be properly collected and analyzed2. Nanoparticles, with their exceptional sensitivity and selectivity in CTC isolation and detection, emerge as a new critical driver among the many technologies studied. This review paper investigates the revolutionary possibilities of nanoparticles in CTC technology, highlighting recent advances, challenges, and prospects in this fascinating field3.

Cancer tumour cells (CTCs) provide real-time information about a patient's cancer status, allowing for non-invasive monitoring of disease progression and treatment efficacy. The detection and analysis of tumour genetics, metastatic potential, and therapy resistance mechanisms have the potential to completely transform personalized medicine4. CTCs occur at a shallow frequency of one per billion blood cells, and they can exhibit a wide range of phenotypes, making detection difficult. Traditional methods frequently lack the sensitivity and specificity required for practical clinical application despite providing important foundational insights5.

Circulating tumour cells (CTCs)

Circulating tumour cells (CTCs) are cancer cells that exit the primary tumour, enter the bloodstream, and then metastasize to another organ, developing into a new tumour. Even though Thomas Ashworth was the first to discover CTCs in blood in 1869, it was widely acknowledged in 1989 that they play an essential role in tumour metastasis.

Metastasis is a complex process with numerous steps. Cancer cells must occasionally alter their behaviour. Cancer cells that have spread from the primary tumour to extensive metastases must first enter and leave the bloodstream, then enter, stay alive, and eventually leave it (Fig. 1). CTCs connect the various stages of metastasis. These cells can only be found in a safe blood sample. Every 10 mL of blood may contain between one and ten cells (i.e., phlebotomy). Even though some CTCs can quickly enter the bloodstream, a metastasis site for aggressively invading cells remains a long way off. A recent study found that single cells have a half-life of 25 to 30 min, while groups of CTCs have a half-life of 6 to 10 min. In addition to half-life, CTCs in groups are more likely to spread than individual cells meaning clusters of CTCs appear to have increased metastatic potential. Circulating tumour cells as a therapeutic target

Recent clinical data show that circulating tumour cells (CTCs) in the bloodstream are closely related to tumour progression, making them a potential biomarker. CTCs have long been used as liquid biopsies, but efforts to target them to prevent or reduce metastases are relatively new.

CTCs express various adhesion molecules, allowing them to interact directly with endothelial cells in specific tissues or indirectly through leukocytes. These molecules promote CTC invasion and survival by attracting immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils during circulation. Advances in CTC neutralization include the creation of nanoparticles and nano-formulations that improve drug half-life and release profiles in circulation, revitalizing specific treatment approaches.

Methods for isolation of CTC

Isolating CTCs is crucial for their use as a diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic marker. Practical techniques are essential to enhance the clinical applicability of CTCs. These methods, classified into biological and physical techniques based on enrichment principles, aim to capture rare CTCs from peripheral blood successfully.

Biological approaches

CTCs can be separated biologically using surface markers such as epithelial cellular adhesion molecule (EpCAM). Multiple methods, including Cell Search and the Mag-Sweeper system, use EpCAM-modified immunomagnetic beads to find reliable CTCs with certain levels of EpCAM expression. Separating CTCs is more accessible with new technologies like the CTC and Nano-Velcro chips (1). Current methods may be unable to effectively separate CTCs with surface antigen expression that is not specific. For example, scientists are looking into new ways to work around this problem and create ground-breaking technologies like the CTC-iChip by combining biological and physical isolation methods (2).

Physical isolation methods

Physical methods for isolating circulating tumour cells (CTCs) are based on their size, membrane charge, and density. CTCs are separated according to these features using microfiltration, dielectrophoresis, and density gradient centrifugation techniques. Platforms such as microfluidics offer the potential to capture CTCs without the need for specialized surface markers. Enrichment and separation techniques for CTC

CTCs are difficult to separate because of the low percentage of CTCs found in a patient's blood, and it is much more challenging to develop effective techniques for detecting viable CTCs for further research15.

CTC technologies generally use three primary strategies: collection and enrichment, detection and identification, and release13. Physical or antibody-antigen interactions between CTCs and materials may be used to collect and enrich CTCs in the initial step of the process. There are various ways to identify CTCs, including fluorescence microscopy, flow cytometry, surface-enhanced Raman scattering, and electrical impedance measurements. Upstream analyses include genomes, transcriptomics, proteomics, and CTC culture.

Isolation methods of CTCs by nanotechnology

Nanoparticles have opened a new era in isolating and finding CTCs16. New research suggests that CTCs may be a way to measure the size of a tumour throughout its development. This is because tumour cells shed into the bloodstream early in the disease17.

The ability to detect tissue abnormalities through traditional imaging methods and histological analyses paves the way for early cancer detection. The use of cytology and histology as the sole means of early cancer detection is also inadequate (5). Isolating CTCs is more difficult because there is only about one CTC for every million peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in the blood. Detection and characterization of CTCs are further complicated by their heterogeneous nature and the biochemical and molecular changes that occur during epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)18–20. Therefore, finding effective ways to isolate CTCs for early cancer detection is still tricky. So far, no technique has successfully isolated CTCs without contamination, even when invasiveness and cell-surface protein expression are considered21.

Recent advances in nanotechnology have opened exciting new possibilities for cancer research, particularly in drug delivery, diagnostics, and imaging22. Nanotechnology-based approaches have shown potential in enhancing CTC detection by utilizing sophisticated nanomaterials such as nanoparticles. These nanomaterials possess a high surface-to-volume ratio, facilitating efficient binding with cells in the complex blood matrix. Moreover, nanomaterials conjugated with antibodies can enhance the specificity and efficiency of CTC isolation20. By leveraging nanotechnology, earlier detection, accurate diagnosis, and treatment of cancerous tumours are achievable, enhancing the efficiency and sensitivity of CTC isolation and detection7.

In recent years, nanomaterials have played an essential role in CTC isolation, owing to their ability to isolate, identify, and characterize CTCs with high specificity when combined with specific molecules. Nanomaterials' unique properties, including their large surface-to-volume ratio, make them ideal for efficient cellular binding in the blood matrix, which other materials struggle with. As a result, nanotechnology shows promise in improving the selectivity and sensitivity of CTC separation, paving the way for better biomedical applications7.

Magnetic nanoparticles

Magnetic nanoparticle (MNP) technology is one of the first and most popular methods for detecting tumour cells in the blood (CTCs). MNPs are ideal for binding and capturing CTCs because of their fast-binding kinetics and larger surface-to-volume ratio. Another advantage is that they do not clump or combine in water. Antibodies specific to CTC membrane proteins, such as EpCAM, are bound to MNPs, making them an increasingly important component of immunoaffinity-based CTC isolation techniques. This allows a magnetic field to bind and separate CTCs from blood samples selectively. MNPs' specificity for CTCs can be improved by functionalizing them with various molecules such as antibodies, aptamers, and peptides32.

Magnetic particle-based separation techniques use a variety of MNPs, including metal NPs, metal oxide NPs, and alloy NPs. These MNPs can selectively bind CTCs using an external magnetic field and release CTCs by removing magnetic field. Antigen expression-based subpopulation profiling of CTCs is also possible using MNPs modified with recognition molecules. One example is successfully detecting breast cancer CTCs with Fe3O4 MNPs functionalized with HER-2 antibodies. Harmful selection methods, such as the CTC-iChip, separate non-target cells before isolating CTCs via magnetophoresis. Finally, magnetic particle-based techniques hold great promise for the efficient and dependable isolation of CTCs, potentially leading to new advances in cancer detection and treatment33.

Detection and analysis methods, such as imaging modalities and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), are compatible with magnetic nanoparticles, expanding their utility beyond CTC isolation. These tools are essential for developing personalized approaches to cancer treatment, as they allow for the complete characterization of isolated CTCs through genetic analysis and molecular profiling. Automated and rapid sample processing is made possible by the easy integration of isolation methods based on magnetic nanoparticles into microfluidic platforms, improving efficiency and throughput. As nanotechnology advances in design and performance, magnetic nanoparticles for CTC detection and analysis are also becoming more practical for use in clinical settings34.

Graphene and graphene oxide

Graphene oxide (GO) is notable for its excellent thermal conductivity, large surface area, and high mechanical strength. Because of these characteristics, it is an excellent choice for various applications, including biosensing. GO's ability to quench fluorescence makes it worthwhile in optical biosensing. Stacking molecules with aromatic domains can result in fluorescence quenching; however, when a targeting biomolecule binds to a specific protein on a cell membrane, the fluorescence returns35,36. Feng et al. used GO modified with an aptamer that recognizes nucleolin (CTCs) when studying circulating tumour cells37.

Similarly, Viraka Nellore et al. (2015) achieved a high capture yield by creating a GO membrane with aptamers that targeted various cancer biomarkers38. Another study also reported that they developed a patterned gold surface using GO nanosheets functionalized with an EpCAM antibody, which can detect CTCs in various cancers. Overall, GO's nanotextured form and versatility make it an effective tool for separating CTCs from blood samples39.

Graphene oxide (GO) has two distinct advantages for biosensing: the ability to quench fluorescence and its adaptability to functionalization with targeted biomolecules. Using GO, researchers successfully isolated circulating tumour cells (CTCs) by focusing on proteins on cell membranes. However, issues such as potential toxicity and complicated synthesis procedures must be addressed before it can be used more widely in biomedicine.

Dendrimers

Dendrimers represent a unique class of nanoparticles composed of polymers distinguished by their distinctive structure. Unlike conventional polymers, dendrimers are characterized by globular molecules with layered branches, often called generations. This precise synthesis results in monodisperse molecules with well-defined properties. One of the notable features of dendrimers is their outer surface, which can be modified with various functional groups such as COOH, COONa, NH2, or OH. This versatility in functionalization enhances the applicability of dendrimers across a wide range of fields, from drug delivery to nanotechnology45.

With high solubility, consistent size and composition, and ease of functionalization due to many surfaces functional groups, dendrimers are especially well-suited for delivering anticancer agents46. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs)

There are two primary types of carbon nanotubes (CNTs): MWCNTs, or multi-walled, and SWCNTs, or single-walled (SWCNTs). MWCNTs have multiple walls, whereas SWCNTs only have one, resulting in diameters ranging from 2 to 100 nm and 0.2 to 2 nm, respectively. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) of both types can vary in size from micrometres to hundreds of nanometers. These nanotubes hold great promise as research tools due to their strong mechanical and electrical properties. They could be used to study circulating tumour cells (CTCs).

A novel approach involves using CNT surfaces to capture CTCs without the need for EpCAM antibodies. CTC adhesion is facilitated without EpCAM antibodies using a 76-element array chip. The procedure entails introducing a droplet of nucleated cells onto the chip, followed by red blood cell lysis. The solid adhesion between cancer cells and the nanotube surface reduces contaminating leukocytes by up to five times. Researchers discovered that a 48-h period promotes optimal cellular adherence. The flat enrichment surface simplifies microscopy and imaging after enrichment, as it is label and size independent. This technique represents a cutting-edge method for CTC capture, though additional steps may be required.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have emerged as promising tools for capturing circulating tumour cells (CTCs) because of their high mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, large surface area, biocompatibility, and versatility. They provide effective binding sites for immobilizing CTCs and can be functionalized with biomolecules for precise targeting. However, obstacles include high costs, potential toxicity concerns, batch-to-batch variability, complex functionalization processes, and limited biodegradability. Despite these limitations, careful handling and safety considerations can help to maximize the benefits of CNTs in CTC capture and research.

Nanostructured substrates for CTC isolation

The unique properties of nanostructured substrates increase their suitability for isolating and enriching circulating tumour cells (CTCs). These substrates, with unique properties ranging from 1 to 1000 nm, allow for improved cell interactions. In comparison to flat substrates, nanostructured substrates provide more binding sites on both the cell's surface and the substrate itself. This improved binding capability leads to better affinity capture and, as a result, efficient isolation of CTCs from blood samples. Researchers have investigated a variety of nanostructured materials for CTC isolation, each with its own set of advantages. These include nanopillars/nanowires, nanofibers, nanoparticles, nanotubes, graphene oxide (GO), and fractal nanostructures. Still unresolved are issues such as the potential cytotoxicity of materials, the complexity of fabrication processes, and the need for additional optimization to achieve peak performance. Despite these challenges, nanostructured substrates hold promise for improving CTC isolation methods as well as detecting and studying circulating tumour cells in clinical settings.

Recent advances in nanostructured substrates have significantly improved the isolation and enrichment of circulating tumour cells (CTCs). Surface area enhancement and the incorporation of affinity capture agents enable these substrates, with dimensions ranging from 1 to 1000 nm, to improve cell interactions. This design improves the efficiency of CTC capture over conventional flat substrates by increasing the substrate's affinity for rare cells and providing more binding sites. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles

Research into large-surface-area mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs), primarily for medication delivery, has increased dramatically in recent years. The MSNs in question have received a lot of attention because of their easily modifiable pore sizes (between 2 and 50 nm) and surface functionalization capabilities. In vitro, MSNs were used as a model for targeting circulating tumour cells in colorectal cancer (CTCs). Nanoplatforms have been designed specifically to target colorectal cancer cells functionalized with the EpCAM antibody. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that MSNs loaded with mifepristone can effectively reduce the number of lung metastases in mice. Initial research suggests that MSNs have the potential to inhibit CTC activity, potentially slowing the metastatic spread of cancer cells. These findings demonstrate the potential of MSNs as adaptable weapons against cancer metastasis73.

Mice given MSNs containing mifepristone also showed a reduction in lung metastases. As a result, our preliminary findings suggest that MSN could be used to inhibit CTC activity, limiting the spread of cancer cells to other parts of the body74,75.

Nano-chip and Quantum Dots-Based 3D Cytosensors for Efficient Detection of Circulating Tumor Cells

A new method for detecting tumour cells in circulation has been developed using cytosensors based on nickel (Ni) micropillars (CTCs). A breast cancer cell line was used to capture these CTCs that express high levels of EpCAM (MCF7). The innovative part is the electrospinning of ultra-long poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanofibers onto the surface of Ni micropillars, resulting in a 3D bionic interface. The interface is intended to capture CTCs that express EpCAM efficiently. Once captured, these cells can be identified on the micro/nanochip using immune complexes composed of quantum dots (QDs-Ab) conjugated with anti-EpCAM antibodies. The amount of free cadmium ions (Cd2 +) in the dissolved QDs of immune complexes triggers an electrochemical response. To prevent CTCs from escaping during rinsing, target cell capture efficiency and detection sensitivity were improved by combining crossed polyethene glycol (PEG) nanofibers with Ni micropillars. This bio-sensing device has the potential to allow for early-stage cancer diagnosis and monitoring of unusual cell populations in clinical settings, making it a promising tool for on-site cancer monitoring76.

Conclusions and perspectives

The quest for early cancer detection is a frontier in oncology. It emphasizes diagnosing cancer early before significant cellular heterogeneity develops and cancer cells spread physically. The critical role of circulating tumour cells (CTCs) in the metastatic cascade highlights their importance in clinical cancer research and the urgent need for effective isolation and detection methods. Recent advances in nanotechnology have ushered in a new era of possibilities, providing innovative solutions to the complex biological challenges posed by CTCs, thanks to their unique properties, such as high surface-area-to-volume ratios and customizable surfaces for increased selectivity and efficiency.

This review delves into various nanotechnology-based advancements for CTC isolation and detection, shedding light on the potential of these methodologies for unravelling cancer mechanisms and facilitating early detection and monitoring of metastatic diseases. Despite these advances, challenges remain, particularly in balancing the sensitivity and specificity of detection methods, mitigating non-specific binding and potential toxicity of nanoparticles, and developing standardized protocols for clinical use.

The nanotechnology and CTC research intersection has enormous potential to transform cancer diagnosis and treatment. Future research should focus on improving nanomaterial-based approaches to increase the purity and viability of isolated CTCs, addressing the limitations of biological-based isolation methods, and leveraging the power of microfluidics and nanotechnology for more efficient CTC detection. Furthermore, developing dual-functional nanomaterials and integrating nanotechnology with microfluidic devices offer promising opportunities to overcome current limitations and achieve more accurate, sensitive, and patient-friendly cancer diagnostics.

Continuing innovation in nanotechnology and its application to CTC isolation and detection is expected to impact the oncology field significantly. By overcoming current obstacles and realizing the full potential of these technologies, we can expect to improve our understanding of tumour biology, improve early cancer detection, and pave the way for personalized therapeutic strategies, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes and a deeper understanding of metastatic processes.
