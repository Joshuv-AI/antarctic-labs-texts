Cortisol Concentration Is Reduced During the Workday but Is not Associated With Depressive Symptoms in Healthcare Workers During the COVID‐19 Outbreak
Authors: Jeel Moya‐Salazar, Rosa Bermudo‐Rodríguez, Eliane A Goicochea‐Palomino, Belén Moya‐Salazar, Víctor Rojas‐Zumaran, Hans Contreras‐Pulache
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe concentration of cortisol varies during the day and is related to high‐stress situations that could lead to the development of mental disorders such as depression. We aimed to determine the level of cortisol and the presence of depression in health workers in the clinical pathology service of a specialized hospital during 2021.MethodsObservational study conducted in personnel of the clinical pathology service through blood sampling at two time points during the day: in the morning at the beginning of their workday and in the afternoon at the end of it. Moreover, the Zung Depression Scale was also administered.ResultsEighty‐one professionals aged 21–67 participated, with an average age of 42 ± 10.3 years. Of them, 50.6% were women, mostly medical technologists (59.3%), and the most frequent length of service was 1–10 years (43.2%). The serum cortisol concentration in the morning had an average of 10.6 ± 4.9 μg/dL, and in the afternoon it was 8.4 ± 5.9 μg/dL, finding differences between both measurements (p < 0.001). The majority of participants (87.7% morning cortisol, 77.7% afternoon cortisol) did not present depression during both measurements. Only 3.7% of workers with cortisol concentrations outside the normal range during the morning (2.5% with low concentration, 1.2% with high concentration) had mild depression, and during the afternoon measurement, 6.2% with high cortisol concentrations had mild depression. We found no correlation between morning (p = 0.580) and afternoon (p = 0.074) cortisol concentrations and depressive symptoms.ConclusionCortisol levels vary between morning and afternoon in clinical pathology personnel; however, they are not related to the presence of depression. Despite being isolated from human contact, they seem to feel safe in their environment due to the extensive security measures established with the arrival of the COVID‐19 pandemic.Keywords: cortisol, COVID‐19, depression, healthcare professionals, laboratory medicine, pathologists, Peru

Introduction

Healthcare professionals have one of the most demanding jobs worldwide due to the great workload, rotating night shifts, as well as overwhelming emotional interactions when dealing with sick people, and daily exposure to various pathogens [1]. For all these reasons, healthcare personnel are susceptible to experiencing high levels of psychological distress that can lead to the development of anxiety, emotional exhaustion, burnout syndrome, and especially depression [2].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), depression is positioned as the most prevalent mental disorder globally, affecting approximately 5% of adults, but more than 75% of affected individuals reside in low‐ and middle‐income countries [3]. Recent research reveals that between 2013 and 2019, the prevalence of depression among healthcare personnel in high‐income countries ranged from 21.53% to 32.77%. These figures are not far from the current situation, as evidenced by a recent meta‐analysis in countries of the Eastern Mediterranean Region, which reported a prevalence of 33.3% for 2020 [2]. Similarly, a national survey conducted in France in 2021, involving more than 10,000 healthcare workers, revealed a high prevalence of depression of 30.2% in all categories of participants, from doctors and nurses to healthcare executives [4].

These psychological distresses are regulated by the Hypothalami‐Hypophysial system (HHS), which is essential for stress response, as it releases the circadian hormone cortisol to restore homeostasis. This glucocorticoid produced in the adrenal gland acts as a chemical mediator regulating a set of physiological functions, but when stress is persistent, its release is prolonged, triggering maladaptive changes in the HHS [5]. This is because basal cortisol levels rise, which can result in hippocampal atrophy [6]. Studies indicate that atrophy occurs in the left superior frontal convolution and the right lingual convolution, specifically in the prefrontal and limbic areas of the brain, and is related to cognitive alterations that lead to psychological disorders such as depressive symptoms [7]. Therefore, patients with severe depressive symptoms or major depressive disorder present high concentrations of cortisol in plasma and cerebrospinal fluid [8].

The dysregulation of the HPA axis, induced by stress, can contribute to the development of mental disorders, and its assessment can be performed by measuring cortisol levels in saliva, urine, or blood plasma [9]. Various studies indicate an association between distress, depression, and burnout with cortisol concentrations in individuals who work. In particular, it is observed that depression is linked to an increase in cortisol concentration upon awakening, followed by a post‐meridian decrease (between 14:00 and 16:00), as well as before sleep [10].

This is relevant in the context of healthcare workers (HCWs), who face high levels of stress due to work‐related exhaustion derived from the constant pressure to meet the healthcare needs of the community. Although a study conducted on Argentinian personnel of a hospital identified that 40% had capillary cortisol values outside the healthy reference range, directly correlating with the level of experienced stress [11], other studies indicate that capillary cortisol levels do not have a clear relationship with depressive symptoms [12].

All this uniqueness in cortisol balance in HCWs could have been dramatically impacted by the COVID‐19 lockdown. It created an extremely challenging work environment characterized by a high workload, high emotional and psychological stress, and the need to deal with high mortality rates [13]. Peru was one of the first countries to take preventive measures once the first case was recorded on March 15, starting with quarantine, social immobilization, suspension of classes for students, and border closure [14]. Despite these measures, Peru was the fourth country with the most infections worldwide and had an excess mortality rate of 315% [15]. All of this had a negative impact on the mental health of the population in general, but especially on healthcare personnel, who, being on the front line of care, experienced psychological distress associated with the potential fear of contracting COVID‐19 combined with work overload [16, 17, 18].

This was a consequence of the excess demand in healthcare services, increased working shifts, few hours of rest, and the fact of not having adequate personal protective equipment or sufficient resources (i.e., beds in hospitalization and intensive care units, oxygen supplies) to adequately supply and treat patients, resulting in stress, burnout syndrome, anxiety, and depression [19, 20]. This phenomenon in Peru has been evaluated with survey‐based studies aiming to identify its frequency and possible causes [19, 20]; however, alterations in stress‐related hormones or depression have not yet been reported.

We aimed to correlate serum cortisol concentrations with depressive symptoms in HCWs during the second COVID‐19 outbreak in 2021. As psychological distress has been a frequent phenomenon during the lockdown, cortisol levels may play a key role in its regulation. In this regard, we propose the following hypotheses: (i) there is a low frequency of depression in HCWs; (ii) HCWs with depression may have regulated serum cortisol levels; and iii) cortisol levels tend to rise after the workday. We consider that understanding this cortisol balance related to one of the main mental health issues during the pandemic is important.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This observational study was conducted in the Department of Clinical Pathology of the National Teaching Hospital Madre Niño San Bartolomé (HONADOMANI SB), during 2021. This HONADOMANI SB is a tertiary‐care hospital (level III‐E) created in 1656 and recognized as the hospital with the highest resolution capacity in the maternal and child health area at the national level, with specialties in pediatrics, pediatric surgery, and obstetrics and gynecology [21]. In addition to the general functional objectives aimed at women's sexual and reproductive health and child and adolescent health, it has an Office of Teaching and Research that, considering agreements with university entities, guides and promotes research and scientific production, as well as protecting the rights of all involved in such projects [22].

Population and Inclusion Criteria

The population consisted of 95 healthcare workers, including pathologist doctors (MD), medical technologists (MT), and a medical lab technician (MLT) (Figure 1). The inclusion criteria were workers of both genders over 18 years of age who were Peruvian and voluntarily agreed to participate in the research. Workers with chronic and immunosuppressive diseases, pregnant workers, and HCWs on sick leave due to the pandemic were excluded. This leave is given to those who test positive for coronavirus and lasts between 5 and 7 days [23]. Thus, the study sample consisted of 81 participants using nonprobability sampling.

Variables, Instruments, and Measurements

The study variables were self‐reported depressive symptoms by HCWs and serum cortisol concentrations measured twice during the assessment day. Within the framework of this study, blood samples were taken from workers at two specific times of the day. The first sample was taken in the morning (between 7:00 and 9:00 hrs, morning cortisol) at the beginning of the workday, and the second was taken in the afternoon (15:00–17:00 hrs, afternoon cortisol), at the end of the daily work shifts. The purpose of these extractions was to measure serum cortisol levels before and after the daily laboratory medicine work. For this purpose, reagents and the iFlash 1200 automatic immunoassay analyzer (YHLO, Shenzhen, China) were used, following the manufacturer's recommendations.

Secondly, the Depression Scale developed by Zung in 1965 was administered [24]. This was adapted to Spanish by Conde V. in 1969 [25] and was validated in Peru to evaluate depression in a general hospital context by Ruiz‐Grosso et al. in 2012 [26]. The scale consists of 20 items distributed into 4 categories: persistent affective (2 items), physiological disorders (8 items), psychomotor (2 items), and psychic (8 items). Assessment is performed on a Likert scale with increasing ordinal character, assigning positive scores for “never or rarely” responses (1 point), “sometimes” (2 points), “frequently” (3 points), and “always or almost always” (4 points). Questions formulated in the negative sense are scored inversely (10 points).

Depending on the scores obtained, depression is categorized as without depression ( < 50 points), mild (50–59 points), moderate (60–69 points), and severe (70 points or more). This survey has been successfully applied in Peruvian healthcare workers, allowing the estimation of depression levels during [18, 27] and before the pandemic [28, 29].

Data Gathering and Statistical Analysis

Upon approval of informed consent, cortisol samples were collected from the workers and immediately transported to the Immunology Area of HONADOMANI SB for processing. The printed questionnaires were reviewed to ensure the quality of the responses.

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS v24.0 (IBM, Armonk, US) for Windows. Initially, descriptive analyses were performed to estimate simple frequencies and measures of central tendency. Subsequently, the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test was conducted to estimate data normality, and Pearson's correlation test was used. To evidence differences, a paired T‐test and one‐way ANOVA with a Bonferroni post hoc test were employed, considering a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and a confidence interval of 95%.

Ethical Considerations

This study has complied with the bioethical principles and guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration [30]. Additionally, it has the approval of the Ethics Committee of the National Teaching Hospital Madre Niño San Bartolomé (File N°293‐2021‐OADI‐HONADOMANI‐SD, June 24, 2021) and the Universidad Norbert Wiener (File 379‐2021, March 22, 2021).

Results

The average age of the workers was 42 ± 10.3 years, ranging from 21 to 67 years. The highest age frequency was in the groups of 31–40 years and 51–65 years, each with 34.6%. The study included 41 (50.6%) females, 48 (59.3%) were MT, and 29 (35.8%) were MLT. Thus, the most frequent years of service were from 1 to 10 years (43.2%), followed by 11–20 years (24.7%). Most workers were on duty (77.8%), most often in 12‐h shifts (68.3%). Regarding employment status, the majority were permanent workers (67.9%), followed by 21% on contractual status. Additionally, 61.7% of the participants had another job (Table 1).

Most participants (87.7% cortisol AM and 77.7% cortisol PM) did not present depression during both measurements, with only 3 (3.7%) workers with cortisol concentrations outside the normal range [2 (2.5%) with low concentration and 1 (1.2%) with high concentration], who presented mild depressive symptoms in the morning. In the PM cortisol measurement, it was observed that 5 (6.2%) workers with high cortisol concentrations had mild depressive symptoms. In this regard, no correlation was found between morning cortisol concentrations (p = 0.580) and PM (p = 0.074) with depressive symptoms (Table 2).

Discussion

This study evaluated cortisol levels and the presence of depressive symptoms in clinical pathology service workers in a tertiary‐care hospital, finding no correlation between cortisol concentration during the morning and afternoon with depressive symptoms, although there were evening cortisol increases mainly in MT. Additionally, few HCWs had concentrations outside the normal cortisol range and presented mild depressive symptoms.

Strengths

One of the main strengths of this study was analyzing laboratory professionals (MD, MT, and MLT) who perform their duties in daily isolation with minimal human contact [31]. The nature of their work predisposes them to develop depressive symptoms. However, they are not usually considered as the target population in both national and international studies [9, 10, 11, 17, 19, 20, 32]. Additionally, most national studies tend to assess depression using questionnaires to determine its frequency and causes, but alterations in the HHS axis are not usually studied [15, 18, 19]. Consequently, this study stands out for using cortisol levels as a biomarker against the alterations that occur when developing depression [15, 18, 19]. Furthermore, few international studies typically include populations from Latin American countries, as they do not usually deeply evaluate changes in cortisol levels during psychological disorders, making it a significant scientific contribution in Spanish‐speaking countries [9, 10, 11, 12, 32].

Main Findings

Our results indicated that the serum cortisol concentration on average was significantly higher in the morning than in the afternoon. However, these concentration changes did not correlate with depressive symptoms.

Our findings are consistent with those found in Brazilian community health agents, where 28.2% had episodes of depression, and those with < 1 year of work had high cortisol levels, although no association was found between both variables [9]. Similar findings were found in Canadian healthcare personnel 3 months after the onset of the COVID‐19 pandemic, where capillary cortisol levels increased by a median of 29%; 10% showed severe depressive symptoms, but no significant relationship was found between cortisol change and depression [32]. This could be due to the time between cortisol dysregulation and depressive manifestations, which may take more than 3 months to be associated, thus requiring longitudinal studies to better understand the psychological phenomenon faced by workers experiencing high stress.

On the other hand, changes in morning and evening cortisol concentrations in Peruvian HCWs could be explained by the occupational context. Studies involving Canadian daytime shift workers across various fields and workplaces have reported that depression is associated with an increase in cortisol concentration upon awakening and a decrease at 2:00 p.m. and 4:00 p.m. [32], as well as before bedtime, due to the higher levels of physical and mental activity demanded by their work, leading to increased HHS axis production. This may suggest that as workers develop depressive symptoms, their cortisol concentrations are higher upon awakening [10]. In this sense, the changes found in our participants should be monitored in follow‐up studies to determine whether they correlate over time with the development of symptoms of mental discomfort.

As mentioned above, the COVID‐19 pandemic had a significant impact on the environment of healthcare workers as they found themselves on the front line of the fight against the virus. This generated multiple challenges and tensions, especially because being in direct contact with patients, they faced a high risk of contagion, which added worry and stress to their daily work [13]. In the case of our participants, it is important to highlight that they are laboratory workers whose work tends to be carried out in isolation, with no direct contact with patients or other colleagues. Especially during the pandemic, when protective measures intensified, including the use of PPE, biosafety in laboratories was strengthened, and the handling and transport of samples improved [31]. However, these additional measures can generate stress in personnel [31], as observed in a study conducted in Singapore, where more than half of the participants experienced fear, anxiety, and depression to varying degrees of intensity [33]. Despite these challenges, implementations in laboratory infrastructure and dynamics create a safe and protected work environment [31], as indicated by the results of a study conducted in Pakistan, where 68% of the staff expressed satisfaction with the measures adopted by their institutions to address the crisis [34]. In this regard, the decrease in cortisol levels and the absence of depressive symptoms observed in our sample may be related to the lack of direct contact with patients and the highly secure environment in which they work.

Limitations

Firstly, the study design was single‐centered and conducted in a hospital located in the capital city. It is essential to consider that this situation may differ from the social reality in other localities and regions, as the number of healthcare facilities, available resources, and number of professionals can vary considerably in healthcare centers located in provinces, rural areas, or indigenous areas [35, 36, 37, 38]. This disparity is relevant, as previous studies have shown that these factors can contribute to increased work stress, which in turn can trigger depressive symptoms and other disorders such as anxiety or burnout [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 39, 40]. Another limitation of the study is that we only evaluated depressive symptoms without considering other mental illnesses such as anxiety, psychological distress, or burnout, which can also influence changes in cortisol levels [10, 19, 20].

Conclusion

Cortisol levels vary among clinical pathology personnel, being particularly high in the morning and low in the afternoon, although there does not seem to be a direct correlation with the presence of depression. Despite being isolated from human contact, the evaluated laboratory staff appeared to feel secure in their environment, possibly due to the strict security measures implemented following the outbreak of the COVID‐19 pandemic in Peru. However, it is important to maintain vigilance and develop strategies to protect and preserve the mental health of laboratory personnel. Additionally, dynamic work environments that allow interaction among colleagues should be encouraged, thereby facilitating the establishment of trust‐based working relationships.
