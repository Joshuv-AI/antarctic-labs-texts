Evaluation of the inhibitory effect of azoles on pharmacokinetics of lenvatinib in rats both in vivo and in vitro by UPLC‐MS/MS
Authors: Mengming Xia, Xueyi Song, Zebei Lu, Yu Wang, Quan Zhou, Peiwu Geng, Shuanghu Wang, Yunfang Zhou, Qingjun Wu, Aixia Han
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLenvatinib is a multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor used in the treatment of a variety of solid tumors. This study aims to investigate the potential pharmacokinetic interactions between lenvatinib and various azoles (ketoconazole, voriconazole, isavuconazole and posaconazole) when orally administered to rats.MethodsA total of 30 Sprague–Dawley rats were randomly allocated into five groups and administered 20 mg/kg of ketoconazole, voriconazole, isavuconazole and 30 mg/kg of posaconazole and 0.5% CMC‐Na, through gavage for a duration of 7 days prior to the commencement of the experiment. On the final day, the rats were given 10 mg/kg of lenvatinib. The blood concentration of lenvatinib was determined using UPLC‐MS–MS. In vitro lenvatinib were incubated with azoles and rat liver microsomes (RLMs) or human liver microsomes (HLMs). Molecular docking was lastly used to examine the binding strength of the enzymes and ligands with Autodock Vina.ResultsAUC and C max of lenvatinib significantly increased with each of the azoles (p < 0.05), whereas CLz/F decreased 0.83‐flod, 0.41‐fold (p < 0.05) and 0.72‐fold (p < 0.01) in voriconazole, isavuconazole and ketoconazole in rats. The IC50 of lenvatinib with the azoles were 0.237, 1.300, 0.355 and 2.403 μM in RLMs and 0.160, 1.933, 3.622 and 1.831 μM in HLMs. Molecular docking analysis suggested that azoles exhibited a strong binding ability towards the target enzymes.ConclusionIt is imperative to acknowledge the potential drug–drug interactions mediated by CYP3A4 between azoles and lenvatinib, as these interactions hold significant implications for their clinical utilization.

INTRODUCTION

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) represent the prevailing therapeutic approach in impeding the proliferation and demise of cancer cells subsequent to cellular activation. The overexpression or activation of tyrosine is a characteristic observed in numerous malignancies and it is directly associated with the processes of carcinogenesis and tumor formation. 1 Lenvatinib, a compound belonging to the quinoline class, functions as an oral multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor (KIT). It effectively inhibits the proto‐oncogenes KIT, along with the vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFR) 1–3, fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFR) 1–4, and platelet‐derived growth factor receptor. 1 , 2 Lenvatinib has been granted approval for various indications, including progressive radiation‐refractory differentiated thyroid cancer, as well as its use as a second‐line therapy with everolimus or pembrolizumab for advanced or metastatic renal cell carcinoma. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6

The recommended oral dosage of lenvatinib for various cancer patients ranges from 8 to 24 mg, with a once daily dosing regimen. 7 Lenvatinib exhibits low solubility and is rapidly absorbed when administered orally, achieving maximum plasma time (T max) with 1–4 h. However, it does not have any impact on systemic inflammatory therapy. 8 Following hepatic metabolism, lenvatinib is primarily eliminated through feces via bile, with a smaller portion excreted in urine. Previous studies have demonstrated that lenvatinib undergoes biotransformation through various pathways including oxidation, hydroxylation, dealkylation, and glucuronidation. 2 Methylation has been identified as the major pathway of lenvatinib biotransformation, resulting in the formation of O‐desmethyl lenvatinib (M2), N‐decyclopropyl lenvatinib (M1), and lenvatinib N‐oxide (M3) as the major metabolites. M2 was derived from liver microsomes of human, mice, rat, and rabbit, while M1 can only be synthesized in human liver microsomes (HLMs). The metabolism of lenvatinib is anticipated to be mainly facilitated by CYP3A4 and CYP1A1 enzymes, along with cytochrome b5. 2

In a study investigating the coadministration of rifampicin and lenvatinib, it was observed that rifampicin, a P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) inhibitor, resulted in an increase in the area under the plasma concentration‐time curve (AUC(0−∞)) and maximum plasma concentration (C max) of lenvatinib when administered as a single dose. 9 However, when rifampicin was administered multiple times, the AUC(0−∞) and C max of lenvatinib decreased. 9 This suggests that P‐gp inhibitors have the potential to enhance the plasma exposure of lenvatinib. 9 Furthermore, the utilization of enzyme inducers and inhibitors lead to drug–drug interactions (DDIs) that are clinically significant for lenvatinib. 7 The prevailing clinical adverse effects of lenvatinib encompass hypertension, proteinuria, thrombocytopenia, fatigue, and gastrointestinal discomfort, which can typically be managed through dose reduction or discontinuation if deemed necessary. 7 , 10

The three primary categories of pharmaceuticals utilized for the prevention and treatment fungal infections consist of azoles, echinocandins and polyenes. Among these, azoles hold a significant position within the antifungal drug classification due to their favorable oral bioavailability and broad‐spectrum efficacy. 11 , 12 , 13 The alteration of ergosterol levels, either through depletion or buildup, has a significant impact on the permeability and structural integrity of fungal cell membranes, leading to the formation of abnormal lanosterol intermediates. 14 , 15 Prominent drugs belonging to the first generation of azoles encompass clotrimazole, miconazole and econazole, all of which incorporate imidazolyl. The second generation of azoles comprises ketoconazole and fluconazole, wherein fluconazole substitutes the imidazole moiety with the triazole ring. The third generation, referred to as triazole antifungals, encompasses itraconazole, posaconazole, efinaconazole and isavuconazole, among others. 16

The fourth generation of azoles was developed as a means to mitigate DDIs resulting from the suppression of the CYP3A4 enzyme. The primary cause of this inhibitory effect lies in the metal‐binding region of the structure. The resulting decrease in potency was compensated for by modifying the side chain, leading to the creation of the fourth generation of azoles. 16 Additionally, conjugative enzymes and transporter proteins, such as uridine diphosphate glucuronosytransferase (UGT) or P‐gp, also serve as substrates and/or inhibitors of the triazoles. 17 Additionally, sulfamethoxazole demonstrated synergistic effects with voriconazole. 18 Atorvastatin, a lipid‐lowering drug, has been found to be effective when combined with voriconazole. Clinical trials have indicated that voriconazole hinders the metabolism of atorvastatin in patients, with the authors proposing that this outcome is due to dual inhibition via CYP3A4 and P‐gp. 19 However, no antagonistic or synergistic effect was observed when AMD combined with voriconazole. 20

The cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzyme system plays a crucial role in liver microsomes, as the majority of drugs undergo metabolism by CYP450 enzymes. CYP enzymes are considered the foremost phase I enzymes, with CYP enzymes accounting for approximately 90% of drug metabolism. Given the immunocompromised state of tumor patients, they are at a heightened risk of developing opportunistic fungal infections, during the course of treatment. These infections have the potential to exacerbate the disease or even result in fatality. Consequently, the concurrent administration of systemic antifungal drugs is imperative in the clinical treatment of cancer. Azoles represent the prevailing class of antifungal medications and undergo hepatic metabolism primarily mediated by CYP450, with CYP3A4 being particularly prominent in this process.

Lenvatinib undergoes hepatic metabolism primarily through the oxidative pathway CYP3A4, and it is known that most azoles act as inhibitors of CYP3A4. Consequently, the aim of the study was to analyze the plasma concentrations of lenvatinib in rats, both with and without various azoles (ketoconazole, isavuconazole, voriconazole, and posaconazole), using UPLC‐MS/MS. Additionally, the study evaluated the potential DDIs between lenvatinib and the azoles by comparing their pharmacokinetic profiles. The aim of this study was to provide valuable insights for the rational utilization of this medication in clinical settings.

METHODS

Chemicals and reagents

Lenvatinib, midazolam, voriconazole, isavuconazole, ketoconazole and posaconazole (purity >98%) were supplied by Aladdin Industrial Corporation. LC‐grade methanol and acetonitrile were purchased from Merck Company. Ultra‐pure water was prepared by a Millipore Milli‐purification system. Rat liver microsomes (RLMs) were prepared in our laboratory. HLMs were purchased from Wuhan PrimeTox Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd.

Instruments and analytical conditions

The UPLC‐MS/MS system was consisted of an ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatograph and mass spectrometer (Waters ACQUITY UPLC I‐Class/Xevo TQD). The separation of lenvatinib and midazolam (internal standard, IS) process employed an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 Column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) chromatographic column, with the column oven temperature maintained at 40°C. Acetonitrile served as mobile phase A, while 0.1% formic acid and a 20 mM ammonium acetate aqueous solution were used as mobile phase B. The flow rate utilized in the experiment was set to 0.4 mL/min. The injection capacity was 5 μL and the run time was 3 min. The gradient elution procedure employed was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 90–70% B; 0.5–1 min, 70–5% B; 1–2 min, 5% B; 2–2.3 min, 5–90% B.

Ion fragmentation was achieved through multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) using a positive ion mode electrospray ionization (ESI) source. The capillary voltage was 1 kV, while the desolvent gas temperature was maintained at 500°C. The source temperature was controlled at 150°C, and the flow rate of the sheath gas was 50 L/h. Additionally, the flow rate of the desolvent gas was set at 1000 L/h. As shown in Figure 1, the ion transitions of lenvatinib, M2 (O‐desmethyl lenvatinib) and IS were m/z 427.32 → 370.12, m/z 413.0 → 339.0 and m/z 325.98 → 339.0, respectively.

Mass spectrum of (a) lenvatinib and (b) midazolam.

Preparation of standard solutions, calibration curves and quality control (QC) samples

Lenvatinib and IS stock solution was prepared at a concentration of 1.00 mg/mL in methanol. Subsequently, the lenvatinib stock solution was diluted to generate the corresponding working solutions, which were utilized to create QC samples and standard curves. The standard curves encompassed concentration levels of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1000 ng/mL. Similarly, three QC samples were prepared with 3, 300, and 3000 ng/mL. All prepared solvents were stored at −80°C for future utilization.

Method validation

This study was validated conducted in accordance with the bioanalytical method validation principles established by FDA. The validation process encompassed the assessment of various parameters, such as selectivity, calibration curve, lower line of quantification (LLOQ), accuracy, precision, matrix effect, recovery and stability. 21

Selectivity

The selectivity of an analytical method refers to its capacity to differentiate and measure analytes amidst other components in the sample. Blank samples, blank samples spiked with standards, and experimental samples were prepared. The peak area of the interfering component should not exceed 20% of the LLOQ and 5% of the IS during the retention time.

Calibration curve and LLOQ

A weighted least squares linear regression analysis (1/x 2) was conducted to establish the standard curve, utilizing the concentration of lenvatinib as the independent variable (X) and the peak area ratio of lenvatinib and IS as the dependent variable (Y). The response of the LLOQ should be at least five times greater than the blank response, with the LLOQ of 1 ng/mL. The back‐calculated concentration of each calibrator should fall within the range of 85%–115%, while the LLOQ should be within the range of 80%–120%.

Precision, accuracy, extraction recovery and matrix effects

Four concentration levels (1, 3, 300, and 3000 ng/mL) QC samples of lenvatinib were prepared to evaluate intra‐ and inter‐day accuracy and precision with six replicates. Accuracy and precision were expressed using relative error (RE%) and precision was expressed using relative standard deviation (RSD%) with the values <15%. The extraction recovery was determined through the chromatographic peak areas of lenvatinib in QC samples. The evaluation of extraction recovery involved calculating the ratio of the peak areas of the analytes before and after plasma preparation, with the requirement that the ratio falls within the range of 85%–115%.

Stability

Stability tests were performed under four different conditions, including a duration of 6 h at room temperature, 4 h in a 4°C injector, 7 days at −80°C and freeze/thaw cycle. The samples were mandated to exhibit stability with RSD < 15%.

Plasma preparation

The plasma preparation process was protein precipitation with acetonitrile. The supernatant of frozen plasma was thawed, and 50 μL plasma was mixed with 100 μL acetonitrile solution containing 200 ng/mL IS. The mixture was thoroughly mixed by vortexing for 30 s. Subsequently, centrifugation was conducted at 17 949 g for 5 min to obtain the supernatant. Finally, 100 μL supernatant was injected into autosampler vials.

Effects of azoles on the pharmacokinetics of lenvatinib in vivo

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (no. xmsq2021‐0409). The rats were raised in dwelling that complied with generally accepted guidelines for the treatment and use of laboratory animals, including a 12‐h day/night cycle, relative humidity of 40%–60%, temperature ranges of 22–26°C, ad libitum access to tap water, and twice‐daily feedings. Male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (220 ± 20 g) were purchased from by Experimental Animal Center of Wenzhou Medical University. A total of 30 male SD rats were randomly assigned to five groups: ketoconazole group, voriconazole group, isavuconazole group, posaconazole group and control group, with each group consisting of six rats of similar weight. Seven days before the experiment, the subjects were administered a daily dosage of 20 mg/kg ketoconazole, voriconazole, isavuconazole and 30 mg/kg of posaconazole dissolved in 0.5% CMC‐Na suspension and 0.5% CMC‐Na via gavage. On the seventh day, after the initial administration of the inhibitor and placebo in each group for 30 min, a dosage of 10 mg/kg lenvatinib was administered in each group. Subsequently, 0.3 mL blood was collected from the tail vein of rats at 0.167, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24 and 36 h and placed in heparin‐sodium‐conjugated 1.5 mL EP tubes. The blood samples were centrifuged at 1699 g for 5 min, and the supernatant plasma was extracted and subsequently stored in a refrigerator at −80°C for further use.

Effects of azoles on lenvatinib metabolism in vitro

The incubation system utilized in this study consisted of 200 μL, comprising of 100 mM pH 7.4 potassium phosphate buffer, 0.5 mg/mL RLMs or 20 mg/mL HLMs, and the varying concentration of lenvatinib (1, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100 and 200 μM). Prior to incubation, the system was preincubated at 37°C for 5 min. The reaction was then initiated by the addition of NADPH at a final concentration of 1 mM, and the incubation was continued for 30 min. Then, 200 μL of acetonitrile solution containing 200 ng/mL IS was added for protein precipitation. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s and subsequently centrifuged at 17 949 g for 5 min.

Molecular docking study

The interaction between lenvatinib and various azoles was studied using the Auto Dock 4.2 docking program. Initially, crystal structure PDB files of the CYP3A4 protein were obtained from the PDB database website (https://www.rcsb.org/). 22 Subsequently, the 3D molecular structures of lenvatinib, ketoconazole, voriconazole, isavuconazole and posaconazole were acquired in SDF file formats from the Pubchem website (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and converted into PDB files via OpenBabel 3.1.1 to PDB files. Lastly, proteins and small molecules underwent pretreatment using Autodock Tools 1.5.7 to remove water and introduce hydrogen atoms, and then molecular docking was performed with Autodock Vina.

Statistical analysis

Graphpad Prism 9.0 was used to draw blood concentration‐time curves. Drug and Statistics (DAS) 3.0 software (Version 3.2.8; Lishui People's Hospital, China) in a nonatrial model was selected to calculate the pharmacokinetic parameters. The relevant data was statistically computed and analyzed using SPSS software (version 27.0), including two tailed‐t‐test, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 to determine significant variations in the rubric.

Effect of azoles on the pharmacokinetics of lenvatinib in vivo

The graphical representation in Figures 3 and 4 illustrate the average blood concentration‐time profiles of lenvatinib, while Tables 3 and 4 provide a comprehensive summary of the principal pharmacokinetic parameters in the nonatrial model. Following a single oral administration of 1.0 mL/kg lenvatinib in rats, rapid absorption occurred, leading to the attainment of Tmax at 0.92 ± 0.2 h, accompanied by an elimination half‐life (t 1/2) of 3.71 ± 0.85 h. Noteworthy alterations in pharmacokinetic parameters were observed subsequent to the oral administration of the four azoles, which were also reflected in the blood concentration‐time profiles. In the voriconazole group, there was no significant variation observed t1/2, as indicated by the lack of statistical significance. The AUC(0−t) and AUC(0−∞) of the drug increased by 4.72‐fold and 4.71‐fold, respectively (p < 0.01). Additionally, the mean residence time (MRT(0−t)) and MRT(0−∞) increased by 0.44‐fold and 0.41‐fold (p < 0.05), while the plasma clearance (CLz/F) decreased by 0.83‐fold compared to the control group.

In the isavuconazole group, there was a notable increase of 50.2% and 50.0% (p < 0.05) in AUC(0−t) and AUC(0−∞), respectively, while a decrease of 41.37% (p < 0.05) in CLz/F. These findings suggest that isavuconazole has the potential to enhance the plasma exposure of lenvatinib and exert an inhibitory effect on its metabolic activity. In the ketoconazole group, AUC(0−t) and AUC(0−∞) increased by 3.01‐fold (p < 0.01), apparent volume of distribution (Vz/F) and CLz/F decreased by 69.0% and 72.2% (p < 0.01), respectively. Moreover, ketoconazole significantly enhances the plasma exposure of lenvatinib, thereby increasing the chance of adverse drug reactions. Conversely, in the posaconazole group, there was a significant increase in the AUC(0−t) and AUC(0−∞) by 94.5% and 94.3%, compared to the control group (p < 0.05). Additionally, Vz/F decreased by 41.37% in comparison (p < 0.05).

Effects azoles on the pharmacokinetics of lenvatinib in vitro

The apparent enzyme kinetic parameter of lenvatinib was determined using the Michaelis–Menten equation, with the Km of 20.73 μM in RLMs and 27.81 μM in HLMs, as depicted in Figure 5. The results obtained, presented in Figures 6 and 7, revealed the IC50 values of ketconazole, voriconazole, isavuconazole and posaconazole to be 0.237, 1.300, 0.355 and 2.403 μM in RLMs, respectively and to be 0.160, 1.933, 3.622 and 1.831 μM in HLMs, respectively. These in vitro results indicated that four azoles strongly inhibit the metabolism of lenvatinib in RLMs, while weak inhibition was observed in HLMs. Meanwhile, the strength of inhibition of lenvatinib by the four azoles was different between RLMs and HLMs.

Molecular docking study

As depicted in Figure 8, the protein CYP3A4 (PDB code: 2j0d) exhibits strong affinity towards lenvatinib (PubChem CID: 9823820) and four different triazoles, while lenvatinib demonstrates tight binding to voriconazole (PubChem CID:71616), ketoconazole (PubChem CID:456201), isavuconazole (PubChem CID:6918485) and posaconazole (PubChem CID:468595) within the same binding pocket. The respective minimum binding energies of CYP3A4 with lenvatinib and ketoconazole were −8.2 and −9.2 kcal/mol. Ketoconazole exhibits hydrogen bonding with Thr‐224 and Pro‐107 amino acid residues in CYP3A4, while lenvatinib forms hydrogen bonds with Arg‐372 and Ala‐370 amino acid residues in CYP3A4. The binding energy of CYP3A4 to voriconazole is recorded as −7.2 kcal/mol, with voriconazole specifically binding to the Ala‐370 amino acid residue through hydrogen bonding with CYP3A4. Similarly, the binding energy of CYP3A4 is measured −7.8 kcal/mol. Additionally, the binding energy of CYP3A4 to isavuconazole was −7.8 kcal/mol, and that of posaconazole was −2.1 kcal/mol. The interaction between posaconazole and CYP3A4 occurs through hydrogen bonding with the Arg‐212 amino acid residue. The inhibitory effect of isavuconazole on CYP3A4 metabolism of lenvatinib may be attributed to the spatial proximity of the substrate lenvatinib to the binding of the azole inhibitor to CYP3A4.

DISCUSSION

In the clinical management of cancer patients, it is common practice to employ a combination of multiple drugs to address both the cancer itself and its associated complications. However, it is important to acknowledge the existence of DDIs that can significantly heighten the occurrence of adverse drug reactions, potentially induce toxic effects and drug resistance, and diminish the desired therapeutic outcomes. In light of its characteristics a low metabolism oral KIT with antiangiogenic properties and the activity to modulate the tumor immune microcirculatory system, lenvatinib presents itself as a viable option for the treatment of various of solid tumors.

However, it is important to rigorously screen drugs that can potentially DDIs with lenvatinib, as it is primarily metabolized by the main metabolizing enzyme CYP3A4. Most azoles have the ability to inhibit or activate CYP3A4. To investigate the potential DDIs between lenvatinib and azole antimicrobial drugs, a UPLC‐MS/MS bioanalytical method was established and successfully utilized in this study to determine the concentration of lenvatinib in rat plasma. Additionally, this method was employed to evaluate the drug interactions between lenvatinib and various azoles antimicrobials. In the current investigation, the in vivo pharmacokinetic parameters of lenvatinib were found to be notably modified upon coadministration with ketoconazole, voriconazole, isavuconazole and posaconazole, exhibiting significant differences. Furthermore, RLMs and HLMs were selected to establish an in vitro model to assess the drug–drug interactions of lenvatinib in this experiment. The in vitro tests revealed the inhibitory effects of the four azoles on the metabolism of lenvatinib, as videnced by alterations in IC50 and K m values. In both models, the four azoles demonstrated different inhibitory capacities and strong inhibition was observed in the HLMs. This discrepancy in inhibitory potency between humans and rats may be due to species differences. Additionally, the outcomes of molecular docking analysis suggest that azoles potentially impede the binding of lenvatinib and CYP3A4 through spatial proximity.

Biological‐based models are available for the analysis and prediction of DDIs in the study of lenvatinib. In a study evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of lenvatinib in combination with regorafenib for breast cancer, it was shown that the combined administration of these drugs exhibited a synergistic effect on the survival of breast cancer cells, with observed variations in drug concentration. 23 Furthermore, the combination of lenvatinib and erlotibib, both VEGRF inhibitors, has been found to enhance the treatment of HCC by overcoming drug resistance. 24

Combining lenvatinib with golvatinib inhibited pericyte‐mediated vascular stability and TEM development. 25 In a case of HCC in which lenvatinib was combined with warfarin potassium, the PT‐INR was elevated, and considering that both drugs are metabolized via CYP3A4 and CYP2C9, it is possible that the metabolism of lenvatinib had an impact on the metabolism of warfarin potassium. 26 It has been found that in rat, dog, monkey and human liver, all species produce M2, M3 and M2’ in an NADPH‐dependent form via the P450‐dependent metabolic pathway, and M3’ via the aldehyde oxidase‐dependent pathway in monkeys and humans, and it can be seen that the metabolism of lenvatinib is species‐specific. 27 Cui et al. established a UPLC‐MS/MS analytical method and found that a low dose (150 mg/kg) of Wuzhi capsule resulted in a significant decrease in Vz, and a high dose (450 mg/kg) of WZC significantly increased AUC and C max at the time of drug administration. 1 The authors also speculated that P‐gp activity was the main reason for the increased plasma exposure to lenvatinib. Ketoconazole was found to have a small effect on the PK parameters of lenvatinib, and given that there was little change in t 1/2 it was thought that the first‐pass effect affected metabolism. 28

Posaconazole is a strong inhibitor of CYP3A4 (IC50 value of 1.3 μM), with >95% protein binding; voriconazole is a moderate inhibitor of CYP3A4 and CYP2C9 (IC50 values of 10.5 μM and 8.4 μM, respectively), a substrate and strong inhibitor of CYP2C19, and a substrate and inhibitor of P‐gp. 29 Venetoclax is metabolized mainly by CYP2C19, however, it is metabolized at a lower level by CYP3A4 and FMO3. 30 Voriconazole was better tolerated and bioavailable than posaconazole. 31 Li et al. established a UPLC‐MS/MS bioanalytical method to observe the pharmacokinetics to study DDIs. 32 When coadministered with posaconazole, the AUC(0−t), AUC(0−∞), C max, and Tmax of posaconazole were significantly increased, and plasma clearance was decreased. 33 Liu et al. evaluated the drug interaction between isavuconazole and flumatinib by three sets of hepatic microsomal models, HLMs, RLMs and recombinant human CYP3A4, respectively. 34 It has been shown that both ketoconazole and itraconazole inhibit the in vivo metabolism of alpelisib. 35 Posazonazole differs from other triazole antifungals in that it is barely metabolized by the CYP450 pathway and 17% is glucuronidated by UGT1A4. The inhibitory potency of posaconazole is reflected in the concentration. 36 In this study, the in vivo pharmacokinetic parameters revealed stronger inhibition by voriconazole than by isavucoanzole, while the in vitro data at RLMs demonstrated stronger inhibition by isavuconazole than by voriconazole.

In conclusion, the UPLC‐MS/MS bioanalytical method employed in this study proved to be a straightforward, expeditious, and accurate approach for determining the concentration of lenvatinib in rat plasma. The investigation on DDIs involving lenvatinib and various azoles (ketoconazole, posaconazole, voriconazole and isavuconazole) revealed that all four azoles exhibited inhibitory effects on the in vivo metabolism of lenvatinib. Notably, ketoconazole and voriconazole demonstrated stronger inhibitory effects with higher concentration of lenvatinib. The in vitro tests also revealed that the four azoles exhibited a stronger inhibitory effect on the metabolism of lenvatinib. This inhibitory effect was subsequently confirmed through molecular docking, indicating that it is mediated by the modulation of the CYP3A4 enzyme. The effects and mechanisms of azoles on lenvatinib metabolism could provide a basis for the rational use of drugs in clinical practice, thereby mitigating the occurrence of adverse reactions and improve the safety of drug therapy.
