Coumarin Conjugation as an Albumin-Binding Strategy to Modulate the Pharmacokinetics of FAPI Radiotracers
Authors: Ju Xiong, Yifei Pei, Xiaoyi Guo, Yuchen Yang, Libing Wu, Liyan Fan, Hongwei Hou, Zhi Yang, Jinghong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fibroblast activation protein (FAP) was a prominent target overexpressed in the stroma of various tumors. However, most current FAP-targeted radiotracers suffer from rapid systemic clearance and limited tumor retention, which limits their imaging contrast and therapeutic efficacy. In this study, we exploited diethylamino coumarin as a novel small-molecule albumin binder to optimize the pharmacokinetic profile of FAP-targeted radiopharmaceuticals. Two probes with different linker lengths, [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU and [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU, were synthesized with high purity (>95%) and stability. The PEG2 variant demonstrated superior cellular uptake (6.72 ± 1.94 IA%/106 cells at 120 min) compared to PEG6 (4.73 ± 0.51 IA%/106 cells). It achieved high tumor accumulation with a tumor-to-heart (T/H) ratio of 14.64 ± 7.24 at 60 min. Crucially, unlike [68Ga]­Ga-FAPI-04, whose tumor-to-muscle ratio declined from 4.18 ± 0.14 to 2.44 ± 0.15 over 120 min, the PEG2 probe’s T/M ratio continuously increased from 3.65 ± 0.18 at 30 min to 5.17 ± 0.54 at 120 min. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of the coumarin-based albumin-binding platform to enhance the pharmacokinetics of FAP-targeted radiopharmaceuticals.

Introduction

Cancer remains the world’s most significant public health challenge. By 2022, approximately 20 million new deaths were recorded globally, with a staggering 9.7 million attributable to cancer. Among these fatalities, nearly half (about 49.2%) occurred within Asian populations. For cancer, early diagnosis and treatment significantly improve survival rates. However, approximately 50% of cancers are diagnosed at an advanced stage, meaning patients may miss the optimal window for curative intervention. To address this, the development of precise molecular tools for both early-stage characterization and effective long-term management is paramount. Achieving high-contrast visualization and sustained therapeutic delivery at the tumor site represents one of the most critical strategies for reducing cancer mortality and improving patient outcomes.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) constitute the primary noncancerous stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment. Fibroblast activation protein (FAP) was widely expressed on the surface of CAFs within the tumor stroma. Fibroblast activation protein (FAP) is a type II transmembrane serine protease. , FAP is upregulated in the stroma of over 90% of epithelial cancers and is closely associated with tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, immune suppression, and prognosis. , Consequently, FAP represents a highly promising biomarker reflecting tumor microenvironment characteristics, making it a diagnostic and therapeutic target for various malignancies. In recent years, several radiopharmaceuticals based on FAP inhibitor derivatives (FAPI-04, FAPI-46) have been developed to achieve high-affinity tumor imaging and targeted therapy. However, their rapid renal clearance results in an insufficient retention time at the tumor site, thereby limiting their overall efficacy in both diagnostic precision and potential therapeutic translation. Therefore, there is an urgent need to reengineer the FAPI scaffold with enhanced tumor retention capacity and improved pharmacokinetic properties for both precise characterization and future therapeutic applications.

Albumin is the most abundant protein in human plasma, accounting for approximately 60% of its total protein content. It plays a crucial role in regulating osmotic pressure and modulating immune responses within the body. Its excellent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and inherently long circulatory half-life made it a highly attractive and safe platform for drug delivery. , To leverage this natural, long-circulating pathway, various strategies have been employed to complex therapeutic agents with albumin, such as covalent conjugation, high-affinity noncovalent binding, or genetic fusion. Classic small molecules that served as reversible albumin-binding groups, including fatty acids, ibuprofen, Evans blue derivatives, 4-(p-iodophenyl)­butanoic acids, , and dansyl derivatives, have been used to modulate the pharmacokinetic profiles of FAPI, PSMA, and other agents. It is reasonable to expect that the application of novel albumin-binding moieties, particularly in fields such as radionuclide imaging and therapy, will continue to evolve rapidly.

Coumarins represented a significant class of benzopyranone derivatives. Initially recognized as natural products with anticoagulant and anti-inflammatory activities, − their potential has been enhanced through systematic structural modifications and functionalization of the benzopyranone core. In medicinal chemistry, research on coumarins has gradually expanded from pharmacological activity to their application as functional albumin binders, , as exemplified by warfarin and 7-hydroxycoumarin maleimide. Notably, covalent conjugation of 7-hydroxycoumarin maleimide to glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) extended its elimination half-life significantly, from 0.6 h to over 6 h. Furthermore, this albumin-binding property of coumarin derivatives has been leveraged to construct artificial metalloenzymes (ArMs). These ArMs can specifically recognize and bind diverse cell surface receptors, achieving selective preferential accumulation in tumors and localized prodrug activation. − From a molecular mechanistic perspective, the aforementioned binding is attributed to the formation of a ground-state complex via a static quenching mechanism, characterized by a stoichiometric ratio of 1:1, wherein the molecule primarily anchors within the hydrophobic cavity of albumin subdomain IIA or IIIA. This interaction is synergistically governed by diverse noncovalent driving forces, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals forces, with the dissociation constant (Kd ) typically falling within the 10–5 M range. Furthermore, the introduction of specific polar substituents may further reinforce the hydrogen-bonding network, thereby augmenting the overall binding affinity. Despite the fact that this distinctive albumin-binding mechanism has already engendered numerous pharmacological applications, it has yet to be explored as a strategy for optimizing radiotracer pharmacokinetics. Extending this motif to FAPI ligands could potentially overcome the rapid washout limitation and enhance the imaging window.

Herein, we developed two novel coumarin-conjugated FAPI ligands (denoted as PEG2-COU and PEG6-COU) derived from the FAPI-04 scaffold. By incorporating a 7-(diethylamino)­coumarin moiety via PEG linkers of different lengths, we aimed to systematically evaluate the impact of albumin binding on the pharmacokinetic profile. We first determined the radiochemical properties of FAPI-COUs, including stability and hydrophilicity (log D 7.4). Subsequently, tumor targetability and pharmacokinetics were evaluated and compared in U87MG tumor-bearing mice via dynamic PET imaging. Finally, the tissue uptake and blood clearance were further profiled. Collectively, these results validate that coumarin can be employed as a promising albumin-binding motif that enhances the pharmacokinetic profile and tumor retention of FAPI-based radiopharmaceuticals.

Materials and Methods

Instruments and Reagents

All the starting materials were obtained from Shanghai Kaiwei Chemical Technology Co., Ltd., Bide Pharmatech Ltd. (China), Energy Chemical, Beijing InnoChem Science & Technology Co., Ltd., and Shanghai Haohong Scientific Co., Ltd. Commercially available reagents were used without further purification, unless noted otherwise. The synthetic compounds were purified using an automated purification system (Orienda BRIX-2860S, Beijing) equipped with a silica gel flash column (200–300 mesh) or a reversed-phase C18 column (10 μm, 20 mm × 250 mm, FLA-3025-10-120 Å). Mass spectra were obtained with an Agilent 1290-6460 LC–MS system. The NMR spectra were recorded on an AVANCE NEO 600 NMR system (Bruker, USA). Human glioma cells (U87MG) were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. [68Ga]­GaCl3 was eluted from a 68Ge/68Ga generator (ITM, Germany) in Beijing Cancer Hospital. Radiochemical purities of the compounds were analyzed using thin-layer chromatography (iTLC-SG strips, Agilent Technologies), a radio-TLC scanner (Bioscan Mini-Scan, USA), and an analytical Radio-HPLC (1200 series, Agilent, USA). Radioactivity and radio counting were measured using a CRC-25R dose calibrator (CAPIN-TEC Inc., USA) and a γ-counter (WIZARD 1, PerkinElmer, USA), respectively. Small animal PET imaging studies were performed using a microPET/CT (Super-Nova, PINGSHENG, China).

Radiochemistry

68GaCl3 was prepared using a 68Ge/68Ga generator. The generator was eluted with 4 mL of 0.05 mol/L HCl using a syringe at a flow rate of 1–2 mL/min. The first 1 mL of HCl was discarded. The remaining 3 mL of eluate was collected, and its radioactivity was recorded. A 10 mM DMSO stock solution of the precursor was prepared. 3 μL (30 nmol) of the precursor solution was pipetted into a vial, a solution of 68Ga3+ ions (radioactivity: 74 MBq) was added, mixed thoroughly, sealed, and reacted at 95 °C and pH 4.5 for 15 min. After reaction completion and cooling to room temperature, the product was purified using a preconditioned Sep-Pak C18 column (preconditioned with 5 mL of ethanol and 10 mL of H2O). Finally, the cartridge was eluted with 0.5 mL of ethanol (80%), and the product was diluted with 5 mL of saline. The labeled product (denoted as [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU or [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU) was sterilized by filtration through a polytetrafluoroethylene filter (0.22 μm) prior to use. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis (stationary phase: fast-flow silica gel paper; developing phase: physiological saline: EDTA = 1:1) and radio-HPLC (Radioactive High-Performance Liquid Chromatography) analysis (ACN, 5%–95%, 20 min) showed compound purity exceeding 95%.

In Vitro Stability

The stability of [68Ga]­Ga-PEGm-COUs was investigated in saline and human serum. Ten μL of labeled product (185–370 kBq) was diluted to 3.7 MBq/mL using saline or human serum albumin (HSA, 5% in saline), respectively. The solutions were incubated at room temperature for 30, 60, 120, and 240 min, and 10 μL of the solutions were aliquoted, and radiochemical purity was determined by radio-HPLC and radio-TLC.

Octanol/Water Partition Coefficient (log D 7.4)

To determine the lipid–water partition coefficient, dispensed 500 μL n-octanol and 490 μL water into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Added 10 μL of the labeled product (185–370 kBq), vortexed to mix, and centrifuged for 5 min at 3500 rpm. Subsequently, 50 μL each from the aqueous phase and organic phase were transferred to radioimmunoassay tubes. Radioactivity counts were measured for each tube using a gamma counter (Wizard II, PerkinElmer Inc., Germany), with three parallel samples prepared for each sample.

Molecular Docking

To investigate and visualize the interactions between FAPI-04 or PEGm-COUs and proteins (including FAP and HSA), molecular docking was performed by using Autodock. The three-dimensional structures of FAP and albumin were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Database with PDB IDs 1Z68 and 1AO6, respectively. Autodock was employed for molecular docking simulations between proteins and compounds.

Fluorescence Titration

Two hundred microliters of the stock HSA (1.00 × 10–5 M) was mixed with 1800 μL of PBS buffer (1×, pH = 7.3) in a 1 cm cuvette, which was placed in a constant temperature cell holder for 10 min to reach thermal equilibrium. The titration of HSA by each scaffold compound was done by adding a 1.00 × 10–3 M methanol solution of the compound to the HSA at 5 μL intervals and recording the fluorescence spectrum (290–500 nm) of the mixture after each addition. The association constant (K a ) was determined by plotting log F against log [D] according to eq . 1 log[F0−FF]=logKa+nlog[D]

Eq is used to derive the binding constant (K a ) and the number of binding sites (n) on the protein from the intercept and the slope of the straight line.

Cell Culture, Cellular Uptake, and Block Assay

The U87MG cancer cell line was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd. It was cultured in MEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5%CO2.

U87MG cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in a 24-well plate and allowed to adhere at 37 °C for 24 h. The cells were divided into an uptake group and an inhibition group. For the uptake group, the labeled product was diluted to 148 kBq/mL in serum-free basal medium. For the inhibition group, a competitive inhibitor (complex precursor, 3 nmol) was added to each well. The old medium in the 24-well plate was discarded, washed 2–3 times with PBS, and fresh serum-free medium (500 μL/well) was added. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 15, 30, 60, and 120 min, respectively. After incubation, cells were lysed using 1 M NaOH (500 μL). The lysate was collected, and the well was washed once with PBS. The wash solution was then combined with the lysate. Radioactivity was measured using a gamma counter, with three replicates per time point.

Tumor Models

All animal experiments were performed according to the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Peking University Cancer Hospital (Approval No. EAEC 2022-01). 4–6-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Biotechnology Co., Ltd. U87MG and A549 cells were harvested and resuspended in prechilled PBS. Cell density was adjusted to 2 × 107 cells/mL and subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank of BALB/c nude mice (100 μL). Animal studies were conducted when tumor volume reached about 300 mm3.

Small-Animal PET Imaging

PET scanning and image analysis were performed using a small animal PET scanner (Super-Nova, PINGSHENG, China). Each U87MG and A549 tumor-bearing mouse received an intravenous tail injection of 200 μL labeled product (∼3.7 MBq). The blocking study was conducted in U87MG tumor-bearing mice. Briefly, each mouse received injections of both the labeled product and the cold precursor (PEGm-COUs, 150 nmol). Following anesthesia, PET signals were acquired at 30, 60, and 120 min postinjection, and PET/CT images were reconstructed.

Biodistribution

Biodistribution was assessed via tail vein injection of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU (1.85 MBq, 200 μL). Mice were euthanized at the indicated time points (30, 60, and 120 min), and various tissues or organs (including blood, heart, liver, spleen, kidney, lung, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, muscle, bone, brain, and tumor) were dissected and weighed. Subsequently, the radioactivity levels in the organs were detected using a gamma counter. Tissue uptake was expressed as the percentage of the injected dose per gram of tissue (% ID/g) and presented as mean ± SD (n = 3 mice).

Pharmacokinetics

Five KM mice (5–6 weeks old, 18–20 g) received an intravenous injection of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU (100 μCi, 200 μL) diluted in saline via the tail vein. Blood samples were then collected from the mice’s tail veins using capillary tubes at 1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min postinjection. The samples were placed in radioimmunoassay tubes, weighed individually, and the radioactivity in each sample was measured using a gamma counter. Blood uptake was expressed as the percentage of the injected dose per gram of blood (% ID/g) and presented as mean ± SD (n = 5 mice). Pharmacokinetic fitting was performed for the probe [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU using GraphPad Prism. The effective half-life in the blood was calculated using a 2-compartment model.

Statistics

All experimental data were repeated three or more times (n ≥ 3) and were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). The means were compared using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons post hoc analysis for statistical analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The levels of significance were indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Chemistry and Radiochemistry

The molecular design strategy and radiolabeling approach were illustrated in Figure . Based on structural modification of FAPI-04, the incorporation of polyethylene glycol chains is intended to modulate molecular lipophilicity: the shorter PEG2 unit may preserve target recognition efficiency, whereas the intermediate chain length of PEG6 may facilitate optimized clearance kinetics while circumventing the attenuation of targeting efficacy that would otherwise result from excessive linker extension. Subsequent introduction of an albumin-binding diethylamino coumarin moiety and a DOTA chelator were incorporated through a lysine-based bifunctional linker to construct PEGm-COUs (m = 2 or 6) (Figure A). The ε-amino group in the lysine of the intermediate A4 was connected via a PEG1 linker to obtain A5. This structure was coupled with a diethylamino coumarin derivative to yield the target compound A6. Finally, the α-amino group in the lysine of the intermediate A6 was further linked to a DOTA chelating group to obtain PEGm-COUs (Figure B and C). Molecular Docking

To elucidate the binding modes of the ligands with FAP, molecular docking modeling was performed. As illustrated in Figure A and B, both PEG2-COU and PEG6-COU demonstrated high spatial complementarity within the FAP catalytic pocket. Specifically, the docking poses of PEGm-COUs closely overlapped with those of FAPI-04 (Figure S9), suggesting that the coumarin-based albumin-binding moiety does not sterically hinder the core pharmacophore’s interaction with FAP. This was further supported by the calculated binding free energies, which were −7.4 kcal/mol for PEG2-COU, −7.3 kcal/mol for PEG6-COU, and −8.2 kcal/mol for FAPI-04. These results indicated that the modification of FAPI-04 with a PEG linker and coumarin preserves the high-affinity binding to FAP.

To further investigate the albumin-binding capacity, docking simulations between PEGm-COUs and HSA were conducted (Figure S10, Figure C and D). PEGm-COUs were found to be well-accommodated within the hydrophobic subdomain of HSA, stabilized by a network of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. For example, in the PEG2-COU/HSA, key residues such as Arg-222, Arg-160, and Gln-196 form multiple hydrogen bonds with the ligand. The calculated binding energies for PEG2-COU and PEG6-COU with HSA were −8.5 kcal/mol and −8.0 kcal/mol, respectively. These favorable scores suggested that the coumarin moiety serves as an effective albumin-binding ligand, potentially prolonging the blood circulation time of the radiotracer through reversible albumin association. Additionally, fluorescence titration analysis revealed that the binding constants K a of PEG2-COU and PEG6-COU to HSA were 5.41 ± 1.22 × 104 M–1 and 1.82 ± 1.19 × 104 M–1, respectively (Figure S17).

Cell Uptake Experiments

To explore the binding capacity of [68Ga]­Ga-PEGm-COUs with FAP protein, cellular uptake assays were performed using FAP-positive U87MG cells. As shown in Figure , both [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU and [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU exhibited a time-dependent increase in radioactivity accumulation, whereas the block groups showed negligible increases. For [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU, the uptake rose from 3.51 ± 0.52 IA%/106 cells at 15 min to 6.72 ± 1.94 IA%/106 cells at 120 min, while the block group remained significantly lower (2.48 ± 0.16 IA%/106 cells at 120 min, p ≤ 0.0001), confirming FAP-mediated specificity. While [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU followed a similar trend, its absolute uptake was markedly lower (4.73 ± 0.51 IA%/106 cells at 120 min). The initial uptake of the [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU variant at 15 min was 25.7% lower than that of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU. These results demonstrated that while both tracers bind specifically to FAP, shorter PEG linkers may facilitate superior cellular internalization or binding kinetics.

Small-Animal PET Imaging

Following the in vitro stability and cellular uptake studies of [68Ga]­Ga-PEGm-COUs, its in vivo diagnostic performance as a tumor-targeting probe was evaluated by PET/CT imaging in U87MG-bearing mouse models. As shown in Figure A–C, [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU exhibited prominent tumor accumulation and high contrast relative to background tissues. Distinct radioactivity was observed in the tumor, kidneys, and heart as early as 30 min postinjection (p.i.). Notably, the significant radioactive signal observed in the heart (blood pool) at 30 min (Figure A) indicated the association of the tracer with HSA, which contributed to an extended blood circulation time. Throughout the 120 min imaging period, [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU maintained a persistently high uptake at the tumor site. Quantitative region of interest (ROI) analysis revealed SUVmax values of 1.97 ± 0.06, 2.04 ± 0.05, and 1.81 ± 0.09 at 30, 60, and 120 min p.i., respectively (Figure B). The tumor-to-nontarget (T/NT) ratios, including tumor-to-liver (T/L), tumor-to-kidney (T/K), tumor-to-muscle (T/M), and tumor-to-heart (T/H), exhibited a progressive increase throughout the 120 min imaging period (Figure C), confirming the superior targeting efficiency and favorable pharmacokinetics of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU. In contrast, [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU exhibited significantly lower tumor accumulation than [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU (Figure D and Figure S11), which was consistent with the lower uptake in the cell uptake experiment, indicating unfavorable increasing PEG chain length. This suggested that increasing the PEG chain length may unfavorably impact the binding kinetics of the tracer. Regarding biodistribution, both [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU and [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU showed intense radioactive signals in the bladder, indicating a primary renal clearance pathway.

To verify the FAP-specificity of the tracers in vivo, PET/CT imaging experiments were conducted in an A549 tumor model with low FAP expression, along with competitive blocking experiments performed by coinjecting an excess of unlabeled ligands. As shown in Figure F and G, the tumor accumulation in the [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU blocking group was significantly suppressed compared to the nonblocking group, with SUVmax value of 0.99 ± 0.03 at 30 min and 0.57 ± 0.01 at 60 min p.i. (p ≤ 0.0001), respectively. This marked reduction in tracer retention across multiple time points confirms that the tumor uptake was saturable and FAP-mediated. Similarly, [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU exhibited a significantly attenuated tumor signal both after blockade and when used with the two probes in the A549 tumor model (Figure S12 and Figure S18).

To further evaluate the efficacy, the PET imaging profiles of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU were compared with those of [68Ga]­Ga-FAPI-04 (Figure H). The T/M ratio of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU increased continuously from 3.65 ± 0.18 at 30 min to 5.17 ± 0.54 at 120 min, whereas the T/M ratio of [68Ga]­Ga-FAPI-04 decreased from 4.18 ± 0.14 to 2.44 ± 0.15 during the same interval. This divergent kinetic profile underscores a time-dependent improvement in imaging contrast for the coumarin-modified probe (Figure I). Furthermore, the T/H ratio of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU also increased significantly over the observation period, reaching nearly twice that of [68Ga]­Ga-FAPI-04 by 120 min (3.42 ± 0.27 vs 1.74 ± 0.10, p ≤ 0.0001) (Figures S13–S16, Table S1). Collectively, these findings highlight the efficient tumor-specific accumulation and prolonged residence time of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU, providing crucial preclinical evidence to support its further development as a tumor-targeted imaging agent.

Ex Vivo Biodistribution and Pharmacokinetics

To quantitatively evaluate the in vivo distribution profile of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU, an ex vivo biodistribution study was performed in U87MG tumor-bearing mice (Figure A, Table S2). At 30 min postinjection, the probe exhibited high tumor uptake (17.83 ± 1.54%ID/g). Significant radioactivity was also observed in the kidneys, blood, and lungs (5.19 ± 2.35, 6.93 ± 3.01, and 6.22 ± 3.07%ID/g, respectively), while uptake in other nontarget organs such as the heart, liver, and spleen remained relatively low (3.25 ± 1.54, 2.72 ± 1.37, and 1.16 ± 0.05%ID/g, respectively). Tumor retention increased slightly by 60 min postinjection, peaking at 19.89 ± 5.54%ID/g. Concurrently, radioactivity in blood, lungs, and kidneys declined by 60 min, indicating efficient systemic clearance. Based on the biodistribution data from this study, we conducted preliminary dosimetric estimates of the potential therapeutic efficacy following labeling with therapeutic radionuclides. Using the Medical Internal Radiation Dose (MIRD) system and extrapolating the mouse data to a human model, the results showed that the absorbed dose was highest in tumor tissue, while the absorbed doses in key organs such as the kidneys and liver were moderate, suggesting that these organs may be dose-limiting. Overall, if therapeutic radionuclides such as 177Lu are labeled, this compound is expected to achieve a relatively favorable T/K dose ratio.

To assess targeting selectivity, T/NT ratios were calculated (Figure B). Prominent and increasing tumor-to-muscle (T/M) and tumor-to-heart (T/H) ratios were observed, with the T/M ratio reaching 12.96 ± 1.70 at 120 min, the T/H ratio reaching 13.76 ± 1.54 at 60 min, and the tumor-to-liver (T/L) ratio reaching 17.14 ± 4.16 at 60 min, underscoring excellent tumor specificity. In contrast, the tumor-to-kidney (T/K) ratio remained low (<5 at 30 and 120 min), consistent with the PET imaging findings and confirming renal excretion as the primary clearance pathway. These biodistribution data collectively confirmed the favorable in vivo profile of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU, characterized by high target accumulation, rapid clearance from nontarget tissues, and high tumor-to-background ratios.

To evaluate the blood clearance kinetics of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU, pharmacokinetic studies were conducted in KM mice. As shown in Figure C, the plasma concentration–time curve was fitted by a two-compartment model, yielding a distribution half-life (t 1/2α) of 2.208 min and an elimination half-life (t 1/2β) of 23.79 min. The relatively short t 1/2α indicated efficient extravasation of the probe into tissues. The moderate t 1/2β was consistent with the heart-pool radioactivity observed in the 30 min PET scans (Figure A), confirming that the albumin-binding moiety effectively sustains the tracer’s blood availability. This pharmacokinetic profile provided a sufficient window for tumor accumulation while ensuring rapid nontarget washout for high-contrast imaging. Based on the curve fit, the AUC for this probe was determined to be 2004 (μCi/mL)·min, the clearance was 0.05 mL/min, and the volume of distribution was 0.48 mL.

Discussion

Continued efforts to optimize the in vivo performance of FAP-targeted radiopharmaceuticals have highlighted the importance of albumin-binding strategies. Thus, there has been sustained interest in exploring diverse molecular approaches to fine-tune blood clearance and tumor accumulation. To this end, we designed and synthesized two novel probes, [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU and [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU, featuring a diethylamino coumarin moiety as a new albumin-binding component. Our findings demonstrated that incorporating a coumarin moiety into the FAPI scaffold successfully facilitated reversible albumin association while preserving a high FAP-binding specificity.

A critical observation during the characterization of this new platform was the influence of the molecular spacer on the biological performance. While in silico docking simulations predicted comparable binding potentials for both [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU and [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU toward HSA and FAP, in vitro cellular uptake assays revealed a distinct divergence. Specifically, [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU exhibited a marked reduction in uptake kinetics as early as 15 min compared to [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU. These in vitro differences were further amplified in vivo, where the compact conformation of PEG2 achieves a more favorable equilibrium between reversible albumin binding and the spatial accessibility of the targeting moiety. In contrast, [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU achieved markedly higher tumor accumulation and superior imaging contrast, suggesting that the PEG2 spacer provides a more favorable spatial arrangement to balance hydrophilicity and target accessibility.

The performance of [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU becomes more evident when compared to the prototypical [68Ga]­Ga-FAPI-04. The coumarin-modified probe displayed moderate blood prolongation, as evidenced by the heart-pool signals in early PET imaging, ensuring sustained tumor exposure. Crucially, while [68Ga]­Ga-FAPI-04 exhibited declining T/NT ratios (e.g., T/M and T/H) by 120 min p.i. due to rapid clearance, [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU demonstrated a continuous upward trend in these ratios over the same period. This sustained increase in contrast underscores its potential to improve imaging quality for delayed diagnostic scans. Comparative analysis with other albumin-binding moieties reveals that differences in binding affinity toward human serum albumin directly govern the in vivo behavior of the corresponding FAPI-conjugated probes. In the case of fatty acid-, Evans blue-, and 4-(p-iodophenyl)­butanoic acid-based conjugates, namely systems characterized by strong to moderate albumin-binding capacity (10–6–10–9 M), excessively tight association with albumin frequently results in prolonged circulatory retention, restricted early tumor uptake, and a pronounced temporal delay in the optimal imaging window. In stark contrast, the coumarin-based moiety, by virtue of its relatively weaker albumin interaction (10–5 M), facilitates more rapid liberation of the free probe from the protein carrier while maintaining a modest degree of circulatory persistence, thereby enabling swift tumor penetration and the attainment of elevated uptake as early as 1 h postinjection (Table S3). Collectively, these findings indicate that a moderate albumin-binding affinity is more conducive to achieving an optimal equilibrium between systemic biodistribution and tumor-targeting efficiency, and that coumarin derivatives exhibit a distinct advantage in realizing both rapid and high-contrast imaging performance.

However, as a first-in-class application of coumarin in nuclear medicine, this study has its limitations. While PEG2 was the more favorable candidate in our study, the coumarin core itself could be further refined to achieve a more pronounced circulation for therapeutic applications. Additionally, our evaluation was restricted to the U87MG model and the linear PEG linkers. Future work will extend this coumarin-based platform to therapeutic radionuclides to exploit their potential in theranostic applications.

Conclusions

In summary, we developed two novel albumin-binding FAP-targeted probes, [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU and [68Ga]­Ga-PEG6-COU, through rational linker engineering. Our results demonstrated that, while both tracers exhibit specific binding to FAP, the shorter PEG2 linker confers superior cellular uptake and higher tumor accumulation. These results not only identified [68Ga]­Ga-PEG2-COU as a promising diagnostic agent but also provided important structural insights for improving the pharmacokinetics and efficacy of albumin-binding radiopharmaceuticals.
