Phenolic profiling of peach (Prunus persica [L.] Batsch) fruit, twigs, and leaves using high-resolution UHPLC-Orbitrap-MS2
Authors: Kwan Joong Kim, Dae-Ok Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA significant proportion of by-products, such as twigs and leaves, is generated during the pre- and post-harvest processes of peaches (Prunus persica [L.] Batsch); however, their phenolic composition remains poorly understood. This study analyzed phenolic compounds in peach fruit, twigs, and leaves using UHPLC-Orbitrap-MS2 and evaluated their in vitro antioxidant capacities. A total of 63 compounds were identified, primarily flavonoids and hydroxycinnamic acids, five of which were reported for the first time in P. persica. Twigs and leaves contained 4.9- and 1.8-fold higher total phenolic contents, respectively, compared with the fruit. The most abundant phenolic compounds in the fruit, twigs, and leaves were 3-O-caffeoylquinic acid, dihydrokaempferol, and kaempferol 3-O-glucoside, respectively. Antioxidant capacities showed a similar trend, with twigs exhibiting the highest levels. These findings suggest that peach by-products represent a promising source of phenolic compounds and may support future applications in the development of functional foods and pharmaceuticals.Keywords: Orbitrap, Untargeted metabolomics, Prunose, Flavanones, Dihydrokaempferol

Introduction

Large amounts of peach by-products, including leaves, branches, kernels, and pomace, are generated during the cultivation and processing of peaches (Prunus persica [L.] Batsch). While these by-products are typically discarded or converted into low-value applications such as compost or animal feed, the associated costs of handling, processing, and disposal present a significant economic challenge for peach growers (Liu et al., 2023; Rudke et al., 2023). Utilizing peach by-products as sources of bioactive compounds offers a promising solution to these challenges, as they contain valuable nutritional components similar to those found in fruit pulp. For instance, pomace produced during peach processing is known to be rich in carotenoids and dietary fiber (Calvache et al., 2015; Han et al., 2024), while the kernel is abundant in unsaturated fatty acids, such as oleic and linoleic acids (Koprivica et al., 2022). Peach leaves are high in phenolic compounds, primarily flavonoids (El-Hawary et al., 2020; Mokrani et al., 2019). Among these by-products, peach twigs have received relatively little scientific attention compared with kernels, pomace, or leaves. For example, in traditional Chinese medicine, dried peach twigs have been used to promote blood circulation and relieve inflammatory symptoms, and recent pharmacological studies have supported these traditional uses by demonstrating the vasorelaxant and anti-inflammatory effects of peach branch extracts (Kim et al., 2019). These findings highlight the opportunity to repurpose peach twigs as high-value ingredients that can be used in nutraceutical, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic applications.

The identification of bioactive compounds in plant extracts is crucial for elucidating their physiological and pharmacological effects. Tandem mass spectrometry (MSn or MS/MS) is a powerful tool for qualitative analysis because it is less dependent on reference standards compared with other detectors, such as diode-array detector (DAD) and evaporative light scattering detector. MS/MS analysis, which provides mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) and characteristic MS fragment patterns, is widely used in food phenolic analysis, especially for identifying phenolic compounds in fruits and vegetables (Lucci et al., 2017). Recently, high-resolution MSn (HRMSn) tools, such as Orbitrap mass analyzer, with resolution exceeding 10,000 full width at half maximum (FWHM), have been increasingly used for qualitative analysis, enabling the identification of compounds that are difficult to distinguish with low-resolution MS and improving the accuracy of structural elucidation (Lucci et al., 2017).

Analytical studies of peach by-products have mostly employed high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) equipped with DAD and low-resolution MSn to identify phenolic compounds. A previous research identified 14 such compounds, including kaempferol and quercetin derivatives, in the leaves of seven peach cultivars using HPLC-DAD and MS/MS (Mokrani et al., 2019). Another study profiled 24 flavonoids in the leaves of three peach cultivars, predominantly hesperidin and luteolin 6-C-arabinosyl-8-C-glucoside, using HPLC-DAD and electrospray ionization (ESI)-MS/MS (El-Hawary et al., 2020). Research on phenolic compounds in peach twigs is limited. One metabolomic study using LC-MS identified 1096 metabolites in peach twigs, including amino acids, lipids, phenolic acids, and flavonoids (Li et al., 2024), while another study identified four flavonoids in peach twigs: prunin, persiconin, dihydrokaempferol, and naringenin (Murata et al., 2022).

The comprehensive characterization of bioactive compounds in complex plant matrices presents significant challenges for researchers in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries. Advanced analytical approaches enabling extensive and reliable identification of phenolic compounds in peach by-products are essential for their broader utilization. However, comprehensive phenolic profiling of peach by-products has rarely been reported.

The main objective of this study is to provide an expanded phenolic profile of peach twigs and leaves. Analysis using an ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC)-Orbitrap-HRMS system, which acquires target ions in selected ion monitoring mode, enables more reliable structural elucidation than low-resolution MS, making it suitable for profiling a wide range of phenolic compounds in peach by-products. Furthermore, the significance of valorizing these peach by-products was assessed through the concurrent identification and quantification of phenolic compounds in the fruit. These phenolic compounds are expected to impart health benefits to humans; therefore, the in vitro antioxidant capacity of peach fruit, twigs, and leaves was also evaluated in this study. This research aims to provide insights into peach by-products as sources of bioactive compounds for use in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries, thereby fostering the development of new high-value alternative uses.

Material and methods

Chemicals and peach samples

MS-grade acetonitrile, MS-grade water, amber vials, and vial caps were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). A 0.2-μm polytetrafluoroethylene syringe filter was purchased from Advantec MFS, Inc. (Dublin, CA, USA), and formic acid was obtained from Merck & Co., Inc. (Rahway, NJ, USA). Standard compounds were purchased from Chengdu Biopurify Phytochemicals Ltd. (Chengdu, Sichuan, China), Extrasynthese (Genay, Rhône, France), and Sigma-Aldrich Co., LLC (St. Louis, MO, USA). Detailed information about the 18 authentic standard compounds is listed in Table S1. Folin & Ciocalteu's phenol reagent, 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine (TPTZ), iron(III) chloride hexahydrate (ferric chloride; FeCl3∙6H2O), 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA), 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co., LLC (USA). Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin/streptomycin, Eagle's Minimum Essential Medium (MEM), F12 medium, Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS), and Dulbecco's Phosphate-Buffered Saline were obtained from Welgene Inc. (Gyeongsan, Republic of Korea).

Fruit, twigs, and leaves were collected at the early ripening stage, specifically corresponding to the early stage III of fruit development (Lombardo et al., 2011). At this stage, the fruit remains firm and green, with no external coloration, and is approximately 67–94 days after full bloom (Lombardo et al., 2011). The plant material used in this study was a wild-type peach tree growing naturally in an unmanaged area without artificial inputs such as fertilizers, irrigation, or pesticides.

The plants were collected from Buan-gun, Republic of Korea, in June 2016, shade-dried at room temperature for three days, and then freeze-dried. Subsequently, the samples were stored under nitrogen-flushed conditions at −80 °C until extraction in January 2025 to minimize moisture and oxygen exposure, thereby preventing oxidation and degradation of phenolic compounds.

For extraction, the dried samples were ground into a fine powder. Subsequently, 50 g of the powder was extracted with 1 L of 99.9 % methanol using an SDN-900H ultrasonic extractor (Sungdong Ultrasonic Co., Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea). Ultrasonication was applied at 40 kHz for 15 min, followed by a 120-min standing period. This cycle was repeated three times. The extracts were then filtered through qualitative filter paper (No. 100, Hyundai Micro Co., Ltd., Anseong, Republic of Korea) and concentrated under reduced pressure to obtain the dried extract. The extracts were stored at −80 °C prior to analysis.

UHPLC-Orbitrap-MS2analysis

Conditions

The extracts of peach fruit, twigs, and leaves, along with standard compounds, were separated using UHPLC (Vanquish Horizon; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and detected with an Orbitrap-MS apparatus (Exploris 120, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The UHPLC system was equipped with an autosampler, a quaternary pump, and a DAD. The UHPLC conditions were as follows: a reversed-phase C18 column (Hypersil GOLD™ Vanquish 2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.9 μm; Thermo Fisher Scientific); mobile phase A, water; mobile phase B, 0.1 % (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile; flow rate, 0.4 mL/min; injection volume, 1 μL; column temperature, 35 °C. The gradient elution was as follows: 100 % A/0 % B at 0–2 min, 80 % A/20 % B at 8 min, 0 % A/100 % B at 16–18 min, and 100 % A/0 % B at 26 min.

MS measurements were conducted in negative ESI mode, using a voltage of 3.8 kV, a vaporizer temperature at 300 °C, sheath gas (N2) at 45 arb, and auxiliary gas (N2) at 10 arb. Higher energy collision dissociation (HCD) was performed using assisted HCD at 30, 45, and 55 eV, and calculated as: normalized collision energy (%) × (precursor m/z)/500 × charge factor). MS1 data were acquired at a resolution of 120,000 (FWHM), and MS2 data were obtained using top 3 data-dependent acquisition (TOP3-DDA) at a resolution of 15,000 (FWHM).

Data processing and qualitative analysis

Raw data were obtained using UHPLC-Orbitrap-MS2, and features of peach extracts were extracted using Compound Discoverer (version 3.1; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Key features, including m/z value, retention time (RT), and peak area, were converted to CSV file format. Features with low intensity (<100,000) and false positives that were indistinguishable from blank input were removed using the Compound Discoverer (version 3.1; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Nineteen phenolic compounds from the fruit, twigs, and leaves of peaches were identified using standard compounds. Forty-six phenolic compounds, without available standards, were identified at two identification levels (ILs). Phenolic compounds at IL 1 were identified using previous references, including RT and MS/MS fragment patterns. Specific structural information, such as the positions of the hydroxyl groups, methoxy groups, and glycosidic bonds, was presented for phenolic compounds identified at IL 1. Phenolic compounds at IL 2 were identified based on MS/MS fragment patterns (e.g., retro-Diels-Alder [RDA] fragments of flavonoids) and were tentatively annotated due to ambiguities in the positions of the hydroxyl groups, methoxy groups, and glycosidic bonds. All MS/MS fragment patterns used for structural elucidation had mass errors of less than 5 ppm. The nomenclature system used for the fragmentation of flavonoid glycosides follows that of a previous study (Domon & Costello, 1988).

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative analysis of phenolic compounds in peach fruit, twigs, and leaves was performed using reference standards or surrogate compounds. Phenolic compounds for which standards were not available were quantified using the following surrogate standard compounds: caffeic acid for hydroxybenzoic acid derivatives; trans-5-O-caffeoylquinic acid for hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives; epicatechin for flavonoid aglycones; quercetin 3-O-glucoside (isoquercitrin) for flavonoid monoglycosides; and rutin for flavonoid diglycosides. Quantification was conducted using FreeStyle software (version 1.8; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Analytical validation of the flavonoid standards was performed using linearity, coefficient of determination (R2), limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantification (LOQ), intra- and inter-day precision, and recovery.

In vitro antioxidant capacity assays

The antioxidant capacities of extracts of peach fruit, twigs, and leaves were determined using ABTS and DPPH radical-scavenging assays and expressed in mg vitamin C equivalents (VCE)/g (Kim & Lee, 2004). Briefly, for the ABTS radical assay, the ABTS radical in phosphate-buffered saline was adjusted to an absorbance of 0.650 ± 0.020 at 734 nm using a spectrophotometer (SPECTRONIC 200; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). The reaction between ABTS radicals and the 200 mg/L samples was carried out at 37 °C for 10 min, after which the decrease in absorbance was measured at 734 nm using the same spectrophotometer.

For the DPPH radical assay, the absorbance of DPPH radicals in 80 % (v/v) aqueous methanol was adjusted to 0.650 ± 0.020 at 517 nm using the same spectrophotometer. The DPPH radicals were allowed to react with 200 mg/L samples at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance of the resulting solution was monitored at 517 nm using the same spectrophotometer.

For the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay, the FRAP reagent was prepared by mixing 300 mM sodium acetate buffer solution (pH 3.6), 10 mM TPTZ in 40 mM HCl, and 20 mM FeCl3·6H2O in a ratio of 10:1:1. The reaction between 950 μL of the FRAP reagent and 50 μL of samples (200 mg/L) was carried out at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance of the resulting solution was then measured at 593 nm using the same spectrophotometer.

Intracellular oxidative stress level assay

The effects of peach fruit, twig, and leaf extracts on intracellular oxidative stress were measured using the rat pheochromocytoma PC-12 cell line (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and the human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cell line (KCLB, Seoul, Republic of Korea). PC-12 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10 % (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin. SH-SY5Y cells were cultured in MEM-F12 medium (1:1) containing 10 % (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. These cell lines were incubated in a BB 15 CO2 humidified incubator (Thermo Electron LED GmbH, Langenselbold, Germany) with 5 % CO2 at 37 °C.

An MTT assay was used to determine the maximum non-toxic concentration of the peach extracts. Briefly, PC-12 (5 × 104 cells/well) and SH-SY5Y (5 × 105 cells/well) cells were seeded in 96-well plates and pre-cultured in an FBS-containing medium for 24 h. They were then treated with an FBS-free medium containing 12.5–200 mg/L of the peach extract samples. After 24 h, the PC-12 and SH-SY5Y cells were treated with 50 μM H2O2 in HBSS for 1 h, followed by treatment with MTT at 0.5 mg/mL for 3 h. The resulting MTT formazan crystals were dissolved in 50 μL of DMSO and analyzed using an Epoch microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) at 570 nm. The maximum non-toxic concentration of peach fruit, twigs, and leaves was determined to be 50 mg/L, which maintained cell viability above 90 % compared with the control group.

The levels of intracellular oxidative stress were evaluated using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. PC-12 (5 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates) and SH-SY5Y (5 × 105 cells/well in 96-well plates) cells were pre-cultured for 24 h. After treatment with 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/L samples for 24 h, both cell lines were incubated with 50 μM DCFH-DA in HBSS for 1 h. The PC-12 and SH-SY5Y cells were then treated with 50 μM H2O2 in HBSS for 1 h. Fluorescence was measured using the Epoch microplate spectrophotometer at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 535 nm. The intracellular oxidative stress level was expressed as the percentage decrease in fluorescence intensity of treated cells compared with the control cells (100 %), which were not stressed or treated with samples.

Statistical analysis

All analyses, including UHPLC-MS2 analysis, phenolic quantification, antioxidant capacity assay, and intracellular oxidative stress level assay, were carried out in triplicate. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis of phenolic quantification, antioxidant capacity, and intracellular oxidative stress level was performed using the SAS software package (version 8.1; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Qualitative analysis of phenolic compounds in peach fruit, twigs, and leaves

A total of 63 compounds including 18 hydroxycinnamic acids, 1 hydroxybenzoic acid, 15 flavones, 15 flavanones, 4 flavans, 3 condensed tannins, and 7 benzoic acid glycosides were identified in fruit, twigs, and leaves of peaches using UHPLC-Orbitrap-MS2 analysis (Table 1 and Fig. 1). Five phenolic compounds from all parts of peaches were reported for the first time in this study: quercetin 3-O-(coumaroyl)hexoside (peak 47), kaempferol 3-O-(coumaroyl)hexoside (peak 50), prunose II (peak 54), prunose I (peak 60), and hexa-O-acetyl-3-O-p-coumaroylsucrose (peak 62).

Hydroxycinnamic and hydroxybenzoic acids

Four hydroxycinnamic acids (peak 13, caffeic acid; peak 14, hydroxycinnamic acid; peak 31, ferulic acid; and peak 44, ferulic acid isomer), three acetylated coumaroylsucrose (peak 54, prunose II; peak 60, prunose I; and peak 62, hexa-O-acetyl-3-O-p-coumaroylsucrose), eleven hydroxycinnamoylquinic acids (peak 4, trans-3-O-caffeoylquinic acid; peak 7, 3-coumaroylquinic acid; peak 9, trans-5-O-caffeoylquinic acid; peak 11, trans-4-O-caffeoylquinic acid; peak 12, 3-O-feruloylquinic acid; peak 18, 5-O-feruloylquinic acid; peak 20, cis-5-O-caffeoylquinic acid; peak 22, 5-O-coumaroylquinic acid; peak 24, 4-O-feruloylquinic acid; peak 26, coumaroylquinic acid; and peak 29, feruloylquinic acid), and a hydroxybenzoic acid (peak 1; protocatechuic acid) were identified in peaches in this study (Table 1).

Caffeic acid (peak 13) and ferulic acid (peak 31) were identified using authentic standards. Caffeic acid showed an MS2 fragment at m/z 135.0450, indicating the loss of CO2. Hydroxycinnamic acid (peak 14) also produced an MS2 fragment at m/z 119.0503 by the loss of CO2, but the position of the hydroxyl group on the phenol ring was not determined. Both ferulic acid (peak 31) and its isomer (peak 44) yielded MS2 fragments at m/z 178.0274 ([M-CH3]−) and 134.0373 ([M-formate-CH3]−). However, ferulic acid and its isomer exhibited distinct fragments at m/z 149.0605 ([M-formate-H]−) and 161.0244 ([M-CH3-OH]−), respectively. Caffeic acid and ferulic acid have been previously identified in peaches, and hydroxycinnamic acid was also tentatively identified based on similar MS/MS fragments (m/z 91 and 119) (Koprivica et al., 2018).

Three acetylated coumaroylsucroses (peak 54, prunose II; peak 60, prunose I; and peak 62, hexa-O-acetyl-3-O-p-coumaroylsucrose) were identified in all parts of P. persica, and these compounds are reported here for the first time in peaches (Table 1). Prunose II, prunose I, and hexa-O-acetyl-3-O-p-coumaroylsucrose exhibited different m/z values of 655.1877, 697.1980, and 739.2087, respectively, due to differences in the number of the acetyl groups (42.0106 Da). The MS2 fragments of the three acetylated coumaroylsucroses were observed at m/z 365.1090 (acetylsucrose moiety), 407.1181 (diacetylsucrose moiety), and 449.1302 (triacetylsucrose moiety), respectively. All the acetylated coumaroylsucroses gave a predominant MS2 fragment at m/z 145.0294 ([M-coumaric acid-H]−), along with a fragment at m/z 117.0345, suggesting a further loss of CO. Acetylated coumaroylsucroses such as mumeose and prunose have been previously found in Prunus mume (Liu et al., 2022), and their characteristic MS2 fragments are similar to those observed in this study (Table 1).

Caffeoylquinic acids, namely chlorogenic acid (trans-5-O-caffeoylquinic acid), cryptochlorogenic acid (trans-4-O-caffeoylquinic acid), and neochlorogenic acid (trans-3-O-caffeoylquinic acid), are reported as major phenolic compounds in peaches (Andreotti et al., 2008; Loizzo et al., 2015; Nowicka et al., 2023). In this study, four caffeoylquinic acids (peaks 4, 9, 11, and 20) were identified, among which trans-3-O-caffeoylquinic acid (peak 4), trans-4-O-caffeoylquinic acid (peak 11), and trans-5-O-caffeoylquinic acid (peak 9) were confirmed by comparison with authentic standards (Table 1). trans-3-O-Caffeoylquinic acid showed predominant MS2 fragments at m/z 191.0560 (loss of caffeic acid), 135.0453 (caffeic acid moiety-formate), and 179.0347 (loss of quinic acid), while trans-4-O-caffeoylquinic acid exhibited similar predominant MS2 fragments at m/z 191.0560, 135.0453, and 179.0347. However, trans-5-O-caffeoylquinic acid gave a predominant fragment only at m/z 191.0560. The MS2 fragment of cis- and trans-caffeoylquinic acids, including 3-O-, 4-O-, and 5-O-caffeoylquinic acids, are likely identical (Xie et al., 2011). Peak 20, which showed MS2 patterns similar to trans-5-O-caffeoylquinic acid, was therefore tentatively assigned to cis-5-O-caffeoylquinic acid (Table 1).

Flavonoids

Flavones

Fifteen flavones including two flavone aglycones (peak 52, quercetin; and peak 58, kaempferol), five flavone monoglycosides (peak 32, quercetin 3-O-galactoside; peak 33, quercetin 3-O-glucoside; peak 37, kaempferol 3-O-galactoside; peak 39, kaempferol 3-O-glucoside; and peak 40, kaempferol 7-O-glucoside), three flavone acetylglycosides (peak 45, kaempferol 3-O-(6′′-O-acetyl)galactoside; peak 48, kaempferol 3-O-(6′′-O-acetyl)glucoside; and peak 49; kaempferol O-acetylrutinoside), three flavone diglycosides (peak 30, quercetin 3-O-rutinoside; peak 36, kaempferol 3-O-glucosyl-7-O-rhamnoside; and peak 42, kaempferol 7-O-rutinoside), and two flavone coumaroylglycosides (peak 47, quercetin 3-O-(coumaroyl)hexoside; and peak 50, kaempferol 3-O-(coumaroyl)hexoside) were identified in fruit, twigs, and leaves of peaches (Table 1).

Two flavone aglycones, quercetin (peak 52) and kaempferol (peak 58), were identified using their authentic standards. Quercetin showed predominant MS2 fragments at m/z 151.0036 ([1,3 A]− RDA fragment), 178.9986 ([1,2 A]− RDA fragment), and 121.0295 ([1,2 B]− RDA fragment). Kaempferol exhibited MS2 fragments at m/z values of 257.0454 ([M-CO-H]−), 229.0506 ([M-2CO-H]−), 185.0609 ([M-3CO-OH-H]−), 169.0659 ([M-2CO-OH-C2H2O-H]−), and 151.0036 ([1,3 A]− RDA fragment) (Table 1). These characteristic fragments were consistent with those reported previously (Fu et al., 2020; Koprivica et al., 2018).

Quercetin 3-O-galactoside (peak 32) and quercetin 3-O-glucoside (peak 33) were identified using their standard compounds. Both quercetin monoglycosides gave MS2 fragments at m/z values of 301.0352 ([M-hexose (Hx)-H]−) as aglycone (Y0−), 300.0273 ([M-Hx-2H]−) as deprotonated radical aglycone ([Y0−-H]•−), 271.0247 ([Y0-CO-2H]−), 255.0298 ([Y0-2CO-2H]−), and 151.0036 ([1,3 A]− RDA fragment) with similar intensity (Table 1). Peaks 37, 39, and 40 were tentatively associated with kaempferol glycosides based on their MS2 fragment patterns, which were similar to that of kaempferol (peak 58). All the kaempferol glycosides (peaks 37, 39, and 40) gave MS2 fragments at m/z 285.0404 (Y0−), 284.0324 ([Y0−-H]•−), and 151.0039 ([1,3 A]− RDA fragment). Peaks 37 and 39 exhibited the most abundant fragment at m/z 284.0324, whereas peak 40 showed the most abundant fragment at m/z 285.0403. The ratio of [Y0−-H]•−/Y0− can be used as a diagnostic criterion to distinguish 3-O-flavonol from 7-O-flavonol: if [Y0−-H]•-/Y0− > 1, it indicates 3-O-flavonol, whereas if [Y0−-H]•−/Y0− < 1, it indicates 7-O-flavonol (Qin et al., 2017). Peaks 37 and 39 were therefore assigned to 3-O-flavone, while peak 40 was indicative of 7-O-flavone. Flavone galactoside reportedly elutes earlier than its glucoside (Nowicka et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023), and 3-O-flavone is most likely to elute earlier than 7-O-flavone (Mokrani et al., 2019; Qin et al., 2017). Taken together, peaks 37, 39, and 40 were identified as kaempferol 3-O-galactoside, kaempferol 3-O-glucoside, and kaempferol 7-O-glucoside, respectively (Table 1).

The acetylated glycosides of flavones, including kaempferol, isorhamnetin, and quercetin in Prunus species have been reported previously (Liu et al., 2024; Mokrani et al., 2019). In our study, three kaempferol acetylglycosides were tentatively identified (Table 1). Peaks 45 and 48, with an m/z of 489.1034, showed the MS2 fragment at m/z 285.0402 ([M-acetylHx-H]−), and peak 51, with an m/z of 635.1613, also exhibited an MS2 fragment at m/z 285.0402 ([M-acetylrutinose-H]−). All flavone acetylated glycosides (peaks 45, 48, and 49) showed MS2 fragment patterns similar to those of kaempferol, with m/z values of 285.0402 (Y0−), 284.0324 ([Y0-H]•−), 255.0306 ([Y0-CO-2H]•−), 257.0454 ([Y0-CO]−), 163.0037 ([Y0-B ring-CO-2H]•−), and 151.0033 ([1,3 A]− RDA fragment of [Y0]−). Based on the [Y0−-H]•−/Y0− ratio, peaks 45 and 48 were assigned to 3-O-acetylglycosides. Peaks 45, 48, and 49 were therefore tentatively associated with kaempferol 3-O-(6′′-O-acetyl)galactoside, kaempferol 3-O-(6′′-O-acetyl)glucoside, and kaempferol O-acetyl-deoxyhexosyl-hexoside, respectively (Table 1).

Quercetin 3-O-rutinoside (peak 30) showed MS2 fragments at m/z values of 301.0352 (Y0−), 300.0273 ([Y0--H]•−), 271.0247 ([Y0-CO-2H]•−), 255.0298 ([Y0-CO2-2H]•−), and 151.0036 ([1,3 A]− RDA fragment of [Y0]−) (Table 1). Two flavone diglycosides, corresponding to peaks 36 and 42, exhibited the same molecular ion at m/z 593.1508. MS2 fragments of peak 36 at m/z 447.0951 ([M-rhamnoside-H]−), 431.0983 ([M-glucoside-H]−), and 430.0903 ([M-glucoside-2H]•−) were found together with Y0− (m/z 285.0403) and [Y0-H]•− (m/z 284.0326), which was consistent with a previous report (Qin et al., 2017). However, peak 42 yielded only the MS2 fragments of the aglycone (i.e., kaempferol), a fragmentation pattern that had been previously reported for several flavone O-rutinosides (Mokrani et al., 2019; Qin et al., 2017). Peaks 36 and 42 were therefore assigned to kaempferol 3-O-glucosyl-7-O-rhamnoside and kaempferol 7-O-rutinoside, respectively (Table 1).

Some flavonoid O-coumaroylglycosides, such as quercetin-3-O-(6′′-O-coumaroyl)glucoside, kaempferol 3-O-(2′′-O-coumaroyl)arabinofuranoside, and malvidin 3-coumaroylglucoside-acetaldehyde, have been reported to occur in the fruits and flowers of Prunus species (Aubert & Chalot, 2020; Fu et al., 2020; Olszewska & Wolbis, 2001). In this study, two flavone O-coumaroylglycosides (peaks 47 and 50) were detected for the first time in twigs and leaves of peaches (Table 1). MS2 analysis revealed the loss of a coumaroyl moiety, resulting in m/z values of 463.0948 for peak 47 and 447.0933 for peak 50. Additionally, peaks 47 and 50 exhibited MS2 fragments characteristic of quercetin and kaempferol, respectively, and showed [Y0−-H]•−/Y0− ratios greater than 1 (Table 1). Therefore, peaks 47 and 50 were tentatively assigned to quercetin 3-O-(coumaroyl)hexoside and kaempferol 3-O-(coumaroyl)hexoside, respectively (Table 1).

Flavanones

Fifteen flavanones, six flavanone aglycones (peak 34, taxifolin; peak 43, dihydrokaempferol; peak 51, eriodictyol; peak 57, naringenin; peak 59, hesperetin; and peak 63, pinocembrin), three methoxyflavanone monoglycosides (peak 28, methoxy tetrahydroxyflavanone O-hexoside; peak 46, dimethoxy dihydroxyflavanone O-hexoside; and peak 56, dimethoxy dihydroxyflavanone O-hexoside), five flavanone monoglycosides (peak 16, dihydrokaempferol O-hexoside; peak 35, eriodictyol 7-O-glucoside; peak 41, naringenin 7-O-glucoside; peak 53, pinocembrin 7-O-glucoside; and peak 55, pinocembrin 5-O-glucoside), and a benzoylglycoside (peak 61, naringenin 7-O-(benzoyl)hexoside) were identified in this study (Table 1).

Flavanone glycosides, including taxifolin, dihydrokaempferol, eriodictyol, naringenin, hesperetin, and pinocembrin, have been previously reported in peaches, and all of these flavanone aglycones were also observed in the present results (Koprivica et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2023). In the MS2 analysis, all flavanone aglycones exhibited 1,3 A− (m/z 151.0037 for two -OH on the A ring) and 1,3 B− (m/z 135.0449 for two -OH on the B ring or one -OH each on the C and B rings) RDA fragments (Fig. 2). The phloroglucinol unit (m/z 125.0245) and further loss of H2O (m/z 107.0137, corresponding to [0,4 A]−) were also frequently identified as MS2 fragments in flavanone aglycones. Taxifolin (peak 34) and naringenin (peak 57) were identified using their authentic standards. In the MS2 analysis of taxifolin (peak 34), fragments at m/z values of 285.0404 ([M-H2O-H]−), 241.0508 ([M-H2O-CO2]−), 151.0401 ([1,3 B]− RDA fragment), and 125.0244 (phloroglucinol unit) were observed as intense peaks. Naringenin (peak 57) showed predominant MS2 fragments at m/z 151.0037 ([1,3 A]− RDA fragment) and 119.0502 ([1,3 B]− RDA fragment). Peak 43, with m/z 287.0558 ([M-H]−), gave diagnostic MS2 fragments at m/z 151.0037 ([1,3 A]− RDA fragment), 135.0449 ([1,3 B]− RDA fragment), and 93.0348 (B ring), indicating two -OH on the A ring, one -OH at the C3 substitution of the C ring, and one -OH on the B ring (Fig. 2a). Peak 51 also has [M-H]− at m/z 287.0559 but showed distinct MS2 patterns compared with peak 43 (dihydrokaempferol). The most intense fragment of peak 51 was m/z 135.0451 ([1,3 B]− RDA fragment), suggesting either two -OH on the B ring or one -OH at C3 of the C ring and one -OH on the B ring (Fig. 2b). However, the presence of m/z 165.0192 ([1,2 A]− RDA fragment) in peak 51 indicates the presence of two -OH on the B ring. Peak 59, with m/z 301.0714 ([M-H]−), produced fragments indicating the loss of a methyl radical (m/z 286.0485, [M-CH3-H]•−), which is typical of MS2 fragments from methoxylated flavonoids. Additionally, the diagnostic fragments (m/z 152.0115 [1,3 A−] and 125.0244 [phloroglucinol unit]) indicate two -OH on the A ring, and the relative RT of the precursor ion was also consistent with those of hesperetin reported in a previous study (Koprivica et al., 2018). Peak 63 had m/z 255.0659 ([M-H]−), and its diagnostic MS2 fragments were observed at m/z 151.0036 (1,3 A−) and 145.0657 (possibly [M-A ring-CO-H]−) (Fig. 2c), which are consistent with the findings of a previous study (Koprivica et al., 2018). Peaks 43, 51, 59, and 63 were therefore assigned to dihydrokaempferol, eriodictyol, hesperetin, and pinocembrin, respectively (Table 1).

Representative structure elucidation of identified phenolic compounds in the fruit, twigs, and leaves of peaches (Prunus persica [L.] Batsch). Dihydrokaempferol (a), eriodictyol (b), pinocembrin (c), dimethoxy dihydroxyflavanone O-hexoside (d), and 4′-O-methylcatechin O-hexoside (e).

Three methoxylated flavanone glycosides (peaks 28, 46, and 56) produced [M-CH3-H]•−, an MS2 fragment typical of methoxylated flavonoid glycosides, with respective m/z values of 302.0431, 300.0637, and 300.0635 (Table 1). Peak 28, with an m/z of 479.1190 ([M-H]−), produced the diagnostic MS2 fragments at m/z 165.0558 (1,3 B−), 165.0194 (1,2 A−), and 151.0037 (1,3 A−), suggesting the presence of two -OH along with -OCH3 on the A ring, as well as two -OH on both the B and C rings (or only in the B ring). Peaks 46 and 56 displayed similar MS2 patterns, suggesting that their aglycone ions are likely to be identical (Table 1 and Fig. 2d). The diagnostic fragments for peaks 46 and 56 were m/z 165.0193 (1,2 A−) and 149.0605 (1,2 B−), indicating two -OH on the A ring and two -OCH3 on both B and C rings. However, because no [Y0−-H]•− was observed in any of the methoxy flavanone glycosides, the position of the glycosidic bond could not be determined. Peaks 28, 46, and 56 were therefore tentatively assigned to methoxy tetrahydroxyflavanone O-hexoside, dimethoxy dihydroxyflavanone O-hexoside, and dimethoxy dihydroxyflavanone O-hexoside, respectively (Table 1).

Five flavanone monoglycosides and a benzoylflavanone glycoside were tentatively identified based on fragments of their aglycone ions (Table 1) and information reported in previous studies, including RTs and MS2 fragment patterns (Koprivica et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2023). Peak 16, with m/z 449.1086 ([M-H]−), exhibited MS2 fragment patterns (m/z 259.0610, 243.0659, 151.0037, 125.0245, and 107.0141) similar to that of dihydrokaempferol. While Y0− (m/z 287.0562) was observed for peak 16, [Y0−-H]•− was not detected, so the exact glycosidic bond position of peak 16 could not be determined. Peak 35 showed a similar MS2 fragment pattern (m/z 151.0037, 135.0452, and 107.0139) to that of eriodictyol (peak 51) and a [Y0−-H]•−/Y0− ratio of lower than 1, suggesting a glucose moiety at the C7 position on the A ring. MS2 analysis revealed that peak 41 had a fragment pattern (m/z 165.0197, 151.0037, and 119.0503) similar to that of naringenin (peak 57). Peak 41 showed only the Y0− (m/z 271.0611); however, a previous report indicates that naringenin 7-O-glucoside (prunin) does not exhibit the [Y0−-H]•− (Zhang et al., 2023). Both isomeric dihydroxyflavanones (peaks 53 and 55) showed similar MS2 fragment patterns: m/z values of 151.0036 (1,3 A−), 145.0655 ([M-A ring-CO-H]−), and 107.0138, which likely indicates the presence of two -OH on the A ring. Additionally, peak 53 exhibited a [Y0−-H]•−/[Y0−]− ratio of lower than 1, suggesting that it is a 7-O-glucoside. In contrast, [Y0−-H]•− was not observed in the MS2 spectrum of peak 55. Because pinocembrin lacks an -OH at the C3 position and no galactosides of pinocembrin have been reported, peak 55 was tentatively assigned to pinocembrin 5-O-glucoside. Peak 61, with an m/z value of 537.1400 ([M-H]−), showed intense MS2 fragments at m/z values of 271.0610 (Y0−; loss of benzoylhexoside), 151.0037, and 119.0502, which were similar to those of naringenin 7-O-glucoside (43).

Peaks 16, 35, 41, 53, 55, and 61 were therefore identified to dihydrokaempferol O-hexoside, eriodictyol 7-O-glucoside, naringenin 7-O-glucoside, pinocembrin 7-O-glucoside, pinocembrin 5-O-glucoside, and naringenin 7-O-(benzoyl)hexoside, respectively (Table 1).

Flavans

Four flavans were identified in peaches, including two flavan-3-ols (peak 8, catechin; and peak 19, epicatechin), a methylflavan (peak 38; 4′-O-methylcatechin), and a flavan methyl monoglycoside (peak 27; 4′-O-methylcatechin O-hexoside) (Table 1).

Peaches have been reported to contain catechin and epicatechin (Zhang et al., 2023). In our study, the two isomeric flavan-3-ols, catechin and epicatechin, were detected and identified using their authentic standards. The predominant MS2 fragmentation pattern of flavan-3-ols was observed at m/z values of 245.0820 (loss of CO2), 203.0715 ([M-C3H2O2-H]−), 179.0352 (loss of B ring), 151.0401 (1,3 B−), 125.0244 (phloroglucinol unit), and 109.0295 (B ring) (Table 1). Peak 38, with m/z 303.0875 ([M-H]−), showed predominant MS2 fragments at m/z 179.0350 (loss of B ring), 164.0116 (-CO-B ring), 151.0401 (1,3 B−), 123.0452 (B ring containing one -OH and one -OCH3), and 109.0295 (B ring), which were highly similar to those of catechin and epicatechin. The flavan methylmonoglycoside (peak 27) showed the same fragment pattern as peak 38, with m/z values of 179.0350, 164.0116, 151.0401, 139.0401, 123.0452, and 109.0295. The intense fragment patterns of peaks 27 and 38 suggest the presence of two -OH on the A ring, one -OH at C3 position on the C ring, and one -OH and one -OCH3 on the B ring (Table 1 and Fig. 2e). Peaks 27 and 38 were therefore tentatively assigned to 4′-O-methylcatechin O-hexoside and 4′-O-methylcatechin, respectively (Table 1). In a previous study, 4′-O-methylcatechin was first identified in peach fruit using nuclear magnetic resonance (Nakagawa et al., 2018). To the best of our knowledge, no previous research has reported the presence of methylcatechin glycosides in peaches (Table 1).

Condensed tannins

Several procyanidins, including dimers, trimers, and tetramers, have been reported from peach germplasm (Zhang et al., 2023). Peaks 3, 6, and 25 showed fragments typical of dimeric procyanidins: m/z 451.1020 (heterocyclic ring fission), 407.0769 (RDA fragmentation), and 289.0708 ([epi]catechin fragmentation) (Table 1). Furthermore, peaks 3 and 6 were identified using our in-house library and were also confirmed by a previous study (Kim et al., 2022). Peaks 3, 6, and 25 were therefore assigned to procyanidin B1, procyanidin B3, and procyanidin B dimer, respectively (Table 1).

Benzoic acid glycosides and others

In this study, two cyanogenic glycoside derivatives (peak 2, prunasin amide; and peak 5, prunasin acid), two cyanogenic glycosides (peak 15, amygdalin; and peak 23, prunasin), and three benzoic acid glycosides (peak 10, benzoic acid dihexoside; peak 17, benzoic acid hexoside; and peak 21, benzoic acid pentosylhexoside) were identified from the fruit, twigs, and leaves of peaches (Table 1).

Cyanogenic glycosides (peaks 15 and 23) and their derivatives (peaks 2 and 5) were detected in peach fruit, twigs, and leaves in this study. All these four peaks had fragments typical of glucose, with m/z values of 161.0455, 113.0243, 101.0244, and 71.0139, but their aglycone ions were not observed in the MS2 analysis (Table 1). All benzoic acid glycosides (peaks 10, 17, and 21) with m/z values of 445.1347, 283.0821, and 415.1245, respectively, produced a single MS2 fragment m/z 121.0295, which corresponds to a benzoic acid moiety. These benzoic acid glycosides (peaks 10, 17, and 21) were observed in peaches. Amygdalin (peak 15) and prunasin (peak 23) have been widely detected in Prunus species, and prunasin amide (peak 2), prunasin acid (peak 5), and benzoic acid glycosides have also been reported (Del Cueto et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2022). Their RTs were consistent with those reported previously in the order: prunasin amide (peak 2) < prunasin acid (peak 5) < amygdalin (peak 15) < benzoic acid hexoside (peak 17) < prunasin (peak 23) (Del Cueto et al., 2017). Cyanogenic glycosides, such as amygdalin and prunasin, can be converted into non-cyanogenic compounds, including prunasin acid and prunasin amide, through turnover that depends on the developmental stage of buds of Prunus species (Del Cueto et al., 2017). Peaks 2, 5, 10, 15, 17, 21, and 23 were therefore tentatively assigned to prunasin amide, prunasin acid, benzoic acid dihexoside, amygdalin, benzoic acid hexoside, benzoic acid pentosylhexoside, and prunasin, respectively (Table 1).

Quantitative analysis of phenolic compounds in peach fruit, twigs, and leaves

To guarantee reproducible results, analytical validation, including linearity, sensitivity, precision, and accuracy, was performed for standard compounds used for quantitative analysis (Table 2). The validation results showed linearity (R2 > 0.99), sensitivity (0.001 ≤ LOD ≤ 0.011 μg/g and 0.003 ≤ LOQ ≤ 0.037 μg/g), precision (expressed as % RSD; intraday, ≤2.53 %; interday, ≤4.14 %), and recovery (93.25 % to 109.78 % across three concentrations).

Predominant phenolic compounds in peach fruit, trans-3-O-caffeoylquinic acid (peak 4; neochlorogenic acid) and trans-5-O-caffeoylquinic acid (peak 9; chlorogenic acid), were observed at concentrations of 141.07 and 91.49 mg/100 g DW, respectively. It was previously reported that in 13 cultivars of peach fruit, trans-3-O-caffeoylquinic acid and trans-5-O-caffeoylquinic acid were the major phenolic compounds, with concentrations of 97 and 80 mg/100 g DW, respectively, in the “Silver Rome” cultivar (Andreotti et al., 2008). Additionally, prunasin amide (peak 2) was present at a concentration of 101.96 mg/g DW (Table 3), likely resulting from the conversion of cyanogenic compounds under certain metabolic conditions, such as flowering (Del Cueto et al., 2017).

The total flavonoid concentration of peach leaves was 493.43 mg/100 g DW, with kaempferol 3-O-glucoside (peak 39) being the most abundant flavonoid (Table 3). Previously, peach leaves extracted using 50 % aqueous ethanol were reported to contain a total of 430.65 mg/100 g DW of phenolic compounds, of which flavone glycosides accounted for 347.65 mg/100 g DW (approximately 80.7 %), with kaempferol 3-O-glucoside being the dominant glycoside (Upyr et al., 2019). Similarly, flavone derivatives (450.72 mg/100 g DW) were the predominant phenolic group in peach leaves in our study, representing approximately 56.4 % of the total phenolic compounds, including quercetin and kaempferol glycosides (Table 3). Phenolic compounds in peach leaves, such as caffeoylquinic acids, flavan-3-ols, and flavones, have been reported at concentrations ranging 642.75–763.64 mg/100 g DW across various peach cultivars, with quercetin 3-O-galactoside being the most abundant compound at 70.79–232.16 mg/100 g DW (Maatallah et al., 2020). However, in our study (Table 3), a lower concentration (59.31 mg/100 g DW) of quercetin 3-O-galactoside (peak 32) was observed in the leaves, possibly due to differences among cultivars. Caffeoylquinic acids (peaks 4, 9, 11, and 20) were identified as one of the major groups of phenolic compounds, with a total concentration of 156.16 mg/100 g DW, contributing approximately 19.5 % to the overall phenolic concentration of peach leaves.

Major phenolic compounds identified in peach leaves are associated with a diverse range of biological activities. Kaempferol 3-O-glucoside has been demonstrated to possess antioxidant and antimicrobial properties (Taiwo et al., 2019). Quercetin-derived glycosides, including quercetin 3-O-glucoside, quercetin 3-O-galactoside, and quercetin 3-O-rutinoside, have several health benefits, such as anti-inflammatory and anti-atherosclerotic activities, attributed to their potent antioxidant capacities (Terao, 2023). trans-5-O-Caffeoylquinic acid, another prominent phenolic compound in peach leaves, has been linked to anti-inflammatory activity by modulating the secretion of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine (Wu et al., 2024). Collectively, the abundance of these bioactive phenolics highlights the potential of peach leaves as a promising source for nutraceutical and pharmaceutical applications.

Among the three parts tested in this study, peach twigs had the highest content of phenolic compounds (2102.58 mg/100 g DW), with dihydrokaempferol (peak 43; 349.97 mg/100 g DW) as the major compound (Table 3). The major class of phenolic compounds in the twigs was flavanone aglycones (875.81 mg/100 g DW), including dihydrokaempferol (peak 43), naringenin (peak 57), hesperetin (peak 60), and pinocembrin (peak 63) (Table 3). Other predominant phenolic compound class found in the twigs were flavanone glycosides (356.26 mg/100 g DW), such as dimethoxy dihydroxyflavanone O-hexoside (peak 16) and naringenin 7-O-glucoside (peak 41), hydroxycinnamic acids (306 mg/100 g DW), such as prunose I (peak 54) and prunose II (peak 60), and flavans (222.71 mg/100 g DW), including catechin (Table 3).

Consistent with our findings, peach twigs reportedly contain substantial levels of flavanones (510 mg/100 g DW), including naringenin 7-O-glucoside, persiconin, dihydrokaempferol, and naringenin (Murata et al., 2022). When excluding persiconin, the total amount of the remaining three flavanones in this study was 688 mg/100 g DW (Table 3), which was 1.6-fold greater than that reported previously (430 mg/100 g DW) (Murata et al., 2022). Furthermore, the total flavanone content identified in our study was approximately three times what was previously reported, largely due to the additional detection of flavanones such as hesperetin and pinocembrin.

Flavanones, such as dihydrokaempferol, naringenin, hesperetin, and pinocembrin, offer numerous health benefits, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-hypertensive, and anti-cancer effects (Khan et al., 2014). Additionally, peach twigs rich in flavanones such as naringenin 7-O-glucoside, persiconin, dihydrokaempferol, and naringenin have demonstrated an ability to inhibit tyrosinase, suggesting potential skin-whitening applications (Murata et al., 2022).

The average concentration of the three hydroxycinnamic acids (acetylated coumaroylsucroses; peaks 54, 60, and 62) in the twigs was 20.6- and 33.4-fold higher than in the fruit and leaves, respectively. Two acetylated coumaroylsucroses, mumeose and prunose, have previously been identified in P. mume (commonly known as Chinese or Japanese plums) (Liu et al., 2022), but these compounds have not yet been reported in peaches. To the best of our knowledge, the acetylated coumaroylsucroses (peaks 54, 60, and 62) were reported here for the first time in the fruit, twigs, and leaves of peaches.

Acetylated p-coumaroylsucrose derivatives, often referred to as prunose or mumeose, reportedly exhibit diverse bioactivities, including antioxidant, anti-diabetic, and anti-platelet effects. Prunose I has demonstrated aldose reductase inhibitory and anti-platelet aggregation activities, supporting its potential use as a bioactive agent (Yoshikawa et al., 2002). Prunose II also has shown significant anti-platelet effects, contributing to the pharmacological utility of constituents derived from P. mume (Yoshikawa et al., 2002).

The abundance of phenolic compounds, including flavanones and acetylated p-coumaroylsucroses, highlights the potential of peach twigs to serve as valuable sources of bioactive constituents. Previous studies have demonstrated the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-diabetic, and potential skin-whitening activities of peach twigs (Kim et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Murata et al., 2022). Given their current status as underutilized agricultural by-products, peach twigs could be effectively harnessed for the development of functional foods and health-promoting formulations. Further research is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms of action and to evaluate the safety profiles of these compounds.

In vitro antioxidant capacity and intracellular oxidative stress

The antioxidant capacity of peach fruit, twigs, and leaves was measured using ABTS, DPPH, and FRAP assays, and intracellular oxidative stress levels were also evaluated in PC-12 and SH-SY5Y cell lines (Fig. 3). The fruit, twigs, and leaves of peaches had antioxidant capacities of 23.1, 111.9, and 49.6 mg VCE/g in the ABTS assay; 12.1, 56.4, and 24.5 mg VCE/g in the DPPH assay; and 12.5, 67.3, and 28.0 mg VCE/g in the FRAP assay, respectively. Among the three parts of peaches, twigs showed the highest antioxidant capacity as well as the highest total phenolic concentration (Table 3 and Fig. 3a). Specifically, the antioxidant capacities of the twigs were significantly (p < 0.05) higher than those of the fruit and leaves: 4.8- and 2.3-fold higher in the ABTS assay, 4.7- and 2.3-fold higher in the DPPH assay, and 5.4- and 2.4-fold higher in the FRAP assay, respectively.

All data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. Bars (means) that do not share any letters are significantly different, as determined by Tukey's multiple comparison test (p < 0.05). In a previous study, the antioxidant capacities of fruits from seven P. persica cultivars reportedly ranged from 28.9 to 90.1 mg Trolox equivalents (TE)/g extract (measured by an ABTS assay) and 16.0 to 48.9 mg TE/g extract (measured by a DPPH assay) (Mokrani et al., 2016). Considering that vitamin C possesses approximately 1.76-fold and 1.21-fold greater antioxidant capacity than Trolox in both ABTS and DPPH assays, respectively (Hwang & Lee, 2023), the antioxidant capacities of seven peach cultivars are approximately equivalent to the values observed for peach fruit in the present study when expressed as VCE (Fig. 3) (Mokrani et al., 2016). Furthermore, peach twigs have exhibited greater oxygen radical absorbance capacity compared with the fruit, consistent with our current findings (Nakagawa et al., 2018).

Peach fruit, twig, and leaf extracts showed dose-dependent decreases in intracellular oxidative stress levels in the PC-12 and SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 3b and c). At the highest extract concentration of 50 mg/L used in this study, the fruit, twigs, and leaves reduced intracellular oxidative stress levels by approximately 32 %, 62 %, and 54 %, respectively, compared with the H2O2 (50 μM)-treated group.

The antioxidant capacities of the three parts of the peaches can be attributed to their respective phenolic compounds, particularly the flavonoid content (Table 3 and Fig. 3). In vitro antioxidant tests showed that the antioxidant capacity was determined by the number of hydroxyl groups capable of donating electrons and the structural features of the antioxidant compounds. Flavonoid glycosides typically have a lower antioxidant capacity compared with their aglycones due to fewer exposed hydroxyl groups and steric hindrance caused by the attached sugar moiety (Kim & Lee, 2004). The high antioxidant capacity of peach twigs can be explained by their high flavonoid aglycone content (1030.23 mg/100 g DW), which accounts for approximately 61.2 % of the total phenolic concentration. Although peach leaves contain similar amounts of hydroxycinnamic and hydroxybenzoic acids to those in the fruit, their significantly higher antioxidant capacities can be attributed to a 3.9-fold higher flavonoid content than that of the fruit.

Conclusion

High-resolution UHPLC-Orbitrap-MS analysis identified 63 compounds in peach fruit, twigs, and leaves, 56 of which were phenolic compounds. Among these 56 compounds, five compounds were reported for the first time in peach tissues: quercetin 3-O-(coumaroyl)hexoside, kaempferol 3-O-(coumaroyl)hexoside, prunose II, prunose I, and hexa-O-acetyl-3-O-p-coumaroylsucrose. Distinct phenolic patterns were observed across the three peach parts: hydroxycinnamic acids predominated in the fruit, flavanones in the twigs, and flavones in the leaves. Antioxidant capacity was higher in the twigs and leaves compared to the fruit, with the twigs exhibiting the strongest effect. Peach fruit, twig, and leaf extracts dose-dependently decreased intracellular oxidative stress levels in PC-12 and SH-SY5Y cells compared with H₂O₂-treated controls. These findings provide an analytical foundation for the valorization of peach by-products in agricultural and nutraceutical applications. Peach by-products may also serve as sustainable sources for developing high-value natural products enriched with bioactive phenolic compounds.
