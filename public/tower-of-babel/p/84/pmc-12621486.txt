Pneumonia caused by Legionella pneumophila serogroup 6 after drowning in a home bathtub: A case report
Authors: Sayumi Koyanagi, Satoshi Ano, Norihiro Kikuchi, Takashi Hatori, Michiko Saegusa, Rie Shigemasa, Nobuyuki Hizawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Legionella pneumophila infections are typically caused by exposure to contaminated water. Herein, we report a case of L. pneumophila pneumonia that likely resulted from the aspiration of contaminated bathwater after the patient drowned in a bathtub at home, rather than from exposure in a public bathhouse or circulating system. A 71-year-old man was brought to our hospital after drowning in a bathtub (day 1). The patient presented with severe respiratory failure and impaired consciousness. Chest radiography revealed extensive infiltrative opacities in the lung fields, and a rapid diagnostic test revealed positivity for influenza antigens. Aspiration and influenza pneumonia were initially suspected, and systemic management including intensive care was consequently initiated. Despite this treatment, the patient's clinical course remained poor. On Day 7 of illness, the patient's urinary antigen test was positive for Legionella, leading to a diagnosis of Legionella pneumonia. Legionella pneumoniae can be caused by aspiration after drowning. Diagnostic difficulties due to nonspecific clinical presentation and poor general condition after drowning should be considered. In cases of drowning that do not respond to standard treatments, L. pneumophila infection should be considered in the differential diagnosis.

Introduction

In Japan, hot springs and large public baths are recognized sources of Legionella infection [1]. Legionella has occasionally been detected in public bathhouses after drowning; however, clinical evidence specifically indicating L. pneumophila infection in cases of drowning-related pulmonary damage is rare. Furthermore, global conventional urinary antigen tests for L. pneumophila are limited to detecting serogroup 1, making diagnosis challenging. Because of severe respiratory failure and delayed diagnosis, patients commonly experience a prolonged course of illness.

Herein, we report a case of L. pneumophila infection that developed in a patient after drowning in a bathtub at home. The introduction of a new urinary antigen test kit enabled prompt diagnosis in this patient, despite the infection being caused by serogroup 6. This case serves as a valuable example of successful treatment of severe Legionella pneumophila pneumonia following drowning.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old man was admitted to our hospital after drowning in the bathtub. The bathtub was a non-jetted wooden tub, and water was replenished incrementally, with approximately one-third replaced at each refill. The patient's medical history included heavy alcohol consumption and diabetes mellitus, both of which known risk factors for Legionella infection. Upon admission, the patient presented with impaired consciousness and severe respiratory failure. Chest radiography revealed extensive lung infiltration (Fig. 1A). Additionally, chest computed tomography revealed infiltrative shadows and ground-glass opacities, predominantly in the dorsal segment of the right lower lung lobe, along with bilateral pleural effusion (Fig. 1B). Influenza antigen test results were positive. Urinary antigen tests for Streptococcus pneumoniae and L. pneumophila were not performed in the present study. Typical clinical indicators of Legionella infection such as elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), were initially absent. However, hyponatremia and hyperlactate dehydrogenase (LDH)-anemia were also observed (Table 1). High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen therapy was initiated at a flow rate of 40 L/min with an FiO2 of 40 %. On Day 1, intravenous peramivir was administered to treat the influenza infection, and ceftriaxone (CTRX) was initiated as empiric antibiotic therapy. Sputum cultures yielded no significant pathogens. On Day 3, pulmonary infiltrates temporarily showed improvement; however, the patient developed a high-grade fever exceeding 39 °C and began exhibiting incoherent speech and behavior. Despite treatment, fever persisted and oxygenation worsened. New infiltrate appeared in the right upper lobe of the lungs. On Day 7, laboratory tests showed a marked elevation of inflammatory markers (CRP 33 mg/dL), raising the suspicion of Legionella pneumonia. A urinary antigen test, which was positive on the same day, was performed. The antibiotic regimen was switched to levofloxacin (LVFX); however, by day 10, inflammatory markers showed minimal improvement, and oxygenation further deteriorated. Quinolone resistance was suspected and azithromycin (AZM) was added to the treatment regimen. By Day 14, the pulmonary infiltrates had progressed to the bilateral lung fields, and respiratory failure had worsened. HFNC support was increased to 50 L/min with an FiO2 of 65 %. Chest radiography revealed dense ground glass opacities in the right lung, and new peripheral infiltrates in the left lung. Secondary organizing pneumonia was suspected, and steroid pulse therapy was initiated, resulting in rapid improvement in the respiratory status. Discussion

Legionella species inhabit various aquatic environments, including domestic water systems, hot springs, and public baths [1]. Although most reported cases of Legionella pneumoniae are associated with public or industrial water sources, this case demonstrates that domestic bathtubs are also a potential source of infection, particularly when aspiration occurs during drowning.

Early diagnosis of Legionella pneumonia is challenging, particularly when patients present with overlapping conditions, such as aspiration pneumonia or influenza. Classic signs (e.g., elevated CRP levels, hyponatremia, and thrombocytopenia) may not manifest immediately, particularly within the typical 2–10-day incubation period. The diagnosis in this case was further complicated by the positive influenza test result [2]. However, in this patient, at least five out of six criteria of the Legionella prediction score were fulfilled, which exceeded the requisite threshold of three or more positive items; thus, the patient was clinically suspected to have Legionella pneumonia [3].

L. pneumophila and influenza viruses can both cause community-acquired pneumonia with respiratory failure, and Legionella infection can lead to influenza infection, which can be potentially fatal. The co-pathology of pandemic influenza and bacteria is characterized by complex interactions between co-infecting pathogens and the host; however, the fundamental causes of secondary bacterial infections during influenza infection remain unclear [4].

The most widely used urinary antigen test detects only L. pneumophila serogroup 1, which limits its utility in identifying the less common serogroups [5]. This case highlights the importance of using novel diagnostic kits to detect multiple serogroups. In particular, the immunochromatographic test employed in the present case enabled rapid identification of serogroup 6, thus facilitating appropriate therapy [6].

Combination therapy with macrolides and quinolones is increasingly recognized for its potential to reduce mortality in severe cases of Legionella pneumonia [7]. Although the role of corticosteroids remains debatable, recent research indicates that they may be beneficial in managing septic shock or severe pneumonia [8]. Studies have further demonstrated that methylprednisolone modulates lung cell response to Legionella infection, possibly by reducing tissue damage [9].

In conclusion, clinicians should maintain a high level of suspicion of Legionella in patients with pneumonia following drowning, particularly when standard therapies are ineffective. Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation and appropriately tailored treatment, including a combination of antibiotics and possibly corticosteroids, may improve outcomes in severe cases.
