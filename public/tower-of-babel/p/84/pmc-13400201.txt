Vacuum-pulsating ultrasound synergy for efficient extraction of bioactive substances from Lonicera japonica
Authors: Dong Wang, Chun Wang, Yifan Zhao, Yao Niu, Zhiyun Chen, Cunliang Li, Pei Li, Yong Wang, Yuejin Yuan, Yang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a vacuum pulsating ultrasound extraction (VP-UE) technique for extracting bioactive compounds (chlorogenic acid(CGA),total flavonoids(TF),polysaccharides(PS)) from uncrushed and microcrushed Lonicera japonica (Flower bud) under laboratory-scale conditions. By combining vacuum pulsation and ultrasonic cavitation, VP-UE creates a bimodal pore structure (macropores and micropores) in the material, enhancing mass transfer and solvent contact. Under optimized conditions (200 W, 25 min,50℃,solid–liquid ratio1:30, 3 pulsation cycles), extraction yields exceeded 90% of those from fully pulverized materials, reaching 2.49% for CGA, 1.27% for TF, and 5.53% for PS. Heatmap analysis revealed correlations (r > 0.8) among the three active compounds, suggesting strong co-extractability under VP-UE conditions, though further pathway analysis is needed. Color parameters (L*, a*, b*) linked PS extraction to colloidal turbidity (L* decrease) and flavonoid pigments to yellow hue. VP-UE is a promising extraction method at the laboratory scale, offering insights for equipment design and extract quality evaluation.Keywords: VP-UE, Honeysuckle, Extraction, Bioactive compounds, Bimodal pore structure

Introduction

Honeysuckle, derived from the dried flower buds or partially opened flowers of Lonicera japonica Thunb. (Caprifoliaceae family) [1], is a widely utilized edible and medicinal plant [2]. Its primary bioactive components, including chlorogenic acid (CGA), total flavonoids (TF), and polysaccharides (PS), exhibit significant pharmacological properties such as heat-clearing, detoxification, and wind-heat dispersion [3]. Therefore, research on the extraction of these active compounds from honeysuckle is of considerable importance. Current extraction methods for bioactive components from honeysuckle primarily include water extraction [4], enzymatic extraction [5], supercritical fluid extraction [6], microwave-assisted extraction [7], and ultrasound-assisted extraction [8]. Ji et al. [9] investigated the effects of different extraction methods on the structure and functionality of soluble dietary fiber from honeysuckle berries, revealing that ultrasound-assisted extraction yielded the highest output. Taticchi et al. [10] demonstrated that high vacuum extraction of olive oil altered cellular structures, disrupted cell walls, and enhanced intra- and intercellular liquid mass transfer. Nguyen et al. [11] combined high-pressure extraction with ultrasound-assisted extraction for sugarcane leaves, finding that the combined approach achieved higher extraction efficiency compared to individual methods. Most existing studies focus on extracting bioactive components from pulverized materials. However, in large-scale industrial production, to avoid equipment clogging caused by powdered materials, it is necessary to extract from micronized or unprocessed bulk materials. Currently, there is limited research on extraction methods that meet industrial requirements for non-pulverized materials. Therefore, developing an efficient extraction method is crucial for improving extraction yields in large-scale applications and providing practical guidance for production.

Current methods for extracting bioactive compounds from plants predominantly rely on chemical or thermal treatments [12]. Research on non-thermal physical extraction methods is relatively scarce, with most studies focusing on single physical extraction techniques. This study simulates the state of materials in large-scale extraction processes, targeting thick, bulk materials for extraction. Ultrasound extraction (UE) enhances extraction efficiency by utilizing cavitation-induced shear forces to disrupt cell walls. However, its limited penetration depth results in minimal impact on the internal cells of bulk materials. Vacuum extraction (VE) accelerates molecular diffusion by lowering the boiling point of the solvent, thereby better preserving heat-sensitive components. Nevertheless, as a passive diffusion process, its contribution to improving extraction yields remains constrained. In this study, we combine vacuum pulsation with ultrasound to leverage the deep penetration of vacuum pulsation and the cavitation effects of ultrasound synergistically for the extraction of unprocessed honeysuckle. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), honeysuckle cells are tightly arranged before extraction. After VPE, a small number of cells rupture due to gas escape under vacuum conditions, and intercellular pores form. In contrast, UE treated honeysuckle exhibits disruption of outer cells caused by ultrasonic cavitation-induced shear forces, but the internal cells remain largely unaffected. However, in VP-UAE treated honeysuckle, not only do intercellular pores form extensively due to vacuum pulsation, but the internal cells are also significantly disrupted by ultrasound, facilitating the dissolution of intracellular substances into the extraction solvent and thereby improving extraction efficiency.

This study is the first to couple vacuum pulsation with ultrasound technology for the efficient extraction of bulk materials. The specific equipment used is shown in Fig. 1(b).Through multi-scale analysis (SEM morphology, heatmap correlation, and physicochemical parameters), the synergistic mechanism of multi-physical fields was revealed, along with the cross-scale regulatory mechanism (microscopic pore structure → macroscopic extraction efficiency). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations demonstrated that VP-UE induces a bimodal pore structure in the material, significantly enhancing the extraction rate of active components in practical production. Furthermore, heatmap analysis was employed to investigate the relationships among the extraction rates of CGA, TF, and PS in honeysuckle extracts, as well as the correlations between color parameters (L*, a*, b*) and total dissolved solids (TDS). This multi-scale analysis elucidates the principles governing the extraction of active compounds using VP-UE technology. The findings provide theoretical support and innovative methodologies for the development of extraction technologies for natural products. Additionally, they establish a scientific foundation for the design of large-scale extraction equipment and the standardized quality evaluation of extract liquid.

Materials and methods

Raw materials and reagents

Dried Honeysuckery (Flower bud) samples were purchased from Hebei Qixin Traditional Chinese Medicine Granule Decoction Pieces Co., LTD. (Ancheng, China).

Chlorogenic acid standard (≥98% purity), rutin standard (≥97% purity), methanol (HPLC grade), sodium hydroxide, absolute ethanol, aluminum nitrate, sodium nitrite, glucose, phenol, and concentrated sulfuric acid were procured from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd.

Single factor experimental design

Building on preliminary trials using unprocessed honeysuckle, single-factor experiments were conducted to evaluate five parameters: ultrasonic power (100, 150, 200, 250, 300 W), extraction time (15, 20, 25, 30, 35 min), temperature (30, 40, 50, 60, 70°C), pulsation cycles (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) (The total extraction time was divided equally according to the number of pulsation cycles. Half of each time to maintain a vacuum environment, half of the time to maintain an atmospheric environment. The alternation of vacuum environment and atmospheric environment is called a vacuum pulsation period.), and solid-to-liquid ratio (1:20, 1:25, 1:30, 1:35, 1:40 g/mL). Each experiment varied one factor while keeping others constant at baseline values determined through preliminary trials: 200 W power, 25 min duration, 50°C temperature, 3 pulsation cycles, and 1:30 g/mL ratio.

Extraction methods

Different extraction methods were designed under the optimized process results obtained by pre-test and single factor test.

(1)Vacuum pulsating Extraction (VPE)

Vacuum pulsating Extraction (VPE)

A weighed quantity of Honeysuckle sample was placed in a beaker with a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:30 (g/mL). Extraction proceeded in a vacuum chamber for 25 min, after which the extracts were analyzed for chlorogenic acid, total flavonoids, and polysaccharide content.

(2)Ultrasound Extraction (UE)

Ultrasound Extraction (UE)

Under identical solid-to-liquid conditions (1:30 g/mL), the sample was treated in an ultrasonic cleaner (200 W power) for 25 min. The resulting extract was similarly analyzed for bioactive compounds.

(3)Vacuum-Pulsating Ultrasound Extraction (VP-UE)

Vacuum-Pulsating Ultrasound Extraction (VP-UE)

Using the same ratio, the sample was processed in a custom-built vacuum-pulsating ultrasound-assisted extraction device for 25 min. The combined vacuum pulsation and ultrasonic cavitation synergistically enhanced compound release, and the extract was evaluated as above.

(4)Vacuum-Pulsating Ultrasound Extraction (VP-UE) (smashing treatment)

Vacuum-Pulsating Ultrasound Extraction (VP-UE) (smashing treatment)

Using the same proportion, the crushed honeysuckle was extracted in a special vacuum pulsation ultrasonic assisted extraction device for 25 min. The combined vacuum pulsation and ultrasonic cavitation synergistically enhanced compound release, and the extract was evaluated as above.

Measurement method of CGA

Chlorogenic acid detection methods include high performance liquid chromatography, capillary electrophoresis and ultraviolet spectrophotometry [13]. The preparation of high performance liquid chromatography is complex and time-consuming, but the analysis of active ingredients is more accurate. Ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometry is simple, convenient and fast [14]. This paper mainly compares the effects of different extraction conditions and techniques on the extraction rate. Therefore, ultraviolet spectrometer (model: UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan) was used to detect CGA, and 327 nm was used as the detection wavelength [15], [16]. Each group of samples was measured three times to take the average.

Measurement method of TF

Spectrophotometry analysis can ensure satisfactory accuracy and accuracy, as well as rapid and economical analysis by direct measurement or colorimetric analysis [17]. Ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometer (model: UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan) was used to determine the absorbance at a wavelength of 510 nm, and the absorbance was measured to calculate the content of flavonoids [18], [19]. Each group of samples was measured three times to take the average.

Measurement method of PS

The polysaccharide content was determined by phenol–sulfuric acid method [20]. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm using an ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometer (model: V-1800, Shimadzu, Japan) to calculate the polysaccharide content. Each group of samples was measured three times to take the average.

The probe was immersed in the extract until readings stabilized, then rinsed with distilled water and gently dried with absorbent paper between measurements to prevent cross-contamination. Triplicate measurements ensured reproducibility.

Results and analysis

Single factor experimental analysis

Effects of ultrasonic power, ultrasonic time and ultrasonic temperature.

The influence of different extraction power

The effects of different ultrasonic power levels on the extraction efficiency of VP-UE treated honeysuckle are shown in Fig. 2(a). As illustrated, CGA extraction reached its maximum yield of 2.49% at 200 W, attributable to its molecular structure containing multiple hydrophilic hydroxyl and carboxyl groups that confer excellent aqueous solubility [25]. In contrast, PS required higher ultrasonic power 250 W to achieve maximal extraction (5.58%), consistent with its macromolecular nature necessitating greater mechanical disruption through enhanced cavitation effects for effective release from the cell wall matrix. The extraction rate of TF showed an increasing trend up to 200 W, reaching 1.27%, and then plateaued. This behavior reflects the inherent hydrophobicity of flavonoid aglycones (characterized by substantial aromatic ring structures) and their dependence on glycosylation (e.g., glucose moieties) for aqueous solubility [26]. The observed stabilization suggests achievement of near-saturation solubility or mass transfer equilibrium under these conditions. In contrast, the TDS consistently increased with higher ultrasonic power, with the rate of increase becoming more pronounced at higher power levels. This phenomenon results from differential solubilization processes involving: the highly water-soluble CGA (low molecular weight phenolic acids), moderately soluble TF glycosides, and macromolecular PS components.The underlying mechanism involves ultrasonic cavitation generating microbubbles whose collapse produces intense shockwaves and microjets capable of disrupting both the polysaccharide-rich cell wall (composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin matrices) and the lipid bilayer membranes. This structural breakdown facilitates enhanced release of intracellular bioactive compounds. This finding aligns with the results reported by Premathilaka et al. [27] who observed similar trends in the extraction efficiency of PS with varying ultrasonic power. Concurrently, vacuum pulsation promotes dissolution of soluble cellular constituents (including small molecular weight sugars, organic acids, amino acids, and mineral ions) into the aqueous phase. The positive correlation between power intensity and TDS increment confirms that enhanced cavitation produces greater cellular disruption and consequently higher soluble solid content in the extract [28].

The effects of different ultrasonic power levels on the color parameters of VP-UE honeysuckle extract liquid are illustrated in Fig. 2(b). As the ultrasonic power increased, the L* value gradually decreased, the a* value initially decreased slowly and then stabilized, the b* value increased slowly, and the △E value showed an upward trend, with the rate of increase becoming more pronounced at higher power levels. L* represents the brightness of the extract liquid, ranging from 0 to 100, where higher values indicate a lighter color and lower values indicate a darker color. As the extraction power increases, the degree of cell disruption intensifies, and the release of substances such as CGA, PS, proteins, pectin, and tiny cell debris increases. These substances can form colloids or suspended particles in water [29]. With the increase of ultrasonic power, the turbidity of the extract is significantly increased and the color is deepened, reducing the light transmittance and thus leading to a decrease in L*. This is highly consistent with the phenomenon mentioned above where the extraction rates of CGA and PS in honeysuckle increase and the TDS value in the extract rises. The a* value represents the red-green component of the extract liquid, with positive values indicating a reddish hue and negative values indicating a greenish hue. The a* value of the extract liquid initially decreased slowly and then stabilized, suggesting that the color of the extract liquid first became greener and then remained relatively unchanged. Although CGA and TF can oxidize to form reddish quinone compounds [30], the extraction conditions (aqueous extraction with vacuum pulsation) minimized oxidation, resulting in negligible changes in a*. The greenish hue of the extract liquid may be attributed to the adsorption of chlorophyll and other pigments by PS, leading to negative a* values. The b* value represents the yellow-blue component of the extract liquid, with positive values indicating a yellowish hue and negative values indicating a bluish hue. The b* value of the extract liquid increased slowly with higher ultrasonic power, indicating a more yellowish color. This is mainly attributed to the increase in the content of TF (especially flavonol glycosides such as rutin and quercetin) in the extract. Flavonoids themselves have chromophotes (benzoyl and cinnamyl conjugated systems), and their structural characteristics (such as hydroxyl and glycosylation) often make their solutions yellow. As the ultrasonic power increased, the extraction of TF also increased slowly and then stabilized [31].

Effect of different extraction time

The effects of different extraction times on the extraction efficiency of VP-UE honeysuckle are shown in Fig. 2 (c). The extraction rates of CGA and TF initially increased and then decreased with extraction time, reaching their maximum values of 2.52% and 1.27%, respectively, at 25 min. The extraction rate of PS increased with extraction time and stabilized at 25 min, reaching 5.53%. Meanwhile, TDS consistently increased with longer extraction times. The initial increase and subsequent decrease in the extraction rates of CGA and TF can be attributed to the degradation of their active components under prolonged ultrasonic treatment, leading to reduced extraction efficiency [32]: The o-diphenol hydroxyl structure of CGA is prone to oxidative degradation or ester bond hydrolysis under the long-term ultrasonic thermal effect. The glycosidic bond of TF may break under cavitation, generating less water-soluble aglycone, which leads to a decrease in the extraction rate. In contrast, PS, as a macromolecular bioactive compound, requires more time to dissolve in water due to its stable attachment within cells. This is related to the macromolecular spatial structure and cell wall binding state of polysaccharides. Long-term ultrasound promotes the swelling and dissociation of cell wall polysaccharides (cellulose-hemicellulose-pectin network). Thus, the extraction efficiency of PS increased with longer extraction times [33]. Prolonged vacuum pulsation and ultrasonic treatment further disrupted cell structures, resulting in higher TDS values in the extract liquid. As the extraction time increased, the intervals between vacuum pulsations became longer, and the later-stage pulsations caused more intense cell disruption. Continuous dissolution of small molecules (chlorogenic acid degradation products quinic acid, caffeic acid, monosaccharides (glucose, fructose) and mineral ions (K+, Ca2+) and increased cell debris (phospholipid bilayer) increased. This explains the rapid increase in TDS at 35 min compared to 30 min.

The effects of different extraction times on the color parameters of VP-UE honeysuckle extract liquid are illustrated in Fig. 2 (d). As the extraction time increased, the L* value gradually decreased, the a* value initially decreased and then increased, the b* value increased slowly, and the △E value showed an upward trend. With prolonged extraction time, PS further dissolved into the extract liquid, Polysaccharide-protein complex and pectin gel network encapsulate cell debris, reducing its light transmittance and causing a decrease in L*. The a* value initially decreased and then increased with extraction time, indicating that the color of the extract liquid first shifted toward green and then transitioned to a reddish hue after 25 min. The early decline (greenish) is due to the release of chlorophyll from the chloroplast, which is electrostatically adsorbed by the negatively charged polysaccharide (if the gum carboxyl group) to form a green colloid; the later increase (reddish) is due to the accumulation of quinones, CGA is oxidized to CGA quinone (reddish brown), and TF is oxidized to form o-quinone structure. The b* value increased slowly with extraction time, a trend similar to the effect of ultrasonic power on the extract liquid. As the extraction time increased, △E also increased, but the rate of increase was slower compared to the effect of ultrasonic power. This suggests that ultrasonic power has a more significant impact on the color parameters of the extract liquid than extraction time.

Effects of different extraction temperatures

The effects of different extraction temperatures on the extraction efficiency of VP-UE honeysuckle are shown in Fig. 2 (e). The extraction rates of CGA and TF initially increased and then decreased with rising temperature. CGA reached its maximum extraction rate of 2.49% at 50°C, while TF peaked at 1.34% at 60°C. In contrast, the extraction rates of PS and TDS increased with temperature, with PS reaching 5.73% at 70°C. However, the rate of increase in TDS slowed as the temperature rose. When the temperature is below 50°C, the extraction rate increases with the increase of temperature, which is caused by the breaking of hydrogen bond and the thermal movement of molecules, thus affecting the increase of solubility. After 50°C, with the increase of temperature, the hydrolysis of ester bond, oxidation reaction and the breaking of glycosidic bond lead to the decrease of extraction rate. This phenomenon aligns with the findings of Sharayei et al. [34] in the extraction of astaxanthin, where elevated temperatures initially enhance the extraction of active compounds by increasing solubility, diffusion, and mass transfer rates, as well as reducing viscosity and surface tension between the solvent and compounds. However, excessively high temperatures can lead to the decomposition of active components. For PS, its solubility in water increases with temperature, resulting in a gradual rise in extraction efficiency [35]. This is because the increase of temperature weakens the hydrogen bond, promotes the decrease of the degree of methyl esterification of cell wall polysaccharides and pectin, and the hydration of carboxyl exposed to enhance the dissolution.

The effects of different extraction temperatures on the color parameters of VP-UE honeysuckle extract liquid are illustrated in Fig. 2 (f). As the extraction temperature increased, the L* value gradually decreased, the a* value initially decreased and then increased, the b* value first increased and then decreased, and the △E value showed an upward trend. The gradual decrease of L* indicated that the solubility of PS increased with the increase of temperature, which was due to the high temperature colloidal effect: the molecular chain of polysaccharides such as pectin expanded at >50°C, forming larger particle size complexes with polyphenols or proteins and accelerating the accumulation of Maillard reaction precursors (reducing sugars or amino acids), resulting in a decrease in light transmittance. The a* value initially decreased and then increased with rising temperature, likely because chlorophyll and other pigments were fully dissolved in the extract liquid at temperatures below 50°C, while degradation of these pigments at temperatures above 50°C caused the a* value to rise. The b* value initially increased and then decreased with increasing extraction temperature, suggesting that the color of the extract liquid shifted toward yellow before 60°C and then became less yellow beyond 60°C. This trend aligns with the changes in the extraction rate of TF, indicating that the yellow color of the extract liquid is primarily derived from TF.

The influence of different pulsation times

The effects of vacuum pulsation times and solid–liquid ratio on the extraction rate of active components, TDS, and color parameters are shown in Fig. 3.

Effects of pulsation times and solid–liquid ratio.

The effects of different pulsation cycles on the extraction efficiency of VP-UE honeysuckle are shown in Fig. 3 (a). As illustrated, the extraction rates of CGA, TF, and PS initially increased and then decreased with increasing pulsation cycles, reaching their maximum values of 2.44%, 1.27%, and 5.53%, respectively, at three pulsation cycles. Meanwhile, TDS initially increased and then decreased, peaking at 614 ppm after four pulsation cycles. This phenomenon is primarily due to the mechanisms of vacuum pulsation. In the first stage, the container holding the solution and material is evacuated and maintained under vacuum for a period. The vacuum pumping makes the low-boiling volatile substances (such as monoterpenes and aldehydes) in the pores of the material escape with the gas, and the expansion and escape of the gas in the porous structure are coupled with the deformation relaxation of the solid matrix. During pressure recovery, the solvent was forced to penetrate into the cells, and the pectin-cellulose network was destroyed by the capillary effect. This pressure difference drives the liquid into the capillary pores of the material through hydrodynamic mechanisms [36], [37]. Additionally, vacuum reduces the boiling point of water and increases the activity of water molecules, enhancing the solubility and dissolution rate of active compounds while better preserving heat-sensitive substances [38], [39]. For bulk materials, increasing the number of vacuum pulsation cycles within a certain range promotes the further release of intracellular active compounds, improving extraction efficiency. However, high-frequency pulsation leads to the rupture of organelles (vacuoles, chloroplasts), the release of proteases and polyphenol oxidase (PPO), and the oxidation of chlorogenic acid to produce quinone polymers. The cell debris released macromolecular impurities such as starch and lignin, which competed with the active components for the solvation layer, resulting in a decrease in the extraction rate. Furthermore, excessive pulsation cycles may induce the decomposition of active components, reducing extraction efficiency [40], [41].

The effects of different pulsation cycles on the color parameters of VP-UE honeysuckle extract liquid are illustrated in Fig. 3 (b). As the number of pulsation cycles increased, the L* value gradually decreased, the a* value initially decreased and then increased, the b* value first increased and then decreased, and the △E value showed an upward trend. The gradual decrease in L* is attributed to the further disruption of cells with increasing pulsation cycles, leading to the dissolution of intracellular substances into the solution. The a* value initially decreased and then increased with higher pulsation cycles, likely because chlorophyll-related substances within the cells were fully dissolved at fewer than three pulsation cycles. Beyond three cycles, the further dissolution of other substances reduced the green hue. The b* value initially increased and then decreased with increasing pulsation cycles, a trend opposite to that of a*. This indicates that the extraction rate of TF in the solution reached its maximum at three pulsation cycles. Further increases in pulsation cycles led to the degradation of TF, resulting in a decrease in the yellow hue.

Effect of different solid–liquid ratio

The effects of different solid-to-liquid ratios on the extraction efficiency of VP-UE honeysuckle are shown in Fig. 3 (c) As illustrated, the extraction rates of CGA, TF, and PS increased with higher solid-to-liquid ratios, reaching 2.49%, 1.27%, and 5.53%, respectively, at a ratio of 1:30. Beyond this ratio, the extraction rates showed only marginal increases. In contrast, TDS decreased as the solid-to-liquid ratio increased, which corresponds to the extraction efficiency. Higher solid-to-liquid ratios resulted in higher extraction rates and lower TDS values in the extract liquid, with 1:30 being the optimal ratio. These findings are consistent with the results reported by Liao et al. [42], who observed similar trends in the extraction of phenolics and anthocyanins from purple eggplant peels using multi-frequency ultrasound.

The effects of different solid-to-liquid ratios on the color parameters of VP-UE honeysuckle extract liquid are presented in Fig. 3 (d). As the solid-to-liquid ratio increased, the L* value gradually increased, the a* value initially decreased and then increased, the b* value first increased and then decreased, and the △E value decreased. With higher solid-to-liquid ratios, the concentration of active substances in the extract liquid decreased, resulting in a lighter color and an increase in L*. The changes in a* and b* were correlated with variations in the effective components in the extract liquid. The decrease in △E indicates that higher solid-to-liquid ratios produced extract liquids with lighter colors, closer to that of distilled water.

Thermal relationship diagram analysis

The thermodynamic relationship diagram of honeysuckle extract liquid parameters is shown in Fig. 4.

Heat map of related parameters of extract.

This diagram systematically reveals the complex correlation network among CGA, TF, PS, TDS, and color parameters (L*, a*, b*) under VP-UE conditions. Through hierarchical clustering, the variables are divided into two functional clusters. The first cluster includes CGA, TF, and PS, which exhibit strong positive correlations (correlation coefficients > 0.8).The strong positive correlations among CGA, TF, and PS (r > 0.8) indicate their high co-extractability under VP-UE conditions, rather than a shared biosynthetic origin. CGA and TF are derived from the phenylpropanoid pathway, whereas PS is a carbohydrate polymer from sugar metabolism and is not directly synthesized via this pathway. However, PS can associate with cell-wall phenolics through non-covalent interactions, as reviewed by Siemińska-Kuczer et al. (2022) [42] regarding polyphenol–cell wall polysaccharide interactions. During extraction, the synchronous leaching of cell-wall-bound phenolics and co-extracted PS leads to a positive correlation among the three compounds, independent of their biosynthetic origins. The phenylpropanoid pathway is one of the most important metabolic pathways in plants, producing over 8000 metabolites [43], [44]: Chlorogenic acid is a direct product of the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway and is a phenylpropionic acid compound. Flavonoids are downstream derivatives of the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway. Therefore, they are all derived from the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway. The polysaccharide belongs to the carbohydrate polymer, and the carbon fixed by photosynthesis is synthesized by sugar metabolism, which is not directly related to the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway. The second cluster consists of color parameters (L*, a*, b*) and TDS. Among these, L* shows significant negative correlations with PS (−0.791) and TF (−0.421) (p < 0.05). This is likely due to the formation of colloids by PS in the solution or the oxidation of TF to produce dark-colored compounds, increasing the turbidity of the extract liquid and reducing light transmittance, thereby decreasing L*. The b* value is positively correlated with CGA (0.627) and TF (0.517), indicating that the accumulation of CGA and TF may influence the color of the extract liquid through the release of yellow pigments, such as flavonoid glycosides. TDS shows weak correlations with other variables (absolute values < 0.5), as it is mainly influenced by factors such as cell disruption and is unrelated to the biosynthetic pathways of active organic metabolites.

Further analysis reveals that changes in color parameters are closely related to processing conditions. When the ultrasonic power increased from 200 W to 300 W, L* decreased from 35.03 to 30.4, while b* increased from 5.44 to 6.98. These changes are consistent with the increased extraction rates of active components (CGA, TF, and PS) and the rise in suspended particles. When the extraction time extended from 25 min to 35 min, L* continued to decrease from 32.94 to 30.08, while b* remained relatively stable, changing only slightly from 6.43 to 6.31. This suggests that prolonged extraction may induce oxidative degradation of TF. As the solid-to-liquid ratio increased from 1:30 to 1:40, L* rose from 32.94 to 33.17, while b* decreased from 6.43 to 6.09. This reflects the dilution of active component concentrations at higher solvent ratios, leading to a reduction in color intensity. The temperature was positively correlated with the extraction rates of CGA, TF and PS, and PS was the most significant (r = 0.519), indicating that the thermal decomposition resistance of PS was the strongest when the temperature increased. The ultrasonic power destroyed the gland cells containing flavonoids by cavitation micro-jet, and drove the release of TF (r = 0.464). The extraction time had the most significant effect on the extraction efficiency of PS (r = 0.420), because macromolecular polysaccharides needed longer time to complete the dissolution equilibrium. Among them, the thermodynamic properties are not strong, because the selection of temperature, ultrasonic power and extraction time can enhance and weaken the extraction effect, so there is no particularly high value of thermodynamic correlation.

The heatmap results are consistent with SEM images.VP-UE forms a bimodal pore structure (coexistence of macropores and micropores) through the synergistic effects of vacuum pulsation and ultrasound, significantly enhancing solvent penetration efficiency and the release of active components (CGA: 2.49%, TF: 1.28%, PS: 5.53%). Changes in color parameters (L*, a*, b*) further validate the regulatory mechanism of the microstructure on the physicochemical properties of the extract liquid. The strong negative correlation between L* and PS (−0.791) indicates the dominant role of PS's colloidal properties in increasing the turbidity of the extract liquid. Meanwhile, TF primarily contributes to the b* value, but the positive correlation between b* and CGA (0.627) suggests that CGA may promote the release of flavonoid glycosides from TF through co-solubility effects. The heatmap analysis reveals the interaction mechanisms among multiple indicators, providing a theoretical foundation for the industrial extraction of active compounds from honeysuckle.

Content comparison of different extraction methods

The extraction yields of active components and microstructural changes under different extraction methods are compared in Fig. 5.

Comparison of different extraction methods.

The extraction rates of active compounds from honeysuckle using different extraction methods are shown in Fig. 5(a) A comparison was made among VPE, UE, VP-UE, and VP-UE (smashing treatment) for the extraction rates of CGA, TF, and PS. Under identical experimental conditions, the extraction rates of various active compounds followed the order: VP-UE (smashing treatment) > VP-UE > UE > VPE. Specifically, the extraction rate of CGA using VP-UE for unprocessed honeysuckle was 2.49%, closely approaching the 2.67% achieved with smashing treatment, with a minimal difference of only 0.18%. Compared to VPE and UE, this represents an increase of 45.61% and 20.29%. The extraction rate of TF using VP-UE for unprocessed honeysuckle was 1.27%, reaching 91.37% of the rate obtained with smashing treatment. This represents an improvement of 63.87% and 21.53% compared to VPE and UE, respectively. For PS, the extraction rate using VP-UE for unprocessed honeysuckle was 5.53%, achieving 94.05% of the rate with smashing treatment, and showing an increase of 55.77% and 16.42% compared to VPE and UE, respectively. These results can be attributed to the following mechanisms: VPE relies on gas escape from the material under vacuum conditions, causing cell rupture and accelerating solvent diffusion to enhance extraction. However, its dependence on passive diffusion limits cell disruption, resulting in the lowest extraction efficiency. UE utilizes the cavitation effect of ultrasound to generate microjets and shockwaves, disrupting surface cells and improving extraction efficiency. However, its impact on the deep tissues of unprocessed materials is limited, making its extraction efficiency only slightly higher than that of VPE. In contrast, VP-UE leverages the synergistic mechanism of vacuum pulsation and ultrasound to enhance extraction efficiency for unprocessed materials. This is achieved by using the periodic pressure changes of vacuum pulsation to drive solvent penetration into deep tissues, combined with the microjets and shear forces generated by ultrasonic cavitation to disrupt cell walls, bringing its extraction efficiency close to that of smashing treatment.

Scanning electron microscope analysis

The effects of different extraction methods on the microstructure of honeysuckle after extraction were systematically investigated using SEM, as shown in Fig. 5.Ⅱ. The green boxes are micropores and the red boxes are macropores. Pores with diameters less than 20 μm were defined as micropores, while those with diameters greater than 50 μm were defined as macropores [45]. The morphological characteristics induced by the three extraction techniques were significantly different, and these features were closely related to their extraction efficiencies and mechanisms.

The SEM images of honeysuckle after VPE reveal a sparsely distributed macropore structure. These macropores likely result from the escape of intracellular gases and the evaporation of internal liquids under reduced pressure, selectively rupturing weak points in the cell walls. Consequently, a distributed macropore structure forms on the surface. Since VPE primarily relies on passive mechanisms, the cell walls are not completely disrupted, leaving large intact areas. This observation aligns with the lower extraction rates of active components from honeysuckle using VPE, as mentioned earlier. In contrast to VPE, honeysuckle treated with UE exhibits a dense network of micropores. This is primarily due to the uniform disruption of surface cell walls by microjets and shockwaves generated through ultrasonic cavitation, which accelerates the dissolution of active components. As a result, the extraction efficiency of UE is higher than that of VPE. The synergistic effect of vacuum pulsation and ultrasound induces a bimodal pore structure in honeysuckle, combining both macropores and micropores. This unique morphology reflects the collaboration between vacuum pulsation and ultrasound. The macropores created by vacuum pulsation provide rapid mass transfer channels, while the micropores offer additional adsorption sites, significantly increasing the contact area between the solvent and the material and enhancing the extraction efficiency of active components. Tian et al. [46] found that pore structures affect the permeability of core samples under ultrasonic treatment. Similarly, Yu et al. [47] observed that increased pressure and porosity in shale oil reservoirs enhance oil recovery, which shares a similar rationale with the improved extraction efficiency achieved by the unique bimodal pore structure formed through vacuum pulsation-ultrasound treatment. Furthermore, Amooie et al. [48] demonstrated in studies on porous media transport that bimodal pore structures can significantly alter flow patterns and diffusion dynamics, even under low permeability ratios. This further supports the strong transport capacity of bimodal pore structures, which facilitates the dissolution of active components and improves extraction efficiency.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the mechanisms for the efficient extraction of CGA, TF, and PS from honeysuckle under industrial production conditions using VP-UE technology. By integrating physicochemical parameters, microstructural morphology, and heatmap correlation analysis, the regulatory effects of process parameters on the release of active components were elucidated. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1)VP-UE technology, compared to other treatment methods, induces a bimodal pore structure in honeysuckle, combining macropores and micropores. This unique morphology reflects the synergistic interaction between vacuum pulsation and ultrasound. The macropores generated by vacuum pulsation provide rapid mass transfer channels, while the micropores offer additional adsorption sites, significantly increasing the contact area between the solvent and the material and enhancing the extraction efficiency of active components.
