Consensus perspectives from the Dr D K Daftary symposium on oral submucous fibrosis: Unravelling the enigma conducted during the 30th National Conference of Indian Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Pathologists
Authors: Punnya V Angadi, Karishma Desai, Vinay Hazarey, Praveen Arany, Raghu Radhakrishnan, Pratibha Ramani, Rajiv Desai, Jay G Ray, Alka Kale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF) continues to be a challenging condition characterised by increasing stiffness of the mucosa, restricted mouth opening, and a risk of malignant transformation. In light of the persistent debates, the absence of a standardised treatment protocol, and the necessity for a cohesive understanding to guide clinical practice and research, a dedicated symposium was held on 5 November 2023 during the 30th National Conference of the Indian Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Pathologists. The aim was to evaluate the latest developments and establish a consensus on particular aspects of OSMF. Six experts, all from the specialty of Oral Pathology but with extensive and diverse research experience in various aspects of OSMF—including molecular mechanisms, histopathology, clinical correlations, emerging therapies, and public health perspectives—delivered presentations and answered focussed questions. The discussions were moderated by two accomplished academicians, who facilitated deliberations and guided the synthesis of consensus. Through thoughtful discussion, a consensus was reached regarding the pathogenesis, potential for malignancy, management strategies, exploration of photobiomodulation, EMT-based and regenerative therapies, and the strengthening public health measures. This report consolidates key deliberations and consensus recommendations from the symposium, aiming to provide a collaborative framework for future OSMF research and management.

INTRODUCTION

Dr. Dinesh K. Daftary, an Oral Pathologist educated at Bombay University, collaborated extensively with the late Professor J. J. Pindborg from University Hospital in Copenhagen, Denmark. He held the position of Professor in the Department of Oral Pathology at Nair Hospital Dental College and was part of the Basic Dental Research Unit at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR). Dr. Daftary conducted studies on oral precancer and cancer in rural India, focussing on epidemiology, histology, and interventions, supported by NIH, USA. The Basic Dental Research Unit at TIFR was designated a WHO collaborating Centre for oral cancer prevention. He authored over 50 research papers and co-edited “Oral Diseases in the Tropics” with S. R. Prabhu, David Wilson, and N. W. Johnson. As a founding member of the International Association of Oral Pathology, he served as president of the Indian Academy of Oral and Maxillofacial Pathologists. The Indian Association established the DR DK Daftary symposium honouring his contributions.

In the year 2023, the Dr DK Daftary symposium was held on the topic: Oral Submucous Fibrosis: Unraveling the Enigma.

Oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF) is a chronic, progressive, and potentially malignant disorder prevalent across the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia.[1] Management outcomes remain suboptimal due to fragmented understanding of its pathogenesis, variable diagnostic criteria, and inconsistent therapeutic responses.[2] OSMF challenges clinicians and researchers due to its chronic nature and malignant transformation potential.[2] OSMF has been considered enigmatic, with many unexplained aspects regarding its pathogenesis, malignant potential, and management. A symposium was organised to develop a comprehensive understanding of fibrosis causes, molecular mechanisms, and therapeutic targets. The aim was to explore new ideas and establish expert consensus on clinical priorities, examine latest developments, and create guidelines for future collaborative research.

KEY THEMES AND HIGHLIGHTS

Oral submucous fibrosis—an overview

Dr Vinay Hazarey (VH) elaborated on the research timeline of OSMF. He covered the evolution of nomenclature from ‘Idiopathica Atrophica Mucosae Trophica Oris’ by Schwartz in 1952,[5] ‘Submucous fibrosis of palate’ by Joshi,[6] and ‘Diffuse oral submucous fibrosis’ by Lal 1953.[7] Dr Hazarey noted that ‘Vidari’ might be a misnomer for OSMF, citing Shloka 63 of Sushruta Samhita that describes Vidari as similar to gangrenous stomatitis, cancrum oris, and carcinoma tonsil. He suggested ‘Vataja Sarvasara’ by Waghbatta (first century AD)[8] as more relevant, comparing its symptoms with OSMF.

Regarding aetiology, Sushruta Samhita mentioned Pugi Phalam and Tambula (areca nut and betel leaf) were traditionally used for benefits but contraindicated in dry mouth. Dr Hazarey highlighted Pindborg, Mehta, Hamner, Daftary, Gupta, Bhonsale, Murti, and Aghi’s landmark research at TIFR, Mumbai.[9] For pathogenesis, Dr Hazarey explained copper’s role in lysyl oxidase activity and collagen cross-linking, copper levels in areca nut (302 nmol/gm), and its link to p53 and malignant transformation.[101112]

Dr Hazarey described his case series of OSMF cases with proliferative verrucous/flat multifocal leukoplakia (PVML).[13] He emphasised the need for targeted therapies for this dual pathology due to high malignant transformation and poor survival. Metronomic chemotherapy for managing PVML with OSMF was discussed.

The expert panel agreed that OSMF is a chronic, potentially malignant disorder strongly linked to areca nut use. Consensus was reached on integrating OSMF awareness into tobacco- and areca-nut-cessation programs, implementing early-screening modules, and advocating for stricter regulation of areca-nut-containing products.

Pathogenesis of oral submucous fibrosis including the genetic aspects

Dr Jay Gopal Ray (JR) emphasised that arecoline stimulates fibroblasts to increase collagen synthesis via TGF-β pathway activation, while tannins and copper inhibit collagen degradation and promote cross-linking.[14] This leads to dense fibrosis of the oral mucosa. Genetic polymorphisms in TGF-β1, MMP-2, MMP-9, GSTT1, and GSTM1 influence susceptibility by affecting cytokine levels and matrix remodelling.[1516] Oxidative stress, inflammation, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) drive disease progression and malignant transformation risk.[17]

Consensus was reached that OSMF is a multifactorial disorder primarily driven by areca nut chewing, which promotes collagen accumulation through TGF-β-mediated synthesis and reduced degradation. Genetic susceptibility, including polymorphisms in TGF-β1, MMPs, and GST genes, influences individual risk and disease severity.[16] Oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) further contribute to fibrosis progression and malignant potential, underscoring the complex interplay of environmental and genetic factors in OSMF.

Malignant Potential of Oral submucous fibrosis

Dr Rajiv S. Desai (RD) summarised that OSMF has a malignant transformation rate of 2–10%, varying by population and habit severity.[18] Theories for this transformation include chronic inflammation, DNA damage from areca nut alkaloids, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and genetic predisposition. Markers like p53, Ki-67, cyclin D1, and MMPs have been linked to malignancy progression.[19] Proliferative Verrucous Leukoplakia (PVL) with OSMF increases risk of multifocal lesions.[13] Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) from OSMF shows less aggressive behaviour than conventional OSCC, with lower nodal metastasis but surgical challenges due to fibrosis, emphasising early detection importance.

OSMF carries a 2–10% risk of malignant transformation. OSCC from OSMF shows less aggressive behaviour but surgical challenges. PVL is recognised as a high-risk lesion in OSMF patients. Early detection, monitoring, and education are essential to reduce malignant transformation risk.

Oral submucous fibrosis as a model of aberrant wound healing with insights on the role of TGF-β

Dr Praveen Arany (PA) emphasised TGF-β1’s central role in OSMF pathogenesis as the fibrotic regulator.[20] Active TGF-β1 promotes collagen synthesis, fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation, and matrix deposition. The cytokine upregulates fibrotic mediators including α-smooth muscle actin, collagens I and III, and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases.[14] TGF-β1 functions as both promoter and resolver of fibrosis depending on context.[20] While chronic activation drives pathological fibrosis, controlled TGF-β1 signalling enables normal wound healing.[21] During resolution, it promotes myofibroblast apoptosis and tissue remodelling. This dual functionality indicates therapeutic interventions must harness its regenerative properties while reducing fibrotic effects. Understanding this balance is crucial for developing OSMF treatments, including photobiomodulation therapy that modulates TGF-β1 activation.

Photobiomodulation (PBM) therapy represents a significant advancement in non-invasive light-based medical treatments, originating from Dr Endre Mester’s 1960s research, where he observed enhanced wound healing and hair growth in mice treated with low-dose laser therapy.[22] PBM utilises non-ionizing light sources including lasers, light-emitting diodes (LEDs), and visible to near-infrared light delivered at low, non-thermal doses.[23] PBM targets three key compartments: mitochondria, cell membrane photosensitive transporters and receptors, and the extracellular milieu.[24] The primary mechanism involves cytochrome C oxidase (CCO), which shows absorption characteristics within the 400–1200 nm spectrum.[25] CCO’s light absorption enhances mitochondrial activity and ATP production and generates reactive oxygen species (ROS).[26272829] PBM modulates transporters and receptors, resulting in responses such as neuronal analgesia, keratinocyte migration, and pulmonary endothelial cell vasorelaxation. Our group showed PBM directly activates latent TGF-β1 via redox-sensitive amino acid, validated through in vitro and in vivo studies.[303132] TGF-β regulates bone biology, immune responses, wound healing, and malignancies. These mechanisms contribute to PBM’s therapeutic effects including pain reduction, inflammatory modulation, and tissue regeneration.

PBM has strong evidence-based support for managing cancer treatment side effects, particularly oral mucositis in patients undergoing radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or stem cell transplantation. Based on systematic review, PBM has been endorsed by MASCC, ISOO, ESMO, NICE, and WALT.[3334] Applications include xerostomia, dysgeusia, radiation dermatitis, lymphedema, trismus, pain, and wound healing. Studies support PBM’s role in mitigating radiation fibrosis-induced trismus.[35] These cases indicate potential for OSMF management. Future directions must focus on personalised PBM protocols based on gene expression and tumour microenvironments. Recent progress in PBM dosimetry and mechanisms enables safe clinical translation.[363738] The non-invasive nature of PBM, with clinical guidelines and approved devices, positions it as an advancement in cancer care.

PA conceptualised OSMF as aberrant wound healing, where areca nut alkaloids trigger fibroblast activation and collagen deposition through TGF-β. This understanding has guided medical and surgical management strategies. He noted photobiomodulation as a promising adjunct that modulates fibroblasts, reduces inflammation, and promotes matrix remodelling.

The panel recognised photobiomodulation as a safe, non-invasive adjunct complementing medical and surgical interventions. Protocol-based PBM application can enhance outcomes and patient quality of life. The therapy shows potential in multimodal OSMF management but faces ethical and regulatory challenges requiring multicentric studies under standardised protocols.

Role of EMT in OSMF

Dr Raghu RadhaKrishnan (RR) and co-authors, Dr Smita Shetty and Dr Mohit Sharma, put forth a concept that MMP9-SPARC-ITGA5 Molecular Trinity Drives EMT-Type II to Type-III switch in OSMF.

Emerging evidence suggests epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) influences OSF progression and malignant transformation.[39] EMT involves E-cadherin downregulation and increased Vimentin, N-cadherin, and Alpha Smooth Muscle Actin expression. OSF and OSCC show upregulation of transcription factors SNAI-1, Twist, and ZEB1, facilitating myofibroblast differentiation in type II EMT.[39] The shift from Type II to Type III EMT in OSF transforms chronic wound healing into oral cancer through MMP9 Activation, SPARC Upregulation, and ITGA5 overexpression.[40] This creates conditions for OSF’s progression to OSCC, with transformation rates of 7–13% globally.[41]

MMP-9 breaks down the extracellular matrix, collagen, and basement membrane components. These disruptions trigger inflammation, converting fibroblasts to myofibroblasts and initiating fibrosis. MMP-9 removes LAP from latent TGF-β, enabling it to stimulate fibroblasts to produce more ECM. This creates a self-reinforcing loop where MMP-9 reshapes ECM while amplifying TGF-β signalling.[39] The study showed OSF increased MMP-9 levels by 65–70% versus normal mucosa, while dysplasia and carcinoma showed 2.5–3 times higher expression, 50–60% above OSF levels, indicating MMP-9’s role across disease progression.[39]

ITGA5 pairs with integrin β1 to form α5β1, a fibronectin receptor enabling fibrosis and cancer through cell anchoring. Our study shows ITGA5’s upregulation drives myofibroblast differentiation via TGF-β-mediated feed-forward loop.[39] Fibronectin (FN) is prevalent in tumours, with excess FN common in OSF. Elevated FN and α5β1 correlate with poor tumour prognosis.[4243] FN bound to cross-linked collagen reveals binding motifs detected by α5β1, enabling cells to increase ECM stiffness and migration towards malignancy.[43]

SPARC (osteonectin/BM-40) is a matricellular protein that communicates rather than provides structure in the ECM. It interacts with TGF-β to activate fibroblasts for collagen production, gradually stiffening tissues. In cancer, SPARC affects cell–matrix interactions, promotes EMT-related factors SNAI1 and SNAI2, and increases cell mobility, potentially facilitating invasion through microenvironment modification.[39]

Prospects of Anti-EMT Strategies in Cancer and Fibrotic Disorders

EMT contributes to fibrosis progression and cancer metastasis, making anti-EMT strategies crucial for OSF-OSCC’s prognosis.[41] Small-molecule inhibitors like salinomycin and curcumin analogues interfere with EMT pathways, including TGF-β/Smad and Wnt/β-catenin signalling.[44] Metabolic inhibitors suppress EMT-related transcription factors like Snail and Twist.[45] EMT’s reversibility complicates complete inhibition.[46] Future approaches may combine EMT blockers with immunotherapy, while epigenetic modifiers show promise.[47] RR emphasised Type 2 EMT’s role in OSMF driving fibroblast activation, and Type 3 EMT’s role in malignant transformation. EMT markers support their involvement in fibrosis and carcinoma development.

The panel agreed that targeting EMT pathways could offer novel therapeutic strategies to modulate fibrosis and prevent malignant progression.

Role of stem cells in OSMF

Dr Pratibha Ramani (PR) highlighted stem cell-based therapies as promising strategies due to their regenerative and immunomodulatory effects. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as a potential therapeutic option. Kheur et al.[48] proposed that MSCs’ therapeutic actions in OSMF include anti-fibrotic, anti-oxidative, and pro-angiogenic effects, along with reduced EMT. These mechanisms may provide symptomatic relief and improve oral function, making MSCs a novel treatment for OSMF management.

Despite encouraging therapeutic potential, ambiguities in MSCs’ mechanism must be addressed as they affect patient prognosis. A key concern is MSCs’ concentration and administration route. Local transplantation leads to higher MSC concentrations in lesions versus systemic administration, enhancing therapeutic effect but increasing cross-talk with precancerous stem cells (pCSCs). These pCSCs are recognised as cancer stem cell precursors.[49] MSCs induce EMT properties in pCSCs through TGF-β1-mediated activation of Zeb1, Twist, Slug, and Snail, promoting malignant progression.[50] OSMF tissues show elevated pro-fibrotic and inflammatory mediators, including IL-1 and TGF-β1, compared to normal mucosa. Higher TGF-β1 could drive MSCs’ transdifferentiation into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), facilitating tumour progression.[51]

MSCs release PGE-2 and COX-2 in response to IL-1-rich microenvironment, inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-8, CXCL1, RANTES, and GRO-α that enhance stemness traits. PGE-2 upregulates β-catenin and activates PI3K-AKT signalling, promoting angiogenesis and tumour cell proliferation. MSCs also secrete matrix metalloproteinases that remodel extracellular matrix, potentially fostering malignant transformation.[48495051]

While MSCs may benefit OSMF, their adverse effects must be considered. Clinical translation requires evaluation of therapeutic and pro-oncogenic actions. Studies should stratify OSMF cases by dysplasia severity and invasion.[52] This will help realise MSC therapy’s potential while addressing tumour risks. PR noted stem cells’ dual roles in OSMF progression and therapy. Cancer stem cells increase malignancy risk, while mesenchymal stem cells modulate fibrosis. Research continues to optimise cell sources and delivery methods.

The panel recognised the promise of stem cell therapies in OSMF for reversing fibrosis and promoting tissue regeneration while emphasising the need for ethical oversight, safety, standardised protocols, and rigorous clinical trials before clinical use.

CONSENSUS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

PAR and KD summarised the consensus and the future directions. They highlighted that the expert panel recognised OSMF as a chronic, progressive, and potentially malignant disorder strongly associated with areca nut use. The disease is multifactorial, with fibrosis driven by TGF-β-mediated collagen accumulation, genetic susceptibility (polymorphisms in TGF-β1, MMPs, and GSTs), oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and EMT.

OSMF carries a 2–10% risk of malignant transformation, with OSCC often less aggressive but surgically challenging. Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL) is increasingly observed as a high-risk lesion in these patients. Early detection, patient education, and regular monitoring are essential to reduce malignancy risk.

The panel highlighted the therapeutic potential of photobiomodulation (PBM) as a safe, non-invasive adjunct to both medical and surgical management. Targeting EMT pathways and stem cell-based regenerative approaches were identified as promising strategies for fibrosis reversal and functional restoration, though ethical, regulatory, and translational challenges remain. A call for standardised multicentric research collaborations was strongly endorsed.

Finally, public health interventions were emphasised, including OSMF awareness campaigns, integration into tobacco- and areca-nut cessation programs, early-screening initiatives, and stricter regulation of areca-nut products for prevention of this disease.

CONCLUSION

The symposium facilitated a constructive exchange of evidence and experience among experts in Oral Pathology and OSMF research. The discussions and consensus provide a unified framework for approaching OSMF from both clinical and research perspectives. with an emphasis on translational and preventive strategies. Emphasis was placed on early detection, patient education, and multimodal management, including physiotherapy, antifibrotic agents, surgery, and photobiomodulation (PBM). The importance of translational research, including biomarkers, EMT-targeted therapies, and regenerative approaches, was highlighted. Public health strategies—such as awareness campaigns, cessation programs, and regulatory measures—are critical for prevention. Collaborative initiatives, multicentric studies, and position papers were identified as essential for standardising diagnosis and treatment. Finally, sustained research efforts are required to unravel the complexities of this enigmatic, indigenous disease and develop sustainable management solutions.
