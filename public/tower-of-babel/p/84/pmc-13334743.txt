Safety and efficacy of chest tube clamping versus non-clamping prior to removal: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mehdi Kazemloo, Fatemeh Naseri Rad, Leila Goudarzimehr, Mohammadsadra Shamohammadi, Omid Bahrami, Ali Heidari, Matin Emami, Mohammad Baadkoube Hazaveh, Danyal Yarahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundManaging chest tubes before removal remains a controversial clinical issue. Clamping a chest tube before removal is a common practice and is often used to assess latent air leaks.MethodsWe systematically searched MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science from inception to 11 February 2026 for studies comparing clamping versus no clamping before chest-tube removal. Two reviewers independently screened and extracted data. Random effect meta-analyses used REML with Hartung-Knapp adjustment, pooling risk ratios for pneumothorax and re-intervention, and mean differences (days) for hospital length of stay.ResultsThe results of this study show no significant differences between the clamping vs. no-clamping in terms of pneumothorax/hemothorax recurrence, re-intervention rates, or hospital length of stay.ConclusionWithin different causes of applying chest tube/ chest drain, routine clamping before chest-tube removal did not reduce early recurrence or the need for re-intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-026-01283-1.

Introduction

Chest tube insertion remains a vital intervention for managing pleural space pathologies, including hemothorax, pneumothorax, empyema, and postoperative pleural drainage [1]. Chest tube management remains an important component of thoracic care, and it impacts complication rates, the course of recovery, and clinical outcomes [2]. There are significant differences in chest tube management practices, including water seal compared to suction, drainage duration, and criteria for tube removal [3, 4].

Removing the chest tube is clinically important, as early or inappropriate removal may result in serious complications such as recurrence of pneumothorax, while unnecessary delay increases infection risk, the length of hospital stay, and patient distress [5]. It is common to clamp the chest tube before removal in some centers, aiming to detect latent air leakage [6, 7]. However, trial clamping before removal remains an ongoing controversy, due to risks of tension pneumothorax and prolonged hospitalization compared to instant removal with the possibility of reinsertion, and approaches are broadly different across centers [8, 9].

Becker et al. conducted a prospective observational study suggesting that clamping might reduce pneumothorax recurrence, although this effect did not reach statistical significance [4]. Also, Chan et al. showed that clamping may potentially prevent re-intervention in some cases. While there was a small increase in early recurrences, clamping was considered to be safe due to the absence of complications like subcutaneous emphysema or tension pneumothorax [10]. In contrast, Rasheed et al. found that the recurrence rates of pneumothorax were not significantly different between the clamping and the control group [11]. Similarly, the study by De Boer et al. reported that trial clamping before removing the chest tube did not reduce the complication rates and led to delayed removal and increased patient distress [12].

Different guidelines and strategies exist on whether chest tubes should be clamped before removal. The American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) published a Delphi consensus statement in 2001 on the management of spontaneous pneumothorax. It advises against routine clamping of chest tubes prior to removal, due to insufficient evidence to support the practice and potential risks associated with clamping [13]. The British Thoracic Society’s 2010 clinical guideline on spontaneous pneumothorax recommends avoiding routine chest tube clamping before removal and applying it selectively with careful monitoring [14].

Although recent studies have attempted to develop new clinical approaches, there are no guidelines for managing chest tubes before removal. As a result, this practice is mostly guided by institutional preference rather than standardized evidence-based guidelines [15, 16]. Previous systematic reviews have discussed chest tube duration or drainage thresholds [17–19]. However, to our knowledge, our study is the first to comprehensively synthesize data on clamping versus non-clamping strategies. Given the variability in clinical practice and the absence of clear guidelines, our study examines whether clamping prior to removal results in any clinical benefit or presents avoidable risks, and evaluates the safety and efficacy of this practice.

Methods

Study design

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [20] (as shown in Fig. 1). Our study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (Registration Number: CRD420251055507).

Search strategy

We searched for observational and randomized clinical trials evaluating chest tube clamping versus no clamping prior to removal. The databases in which we searched are PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase databases from inception to 11 February 2026. The keywords used for the search are: (“chest drain” OR “chest tube”) AND (“clamp” OR “clamping”) (as shown in Table S1). In addition, supplementary searching was performed through Google Scholar and backward snowballing of the reference lists of eligible studies and relevant reviews, according to established snowballing guidance [21]. One author did the searching across the databases, and two authors independently screened the records for eligibility. After selecting eligible studies, five authors independently extracted the data from studies that met the study’s PICO. Two authors resolved the discrepancies. We restricted eligibility to full-text articles published in English; no geographic restrictions were applied.

Eligibility criteria

Observational or clinical studies that had two parallel groups with a clear statement of performing chest tube clamping only for one group and having comparable data for at least one of the study’s outcomes were included. Other study types, including case reports/series, reviews and meta-analyses, study protocols, and letters, are excluded. We excluded non-comparative designs and studies without extractable group-level outcome data; otherwise, the population was not restricted by etiology or clinical setting. The study followed PICO (patient, intervention, comparison, outcome) principles for the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Population (P)

Studies involving patients who had undergone chest tube placement (tube thoracostomy) for various indications, regardless of underlying etiology or clinical setting.

Intervention (I)

Chest tube clamping prior to removal, including any form of clamping such as intermittent clamping or clamping trials.

Comparator (C)

No clamping prior to chest tube removal.

Outcomes (O)

Outcomes (O): (1) recurrence of pneumothorax or hemothorax following chest tube removal; (2) requirement for re-intervention, such as chest tube reinsertion or additional pleural drainage; (3) duration of hospital stay; and (4) duration of chest tube placement. The chest tube duration is most likely affected by the baseline characteristics of patients and is an exploratory outcome rather than a primary outcome and must be interpreted as a descriptive outcome.

Risk of bias assessment

The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool, version 2 (RoB 2), and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool were used for methodological quality assessment of randomized clinical trials and observational studies, respectively, by two reviewers independently [22, 23] (shown in Figures S1-S2).

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. The certainty of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low according to five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative syntheses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2) using the meta and metafor packages. Dichotomous outcomes (recurrence of pneumothorax/hemothorax after tube removal and the need for pleural re-intervention) were summarized as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous outcomes (hospital length of stay and chest-tube duration) were pooled as mean differences (MDs) in days. When continuous outcomes were reported as medians with interquartile ranges, the sample mean was estimated using the method described by Luo et al., and the standard deviation was estimated using the method described by Wan et al., allowing consistent pooling across studies [24, 25].

For each outcome, we fitted both a common-effects (inverse-variance) model and a random effect model. The random effect estimates were treated as primary because variation across studies was expected (e.g., differences in indications for tube placement, removal thresholds, and local postoperative pathways). Between-study variance (τ²) was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), and random effect CIs were computed using the Hartung-Knapp adjustment to provide more reliable inference when the number of studies is modest. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using I² (reported as percentages) and τ², and formally assessed with Cochran’s Q test. Where appropriate, prediction intervals were also derived to reflect the range of effects that might be expected in a comparable future setting.

Binary meta-analyses were performed on the log(RR) scale and then back-transformed for reporting. Studies with zero events in both arms were excluded from RR pooling because they do not inform the relative effect; when a single arm contained zero events, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied. Forest plots were generated in a RevMan-style format, presenting study-level effects and displaying both common-effects and random effect weights.

Small-study effects were explored using funnel plots, complemented by Egger’s regression; trim-and-fill was applied as a sensitivity analysis, interpreted cautiously given limited power with few studies. Robustness and influence were examined via leave-one-out refitting and Baujat diagnostics [26], including decomposition of the Q statistic to identify studies contributing most to heterogeneity and to the pooled effect.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 1218 unique records were retrieved through database searches, Scopus (n = 382), Embase (n = 386), Web of Science (n = 267), and PubMed (n = 181), along with 11 additional records identified from other sources (two from websites and nine through backward snowballing of reference lists). After 418 duplicates were removed, 798 titles and abstracts were screened; 747 were excluded. Forty‑eight full‑text articles were reviewed for eligibility, and forty of these were excluded because of non‑comparative design, insufficient outcome data, or an ineligible patient population. Nine further records located through supplementary methods underwent full‑text assessment, and eight were excluded. Ultimately, nine studies met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

The nine included studies (N = 2457) comprised five retrospective cohorts, three randomized clinical trials (Table 1). Mean age varied between 32.9 and 62.0 years, and male representation ranged from 37.8% to 92.6%. Geographically, two studies were conducted in the USA, three in China, and one each in the United Kingdom, Netherlands, Japan, and Pakistan. Intervention protocols, specifically the duration and technique of clamping, and baseline risk factors differed moderately across studies; these sources of heterogeneity were examined in sensitivity and subgroup analysis. Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated separately for randomized and non-randomized evidence (Figures S1-S2). Among the randomized trials assessed with RoB 2 (Figure S1), two studies were judged low risk of bias across domains, supporting good internal validity. One trial showed some concerns overall, driven mainly by potential deviations from the intended intervention, while other domains remained reassuring.

For non-randomized studies assessed with ROBINS-I (Figure S2), most were rated low risk of bias, indicating generally credible outcome ascertainment and reporting. However, two observational studies were judged moderate risk of bias, reflecting typical vulnerabilities in participant selection and residual confounding. Notably, Veale et al. (2025) [27] was rated serious risk of bias overall, primarily due to confounding, with additional moderate concerns across several domains. Collectively, these patterns suggest that the evidence base is strengthened by several low-risk studies but remains sensitive to limitations in specific observational designs.

Outcomes analysis

Pneumothorax/Hemothorax

Six studies (695 clamped, 1054 non-clamped) reported new pneumothorax/hemothorax after chest-tube removal. Random effect pooling yielded a risk ratio of 1.19 (95% CI 0.79–1.77), indicating no significant difference between clamping and non-clamping (Fig. 2). Heterogeneity was low (I² = 11.2%; τ² = 0.0280; p = 0.3439).

Re‑intervention rate

Six studies (893 clamped, 1082 control) contributed re‑intervention data (e.g., reinsertion or aspiration). The pooled risk ratio was 0.88 (95% CI 0.44–1.76; Fig. 3), again showing no significant difference. Heterogeneity remained low (I² =31.0%; τ² = 0.1277, p = 0.2026).

Hospital length of stay (LOS)

Six studies (849 clamped, 1063 control) provided length‑of‑stay data. The pooled mean difference was not significant and near zero difference (MD = 0.00; 95% CI -0.90 to 0.91; Fig. 4), a non‑significant finding accompanied by substantial heterogeneity (I² =81.4%; τ² = 0.6233; p < 0.0001).

Chest tube duration

Seven studies (917 clamped, 1117 control) reported time to tube removal. The pooled mean difference was 0.04 (95% CI -1.22 to 1.30; Fig. 5), reflecting virtually identical durations. Heterogeneity was high (I² = 90.3%; τ² =1.1708; p < 0.0001), consistent with wide variation in protocols and patient characteristics. Because the chest tube duration is mostly affected by the patients’ characteristics, it must be interpreted as only a descriptive outcome and not as a primary outcome.

Summary of meta-analyses

Across every endpoint evaluated, pre‑removal chest‑tube clamping exerted no measurable influence on patient safety or efficiency metrics. Post‑removal pneumothorax rates and re‑intervention requirements were essentially equivalent in clamped and non‑clamped cohorts, with little variability among studies. Clamping likewise failed to affect time to tube removal or overall length of hospitalization, although these continuous endpoints displayed greater heterogeneity, likely reflecting protocol and population differences. Taken together, the evidence indicates a uniform null effect of clamping across both binary and continuous outcomes (Table 2).

Certainty of evidence

Using GRADE, the certainty of evidence was low for pneumothorax/hemothorax, re-intervention, hospital LOS, and chest tube duration, mainly because of risk of bias and imprecision, with possible small-study effects for pneumothorax/hemothorax (Table S4).

Publication bias analyses

Small-study effects were examined using funnel plots, complemented by Egger’s regression, and supported by trim-and-fill as a sensitivity approach. Figure 6 suggests broadly symmetrical distributions for re-intervention, hospital length of stay, and chest-tube duration, and Egger’s tests were clearly non-significant for these outcomes (p = 0.956, p = 0.918, and p = 0.521, respectively). In contrast, the pneumothorax/hemothorax endpoint showed a statistically significant Egger signal (p = 0.0179), raising the possibility of funnel plot asymmetry and small-study effects for this outcome. This pattern warrants caution rather than alarm. With only six to seven studies contributing to most analyses, the power of asymmetry tests remains limited, and visual interpretation can be unstable.

Influence and sensitivity analyses

To evaluate robustness, we combined influence diagnostics with leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity testing (Table S2 and Figures S3-S6). Baujat plots (Fig. 7) map each study’s contribution to heterogeneity (x-axis) against its influence on the pooled estimate (y-axis), highlighting whether variability is driven by a single outlier or reflects broader between-study differences. For pneumothorax/hemothorax, heterogeneity was modest and mainly concentrated in a small subset of studies (with the largest contributor accounting for ~ 35% of Cochran’s Q), yet the pooled inference remained stable. Each study’s contribution to heterogeneity is shown in Table S3. Study-level decomposition of Cochran’s Q identified the main contributors to heterogeneity for each outcome: Chan et al. (2021) for pneumothorax/hemothorax, Becker et al. (2020) for re-intervention and hospital length of stay, and Veale et al. (2025) for chest-tube duration. For hospital length of stay and chest-tube duration, heterogeneity was more dispersed across several cohorts rather than dominated by a single influential study, consistent with protocol and population variation.

LOO analyses showed that omitting any individual study yielded overlapping confidence intervals and did not shift the interpretation away from a null effect. A comparable pattern was observed for re-intervention: one trial contributed roughly one-third of the heterogeneity signal, but sequential exclusion did not materially change the summary estimate. Continuous outcomes behaved differently. For hospital length of stay and chest tube duration, heterogeneity was more dispersed across several cohorts rather than dominated by a single influential study, consistent with protocol and population variation. Importantly, LOO results kept pooled mean differences close to zero, while high residual heterogeneity persisted, supporting robustness of direction, but also signaling genuine clinical diversity across settings.

Subgroup analysis

According to the etiology of chest tube implantation, one of the most or the most important routine etiology is implanting chest tube for pneumothorax or hemothorax. Therefore, we conducted a subgroup analysis based on this specific etiology and the results of pooled meta-analysis did not change and none of the outcomes became significant, indicating the fact when we consider the etiology of chest tube implantation, the direction of effects do not change, showing the robustness of our results (Table 2 and Figure S7-S10).

Discussion

Principal findings

Managing chest tubes before removal remains a controversial clinical issue, with practices varying widely across institutions. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the safety and effectiveness of clamping versus not clamping before chest tube removal, combining data from nine studies involving 2,457 patients. The results showed no significant differences between the two approaches in pneumothorax/hemothorax recurrence (RR 1.19, 95% CI 0.795–1.769), re-intervention rates (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.438–1.760), chest-tube duration (MD 0.04 days, 95% CI -1.216 to 1.304), or length of hospitalization (MD 0.00 days, 95% CI -0.905 to 0.908). This finding aligns with the study by Rasheed et al., who indicated that the frequency of recurrent pneumothorax in the two groups was not statistically significant and it is unnecessary to clamp prior to removal [11].

Chest tube management remains an important focus in thoracic surgical care [3, 29]. Although it is the current standard intervention in the majority of thoracic injuries, limited research has been done to address the most effective protocols for chest tube removal [1, 11]. Clamping chest tubes before their removal is a widely controversial procedure, and typically relies on routine approaches, clinical expertise, and the possibility of recurrent pneumothorax [30].

Clinicians use different methods to manage chest drains across different countries and institutions. Some remove the drain right away and replace it if pneumothorax returns [12, 31]. Others clamp the drain and observe, allowing quick action if needed [32].

The heterogeneity pattern appears to reflect clinical and methodological diversity rather than a truly divergent effect of clamping. Chan et al. (2021), which contributed most to heterogeneity for pneumothorax/hemothorax, focused on spontaneous pneumothorax, a population with a different baseline risk profile and post-removal surveillance pathway compared with trauma or postoperative cohorts [10]. Becker et al. (2020), the main contributor for re-intervention and hospital length of stay, was conducted in a trauma setting, where decisions regarding repeat pleural intervention, imaging, and discharge are strongly influenced by local practice patterns [4]. Veale et al. (2025), which contributed most to heterogeneity for chest-tube duration, was a multicenter observational study, and this outcome is particularly vulnerable to variation in institutional removal protocols, perioperative pathways, and case-mix. Taken together, these findings suggest that the observed heterogeneity is more plausibly explained by differences in clinical context and management strategy than by an inconsistent effect of clamping itself [27].

Outcome 1: Recurrence of pneumothorax/hemothorax

In thoracic trauma, a randomized trial found that early recurrence after removal did not differ significantly between those who received a clamp trial and those who did not (10% vs. 4.5%), arguing that clamping is unnecessary for detecting early recurrence in this context [11]. In spontaneous pneumothorax, a large retrospective cohort reported more 24‑hour recurrences after clamping (17.6%) than without clamping (7.4%), while noting that clamping saved a reinsertion in ~ 12% of episodes; importantly, no tension pneumothorax occurred during clamp trials in that series [10]. Trauma‑center experience likewise shows that, despite a protocolized 6‑hour clamp with imaging, 9.7% developed recurrence or symptoms during the clamp and 6.7% required reinsertion after “passing” the trial, highlighting imperfect negative predictive value [33]. In patients managed with digital drainage, a randomized non‑inferiority trial demonstrated similar 24‑hour and 30‑day recurrence between no‑clamp removal and clamp‑trial strategies; a notable fraction of clamp trials led to progression of pneumothorax and resumed drainage [12]. Overall, these data suggest that routine clamping does not reliably reduce early recurrence and may precipitate radiographic progression in a subset. The retrospective study by Imtiaz et al. (2016) involving 2,661 patients showed chest tube removal without clamping was safe, with a low complication rate (2.1%), including 0.67% recurrent pneumothorax and 0.15% tension pneumothorax. It challenges clamping, as their results showed no increased risk when tubes were removed based on clinical criteria alone (e.g., absence of air leak, minimal fluid drainage, lung expansion) [34]. Similarly, Abbasi et al. (2013) conducted a trial in trauma ICU patients and found no difference in complications between early (5–7 days) and late (post-ventilation) removal, regardless of clamping. Their study emphasized that clamping did not reduce pneumothorax recurrence, supporting our meta-analysis [35].

Dezube et al. (2022) studied 200 lung resection patients, finding 55% developed post-pull pneumothorax (PP-PTX), mostly slight/trace, and none needed intervention. Clamping trials linked to higher PP-PTX risk (OR 2.48, p-value = 0.024), indicating clamping may increase complications. Our analysis also showed no benefit of clamping in preventing recurrence or re-intervention [36].

Outcome 2: Need for re-intervention

Evidence on re-intervention is mixed and context-dependent. In trauma, a large adjusted cohort associated clamp trials with fewer subsequent invasive pleural drainages (adjusted OR ≈ 0.41), without documented tension events, although this association attenuated when calendar year was added, suggesting potential confounding by evolving practice [4]. Conversely, in the randomized trauma trial, reinsertion needs did not differ significantly between clamped and non-clamped groups [11]. In spontaneous pneumothorax, clamping occasionally spared reinsertion (≈ 12%) but coincided with more early recurrences, many of which still necessitated additional imaging or monitoring [10]. In the digital-drainage randomized trial, 24-hour reinsertion was rare and statistically non-inferior with no-clamp removal (0/44 vs. 1/39), supporting omission of clamp when objective air-flow thresholds are satisfied [12].

Outcome 3: Chest‑tube duration

One surgical cohort (propensity‑matched) found that intermittent clamping, integrated into a structured post‑lobectomy protocol, shortened drainage by ≈ 0.9 days versus traditional management [37]. A subsequent randomized trial in carefully selected lobectomy patients without air leak confirmed shorter chest‑tube duration (median 2 vs. 3 days) and lower cumulative drainage with intermittent clamping, without excess adverse events [38]. In contrast, when removal is guided by digital air‑flow (< 20 mL/min) and fluid (< 250 mL/day) criteria, clamping did not shorten chest‑tube duration compared with direct removal, and some clamp trials prolonged drainage due to radiographic progression during the clamp [12]. Early “provocative” clamping for high‑grade air leaks after lung resection appears feasible with close observation, but these data are single‑center and descriptive [29]. Taken together, any duration benefit seems protocol‑ and population‑specific (elective surgery pathways), not a general effect of clamping.

Outcome 4: Length of stay

Mirroring drainage duration, a surgical series reported a modest reduction in postoperative stay with intermittent clamping in matched analysis (approximately 0.7 days) [37], while a randomized trial without air leak did not target length of stay as a primary endpoint but showed no safety trade‑offs accompanying a 1‑day reduction in median drainage time [38]. In the digital‑drainage randomized trial spanning post‑surgical and pneumothorax patients, hospital stay was comparable between clamp and no‑clamp groups, underscoring that digital criteria can streamline removal without extra inpatient time [12].

Clinical implications

First, routine clamping before chest-tube removal is not required for most patients when clear, objective removal criteria are applied (e.g., digital air-flow and fluid-output thresholds). This approach is non-inferior for preventing early reinsertion and avoids clamp-related radiographic progression observed in a minority during trial clamping [12]. Second, intermittent or provocative clamping can be considered within well-supervised thoracic-surgical pathways, particularly where teams have demonstrated reduced drainage time without safety penalties, but clinicians should recognize the population-specific nature of these gains [37, 38]. Third, in the outpatient management of prolonged air leaks using one-way valves, complications (including empyema and readmissions) are not trivial; in this scenario, a brief, supervised clamp trial before removal may remain prudent as part of a risk-managed protocol [39]. Finally, in spontaneous pneumothorax, clamping appears safe under proper monitoring, but may increase early recurrences; the decision to clamp should therefore be individualized rather than routine [10].

Shimoda et al. assessed a pre-removal clamping test in primary spontaneous pneumothorax and found that a subset re-collapsed during clamping, which effectively functioned as an early stress test while the drain remained in situ; incomplete re-expansion, visible lung fluctuation, and smoking history were highlighted as potential indicators for selective use [28]. Veale et al., in a multicenter cohort, reported that clamping was common but was not associated with fewer reinsertion events or lower short-term recurrence, and it could delay removal when applied routinely [27]. Together, these data support a nuanced approach: clamping may be most defensible as a targeted safety check in higher-risk patients rather than a universal step. Clamping a chest tube before removal is a common practice and is often used to assess latent air leaks. However, our results show that this practice does not improve patient outcomes.

Limitations

There are several limitations to the current research. Our study included fewer than 10 studies per outcome. Therefore, we assessed publication bias using trim-and-fill funnel plots with Egger’s test, potentially underestimating small-study effects or selective publication bias. Additionally, the high heterogeneity in continuous outcomes suggests variability in study protocols and patient characteristics. This might hide subtle effects of clamping in certain subgroups. Furthermore, inconsistencies in clamping duration and techniques across studies complicate interpretation. For example, Becker et al. used prolonged clamping while others implemented brief trials, which may result in different outcomes [4].

Future directions

Multicenter randomized trials should stratify by indication and air‑leak severity, compare digital‑guided no‑clamp removal against protocolized clamp strategies, and include patient‑centered outcomes (pain, mobility, anxiety), resource use (imaging, nursing workload), and health‑economic endpoints. Trials embedded in outpatient one‑way‑valve pathways are also needed to refine safe removal protocols and mitigate infectious risk [39].

Conclusion

Across the included comparative studies spanning trauma, spontaneous pneumothorax, and post‑operative cohorts, a routine clamp trial before chest‑tube removal did not confer a consistent reduction in early recurrence or need for re‑intervention. Signals in specific surgical pathways (e.g., protocolized intermittent or “provocative” clamping) suggest possible operational advantages for selected patients, but findings were not uniform and were sensitive to setting and protocol. Contemporary randomized evidence with digital drainage indicates that omission of clamping is non‑inferior for safety when objective air‑flow criteria are met. Clinicians should tailor their decisions to each patient’s specific circumstances instead of following routine protocols, promoting a more evidence-based approach to chest tube management.
