Mesenchymal Stem Cell Utilization for In Vitro Donor Liver Machine Perfusion Preservation: Current Status and Future Directions
Authors: Yu Fu, Yi Wang, Liwei Liang, Mingzi Gu, Yi Gao, Lei Feng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver transplantation is the only effective treatment for end-stage liver disease. Currently, the shortage of high-quality donors has led to the exploration of the use of marginal organs. However, several factors limit the in vitro long-term preservation and long-distance transport of livers, which can also lead to ischemia-reperfusion injuries, resulting in poor prognosis. Therefore, an efficient and convenient strategy to improve this situation is urgently required. Normothermic machine perfusion (NMP) is expected to improve the liver environment in vitro and provide better evaluation indices for organ repair mechanisms. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) can repair damaged hepatocytes or exert their protective effects via paracrine mechanisms, such as the release of extracellular vesicles (EVs). We hypothesized that combining the regenerative ability of MSCs and the significant advantages of NMP may improve the quality and utilization rate of organs, especially marginal organs. In this study, we review different strategies for liver preservation in vitro, as well as their strengths and weaknesses. We also introduce MSCs, derived EVs, and MSCs applications in liver preservation in vitro. Finally, we discuss the current challenges and future trends of MSCs applications for in vitro liver preservation. We envision novel bioreactor designs that employ 3D cell culturing and offer the possibility to reconstruct MSCs microenvironments to promote cell growth and biofunction expression. Large-scale MSCs production can be combined with normothermic machine perfusion to enhance in vitro liver preservation, thereby promoting donor organ function to benefit recipients in need of liver transplantation.Keywords: mesenchymal stem cells, ischemia-reperfusion injuries, normothermic machine perfusion, liver preservation, liver transplantation

Significance Statement

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) had shown great therapeutic potential for tissue damage repair. Moreover, the use of MSCs in liver preservation also shown great therapeutic potential. In this review, we present current literature regarding different strategies for in vitro liver preservation and investigate the potential application of MSCs and derived EVs as an innovative therapeutic tool to precondition grafts before transplantation as well as to prevent ischemia-reperfusion injury.

Introduction

Liver transplantation is the only viable treatment for end-stage liver disease and its success depends on the 3 cornerstones, effective in vitro liver preservation, vascular surgery, and immunosuppression.1 In the past 10 years, there has been an increase in the number of liver operations, and the demand for donors remains urgent. Owing to this high demand, doctors have lowered donor selection standards and now make use of marginal organs,2 including those obtained from donors after cardiac death (DCD) and expanded criteria donors.3 Simultaneously, the long-term preservation and long-distance transport of donor livers may result in ischemia-reperfusion injuries (IRIs) during transplantation. Therefore, efficient donor organ preservation is a prerequisite for successful liver transplantation.

Currently, several techniques are used to improve the quality of expanded criteria donor and discarded kidneys to render them suitable for transplantation. In 1812, Dr. Le Gallois was the first to propose maintaining organ activity in vitro through the circulatory system; however, the duration of organ vitality using this process was short, and complications, such as bacterial infection, were not addressed.4 Carrel proposed in situ and in vitro perfusion in 1935 and successfully developed an equipment for conducting multiple organ perfusions.5 In the 1980s, machine perfusion (MP) combined with static cold storage (SCS) gradually emerged with the support of improved surgical techniques, the implementation of perioperative nursing care, and the application of immunosuppressive drugs.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), which can be isolated from diverse tissues, can exert regenerative effects on damaged tissues, including liver tissue.6 More than 1000 MSC-related studies were registered at http://clinicaltrials.gov in October 2022, and some show promising results in the treatment of liver injury from different etiologies. MSCs not only replace damaged cells but also exert protective effects via their ­anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antiapoptotic, antifibrotic, and proangiogenic properties by means of paracrine mechanisms, such as the release of extracellular vesicles (EVs), growth factors, and cytokines.7

Although important breakthroughs have been realized regarding in vitro liver preservation and MSCs culture methods, the application of MSC treatment in this context remains at the laboratory research level and early clinical trial phases. Indeed, some of the challenges that still limit the development of large-scale MSC-based treatment for liver preservation in clinical practices (Fig. 3) include: (a) Cell sources and a suitable bioreactor: The lack of suitable cell sources and problematic designs of bioreactors with respect to mass transfer efficiency and microenvironment construction lead to unsatisfactory clinical outcomes for MSC applications. (b) Technical challenges: Although EVs derived from MSCs show broad therapeutic prospects, their clinical application is limited by technical challenges, such as achieving large-scale isolation and purification and increasing overall yield. (c) Standardization and cost issues. MSCs comprise living cells, which are strictly regulated in terms of industrial and commercial use. Further, their application in liver preservation in vitro is also intensive with respect to in time and cost.

In vitro liver preservation

Currently, SCS, the gold standard for liver preservation, is a commonly used method that retains donor organs at 0-4 °C. Traditional organ preservation solutions, such as University of Wisconsin (UW), hypertonic citrate adenine, histidine–tryptophan–ketoglutarate, and Celsior solutions, were specifically designed with SCS in mind.8 Further studies in recent years have focused on the development of new organ preservation solutions, as well as the optimization of those already in use. The addition of active ingredients, such as the ubiquitin proteasome system9 and hormones10 in UW solution can reduce hepatic IRI. Further, adding AP39, fucoidan, and hydrogen sulfide to Institut Georges Lopez-1 solution has also effectively reduced liver damage and improved liver function.11 Cryopreservation, in which donor organs are stored at temperatures between -6 and 0 °C, is another emerging technology. De Vries et al12 reported that the viability of human livers remains unchanged before and after supercooling. Rapid organ cooling slows metabolism and minimizes the effects of thermal ischemia; however, the graft still faces complications, such as cellular edema, acidosis, mitochondrial damage, energy deficiency, and reperfusion injury under cold preservation (Table 1).17

These issues have led to the development of donor liver machine perfusion (MP) (Fig. 1), a novel procedure in terms of liver preservation. MP systems, which consists of a machine pump, preservation solution, perfusion circuit, temperature control device, and an oxygen carrier, are categorized according to different perfusion temperatures used as: hypothermic machine perfusion (HMP, 4-6 °C), normothermic machine perfusion (NMP, 37 °C), and subnormothermic machine perfusion (SNMP, 20-25 °C) systems.18 HMP retains the features of SCS and offers high safety to patients. SCS can still preserve organs in vitro efficiently in the event of MP equipment failure.19,20 At low temperatures, the liver has low metabolic activity and low oxygen consumption, which minimize energy consumption and reduce the impact of metabolic waste on the organ, thus preserving liver activity.21 However, a low-temperature environment also increases damage caused by cold ischemia.22 SNMP may play the role of an intermediate bridge by not only satisfying metabolic needs to ensure organ vitality evaluation, but also by improving graft function.23,24Berendsen et al25 used SNMP to resuscitate rat liver that had been cryopreserved at −6 °C for 4 days and then successfully used it to performed liver transplantation. The authors improved their protocol, and this ultimately allowed the extension of human liver vitality by 27 h.12 NMP maintains the normal metabolism of donor organs in a near-physiological state and promotes repair of damaged tissues.26 By continuously providing oxygen and nutrients, NMP technology restores the energy reserve of the donor organ and reverses damage caused by ischemia and reperfusion.17 In addition, the biochemical indicators of the perfusion solution can be continuously monitored and liver function can be improved through intervention measures. Nasralla et al16 completed a randomized trial with 220 liver transplantations and reported that NMP is associated with a 50% lower level of both graft injury and organ discard and a 54% longer mean organ preservation time compared to SCS; however, there was no significant difference in bile duct complications, graft survival, or patient survival between the 2 systems. Eshmuminov et al27 used 7 clinically abandoned livers to conduct NMP for 1 week. Among these, 6 livers were well preserved in terms of function and structural integrity, which represents the longest duration of liver preservation via MP reported to date. NMP has advantages over cold preservation in terms of coagulation factors and protein synthesis, bile secretion, and reduced IRI; however, NMP requires more oxygen and energy to maintain liver function; this leads to an increase in liver metabolites. An ideal organ preservation technique should not only preserve the isolated donor organs but also provide valid recuperation and functional capacity before liver transplantation. The efficient combination of NMP with other treatment modes is a critical challenge that requires attention.

Different Therapeutic Strategies for Liver NMP

The perfusion solution used in NMP usually includes a crystal or colloidal solution, oxygen carrier, calcium, a broad-spectrum antibiotic, insulin, and heparin.28-30 Frequently, drugs are added to improve the functioning of the donor liver. In pig donor livers, Nassar et al31 used prostacyclin, which significantly enhanced vascular microcirculation and improved liver structure after 10 h of NMP. Beal et al32 added endorphins to NMP solutions to stimulate the opioid receptor signaling pathway. This prevented mitochondrial dysfunction and increased the expression of prosurvival/anti-apoptotic signaling pathways. Research on gene therapy using NMP platforms is still at the early stages. It has also been observed that gene expression response is faster under homeostasis provided by NMP.33 Some researchers have used adenovirus vectors in gene therapy. Specifically, Gillooly et al34 used siRNA against Fas receptors (involved in apoptosis) for 4 h during mouse liver NMP and confirmed that siRNA was ingested by endothelial cells and central veins, thereby reducing injury to the donor liver. The preservation effect of gas perfusion has also been verified in animal donor lungs35,36; however, studies on its use for the preservation of donor livers, for which clinical trials have been unsuccessful due to the possibility of gas embolism occurring after graft transplantation, are limited. Laing et al37 added human bone marrow-derived adult multipotent progenitor cells to the preservation solution, and after 6 h of perfusion, observed no adverse reactions and the liver structure was significantly better than was the case before perfusion. In addition to the above additives, it has recently been found that adding MSCs to NMP can better protect and restore liver function.

Characteristics of MSCs and Derivatives

Mesenchymal stem cells are multipotent cells which are abundant in the mesenchymal tissue of various organs and tissues, such as the placenta, umbilical cord, bone marrow, and adipose tissue. They can differentiate into adipocytes, osteocytes, chondrocytes, and other cell types under the stimulation of specific signals and the appropriate local microenvironment.38 Phenotypically, MSCs are recognized by the expression of surface markers, CD105, CD73, and CD90, and an absence of the expression of CD34, CD19, CD45, CD11a, CD31, and HLA-DR. Early research on the mechanisms of stem cell function mainly focused on their differentiation potential and tissue regeneration ability.39 Studies have shown that MSCs have a good immunomodulatory effect that can inhibit tissue damage caused by excessive inflammation.40 They can exert a powerful immunosuppressive function through paracrine action, inhibit the proliferation of activated lymphocytes, and stimulate the induction of regulatory immune cells.41 To activate the endogenous cell repair program, MSCs can also release various growth factors, such as hepatocyte growth factor, fibroblast growth factor, keratinocyte growth factor, erythropoietin, epidermal growth factor, insulin-like growth factor, single nuclear cell chemoattractant protein-1, and stromal cell-derived factor-1,.42,43 Further, emerging evidence indicates that MSC-derived EVs can modulate several pathways responsible for IRIs, including inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, fibrosis, and microvascular remodeling (Fig. 2).44,45

There are 2 main steps for obtaining a purified MSC-EV sample. First, the MSC-EVs are separated from cellular debris and larger vesicles in the MSC-conditioned medium. To achieve this, low centrifugation and dead-end and tangential flow filtration can be performed. Second, the MSC-EVs are purified to remove non-EV content. This can be achieved using several techniques, eg, polymer precipitation, which is based on decreasing EV solubility and allowing isolation via lower g-force centrifugation. Dead-end filtration can also be used. This technique relies on flow perpendicular to the filter surface. Additionally, a wide variety of chromatography-based techniques can be used. Particularly, size-exclusion chromatography can be used to purify MSC-EVs based on size, while anion exchange and affinity chromatography rely on the negative MSC-EV charge and the use of specific ligands to capture MSC-EVs, respectively. Recent purification methods based on filtration have been developed. Asymmetrical flow field-flow fractionation, which is based on the asymmetrical flow of the filter surface, has also been reported. The most used filtration technique for this purpose is tangential flow filtration, wherein the flow goes parallel to the filter.

Potential mechanisms of MSC-EVs-mediated-attenuation of liver IRI

There is growing evidence that several factors, such as oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium overload, microvascular dysfunction, and the activation of cell death pathways, participate in IRI pathogenesis.46-48 Yao et al49 showed that the mechanism by which EVs reduce oxidative stress in a hepatic IRI model involves the enzyme, manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD), which has anti-apoptosis and anti-oxidation ability, being encapsulated in ­huc-MSCs-EVs. Further, Du et al50 demonstrated that ­hiPSC-MSCs-Exo could alleviate hepatic I/R injury via the activation of sphingosine kinase and the ­sphingosine-1-phosphate pathway in hepatocytes and the promotion of cell proliferation. Nong et al51 found that hiPSC-MSCs-Exo administration resulted in a marked suppression of hepatocyte necrosis and sinusoidal congestion, significantly lower levels of AST, ALT, and inflammatory markers, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-6 and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). Further, Haga et al52 reasoned that EVs from bone marrow-derived MSCs increase hepatic NACHT, LRR, and PYD domain-containing protein 12, and chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 1 messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels and reduced the mRNA expression levels of several inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 during IRI. MSC-EVs was also found to increase cell viability and suppress both oxidative injury and nuclear factor kappa B activity in murine hepatocytes in vitro.

However, the exact mechanism leading to IRI remains unknown and effective interventions to reduce this injury remain limited. Therefore, it is critical to develop novel therapeutic targets and strategies. Several studies with a focus on the mechanisms of action of MSC-EVs in the treatment of IRI have been conducted owing to their excellent therapeutic potential (Table 2). MSC-EVs improve liver function, decrease the infiltration of neutrophils and inflammatory markers, reduce apoptosis and tissue necrosis, and alleviate oxidative stress.

MSCs and MSC-EVs Therapy in Liver Preservation

Studies have shown that the role of MSCs and their derived EVs in repairing target organs involves a variety of mechanisms, such as alleviating IRI, regulating target cell proliferation, promoting angiogenesis, and inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition.55 Casiraghi et al pointed out that using MSCs to interfere with the donor organ before transplantation can increase the survival rate of the graft compared with that observed without the use of MSCs.56 In most recent studies, MSCs application was investigated as a perfusate additive (Table 3), making use of NMP to maximize the vitality of the MSCs. Rigo et al64 found that human liver stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (HLSC-EVs) treatment, even for a short duration, effectively reduces liver injury during hypoxic NMP. They established an ex vivo murine NMP model in which livers were perfused with HLSC-EVs for 4 h. After perfusion, the proportion of hepatocyte apoptosis markers was significantly reduced, while that of liver function markers increased. Stefano et al65 also found that HLSC-EVs treatment significantly reduced the levels of 2 common liver enzymes, alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST). Moreover, HLSC-EVs enhanced liver metabolism by promoting phosphate utilization and pH self-regulation, and a higher dose of HLSC-EVs was found to be associated with significantly higher bile production, lower intrahepatic resistance, reduced necrosis, and enhanced cell proliferation. These results suggest that a novel cell-free therapeutic strategy targeting paracrine effects can protect the liver in vitro. Unfortunately, no similar reports are available for large animal models.

Challenges of MSCs for Liver Machine Perfusion Preservation

In this section, we focus on possible approaches for overcoming some of the aforementioned challenges. In recent years, tremendous advances have been made in the field of tissue engineering due to emerging biotechnologies. However, few studies have considered utilizing these technologies to improve the efficiency of MSCs treatment for in vitro liver preservation. Therefore, we discuss how biotechnologies can contribute in this regard in terms of the large-scale culturing of MSCs and adaptations in the application methods.

Large-Scale Acquisition of MSCs and MSC-EVs

MSCs production processes can be divided into 2 main types: upstream process development (USP) and downstream process development (DSP).66 On the one hand, during USP, cells are isolated, stored (cell banking), and expanded, and EVs-containing conditioned media are also obtained. Regarding EV production from cell culture, it is important not only to carefully define the best cell source but also the media, growth conditions, sera, type of culture (eg, 2D or 3D), metabolic preconditioning, and other culture conditions. On the other hand, DSP comprises several steps, including separation, cell disruption, capture, and concentration steps, as well as extraction, purification, polishing, and formulation.67 A greater number of MSCs provide better protection of liver function in vitro. To date, the number of MSCs used for such preservation varies greatly, with most studies reporting the application of approximately 1 × 107 cells.57,68 Besides, the sources of MSCs used in these studies differed. The most common sources of MSCs assessed in clinical trials have been umbilical cord, bone marrow, and adipose tissue.69 Although hESC,70 HFMSCs,71 and iPSCs72 have also been used, there are fewer reports on them in relation to liver IRI prevention. Further, the induction schemes used by various teams are inconsistent and large-scale applications to the bioreactor have not been reported.

Even with the latest tissue engineering techniques, at least 1 × 1010 cells are required to achieve an ideal therapeutic effect, the provision of which poses a formidable challenge. Conventional 2D cell cultures on flat substrates have low efficiency and high space utilization, and the scope for nutrient and waste metabolite exchange therein is greatly limited. Therefore, suitable 3D cell culture methods are essential. Bioreactors represent one option for maintaining the viability and stability of MSCs while providing sufficient space for MSCs growth and large-scale culturing (Fig. 3).

At present, studies on MSCs and MSC-EVs for liver preservation are still limited to mouse and rat models. No related large-animal studies have been reported so far, and as a stepping stone toward clinical application in humans, such experiments are indispensable. Thus, another challenge arises, namely, large quantities of MSCs and MSC-EVs are used during animal treatment, and using conventional cell culture and enrichment strategies to meet this quantitative need for clinical treatment is still challenging. Our team has long been committed to investigating large-scale cell culturing and the enrichment of MSC-EVs and to conducting research on bioartificial livers. On the basis of pre-studies, we propose the large-scale cultivation of MSCs in a bioreactor. When the cultivation meets the therapeutic needs, the entire bioreactor can be used in combination with NMP to preserve pig livers during treatment, with EVs continuously being secreted from the bioreactor. Our results in this regard will be published in the near future.

MSCs Application Methods

The method of MSCs application for vitro liver preservation is another core element to consider. To date, the 2 most commonly used techniques in this regard are: adding MSCs directly to the perfusion solution or injecting them into the portal vein. These pose the risk of vascular occlusion, tumorigenesis, and infection.73 In our opinion, culturing MSCs in a bioreactor can avoid the adverse reactions associated with cells entering vasculature. Notwithstanding, small EVs and cytokines secreted by MSCs can still enter the vasculature through the semi-permeable membrane of the bioreactor to play a therapeutic role (Fig. 3). Based on this premise, our team has constructed a novel method for realizing NMP based on the use of an MSC bioreactor that has been verified in large animal experiments.

Prospects and Conclusions

NMP has several advantages for improving donor liver function and reducing postoperative transplantation complications. However, several MP concerns remain, such as the lack of uniform standards for donor liver preservation across various research centers. Further, no uniform consensus or specifications exists for certain parameters measured during NMP, such as hepatic artery and portal vein pressure, perfusion temperature, flow velocity, and oxygenation. The application of NMP in clinical transplant centers should be verified in a large number of multicenter clinical trials. Further, the prevention of cell edema is one of the most important functions of the organ-preservation solution used during NMP. Currently, most solutions used in MP schemes are based on UW, HTK, and Celsior solutions, which contain nutrients, oxygen carriers (hemoglobin or artificial hemoglobin), vitamins, antibiotics, vasoactive drugs, and various additives. Even though these differing schemes can protect the liver, no optimal solution has been formulated yet.

The ideal therapeutic goal of MSCs-MP relies on large-scale MSCs production and efficient biomolecule transport between MSCs and blood. These require the optimization of both MSCs and bioreactor design. Decades of research have led to substantial progress in this regard, and advanced biotechnologies (such as the large-scale culturing of MSCs) can provide new cell sources for bioartificial livers. Novel bioreactor designs utilizing 3D cell culturing can be used to reconstruct MSCs microenvironments and promote cell growth and biofunction expression. All these technologies show promise for the design, manufacture, and application of MSCs-NMP treatments in terms of large-scale cell culture, biological function enhancement, and mass transfer efficiency. We envision an ultimate clinical application of MSCs for in vitro liver preservation that will benefit a large number of donor liver recipients in the future.
