Progressive Thickening of Retinal Nerve Fiber and Ganglion Cell Complex Layers Following SDM Laser Vision Protection Therapy in Open-Angle Glaucoma
Authors: Jeffrey K. Luttrull, Radouil Tzekov, Sathy V. Bhavan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis work aims to determine the effect on nerve fiber layer (NFL) and ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness trends in eyes with open-angle glaucoma (OAG) treated with Vision Protection Therapy™ (VPT).MethodsA retrospective analysis of spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT) measured NFL and GCC thickness trends was performed, excluding eyes with poor-quality scans and principal diagnoses other than OAG. This study compares eyes with OAG managed conventionally with IOP control alone (controls) to eyes managed with the addition of VPT (VPT eyes). The direction (+ or −) and magnitude (microns/year) of the OCT trends were the study endpoints.ResultsSeventy-eight control eyes of 40 patients and 61 VPT-treated eyes of 39 patients were included in the study. Positive NFL trends were noted in 5% of control eyes vs. 71% of VPT eyes (p < 0.0001). Positive GCC trends were noted in 8% of control eyes vs. 43% of VPT eyes (p < 0.0001). Mean NFL trends (µm/year) were − 0.692 for controls vs. 0.347 for VPT (p < 0.0001). Mean GCC trends (µm/year) were − 0.554 for controls vs. − 0.148 for VPT (p = 0.0175).ConclusionsThe addition of VPT to the conventional management of OAG resulted in highly significant improvements in NFL and GCC trends, indicating a reversal of key indicators of glaucoma severity and progression.

Key Summary Points

Glaucomatous optic atrophy (GOA) typically progresses in open-angle glaucoma (OAG) despite intraocular pressure (IOP) reduction.Progressive thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer (NFL) and ganglion cell complex (GCC) are hallmarks of GOA and key clinical indicators of disease progression.Progressive thickening, which indicates disease reversal, of NFL and/or GCC layer thicknesses measured by optical coherence tomography (OCT) has not been previously observed in response to any therapeutic intervention.Panmacular low-intensity/high-density subthreshold diode micropulse laser (SDM) performed on a regular basis to maintain treatment effects (vision protection therapy, VPT) has been shown to safely improve retinal, optic nerve, and visual function in OAG.In the current retrospective study, SDM VPT was found to significantly reduce NFL and GCC thickness loss in eyes managed with VPT compared to IOP control alone, leading to net increases and restoration of NFL and GCC layer thicknesses in a high percentage of eyes. If confirmed by further study, the addition of VPT to IOP-lowering treatments in patients with OAG may prove effective in slowing or preventing disease progression and glaucomatous visual loss.

Introduction

Open-angle glaucoma (OAG) is associated with a progressive characteristic atrophy of the optic nerve, described as “cupping”, or glaucomatous optic atrophy (GOA) [1–8]. Although associated with an elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), GOA may occur in eyes with low or normal IOPs, and tends to progress despite lowering of IOP. Thus, GOA is the rule in OAG. This suggests factors independent of IOP may be drivers of the progressive degeneration of the optic nerve in OAG [9]. Anatomically, GOA can be documented by characteristic changes in the appearance of the optic nerve head [1–8]. Furthermore, in recent years, by high-resolution imaging and measurement of the retinal ganglion cell complex (GCC) layer and their axons, the retinal nerve fiber layer (NFL), by optical coherence tomography (OCT) have demonstrated progressive thinning of NFL and GCC beyond that associated with normal age-associated changes in thickness [1–8]. Many OCT devices can determine the thickness of the GCC and NFL and plot the slope of changes in thickness over time in micrometers/year. A negative slope in these trends typically indicates disease progression and progressive optic atrophy [1]. In moderate to advanced OAG, such negative trends presage development and progression of visual field loss [1–9]. Disease-related thinning of the GCC and NFL in OAG has been attributed to a loss of retinal ganglion cells [9]. Progressive thickening of the NFL or GCC over time has not been observed either in normal aging or in OAG, except as a measurement artifact or statistical aberration [1].

In a prior study of eyes with OAG, panmacular SDM™ treatment (low-intensity / high-density subthreshold diode micropulse laser) was followed by significant optic nerve, retinal ganglion cell (RGC), and visual function improvements measured by visually evoked response (VER), pattern electroretinography (PERG), chart visual acuity (VA), mesopic visual acuity, and mesopic visual fields [10]. Similar treatment responses have been reported in chronic progressive retinopathies (CPRs), including dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), and inherited and metabolic retinal degenerations [11–13] (Fig. 1). Since worsening of these measures of retinal and visual function are recognized as surrogate indicators of increased risk of disease progression and visual loss, the ability to improve these same functional measures following SDM suggests a reduced risk of progression and future visual loss. NFL thickness is not typically a major point of interest for the clinical retina subspecialist. However, the author (JKL) has noted that eyes managed with Vision Protection Therapy™ (VPT, regular periodic panmacular SDM to maintain treatment effects over time in chronic disease) frequently exhibited increased NFL thicknesses. This is a seemingly novel finding, as progressive thinning of the retina and retinal sublayers with both age and disease are the norm [1–9, 14]. The following retrospective study was performed to see if the improvements in NFL and GCC sublayer thicknesses represented chance artifacts or a tendency toward actual improvement in OAG eyes treated with VPT.

Methods

OCT data were obtained from two sources: VPT-treated eyes from a vitreoretinal subspecialty practice employing VPT in the management of patients with chronic progressive retinopathies (CPRs); and conventionally managed OAG eyes without VPT (IOP control alone) from a glaucoma subspecialty practice. The OCT data reflected the customary OCT use of the different practice type: VPT-treated eyes in the retina practice were monitored with 6 × 6-mm macular raster scans from a Topcon Maestro 2 OCT system (Topcon, Inc, Livermore, CA, USA), while the conventionally managed control eyes in glaucoma practice were monitored using peripapillary scans from the Optovue OCT system. (Optovue Inc, Fremont, CA, USA). The use of data from two different OCT makers was necessitated by the need to obtain data from two independent clinical practices. Comparing peripapillary to macular scans was required as glaucoma and retina subspecialty practices tend to have different foci of interest, and this is reflected in their data collection. To mitigate any differences that might arise from these factors, only layer thickness differences—not absolute measures—over time were tabulated and compared. As both OCT devices used in this study are certified and approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as producing accurate, reliable, and reproducible objective measurements of retinal sublayer thicknesses, no significant difference in layer thickness difference measures is expected. Reflecting this, the layer thickness trend (thickness difference over time) results for each eye represent measures from the same machine and scan type in the same eye, rather a comparison of absolute measures between the two different machines and scan types. In addition, the direction of the trend slope (positive, improving; negative, worsening) was also selected as an endpoint, as the direction of the trend slope should be independent of the device and scan protocol. Finally, we note that prior studies have shown that measurements of average NFL and GCC layer thicknesses and thickness changes over time from different spectral domain OCT systems are comparable; as are average retinal sublayer thickness measures derived from peripapillary or macular scans, with peripapillary scans possibly correlating better to visual field loss in early glaucoma and macular scans better in later glaucoma [4–6]. The primary endpoint of the current study was to compare the retinal layer thickness trends over time in the OAG eyes managed with and without VPT. The technique and parameters of panmacular SDM and the VPT strategy have been described in detail elsewhere [10–15].

Inclusion criteria included only eyes with a prior diagnosis of open-angle glaucoma treated with either or a combination of topical medications, selective laser trabeculoplasty, and/or glaucoma surgery. Included eyes had at least 1 year of follow-up and at least four high-quality (~ linearly arranged, without notable outliers) sequential OCT examinations and the primary diagnoses of OAG (Fig. 2). Retinal layer measures and trends were generated by each OCT device automatically, producing a calculation of “best fit” slope for layer thickness readings over time. SDM in the VPT-treated group was performed for the diagnosis of OAG, evidenced by use of treatments to lower intraocular pressure, glaucomatous optic nerve cupping, and/or glaucomatous visual field loss by automated perimetry in hopes of slowing glaucomatous optic atrophy and improving visual function, informed by prior studies showing highly significant improvements in all functional measures in OAG and chronic retinopathies following SDM in the absence of adverse treatment effects [10–14]. Exclusion criteria included uncontrolled IOP defined as an IOP over 26 mmHg and/or that requiring major changes in glaucoma management over the course of observation such as glaucoma surgery other than selective laser trabeculoplasty or addition of oral carbonic anhydrase inhibitors; poor-quality or unreliable OCTs such as shown in Fig. 2, advanced AMD, or other confounding disorders such as other optic nerve diseases, high myopia, epiretinal membrane, vitreomacular traction, diabetic retinopathy, prior retinal detachment or vitreous surgery, macular edema, current or prior retinal vascular occlusion, macular neovascularization, macular edema, current or prior intravitreal drug injections, major ocular or glaucoma surgery such as trabeculectomy or tube shunt placement, or other surgery performed due to inadequate IOP control within the data observation window.

Data were obtained from the EMR included patient age, sex, eye, lens status, initial IOP, final IOP, initial VA, final VA, number of topical medications, and prior glaucoma surgeries.

All study data de-identified prior to analysis. As this study is retrospective and limited to analysis of patient unidentified data, patient informed consent was not required or obtained, and exempted from approval by the Western Investigational Review Board (WCG IRB). The study adhered to the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and Helsinki Declaration for Medical Research.

Statistical Analysis

For analysis of the changes in NFL and GCC thickness trends in µm/year, the difference in least-squares means, 95% confidence interval for difference, and p value are derived from a linear mixed model with treatment group as a fixed effect and subject as a random effect. Analysis of the layer thickness trend directions (+ or –) derived the p value from Fisher’s exact test, comparing the treatment group, with a null hypothesis of odds ratio equal to 1. The demographic variables were compared using Welch’s two-sample test using R statistical software https://www.r-project.org/.

The frequency of positive GCC and NFL trends was markedly higher in VPT-treated eyes (p < 0.0001), with positive GCC trends in 42.6% of VPT eyes vs. 7.7% of control eyes; and positive NFL trends in 70.5% if VPT eyes vs. 5.1% of controls. Likewise, the average rate of GCC loss (µm/year) was significantly lower in VPT-treated eyes (avg. − 0.148 µm/year for treated vs. − 0.554 µm/year for controls) (p = 0.0175), while the NFL layer of control eyes decreased at an average rate of − 0.692 µm/year, while VPT-treated eyes increased and avg. + 0.347 µm/year (p < 0.0001). There were no significant inter-eye interactions (Table 2).

Discussion

With the aging of the world population, neurodegenerative diseases have come to the forefront of public health concerns. In the current study, we report anatomic reversal of optic nerve neurodegeneration in OAG, measured objectively by OCT.

Thinning of the NFL and GCC with age and disease is the rule and a cardinal feature of disease progression in OAG [1–8]. Long-term progressive thickening (thus improvement) of the NFL and GCC has not been previously observed in OAG as other than an artifact [1–8]. In the current study, positive NFL and GCC trends were noted predominantly in VPT-treated OAG eyes (p < 0.0001 each). Regarding the magnitude of change, while control eyes demonstrated an average NFL loss of − 0.57 µm/year, VPT-treated eyes showed a net gain of + 0.42 µm/year, for a + 0.99 µm/year difference favoring VPT eyes (p < 0.0001). Likewise, VPT-treated eyes demonstrated less GCC loss, for a + 0.24 µm/year advantage of VPT eyes compared to control eyes (p = 0.0175). These findings in VPT-treated eyes go against the grain of expectation in OAG [1–16]. How might these findings be understood?

The mechanism of action of SDM has been discussed in several prior publications and supported by numerous in vitro and in vivo studies from many different laboratories [10–40]. SDM preferentially affects the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and has no direct effect on the neurosensory retina, transparent to the 810-nm wavelength [10–15, 18–27, 35]. SDM causes photothermal activation of RPE heat-shock proteins (HSPs) at energy levels sublethal to the RPE, upregulating and accelerating the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) unfolded protein response (UPR) to improve and normalize cell function and inhibit apoptosis [28–31, 34, 40]. The response to SDM is thus an example of thermal hormesis [14]. The reaction is catalytic and triggers multiple secondary cascades of restorative processes, including inflammation reduction and therapeutic local and systemic immunomodulation [14, 17–20]. By so doing, SDM incites a physiologic “reset”, normalizing RPE and thence neurosensory retinal function, acting as a “non-specific trigger of disease-specific repair” “agnostic” to the cause of protein misfolding and subsequent cellular dysfunction [14, 28–34, 37, 40]. Normalization of RPE function necessarily normalizes RPE paracrine function critical to normal retinal and thus optic nerve function and autoregulation [10–26].

The fundamental effect of the SDM-elicited reset mechanism, and HSP activation in general, is normalized (improved) cell function. Thus, SDM has no notable effect on already normally functioning cells. In this, it has been described as “pathoselective” [11, 14, 35, 36, 38]. Prior studies have shown that SDM-elicited responses occur in proportion to the level of baseline dysfunction; the greater the dysfunction, the greater the degree of improvement [10–12]. Thus, a response to SDM indicates pre-existing RPE dysfunction [10–12, 14]. The observations in the eyes reported in the current study are consistent with prior observations, suggesting the presence of a modifiable and previously unrecognized abnormality of the RPE in eyes with OAG, which has been described as “the retinopathy of OAG” (ROAG) [10–12, 14]. ROAG appears to be clinically characterized by a hyponeurotropism specific to OAG leading to characteristic glaucomatous optic atrophy [10, 13, 14] (Fig. 1). The therapeutic response following SDM treatment of the panmacular RPE in OAG is characterized by improvements in visual acuity, visual fields, mesopic visual function, RGC function by PERG, and optic nerve function by VER shown in prior studies; as well as the NFL and GCC layer improvements reported here following VPT [10–15] (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Fundus photograph and standardized automatic perimetry of left eye of an 84-year-old patient with advanced open-angle glaucoma, from presentation in May 2023 prior to initial SDM treatment (top), through March 2024 (bottom), after completing low-intensity/high-density subthreshold diode micropulse laser (SDM) vision protection therapy in May, August, and November of 2023. Impaired mesopic visual function and electrophysiology in glaucoma predict progression and future visual loss. Thus, the improvements in electrophysiologic and visual function in OAG following SDM represent temporary disease reversals that may serve as surrogate indicators of reduced risks of long-term visual loss. This principle has been demonstrated in other disorders, such as slowed progression of geographic atrophy and inhibition of neovascular conversion in dry AMD, and resolution of diabetic macular edema and reversal of retinopathy severity in diabetes following SDM [10, 11, 14, 15, 20, 24, 26, 41–46]. Thus, while retinal layer thickening in OAG eyes managed with VPT we report is novel, it is consistent with the anatomic improvements following SDM VPT observed in other chronic progressive ocular neurodegenerative disorders that lead to significant long-term treatment benefits.

Hou and associates found an average rate of GCC thinning of − 1.18 µm/year in OAG, while Shin et al. found average GCC thickness loss was significantly higher in eyes with progressive disease (− 1.05 ± 0.98 μm/year for mild glaucoma and − 0.66 ± 0.30 μm/year for moderate to advanced glaucoma) than eyes without progression (– 0.47 ± 0.54 μm/year for mild glaucoma and − 0.31 ± 0.50 μm/year for moderate to advanced glaucoma) [7]. The average rate of GCC loss in control eyes in the current study is thus intermediate to these measures (− 0.554 μm/year), while GCC loss in VPT-treated eyes was much lower (− 0.148 μm/year), for a net VPT treatment effect of + 0.406 μm/year (p = 0.0175). Miki and associates found the estimated mean rate of NFL loss significantly associated with visual field loss (− 2.02 μm/year in eyes with visual field loss vs. − 0.82 μm/year without, P < 0.001), finding that each 1μm/year increased rate of NFL loss was associated with a 2.05 times higher risk of visual field loss [6]. In the current study, control eyes had an average NFL loss of − 0.692 μm/year, while NFL trends of VPT-treated eyes demonstrated positive trends, with net average thickening of the NFL of + 0.347 μm/year, for a net VPT treatment effect of + 1.039 μm/year (p < 0.0001). Any age-related contribution to thinning of the retina would favor the control group, who were younger [1–8]. Although the observation window of the VPT group was shorter (median 708 vs. 730 days), the first-order linear layer thickness trend lines illustrated in Fig. 2A suggest this difference would have little effect on the findings. Thus, the improvements we report in the current study represent significant departures from the typical course of OAG, indicating a reversal of disease progression at the anatomical level [3–7]. Virtually all eyes in the VPT group began VPT treatment either prior to, or at the initiation of, OCT testing. Thus, any post-treatment change in the trend slopes could not be captured. It is therefore interesting to note the prompt change (improvement) in NFL and GCC trend slopes coincident with initiation of VPT treatment in an eye with normal-tension glaucoma and stable IOP throughout the course of the OCT trend analysis (Fig. 2C).

Thinning of the NFL and GCC (and thus negative trend slopes) in OAG has been historically attributed to a loss of RGCs and their axons [1–9]. Prevention of this loss would represent effective neuroprotection [1–9]. The progressive NFL and GCC layer thickening in many of the eyes we report following VPT represent anatomic restoration, or reversal of those prior disease-related losses [1–8, 47, 48]. Retinal thickening, such as from macular edema or vitreoretinal traction, or retinal thinning such as in myopia, does not typically affect OCT-measured NFL or GCC layer thicknesses and thus is unlikely to explain our observations [1–8]. It is possible that the retina layer thickenings observed in this study could be due to thickening of other components in those layers, such as glial tissue [48]. While possible, thickening (by laser-stimulated hypertrophy or hyperplasia) of non-neural elements would be unlikely to account for the functional electrophysiologic and visual improvements following SDM in OAG documented in other studies [10–14]. The improved electrophysiologic and visual function measures observed following SDM in OAG represent neuroenhancement [1–9]. Neuroenhancement does not require anatomic change or improvement, only functional, and thus cannot explain the anatomic findings we report [1–9]. Neuroregeneration would, however, be expected to produce anatomic improvements such as observed by OCT in the current study. While central nervous system neuroregeneration has not yet been observed in humans, it is of great interest and expected to be achievable under the right conditions, if the number of university laboratories devoted to the pursuit is any indicator [9, 47, 54–69]. Since individual RGC axons and cell bodies cannot currently be directly visualized or followed prospectively in vivo in humans, it is impossible to directly document neuroregeneration in patients like those reported here [67–69]. For the same reason, it cannot be ruled out, however unlikely. Further study will be required. In any case, because progressive thinning of NFL and GCC layers is the rule in OAG and indicative of disease progression, long-term reductions and actual reversals of retinal layer thinning in OAG such as those reported here following SDM represent an encouraging and potentially important new finding that may lead to improvements in glaucoma management that currently relies on IOP reduction alone.

Conclusions

Being retrospective, this study has inherent limitations. The study groups were slightly different, with VPT patients being slightly older (median 78 years) than controls (median 73 years). This age difference would, if anything, favor the controls who would be expected to have less age-related retinal layer loss independent of OAG. Control patients were followed slightly longer than VPT patients (median 730 vs. 708 days), but the linear nature of the retinal layer trends over time suggests this difference would have little if any effect on the overall results (Fig. 2A). Significant macular disease was excluded making a confounding response from macular disease unlikely. Because the current study is retrospective, objective measures of the treatment response, the rate (difference over time) and direction of NFL and GCC layer thickness change over time by OCT, were chosen. Because these comparisons were made only of differences between measures from the same machine on the same eye over time using the same scan protocol, differences between machines and scan protocols were minimized [4–6]. The results we report are sufficiently robust that statistical analysis is not necessary to appreciate the marked differences in both retinal layer trend direction and magnitude between the study groups, suggesting they should withstand scrutiny in further study. Recognizing the limitations of retrospective study, we do not claim that the data we present represent proof of anything, or “truth”. Instead, the data we present are novel, interesting, having potentially important implications that require and recommend further study. Our findings suggest that progressive optic atrophy in OAG may not be inevitable but might be reducible and even reversible by treatment, reducing the risks of visual loss beyond IOP lowering alone. Every stage of investigation is important in the pursuit of knowledge. Every advance begins as an anecdotal observation (discovery being the most important event), followed by exploratory case series such as the current study, then, if warranted by those observations, confirmation by prospective study. Final validation rests not in an idealized RCT, but in a real-world application. Thus, while the confirmatory role of prospective study in an RCT is important as the next step in investigation, the costs in time and money must be justified (and financial sponsors attracted) by compelling data. We believe that the data we present are precisely the type of compelling evidence needed to inspire further study [67–69].
