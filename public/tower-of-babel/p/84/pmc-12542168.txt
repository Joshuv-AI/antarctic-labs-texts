Nurses’ work characteristics, perceptions of individualized care and nursing care quality: a correlational study
Authors: İbrahim Kavaslar, Betül Sönmez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo determine the relationship between nurses’ work characteristics, their perception of individualized care and quality of care.BackgroundIt is important to evaluate the quality-of-care perceptions of not only patients but also nurses in the planning of interventions to improve the quality of care.MethodsA cross-sectional and correlational research design was used and reported according to the STROBE checklist. Data were collected between May and December 2019 from the nurses who work in the inpatient units of two public and two private hospitals. The sample of the study consisted of 749 nurses. Participants completed measures regarding their demographic and work characteristics, in addition to the Individualized Care Scale and Nurse-perceived Quality of Patient Care Scale. In addition to various difference tests, correlation analysis, linear and logistic regression analysis, a descriptive analysis was also conducted.ResultsThe nurses’ perception of individualized care and the perception of the quality of nursing care were weakly correlated. Some work-related factors affected the perceptions of individualized care. The increase in the monthly working hours, the number of patient beds in the unit, and the state of being sure that the patients can take care of themselves after discharge reduced the quality-of-care perception. Nurses who thought that they provided more individualized care were more likely to evaluate the quality of care as good and excellent.ConclusionThis study reveals that nurses generally perceive their individualized care and the quality of care they deliver as above average. However, notable inconsistencies were observed, particularly in unit-level care assessments and patient discharge readiness. The weak correlation between individualized care perceptions and care quality suggests the influence of additional factors, such as workload, nurse-patient ratios, and working conditions. Supportive work environments and targeted education are essential for improving care quality. Further research is necessary to address these gaps and enhance the consistency of high-quality, patient-centered care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Individualized care, Nurse, Patient-centered care, Quality of care, Work characteristics

Introduction

The quality of care provided by nurses, who make up the majority of healthcare staff and spend more time with patients, is important to meet the highest standards of healthcare. Increasing the quality of care lowers the in- hospital mortality rate, and the length of stay in the hospital increases the recovery rate, lowers the rate of development of complications, allows for early detection of complications and side effects and rapid intervention, and reduces the cost of care [1]. As the quality-of-care increases, patient satisfaction increases and the institution becomes more attractive to society [2]. High quality care helps to increase the motivation and job satisfaction of nurses and contributes to the well-being of patients and the institution. For this reason, when planning measures to improve the quality of care, it is important to assess the perception of the quality of care not only by the patients but also by the nurses [3–5].

Background

Previous studies reveal that nurses generally rate the quality of nursing care provided to patients in their units as moderate/poor between 11.4% and 47% [5–7]. In a study [3], nearly half of the nurses (45.27%) evaluated the quality of care in their units as moderate. In another study [4], 19% of the nurses working in the service and 12% of the nurses working in the intensive care unit evaluated the quality of care as poor. It is stated that many factors affect the quality of care/patient satisfaction, such as the working environment, patient-nurse relations, educational status, institutional policies, and the approach of the managers [8–12]. Therefore, examining the factors affecting the quality of care while evaluating the nurses’ perceptions of care quality is important in improving the quality of care.

To provide quality nursing care, it is not sufficient to provide nursing care only according to the socio-demographic characteristics of the individual such as illness, age, gender, and education level, it is also important to make a holistic assessment and to give individualized care, taking into account the uniqueness of the individual [13]. The structuring of nursing care practices according to the individual’s values, beliefs, thoughts, feelings, experiences, and preferences is expressed as “individualized care” [14]. It is important to evaluate the perception of individualized care and to ensure that nurses provide individualized care, to increase patient satisfaction, and maintain higher-quality nursing care [15]. In the literature, it is seen that studies evaluating nurses’ perceptions of individualized care have increased in recent years.

In a comprehensive study [15] focused on assessing nurses’ individualized care perceptions on an international scale, the findings reveal a commendable degree of individualized care perception among nurses. Moreover, the study identified discernible variations in these perceptions across different countries. In a study conducted within Türkiye, it was discovered that nurses’ perception of individualized care surpassed the average level. Notably, the study also revealed that the nurses’ perceptions of individualized care provided during their most recent shift were notably higher than their overall perceptions [16]. In another international study [17], in which patients and nurses’ perceptions of individualized care were compared, it was determined that the reason for the difference between countries was the different nursing education systems, health policies, the different value given to individualized care, and health system processes in each country.

It is known that nurses’ perception of individualized care is affected by many factors such as education, working environment, working style, working experience, communication skills, and adequacy of time allocated to the patient [9, 18–20]. According to one study [9], nurses acknowledge the significance of individualized care; however, challenges such as staffing shortages, intra-team communication issues, and educational insufficiencies impede their ability to effectively implement personalized care for patients. Besides, the International Union of Nurses [21] states that despite the demand for quality nursing care, nurse employment is not sufficient, which is the underlying cause of negative experiences and adverse outcomes in some countries.

As mentioned above, although there were studies on nurses’ perception of individualized care and care quality in previous studies, only one study was found that examined individualized care and nurses’ perception of care quality together. In the previous literature, nurses’ perceptions of individualized care and care quality were mostly examined separately, and only one study was found that evaluated these two dimensions together [22]. To address this gap, the relationship between nurses’ work characteristics, individualized care perceptions, and perceived quality of care was investigated in a multi-center sample consisting of two public and two private tertiary hospitals. The analyses were conducted using multivariable models adjusted for work characteristics such as unit type, shift duration, number of patients and nurses per shift, number of beds in the unit, and individualized care training. Through this approach, a more comprehensive view was provided of how micro-level perceptions of individualized care intersect with macro-level evaluations of care quality. The findings are expected to contribute both to clinical nursing practice, by strengthening individualized care, and to institutional and policy-level strategies for workforce planning and quality improvement.

The study and method

Aims

The study aimed to determine the relationship between nurses’ work characteristics, their perception of individualized care and quality of nursing care.

Design

A cross-sectional and correlational quantitative design was used. This study has been reported using the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) [23] guideline.

Participants

The study population consisted of nurses employed in the inpatient units of two public and two private tertiary hospitals in Istanbul and Kocaeli, Türkiye (N = 1,410). The proportional quota method, one of the non-probability sampling methods, was used as the sampling method for the study. First, stratification was performed by hospital (Hospitals 1, 2, 3, and 4), and the number of nurses in each hospital was weighted according to their proportion in the total population of nurses. This procedure ensured that the distribution of the sample reflected the distribution of the population. Based on the population parameters, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 302 nurses (n = 302) with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. Eligibility criteria included: (1) direct involvement in patient care and (2) working in an inpatient unit. Nurses in non-clinical positions, such as nurse educators or infection control nurses, were excluded. Unlike the stratified random sampling, 749 nurses who responded to the data collection tools using the convenience sampling method without randomization formed the study sample.

Data collection

Data were collected between May and December 2019. The researcher personally distributed the printed questionnaires to nurses working in the inpatient units of the participating hospitals and collected them after completion. A total of 1,410 questionnaires were distributed, corresponding to the full nurse population in the four hospitals. Of these, 749 were returned fully completed and included in the analysis, yielding a response rate of 53.1%. Partially completed questionnaires were excluded; therefore, no missing data handling or imputation procedures were required.

Measurements

Introductory information form

The form developed by the researcher consisted of 25 descriptive questions about the nurses’ demographic and work-related characteristics. These questions included age, gender, marital status, education level, work experience, unit and the institution worked at, shift hours, companion involvement status in care, ease of access to necessary equipment, receiving individualized care training, number of beds, nurses, and patients cared for.

Individualized care scale (Nurse version)

Nurses’ perceptions of individualized care were assessed using the Individualized Care Scale (ICS)-Nurse Version, Turkish version. The scale was originally developed by R Suhonen, ML Gustafsson, J Katajisto, M Valimaki and H Leino-Kilpi [24]. In the first part of the scale, nurses’ perceptions of supporting the individuality of patients in their care practices (ICS-A), and in the second part, individualizing the patient’s care (ICS-B) perceptions are evaluated. Both sections can be evaluated separately and consist of three sub-dimensions: Clinical situation, personal life situation, and decisional control, and a total of 17 items. The scale is a 5-point Likert (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The sub-dimensions and total scale score being high shows that nurses have a high perception of supporting the individuality of the patients (ICS-A) and individualizing the patient’s care (ICS-B). Turkish validity and reliability of the scale were conducted by R Acaroglu, R Suhonen, M Sendir and H Kaya [25]. In the Turkish version, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the ICS-A sub-dimensions ​​were between 0.71 and 0.83, and the ICS-A overall scale was 0.92, respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the ICS-B sub-dimensions ​​were between 0.80 and 0.89, and the overall ICS-B scale was 0.93 [25]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the ICS-A sub-dimensions ​​were between 0.80 and 0.87, and of the ICS-A overall scale was 0.92, respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the ICS-B sub-dimensions ​​were between 0.76 and 0.89, and the overall ICS-B scale was 0.93.

Nurse-perceived quality of patient care scale

Nurses’ perceptions of the quality of nursing care provided in their units were assessed using the Nurse-perceived Quality of Patient Care Scale (NQPC), Turkish version. The scale was originally developed by LH Aiken, SP Clarke and DM Sloane [6] and consists of four items: (1) How would you describe the quality of nursing care provided to patients in your unit in general?; (2) How would you describe the quality of nursing care provided in your last shift?; (3) In general, how would you rate the quality of patient care provided at your hospital in the past year?; and (4) How confident are you that your patients will be able to care for themselves after they are discharged from the hospital? The first, second, and fourth items are rated on a four-point Likert scale (1 = Poor/Not at all confident, 2 = Fair/Somewhat confident, 3 = Good/Confident, 4 = Excellent/Very confident), while the third item is rated on a three-point Likert scale (1 = Deteriorated, 2 = Remained the same, 3 = Improved).

The Turkish adaptation of the NQPC was previously validated by Ö İspir Demir, A Yıldırım, B Sönmez and S Duygulu [26], with a content validity index (CVI) of 0.95 and test–retest reliability coefficients (ICC) ranging from 0.69 to 0.79 [27, 28]. Since the scale consists of four questions, Cronbach’s α coefficient could not be calculated. In this study, each question of the NQPC was evaluated as a separate variable.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 22 and NCSS (Number Cruncher Statistical System) 2007. Continuous variables were summarized as means, standard deviations (SD), medians, and ranges, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The internal consistency of the scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, histogram, Q–Q plots, and box plots. Associations between continuous variables were examined using Pearson correlation analysis. To identify factors associated with ICS-A and ICS-B total scores, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted using the backward elimination method. Multicollinearity was evaluated with tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) statistics, which indicated no violations of assumptions. Binary logistic regression analyses were applied to determine the predictors of nurse-perceived quality of care; variables that demonstrated significant associations in preliminary analyses were entered into the models, and categorical variables were dummy-coded prior to inclusion.

In this study, the variable ‘units using the total patient care model’ refers to wards and intensive care units where patients are allocated among nurses, enabling the calculation of the number of patients per nurse. In contrast, emergency departments typically use the functional nursing care model, in which patients are not allocated to individual nurses; therefore, these units were excluded from analyses requiring this variable. Statistical significance was set at p < .05 for all tests.

Validity and reliability

The measurement tools in the study had been previously translated into the Turkish language and had demonstrated good reliability and validity, as reported.

Results

Participant demographics and work-related characteristics

Among the 749 participants included in the analysis, 574 (76.6%) were female and 175 (23.4%) were male, with a mean age of 26,6 years (SD 5.4 years, range = 19–60 years). Most nurses’ (n=512, 68.4%) had a bachelor’s and 554 of them (74%) were working in a public hospital, whereas 195 (26%) were working in a private hospital. It was determined that 715 nurses (95.5%) were staff nurses, 527 (70.4%) worked in wards, 201 (26.8%) in intensive care units, and 21 (2.8%) in emergency services.

Participants’ mean working year in the unit was 2.37 (SD 2.88, range = 1–27 years); was 2.99 (SD 3.62, range = 1–33 years) in the institution and overall working experience was 4.6 years (SD 5.38). Of the nurses, 639 (85.3%) worked in shifts day and night, while 333 (44.5%) worked in 16-hour shifts. Most participants (n=559, 74.6%) stated that their patients had a companion, and they participated in the care of the patients 526 (70.2%). Of the nurses, 616 (82.2%) used the total patient care model and 133 (17.8%) used functional nursing as the care delivery model in nursing. Of the nurses, 313 (41.8%) stated that they spent 51–75% of their time directly on patient care during their shift. Fewer participants (n=327, 43.7%) worked between 161 and 180 h per month.

The average number of patient beds in the unit was 24.81 (SD 10.86, range = 4–40), the number of patients a nurse cares for during the day was 4.43 (SD 2.65 range = 0–14), and was 7.46 (SD 5.47, range = 2–20) at nights. The average number of nurses working at night shift was 4.75 (SD 3.94, range = 0–18) and 5.86 (SD 3.53, range = 0–18) during the day. Of the nurses, 644 (86%) had ease of access to the materials in the clinic, and 412 (55%) stated that there was no service policy and practices for individualized/patient-centered care in their hospitals. The majority of nurses (n=416, 55.5%) have received training in delivering specialized, individualized care.

The mean score for the quality of care provided by the nurses in the unit was above average (moderate + good), and the mean score for the quality of care provided in the last shift was found to be high (good + excellent). Nurses rated the quality of care provided at the hospital in the last year as above average (stayed the same + improved). However, nurses were moderately confident that the patients could take care of themselves when they were discharged from the hospital.

The regression model created for the ICS-A total score was found to be statistically significant (F = 6.578; p < .001; R2 = 0.165). It was found that a one-unit increase in the scores of those receiving individualized care training would cause an increase of 0.225 units in the ICS-A total score while working in the intensive care unit would cause a decrease of 0.275 units. It was found that one unit increase in the number of patient beds in the unit decreased the total score by 0.017 units, an increase in the number of day patients increased the total by 0.049 units, and an increase in the number of day nurses increased the ICS-A total score by 0.044 units (p < .05). There was no significant effect of other independent variables (p > .05) (Table 3).

The regression model created for the ICS-B total score was found to be statistically significant (F = 8.187; p < .001; R2 = 0.197). It was found that a one-unit increase in the study in private hospitals increased the ICS-B total score by 0.263 units. In addition, the ICS-B score increases by 0.181 units when the patient’s companion joins the care, 0.170 units when clinical materials are easily accessible, and 0.171 units when individualized care training is received. The increase in the number of patient beds in the unit decreased the ICS-B total score by 0.016 units, along with working in the intensive care unit would cause a decrease of 0.217 units. It was determined that the increase in the number of patients cared for during the day increased the ICS-B total score by 0.041 points, and the increase in the number of daytime nurses increased the ICS-B total score by 0.040 points (p < .05). There was no significant effect of other independent variables (p > .05) (Table 3).

Discussion

Our results showed that nurses’ ICS-A and ICS-B total and sub-dimension scores were found to be higher than the average, similar to the studies conducted in Turkey [16, 29, 30] and internationally [31, 32]. Similar to this study, nurses’ perceptions of individualized care (ICS-B score) in the last shift were higher than their general perceptions [9, 16]. This may be because nurses evaluate other nurses while making general assessments, and make assessments by thinking about themselves when evaluating for the last shift.

Our study also highlighted that the nurses’ perceptions of the care provided in the unit, in general, were fair and good, which is inconsistent with other studies. Nurses mostly evaluate the quality of care provided in the unit as fair [3], and almost half of them evaluate it as poor and fair [26, 33]. Furthermore, various studies found higher perceptions of nurses on care quality [34, 35]. Similar inconsistencies also occurred regarding the care quality perception on the last shift. Whereas our study found that most nurses assessed the care quality in the last shift as good and excellent, the perception differed according to the countries or hospitals, and it was evaluated as lower or higher [22, 36].

Based on our results, nurses rated the quality of care provided at the hospital above average (remained the same & improved) in the past year, showing another conflict. In previous studies, there are more negative evaluations [5, 36]. As a matter of fact, in a study conducted on nurses from 12 countries, it was found that the rate of those who answered as “deteriorated” to the same question ranged from 18% to 43% [5]. Another non-consensus took place regarding the extent to which nurses are confident that patients could care for themselves when they are discharged from the hospital. Our study showed that nurses were moderately confident (58.7% not at all confident + somewhat confident; 41.2% confident + very confident). While studies in the USA, Canada [6, 7], and Turkey [36] found more confidence levels and studies in the UK, Scotland, Germany [6, 7], and Pennsylvania [37, 38] found less confidence level.

Nurses’ individualized care perception (in general and in the last shifts) and the mean scores of the nursing care quality given (in the unit and the hospital) were weakly correlated. This may be partly due to conceptual differences between the instruments: while the ICS evaluates micro-level, nurse–patient interaction aspects of individualized care, the NQPC reflects broader, institutional-level perceptions of nursing care quality. In addition, organizational factors such as staffing shortages and heavy workloads may weaken the link between individualized care and institutional quality evaluations. Nurses may perceive that they provide individualized care to their patients, yet still assess the overall quality of care at the institutional level less positively due to systemic constraints. The only study [22] on the subject showed that the increase in the perception of individualized care given in the last shift significantly improved quality evaluations in the USA. This divergence may be explained by the different conditions in which care is provided. Indeed, according to OECD (2023), the nurse density per 1,000 population is 12 in the USA but only 2.8 in Turkey, which may contribute to the weaker association observed in our context [39].

The independent variables in the regression model explained 16.5% of the nurses’ individualized care perception (ICS-A) variance. The explanatory coefficient was low and this may indicate that there might be other factors affecting nurses’ perceptions. However, the model posited that engaging in individualized care education yielded a beneficial outcome a notion potentially reinforced by another study. This study [9] identified obstacles to delivering individualized care, including nurses’ insufficient education, the absence of a robust educational framework, and a curriculum tailored to individualized care. In addition, the increase in individual-oriented care expectations of accreditation institutions and the presence of private hospitals accredited by international accreditation institutions in the sample might explain this result.

In the model, working in the intensive care unit had a negative effect on the ICS-A score. The fact that the general health status of the patients in the intensive care unit is critical and their level of participation in the decisions about their care is low may have suggested that the nurses take the initiative in this regard and offer the patient less choice in the decisions about the care. On the other hand, in Turkey, in accordance with the Standards of Intensive Care Units Circular Order (2008/53), a nurse provides care to a maximum of three patients. A comprehensive study [40] found that if nurse-patient ratios are inadequate, it will result in emotional exhaustion in nurses and job burnout among nurses. Accordingly, the perception of individualized care may be lower due to the workload of intensive care nurses who care for patients with high care needs. Correspondingly in the current study, the number of patient beds in the unit also had a negative effect on the ICS-A score. The increase in the number of patients might have led to the thought that the level of individualization of care would decrease. In contrast, the number of patients cared for during the day had a positive effect on the ICS-A. Because the number of patients assigned to each nurse can be determined only in units using the total patient care model, data from these units were included in the analysis. Therefore, this issue should be re-examined in future research.

Another positive effect on the individualized care perception was derived from the number of nurses working during the day. A study [41] determined that for each increase in the number of nurses per patient, inpatient mortality rates in hospitals decreased by 14.0%. This finding may be explained by the fact that nurses giving care to more patients increase their workload and therefore the perception of individualized care might be lower. Because acute and chronic fatigue negatively affects the perception of individualized care in the last shift [22].

The independent variables in the regression model explained 19.7% of the nurses’ individualized care perception (ICS-B) variance. The explanatory coefficient was low and this may indicate that there might be other factors affecting nurses’ perceptions. In the model, individualized care education, the number of patients cared for during the day in units using the total patient care model, and the number of nurses working during the day—variables that had a significant effect on ICS-A—also had a positive effect on the ICS-B score. In addition to similarity, working in the intensive care unit and the number of patient beds in the unit had a negative effect on the ICS-B score. These findings suggest that the results are consistent with each other.

Working in a private hospital had a positive effect on the ICS-B score. No study was found in the literature that directly compared private or public hospitals in terms of the effect on individualized care perception. However, another study [42] found that nurses’ perceptions of the level of individualized care were related to the type of health institution’s service type; primary, secondary, tertiary, or a university hospital. As mentioned above, the fact that the private hospitals where the study was conducted were accredited by many organizations targeting patient-centered care, and that there were accreditation activities specifically aimed at individual-centered care may have been influential in this result.

In the model, the companion participation in the care had a positive effect on the ICS-B score, consistent with other a study [43]. It can be said that nurses feel that the companion gives them more information about the patient’s preferences and that they individualize the care they provide more by involving the patient in care decisions. Another contributing factor was nurses having ease of access to the pieces of equipment. As suggested by Ö Karayurt, FE Ursavaş and Ö İşeri [9], one of the barriers to individualized care delivery is the lack of materials. This being resolvable through an arrangement to be made at the organizational level, it is recommended for institution administrations to determine the materials needed by nurses, the level of access to them, and the materials that hinder the individualization of care.

The independent variables in the regression model explained 21.8% of the nurses’ perceived quality of care variance NQPC-question1. The explanatory coefficient was low and this may indicate that there might be other factors affecting nurses’ perceptions. The increase in the monthly total working hours would decrease the NQPC-question1 score. In concordance, another study [4], the perception of quality of care was negatively affected as the number of working hours increased. The International Labour Organization (ILO) recommends that the working hours of nurses should not exceed 40 h per week and 8 h per day [44]. In addition, night shifts, long, alternating, and consecutive shifts result in fewer hours of sleep and poor sleep quality for nurses, as evidenced [45]. This situation causes fatigue, excessive daytime sleepiness, and poor performance in nurses [46] and causes problems in safe and quality patient care [47]. As a matter of fact, nurses with higher acute and high chronic fatigue levels were found to be significantly less likely to evaluate the quality of care as excellent [22]. In this respect, it can be said that it is an expected finding that the increase in working hours negatively affects nurses’ perceptions of care quality. One of the determinants which negatively affects the quality perception (NQPC-question1) was the increase in the number of patient beds. Since the increase in the number of patients will lead to a decrease in the time that the nurse will allocate to each patient, it may lead to a decrease in the nurses’ perception of the quality of care.

The case on the determinants of nurses’ perceived variance in quality of care (NQPC-question4) was more related to patient discharge processes. For participants who stated “not at all confident” for NQPC-question1, the scores for the NQPC-question4 significantly decreased. In studies examining patients’ readiness for discharge, it is reported that many factors such as age, gender, socio-economic status, education level, and concomitant diseases are effective [48]. In addition, the perceptions of patient readiness for discharge and the evaluation of the patient readiness for discharge by healthcare providers may differ. It is also stated that health professionals generally believe that patients are ready for discharge, but patients themselves might disagree [49]. In this study, the fact that the patients’ readiness for discharge was not evaluated by themselves, and the factors affecting the readiness for discharge were not examined, may have caused the nurses’ perceptions of the quality of care to be negatively affected by the state of being sure.

In this study, only the “clinical status” of the ICS-A sub-dimensions was found to have a significant positive effect on the quality-of-care perception in the unit. While another study [22] found that nurses who think that they provide individualized care in the last shift (for all three sub-dimensions) have a high probability of evaluating the quality of nursing care as excellent. The evaluation of criteria related to the clinical condition of the patient in the quality indicators of the hospitals and the fact that the nurses focus more on the clinical situation of the patient in individualizing the care may explain this positive effect.

Although the data were collected prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, they remain meaningful for several reasons. First, the findings provide a valuable baseline for understanding nurses’ perceptions of individualized care and care quality before the systemic disruptions introduced by the pandemic. Second, the structural challenges identified in this study, including workload, staffing shortages, and the need for supportive working environments, have continued to persist in the post-pandemic era. Therefore, these results are not only relevant for understanding pre-pandemic conditions but also provide a crucial point of comparison for future research examining the long-term impact of the pandemic on nursing practice and care quality.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. Data were collected through self-reported measures, which are inherently subject to social desirability bias; as a result, nurses may have overestimated the quality of care they provided. In addition, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships. Although both public and private hospitals were included in the sample, the fact that the study was conducted in a single country/region also restricts the generalizability of the findings.

Another limitation is that data were collected prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. Given the profound impact of the pandemic on nurses’ working conditions, workload, and care processes, it is possible that different results might be obtained if the study were repeated in the current post-pandemic context. Nevertheless, these data remain valuable as they provide a baseline for understanding nurses’ perceptions before a global health crisis and allow meaningful comparisons with post-pandemic findings. Future research should therefore take into account both baseline and post-pandemic perspectives when evaluating individualized care perceptions and quality of care.

Conclusions

This study provides important evidence regarding nurses’ perceptions of individualized care and the quality of care in hospital settings. While nurses generally evaluated both their individualized care practices and the quality of care as above average, inconsistencies emerged in unit-level assessments and in their confidence regarding patients’ readiness for discharge. The weak correlation between individualized care perceptions and quality of care indicates that systemic factors—such as workload, nurse–patient ratios, and working conditions—may exert a stronger influence than individual efforts alone.

These findings highlight several implications for practice and policy. In clinical practice, supportive working environments, adequate staffing levels, and targeted educational interventions are essential for ensuring the consistent delivery of individualized care. At the policy level, safe nurse–patient ratios, integration of individualized care principles into national nursing standards, and provision of institutional resources should be prioritized to enhance both care quality and workforce sustainability.

Taken together, the results underline the need to strengthen organizational structures alongside individual nursing competencies in order to achieve sustainable improvements in care quality. By addressing these factors, individualized care can be more effectively translated into measurable improvements in patient outcomes, while simultaneously supporting nurse well-being and retention. Future research should build on these findings to monitor changes in care processes and outcomes in evolving healthcare contexts.

Implications for nursing & health policy

This study suggests several key implications for nursing and health policy. Ensuring adequate nurse-patient ratios is crucial to maintaining high-quality, individualized care, especially in intensive care units where the workload is intense. Health policies should support ongoing education in individualized care, integrating it into both nursing curricula and professional development programs. Additionally, providing sufficient resources and equipment is essential for effective care delivery, requiring targeted policy interventions. Finally, standardized care quality metrics should be incorporated into accreditation processes to promote consistent, patient-centered care across all healthcare settings.
