Molecular mechanisms of inhalable iron-containing particles driving lung carcinogenesis through the LCN2 signaling axis revealed by transcriptomic analysis
Authors: Lei Wang, Boyi Jiang, Rui He, Xiuyun Zhao, Dong Wang, Yunjie Lv, Guoxue Zhao, Xianfeng Shao, Guangjian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIron, an essential trace element ubiquitously present in living organisms, is integral to various biological processes. Epidemiological studies have shown an elevated relative risk of lung cancer among male individuals with occupational exposure to high iron environments (such as those working near iron and steel production facilities). This suggests that respirable iron-containing particulate matters (RICPMs) may pose a potential carcinogenic risk; nevertheless, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. The objective of this study is to explore the potential carcinogenic mechanisms of RICPM through integrative bioinformatics analyses combined with experimental validation.MethodsSequencing data of a welding fume-exposed mouse model (GSE34056) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Ferroptosis-related genes were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas-lung adenocarcinoma (TCGA-LUAD), Molecular Signatures Database (MSIGDB), and GeneCards® Human Gene Database (GENECARDS) databases. Key genes were screened and analyzed for expression profiles, single-gene pan-cancer relevance, single-gene Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), and drug sensitivity. Experimental validations included measurements of reactive oxygen species (ROS), lipocalin-2 (LCN2) expression, ferroptosis-associated antioxidant genes [glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1)], and mitochondrial ultrastructure changes via transmission electron microscopy (TEM).ResultsNano-Fe2O3 particles induced overexpression of LCN2 in bronchial epithelial cells, adenovirus 12-SV40 2B immortalized (BEAS-2B) cells, a phenomenon observed across multiple cancer types. The results indicate that RICPM may contribute to lung carcinogenesis through ROS-mediated chemical carcinogenesis pathway, upregulation of ferroptosis resistance mechanisms (e.g., GPX4 and FTH1 overexpression), inhibition of ferroptosis, and mitochondrial damage leading to impaired electron transport chain (ETC). Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between LCN2 and various pro-inflammatory cytokines. Drug sensitivity analyses suggested that high LCN2 expression may be linked to increased resistance to anticancer agents.ConclusionsThe findings elucidate the potential carcinogenic pathways of RICPM and underscore LCN2 as a key mediator, which offer fresh perspectives on the pathogenesis of lung cancer in individuals exposed to high-iron environments and identify prospective therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of lung cancer.Keywords: Respirable iron-containing particulate matter (RICPM), lung cancer, ferroptosis, lipocalin-2 (LCN2), reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Key findings

• Inhalable iron particles may promote the occurrence of lung cancer by upregulating the lipocalin-2 (LCN2) gene.

What is known and what is new?

• The specific carcinogenic mechanism of inhalable iron particles is unknown.

• Upregulation of LCN2 may synergistically inhibit cell ferroptosis and reactive oxygen species damage cells, leading to the occurrence of lung cancer.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Further research is expected to warn people working in high-speed rail environments, as well as developing preventive measures and drugs for high-speed rail environments (elders, chefs, steel factory workers, etc.), in order to reduce the hazards of inhaled iron and the incidence of lung cancer.

Introduction

Globally, lung cancer ranks as both one of the most common cancers and the leading contributor to cancer mortality (1). Welding fumes, composed of iron, nickel, lead, and other metals with particle sizes ranging from 50 to 1,000 nm, are a common source of inhalable particles that can deposit in pulmonary tissues (2,3). Emerging evidence suggests a potential etiological association between welding fume inhalation and pulmonary carcinogenesis, particularly among welders (4).

Ferroptosis, first described by Dixon et al. in 2012, is a novel iron-dependent, Erastin-induced form of programmed cell death that mechanistically differs from canonical cell death pathways including apoptosis, pyroptosis, necrosis, and autophagy (5). Unlike apoptosis which involves chromatin condensation and margination, cellular shrinkage and apoptotic body formation, necrosis which causes cytoplasmic swelling and organelle dilation, or autophagy which involves double-membrane autophagosomes, ferroptosis features condensed mitochondria with increased membrane density, reduced numbers, and diminished or absent cristae (5-8), along with excessive accumulation of iron, lipid peroxides and reactive oxygen species (ROS). Ferroptosis exhibits as a novel form of programmed cell death centered on oxidative damage. Notably, ferroptosis in one cell can also rapidly propagate to adjacent cells (6,9,10).

Iron is ubiquitously distributed in various organisms and serves as an essential component of numerous macromolecules, including enzymes. Iron plays a vital role in fundamental biological processes such as oxygen transport, cell cycle regulation, DNA synthesis and repair, and energy metabolism (11,12). It primarily exists in Fe3+ and Fe2+ forms; Fe3+ is more stable and essential for iron storage and transport, while Fe2+ facilitates electron transfer and participates in redox reactions. The Fenton reaction, in which Fe2+ generates ROS, can lead to lipid peroxidation (LPO), tissue damage (13), and ferroptosis. Intracellular iron content affects ferroptosis sensitivity—increased iron promotes ferroptosis, while its reduction offers protection (5). Additionally, alterations in iron-regulatory genes like FTH1 and TFRC modulate the susceptibility of cells to Erastin-induced ferroptosis, and positively correlate with intracellular iron levels (14). Central to iron’s function is its redox capability, particularly its interaction with oxygen. In vivo, iron exists in various oxidation states, commonly interconverting between ferrous (Fe²⁺) and ferric (Fe3+) forms. Ferric iron is generally stable and exerts its biological effects when bound to proteins (transport proteins), whereas ferrous iron is highly reactive and, when present in excess, can generate substantial ROS via the Fenton reaction. This ROS generation is associated with damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, ultimately resulting in cellular damage or death (15,16). Uncontrolled proliferation and impaired apoptosis are hallmarks of tumorigenesis (17). Since the introduction of the ferroptosis concept in 2012, iron metabolism has become a focal point of research, establishing itself as a pivotal research domain for elucidating the pathogenesis of various diseases including cancer (18).

Lipocalin-2 (LCN2), alternatively referred to as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), 24p3, SIP24, or siderocalin, is a soluble protein classified within the lipocalin family. It is expressed in macrophages, epithelial cells, adipocytes, and neutrophils (19,20). LCN2 functions as an iron-regulatory protein under both physiological and inflammatory conditions, inhibiting bacterial growth by sequestering iron-loaded siderophores (21). Cancer cells may exploit LCN2 for iron acquisition (19). Notably, LCN2 demonstrates elevated expression in malignancies, including lung cancer (22), liver cancer (23), and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (24). Moreover, LCN2 promotes cachexia progression in lung cancer by inducing ferroptosis in adipose and muscle tissues in mice (25).

Epidemiological evidence shows that men occupationally exposed to iron dust near iron and steel plants have an increased relative risk of lung cancer that correlates with cumulative exposure. The exact oncogenic mechanisms triggered by the pulmonary deposition of respirable iron-containing particulate matters (RICPMs), however, are not yet fully elucidated. Additionally, non- small cell lung cancer, small cell and cancers metastatic to lung may behave differently on a genetic level in the context of Fe driven carcinogenesis, highlighting critical avenues for future research (26-28). RICPM may disrupt normal ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death in lung cells. In this study, datasets from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), Molecular Signatures Database (MSIGDB), GeneCards® Human Gene Database (GENECARDS), and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were integrated to link RICPM to both ferroptosis and lung cancer. Through differential expression analysis, Gene Ontology (GO)/Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, immune infiltration, pro-inflammatory cytokine correlation, and single-gene analyses, potential pathogenic pathways were explored. Using a nano-Fe2O3 cell co-culture model, key gene and ferroptosis biomarker expression were validated, providing new therapeutic targets for lung cancer prevention and treatment. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-2152/rc).

Methods

Data collection

To investigate the relationship between RICPM and lung cancer at the genetic level, sequencing data of a welding fume-exposed mouse model were retrieved from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE34056 contains data from mice exposed to 40 mg/m3 gas-phase metallic arc stainless steel (GMA-SS) welding fume for 10 days (3 hours/day). Microarray analysis was performed on the lungs, blood, and aorta of 4 mice exposed to welding fumes or air (control). GSE20174 includes data from 4 mice per group, exposed to 5 mg/kg mild steel (MS) welding fumes, stainless steel (SS) welding fumes, or saline vehicle via pharyngeal aspiration four times, with full-lung microarray analysis performed 28 days and 16 weeks after the last exposure. GSE43458 includes microarray data from 80 lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) samples from never-smokers (n=40) and smokers (n=40). Human LUAD genes (TCGA-LUAD) were obtained from the TCGA database (https://www.cancer.gov/), and ferroptosis-related genes were sourced from the MSIGDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) and GENECARD databases (https://www.genecards.org/). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Identification and evaluation of candidate genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the “limma” R package, with criteria set at an adjusted P value <0.05 and an absolute |log fold change (FC)| >0.585. Overlapping Venn diagrams were constructed using an online tool designed for generating Venn diagrams. The diagnostic performance of an online generator was evaluated by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.

KEGG, GO, and expression and prognostic analysis of LCN2 in LUAD

KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were carried out using the “enrichKEGG” and “enrichGO” functions from the “clusterProfiler” R package. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was made to examine the correlation between LCN2 expression and overall survival (OS) and first progression (FP), with reference to the Kaplan-Meier plotter.

Single-gene Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and pan-cancer analysis

Correlation analysis was performed for four target genes with other genes, and relevant gene sets were determined based on the strength of these correlations. The clusterProfiler package (v4.10.1) was applied to the GSEA. Pan-cancer analysis was made using TCGAplot (https://github.com/tjhwangxiong/TCGAplot).

Pro-inflammatory cytokine correlation and drug sensitivity analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was computed to evaluate the link between target genes and pro-inflammatory cytokine gene expression levels. Significant associations were identified with P<0.05. A heatmap was generated using ggplot2, with color gradients reflecting correlation coefficients (range: [−1, 1]) and significance markers (*, P<0.05; **, P<0.01; ***, P<0.001), visually representing gene expression regulation. Drug sensitivity data for 192 drugs were obtained from the Cancer Drug Sensitivity Genomics database (GDSC: https://www.cancerrxgene.org/), and the correlation between LCN2 expression and half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values was analyzed using the pRRophetic R package.

Statistical analysis

The R software (version 4.0.3) was used in the foregoing bioinformatics analysis and R package. Spearman’s correlation analysis was applied to measure the correlation, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to compare the significance between two groups. Results with P below 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Cell culture

The human bronchial epithelial bronchial epithelial cells, adenovirus 12-SV40 2B immortalized (BEAS-2B) cell line (Kunming Cell Bank, Kunming, China) was cultured in complete medium consisting of 10% fetal bovine serum (Excell), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), 60% Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), and 29% 1640 medium, under humidified conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were subcultured every 2 to 3 days after trypsin digestion. The cells were divided into normal and nano-Fe2O3 co-culture groups.

Establishment of nano-Fe2O3 co-culture cell model

Normal human bronchial epithelial cells from the Kunming Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Kunming, China) were cultured in 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubators. A nano-Fe2O3 co-culture cell model was established by culturing the cells under conditions of 50 µg/mL iron for 24 hours.

Intracellular Fe3+ detection

Total iron levels in BEAS-2B cells were measured using a total iron colorimetric assay kit (E-BC-K880-M, Elabscience, USA). In this process, iron ions in the samples were converted to ferrous ions through a reducing agent and subsequently bound to a detection probe. The resulting substance exhibited a strong absorption peak at 593 nm, and its optical density (OD) was linearly correlated with iron concentration within a specific range. OD values were recorded at 593 nm using a microplate reader (2550, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). GraphPad Prism 8.0 was applied to statistical analysis.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Healthy-growing cells were plated into 96-well plates at densities ranging from 5,000 to 10,000 cells per well, with blank controls (no cells, but with an equal volume of culture medium). Cells were cultured and treated according to the experimental design [control group (CON), nano-Fe2O3 co-culture group, coal smoke dust group]. A volume of 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Abmart, USA) was added to each well and incubated for 2 hours (CCK-8 incubation conditions were identical to cell culture conditions). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Total cellular ROS measurement

Total cellular ROS levels were measured using the ROS detection kit containing 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin (DCFH-DA) (E-BC-K138-F, Elabscience, Houston, USA). DCFH-DA reacts with ROS to produce dichlorofluorescein, a fluorescent product. ROS levels in the cells were detected by flow cytometry. After trypsin digestion, cells were co-cultured with 1 µM DCFH-DA for 2 hours and washed with PBS. Similarly, LPO levels were measured using a fluorescence-based LPO assay kit (E-BC-F003, Elabscience, USA), which contains a fluorescent probe reacting with lipid free radicals in LPO pathways. The cells were set to 10,000 for detection using the FL1-A: FITC-A channel, and results were visualized using FlowJo (v10.6.2).

Western blot

Cell lysis buffer [Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer with protease inhibitor cocktail] was employed in total protein extraction. After centrifugation at 14,000 ×g for 10 minutes at 4 °C, protein concentrations in the lysates were determined by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method. Protein samples were separated using PAGE gel (Epizyme, Shanghai, China) and transferred to Polyvinylidene Difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with Western blocking buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and washed five times with Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) buffer for 3 minutes each. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies: FTH1 antibody (1:10,000; Proteintech, Rosemont, USA), GPX4 antibody (1:10,000; Proteintech, USA), and LCN2 antibody (1:5,000; Proteintech, USA). After washing with TBST, membranes were then incubated with secondary antibodies (1:10,000) for 1.5 hours at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized and captured via multifunctional gel imaging system (BioRad, California, USA) and quantified based on Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, USA).

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was performed using FITC-conjugated anti-rabbit (P0186, Beyotime, China) and anti-mouse (P0196, Beyotime, China) antibodies. BEAS-2B cells (1×105 cells/well) were seeded on sterile glass coverslips in a 6-well plate and grown for 24 hours. After experimental treatments, cells were stained as per the kit protocol, followed by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) incubation for 15 minutes and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washes (3 times, 10 minutes each). Anti-fade mounting medium was added, and fluorescence was observed under a microscope (Leica DM4000, Wetzlar, Germany). Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Results

Differential expression analysis and target gene selection

Differential expression analysis was performed on four mice exposed to welding fumes or air (control) (n=4) using the GSE34056 dataset. The volcano plot (Figure 1A) and heatmap (Figure 1B) highlighted substantial variations in gene expression between the welding fume-exposed samples and the CON, where red denotes upregulated genes and blue signifies downregulated genes. The volcano plot (Figure 1C) shows the gene expression differences between lung cancer and normal lung tissue in the TCGA. The Venn diagram (Figure 1D) intersected the DEGs from the samples with genes related to LUAD (TCGA-LUAD) and ferroptosis, resulting in the identification of five genes: LCN2, CP, ALAS2, ENO1, and TF. These five genes were selected for further analysis.

To further investigate the biological significance of LCN2, ENO1, ALAS2, CP, and TF, the functional enrichment analyses of GO and KEGG were launched. The results indicated that these genes are primarily located in blood microparticles, endoplasmic reticulum lumen, secretory granule lumen, and cytoplasmic vesicle lumen. They are involved in iron ion transport, intracellular iron ion homeostasis, and transition metal ion transport, and are most closely associated with iron ion binding functions (Figure 1E,1F). Based on KEGG pathway analysis, it is discovered that these genes are enriched in pathways like Ferroptosis, Porphyrin metabolism, HIF-1 signaling pathway, glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, mineral absorption, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis. These pathways play crucial roles in cellular damage, oxygen transport, energy metabolism, mineral absorption, and glycolysis. Notably, the enriched signaling pathways, particularly HIF-1 signaling pathway and mineral absorption-related pathway, are closely linked to the content explored in this study (Figure 1G,1H).

Expression of target genes and ROC curve validation of model reliability

Using the expression levels of LCN2, ENO1, CP, ALAS2, and TF as influencing factors, ROC curves were constructed, and AUC values were calculated. Differential comparisons and ROC curve construction were conducted on the TCGA-LUAD and GSE34056 datasets to validate the findings. In the TCGA-LUAD dataset, the differential expression of all five genes between the tumor group and the CON was statistically significant (P<0.05), with the following AUC values: LCN2 [AUC: 0.793; confidence interval (CI): 0.740–0.840], ENO1 (AUC: 0.904; CI: 0.873–0.933), CP (AUC: 0.896; CI: 0.864–0.927), ALAS2 (AUC: 0.893; CI: 0.847–0.932), and TF (AUC: 0.650; CI: 0.584–0.710) (Figure 2A-2E). In the GSE34056 dataset, all five genes showed statistically significant differences between the welding fume-exposed group (WFs) and the CON, with the following AUC values: LCN2 (AUC: 1.000; CI: 1.000–1.000), ENO1 (AUC: 0.984; CI: 0.906–1.000), CP (AUC: 1.000; CI: 1.000–1.000), ALAS2 (AUC: 0.844; CI: 0.625–1.000), and TF (AUC: 0.984; CI: 0.906–1.000) (Figure 2F-2J). Furthermore, the expression trends of LCN2 (Figure 2A,2F), ENO1 (Figure 2B,2G), ALAS2 (Figure 2D,2I), and TF (Figure 2E,2J) were consistent across both the TCGA-LUAD and GSE34056 datasets, with LCN2, ENO1, and CP being upregulated in LUAD and upon exposure to welding fumes, while ALAS2 expression was downregulated. The results were further validated using the GSE43458 dataset for TCGA-LUAD (Figure S2A-S2E) and the GSE20174 dataset for GSE34056 (Figure S2F-S2H). Consequently, the four genes with consistent expression trends were selected for further analysis.

To explore potential carcinogenic pathways induced by welding fume exposure, single-gene GSEA analyses were performed for the four selected genes. The GO enrichment results showed that these genes are associated with the electron transport chain (ETC) (Figure 3A), adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis coupled electron transport (Figure 3B), endoplasmic reticulum stress response (Figure 3C), and inflammatory response (Figure 3D). KEGG enrichment results revealed that these genes are involved in pathways related to chemical carcinogenesis—ROS (Figure 3E), oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (Figure 3F), TNF signaling pathway (Figure 3G), and neutrophil extracellular trap formation (Figure 3H). Notably, both ROS and electron transport are closely bound up with iron, which may offer valuable insights into the mechanism of action related to welding fume exposure. Relationship between LCN2 expression and lung cancer prognosis

Figure 4 illustrates the correlation between LCN2 expression levels and survival durations of patients diagnosed with LUAD and lung squamous cell carcinoma in the Kaplan-Meier plotter (KM plotter) database. Elevated LCN2 expression was strongly linked to poorer outcomes in both OS and FP for LUAD (Figure 4A,4C). In contrast, LCN2 expression exhibited no significant correlation with survival rates in lung squamous cell carcinoma. These results suggest that high LCN2 expression may act as an independent prognostic risk factor for OS and FP in LUAD.

Cellular validation

Protein expression profiling was performed via Western Blot to assess nano-Fe2O3-induced modulation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), and LCN2 under co-culture conditions. The results demonstrated that the nano-Fe2O3 group exhibited markedly higher expression levels of LCN2 (Figure 5A; P=0.006), GPX4 (Figure 5B; P=0.03), and FTH1 (Figure 5B; P=0.002) compared to the CON. with these differences being statistically significant. Flow cytometry confirmed a significant increase in ROS levels in cells co-cultured with nano-Fe2O3 for 24 hours (Figure 5C). Mitochondrial changes in BEAS-2B cells were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) following 24-hour exposure to nano-Fe2O3. In the treated group, mitochondria (red arrow) exhibited discernible alterations such as a reduction in cristae and swelling (green arrow), compared to those in untreated cells (Figure 5D). Immunofluorescence staining was employed to verify the protein expression levels of GPX4 (Figure 5E; P=0.047) and FTH1 (Figure 5F; P=0.03) in both groups. We have plotted the Western Blot results of LCN2 (Figure 5G), GPX4 (Figure 5H), and FTH1 (Figure 5I) into charts for a more intuitive presentation. The results further confirmed a significant upregulation of these proteins in the nano-Fe2O3 group relative to the CON (P<0.05). The changes in Fe3+ content in BEAS-2B cells following co-culture with nano-Fe2O3 for 24 hours were quantitatively analyzed. The results revealed a statistically significant elevation of Fe3+ levels in the nano-Fe2O3 group compared to the CON (Figure 5J; P<0.001). We also plotted the immunofluorescence results of GPX4 (Figure 5K) and FTH1 (Figure 5L) in a graph for a more intuitive display.

Correlation analysis of pro-inflammatory cytokines and LCN2 drug sensitivity

To evaluate the relationship between the selected target genes and pro-inflammatory cytokines, correlation analyses were performed on key genes implicated in inflammatory signaling pathways like VCAM1, TRAF2, and TRAF3. They are known to be vital in inflammation and immune responses (Figure 6A). The results suggested a positive correlation (P<0.05) between the target genes and multiple key inflammatory signaling pathways, suggesting that inflammation may contribute to disease progression in lung cancer. Furthermore, the correlation between LCN2 expression and the IC50 values of 192 anticancer drugs was analyzed. The analysis concluded that patients with elevated LCN2 expression were resistant to several anticancer agents used in lung cancer treatment, including A-770041, BAY 61-3606, Bleomycin, CMK, BX-912, and doxorubicin (Figure 6B-6G). It suggests that lung cancer patients with elevated LCN2 expression may suffer poorer clinical prognosis.

Correlation analysis of the target gene with pro-inflammatory cytokines (A) and the correlation between LCN2 expression and IC50 values of anti-cancer drugs (B-G). IC50, half maximal inhibitory concentration.

Single-gene pan-cancer analysis

Based on RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from 33 tumor types in the TCGA database, a systematic evaluation of the expression profiles of LCN2, ENO1, CP, and ALAS2 in pan-cancer tissues was conducted. The following observations were made: (I) LCN2 was found to be expressed at lower levels in uveal melanoma (UVM) but higher levels in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD). Both paired and unpaired analyses revealed significant upregulation of LCN2 in LUAD, colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) compared with adjacent tissues (P<0.05; Figure 7A,7B). (II) ENO1 showed low expression in normal bile duct tissue but high expression in kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC). Both paired and unpaired analyses demonstrated that ENO1 was significantly upregulated in LUAD, bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), COAD, esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), KIRC, LIHC, lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), and UCEC compared with adjacent tissues (P<0.05; Figure 7C,7D). (III) CP was expressed at low levels in kidney chromophobe (KICH) but high levels in normal hepatocytes. The results indicated that CP was expressed at higher levels in LUAD, glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), KIRC, LUSC, STAD, and UCEC compared to adjacent tissues; however, in contrast to LCN2 and ENO1, CP was significantly downregulated in breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), COAD, HNSC, KIRP, LIHC, pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), and THCA (P<0.05; Figure 7E,7F). (IV) ALAS2 displayed low expression in normal skin tissue abut high expression in acute myeloid leukemia (LAML). Both paired and unpaired analyses showed that ALAS2 was significantly downregulated in LUAD, BRCA, COAD, KICH, LIHC, LUSC, STAD, and THCA (P<0.05; Figure 7G,7H). Notably, the expression trends of LCN2, ENO1, and ALAS2 in human tumors were highly consistent with the differential expression patterns observed in welding fume-exposed mouse tissues (GSE34056), suggesting that these genes might be involved in tumorigenesis induced by welding fume exposure.

Discussion

Cancer, as the primary cause of death among human diseases, severely impacts human health and societal development. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) published the 2022 global incidence and mortality statistics for 36 cancer types across 185 countries (https://gco.iarc.who.int). Among these, lung cancer, a common malignancy, revealed alarming figures of 2.5 million newly diagnosed cases and 1.8 million fatalities in 2022 alone. Representing 12.4% of all newly reported cancer cases and 18.7% of all cancer-related deaths, lung cancer claimed the top position in both categories (1), thus imposing an overwhelming socioeconomic burden. Consequently, the quest for novel biomarkers becomes imperative in facilitating the early diagnosis and prevention of lung cancer. Fine particulate (<2.5 µm in diameter) can enter and deposit in the lungs via the respiratory tract, leading to long-term health hazards (29). Furthermore, epidemiological evidence indicates that males with occupational exposure to high-iron environments (e.g., areas near iron foundries or steel mills where iron-containing dust is present) exhibit an increased relative risk of developing lung cancer (26,27). These observations suggest that RICPM may harbor potential carcinogenic mechanisms; however, the specific mechanisms remain largely unexplored. This study seeks to determine if deposition of RICPM in the lungs induces systemic damage and to uncover the oncogenic mechanisms by which they drive lung cancer development.

Ferroptosis, first described by Dixon et al. in 2012, is an iron-dependent, GPX4-regulated form of programmed cell death distinct from apoptosis, pyroptosis, and autophagy; it is characterized by excessive accumulation of ROS and lipid peroxides (5). Cytologically, ferroptosis is marked by mitochondrial shrinkage, loss of cristae, reduced mitochondrial numbers, membrane permeabilization, and increased membrane density. Cellular iron accumulation is a hallmark of ferroptosis, and various iron-regulatory proteins such as FTH1 and transferrin receptor (TFR) are involved in this process. Research has shown that excess iron can induce ferroptosis through the Fenton reaction, resulting in ROS generation; the heightened uptake of iron coupled with a diminished capacity for iron storage may culminate in iron overload, thereby triggering ferroptosis (30-33). Extensive research on ferroptosis in cancer has advanced its potential as a therapeutic target (6). Co-culture of BEAS-2B cells with nano-Fe2O3 resulted in upregulated GPX4 and FTH1 expression, which implies exacerbated intracellular oxidative stress and impaired iron homeostasis (Figure 5A,5B,5E,5F). Nevertheless, ferroptosis may serve as a double-edged sword, as its occurrence is driven by ROS and lipid peroxides—molecules that themselves can promote carcinogenesis (34). Despite an upregulation of GPX4 and FTH1 that bolstered antioxidant defense in BEAS-2B cells, intracellular ROS and Fe3+ levels remained elevated compared to normal cells (Figure 5C,5J). This indicates an inadequate clearance capacity that may culminate in damage induced by deposited RICPM. While cells may upregulate antioxidant defense mechanisms to counteract the accumulation of ROS and lipid peroxides, enhanced antioxidant capacity is closely linked to tumorigenesis (35-37). This study confirmed that under experimental conditions, nano-Fe2O3 co-culture induced significant overexpression of GPX4 and FTH1 in BEAS-2B cells (Figure 5B,5E,5F,5K,5L). GPX4 is involved in the reduction of phospholipid hydroperoxides on cell membranes, thereby protecting cells against ferroptosis (38). whereas FTH1, a ferritin with ferroxidase activity, converts ferrous iron to ferric iron for storage (39) and its overexpression can suppress ferroptosis or even reduce the efficacy of anticancer drugs (40). Moreover, LCN2 is closely bound up with ferroptosis; research has proved that silencing LCN2 promotes ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma, whereas its overexpression inhibits ferroptosis (41). The experimental results in this study also confirmed that, following a 24-hour co-culture with nano-Fe2O3, LCN2 is markedly overexpressed in normal human bronchial epithelial cells (Figure 5A,5G). Thus, it is posited that upon entering the lung, RICPM may enhance the antioxidant defense mechanisms of lung cells while inhibiting ferroptosis, thereby potentially favoring tumorigenesis.

In this study, the differential gene expression between lung tissues of mice exposed to welding fumes and those of unexposed controls was analyzed using data from the GEO database. By intersecting these DEGs with those associated with LUAD (TCGA-LUAD) and ferroptosis-related genes, five key genes—LCN2, CP, ALAS2, ENO1, and TF—were identified (Figure 1D). The reliability of the selection model was subsequently validated through ROC curve analyses (Figure 2A-2J). GO enrichment analysis revealed that the enriched genes were predominantly involved in many biological processes like iron ion transport, intracellular iron ion homeostasis, and transition metal ion transport. This suggests that iron is vital in mediating lung tissue damage following welding fume inhalation. Moreover, molecular function (MF) analysis further demonstrated that these genes are principally involved in iron ion binding (Figure 1E). The role of iron in cancer development and progression is well established, as it promotes tumor cell invasion and migration (11), Central to iron’s function is its redox capability, particularly its interaction with oxygen. In vivo, iron exists in various oxidation states, commonly interconverting between ferrous (Fe²⁺) and ferric (Fe3+) forms (Iron exists in a wide range of oxidation states). Ferric iron is generally stable and exerts its biological effects when bound to proteins (transport proteins), whereas ferrous iron is highly reactive and, when present in excess, can generate substantial ROS via the Fenton reaction. This ROS generation is associated with damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, ultimately resulting in cellular damage or death (12,16). It is currently believed that iron-induced carcinogenesis is primarily mediated by DNA damage resulting from ROS generated through the Fenton reaction (42-44). Consistent with these findings, subsequent experiments measured intracellular Fe3+ (Figure 5J) and ROS levels (Figure 5C) in cells co-cultured with nano-Fe2O3 for 24 hours; the results revealed that nano-Fe2O3 markedly elevated both Fe3+ and ROS levels, aligning with the findings from the bioinformatics analyses. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the enriched pathways, such as Ferroptosis, the HIF-1 signaling pathway, and Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis, played significant roles in tumor progression (36,45,46), Both unpaired and paired comparisons further demonstrated that the expression trends of LCN2, ENO1, and ALAS2 in various tumors (Figure 7A-7H) were consistent with those observed in the GSE34056 dataset (Figure 2A-2J). These findings suggest that iron may contribute to lung cancer by modulating the normal processes of ferroptosis via its effects on LCN2, ENO1, and ALAS2. Notably, as an iron-regulatory protein, LCN2 is likely to be involved in this process (25).

LCN2 is a secreted glycoprotein widely expressed in a number of tissues and cell types, which mediates physiological and pathological processes such as cell differentiation, apoptosis, and inflammation (19,21,47-49). Although LCN2 does not directly bind iron, it interacts with iron through the formation of a ternary complex with iron carriers, thereby exhibiting high affinity for insoluble ferric ions (21), The results confirmed that following a 24-hour co-culture with nano-Fe2O3, intracellular Fe3+ levels were significantly elevated (Figure 5J). In addition to promoting iron accumulation in tumor cells, LCN2 also enhances tumor cell migration and invasion (19). Some studies have suggested that the oncogenic role of LCN2 in LUAD may be associated with the Nrf2/HO-1 and NF-κB signaling pathways (50,51). Accordingly, the expression levels of LCN2 were evaluated in normal human bronchial epithelial cells following co-culture with nano-Fe2O3 (Figure 5A,5G), and the results demonstrated that nano-Fe2O3 promoted LCN2 overexpression under the experimental conditions, thereby validating the analytical findings.

To further elucidate the functions of LCN2, ENO1, CP, and ALAS2, single-gene GSEA analyses were conducted. Intriguingly, high LCN2 expression was found to be associated with the ETC (Figure 3A) and chemical carcinogenesis-ROS (Figure 3E). Experimental results demonstrated that under the specified conditions, exposure to nano-Fe2O3 significantly increased intracellular ROS levels, implying that ROS may be one of the key carcinogenic factors following the pulmonary deposition of RICPM. In contrast, high ENO1 expression was correlated with ATP synthesis coupled electron transport (Figure 3B) and OXPHOS (Figure 3F). The ETC, an essential process in the inner mitochondrial membrane of mammalian cells, governs energy production in eukaryotic cells via OXPHOS (52,53), dysregulation of the OXPHOS metabolic pathway is a common feature of cancer cells (54). Indeed, Observations in BEAS-2B cells co-cultured with nano-Fe2O3 for 24 hours revealed alterations in mitochondrial morphology, including reduced cristae and swelling (→). These data suggest that RICPM may impair mitochondrial function and disrupt the normal progression of the ETC in lung cells, potentially contributing to lung carcinogenesis. However, the precise mechanisms require further investigation.

Iron, an essential trace element, exhibits a dual role in human health; both deficiency and overload can be detrimental and potentially promote cancer initiation and recurrence (55). This is supported by epidemiological data linking higher dietary iron intake to elevated risks for multiple cancer types, including esophageal, colorectal, liver, lung, and laryngeal cancers (56). Furthermore, the excessive accumulation of iron-mediated ROS in cells, which damages DNA and organelles (e.g., mitochondria), is also considered a key carcinogenic mechanism (42,43). Cigarette smoke contains a multitude of harmful substances, such as nicotine, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH) and N-nitrosamines. Long-term accumulation of these compounds is known to induce DNA damage and mutations, thereby increasing the risk of lung cancer. Lifelong smokers have been shown to exhibit a 20-fold higher incidence of lung cancer compared to non-smokers (57,58). Therefore, the mechanistic overlaps between smoke-induced lung injury and RICPM toxicity offer insightful directions for further exploration.

While this study investigates the potential lung cancer-causing pathways of RICPM and provides certain insights, it is essential to acknowledge its limitations. In the cellular model, only nano-Fe2O3 particles were utilized; however, in real-world scenarios, individuals are exposed to more complex iron oxide mixtures, highlighting the intricate carcinogenic mechanisms associated with RICPM. Moreover, this study lacks a deep investigation into the specific carcinogenic mechanisms of RICPM and additional functional experiments to validate the precise roles of these genes during carcinogenesis. Due to the time constraints, a comprehensive exploration of the specific carcinogenic mechanisms of RICPM was not feasible. Therefore, future research should prioritize focusing on these core genes to offer more strategies for the prevention and treatment of lung cancer (Figure S1).

Conclusions

In summary, comprehensive bioinformatics analyses reveal potential mechanisms by which RICPM may contribute to lung carcinogenesis, with LCN2 emerging as a key effector gene. Future investigations focusing on elucidating the detailed carcinogenic mechanisms of these particulates are expected, which may ultimately inform novel preventive strategies for individuals in high-risk occupational settings and provide new therapeutic targets for lung cancer.
