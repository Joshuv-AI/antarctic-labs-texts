Using 3D-printing technology for patient education: a review of the literature
Authors: Sarah Masanet, Marthe-Aline Jutand, Gaëlle Margue, Hélène Hoarau, Jean-Christophe Bernhard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground3D printing’ is increasingly present in the health sector. The introduction of 3D printed models into the patient care pathway can be seen as a new patient education tool based on the principle of 'see to understand'. The aim of this review is to describe studies investigating the contribution of printed models to patient care and education, and to examine their limitations.Main body of the abstractA comprehensive PubMed database search was conducted to identify relevant studies. No date, author or language restrictions were imposed. This review focused on studying the impact of a 3D organ model on 5 categories: understanding of the disease and/or the anatomy of the organ, understanding of the surgical plan and its implications, doctor-patient communication, patient satisfaction and patient anxiety. The review selected 45 articles published between 2015 and 2024. Of these, 41 articles investigated the effect of using a 3D model on understanding of the disease and/or the organ concerned. 33 articles evaluated the understanding of treatment, and the risks associated. 13 articles assessed the effect of the model on doctor-patient communication. Patient satisfaction was measured in 22 articles, and 9 articles measured patient anxiety.ConclusionMost of the articles analyzed—27 out of 45—demonstrate a significant enhancement in at least one category of patient education, underscoring the promising potential of 3D technology in this field. However, several methodological limitations temper these promising findings, highlighting the need for further research. Future studies should address these limitations and explore new methodologies to fully exploit 3D's potential.Keywords: Three-dimensional, Patient experience, Review, 3D printed models

Background

“3D printing” emerged in the 1980s, mainly in the design field, initially to visualize new products before they were marketed. The first prosthesis implanted in a human appeared in 1999. But it's only in the last fifteen years that this technology has been developed, particularly in the health sector. It now has a number of different functions. Specifically, it can be used for drug manufacturing, bioprinting, and medical device manufacturing [1]. Medical devices include several areas: production of surgical guides, production of patient implants, and production of anatomical models [2].

Many sites on the Internet offer anatomical models of various body parts, at high prices. These models are not limited to healthy organs but also include anatomical models representing organ-specific pathologies.

Exploring the potential effects of integrating anatomical models into the patient's care pathway presents a new area of research. This integration could alter various aspects of the interaction between healthcare professionals and patients, potentially enhancing the patient's comprehension and knowledge of their pathology and/or the printed organ. By fostering a deeper understanding of the surgical implications of their condition, patients may experience increased satisfaction with their overall care [3].

Making medical jargon understandable to patients and their families is a key point in improving patient care [4]. The doctor-patient relationship can be defined as asymmetrical, meaning that the doctor has medical knowledge while the patient does not. However, the healthcare relationship requires the sharing of some of this knowledge to explain or even educate the patient about his or her condition. 3D printed models can complement existing tools, such as diagrams and scans, used during medical consultations to enhance the transfer of information and improve patient understanding. Two types of 3D printed models are used: personalized models or generic models. The incorporation of 3D printed models into patient care serves as an innovative educational tool, adhering to the principle of "see to understand" with varying degrees of personalization.

This review aims to describe studies that have investigated the role of printed models in patient care and education, and to examine the limitations identified within these studies.

Methods

A bibliographic search of the Pubmed database was conducted using the terms '3D print', '3D printing', '3D Printed', 'three-dimensional print', 'three-dimensional printing', 'three-dimensional printed' in conjunction with the terms 'patient education', 'patient information', 'patient communication' or 'patient experience'. Initially, no date, author or language restrictions were imposed for the bibliographic search. The search was conducted in July 2024.

The first step was to carry out a selection based on the title, using the following inclusion criteria: the articles had to be full-text and written in either English or French, and they had to examine the impact of 3D-printed organ models on patient experience (see Fig. 1). This was followed by an evaluation of the abstract, and then of the full article, according to the predefined inclusion criteria. Articles focusing on the use of this technology for surgical training or evaluating its impact as perceived by caregivers were excluded. Case studies and case series were also excluded from this analysis. From each of the targeted literature reviews, articles addressing patient education through a 3D model were identified and included in the final corpus.

For each article, the following data were collected: authors, year of publication, pathology and organ studied, type of 3D modelling (generic or patient specific such as 3D printing from individual CT scan), number of groups and patients in each group, study design, and study status concerning each of the specified categories.

Results

Study identification and selection

Two hundred and eight articles were identified with the bibliographic search equation. After the selection process, presented by Fig. 1, the final corpus comprised 45 research articles described in Table 1.

General characteristics of the included studies

The articles, included in this review, were published between 2015 and 2024. The publication years showed variation, with ten articles published in 2018 and a singular article in 2015. Additionally, eight articles appeared between 2021–2022, six in 2023, and two from January to June 2024.

Among all the articles, urology emerged as the most studied specialty (n = 11). Within this field, 8 articles focused on 3D printed kidney, 2 on kidney and prostate model, and 1 on excretory tract model. In orthopaedics (n = 9), various fracture types were represented, including articular fractures of the calcaneus (n = 2), tibial plateau, distal radius (n = 2), tibia, humerus, trimalleolar, among others. Seven studies explored neurosurgical models, with aneurysm modeling being the most common (n = 3), alongside models of the brain modeling, skull base meningioma, brain arteriovenous malformation, brain tumors and surrounding functional areas. In cardiology, heart models were predominant (n = 4). Additionally, models of the liver (n = 2), stomach, rectum, pancreas, lung, lumbar vertebrae and nerve, thyroid, hand, retroperitoneal tumors and mesenteric tumors were developed across various studies.

The effect of a personalized 3D printed model on patient education and information was evaluated in 34 studies [6–12, 14–30, 33, 34, 37, 38, 40, 45–47, 49, 50]. The remaining 11 studies examined the use of generic 3D printed models [13, 31, 32, 35, 36, 39, 41–44, 48]. There are no studies comparing the effects of introducing a generic 3D printed model and a personalized 3D printed model.

The selected studies adopted two types of study design: randomized controlled trials (RCT) and before-after studies. In 24 studies, participants were randomly assigned to one of the study groups: 20 studies employed a two-arm design (control and experimental), while 4 studies used three comparison arms. Participants received information according to their group allocation (e.g., 3D printed model, virtual 3D model, scan). The control group typically received information based on the patient's Computed Tomography (CT) or Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scan, a 2D diagram (such as a doctor's sketch), or standardized information sheets. Twenty-one studies used a single-group method, in which the 3D printed model was presented to all participants.

Forty-three studies employed questionnaires, one used qualitative semi-structured interviews [19], and one applied a mixed method involving both semi-structured interviews and questionnaires [36]. The categories assessed with these instruments varied among studies. Patient understanding of the disease and its treatment was measured using scores derived from true/false questions. Likert-type questions were utilized to assess patient perception, satisfaction, and communication quality with the physician. For patient anxiety, three studies used the State Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) and two employed the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS).

Sixteen studies used descriptive analysis to depict the effect of 3D models on patients, while the remaining studies compared these effects using statistical tests. In studies employing descriptive analysis, the authors calculate the mean and standard deviation of patient scores on ordinal scales. However, questionnaires are only administered after the 3D model has been used, which makes statistical comparison impossible.

Understanding of disease and/or organ anatomy

Forty-one articles investigated the effect of using a 3D model during preoperative visits on the understanding of the disease and/or organ anatomy (see Table 2). Twenty-six articles demonstrated a significant enhancement in understanding with the use of a 3D printed model, including 17 randomized controlled trials and 9 "before-after" studies.

Results of 41 studies investigating the impact of 3D-printed organ models on disease understanding

The beneficial impact of 3D model on knowledge levels has been confirmed across various surgical contexts, including cerebral aneurysm [24, 46], percutaneous nephrolithotomy [12], deep brain stimulation [39], endoscopic sinus surgery [13], hepatobiliary surgery [45] and partial nephrectomy [7, 17, 37, 38].

Additionally, the use of 3D models has shown positive effects in patients with diverse pathologies including different types of cancer [25, 27, 31, 32], fractures [15, 16, 28, 30], coronary artery disease [11] degenerative lumbar disease [26], femoroacetabular impingement syndrome [49], or complex staghorn kidney stones [40].

Four studies also found significant improvements in disease understanding in parents of children with retroperitoneal tumor [47], ventricular septal defect [35] or hepatic tumor [14], and in patients undergoing cardiac catheterization [43]. Two comparative studies did not demonstrate significant improvement with 3D-printed models [6, 44]; however, comprehension scores were higher in the 3D-printed model groups than in the control groups.

Finally, 13 studies asked patients to rate the extent to which using 3D models had improved their understanding [8–10, 18, 22, 23, 29, 34, 36, 41, 42, 48, 50]. This measurement was only taken after the model had been visualised, so it was described without comparison and no statistical tests were performed.

Results of 33 studies investigating the impact of 3D-printed organ models on understanding the surgical plan

Of the 13 studies comparing an experimental group (3D printed model) to a control group, ten [13, 15, 16, 27, 28, 35, 40, 43, 46, 49] reported significantly higher surgical comprehension scores in the experimental group. A similar significant improvement in comprehension scores was observed in seven "before and after" studies that conducted comparison tests following the use of a 3D-printed model [7, 12, 14, 17, 31, 37, 38].

Four studies found no significant difference in understanding of the proposed treatment between the experimental (3D-printed model) and control groups [25, 39, 44, 45]. In the study by Hirt et al. [39], 95% of participants (across both control and experimental groups) reported that the preoperative consultation, whether with or without the model, aided their understanding of the surgical plan. The study by Grab et al. [44], which compared three patient education tools (standard paper-based education, education on a virtual 3D heart model and education on a 3D printed model) found no significant difference in understanding of the surgical procedure between the experimental group (3D printed model) and the other groups. However, it did reveal a significantly higher mean comprehension score in the group using the Virtual 3D model compared to the control group.

Doctor–patient communication

Thirteen articles investigated the impact of 3D printed models on doctor–patient communication (see Table 4), including only one with the generalized 3D-printed model. Only two studies conducted comparisons between different groups and reported a significant improvement in doctor–patient communication when the 3D printed-model was used [15, 33]. In the remaining 11 studies, the authors provided descriptive data [8, 10, 16, 18, 19, 21–23, 28, 29, 39]. Twelve of the studies introduced a personalized 3D printed model and only one, a generic 3D printed model.

In the study by Grab et al., [44] anxiety, was measured using the STAI and the VAS scale, in patients before and after an education period: the control group received a diagram-based education, another group used the 3D printed model, and the final group used the virtual 3D model. The VAS scale measurement showed a non-significant decrease in anxiety for the control and printed model groups, but a significant reduction for patients in the virtual 3D model group. Using the STAI measure, the authors described a non-significant decrease in anxiety for the control and virtual reality groups, and a non-significant increase for the 3D model group.

In the study by Sander et al., [13] involving a sinus model, the majority of the 100 patients surveyed indicated that their anxiety decreased following the explanations with no significant difference between the control group (n = 32) and the 3D group (n = 39).

Three studies assessed anxiety levels using a multiple-choice question without employing statistical tests [11, 36, 45]. In the study by Biglino et al., 32% of participants reported increased anxiety about their health after using a patient-specific 3D model of congenital heart disease [11]. In another study using semi-structured interviews, emotions such as fear, and anger were also reported after viewing the 3D printed model. The researchers mentioned that the images seemed 'less real' than the model, which made the tumor feel more personal or real [19].

Conclusion

Discussion

Forty-five research articles on the use of three-dimensional printing of organs for patient education were analysed in this literature review. The positive impact of this technology on patient education was evident in most articles reviewed: 27 of the 45 articles demonstrated significant improvement in at least one category. The primary categoriesexamined in the articles were: understanding of the disease and/or organ anatomy as well as understanding of the surgical plan and its implications. Specifically, 41 articles evaluated the knowledge score related to disease and anatomy, with 26 reporting significant improvement using the 3D printed model. Similarly, 33 articles focused on understanding treatment and its effects, of which 17 showed a significant improvement with the 3D printed model.

In terms of patient experience, the review focused on three categories: the quality of clinician-patient communication, patient satisfaction with their treatment, and anxiety associated with using the model. These categorieswere explored in less than one-third of the articles, including only one with the use of a generalized 3D printed model. Regarding communication, the findings were not sufficiently detailed across articles to conclusively determine whether a 3D printed model improves interaction. Patient satisfaction appeared to increase with the use of a 3D printed model.

Anxiety was investigated in only nine studies, employing various tools and yielding inconsistent results, and only two of them with personalized 3D printed model. Most studies did not show a reduction in patient anxiety, and some suggested that the model might have an adverse effect. Visualizing the organ and its pathology could evoke negative emotions by imparting a tangible reality. To understand these discrepancies, it is critical to conduct a deeper analysis of patients' reactions to the models. Identifying factors that may explain these differences will require consideration of the patients' relationships with their illness, as well as their overall environment. Two studies are currently underway into the patient experience of 3D-printed models (NCT06035211 and NCT06379698).

One limitation in comparing study results arises from the definition of few categories studied such as communication or satisfaction which are particularly challenging to define. It is often unclear what researchers refer to when discussing communication, which might encompass active listening by the patient or a clear and detailed explanation of symptoms. This ambiguity contributes to the heterogeneity of the results obtained.

These findings are also constrained by the methodological choices across various studies. Notably, many studies had small sample sizes, with 17 conducted on groups of 30 patients or fewer. This methodological limitation results in insufficient statistical power for conducting robust tests. Additionally, the heterogeneity of data collection tools should also be emphasised, partly caused by the diversity of pathologies and treatments, which limits study comparability. Knowledge questionnaires, for example, are often created ad hoc by researchers without validation. These tools are tailored to the organ, disease, or treatment being presented to the patient. Future studies should encourage the use of standardized questionnaires, such as the one developed by Schlegel et al., [5] to facilitate cross-study comparisons. However, such standardization may not capture the specificities associated with each organ.

Further limitations may relate to study design. In exposure/non-exposure studies, participants are made aware of different experimental groups without knowing their assigned group. These studies may incorporate additional sessions into the care pathway raising the question: does the model alone enhance understanding, or is an educational session, which was absent prior to the study, also required? This hypothesis cannot be verified without knowledge of the patient's prior clinical management. Future studies should consider this aspect in their design. Furthermore, satisfaction is particularly sensitive to context; thus, patients assigned to the experimental group may be biased when responding to questionnaires. For instance, the control group (i.e. the group without access to a 3D printed or virtual model) may report lower satisfaction compared to the experimental group due to the absence of innovation.

Some results [44] suggest that a virtual model may be more beneficial than a 3D printed model in terms of patient information. However, other studies report the opposite [26, 27], with no consensus reached on this issue. The lack of convergence in results may be explained by visual differences between 3D printed and virtual models. Moreover, the utility of 3D modelling may vary depending on the organ printed and the complexity of the surgical procedure. In most articles, the comparative results between 3D printed and virtual models are not thoroughly detailed. As with a 3D-printed model, a virtual 3D model provides visual assistance to the patient. Nevertheless, the production time and costs for 3D virtual models are lower than for a 3D-printed model. These factors must be given full consideration when contemplating the integration of these novel tools into routine patient care.

Most of the articles reviewed employ a quantitative methodology based on questionnaires, which allows for the measurement of the impact of these new tools at specific points in the patient's care pathway. However, a limitation is that these studies do not often consider or describe the entire patient pathway. The care pathway is specific to each disease and may vary according to the patient's medical situation. This variation and lack of comprehensive pathway descriptions limit the analysis of the impact of these new tools on the overall patient experience. The results are evaluated within the context of clinical research, potentially influencing the behaviour of participants (both carers and patients) and introducing bias. The study and effect of this tool should extend beyond a single consultation to enhance understanding of its integration into the care pathway. It is crucial to consider and investigate the long-term use of these tools within a department, involving various carers and patients. Future research should account for the environment into which the 3D printed model is introduced, necessitating mixed-methods studies.

Conclusion

A strength of this work is its broad scope, encompassing various surgical specialties or pathologies, thereby providing an overview of the established data on the use of 3D printing for patient education. However, there is a risk that the results are not exhaustive, given the limitation of the search to the PubMed database and the choice of keywords in the search equation. These factors challenge the generalization of the findings.

To our knowledge, this analysis represents the first comprehensive examination of the use of 3D printing for patient education without focusing on specific surgeries or organs.

The extensive literature on 3D printing in the medical field underscores the growing interest in this innovative technology. However, its application for patient education remains underexplored. This literature review provides an overview of the existing research on the use of a 3D model for educational purposes and highlights potential avenues for the future applications in healthcare.

Several methodological limitations identified in this review should guide future studies in this area. As 3D modeling tools offer opportunities to enhance healthcare practices and patient experiences, it is imperative to continue field-based research on their use. Future studies should especially consider the broader healthcare environment and the importance of employing standardized measurement tools. Additionally, understanding the perceptions of healthcare providers and examining caregiver-patient interactions involving 3D models are essential for optimizing the integration of this technology into clinical practice.
