Synthesis, in-vitro and in-silico bioactivity and computational studies of pyrene and p-methoxycinnamaldehyde linked 4-hydroxycoumarins
Authors: Sachu Sadan, Elambalassery G Jayasree, Archana Velloth, Durga Nandan, Rajaguru Aradhya, Padmaja Parameswaran Nampi, Preetha G Prasad, Zeena S Pillai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract4-Hydroxycoumarin is a well-known pharmacophore of significant relevance in medicinal and pharmaceutical chemistry. The pursuit of multifunctional therapeutic agents continues to inspire the exploration of coumarin-based scaffolds. The derivatives of 4-hydroxycoumarin have been intriguing to researchers for drug design, synthesis, and the investigation of their therapeutic potential particularly against microbial infections and cancer. This study reports the design, synthesis, structural characterization and comprehensive in-vitro and in-silico evaluation of two novel 4-hydroxycoumarin derivatives: 3,3′-(pyren-1-ylmethylene) bis(4-hydroxy-2 H-chromen-2-one) (PBHC) and (E)-3,3′-(3-(4-methoxyphenyl) prop-2-ene-1,1-diyl) bis(4-hydroxy-2 H-chromen-2-one) (MPBHC). The antibacterial and anti-cancer activity of these derivatives are evaluated. The cytotoxicity was assessed on HEK-293 normal cells to evaluate selectivity. PBHC and MPBHC were synthesized and structurally confirmed through, FT-IR, NMR, HRMS and UV–Vis spectroscopic techniques. Quantum chemical investigations employing density functional theory (DFT, B3LYP/6-311 + G(d, p)) and time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) accurately reproduced the electronic absorption spectra and provided insights into electronic transitions Molecular electrostatic potential (MESP) mapping, Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis and topological investigations consisting of ELF, LOL, ALIF, NCI-RGD, and QTAIM analyses were conducted to identify the reactive centers and characterize the non-covalent interaction networks. As per the biological evaluation studies it was observed that PBHC displayed significant antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and exhibited selective cytotoxicity toward HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells (IC₅₀ = 46.69 ± 0.021 µM, the cytotoxicity of PBHC towards HEK-293 normal cells shows an IC50 of 10.24 ± 1.2 µM. In contrast, MPBHC displayed enhanced cytotoxic potency against HepG2 cells (IC₅₀ = 9.407 ± 0.300 µM) with comparatively lower cytotoxicity toward HEK-293 normal cells (IC₅₀ = 47.59 ± 2.5 µM), moderate activity toward MCF-7 breast carcinoma cells (IC₅₀ = 47.33 ± 0.19 µM), and strong anti-biofilm efficacy against S. aureus (MBIC = 0.1 µg/mL). Pharmacokinetic predictions and toxicity profiling further indicated favourable drug-likeness and safety (oral LD₅₀ ≈ 3000 mg/kg). This study reveals that structural alteration of 4‑hydroxycoumarin derivatives can substantially affect the drug‑like properties and their bioactivity, offering vital direction for the development of future antibacterial and anticancer medicines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54724-5.Keywords: 4-hydroxy coumarin, DFT, Cytotoxicity, Antibacterial, Antibiofilm, ADME, Molecular docking, In-silico toxicity studySubject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer, Chemical biology, Chemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Bacterial contamination poses a critical global threat across healthcare, food safety, water purification and environmental systems. The increasing spread of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria, covering both Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains, has become a serious global concern. This escalation is largely linked to the excessive and often uneven use of antibiotics, along with the ability of microbes to adapt and develop resistance. Over the last twenty years, the frequency of MDR cases has increased considerably, and infections associated with biofilm formation have made the problem even more persistent. Therefore, designing and developing new small-molecule antibacterial agents with specific and selective modes of action has become essential1–4. In parallel, cancer particularly hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2) and metastatic breast cancer (MCF-7) continues to demand innovative therapeutics with enhanced efficacy, selectivity, and safety.

Over the past decade, researchers have been developing novel antibacterial agents utilising 4-hydroxycoumarin (4HC), a vital precursor in chemical synthesis5. Numerous pharmacological effects are reported to be elicited by 4-hydroxycoumarin and its derivatives. The Cespedes research group conducted an initial in vitro screening, which indicated that coumarins exhibit antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative microorganisms. They further suggested that the hydroxyl group in 4-HC lowers lipid solubility, indicating that structural modifications to improve solubility may strengthen its antibacterial potential6. This resulted in the extensive synthesis of 4HC-based analogues worldwide in pursuit of a potent antibacterial agent. Lin et al. synthesised open ring amidoethylated 4HC derivatives by reacting 4HC anions with activated aziridines7. Multiple studies confirm that the structural modification of 4-hydroxycoumarin scaffold such as introducing aryl, heteroaryl, alkyl, or hydrazyl groups, or forming inclusion complexes can both increase aqueous solubility and strengthen antibacterial effects. For example, forming inclusion complexes with cyclodextrins increased the solubility of a 3-substituted 4-hydroxycoumarin derivative by over 60 times. This increase directly related to better antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria can be attributed to enhanced membrane permeability8.Similarly, Mannich base, azo, piperazine, and sulfonamide derivatives of 4-hydroxycoumarin have shown better antibacterial strength with lower minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains compared to the parent compound9–16. These studies demonstrate that substitution with suitable functional groups and molecular modification play a crucial role in enhancing the biological activity, thereby providing a strong foundation for the rational design of new coumarin-based derivatives with improved pharmacological properties. In this context, and to further explore the structure–activity relationship, we have synthesised two novel 4HC derivatives: 3,3′-(pyren‑1‑ylmethylene) bis(4‑hydroxy‑2H‑chromen‑2‑one) (PBHC) and (E)-3,3′-(3-(4‑methoxyphenyl) prop‑2‑ene‑1,1‑diyl) bis(4‑hydroxy‑2H‑chromen‑2‑one) (MPBHC) via aldol condensation17. PBHC includes a polycyclic aromatic pyrene part, while MPBHC has a methoxy-substituted cinnamaldehyde fragment. These changes were systematically intended to address the modest bioactivity of 4HC by providing prolonged aromatic conjugation (in PBHC) and altered electronic/steric environments (in MPBHC), thereby increasing molecule polarity, binding interactions, and biological activity.The antibacterial activity of PBHC and MPBHC was tested against both gram-positive and gram-negative species. The focus was on Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), which is a major cause of hospital-acquired infections. Since biofilm formation plays a major role in bacterial resistance and survival, MPBHC was also evaluated for its biofilm inhibitory activity. In parallel, the cytotoxic potential of both derivatives was assessed against MCF-7 (human breast adenocarcinoma) and HepG2 (human hepatocellular carcinoma) cell lines. Cytotoxicity assays on the HEK-293 (human embryonic kidney) cell line were conducted to assess the relative safety of the compounds and their potential therapeutic relevance.

In order to support the experimental observations, computational calculations were carried out. The structural parameters and molecular reactivity of the compounds were analyzed using density functional theory (DFT) at the B3LYP/6-311 + G(d, p) level18. In order to investigate the UV–visible absorption spectra, time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations were performed19–24. These studies have been widely employed in the literature to accurately predict electronic transitions, absorption maxima, and excited-state properties of organic molecules. Molecular docking studies were conducted in order to identify potential binding modes and affinities. Toxicity studies and in silico pharmacokinetic evaluations were also carried out using SwissADME and ProTox-III to assess drug-likeness, absorption, distribution, metabolism, and safety characteristics by correlating structural change with physicochemical properties, antibacterial and anticancer activities, and pharmacokinetic profiles, it offers deeper insights into the structure–activity relationships of 4HC-based derivatives.

Results and discussion

Synthesis

The synthesis of two novel bis(4-hydroxycoumarin) derivatives, PBHC (3,3’-(pyren-1-ylmethylene) bis(4-hydroxy-2H-chromen-2-one) and MPBHC ((E)-3,3’-(3-(4-methoxyphenyl) prop-2-ene-1,1-diyl) bis (4-hydroxy-2H chromen-2-one)) were accomplished by refluxing two equivalents of 4-hydroxycoumarin with one equivalent of an aromatic aldehyde in ethanol. The condensation of 4-hydroxycoumarin (1) with pyrene-1-carboxaldehyde (2a) gave dimeric coumarin derivative PBHC (3a) bearing an aryl substituent in the central methylene linker (Scheme 1), while the reaction of 4-hydroxycoumarin (1) with trans-4-methoxycinnamaldehyde (2b) gives MPBHC (3b) (Scheme 2).The chemical identities and structures of PBHC and MPBHC were confirmed by FT-IR, ¹H, ¹³C NMR spectroscopy and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (supplementary data).

The FT-IR spectrum of PBHC showed a strong carbonyl (C = O) band at 1659 cm⁻¹, an aromatic C–H stretch at 3041 cm⁻¹, a broad hydroxyl stretching band at 3449 cm⁻¹, and C = C aromatic stretching at 1607 cm⁻¹. The phenolic -OH protons are responsible for the two distinctive singlets at δ 11.41 and 11.24 ppm in the ¹H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl₃) spectrum. Pyrene protons are represented by multiplets in the aromatic region between δ 8.17 and 7.87 ppm (10 H), whereas other aromatic coumarin protons were found between δ 7.61 and 7.23 ppm. The central methine proton is found as a singlet at δ 1.57 ppm. The ¹³C NMR spectrum revealed a methine signal at δ 35.5 ppm along with resonances for carbonyl and aromatic carbons across δ 169.1 to 105.7 ppm. In agreement with the calculated mass for C₃₅H₂₀O₆Na⁺ (calc. 559.117), HRMS (ESI-MS) showed a molecular ion peak at m/z 559.117 ([M + Na] ⁺). The observed FT-IR and NMR spectral features of PBHC are in good agreement with previously reported bis(4-hydroxycoumarin) derivatives17. For instance, the carbonyl (C = O) stretching frequency at 1659 cm⁻¹ closely matches reported values (1658 cm⁻¹), while the hydroxyl proton signals at δ 11.41 and 11.24 ppm are consistent with literature data. The methine carbon signal at δ 35.5 ppm also correlates well with reported values (36 ppm), and the aromatic proton resonances fall within the expected range. These consistencies strongly support the successful synthesis and structural assignment of PBHC. The FT-IR spectrum for MPBHC showed strong O-H and aromatic C-H stretches at 3451 cm⁻¹ and 3084 cm⁻¹, respectively. It also displayed alkene C = C stretching at 1641 cm⁻¹ and C = O stretching at 1720 cm⁻¹. C–O–C and C–O vibrations are represented by additional signals at 1195 and 1116 cm⁻¹. The ¹H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl₃) spectrum revealed a singlet at δ 8.45 ppm (2 H), several doublets and multiplets in the δ 7.91–6.86 ppm range that correspond to the aromatic protons, and aliphatic signals between δ 5.63 and 2.54 ppm that correspond to the OCH₃ group and the methine and methylene protons of the cinnamonyl bridge. The ¹³C NMR spectrum revealed aromatic/aromatic ether carbons from δ 153 to 101.4 ppm, carbonyl signals between δ 161.9 and 158.6 ppm, and methoxy and aliphatic signals at δ 73.9, 55.3, 34.9, and 34.1 ppm. HRMS (ESI-MS) analysis revealed a [M + H] ⁺ peak at m/z 469.13, which matches with the theoretical mass of C₂₈H₂₀O₇⁺ (calc. 468.46). The observed spectral data for MPBHC are in good agreement with previously reported coumarin-based derivatives17, particularly those containing methoxy-substituted aromatic systems. For instance, the broad O–H stretching band observed at 3451 cm⁻¹ is comparable with reported values (3480 cm⁻¹), while the carbonyl stretching frequencies are consistent with literature ranges. The methoxy group signals observed in both ¹H and ¹³C NMR spectra also align well with reported values. These consistencies strongly support the successful synthesis and structural assignment of MPBHC.

Thermal stability

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) evaluates the thermal stability and degradation profile of compounds. This involved monitoring weight loss at different temperatures, which offered insights into their thermal stability (Fig. 1a,b) which is crucial for evaluating their applicability in pharmaceutical and biological fields25,26. Both PBHC and MPBHC exhibit a stable thermal profile. An initial weight loss of about 38% is found in the case of PBHC between 190 and 360 °C which is attributed to the breakdown of the pyrene moiety. The second notable weight reduction of 30% from 360 to 780 °C is due to the loss of one 4-hydroxycoumarin unit. A total weight loss of 69.54% was noted, hence verifying the multistep heat degradation of PBHC.In the case of MPBHC, a weight reduction of 70.92% occurs between 170 and 440 °C due to the thermal breakdown of the dicoumarol component in the molecule. Furthermore, the loss of 4-methoxystyrene, which constitutes 9.12%, transpires between 440 and 780 °C. The TGA study of MPBHC indicates a cumulative weight reduction of 80.04%. The TGA-DTG profiles of both PBHC and MPBHC is shown in Supplementary information (Figs. S1, S2). These higher thermal stabilities of PBHC and MPBHC indicates their potential for biological and pharmaceutical applications and their desired drug-like quality.

Optical properties

The UV-Vis absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy techniques were used to thoroughly assess the optical characteristics of PBHC and MPBHC in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The important photophysical properties, including Stokes shift, emission characteristics, molar extinction coefficients, and absorption maxima, were determined. Time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations were performed to provide theoretical support for the experimental observations.PBHC exhibited a structured absorption spectrum with three prominent bands: λmax = 280 nm (ε = 40492.9 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹) which can be attributed to π→π transitions localized within the coumarin chromophore, λmax = 335 nm (ε = 48446.9 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹.) assigned to n→π* transitions involving the hydroxyl oxygen lone pair and a higher band λmax = 352 nm (ε = 50905.4 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹.) corresponds to intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) transitions facilitated by the extended conjugation between the coumarin core and the pyrene moiety. PBHC exhibits a well-resolved emission maximum at 390 nm (λexc = 300 nm). The Stokes shifts observed suggests pronounced excited state relaxation and solvent reorganization effects. In contrast, MPBHC displayed a significant absorption maximum at λmax = 303 nm (ε = 40558 L·M⁻¹·cm⁻¹), arising from π→π* transitions over the conjugated cinnamaldehyde–coumarin framework, with additional ICT contributions from the electron-donating methoxy substituent. MPBHC shows a well-defined emission spectrum with an emission maximum at 398 nm (λ exc 250 nm). This corresponds to a stokes shift of Δλ = 95 nm, indicative of a significant relaxation and ICT process in the excited state. The TD-DFT calculations of both PBHC and MPBHC are consistent with the experimental results (Supplementary information Figs. S3, S4). Structural properties: computational evaluation

The density functional theory (DFT) is used to determine the structural and chemical properties of PBHC and MPBHC. The optimized molecular structures of both PBHC and MPBHC show a non-planar structure. (Fig. 3). MPBHC exhibits significant polarity with a high dipole moment (µ = 7.77 Debye) whereas PBHC shows a moderate dipole moment (µ) = 3.810 Debye. The phenomenon of polarity is essential for predicting the bioactivity of the substances against the microorganisms. The dipole moment vectors of the PBHC and MPBHC (Supplementary information Figs. S5, S6) show the spatial distribution of charges, which influences the bioactivity of the compounds by promoting the membrane permeability, favourable pharmacokinetics and reduced off-target effect. This impacts the antibacterial efficiency of substances that penetrate the lipophilic membrane of microorganisms. Bond lengths and angles of PBHC and MPBHC fall within standard ranges for conjugated aromatic systems, indicating structural stability in conjugated regions. The Frontier Molecular Orbitals (FMO) of PBHC and MPBHC were thoroughly investigated using density functional theory (DFT) (Fig. 4). The HOMO is referred to as an electron donor whereas the LUMO, is composed of electron acceptance areas. The ionisation potential is directly associated with HOMO energy, whereas electron affinity is directly associated with LUMO energy. The HOMO - LUMO energy gap, a crucial stability component for the molecule, while evaluating the chemical reactivity, ionization potential, electron affinity, and intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) capability of the molecule. HOMO and LUMO energies were calculated for PBHC as − 5.353 eV and − 1.842 eV, respectively, with an energy gap (ΔE) of 3.510 eV, indicating the moderate chemical reactivity coupled with thermodynamic stability. The energy of the HOMO and LUMO in the case of MBHC is − 5.487 eV and − 1.839 eV respectively. A slightly wider energy gap of 3.648 eV, suggests a better structural stability. The HOMO in PBHC is located on the pyrene fragment, whereas in MPBHC it is concentrated on the methoxy-cinnamaldehyde linker. The 4-hydroxy-2 H-chromen-2-one units, accommodates the LUMO. A greater π-conjugation and enhanced potential for intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) is expected in MPBHC as the LUMO spreads across both coumarin units. This intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) facilitates non-covalent interactions, a key determinant of antibacterial and anticancer efficacy. The Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MEP) offers a visual representation to comprehend the relationships among electron density, dipole moment, electronegativity, and reactive areas inside a molecule, hence identifying the sites of reactivity29–31. The calculated MEP of the both PBHC and MPBHC is depicted in Fig. 5. The produced MEP maps, based on DFT-optimised geometry, graphically illustrate the distribution of electrostatic potential via a colour gradient ranging from red (electron-rich) to blue (electron-deficient). The red hue denotes electrophilic sites (negative potential), whereas the blue colour signifies nucleophilic sites (positive potential)32. Green signifies a region having a neutral electrostatic potential. In Both PBHC and MPBHC the blue hue was linked to the hydroxyl groups, whereas the red hue was ascribed to the electronegative oxygen atom. Consequently, sections exhibiting red due to elevated electron density are very prone to electrophilic assault, whereas areas appearing blue and green signify electron deficit and are sensitive to nucleophilic attack. The elcetrostactic potential of PBHC and MPBHC in the gas phase ranges form − 7.0 e− 2 to 7.0 e− 2 a.u, and − 7.50 e− 2 to 7.5 e− 2 a.u respectively. This indicates a moderate yet biologically significant polarisation within these molecules. Natural Population Analysis (NPA) was performed to evaluate the electron distribution of PBHC and MPBHC for orbital interactions and charge delocalization33. The result indicate that hydrogen atoms uniformly possess positive charges, whereas oxygen atoms are negatively charged reflecting their potential roles as hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors. A complete set of Mulliken and NPA charge values is provided in the Supplementary Information (Tables T5–T8, Fig. S7) for detailed reference.

Nonlinear optical properties

Non-Linear Optical (NLO) components are used in signal processing, laser technology, optical communication, and data storage. The potential for the polarisation of p orbitals and charge transfer between donor and acceptor levels provides the foundation for the nonlinearity of a molecule34. The conventional method for determining whether a substance is NLO active is to evaluate its nonlinear properties and compare them to those of the prototype substance, urea35. There is a significant amount of NLO activity in organic materials, and they are difficult to degrade in optical applications36. These calculations are sufficiently similar to experimental results to be beneficial37. DFT investigations are employed to quantify the NLO response of PBHC and MPBHC in the gas phase. The dipole moment (µ), first-order hyperpolarizability (β) and second-order hyperpolarizability (γ) were obtained from the optimized geometries. The dipole moment (µ) values were found to be 3.810 Debye for PBHC and 7.770 Debye for MPBHC, both substantially higher than that of urea (µ = 1.3732 Debye) indicating greater molecular polarity. The calculated hyperpolarizability values obtained in atomic units (a.u.) were converted into electrostatic units (esu). The first-order hyperpolarizability (β) values derived from the tensor components reveal a significant nonlinear optical response for PBHC and MPBHC. The β value of PBHC was calculated to be 1.49 × 10⁻³⁰ esu, while MPBHC exhibited a much larger value of 2.21 × 10⁻²⁹ esu. These values are higher than that of urea (β = 0.372 × 10⁻³⁰ esu), a widely used reference NLO material. The second-order hyperpolarizability (γ) values were obtained from the static Gamma (0;0,0,0) calculations. The γ value of MPBHC (1.31 × 10⁻³⁴ esu) was found to be significantly higher than that of PBHC (0.742 × 10⁻³⁴ esu), this shows the strong third-order NLO behaviour of MPBHC. These results indicate that both PBHC and MPBHC exhibit higher β and γ values compared to urea, demonstrate their enhanced NLO activity and hold potential as efficient organic NLO materials.

Topological analysis

The Electron Localisation Function (ELF) and localised Orbital Locator (LOL) are the topological studies derived from molecular wavefunction analysis and are crucial for the investigation of the behaviour and stability of molecules. These provides insights into the chemical bonds in atomic and molecular systems and identify areas where electrons are localised38. The ELF and LOL map surfaces were calculated using the Multiwfn application39. ELF elucidates electron pair density, while LOL defines the maximum localised orbitals that intersect as a result of the orbital gradient. The maximum number of electron pairs in the intermolecular region, resulting from covalent bonding, establishes the foundation of the baseline of the ELF and LOL maps40. Figure 6a,b illustrates the ELF and LOL colour maps for PBHC and MPBHC. The colour indications span from blue to red, with a range of 0 to 1.0 Bohr for ELF values and 0 to 0.8 Bohr for LOL figures. ELF and LOL both possess an interval scale, where 0.5 to 1.0 indicates bound and non-bound local electronic regions. A delocalised electron area is indicated by a value less than 0.5. The regions red and orange shows high electron localisation. The blue region represents the weak n-delocalised orbital, while the red colour represents the strong n-delocalised orbital41. The hydrogen atoms are red, while the carbon atoms are surrounded by a blue colour. In PBHC, high electron localization is observed around oxygen atoms of the hydroxyl and carbonyl groups, depicted in red and orange, suggesting strong electron density and potential as hydrogen bond acceptors. The blue regions, particularly surrounding the aromatic carbon atoms, indicate areas of π-electron delocalisation, thereby enhancing the molecule’s propensity for π–π stacking interactions with biological macromolecules. The multifunctional binding properties of PBHC is validated by ELF map. The ELF map for MPBHC indicates a dual electronic nature suggesting a balanced reactivity profile appropriate for π- π and electrostatic interactions, pertinent to antibacterial and anticancer mechanisms. The LOL analysis of both PBHC and MPBHC reveals that red regions indicate strongly localised lone pairs and bonding electrons, particularly around hydrogen and electronegative oxygen atoms, while blue regions suggest delocalised orbitals primarily associated with aromatic carbons. The LOL map indicates that the carbon atoms within the π-system are predominantly encircled by delocalised electronic environments, while the hydroxyl hydrogen atoms demonstrate significant localisation, which is favourable for hydrogen bond donation. The LOL map of MPBHC shows a similar pattern. Localization is mainly around the hydrogen atoms (red). The aromatic core has blue regions, which relate to π-electron delocalization. This electronic distribution highlights the possibility of both PBHC and MPBHC for non-covalent interactions. These include hydrogen bonding, dipole-dipole forces, and stacking interactions. They play a crucial role in molecular recognition and biological activity.

(a) Electron localisation function (ELF) map of PBHC and MPBHC, (b) Localised Orbital Locator (LOL) map of PBHC and MPBHC, (c) ALIE surface map of PBHC and MPBHC.

The Average Localised Ionisation Energy (ALIE) serves as a rigorous tool for measuring the local reactivity of molecules by outlining the ionisation potential at every spatial coordinate. ALIE values aids to understand the molecular stability and by demonstrating the unique portions of a molecule towards the electrophilic attack42. The ALIE surface analysis (Fig. 6c) indicates unique reactivity patterns for both PBHC and MPBHC43. The ALIE values for PBHC range from − 14.6 to 14.6 Bohr, while the mapped energies stretch from 0.00 to 2.00 a.u., with a colour gradient from blue (low ionisation potential) to red (high ionisation potential). In MPBHC, the ALIE values cover a greater range, from − 10.3 to 20.6 Bohr, but the energy scale keeps stable at 0.00 to 2.00 a.u. The greenish-blue regions identified on the ALIE maps represent areas of electron delocalisation, generally centred on conjugated carbon frameworks, indicating regions of moderate reactivity. These regions correspond to the chemically relevant sites, such as π-systems and lone pairs, which may influence molecular recognition, charge transport, or bioactivity. This ALIE study provides a clear understanding of the localised electronic environment and shows how both localised and delocalised electrons contribute the chemical reactivity and stability of PBHC and MPBHC.

The noncovalent interactions (NCI) and reduced density gradient (RDG) analysis are sophisticated approaches for characterising weak intermolecular interactions44–47. Understanding of these weak intermolecular pressures is critical for unravelling the molecular mechanics of drug–target interactions, membrane penetration, and receptor affinity. The reduced density gradient (RDG) is a basic dimensionless quantity. The NCI index characterises intermolecular interactions and examines the features of weak interactions. The NCI index is based on the reduced density gradient (RDG) and gives further evidence for non-covalent interactions. The quantification of the electron density of the sign (λ2) ρ peaks in relation to RDG provides insights into the type and intensity of molecular interactions. In the molecular system, blue signifies an attractive interaction, while red signifies a repulsive interaction. The sign (λ2) ρ value is essential for predicting the nature of the interaction. For instance, a sign(λ2) ρ value of less than zero indicates an attractive interaction (bonded), whereas a sign(λ2) ρ value of more than zero indicates a repulsive interaction (non-bonded). A colour-filled RDG map with chromatic bars was generated using Multiwfn 3.8 software to illustrate limited interaction in the real space48. The RDG surface maps of PBHC and MPBHC are illustrated in Fig. 7a,b. The steric effect is the dominant effect in the red areas, while van der Waals interactions are feeble in the green areas. In the RDG surface map PBHC, the green coloured isosurfaces corresponding to van der Waals interactions, particularly around the aromatic core and between the hydroxyl and carbonyl groups. These weak but spatially extended interactions suggest that PBHC can effectively interact with hydrophobic environments, such as the lipid bilayers of bacterial membranes or hydrophobic pockets of enzymes. The red portions signify steric repulsions, near highly substituted sites, whereas the blue areas, albeit less evident, show possible hydrogen bonding locations, hence enhancing the interaction potential of PBHC with polar biomolecular targets. A similar RDG/NCI profile is reported in MPBHC. The green isosurfaces prominently occupy the interaction space, signifying the presence of van der Waals forces, which are essential for binding to nonpolar receptor domains or lipophilic bacterial membranes. The red zones indicate areas of steric congestion, mainly situated around fused ring systems or large substituents. These regions may affect the bioavailability or receptor compatibility of MPBHC. The blue regions indicate localised attractive interactions, due to the formation of hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl and carbonyl groups and polar residues in proteins.

The topological features of MPBHC and PBHC were explored using Bader’s Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM)49, employing the wavefunction files generated from DFT calculations (B3LYP/6-311 + G(d, p)). In QTAIM analysis, hydrogen bond interactions can be effectively characterised and quantified using critical points (CPs) and various topological parameters: electron density ρ(r), its Laplacian ∇²ρ(r), kinetic energy G(r), potential energy V(r), kinetic energy of the Hamiltonian H(r), and binding energy50. The most important factors are the electron density ρ (r) and its Laplacian ∇2 ρ (r). These are used to determine the type of bond or interaction. A high ρ (r) (> 0.20 a.u) and negative ∇2 ρ(r) values mean that there is a covalent bond. On the other hand, a small ρ (r) (< 0.10 a.u) with negative ∇2 ρ(r) values indicates the presence of ionic or hydrogen bonding51,52. The Multiwfn 3.8 software was utilised for the analysis of QTAIM. Figure 7c illustrates the QTAIM graphs of PBHC and MPBHC. The QTAIM graph shows three types of critical points: the nuclear critical point (NCP) is shown by a large red spot (+ 3, − 3), the bond critical point (BCP) is shown by a small orange spot (+ 3, − 1), and the ring critical point (RCP) is shown by a yellow spot (+ 3, + 1). The BCPs are a reliable way to measure how far apart atoms are in a molecular system.

The analysis for MPBHC identified 25 (+ 3, − 3) critical points corresponding to nuclear attractors, 20 (+ 3, − 1) critical points related to bond paths, and 5 additional critical points indicatives of ring or cage structures. The central nuclear critical point exhibited a maximum electron density (ρ) of 1195.93 a.u. Correlation of SAR (structure activity relationship), DFT and biological activity

The present study demonstrates the link between the structural features of PBHC and MPBHC, their calculated electronic properties, and their observed antibacterial and anticancer activities. Both molecules have the 4-hydroxy-2H-chromen-2-one core, which includes hydroxyl and carbonyl groups that act as hydrogen bond donors and acceptors. These functional groups, along with the extended aromatic systems, create sites for π-π stacking and electrostatic interactions, which are crucial for molecular recognition in biological targets. The key structural difference between PBHC and MPBHC is in their substituents. PBHC has a pyrene moiety, while MPBHC includes a para-methoxy cinnamaldehyde linker. The methoxy (-OCH₃) group, being an electron-donating substituent, increases electron density in the aromatic ring and strengthens non-covalent interactions with proteins66,67. This change in MPBHC leads to greater conjugation and planarity. It improves intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) and strengthens non-covalent interactions with proteins. Time-dependent DFT calculations show that, in PBHC, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) is mainly found on the pyrene part, while the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) is found on one coumarin unit. On the other hand, in the case of MPBHC, HOMO spreads over methoxy-cinnamaldehyde and LUMO extends over both coumarin units. The calculated HOMO-LUMO energy gaps (ΔE) for PBHC and MPBHC (3.510 eV and 3.648 eV) further supports these observations. This in turn suggests moderate chemical reactivity. The electronegativity values (χ) are 3.598 eV for PBHC and 3.663 eV for MPBHC. The chemical potentials (µ) are − 3.597 eV and − 3.663 eV, respectively, showing their ability to attract electrons. Global hardness (η) and softness (S) values indicate that both compounds remain stable enough even while allowing effective interactions with biological targets. The electrophilicity index (ω) values are 3.69 eV for PBHC and 3.678 eV for MPBHC, indicating a slightly better activity for MPBHC. The charge distribution around the carbonyl and hydroxyl oxygens and positive potential on the hydroxyl hydrogens supports hydrogen bonding interactions. Topological analyses, including ELF, LOL, NCI, RDG, and QTAIM, show that MPBHC has a more favourable distribution of delocalized π-systems and hydrogen-bonding sites compared to PBHC, which further substantiates its improved biological activity. In experiments, MPBHC shows higher antibacterial effectiveness against Staphylococcus aureus and stronger anti-biofilm activity (MBIC = 0.1 µg/mL) than PBHC. Cytotoxicity study shows that MPBHC selectively targets HepG2 cells, having lower IC₅₀ values than PBHC. Both compounds have moderate activity against MCF-7 cells. Molecular docking studies back up these results, with MPBHC displaying stronger binding to the Gram-positive protein 1n67. This reflects its electronic and structural properties. The combination of SAR analysis, DFTderived descriptors, topological studies, and experimental results indicates that adding the methoxycinnamaldehyde moiety to MPBHC improves electron transfer, ICT, and pharmacophoric features. These enhancements lead to better antibacterial and anticancer activities, making MPBHC a promising candidate for further therapeutic development.

In silico pharmacokinetic, drug-likeness and ADME prediction

The pharmacokinetic properties of individual components affect the overall pharmacological profile of a drug. Comprehending pharmacokinetic and ADME profiles enhances the prediction of bioavailability, permeability, and absorption features of prospective drug candidates, thus helping in the selection of molecules with better absorption characteristics. Properties such as Blood-brain barrier permeability and gastric absorption extensively influence medication distribution inside the body. The cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme superfamily is essential for drug clearance via metabolic biotransformation. Consequently, the inhibition of CYP enzymes may affect medication metabolism and the likelihood of drug-drug interactions. According to Lipinski’s rule69, potential molecules should possess the following physicochemical properties: (i) fewer than 5 hydrogen bond donors (2) (HBDs), (ii) fewer than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors (7) (HBAs), (iii) a molecular mass of less than 500 g/mol, (iv) a logP (lipophilicity range) not exceeding 5, and (v) a total polar surface area (TPSA) not greater than 140 Å². In-silico toxicity study

In-silico toxicity analysis was achieved by the ProTox-3.0 web tool (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/)73. According to the results, MPBHC is nontoxic, as its LD50 (Lethal dose) values come under the toxicity class V (LD50: 3000 mg/kg) (Supplementary information Fig. S11). On the other hand, PBHC possesses class 3 toxicity with LD50 of 233 mg/kg (Supplementary information Fig. S12). Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) can manifest from asymptomatic elevations in liver enzymes to acute liver failure74,75. In silico toxicity predictions indicate that MPBHC is hepatotoxicity-inactive. Hepatotoxicity refers to liver damage or dysfunction caused by xenobiotics, including drugs. These findings suggest that MPBHC may serve as a safer antibacterial candidate with potential benefits for both patients and healthcare systems.

Conclusion

This works presents a detailed investigation of two novel 4‑hydroxycoumarin derivatives, PBHC and MPBHC. Their molecular structures were confirmed by spectroscopic techniques. The DFT studies of PBHC and MPBHC support their observed antibacterial and anticancer activities, suggesting favourable pharmacophoric features such as optimal polarity, functional group orientation, and hydrogen‑bonding potential. Biological evaluations demonstrated that both PBHC and MPBHC exhibit significant antibacterial and anticancer properties. Antibacterial tests showed clear selectivity toward Staphylococcus aureus over Escherichia coli. MPBHC exhibited strong anti-biofilm activity with a low MBIC of 0.1 µg/mL. Cytotoxicity tests demonstrated that MPBHC had strong and selective activity against HepG2 liver cancer cells (IC₅₀ = 9.407 ± 0.300 µM) compared to PBHC (IC₅₀ = 46.69 ± 0.021 µM). Both compounds showed moderate toxicity toward MCF‑7 cells. To evaluate the toxicity toward normal cells, the cytotoxicity assays were performed on HEK-293 human embryonic kidney cells. The IC₅₀ values for PBHC and MPBHC on HEK-293 cells were 10.24 ± 1.2 µM and 47.59 ± 2.5 µM, respectively. These results show that MPBHC has lower cytotoxicity towards normal cells, suggesting a safer profile, while PBHC has less favourable selectivity. Molecular docking studies against bacterial targets revealed greater binding affinity for the Gram‑positive protein 1n67, with PBHC and MPBHC recording values of − 10.9 kcal/mol and − 11.9 kcal/mol, respectively, corroborating the experimental antibacterial findings. Pharmacokinetic and ADME analysis showed that only MPBHC followed Lipinski’s rule of five without any violations. This confirms its favourable drug-like profile. Toxicity predictions rated MPBHC as non-toxic (LD₅₀ = 3000 mg/kg, Class V). In contrast, PBHC was classified in Class III toxicity (LD₅₀ = 233 mg/kg), indicating moderate toxicity. From the perspective of drug development, MPBHC seems to be a better option for future progress, particularly for targeting liver cancer and Gram-positive bacterial biofilms and relatively low toxicity towards normal cells. Future studies should examine the toxicity, metabolic stability, and effectiveness of both derivatives in living organisms, and also explore further modifications to improve the drug’s absorption, strength, and selectivity.

Future perspective

Future research is highly recommended to broaden the scope of cytotoxicity assays to include more cancer cell lines and appropriate healthy cell lines, such as MCF10A and HUVEC to better assess selectivity and safety. Antibacterial research should cover a vast range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, as well as clinically relevant fungi. It is proposed to perform mechanistic investigations, spanning apoptosis and other experiments, to understand the fundamental mode of action. Finally, in vivo studies focusing on pharmacokinetics, metabolic stability, and toxicity are essential to validate the therapeutic potential and preclinical development of PBHC and MPBHC.

Computational methodology

The GAUSSIAN 16 software suite77 was employed to optimise the molecular geometry and electronic structures of PBHC and MPBHC using density functional theory (DFT). The calculations were performed using B3LYP (Becke, three-parameter Lee–Yang–Parr) exchange-correlation functional78,79 in conjunction with the 6-311 + G (d, p) basis set80. Analyses included frontier molecular orbital (FMO) mapping, molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) surfaces, Mulliken charge distribution, and electron localization functions (ELF/LOL). These derivatives were additionally evaluated for topological parameters (ELF, LOL, RDG), non‑covalent interaction (NCI), average localized ionization energy (ALIE) and nonlinear optical (NLO) property calculations. The electronic UV-visible absorption was investigated using time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculation81,82. The Multiwfn program83 was used to conduct the topological analysis on PBHC and MPBHC and identify the binding areas and reactive sites.

Biological evaluation

Cell cultures

The National Centre for Cell Sciences (NCCS) in Pune, India, provided the human breast adenocarcinoma cell line MCF-7 (metastatic), the HepG2 cell line (hepatocellular carcinoma) and HEK-293 (human embryonic kidney cells) cell line. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)84 supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic solution was used to cultivate the cells (10,000 cells/well) in 96-well plates for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Cell viability assay (MTT Assay)

The cytotoxic activity was assessed by MTT assay, i.e., the reduction of (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) (MTT) by mitochondrial dehydrogenase in viable cells, yielding a blue formazan product quantifiable using UV–Vis spectrophotometry. MTT colourimetric experiments were conducted using cells (10,000 cells/well) grown in 96-well plates for 24 hours85,86. Following a 24-hour period, MCF-7 (metastatic), the HepG2 cells were subjected to treatment with varying doses of the test substance (0, 1, 10, 50, 100, 250, 500, and 1000 µM). After a 24-hour incubation, MTT solution was added to the cell culture at a final concentration of 250 µg/ml and incubated for an additional 2 h. Following the experiment, the culture supernatant was discarded, and the cell layer matrix was solubilised in 100 µl of Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO) and then examined with an Elisa plate reader (iMark, Biorad, USA) at wavelengths of 540 nm and 660 nm. Data were obtained from three duplicate wells. The cytotoxic effects were evaluated against the negative control (vehicle-treated cells). Cytotoxicity was measured as the mean percentage increase relative to the unexposed control (mean ± standard deviation). Control values were set at 0% cytotoxicity. The IC50 was ascertained with the program GraphPad Prism 6. Images were captured under an inverted microscope (Olympus ek2) using a camera (AmScope digital camera 10 MP Aptima CMOS). HEK-293 (human embryonic kidney cells) Cells were then subject to treatment in triplicate with each compound (0–400 µM). Following a 24-hour incubation, the MTT assay was performed, where the cells were incubated (37 °C, 4 h) with MTT substrate (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) (0.5 mg/ml). After that, supernatants were aspirated, and formazan crystals were solubilized in dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO; 100 µl/well) for 10–15 min. Optical density (OD) was then measured at 570 nm with a plate reader (BioTek Epoch-2). Percentage cell viability was calculated relative to the untreated control. Data were expressed as mean ± SEM from three independent biological trials. Log-transformed concentration-response curves were generated in Python using a non-linear regression model (4-parameter logistic fit) to extrapolate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) and the coefficient of determination (R2).

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial efficacy of PBHC and MPBHC were assessed using the agar well diffusion technique against the gram-positive strain Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and the gram-negative strain Escherichia coli (E. coli)87. A newly prepared bacterial culture with a concentration of 1 × 108 colony-forming units per millilitre (CFU/ml) was aseptically injected into Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) plates, ensuring even distribution. The medium was produced by dissolving 33.8 g of commercially available Muller Hinton Agar Medium (MHI Agar Media) in 1000 cc of distilled water. The dissolved media was subjected to autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min under 15 lbs of pressure. In molten state the autoclaved medium was well mixed and distributed onto 100 mm petri plates (25–30 ml/plate). Petri plates containing 20 ml of Muller Hinton Agar Medium were inoculated with bacterial cultures of Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, calibrated to a McFarland Standard of 0.5%. The well cutter was used to drill wells with an estimated diameter of 10 mm. Streptomycin (15 µg) served as the positive control. Experiments were performed using 20 µg of PBHC and MPBHC, with DMSO serving as the negative control. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for a duration of 24 h. The width of the inhibition zone around the well was used to assess antibacterial activity (NCCLS, 1993).

Determination of minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The MIC was determined as the lowest concentration at which the observable bacterial growth was inhibited. 0.5 McFarland Standard dilution of microbes is used for the study. 100 µl diluted log cultures of bacteria (S. aureus – MTCC 96) were added to the microcentrifuge tube and added with 5 µl of prepared treatment dilutions of different concentrations (0–1000 µg/ml) to the defined tubes and incubated for 24 h. After incubation all content was transferred to the 96-well plate, and turbidity reading was taken by the Elisa Plate Reader at 630 nm. Streptomycin (10 µg) was used as a positive control.

Biofilm formation assay

Biofilm formation was assessed as per the reported procedure88. The inhibitory activity of the MPBHC on the biofilm formation was evaluated by a 0.5 McFarland standard log phase bacterial culture (S. aureus - MTCC96). Log phase culture of the microbe was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard, and 1 ml of 10 times diluted 0.5 McFarland standard culture was placed on a coated coverslip in a 50 mm Petri plate (separate plates for control and treated groups were prepared). 10 µl of this culture was inoculated in 100 µl of the medium in a coated glass slide and incubated at 35 °C for 24 to 48 h. Only 200 µl of bacterial suspension was added to the well for control. After incubation, the slide was swirled gently, and the medium was pipetted out, leaving behind the biofilm. Coverslips were air-dried and washed with water and again air-dried for 15 min. This coverslip with biofilm was mounted on a slide using the antifade mountant DAPI. Photos were captured using a confocal microscope (Inverted Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (ILSCM (ZEISS, LSM 900)) at 63X and analysed with the help of ImageJ Version 1.54 software.

Molecular docking protocol

The molecular docking study can be used to accurately predict the protein-ligand interactions. For this study we specifically chose the most relevant proteins 1e8c (MurE ligase) from E. coli and 1n67 (Clumping Factor A) from S. aureus for this study. The RCSB online data repository was employed to obtain the desirable structure in PDB format. The protein was prepared for docking using the AutoDock 4 software. The in-silico synthesis of ligands PBHC and MPBHC was conducted using ChemDraw software. By anchoring the ligands to the protein’s appropriate functional sites, the docking energy was decreased. The Discovery Studio software was employed to verify the binding interactions. The interaction energy is calculated using the dock score, a mathematical method that is both rapid and approximate. This method is employed to predict the intensity of the non-covalent contact between two molecules following the docking procedure. Scoring functions are typically composed of molecular mechanics force fields that are based on physics and are employed to determine the energy of a system. A stable system is indicated by a low energy value (negative), which suggests a probable binding relationship89. The conformation with the lowest binding energy was chosen after a minimum of eight experiments conducted on the ligand PBHC and MPBHC. All docking computations and visualizations were carried out using AutoDock and Discovery Studio90,91.

In-silico ADME drug-likeness study

In drug discovery and candidate selection, the concept of drug‑like chemical entities is widely applied. Such entities are characterized by pharmacokinetic properties suitable for sustaining efficacy and stability throughout human clinical trials92. The SwissADME database was utilised to assess the physicochemical characteristics, pharmacokinetic profile, medicinal chemistry, drug similarity, water solubility, and lipophilicity of the compounds93,94. The 2D structures of the PBHC and MPBHC were transformed into a string-based search format such as SMILES, which made it possible to filter and examine possible medication possibilities95.

In-silico toxicity study

The in-silico toxicity of both the derivatives PBHC and MPBHC was assessed using the ProTox prediction tool https://tox.charite.de/protox3. The toxicity classification and LD50 values of the compounds were established96. Toxicity data must be correlated with in vitro safety assessments prior to testing in suitable laboratory animal models. Furthermore, we predict the computational toxicity issues associated with PBHC and MPBHC, such as hepatotoxicity, carcinotoxicity, and cytotoxicity.
