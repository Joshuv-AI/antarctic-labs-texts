Neuroimaging studies of resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging in eating disorders
Authors: Xiong Chen, Chunqi Ai, Zhongchun Liu, Gang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Eating disorders (EDs), including anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa (BN), binge-eating disorder (BED), and pica, are psychobehavioral conditions characterized by abnormal eating behaviors and an excessive preoccupation with weight and body shape. This review examines changes in brain regions and functional connectivity in ED patients over the past decade (2013–2023) using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). Key findings highlight alterations in brain networks such as the default mode network (DMN), central executive network (CEN), and emotion regulation network (ERN). In individuals with AN, there is reduced functional connectivity in areas associated with facial information processing and social cognition, alongside increased connectivity in regions linked to sensory stimulation, aesthetic judgment, and social anxiety. Conversely, BED patients show diminished connectivity in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex within the salience network and increased connectivity in the posterior cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal cortex within the DMN. These findings suggest that rs-fMRI could serve as a valuable biomarker for assessing brain function and predicting treatment outcomes in EDs, paving the way for personalized therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-024-01432-z.

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs) are complex mental health issues that have garnered widespread attention in modern society [1–3]. These disorders are typically associated with food intake, body image, and self-evaluation, concealing deep-seated psychological and emotional problems [4, 5]. The primary types of eating disorders include Anorexia Nervosa (AN), Bulimia Nervosa (BN), Binge Eating Disorder (BED), and Pica [6]. With societal evolution, especially in contemporary times, food is not merely for survival but is also intertwined with aesthetics, social interactions, and psychological factors. The media and social platforms frequently emphasize the “ideal” body shape and appearance, contributing to the prevalence of eating disorders, particularly among young women.

In recent years, there have been significant shifts in the pathophysiological and etiological frameworks of eating disorders, although their exact etiology remains elusive [7]. Biologically, EDs are believed to result from complex interactions between genetic predisposition and environmental factors. Research indicates a substantial genetic susceptibility in Anorexia Nervosa and Bulimia Nervosa. Family and twin studies suggest a genetic basis, with heritability estimates ranging from 50 to 80% 8. Specific genetic variants, such as those associated with serotonin (5-HT) and dopamine (DA) pathways, have been found to correlate with the pathophysiology of eating disorders [9].

From a psychological standpoint, individuals with EDs often experience heightened levels of anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem, which can exacerbate their symptoms. For example, anxiety may lead to excessive preoccupation with food and weight, while depression can contribute to episodes of binge eating [10]. EDs are closely intertwined with cognitive distortions, difficulties in emotional regulation, and maladaptive behavioral patterns. Those with AN frequently exhibit extreme perfectionism and a strong desire for weight control, traits closely associated with pathological concerns regarding body image and fear of weight gain [11]. Individuals with BN and BED commonly display impulsivity and emotional instability, which are linked to binge eating and purging behaviors. These psychological issues underscore the significant role of emotional dysregulation in the pathophysiology of eating disorders. Moreover, research indicates that psychological traits may also be correlated with abnormalities in brain function, particularly in regions involved in emotional regulation and cognitive control [12].

Social factors play a crucial role in the onset and maintenance of eating disorders. In contemporary society, the idealization of thinness and the pursuit of a perfect body image, influenced significantly by media and social platforms, greatly amplify individuals’ dissatisfaction with their body image and anxiety about weight. These societal pressures in cultural environments particularly impact adolescents and young adults, making them more susceptible to EDs. Sociocultural expectations regarding body image and the stigmatization of overweight individuals further exacerbate the risk of developing eating disorders. Research indicates that social comparison and peer pressure are significant risk factors, especially among females [13]. When faced with pressures regarding body image and appearance from peers and media, young women often resort to extreme dietary behaviors in pursuit of an ideal body image, which can lead to EDs [14].

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) is a non-invasive brain imaging technique that measures functional connectivity between brain regions during rest, revealing dynamic activity patterns of brain networks [15]. This technique has played a crucial role in eating disorders research, aiding in the identification of specific brain regions and networks implicated in these disorders, and understanding how neuronal circuits relate to behavior and pathophysiology. However, findings across multiple studies have been inconsistent [3–5, 16], and the specific limitations of neuroimaging studies are continually challenged as our understanding of the disorder evolves, particularly in studies involving multiple individuals [17]. For instance, studies have found increased functional connectivity within the default mode network (DMN) and decreased connectivity within the salience network (SN) in patients with AN [18]. These changes in functional connectivity may be associated with abnormal responses to food rewards and excessive preoccupation with body image in patients. In BN and BED, studies have identified abnormal functional connectivity patterns in reward circuits, including the striatum and orbitofrontal cortex, regions involved in food reward processing and impulsive behaviors. These findings suggest that dysfunction in reward networks may underlie compulsive thoughts about food and binge eating behaviors [19].

Neuroimaging techniques are not only pivotal in understanding the neurobiological mechanisms of EDs but also hold significant potential for clinical applications. By identifying abnormal brain functional patterns in patients with EDs, neuroimaging can provide a basis for personalized diagnosis and treatment. For example, using rs-fMRI to detect changes in functional connectivity in specific brain regions can help predict patient responses to treatment, thereby optimizing therapeutic strategies [20]. Additionally, neuroimaging techniques can serve as biomarkers to assess treatment efficacy and patient recovery progress in eating disorder patients. Studies indicate that monitoring changes in brain function before and after treatment can evaluate treatment effectiveness and patient recovery [21]. These findings suggest that neuroimaging has broad prospects in future research and treatment of EDs. Building on previous studies, this review summarizes research from 2013 to 2023 on the application of resting-state MRI in eating disorders, focusing on AN, BN and BED. The review encompasses studies on brain structure, function, connectivity, and metabolism, providing a macroscopic understanding of brain regions and potential functional connectivity mechanisms involved in EDs’ neuroimaging. It aims to serve as a valuable reference for future personalized research and treatment approaches.

Materials and methods

Search methodology

In this study, we retrieved neuroimaging literature on eating disorders published between 2013 and 2023 from databases such as Web of Science, PubMed and Google Scholar. The search terms were identified as: ‘resting state functional connectivity and eating disorders’, ‘resting state functional connectivity and anorexia nervosa’, ‘resting state functional connectivity and bulimia nervosa’, ‘resting state functional connectivity and binge-eating disorder’, ‘resting state functional connectivity and pica’; ‘rsfMRI and eating disorders’, ‘rsfMRI and anorexia nervosa’, ‘rsfMRI and bulimia nervosa’, ‘rsfMRI and binge-eating disorder’, ‘rsfMRI and pica’. The studies employed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) methodology to derive their outcomes [22], which investigated the impact of resting-state magnetic resonance imaging on functional and structural brain systems in conditions such as anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, and pica, or predicted the pathomechanisms of these diseases. The current clinical research, widely utilizing neuroimaging evidence, supports the pathophysiology of EDs, including dysregulation of multiple large-scale brain networks, alterations in brain functional structures, etc [23–26]. Furthermore, to ensure no relevant literature was omitted, we manually searched the references from existing review articles on related topics. Figure 1 shows the search process methodology.

Inclusion criteria

The original research articles included in this review were published in English between January 2013 and December 2023. These articles utilized resting-state magnetic resonance imaging methods to investigate changes in brain functional imaging related to eating disorders. We selectively employed the following imaging techniques: resting-state functional MRI (fMRI).

Exclusion criteria

Studies involving healthy participants without eating disorders were not included in the investigation. Additionally, research on eating disorders combined with organic diseases or studies employing brain imaging (< 24 h) were also excluded. Results that did not report statistically significant trends (p-value > 0.05) or results not adjusted for multiple comparisons by the original authors were excluded. Studies involving magnetoencephalography and electroencephalography were not considered.

Results

Over the past decade, researchers have primarily focused on the neuro-psychological characteristics of individuals with EDs. Consistent findings in the literature indicate that these patients often exhibit poor decision-making abilities, high levels of cognitive rigidity, impaired set-shifting, and visual-spatial impairments [37, 38]. In recent years, substantial advancements have been achieved in the exploration of EDs through the application of rs-fMRI [39]. This methodology entails the measurement of blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals reflecting live brain activity during a resting state, allowing for the investigation of brain functional dynamics [40]. The use of rs-fMRI has unveiled distinctive neural activity variations in individuals with EDs, encompassing changes in brain regions and alterations in functional connectivity associated with appetite, reward systems, cognitive control, and other relevant domains [41–44]. The included studies reported 14 articles on rs-fMRI research in AN, with a total of 293 subjects; 6 articles on rs-fMRI research in BN, with 80 subjects; and 2 articles on rs-fMRI research in BED, with 32 subjects. Due to the smaller number of reports on BED, and the mechanisms of BED and BN being closely related, this review includes subjects with BN and BED together for the study. In addition, 395 healthy subjects were included as controls. No patients with pica were temporarily observed, which may be related to the low prevalence rate of this disorder(Table 1).

Anorexia nervosa involves functional connectivity of brain regions and the whole brain

Brain regions and mechanisms of brain networks. Patients with AN exhibit reduced cortical gray matter volume, particularly in areas involved in self-perception and cognitive control, such as the insula and anterior cingulate cortex [45]. These structural changes in brain regions may contribute to distorted body image perception and difficulties in emotional regulation. Additionally, research has revealed alterations in the patterns of brain functional connectivity among individuals with EDs, affecting several critical brain networks including the Default Mode Network (DMN), Salience Network (SN), and Central Executive Network (CEN). Dysfunction within these networks may serve as the neural basis for characteristic symptoms of AN.

The DMN is one of the most prominent brain networks active during rest, comprising regions such as the Medial Prefrontal Cortex (mPFC), Posterior Cingulate Cortex (PCC), and Posterior cingulate gyrus(PCG). The DMN primarily facilitates self-referential thinking and introspection, which are crucial for studying self-awareness and emotional processing abnormalities in AN patients [46]. Dysfunction in the mPFC may lead to distorted self-evaluation and cognitive biases, intensifying pathological concerns about weight and body image [47], while the PCC plays a crucial role in self-reflection and episodic memory. The SN includes the Anterior Insula (AI) and Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC), which are pivotal for identifying and processing emotional and sensory stimuli [48]. The AI is essential for perceiving internal bodily states (e.g., hunger and satiety) and regulating emotions. The ACC is involved in emotional regulation and impulse control. The Limbic System involves emotion processing and memory formation, primarily including the Amygdala and Hippocampus, with the Amygdala playing a critical role in emotional processing and threat response. The Mesolimbic-Cortical System encompasses reward processing and motivational behavior, mainly including the Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) and Nucleus Accumbens (NAcc), crucial for reward and motivational behaviors. The Orbitofrontal Cortex (OFC) plays a pivotal role in decision-making and reward processing. The Executive Control Network (ECN) involves cognitive control and decision-making, primarily including the Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex (DLPFC) and Frontal Pole (FP) [49]. The DLPFC is crucial for cognitive control and planning behavior, and its dysfunction may underlie erroneous decisions in weight management [50]. The FP is essential for high-level cognitive control and goal-directed behavior. Dysfunctional connectivity in the FP in AN patients may lead to distorted cognitive and behavioral patterns in weight and food control [51]. The Sensorimotor Network (SMN) involves body sensation and motor control, primarily including the Primary Sensory Cortex and Primary Motor Cortex, crucial for sensory processing and motor control. Studies indicate that AN patients exhibit abnormal functional connectivity in these areas, potentially resulting in abnormal perception and control of body parts, further exacerbating distorted perceptions of body image and movement behaviors [52]. The Supplementary Motor Area (SMA) plays a crucial role in motor planning and execution.

Functional Connectivity Decreasing. Patients with AN exhibit decreased functional connectivity in multiple brain regions, which may lead to varying degrees of emotional and cognitive abnormalities. Studies have found reduced functional connectivity in AN patients involved in facial information processing and social cognition in several brain regions, including the left caudate nucleus, left posterior cingulate gyrus, and cerebellum [52–57]. Other research indicates decreased functional connectivity in subcortical brain regions among AN patients, predominantly involving the amygdala, hypothalamus, cingulate gyrus, bilateral caudate nuclei, and posterior insula. These changes in connectivity may be related to abnormal internal sensations and altered pain processing in AN patients [59, 60]. Research has identified weakened functional connectivity between the DMN and other brain areas in AN, potentially disrupting self-reflection and introspection processes. Specifically, reduced connectivity between the mPFC and PCC may contribute to negative self-perception and pathological preoccupation with body weight [61]. Connectivity reductions in areas related to salience and emotion regulation, such as the anterior insula (AI) and anterior cingulate cortex ACC, may contribute to anomalies in emotional regulation and internal state perception. These anomalies provide a neural basis for the disturbed eating behaviors and weight management concerns observed in AN [62]. Studies by Lips MA and others have indicated decreased functional connectivity in the AI region of AN patients, leading to abnormal perceptions of hunger and satiety, which may underlie dysfunctional control over food intake [63]. Wang JN et al. demonstrated reduced connectivity in the ACC of AN patients, potentially contributing to diminished emotional regulation and exacerbating avoidance behaviors towards food and abnormal weight control [21]. Hentschel J reported decreased connectivity in the amygdala of AN patients, potentially exacerbating negative emotional responses to food and weight, thereby increasing fear and avoidance behaviors related to food intake [64]. The hippocampus plays a crucial role in memory formation and emotional regulation. Keeler J reported decreased functional connectivity in the hippocampus of AN patients, which may impact their processing of weight- and food-related memories, further exacerbating avoidance behaviors towards food [65]. Furthermore, studies suggest reduced connectivity in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and NAcc regions among AN patients, potentially leading to diminished perception of food reward and reduced motivation to eat [66, 67]. These connectivity reductions may contribute to the lack of interest in food observed in AN patients. Decreased functional connectivity in the OFC of AN patients may result in reduced responses to food rewards, thereby intensifying excessive preoccupation with weight control and related behaviors [68]. Research by Woodside DB demonstrated reduced connectivity in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (DMPFC) of AN patients, which may lead to impaired decision-making and control behaviors when faced with food and weight issues [35]. Li G et al. found reduced connectivity in the supplementary motor area (SMA) of AN patients, potentially affecting behavioral control over food intake and weight management [69]. Haynos et al. [34] similarly found altered resting-state functional connectivity in brain regions involved in reward processing and habit learning in AN patients, noting weakened connectivity between the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and superior frontal gyrus, ventral caudate and orbitofrontal cortex, and dorsal caudate and frontal, temporal, and basal ganglia regions, with no evidence of enhanced connectivity. Scaife et al. conducted studies on 12 patients with restrictive AN and 14 patients recovering from the disorder, revealing reduced functional connectivity in the lateral visual network during acute AN phases, with persistent changes even after weight restoration [30]. Additional studies have shown lower resting-state functional connectivity between the NAcc and superior frontal gyrus(SFG), ventral caudate and frontal-posterior regions of the caudate nucleus, and dorsal caudate and frontal, temporal, and posterior regions in the AN group [69–71]. Moreover, research has indicated decreased activation in the anterior ventral striatum and insula during hunger states in AN patients [72–75].

Functional Connectivity Increasing. Further research has revealed increased functional connectivity in certain brain regions among individuals with AN, reflecting abnormalities in specific cognitive and emotional processes. These studies highlight heightened connectivity in areas involved in sensory stimulation, aesthetic judgment, and anxiety related to social rejection, such as the right inferior temporal gyrus, left superior parietal lobule, and lateral cortical regions of the occipital lobe [76–79]. AN patients also demonstrate enhanced functional connectivity within the executive control network (ECN) and sensorimotor network (SMN), which may be linked to their excessive focus on weight and abnormal control behaviors concerning food. Moreover, increased connectivity between the posterior cingulate cortex PCC and other regions of the DMN suggests heightened attention to past negative experiences during introspection, thereby exacerbating anxiety related to weight and food. The PPC, critical for spatial cognition and self-awareness, shows heightened functional connectivity in AN patients, potentially leading to distortions in body size and shape perception, influencing their body image cognition [81]. Studies by Davidson TL et al. have identified significantly increased functional connectivity in the mPFC of AN patients, likely associated with their excessive preoccupation with self-image and weight [46]. Heilbrun AB et al. (1999) identified neglect of internal sensations as a risk factor in the development of anorexia nervosa, with subsequent research by Gondo et al. reporting increased functional connectivity between the angular gyrus and fronto-parietal networks, as well as between the anterior insula and default network, possibly linked to excessive cognitive control and heightened self-focus in AN patients [81–84]. Frank et al. found increased functional connectivity between the left nucleus accumbens and left orbitofrontal cortex in underweight AN patients, but after weight restoration, no significant differences were observed in connectivity compared to normal control groups [85]. Time consistency was enhanced between the DMN and prefrontal and dorsolateral prefrontal cortices, as well as the inferior frontal gyrus in the recovery group of AN patients [37].

Other Changes In Functional Connectivity. Several studies have documented alterations in brain functional connectivity among individuals with AN, although they have not explicitly specified enhancements or reductions. Research indicates that changes in resting-state functional connectivity between the NAcc and the left orbitofrontal cortex(OFC) may be linked to impaired reward processing and decision-making abilities in AN patients [86–89]. Sanders’ study suggests that the lateral visual network, including the occipital lobe, occipito-temporal junction, and superior parietal lobule, primarily engages in visual and spatial attention processes relevant to body image processing in eating disorder patients [90]. Lai et al. found increased fALFF values in the right hippocampus and left superior frontal gyrus, and decreased fALFF values in the left rectus and left middle occipital gyrus, indicating alterations in spontaneous brain activity in drug-naive female patients with bulimia nervosa, particularly in frontal, hippocampal, and occipital regions [27]. Moreover, studies have identified intergroup differences in functional connectivity maps within the executive control network (ECN), which reversed after weight restoration. Specifically, AN patients exhibited reduced temporal correlation in ECN functional connectivity maps with the anterior cingulate cortex, given these networks and their regions predominantly involved in cognitive control and emotional processing. These findings may elucidate the impaired cognitive flexibility of AN patients concerning body image and appetite 30 [31, 58, 91–97],.

In summary, these research findings bolster the notion of functional connectivity impairments in regions associated with self-referential processing during resting state, suggesting that cognitive control may serve as a vulnerability marker in the development of anorexia nervosa. The brain regions implicated in anorexia nervosa include the left caudate nucleus, left posterior cingulate gyrus, cerebellum, right temporal pole, left superior parietal lobule, and lateral occipital cortex in the occipital lobe(Figure2. Additionally, the amygdala, hypothalamus, cingulate gyrus, bilateral caudate nuclei, and posterior insula, as well as the occipital lobe, occipito-temporal junction, and superior parietal lobule, are involved. See attached file S1 for specific brain regions, and use BrainNetViewer to generate brain region imaging (Fig. 3). Concerning whole-brain functional connectivity, there are widespread functional connections, such as those between the left inferior frontal gyrus and the left supplementary motor area, and between the left external pallidum and the left thalamus. Extensive functional connections are also observed between the basal ganglia (internal and external pallidum, left tail of the caudate nucleus) and cortical regions (superior frontal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, insula, precuneus, angular gyrus, superior temporal gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus), as well as the cerebellar vermis region.

Bulimia nervosa involves functional connectivity of brain regions and the whole brain

Brain regions and mechanisms of brain networks. BN is characterized by disordered food intake. The brain regions involved in BN include the mid-insula, superior frontal gyrus, putamen, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and amygdala, with increased activation in these areas potentially linked to binge eating behaviors [98]. The posterior PCC plays a crucial role in self-reflection and episodic memory. The PFC is key for cognitive control, decision-making, and emotion regulation. The DLPFC is essential for cognitive control and planning behavior. The VLPFC is important for emotion regulation and impulse control. The OFC is critical for decision-making and reward processing. The mOFC plays a significant role in food reward evaluation and decision-making. The AI is crucial for perceiving internal bodily states and emotion regulation. The ACC is key for emotion regulation and impulse control. The limbic system, involved in emotion processing and memory formation, includes the amygdala and hippocampus. The amygdala is vital for emotional responses and fear processing, while the hippocampus plays a significant role in memory formation and emotion regulation. Additionally, the SMA is crucial for motor planning and execution.

Functional Connectivity Decreasing. The DMN includes regions such as the mPFC, PCC, and PPC. This network is primarily involved in self-referential thinking and introspection. The SN comprises the AI and the ACC. It plays a critical role in detecting and processing emotional and sensory stimuli. The SMN includes the primary sensory cortex and the primary motor cortex, which are involved in bodily sensations and motor control. Mesolimbic-Cortical System is involved in reward processing and motivational behaviors. Key regions include the VTA and the NAcc. The VTA and NAcc are crucial for reward and motivational behaviors. The OFC also plays a significant role in decision-making and reward processing. Research has shown that patients with BN exhibit decreased functional connectivity in various brain regions, which may lead to difficulties in cognitive control and emotional regulation, contributing to binge eating behaviors. Key findings include that reduced functional connectivity in the DLPFC may impair self-control in the presence of food stimuli, triggering binge eating behaviors [99]. Studies by Steward T et al. report decreased connectivity in the VLPFC, which might hinder the ability to inhibit binge impulses during emotional fluctuations [100]. Steding J et al. found reduced connectivity in the mOFC, potentially leading to abnormal perception of food rewards, thereby increasing the motivation to binge eat [18]. BN patients show decreased connectivity in the dACC of the SN, impacting emotional and sensory processing [101]. Research by Mele G et al. indicates reduced connectivity in the hippocampus, affecting memory processing and emotional regulation related to binge eating behaviors [102]. Steward T et al. also observed decreased connectivity in the AI, possibly leading to abnormal perception of hunger and satiety, affecting food control [100]. Frank GK’s study reveals reduced ACC connectivity, making it difficult for patients to control binge behaviors during emotional fluctuations [103].

Primary Sensory Cortex and Primary Motor Cortex, Ehrlich S et al. report reduced connectivity in these regions, which may result in abnormal body perception and motor control, exacerbating binge eating behaviors [104]. Gaudio S indicates that reduced connectivity in the SMA could impair motor control when facing food temptations, leading to binge eating [105].

Kullmann S et al. show decreased connectivity in the VTA and NAcc areas, potentially reducing the perception of food rewards and increasing binge eating behavior [106]. Giel KE’s research suggests that reduced connectivity in the OFC may lead to abnormal food reward perception, heightening binge impulses [107]. Somatosensory Network: Other studies report decreased resting-state connectivity in the somatosensory network, including the posterior cingulate cortex, right middle occipital gyrus, and right precuneus, which may be linked to distorted body image perception in female BN patients [108]. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the neural mechanisms underlying BN to develop more effective treatments targeting these functional connectivity abnormalities.

Functional Connectivity Increasing. Research has identified areas where patients with BN exhibit increased functional connectivity, which may be linked to their cognitive and emotional processing abnormalities. Brooks SJ found that BN patients have increased connectivity in the mPFC, which may relate to their heightened concern with weight and body image. This increased connectivity could lead to abnormal self-reflection and emotional fluctuations when facing food temptations [109]. Domakonda M discovered enhanced connectivity between the PCC and other regions of the DMN in BN patients, potentially leading to more intense negative emotional memories associated with binge eating, thereby exacerbating emotional distress [110]. Zhang J et al. noted increased functional connectivity in the amygdala, suggesting that BN patients may exhibit stronger emotional responses and a greater tendency to binge eat when confronted with food stimuli [111]. Studies have shown enhanced connectivity in the dorsal medial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) within the DMN in BN patients, although no significant differences in resting-state functional connectivity were found in the executive network [112, 113]. A study involving 33 adolescent female BN patients found increased resting-state functional connectivity between the ventral attention network and the DMN. These connectivity changes were associated with excessive concern about body weight or shape and the frequency of binge eating or purging behaviors [114]. Other studies have highlighted the involvement of two parts of the attention network in BN patients. The ventral attention network(VAN), which includes the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex and temporoparietal junction, is activated by significant or novel environmental stimuli during attention orientation or reorientation. The dorsal attention network (DAN), comprising brain regions adjacent to the intraparietal sulcus and frontal eye fields in both hemispheres, is activated during tasks requiring voluntary attention [115–118]. These findings emphasize the complex neural underpinnings of BN and highlight the need for targeted interventions that address these specific connectivity patterns.

Other Changes In Functional Connectivity. BN patients exhibit abnormal functional connectivity in the PFC region, which may lead to impaired decision-making and emotional responses when confronted with food and weight issues [119]. Abnormal functional connectivity in the OFC region may affect BN patients’ perception and response to food rewards [120]. Goller et al. found functional and structural abnormalities in the fronto-striatal circuits of BN patients [121, 122]. Stopyra et al. discovered that within the SN, the dACC and the medial PFC within the DMN exhibit abnormal functional connectivity in patients with BED, with differences in connectivity patterns between BED and BN in each network. Seed-based correlation analysis showed stronger synchronous dACC-retrosplenial cortex activity in the BN group [2]. Other studies indicated that in response to low-calorie food images, BED patients show different neural responses, with decreased activity in the temporal lobes and increased activity in the occipital lobes. The limbic system, occipital lobe, and temporal lobes show significantly more active neural responses, while the frontal lobe and limbic system respond to high-calorie food images [28]. Another study found that compared to healthy controls, BN patients have reduced fALEE values in the left insula and increased fALEE values in the bilateral inferior parietal lobules [29]. Additionally, compared to healthy controls, BN patients exhibit altered functional connectivity between the left and right insula, left inferior occipital gyrus (IOG) and left ACC, left IPL, left medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC) and left PCC, and right IPL and left middle occipital gyrus (MOG), with these changes including both increases and decreases [123–129]. Specifically, after partial weight restoration, activation decreases in the right middle occipital cortex while activation increases in bilateral DMN regions, including the medial PFC, PCC, Precuneus, and inferior parietal lobule, which are the so-called “task-negative” DMN regions. Another study using independent component analysis found no significant differences in the functional connectivity of the dorsal ACC with the rest of the brain in BN patients [58].

In summary, the aforementioned studies predominantly support the notion that the onset mechanisms of bulimia nervosa primarily involve excessive focus on one’s own body weight or shape, as well as theories related to disrupted food intake. The brain regions implicated in bulimia nervosa include the left central insula, superior frontal gyrus, caudate nucleus, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and amygdala(Figure4). See attached file S2 for specific brain regions, and use BrainNetViewer to generate brain region imaging (Fig. 5). Additionally, regions such as the lateral prefrontal cortex and temporo-parietal junction, posterior cingulate cortex, right precuneus, left inferior occipital gyrus, right cuneus, postcentral gyrus, limbic system, occipital lobe, and temporal lobe are involved. Whole-brain functional connectivity highlights connections such as the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex’s connectivity within the salience network, functional connections between the ventral attention network and the default mode network, connectivity between the left inferior frontal gyrus and the left supplementary motor area, as well as the left external pallidum’s connectivity with the left thalamus. Connectivity of the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex with other brain regions is also emphasized.

Binge eating disorder involves functional connectivity of brain regions and the whole brain

Brain regions and mechanisms of brain networks. Literature reports indicate that BED involves several key brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex, DLPFC, OFC, mOFC, amygdala, and hippocampus. The prefrontal cortex is crucial for cognitive control, decision-making, and emotion regulation. The DLPFC plays a significant role in cognitive control and planning behavior. The VLPFC is involved in emotion regulation and impulse control, while the OFC is key in decision-making and reward processing. The mOFC is important for food reward evaluation and decision-making. The limbic system, including the amygdala and hippocampus, is essential for emotion processing and memory formation. The amygdala plays a critical role in emotional responses and fear processing, while the hippocampus is vital for memory formation and emotion regulation. Additionally, the SMA is crucial for motor planning and execution. The mesolimbic-cortical system, which includes the VTA and NAcc, is involved in reward processing and motivational behavior. The VTA and NAcc are key players in reward and motivation. The PCC is involved in self-reflection and contextual memory, while the AI is important for perceiving internal body states and emotion regulation. The ACC is crucial for emotion regulation and impulse control. The DMN comprises regions such as the medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and posterior parietal cortex. It primarily facilitates self-referential thinking and introspection. The SN includes the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex, crucial for identifying and processing emotional and sensory stimuli. The SMN encompasses the primary sensory cortex and primary motor cortex, involved in bodily sensation and motor control.

Functional Connectivity Decreasing. Steward T’s research suggests that BED patients exhibit reduced functional connectivity in the DLPFC, potentially leading to ineffective self-control in the face of food temptations, thereby triggering binge eating behaviors [100]. Val-Laillet D et al. reported that BED patients show decreased connectivity in the VLPFC, making it difficult to inhibit binge eating impulses during emotional fluctuations [52]. Steding J’s study indicates that reduced functional connectivity in the mOFC of BED patients may result in abnormal perception of food rewards, thereby enhancing the motivation to binge eat [18]. Schienle A’s research shows that BED patients have decreased functional connectivity in the hippocampus, affecting memory processing and emotional regulation related to binge eating behaviors [130]. Hege MA et al. reported that reduced connectivity in the AI region of BED patients could lead to abnormal perceptions of hunger and satiety, impacting food control [131]. Additionally, studies have found that BED patients exhibit reduced functional connectivity in the ACC, which may lead to difficulties in effectively controlling binge eating during emotional fluctuations [132]. Other reports indicate that reduced connectivity in the primary sensory and primary motor cortices of BED patients may result in abnormalities in bodily perception and motor control. This reduction in functional connectivity could affect the patients’ perceptions of hunger and satiety, thereby exacerbating binge eating behaviors [133]. McClelland J et al. found that BED patients have decreased connectivity in the SMA, potentially leading to ineffective motor control when facing food temptations and triggering binge eating. The reduction in connectivity in these regions may decrease their perception of food rewards, thus enhancing binge eating behaviors [134]. In the OFC region, reduced connectivity in BED patients might lead to abnormal perception of food rewards, increasing the impulse to binge eat [18]. Kullmann et al. discovered that BED patients show increased connectivity in the mPFC and PCC regions, possibly related to their heightened focus on food and self-reflection [135]. Another study found that decreased connectivity in the OFC of BED patients might cause abnormalities in food reward and decision-making processes, thereby increasing the tendency to binge eat [136]. Literature also indicates that reduced connectivity in the hippocampus of BED patients may affect memory processing and emotional regulation related to binge eating behaviors [137].

Functional Connectivity Increasing. Several studies highlight increased functional connectivity in individuals with BED. It has been observed that BED patients show heightened connectivity in the amygdala, potentially leading to stronger emotional responses and increased tendencies towards binge eating when exposed to food cues [138]. Additionally, research indicates enhanced connectivity between the PCC and other regions of the DMN in BED patients. This heightened connectivity may deepen negative emotional memories associated with binge eating, thereby exacerbating emotional distress [139]. In the mPFC, BED patients exhibit increased functional connectivity, which could be linked to their heightened concerns regarding weight and body image. Such increased connectivity may contribute to abnormal self-reflection and emotional fluctuations when faced with food temptations [35]. Stopyra et al. further identified increased connectivity between the posterior parietal cortex and other DMN regions in BED patients, potentially intensifying negative emotions and memories following binge eating episodes [2]. Moreover, Hege et al. found enhanced connectivity in the NAcc and VTA in BED patients, suggesting altered perception of food rewards that might trigger binge eating behaviors [140]. Mele et al. reported increased functional connectivity in the amygdala of BED patients, associated with heightened emotional responses and increased tendencies towards binge eating in the presence of food cues [102].

Other Changes In Functional Connectivity. Patients with BED exhibit abnormal functional connectivity in the PFC region, which may result in atypical decision-making and emotional responses when confronted with food temptations [20]. Additionally, abnormalities in the OFC region’s functional connectivity among BED patients may influence their perception and response to food rewards [21].

In summary, the aforementioned studies suggest that the pathogenesis of BED primarily involves abnormal perception of food rewards and a lack of impulse control. Brain regions associated with BED include the prefrontal cortex, DLPFC, OFC, mOFC, amygdala and hippocampus. Additionally, other implicated regions include the SMA, VTA, NAcc, PCC, AI and ACC(Figure6). BED patients exhibit impaired cognitive control and decision-making functions in the prefrontal cortex, with reduced functional connectivity in the DLPFC potentially leading to a lack of self-control when faced with food temptations. Abnormal functional connectivity in the OFC and mOFC may result in altered perception of food rewards, thereby enhancing the motivation for binge eating. Changes in the functional connectivity of the amygdala and hippocampus, which are involved in emotion processing and memory formation, may make patients more prone to binge eating during emotional fluctuations. Reduced functional connectivity in the SMA may affect motor control, exacerbating binge eating behavior. Changes in functional connectivity within the VTA and NAcc, regions associated with reward and motivational behaviors, are also related to binge eating behavior. Alterations in the PCC, AI, and ACC may affect patients’ self-reflection and emotional regulation capabilities. Overall, the changes in brain functional connectivity in BED patients involve multiple brain regions and networks, reflecting abnormalities in cognitive control, emotional regulation, and reward processing.

Discussion and conclusions

This review summarized the research over the past decade on eating disorders related to various resting-state brain networks and their plasticity (Fig. 8). Due to the existing studies employing different analytical methods (e.g., seed-based and data-driven, theory-driven, whole-brain analysis), often with small sample sizes and overlapping reports, and the variation in study designs (randomized controlled trials, assessment time points before and after weight restoration, open-label) and clinical populations, the degree of convergence in research findings remains relatively low. Previous studies have detected overall brain volume reductions and regional gray matter (GM) volume decreases, as well as white matter (WM) microstructural changes. The primary affected regions include the prefrontal, parietal, and temporal cortices, hippocampus, amygdala, insula, thalamus, cerebellum, and various brain tracts such as the corona radiata, superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), and fornix. In terms of functional imaging, researchers have highlighted alterations in large-scale brain networks, such as the DMN, ECN, and SN [2, 141–144].

Furthermore, other studies have identified that the pathogenesis of eating disorders is also associated with altered insular function, reduced fornix integrity in limbic pathways, and repetitive reward pathways involving the insula and striatum [145, 146]. For instance, changes in resting-state functional connectivity may be linked to abnormal self-body image perception in patients with anorexia nervosa. The functional connectivity of the auditory network is also diminished, mainly involving the precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, and insula. The precentral and postcentral gyri correspond to primary motor and somatosensory areas, respectively, indicating decreased somatosensory motor responses in patients with anorexia nervosa. Altered insular connectivity may be associated with abnormalities in appetite regulation, taste information processing, and pain sensitivity in these patients. Patients with bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder exhibit hyperactive reward systems. Compared to healthy controls, patients with bulimia nervosa show higher activity in the brain’s reward centers, such as the ventral striatum, during the resting state [147–152]. There is evidence of disrupted connectivity in emotion regulation centers, such as the anterior cingulate cortex and other brain regions related to emotional regulation, during the resting state in bulimia nervosa patients [153–155]. Reduced functionality in brain regions associated with decision-making and self-control has been linked to repetitive binge-eating behaviors [156–158]. In most individuals, brain function appears to reverse after weight restoration. However, current research continues to provide crucial insights and future directions regarding the impact of eating disorders on functional brain circuits and clinical outcomes.

The application of rs-fMRI imaging technology has not only helped reveal the abnormal functional connectivity in the brains of patients with EDs but also opened new possibilities for diagnosis, intervention, and treatment. By analyzing the resting-state functional connectivity of patients with eating disorders, specific abnormal patterns in brain regions and networks can be identified, providing objective evidence for diagnosis and assessment. For example, combining the functional connectivity patterns of the DMN and SN can more accurately evaluate patients’ abnormal cognition and emotional responses related to weight and food. As treatment progresses, the functional connectivity in the brains of eating disorder patients may change. Rs-fMRI technology can be used to monitor treatment response and evaluate changes at the brain function level. For instance, whether the functional connectivity of the DMN and ECN returns to normal post-treatment can serve as an important indicator of treatment effectiveness. Based on the resting-state functional connectivity patterns of different patients, personalized treatment strategies can be developed. For example, neurofeedback training or neuromodulation techniques targeting specific functional connectivity abnormalities may positively affect the restoration of normal eating behaviors and weight management. Despite the progress made in the field of resting-state MRI studies of patients with eating disorders, research on their resting-state functional connectivity abnormalities still faces several challenges and unresolved questions.

Future research could be expanded in the following areas. Multimodal Imaging Studies: Integrate resting-state fMRI with structural MRI, electroencephalography (EEG), and magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) to further explore the complex relationships between brain structure and function in patients with BN and BED. Longitudinal Studies: Conduct long-term follow-up studies to monitor changes in brain functional connectivity in BN and BED patients post-treatment, and investigate the relationship between these changes and the long-term course of the disorder, as well as the risk of relapse. Genetic and Environmental Factors: Examine the roles of genetic and environmental factors in the development of abnormal brain functional connectivity in BN and BED patients, aiming to uncover the complex etiological mechanisms involved. Neurofeedback and Neuromodulation Therapies: Develop and apply neurofeedback and neuromodulation treatment strategies based on resting-state fMRI to assess their effects on regulating brain functional connectivity in BN and BED patients.

This review primarily summarizes resting-state functional imaging data related to eating disorders from the past decade and does not include task-based fMRI data or studies employing other neuroimaging methods. As a result, there may be limitations. Future neuroimaging research should incorporate a more comprehensive range of literature to provide reliable insights for subsequent studies.
