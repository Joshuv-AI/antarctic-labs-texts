Phenome-wide analysis of downstream health outcomes following second-line antidiabetic agent prescriptions in All of Us
Authors: Maxwell Salvatore, Bingyu Zhang, Huilin Tang, Yiwen Lu, Dazheng Zhang, Ting Zhou, Yuan Lu, Anastassia Amaro, Marylyn D. Ritchie, Yong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are increasingly prescribed for type 2 diabetes (T2D) and weight management, yet their real-world health impacts remain understudied. Using a retrospective cohort design with electronic health record data from 17,267 adults with type 2 diabetes in the All of Us Research Program, we conduct propensity score-matched phenome-wide association studies comparing diagnoses following GLP-1 RA prescription (including semaglutide-specific analyses) to those following sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP4i) prescriptions between January 2018 and October 2023. We employ both intention-to-treat and per-protocol Cox proportional hazards models alongside restricted mean survival time analyses evaluating up to 974 phenotypes. We identify multiple phenome-wide significant and suggestive associations, including for cardiovascular, genitourinary, dental, and metabolic outcomes. Compared to SGLT2i, semaglutide demonstrates reduced risk for genitourinary infections in women (e.g., candidiasis of vulva and vagina (per-protocol hazard ratio 0.31, 95% confidence interval (0.17-0.55)). Compared to DPP4i, GLP-1 RAs are associated with reduced risk of diseases of hard tissues of teeth (0.45 (0.33-0.61)). Time-to-event analyses reveal modest delays for key diagnoses. These findings underscore differences in downstream diagnostic associations across second-line T2D therapies and highlight semaglutide’s distinct profile, with implications for clinical decision-making and personalized prescribing.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) is a pervasive chronic condition that affects millions globally, with devastating complications when poorly managed1–3. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have revolutionized diabetes management since exenatide’s approval in 20054–7. These medications mimic incretin hormone action, inhibiting glucagon release and slowing gastric emptying to improve glucose regulation. The FDA’s 2021 approval of semaglutide for chronic weight management marked a significant expansion of GLP-1 RA use beyond glucose control8, with semaglutide demonstrating superior efficacy (15% weight loss within one year versus 5-10% with earlier agents9–14) and unique pharmacokinetic properties15, becoming the most commonly prescribed GLP-1 RA through September 202516.

This approval sparked enormous public interest, with GLP-1 RA prescriptions increasing 3% (September–December 2023), 21% (December 2023–March 2024), 8% (March–June 2024), and 12% (June–September 2024)17. Concurrently, telehealth platforms dramatically expanded access18–21. As of November 2025, 1 in 8 American adults reports current GLP-1 RA use22. This rapid adoption through novel care delivery models underscores the urgent need for robust safety surveillance and comparative effectiveness research, including those specifically focused on semaglutide.

Despite their growing utilization23–26 and the media’s portrayal of expected and unexpected benefits of GLP-1 RAs27,28, there exists a paucity of real-world data examining their downstream health effects. Current literature primarily focuses on clinical trial outcomes10,11,14,29–31 and on cardiovascular32–37, kidney32,34,36–38, neurocognitive39–42, and substance use outcomes43–47, leaving a substantial gap in our understanding of the real-world impacts of these medications. Understanding the potential benefits and, more importantly, any associated harms of GLP-1 RA use is crucial.

Using the US Department of Veterans Affairs databases, Xie, Choi & Al-Aly48 systematically tested for associations across 175 clinical outcomes comparing GLP-1 RA prescription to three anti-diabetes therapies among individuals with T2D. They identified reduced risk for substance use and psychotic disorders, seizures, neurocognitive disorders, coagulation disorders, cardiometabolic disorders, infectious illnesses, and several respiratory conditions among GLP-1 RA users. These findings align with prior evidence from multiple studies32–37,43–47,49–53. The study also found increased risk of gastrointestinal disorders, hypotension and syncope, arthritic disorders, nephrolithiasis, interstitial nephritis, and drug-induced pancreatitis associated with GLP-1 RA use, with some findings consistent with known adverse effects or potential protective effects of alternative medications54–63. As a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS), this systematic investigation is hypothesis-generating and crucial for identifying knowledge gaps and generating information.

Our study extends beyond the work of Xie and colleagues by examining an even broader range of clinical outcomes (up to 974 phenotypes) while leveraging electronic health record (EHR) data from the All of Us Research program (AOU)64, a nationally recruited EHR-linked biobank specifically designed to oversample groups historically underrepresented in biomedical research. This diverse cohort provides a significant opportunity to validate previous findings in a demographically different population from the predominantly older, White, male veterans studied by Xie and colleagues.

By examining real-world usage patterns and health outcomes, we aim to investigate the downstream phenotypes (i.e., observed clinical outcomes based on diagnosis codes) associated with GLP-1 RA use. Specifically, this study compares diagnoses post-treatment initiation between those prescribed GLP-1 RAs and those prescribed other second-line anti-diabetes therapies, namely sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP4i). To achieve these objectives, we employed a robust methodological approach combining phenome-wide propensity-score matched intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) Cox proportional hazard models, alongside restricted mean survival time (RMST) analyses. The ITT analysis preserves randomization by analyzing participants according to their initial treatment assignment regardless of adherence. In contrast, the PP analysis considers only those who adhered to the treatment, providing complementary perspectives on prescription impacts. This approach (Fig. S1) allows for evaluating relative and absolute associations between GLP-1 RA prescription and a wide array of health outcomes in a demographically diverse population.

Substantial baseline differences were observed across cohorts. The GLP-1 RA cohort was diagnosed with T2D at a younger age (mean age 50.7 years vs. 55.2 years for SGLT2i and 54.7 years for DPP4i), more likely to be female (67% vs. 45% for SGLT2i and 58% for DPP4i), and more likely to have obesity (73% vs. 55% for SGLT2i and 51% for DPP4i). The SGLT2i cohort, on average, had longer EHR follow-up (12.8 vs. 11.9 years for GLP-1 RA and 10.7 years for DPP4i) and had higher CCI scores (3.3 vs 2.7 for GLP-1 RA and 2.7 for DPP4i). Individuals prescribed DPP4i were less likely to be non-Hispanic White (42% vs 51% for GLP-1 RA and SGLT2i).

Patients prescribed semaglutide were considered a subset of the GLP-1 RA cohort. Compared to those prescribed non-semaglutide GLP-1 RAs, those prescribed semaglutide were diagnosed with T2D at an older age (mean age 51.3 vs 50.4), more likely to be female (71% vs. 65%), more likely to be White (53% vs 48%), more likely to have obesity (79% vs. 70%), and have lower CCI scores (2.6 vs. 2.7). Despite these differences among individuals prescribed different GLP-1 RAs, those prescribed semaglutide, like the overall GLP-1 RA group, were more likely to be diagnosed with T2D at a younger age, to be female, and to have obesity compared to those prescribed SGLT2is and DPP4is (Supplementary Data 1).

This PheWAS explored 600 phenotypes with sample sizes ranging from 2034 for hypertension to 8772 for chills (without fever).

Restricted mean survival time

In phenome-wide comparisons of time-to-diagnosis, GLP-1 RA prescription was associated with heterogeneous RMST differences relative to SGLT2i and DPP4i prescription (Fig. S2). Compared to SGLT2i in intention-to-treat analyses, GLP-1 RAs were not significantly associated with differential time to diagnosis for any phenotype, after accounting for multiple testing; across 63 suggestive phenotypes, GLP-1 RA was associated with earlier diagnoses for 87% of traits and later diagnoses for 13% (Fig. S2a). In per-protocol analyses, however, GLP-1 RA prescription was significantly associated with earlier diagnosis of pain in the knee (−36.9 days, 95% CI: −54.3, −19.6; Fig. S2b). Across 92 significant and suggestive phenotypes in the per-protocol comparison, GLP-1 RA prescription was associated with earlier diagnoses for 63% of traits and later diagnoses for 37%. Comparisons between GLP-1 RA and DPP4i prescriptions revealed a significant delay in time to diagnosis for diseases of hard tissues of teeth ( ~ 21 days over 3 years) in the per-protocol analysis (Fig. S2d), consistent with the Cox proportional hazards findings. When analyses were restricted to semaglutide (Fig. S3), a stronger tendency toward delayed diagnosis was observed: semaglutide was associated with later time to diagnosis for 50–61% of traits compared to SGLT2i and 70-77% of traits compared to DPP4i, with consistent enrichment in cardiovascular and endocrine/metabolic conditions. Notably, semaglutide was significantly associated with longer time to genitourinary diagnoses compared to SGLT2i, corroborating the Cox proportional hazards findings. Across all analyses, several phenotypes—such as hypertension and hyperglycemia—exhibited large RMST differences, with magnitudes often exceeding 30 days over three years, though most differences were modest (typically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ < $$\end{document}<1 month).

Discussion

GLP-1 RAs, including semaglutide (e.g., Ozempic), have garnered widespread attention, largely due to media coverage of their substantial weight loss effects in the wake of semaglutide’s FDA approval for weight management in 2021. As many as 1 in 8 Americans self-report current GLP-1 RA use22, with rapidly growing uptake17,23,24. This study compared clinical outcomes associated with GLP-1 RA and semaglutide prescription to those with SGLT2 and DPP4 inhibitors between January 2018 and October 2023 among individuals with T2D in the NIH All of Us Research Program.

Our phenome-wide association study identified four key clinically meaningful findings. First, GLP-1 RAs were consistently associated with reduced risk of genitourinary infections in women compared to SGLT2is, with even stronger protective effects seen specifically with semaglutide (PP HR (95% CI) 0.37 (0.25, 0.55) for inflammatory disease of cervix, vagina, and vulva). Second, semaglutide demonstrated superior protection against atrial fibrillation and flutter compared to DPP4is (ITT HR 0.43 (0.27, 0.66)), suggesting potential cardiovascular benefits beyond those previously documented for major adverse cardiac events59,65,66, possibly attributable to reduced alcohol consumption. Third, compared to DPP4is, GLP-1 RAs were significantly associated with reduced risk of dental conditions (PP HR 0.45 (0.33, 0.61) for diseases of hard tissues of teeth), a novel finding with implications for oral health management in patients with T2D. Fourth, GLP-1 RA prescription was associated with malignant neoplasm of the skin (ITT HR 1.54 (1.25, 1.90); not significant in PP analysis) compared to SGLT2i prescription. This finding contrasts with prior evidence showing null or protective associations (Xie and colleagues ITT HR 1.05 (0.96, 1.15); others67,68) and likely reflects differential surveillance rather than increased malignancy risk. As injectables, GLP-1 RAs may cause injection site reactions, prompting dermatology visits and subsequent incidental screenings.

Our findings corroborate and extend those reported by Xie and colleagues48 in their comprehensive analysis of GLP-1 RA effects. Like their study, we observed reduced risk of genitourinary conditions among GLP-1 RA users compared to SGLT2i users, particularly inflammatory diseases of female pelvic organs (ITT HR 0.56 (0.42, 0.75), suggestive in our study, versus ITT HR 0.63 (0.56, 0.71) in Xie et al.), aligning with existing literature documenting increased risk of vulvovaginal candidiasis among female SGLT2i users with T2D69–71. However, our analyses reveal more nuanced patterns, especially for semaglutide-specific effects. While Xie found no significant association between GLP-1 RAs and cardiac dysrhythmias (HR 1.02 [95% CI 0.99, 1.05]), our semaglutide-specific analysis demonstrated substantial protection against atrial fibrillation and flutter (ITT HR 0.43 [95% CI 0.27, 0.66]) compared to DPP4 inhibitors (Table 2). Similarly, our finding of reduced dental disease risk with GLP-1 RAs differs from Xie’s more modest, non-significant reductions in dental conditions. Anti-inflammatory processes associated with GLP-1 RA action may reduce oral inflammation risk74,75, while limited evidence suggests dry mouth from DPP4i use76,77 might facilitate dental caries development78,79. Similarly, Xie and colleagues reported reduced risk for fluid and electrolyte disorders among GLP-1 RA users (ITT HR 0.97 (0.95, 0.99)); we identified a significant reduction in mixed acid-base disorders among those prescribed semaglutide (ITT HR 0.46 (0.32, 0.66)).

Given recent interest in the use of GLP-1 RAs to treat substance use disorders, we highlight that GLP-1 RA (PP HR 0.45 (0.29, 0.69) for alcohol use disorders) and semaglutide (PP HR 0.51 (0.30, 0.85) for current tobacco use and nicotine dependence) prescriptions suggested a reduced risk of substance use compared to those prescribed DPP4is.

RMST analyses revealed systematic differences in time to diagnosis, though often modest (typically <1 month over 3 years). However, semaglutide led to longer delays in several phenotypes, including an 86-day delay in hypertension diagnosis, highlighting the value of combining relative and absolute measures to assess clinical impact and suggesting that observed patterns may reflect differences in surveillance or disease progression across therapies.

GLP-1 RA PheWAS were better powered (n = 8219) than semaglutide-specific analyses (n = 2720). GLP-1 RA analyses may be affected by heterogeneity in effects14,29,80–82 and prescribing practices83,84, as shown by statistically significant baseline covariate differences between semaglutide and other GLP-1 RAs (Table 1).

These findings have important implications for clinical practice and public health oversight. For clinical decision support, providers may consider prioritizing GLP-1 RAs, particularly semaglutide, for women with T2D who have recurrent genitourinary infections or are at high risk for such infections (e.g., those prescribed SGLT2i therapy). The observed cardioprotective effects of semaglutide against arrhythmias may warrant consideration in patients with or at risk for cardiac conduction disorders. For patient education, these data support discussing the potential reduced risk of dental problems with GLP-1 RAs compared to DPP4is. From a regulatory perspective, the differential effects observed between semaglutide and other GLP-1 RAs highlight the need for drug-specific rather than class-wide safety assessments, particularly as telehealth prescribing limits systematic follow-up.

This nuanced approach is essential because GLP-1 RAs vary by duration of action and pharmacodynamic features, which influence prescribing decisions based on glycemic targets, weight loss goals, side effect profiles, and patient preferences. While GLP-1 RAs typically offer the greatest reductions in HbA1c and weight7,85–88, SGLT2is and GLP-1 RAs provide cardiovascular and renal benefits89–91. DPP4is are weight-neutral7,92,93. The emerging pharmacogenomic literature, such as Dawed and colleagues’ identification of ARRB1 variants with implications for the timing of GLP-1 RA treatment initiation81, and Jakhar and colleagues’ work on inter-individual variation in drug response (primarily focused on exenatide and liraglutide)82, further supports the need for precision in prescribing decisions. However, combination therapy, such as GLP-1 RA combined with SGLT2i, requires additional investigation94–96.

This study has several strengths. First, the use of propensity score matching with a small caliper, in theory, reduces systematic baseline differences between treated and untreated participants, mitigating concerns related to confounding bias. Second, we present relative (via hazard ratios) and absolute (via RMST differences) differences in risk following ITT and PP approaches, better contextualizing results, and enhancing interpretability. Comparing results across ITT and PP approaches enables us to consider the roles that adherence, switching, and discontinuation play in outcomes. Consistency across approaches strengthens inference while differences help identify outcomes that may be sensitive to exposure duration or post-initiation treatment changes. Third, AOU is a large, diverse cohort that spans the USA, which can capture differences and trends potentially missed or absent using single medical center-based/regional cohorts or those that are demographically homogenous. Fourth, we explore semaglutide-specific analyses. While many recent analyses consider GLP-1 RAs as a class, in part to increase sample size and because of relatively similar pharmacogenomic actions of drugs within the class, there may be heterogeneity in how different GLP-1 RAs are prescribed. Additionally, semaglutide has received targeted attention recently, so results looking at semaglutide specifically are particularly relevant.

This study also has several important limitations. First, as with any agnostic association analysis using observational data, positive results require follow-up mechanism-focused studies. Despite efforts to reduce measured confounding, unmeasured confounding may still be present. Second, propensity score models are sensitive to model specification97, which may be misspecified here. Additional analyses can be considered, exploring various propensity models (e.g., higher-dimensional, wider caliper width), including non-parametric and data-driven approaches. Third, our focus on T2D patients limits the generalizability to the rapidly growing population using GLP-1 RAs for weight management (prescriptions rose 27% for GLP-1 RAs and 41% for semaglutide between March and June 2024)17. However, sample size constraints prevented comparisons with other anti-obesity medications. These constraints also preclude us from considering stratified analyses or considering oral and injectable formulations, which may have clinically meaningful differences (e.g., injection site reactions may lead to increased incidental dermatological diagnoses). Fourth, GLP-1 RA use is likely underreported in EHR data, particularly for telehealth prescriptions—only 5.3% of All of Us participants had documented GLP-1 RA prescriptions versus 12.5% self-reported use in recent surveys98, potentially biasing our estimates toward the null. More advanced methods to account for underreported exposure status can be explored and incorporated in future analyses99. Fifth, prescription records do not guarantee medication adherence, a limitation inherent to all EHR-based pharmacoepidemiology studies. Sixth, we targeted potential incident cases by excluding individuals with a record of the outcome within 2 years prior to the index date. While this phenome-wide application has been used elsewhere48, it may be less effective for chronic conditions, including cardiovascular diseases and cancers. Seventh, while we include the history of metformin and insulin use as covariates in the matching model, concomitant use of alternative antidiabetes medications is not considered, potentially masking clinically meaningful heterogeneity in treatment response. Finally, because this study compared GLP-1 RA with other glucose-lowering drug classes, the observed associations may partly reflect the effects of the comparator treatments rather than the direct pharmacologic effects of GLP-1 RA. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as comparative rather than absolute effects.

This study examines downstream impacts of GLP-1 RA prescription compared to two other, common second-line T2D therapies— SGLT2 inhibitors and DPP4 inhibitors—in a national, diverse, real-world cohort of individuals with T2D. We observed that GLP-1 RAs, particularly semaglutide, were associated with reduced risk of several clinically relevant outcomes, including genitourinary infections in women, dental conditions, cardiac arrhythmias, and hyperglycemia. Conversely, we also identified potential adverse associations, such as increased risks of dysthymic disorder and vitamin D deficiency, underscoring the importance of comprehensive safety and effectiveness profiling.

While this study provides an initial view into the broader health implications of GLP-1 RA prescription during a time of high public interest and rapidly growing uptake, more work is needed. Future work should investigate the effects of concomitant drug use, particularly GLP-1 RA and SGLT2i co-prescription, and GLP-1 RAs in populations without T2D, particularly those using these medications for weight management, and compare outcomes with other anti-obesity therapies. Stratified analyses by age, race/ethnicity, and BMI category in cohorts with sufficient power will be essential to identify population-specific benefits and risks. In parallel, EHR-linked biobanks offer a unique opportunity to explore genetic predictors of individual response to GLP-1 RAs and semaglutide, enabling more personalized treatment strategies. These findings reinforce the need to consider comorbidities, sex-specific risks, and pharmacogenomic variation in clinical decision-making and point toward the promise of precision prescribing in T2D and obesity care.

Methods

Data description

The All of Us Research Program (AOU) protocol was approved by the AOU Institutional Review Board; all participants provided informed consent. AOU uses a data-passport access model for authorized researchers, offering access to de-identified data, which does not require project-specific IRB review within the Workbench. All data privacy and reporting requirements defined by AOU for data use were adhered to.

AOU is a nationally recruited EHR-linked biobank at the US National Institutes of Health, oversampling groups historically underrepresented in biomedical research64,100. Adults can join directly or at over 340 recruitment sites across the US, where they can consent to EHR data access, provide biospecimens, and complete surveys.

We used the curated data repository version 8101 and incorporated demographic (n = 633,547), International Classification of Diseases (ICD; 9th and 10th revisions) code (n = 345,151), and medication data (n = 334,645). ICD codes were mapped to clinically meaningful diseases (phenotypes) called phecodes (version X)102, as previously described103 (see section “Outcomes of interest”).

The analytic cohort included individuals with self-reported male or female sex assigned at birth, a T2D phecode (EM_202.2; underlying ICD codes listed in Supplementary Data 6), and a prescription for a qualifying medication (including GLP-1 RA, SGLT2i, or DPP4i). To ensure new-user design and adequate baseline characterization, individuals were included only if they: (i) initiated a qualifying drug prescription after January 1, 2018 (after T2D diagnosis; the date of exposure or index date), (ii) had at least one year of medical records before the index date (iii) had no record of qualifying drug within 120 days before the index date, (iv) were not prescribed multiple qualifying drugs on the index date, and (v) had no contraindication (multiple endocrine neoplasia type II, gastroparesis, low eGFR ( < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2), medullary thyroid cancer, hypoglycemia with coma, dialysis, or kidney transplant) in the five years prior to the index date (Supplementary Data 7). After applying these criteria, the final study cohort included 17,267 individuals classified into GLP-1 RA (n = 8219), SGLT2i (n = 5405), and DPP4i (n = 3643) initiators. The study period spanned from January 1, 2018 (shortly after FDA approval of semaglutide) to October 1, 2023 (administrative data cutoff).

Treatment and comparison groups

Our primary treatments of interest were (i) GLP-1 RAs (class) and (ii) semaglutide (drug-specific). GLP-1 RAs included albiglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide, liraglutide, lixisenatide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide (which, despite being a dual GLP-1 and gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP) agonist, has been included in GLP-1 RA class analyses). Our comparison groups were (i) SGLT2i and (ii) DPP4i. SGLT2i consisted of bexagliflozin, canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ertugliflozin, and sotagliflozin. DPP4i consisted of alogliptin, linagliptin, saxagliptin, and sitagliptin.

Drugs were identified using Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) concept IDs corresponding to valid, standard RxNorm and RxNorm Extension drug ingredients that were FDA-approved for use in humans during the period of interest. Consistent with All of Us SQL query practice, concepts corresponding to these OMOP concept IDs and their OMOP-defined descendant concepts were queried. The complete list of concept IDs is presented in Supplementary Data 8.

Outcomes of interest

Outcomes were defined using phecodes, which aggregate ICD codes into interpretable clinical conditions (phenotypes) to facilitate high-throughput phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS)103. We used Phecode X, which contains 3612 unique disease outcomes grouped into 18 clinical disease categories (e.g., cardiovascular, endocrine, neurological)102,104. For each ICD code diagnosis, it was mapped to the corresponding phecode(s), and the date the ICD code was recorded was retained. Mapping was performed using the PheWAS R package (version 0.99.6-1)105,106.

For each treatment–comparator PheWAS, we considered the outcomes with at least 100 occurrences after the index, yielding up to 974 potential disease outcomes. Individuals with a record of the same outcome within the two years prior to the index date were excluded for that outcome to reduce misclassification of prevalent disease.

Obesity, sleep apnea, and diabetes-related conditions were excluded from the main analyses because of their strong association with treatment indication and coding variability. Preliminary analyses revealed apparent positive associations between GLP-1 RA use and phecodes for overweight and obesity, (pre)diabetes, and sleep disorders (Fig. S4); however, we determined these to be artifacts of clinical coding practices rather than true associations. Comparison of BMI trajectories with the timing of obesity phecode assignment showed that qualifying BMI values and recorded diagnoses were poorly correlated in time (Fig. S5), and clinicians specializing in metabolic and endocrine disorders confirmed that obesity-related ICD codes are often recorded with substantial delay or to facilitate medication access. Results for these conditions are therefore not interpreted as primary findings.

Covariates

Covariates were selected to account for potential confounding and to balance treatment groups through matching. Demographics included age at T2D diagnosis, sex assigned at birth, race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic Asian, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, Other/Unknown), and body mass index (BMI) category (based on the most recent measurement before or on the index date). Neighborhood-level characteristics, estimated from the 2017 American Community Survey, include the local proportion with a high school education, the proportion without health insurance, and the median household income. Each measure was categorized as low (bottom 25%), middle (middle 50%), high (top 25%), or unknown. Healthcare utilization measures included the length of follow-up (in years, between the first ICD code diagnosis and the index date) and the number of encounters (number of days with a visit) that occurred before or on the index date. Comorbidities: presence of 17 conditions included in the Charlson Comorbidity Index107,108, defined using ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes (see Supplementary Data 9) and mapped via the comorbidity R package (version 1.1.0.9000)109,110. Laboratory data: number of test results recorded in the two years before the index date for aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood pressure, C-reactive protein, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), glucose, and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), defined using LOINC codes (see Supplementary Data 10). These lab tests were identified using the OMOP concept ID obtained in the AOU cohort builder. Individuals without a record were coded as 0 for that test. We also included indicator variables for history of metformin use and insulin use during the baseline period (see Supplementary Data 8).

Cox proportional hazards models

Outcomes were included if at least 100 events occurred after the index date. Individuals with a record of the corresponding outcome phecode within 2 years before the index date were excluded. Outcomes with fewer than 50 occurrences after matching were excluded from further modeling, resulting in up to 698 outcomes analyzed.

Software and tools

Analyses were conducted using R 4.5.0. Relevant R packages included coxphf (version 1.13.4)115–117 for Firth-corrected Cox proportional hazards models, MatchIt (version 4.7.0)118,119 for propensity score matching, and survival (version 3.8-3)120,121 for Cox proportional hazards models, and survRM2 (version 1.0-4)122 for restricted mean survival time estimation. The code to carry out the analyses performed in this paper is available on GitHub (https://github.com/RitchieLab/glp1-phewas/) and archived via Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.19190669)123.

Ethical considerations

This study was performed using de-identified, publicly available data. Data privacy and reporting requirements defined by the All of Us Research Program for data use were adhered to.
