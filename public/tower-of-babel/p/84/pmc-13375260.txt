Standard malnutrition screening tools inadequately capture comprehensive nutritional needs in outpatients with inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Aileen Xu, Michelle Schneider, Elisabeth Gorecki, Sonya Dave, Chanelle Chua, Poonam Beniwal-Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition is common in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients and associated with worse outcomes and quality of life. This study aimed to assess the feasibility of referring outpatient IBD patients with qualifying modified malnutrition universal screening tool (mMUST) scores to in-person registered dietitian (RD) evaluations. The secondary aim was to analyze the nutritional data collected.MethodsIBD patients seen at a tertiary care center between August and December 2022 were screened with the mMUST. Those with mMUST ≥1 were referred to an RD for comprehensive nutritional evaluation. Data were extracted via chart review and analyzed using t-tests and Fisher’s exact tests.ResultsOf 1698 patients, 990 (58%) were screened with the mMUST. Of those who screened positive and were referred, 19 (20%) completed RD evaluations. Among these, 37% consumed less than 75% of the nutritional needs. Body mass indices were evenly distributed between low, normal, and high. Though 26% of patients met a formal definition for moderate or severe protein-calorie malnutrition, 53% had muscle depletion and 42% had fat depletion.ConclusionsA simple screening tool can identify IBD patients at risk for malnutrition; however, patient- and provider-sided barriers prevent appropriate evaluation. The RD evaluations revealed body composition changes and signs of malnutrition that were not reflected in weight or routine labs. These findings highlight the need for routine malnutrition screening and comprehensive dietitian evaluation for all at-risk IBD patients, not just those who appear underweight or frail.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is associated with an increased risk of malnutrition. Two widely used criteria for malnutrition are from the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) and the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics (Academy)/American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN). The GLIM definition uses a validated screening tool and then at least one phenotypic criterion (unintended weight loss, low body mass index, or reduced muscle mass) and at least one etiologic criterion (reduced food intake or inflammation/disease burden).1 The Academy/ASPEN requires two out of six criteria: (1) insufficient energy intake, (2) weight loss, (3) loss of muscle mass, (4) loss of subcutaneous fat, (5) localized or generalized fluid accumulation, or (6) diminished functional status.2

Malnutrition leads to body composition alterations and sarcopenia, which negatively impacts physical function and quality of life. Mechanisms through which malnutrition in IBD patients develops include reduced and altered oral intake; altered absorption due to disease-related bowel surgeries; increased nutritional requirements due to inflammation, malabsorption, and treatments that can affect nutrient absorption, including corticosteroids, methotrexate, and sulfasalazine.3,4 Estimates of the malnutrition prevalence in IBD patients vary depending on setting and population but are as high as 50% in the outpatient setting.5

The association between malnutrition and unfavorable clinical outcomes is well established in the general population and in the IBD population. The odds of having an IBD flare during a 12-month period were more than doubled in those with impaired nutrition.6 Sarcopenia was associated with two times the odds of treatment failure in IBD patients and an increase in odds for needing surgical rather than just pharmacological interventions.7 In IBD patients specifically, malnutrition was associated with higher rates of in-hospital mortality, longer length-of-stays, and higher costs.8 Additionally, malnutrition correlated with decreased quality of life based on validated questionnaires that evaluated physical symptoms, physical function, emotional function, and social function.9,10

Given the growing body of evidence for the clinical impact of malnutrition, several professional organizations support malnutrition evaluations as part of IBD patient care. In 2017, the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) published guidelines for IBD nutrition management, which included a 96% consensus among an expert panel that malnutrition screening should not only be done at initial diagnosis but also regularly thereafter.11 The American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) guidelines for the management of Crohn’s Disease (CD) include malnutrition screening as part of the initial evaluation at diagnosis.12 The American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) published a clinical practice update in 2024 recommending regular malnutrition screening for all IBD patients, though it did not provide specific guidance on malnutrition screening tools or incorporating comprehensive dietitian evaluations.13

Previous studies have examined the use of IBD malnutrition screening tools. One study found that patient-completed screening during outpatient visits with the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST) was feasible, as 92% of patients found it “easy” to “very easy” to complete. In addition, there was concordance between patient-performed and the provider-performed MUST in patients who were deemed moderate to high risk for malnutrition.14 Other studies have shown that implementing a modified MUST using a point system for body mass indices (BMIs), unintentional weight loss in the last 6 months, and active IBD symptoms in the last 2 weeks also increased malnutrition screening significantly.15 However, a multi-center pilot study in the outpatient setting found that, although 92% of IBD patients completed the screening, barriers including limited availability of dietitians and patient factors restricted access to dietitian follow-up.16

This study aims to assess the feasibility of referring outpatient IBD patients with qualifying modified MUST scores to comprehensive in-person dietitian evaluations. The secondary aim is to analyze the detailed nutritional data collected during these dietitian assessments.

Materials and methods

Adult patients (≥18 years old) with Ulcerative Colitis (UC) or Crohn’s Disease (CD) who attended an in-person or virtual IBD clinic visit at a tertiary care center between August and December 2022 were included. During visits, providers screened patients using a modified MUST (mMUST) and recorded the results in the visit clinic note. The mMUST assigned a point for each of the following: (1) BMI below 18.5, (2) 5% weight loss within the past 6 months, and (3) IBD flare with poor intake lasting more than 5 days within the past 6 months as reported by the patient. Figure 1 summarizes the mMUST criteria used. The criteria used were broadened from previous versions of the mMUST to capture patients who recently had a flare, not just the ones who were actively in a flare, as the screening was conducted in a non-acute, outpatient setting. Additionally, the 6-month look-back period for flare was then consistent with the 6-month look-back period for weight loss.

Modified Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool.

A mMUST ≥ 1 indicated a positive malnutrition risk screen; these patients were referred to a registered dietitian (RD) for a separate visit. This in-person evaluation included a detailed medical and nutrition history, a nutrition focused physical exam, anthropometric measurements, and a review of pertinent labs and weight trends. Figure 2 details the components included in the RD evaluation. The RD also determined whether the patient had malnutrition, based on the standardized Academy/ASPEN criteria.2 Figure 3 shows a flowchart of the inclusion criteria for the study.

Results of the mMUST and dietitian evaluations were extracted via chart review. Exploratory analyses were performed using t-tests for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

Results

Of the 1698 patients who met the criteria for malnutrition screening, 990 were successfully screened using the mMUST (58%). Of these, 118 patients (12%) screened positive for malnutrition risk with a mMUST ≥1; 96 were referred to an RD. Of those referred, 19 patients (20%) completed an RD evaluation.

Of the 19 patients who completed RD evaluations, the mean IBD duration was 14 years. Seventeen patients (89%) were on biologics, and 10 (53%) had a history of IBD surgery. Thirteen (68%) had a disease flare in the 3 months prior to evaluation. Thirteen patients had CD, and 6 patients had UC. The only statistically significant difference in patient and disease characteristics between CD and UC patients was the proportion who had a history of IBD surgery (77% CD vs 0% UC, P < .01). UC patients had higher rates of recent steroid use and flare compared to CD patients, but these were not statistically significant.

Overall, 37% were consuming less than 75% of their nutritional intake needs, and 63% reported self-imposed dietary restrictions. There was no statistically significant difference between the laboratory or nutritional history characteristics between CD vs UC patients or IBD surgery vs no IBD surgery patients.

Table 2 summarizes the findings from the dietitian evaluations, split by disease type and IBD surgery history. The mean BMI was 23 kg/m2, and the mean percent of ideal body weight was 107%. The distribution of patients by BMI category was approximately equal: 32% had BMI <18.5, 36% had BMI 18.5-25, and 32% had BMI >25. Alterations of the hair, eyes, mouth, skin, or nails on the micronutrient exam were observed in 53% of patients, though the etiology of alterations was not definitively identified as micronutrient deficiency in all cases. Reduced handgrip strength in at least one hand was noted in 18%, 16% met criteria for moderate protein-calorie malnutrition, and 10% met criteria for severe protein-calorie malnutrition based on the Academy/ASPEN criteria.2 However, 53% exhibited depletion in at least one muscle group, and 42% had evidence of fat depletion. The only significant difference between comparison groups was that patients with IBD surgery history had significantly higher rates of abnormal micronutrient exams compared to those who did not have IBD surgery history (89% vs 13%, P <.01).

Discussion

This study uniquely investigates both the feasibility of malnutrition screening implementation and the in-depth assessments by RDs of patients who screened positive. The granular dietitian-collected data provided further insights into the value of comprehensive malnutrition evaluations. Notably, most patients had BMIs in the normal or overweight range, highlighting the importance of placing a nutrition referral for all patients with IBD at risk for malnutrition, not only for those who are underweight or appear frail.

Although 26% of evaluated patients met the formal criteria for malnutrition, a greater proportion had concerning findings. Nearly 40% of patients consumed less than 75% of their nutritional needs, and over 60% self-restricted their diets. Patients with a history of IBD surgery had higher rates of abnormal micronutrient exam (89%), muscle depletion (60%), and fat depletion (50%), suggesting increased vulnerability to micronutrient deficiency and sarcopenia. Additionally, among the 19 patients evaluated, none had B12 or folate deficiencies, and only 22% had low ferritin. These findings suggest that routine labs may not capture important micronutrient data that can lead to meaningful muscle deterioration.

The mMUST was created as part of the IBD-Nutrition Care Pathway (IBD-NCP), specifically developed for IBD patients in the outpatient setting.16 Despite its simplicity, comprising of three questions that could be embedded into providers’ clinic notes, 42% of eligible patients in this study were not screened. This suggests that there are provider-sided barriers to implementation that persist despite having a standardized tool readily available. Strategies to improve screening rates include increasing provider buy-in and workflow integration, such as incorporating the mMUST into the rooming process.

Additionally, of those referred to a RD, only 20% completed a full evaluation. This suggests that there are patient-sided barriers as well, including limited understanding of the importance of the dietitian evaluation, difficulties in finding an available and convenient dietitian appointment, or lack of insurance coverage. Addressing these obstacles will also be crucial. Other studies have similarly found barriers to implementing universal screening. In another large tertiary care academic IBD center that trialed universal malnutrition screening for all new patients, 19% of patients were not screened, and 44% of patients who had a positive screen were not seen by an RD.17

Conclusion

The prevalence and multifactorial risk of malnutrition in the IBD population highlights the need for regular malnutrition screening and evaluations. Our findings show that though standardized screening protocols such as the mMUST are feasible to adopt, effective implementation will require addressing provider- and patient-level barriers. Importantly, malnutrition evaluation should not be limited to underweight individuals or those with abnormal nutrient lab results. A broader understanding of the diverse risk factors for malnutrition in IBD is essential to better identify at-risk patients. There remains significant potential for future studies on the clinical impact of structured malnutrition screening and intervention in the IBD population.
