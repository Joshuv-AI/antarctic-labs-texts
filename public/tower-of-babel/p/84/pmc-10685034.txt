Transcriptome profiling reveals SLC5A5 regulates chicken ovarian follicle granulosa cell proliferation, apoptosis, and steroid hormone synthesis
Authors: Xiaoxu Shen, Xiyu Zhao, Haorong He, Yao Zhang, Qing Zhu, Huadong Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The egg-laying performance of hens holds significant economic importance within the poultry industry. Broody inheritance of the parent stock of chickens can result in poor options for the improvement of egg production, and is a phenomenon influenced by multiple genetic factors. However, few studies have been conducted to delineate the molecular mechanism of ovarian regression in brooding chickens. Here, we explored the pivotal genes responsible for the regulation of ovarian follicles in laying hens, using RNA-sequencing analysis on the small ovarian follicles from broody and laying chickens. Sequencing data analysis revealed the differential expression of 200 genes, with a predominant enrichment in biological processes related to cell activation and metabolism. Among these genes, we focused on solute carrier family 5 member 5 (SLC5A5), which exhibited markedly higher RNA expression levels in follicles from laying compared with broody chickens. Subsequent cellular function studies with knockdown of SLC5A5 in chicken ovarian follicle granulosa cells (GCs) led to the down-regulation of genes associated with cell proliferation and steroid hormone synthesis, and concurrent promotion of gene expression linked to apoptosis. These findings indicated that SLC5A5 deficiency led to the inhibition of proliferation, steroid hormone synthesis and secretion, and promotion of apoptosis in chicken GCs. Our study demonstrated a pivotal role for SLC5A5 in the development and function of chicken GCs, shedding light on its potential significance in the broader context of chicken ovarian follicle development, and providing a prospective target to improve the egg-laying performance of chickens via molecular marker-assisted breeding technology.

INTRODUCTION

Chickens are a vital domestic poultry species, providing a significant supply of both meat and egg products for human consumption. Over the past century, the insufficiency of original breeds in terms of meat and egg production capacity has prompted the emergence of extensive artificial breeding programs, leading to a substantial enhancement in chicken production performance (Lawal and Hanotte, 2021). The innate broodiness instinct for laying hens, which causes them to cease laying eggs and commence incubation on their nests, has been gradually phased out through continuous artificial selection (Jiang et al., 2005). This deliberate breeding strategy aims to prolong the egg-laying period and elevate overall egg production (Jiang et al., 2010). Broodiness in chickens represents a normal physiological process, accompanied by ovarian atrophy and atresia of ovarian follicles. This intricate phenomenon is regulated by various genetic factors (Shen et al., 2016).

Granulosa cells (GCs) are a pivotal component and among the most influential factors in the development and atresia of ovarian follicles. Genes implicated in GC development play fundamental roles in chicken egg formation. Genes associated with positive regulation of the cell cycle and proliferation, such as cyclin-dependent kinase 2 (CDK2), cyclin Ds (CCNDs), and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), promote the proliferation of GCs, thereby sustaining the pool of GCs (Wei et al., 2022). Conversely, genes controlling cell apoptosis, including cysteinyl aspartate specific proteinase (CASPs), facilitate GC apoptosis, leading to depletion of the GC pool and subsequent ovarian follicle atresia (Quirk et al., 2004). Additionally, genes involved in steroidogenesis, such as steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (STAR), cytochrome P450 family 19 subfamily A member 1 (CYP19A1), and cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (CYP11A1), exert positive regulation on follicle development by enhancing the synthesis of estradiol and progesterone (Chou and Chen, 2018). These genes have a pivotal influence on the fate of follicle development and atresia. Nonetheless, many other crucial genes governing the regulation of ovarian follicle development have yet to be fully explored.

The Tianfu chicken is a locally bred Chinese chicken variety that has undergone continuous selection and breeding. Interestingly, a portion of these chickens still retain the broodiness instinct, while others have been selectively bred to lose it. Consequently, both normal laying and broody Tianfu chickens serve as valuable research models for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying ovarian follicle atresia in chickens. The primary objective of this study was to determine the pivotal genes influencing ovarian follicle development in Tianfu chickens through transcriptome analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and Sample Collection

All animal experiments received ethical clearance from the Animal Welfare Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (approval number: 2019102013). These experiments took place at the poultry breeding farm of Sichuan Agricultural University (Yaan, China). The 200-day-old Tianfu chickens were raised following standard protocols. The selection process, ovarian development histomorphology assessments, and serum biochemical tests for both laying and broody chickens were shown in our prior study (He et al., 2022). In this study, the small ovarian follicles (white follicle and small yellow follicle mixture) from 3 laying chickens and 3 broody chickens were collected for RNA sequencing and gene expression validation. Furthermore, the follicles at different developmental stages including small white follicles (SWF, <2 mm), big white follicles (BWF, 3–5 mm), small yellow follicles (SYF, 6–8 mm), big yellow follicles (BYF, 9–12 mm), and F1 to F5 grade follicles from 3 laying chickens were collected for gene expression validation. Besides, the grade follicles from 24 chickens were used for GC culture (2 chickens were used for cell culture at each time, and a total of 12 times of cell culture were performed). Subsequently, the experimental chickens were humanely euthanized through intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital at a dosage of 30 mg/kg, after which samples were collected or primary cells were extracted.

Transcriptome Analysis

After removing graded follicles and ovarian matrix, the small ovarian follicles were collected from 3 laying chickens and 3 broody chickens, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently ground into a fine powder. RNA extraction from these powdered samples was carried out using RNAiso plus reagents (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) following standard procedures. The quality of the total RNA was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Only the RNA samples that met the required quality standards were employed in constructing cDNA libraries, which were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, Beverly, MA). These libraries were then subjected to sequencing using the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina). To ensure data quality, the sequencing raw data underwent rigorous quality control, involving the use of FastQC software (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) for initial assessment. The clean reads obtained from this process were subsequently aligned to the chicken genome (Gallus gallus 5.0) using Hisat2 software (https://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2/). Following alignment, the reads were assembled using the StringTie software (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/index.shtml). Subsequently, gene expression levels were quantified in terms of Fragments Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped fragments (FPKM) and were counted utilizing the FeatureCounts software (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/Rsubread.html). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined using the DESeq2 software (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html). Lastly, gene ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses for the DEGs were conducted using the ClusterProfiler R package (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler.html). The raw sequencing data were available in the SRA database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) with the accession number PRJNA721929.

Cell Culture and Transfection

Chicken GCs were isolated from the ovarian follicles of Tianfu chickens. Initially, grade follicles were meticulously selected, and the granular layer within each follicle was carefully extracted. Subsequently, the granular layer tissue was cut into 1 mm³ pieces using ophthalmic scissors. These tissue pieces were then subjected to digestion with 20 mL of 0.1% type II collagenase (BioFroxx, Einhausen, Germany) for 10 min at 37°C. The digestion process was halted by introducing an equal volume of complete media, which comprised 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Gibco, Langley, OK), 89% Medium 199 (Gibco), and 1% Penicillin and Streptomycin mix (Gibco). The resulting mixture was filtered through a 70 μm cell sieve, and the collected filtrate was obtained. Following centrifugation (500× g, 5 min), the cells were resuspended in an appropriate quantity of complete medium. Subsequently, these cells were introduced into cell culture plates with varying specifications as dictated by the requirements of subsequent experiments. These culture plates were then placed within an incubator, maintaining a constant temperature of 37°C with 5% CO2. After a 3-h attachment period, the culture medium was replaced to sustain continued growth.

For gene silencing experiments, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting SLC5A5 mRNA (GenBank ID: XM_025144293.3) were designed and chemically synthesized by GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). These siRNAs were further transfected into the chicken GCs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and Opti-MEM (Gibco), following the manufacturer's instructions. The sequences of siRNAs are provided in the Supplementary Table S1.

RNA Expression Analysis

Total RNAs from the GCs were isolated following the manufacturer's instructions using RNAiso plus reagents from Takara. Subsequently, the quality of the total RNA was assessed using the Nanodrop 2000 nucleic acid and protein detector (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). High-quality RNA samples were then subjected to reverse transcription into complementary DNAs (cDNAs) using the PrimeScript RT Reagent with the gDNA Eraser Kit (Takara). For the analysis of gene expressions in the cDNA samples, Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out using the TB Green PCR Master Mix (Takara), following the manufacturer's recommended protocol. The amplification primers were designed using Primer 5 software, and their sequences can be found in Supplementary Table S2. As an internal reference, the housekeeping gene β-actin was selected. The qRT-PCR data was subsequently analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western Blot Analysis

Total proteins were extracted from chicken GCs using RIPA lysate (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following established protocols. The protein concentration was quantified using the BCA protein quantification kit (Beyotime). Subsequently, the protein samples were separated via SDS-page gel electrophoresis, and the separated proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Boston, MA) using the wet transfer method. The PVDF membrane, carrying the target protein, was incubated with specific primary antibodies for 12 h at 4°C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at 4°C. Finally, the target proteins were detected using the ECL Chemiluminescent Substrate Kit (Beyotime). Details about all the antibodies used in this study can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) Assay

Chicken GCs were cultivated in a 96-well cell culture plate and transfected with siRNAs for 36 h. Subsequently, the Cell-Light EdU Apollo 567 In Vitro Kit (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China) was employed to analyze EdU-stained proliferating GCs, while Hoechst 33342 was used to stain the cell nuclei. The relative proliferating GCs (calculated as EdU-positive GCs divided by Hoechst 33342-positive cell nuclei) were determined using Image Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD).

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Chicken GCs were cultured in a 6-well cell culture plate and transfected with siRNAs for 48 h. For cell cycle analysis, cells were then collected and subjected to PI/RNase Staining Buffer Solution (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). For Cell apoptosis analysis, the cell apoptosis rate was analyzed using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences). Cell fixation in 70% ethanol for 24 h was necessary before cell cycle analysis, while cell apoptosis analysis did not require fixation. The Flow cytometry analysis was conducted using a BD AccuriC6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Enzyme-linked-immunosorbent Serologic Assay (ELISA)

Chicken GCs were cultured in a 6-well cell culture plate and transfected with siRNAs for 48 h. Subsequently, the medium from the 6-well cell culture plates was collected and subjected to hormone concentration analysis using the Estradiol (E2), Progesterone (P4), Triiodothyronine (T3), and Tetraiodothyronine (T4) ELISA kits from KaMaiShu Biotech (Shanghai, China).

Statistical Analysis

All data have been expressed as Mean ± Standard Error of the Mean (SEM) from at least 3 independent repeats, and statistical significance between experimental and control groups was assessed using the independent-sample t test, following Duncan's method for multiple comparisons. Meanwhile, the statistical differences between multiple groups were analyzed using the one-way ANOVA. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v20 software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY), with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Transcriptome Analysis of Small Ovarian Follicles from Laying and Broody Chickens

RNA sequencing was employed to compare the transcriptome of small ovarian follicles from broody and laying chickens, denoted broody follicles (BF) and laying follicles (LF), respectively. The raw sequencing reads displayed base error rates below 0.1%, and both the Q20 and Q30 indices exceeded 90%, meeting the requisite standards for subsequent analysis (Supplementary Table S4). Following the removal of low-quality reads, the clean reads were aligned to the chicken genome (Gallus_gallus-5.0), with over 90% of reads successfully mapped. Among these, approximately 88% corresponded to unique matches, while the remaining 3% accounted for multiple matches (Supplementary Table S5). More than 70% of the total mapped reads aligned with exon regions (Supplementary Table S6). These findings confirmed the high quality of sequencing data, making it suitable for further analysis.

The sequence assembly provided comprehensive insights into transcript expression levels. The principal component analysis demonstrated a notable distinction between the sequenced samples of the BF and LF groups. Interestingly, the LF group exhibited more closely clustered samples, whereas the BF group displayed a more scattered distribution (Figure 1A). DEG Analysis of Laying and Broody Follicles

The DEG analysis identified a total of 200 DEGs when comparing the BF and LF groups (Supplementary Table S7). A volcano plot showcased that 131 genes exhibited significantly higher expression levels in the LF, whereas 69 genes displayed elevated expression levels in the BF (Figure 1B). These DEGs were further illustrated by a heat map, providing a visual representation of the expression patterns of DEGs across the 2 groups (Figure 1C). The GO analysis showed that these DEGs were predominantly enriched in functions related to cell activation, immune systems, and protein bindings (Figure 1D). Additionally, the KEGG analysis indicated that these DEGs were primarily associated with functions related to cell metabolism, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and the transforming growth factor β signaling pathway (Figure 1E).

Of particular interest, given the distinct atrophy phenotype observed in BF, were genes exhibiting higher fold changes. The top 10 up-regulated and top 10 down-regulated known DEGs were selected as candidates (Table 1). Among these genes, SLC5A5 was selected because it displayed more than 50-fold higher expression in small ovarian follicles of laying chickens compared with limited expression in that of broody chickens (Figure 2A). This observation was further substantiated by qRT-PCR analysis, which showed expression levels of SLC5A5 in LF and BF that were aligned with the sequencing data (Figure 2B). In addition, the expression of SLC5A5 gradually increased during small ovarian follicle development, and it reached highest at the beginning of entering grade follicles, then gradually decreased following grade follicles development (Figure 2C). SLC5A5 Knockdown Regulates the Proliferation of Chicken GCs

To investigate the role of SLC5A5 in the development and functions of chicken GCs, 3 siRNAs were employed to modulate SLC5A5 expression in chicken GCs. Following comprehensive qRT-PCR analysis, the most effective siRNA (siRNA-2) was selected for subsequent experiments (si-SLC5A5, Figure 2D). After the knockdown of SLC5A5 in GCs, there was a noticeable reduction in transcripts of cell proliferation-related genes, alongside a significant decrease in the expression levels of CDK2 and PCNA protein, as demonstrated by qRT-PCR and western blot analysis, respectively (Figures 3A–3C). The flow cytometric analysis revealed that SLC5A5 knockdown hindered the transition of GCs from the G0/G1 phase into the S and G2/M phases (Figures 3D and 3E). Furthermore, the EdU assay results indicated that SLC5A5 knockdown led to a reduction in the number of proliferating GCs (Figures 3F and 3G). SLC5A5 Knockdown Affects the Apoptosis of Chicken GCs

In addition to its role in cell proliferation, we investigated the impact of SLC5A5 on the apoptosis of chicken GCs. The qRT-PCR analysis revealed that SLC5A5 knockdown led to increased expression levels of transcripts for cell apoptosis-promoting CASPs and a decrease in transcripts for the antiapoptotic B-cell lymphoma-2 gene (BCL2) (Figure 4A). Western blot analysis corroborated the gene expression findings (Figures 4B and 4C). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis indicated that the knockdown of SLC5A5 upregulated the rate of apoptosis in GCs (Figures 4D and 4E). SLC5A5 Knockdown Modulates Steroid Hormone Synthesis in Chicken GCs

The influence of SLC5A5 on steroid hormone synthesis was examined in chicken GCs. Both qRT-PCR and western blot analyses demonstrated that the knockdown of SLC5A5 significantly reduced levels of RNAs and proteins, respectively, related to steroid hormone production (Figures 5A–5C). Moreover, ELISA analysis revealed a reduction in the production and secretion of estradiol and progesterone following SLC5A5 knockdown (Figures 5D and 5E). SLC5A5 Regulates the Development and Function of Chicken GCs in a Thyroxine-independent Manner

The SLC5A5 transporter protein is primarily responsible for iodide ion uptake by cells to facilitate thyroxine synthesis (Leung et al., 2010). However, current research predominantly supports thyroxine synthesis occurring within thyroid cells (Mullur et al., 2014), and additional evidence is required to ascertain whether it can be synthesized by other cell types. Given the intriguing role of SLC5A5 in chicken GCs, we initially investigated whether chicken GCs are capable of producing thyroxine, specifically T3 and T4 (Senese et al., 2019). Our data demonstrated that chicken GCs express genes associated with thyroxine synthesis (Supplementary Table S8). However, ELISA analysis results revealed almost no thyroxine was detected in the medium of cultured chicken GCs, compared with negative control including phosphate-buffered saline and unused complete culture medium (Supplementary Figure S1). These findings collectively suggest that chicken GCs are not proficient in synthesizing thyroxine and that SLC5A5 does not regulate cellular thyroxine production in chicken GCs, but potentially exerts its influence on follicle development and function through other unknown mechanisms.

DISCUSSION

The agriculture industry plays a vital role in providing essential resources for human life. In livestock production, animals are raised to yield meat, eggs, milk, and various other products (Henchion et al., 2021). However, it is important to note that nearly all farm animals have undergone domestication from primitive wild ancestors over an extensive period (Zhang and Colli, 2020). With the advancement of modern molecular biology techniques, the time-consuming methods used for phenotyping primitive animals are no longer sufficient to meet current demands. As a result, efficient molecular marker-assisted breeding technology has emerged as a new and valuable tool in the modern livestock industry (Beuzen et al., 2000; Dekkers and Hospital, 2002). Molecular marker-assisted breeding technology involves the selection of agricultural animals with specific target traits by directly analyzing the genotype of animals (Margawati, 2012). For instance, mutation loci in genes like the myostatin gene can be utilized to identify agricultural animals with enhanced meat production capabilities (Bellinge et al., 2005). Another example is the use of the ryanodine receptor 1 gene as a screening marker to identify stressed pigs with low-quality pork (Guàrdia et al., 2004). Genes that govern various traits in agricultural animals are pivotal components of molecular marker-assisted breeding technology, enabling the rapid enhancement of economic traits in these animals (Cui et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020). Consequently, the primary objective of this study was to identify key genes that regulate ovarian follicle development in chickens, and to further leverage these genes for the improvement of egg-laying productivity in underdeveloped regions through the application of molecular marker-assisted breeding techniques.

RNA sequencing represents a pivotal tool in the identification of functional genes and has extensive applications in molecular biology studies (Hrdlickova et al., 2017), including those elucidating the developmental and physiological processes of agricultural animals (Yu et al., 2018). Previous research used transcriptome analysis in chicken small yellow follicles and F6 grade follicles to identify 855 DEGs during chicken ovarian follicle development. A critical gene was found to be the very low-density lipoprotein receptor gene, which was stimulated by follicle-stimulating hormone in chicken GCs (Chen et al., 2020). Further studies have involved transcriptome sequencing of chicken follicles with varying egg-laying rates, uncovering 544 DEGs found to be enriched in numerous signaling pathways potentially associated with egg production traits in laying hens (Chen et al., 2021). While various studies have provided valuable insights into the molecular regulation underlying follicle development and egg production capacity in chickens, there remains room for improvement in the identification of key genes in chicken follicle development and function (Zhou et al., 2020; Nie and Zheng, 2022). In the current study, RNA sequencing was used to analyze potential genes involved in chicken ovarian follicle development. Importantly, this analysis identified SLC5A5 as a key gene, allowing investigation into its role in the development of chicken GCs. This research provides a more comprehensive understanding of the intricate processes and key genes involved in chicken follicle development.

SLC5A5 regulating thyroxine production and primarily functions as a transporter protein responsible for the uptake of extracellular iodide ions into thyroid cells to facilitate thyroxine synthesis (Solis et al., 2011). Reports have indicated the loss of function of SLC5A5 leads to dyshormonogenic congenital hypothyroidism due to an iodide transport defect (Martín et al., 2021). Notably, SLC5A5 deficiency can also impact the therapeutic efficacy of radioactive iodine in the treatment of thyroid cancer (Oh and Ahn, 2021). Another study revealed that SLC5A5 was induced by P53, resulting in increased SLC5A5 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, and rendering these cells resistant to apoptosis (Guerrieri et al., 2013). Furthermore, research has shown that SLC5A5 is expressed in tissues other than the thyroid gland, and performs functions beyond thyroxine synthesis (Nicola et al., 2009). SLC5A5 has been detected in the ovaries, possibly accounting for the higher iodine content observed in ovaries compared with most other tissues, except the thyroid gland (Perron et al., 2001; Slebodziński, 2005). Additionally, SLC5A5 expression has been identified in the ooplasm and GCs of ovarian early follicles in female zebrafish (Holloway et al., 2021). The expression of SLC5A5 is influenced by the estrous cycle, and is upregulated when estrogen levels peak before ovulation in the rat ovary (Riesco-Eizaguirre et al., 2014). These findings collectively suggest that SLC5A5 may play an important role in follicle development. The present study found that SLC5A5 expression was significantly suppressed in BF. Subsequent experimental analyses demonstrated that SLC5A5 deficiency resulted in impaired proliferative viability, reduced steroid hormone synthesis, and increased apoptosis in chicken GCs. Consequently, we hypothesize that SLC5A5 deficiency expression causes the destruction and dysfunction of the GC pool, and may contribute to the disruption of follicular development, shedding light on its potential role in this crucial biological process.

The primary biological role of iodide is to act as a vital component in thyroxine synthesis, and its functionality in animals is largely dependent on thyroxine (Bílek et al., 2020). Research has demonstrated that iodine can be absorbed extensively by the ovaries and uterus (Mathews et al., 2021). However, it is noteworthy that the ovaries and uterus are not responsible for thyroxine synthesis. The current findings have demonstrated that GCs within chicken ovarian follicles are incapable of synthesizing and secreting thyroxine. This finding raises the intriguing possibility that SLC5A5-mediated iodine uptake by the ovary may influence follicle development through alternative mechanisms. Additionally, SLC5A5 has been identified as a transporter for other anions, including thiocyanate and perchlorate (Van Sande et al., 2003). The present study determined that SLC5A5 does not affect GC function by modulating thyroxine production. We propose that SLC5A5 may exert its influence by regulating the uptake of these ions. However, we have not yet established methods to assess the ability of GCs to uptake these ions. Further investigation is required to ascertain whether SLC5A5 regulates ovarian follicle development through the cellular uptake of anions such as iodine, thiocyanate, and perchlorate.

The world has a diverse array of chicken breeds, yet only a limited subset of these breeds exhibit high yields of eggs, with the majority of chicken breeds falling into the categories of primitive or developing breeds. One of the most significant determinants of chicken egg production is broodiness, a phenomenon primarily controlled by genetic factors. These factors include genes encoding prolactin, dopamine receptor D2, and various other reproductive hormones (Liu et al., 2018). However, despite the importance of these factors, many other genes that govern ovarian follicle development and, by extension, egg production in chickens remain undiscovered. Therefore, the current study focused on comparing broody hens, characterized by arrested ovarian follicle development, with normal egg-laying hens. We employed RNA sequencing to identify genes that may play a role in the regulation ovarian of follicle development in chickens. Our investigation revealed that SLC5A5 has the potential to regulate chicken follicle development by influencing the survival and function of GCs. This discovery holds promise as a key candidate for utilization in molecular marker-assisted breeding techniques, with the goal of enhancing the egg production capacity of primitive or developing chicken breeds.
