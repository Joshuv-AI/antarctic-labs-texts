Simultaneous prefrontal-occipital high-definition transcranial direct-current stimulation modulates beta and gamma oscillations serving abstract reasoning
Authors: Tony W. Wilson, Madison A. Bravo, Peihan J. Huang, Kellen M. McDonald, Jason A. John, Grant M. Garrison, Kennedy A. Kress, Lauren K. Webert, Jake J. Son, Hannah J. Okelberry, Maggie P. Rempe, Yasra Arif, Nathan M. Petro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent studies have shown that prefrontal, parietal, and occipital cortices are critical for abstract reasoning and other fluid intelligence tasks, although the contribution of individual regions and possible laterality effects remain poorly understood. Herein, we use an innovative dual-montage, high-definition transcranial direct-current stimulation (HD-tDCS) approach to examine the impact of simultaneously stimulating prefrontal and occipital cortices on the neural oscillatory dynamics underlying abstract reasoning in 44 healthy adults. Across three sessions, participants underwent concurrent anodal stimulation of the midline occipital and either left or right dorsolateral prefrontal cortices (DLPFC), or sham to both sites. Participants then completed an abstract reasoning task during magnetoencephalography (MEG) and the resulting data were imaged in the time-frequency domain and examined voxel-wise using 1 × 3 repeated-measures ANOVAs. Significant stimulation-related effects included stronger beta oscillations (i.e., more negative relative to baseline) in the left DLPFC following right DLPFC-occipital HD-tDCS relative to both left DLPFC-occipital stimulation and sham. Stronger beta oscillations were also found in the right temporoparietal junction (TPJ) following either type of active stimulation relative to sham. Additionally, we found increased gamma activity in the left inferior parietal, left supplementary motor area, and left somatosensory cortices following both types of active stimulation relative to sham, and the strength of such gamma activity was significantly related to reaction time. In conclusion, we found that anodal stimulation of the DLPFC-occipital cortices strengthened beta and gamma oscillations in key regions for abstract reasoning, with some evidence for laterality effects on prefrontal stimulation and implications for behavior.

Introduction

Due to its critical role in moment-to-moment cognitive function, many studies have examined whether fluid intelligence (Gf) can be improved using a variety of behavioral and neuroimaging techniques. Gf is often measured using tests that require adaptation to new information, such as abstract reasoning and working memory tasks, which necessitate that the participant utilize the new information to complete the task (Arif et al., 2021; Cattell, 1963; Nisbett et al., 2012; Yuan et al., 2006). Previous studies using working memory training have demonstrated improvements in Gf, with one study even showing increased reasoning and processing speeds in children from low socioeconomic status after as little as eight weeks (Jaeggi et al., 2008; Mackey et al., 2011). However, several recent studies have challenged this view by showing no evidence of Gf improvement following working memory training, despite significant transfer effects on n-back tasks (Colom et al., 2013; Redick et al., 2013; Soveri et al., 2017; Thompson et al., 2013). Presumably, this reflects the well-known links among different working memory tasks, as well as construct divergence between working memory and Gf.

Another promising approach for modifying Gf and other brain functions is noninvasive brain stimulation, including methods such as transcranial direct-current stimulation (tDCS). tDCS is thought to alter the local ionic environment without inducing action potentials, thus changing the excitability of local neural populations (Liebetanz et al., 2002; Nitsche et al., 2003; Nitsche and Paulus, 2001). tDCS has been shown to improve working memory and phonemic fluency in those with clinical disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, but the key brain regions have remained unclear, as most of this work has used conventional tDCS which involves large sponge electrodes and diffuse current flow (Boggio et al., 2006; Lawrence et al., 2017; Pereira et al., 2013). Advances in transcranial stimulation have led to a closely related technique known as high-definition tDCS (HD-tDCS), which utilizes more focused arrays of metal electrodes to improve the spatial focality (Datta et al., 2009; Edwards et al., 2013; Son et al., 2025; Erker et al., 2025). HD-tDCS has been shown to improve symptoms of different disorders such as major depressive disorder (Zhang et al., 2024) and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD; Parlikar et al., 2019), with some suggesting that the improved focality of HD-tDCS may help avoid side effects and improve outcomes (Jog et al., 2023; Parlikar et al., 2019). Given this, it has been suggested that using stimulation techniques such as HD-tDCS or high-definition transcranial alternating-current stimulation (HD-tACS) might be a promising method to improve Gf (Arif et al., 2021; Liebetanz et al., 2002; Nitsche et al., 2003; Pahor and Jaušovec, 2014; Spooner and Wilson, 2023). Studies to date have targeted specific cortical regions that are strongly associated with Gf, but only a few have used HD-tDCS on healthy adults and directly examined how cortical activity may be modulated during abstract reasoning tasks (Arif et al., 2021; Erker et al., 2024). In terms of the brain regions which may be promising targets for tDCS, the well-known parieto-frontal integration theory of intelligence (P-FIT) suggests that the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), inferior and superior parietal cortices, and anterior cingulate are crucial regions, but also areas within the temporal and occipital cortices (Jung and Haier, 2007). Indeed, in a large peri-adolescent sample (N = 1601), Gur et al. (2020) found that activation and gray matter volume within classic P-FIT regions such as the DLPFC and parietal varied as a function of Gf, and that these same relationships with behavior held for areas within the inferior frontal gyrus and multiple occipital regions. Such involvement of extended occipital regions agrees with other imaging work (Hilger et al., 2017; Basten et al., 2015; Estrada et al., 2019) examining abstract reasoning and/or Gf more broadly. Other studies have suggested that differences in Gf may be explained by the efficiency of these extended P-FIT network regions rather than purely their activation, per se. This neural efficiency hypothesis (NEH; Dunst et al., 2014; Neubauer and Fink, 2009) suggests that frontal cortices coordinate network level brain activity and is partially based on observations that extraneous non-P-FIT deactivations are also indicative of improved Gf (Arif et al., 2021; Haier et al., 1988; Langer et al., 2012). Thus, the networks supporting Gf may involve more regions and more nuanced neural responses than envisioned in earlier work.

Beyond these nuances, the regions comprising the most promising targets for modulating Gf are relatively clear. For example, a previous cross-sectional study of abstract reasoning showed that neural oscillations in the occipital cortices became stronger with increasing age in typically developing youth, while such developmental effects were sex-specific in multiple DLPFC and parietal regions (Taylor et al., 2020). Later longitudinal work using the same task showed age-related changes in the strength of beta oscillations in the left prefrontal cortices, and that developmental changes in the strength of gamma and theta oscillations in the inferior parietal and occipital regions were associated with improvements in behavioral performance (i.e., faster responses; Taylor et al., 2022). Likewise, an aging study of abstract reasoning found robust theta, alpha/beta, and gamma oscillations, with prefrontal alpha/beta activity and parietal responses exhibiting the largest aging effects (Penhale et al., 2024). Previous work using HD-tDCS to target the prefrontal cortices in the context of abstract reasoning found task-related neural responses across occipital, parietal and prefrontal regions in the theta, alpha/beta, and gamma range, with stimulation of the right prefrontal cortices being associated with faster reaction times and weaker alpha/beta connectivity in the left fronto-parietal relative to sham (Arif et al., 2021). A later HD-tDCS study of abstract reasoning targeted the parietal cortices and found laterality effects in both the alpha/beta and gamma ranges, with the occipital, parietal, and prefrontal cortices again being critical (Erker et al., 2024). Such lateralized effects have also been seen in other reasoning and Gf studies, but their net impact is still debated (Figeys et al., 2023; Marinsek, 2018; Santarnecchi et al., 2021). In addition to these lateralization effects, polarity-specific responses have been demonstrated in the occipital cortices during a variety of tasks, with anodal stimulation being shown to selectively increase spontaneous alpha rhythms (Wilson et al., 2018; Heinrichs-Graham et al., 2017; Wiesman et al., 2018; McDermott et al., 2019) and the strength of local gamma oscillations (Wilson et al., 2018). Some of these studies also showed prefrontal effects distant from the stimulation site, including increased gamma oscillations in the left prefrontal cortex and frontal eye fields (Wilson et al., 2018) and reduced theta oscillations in bilateral prefrontal (McDermott et al., 2019) following anodal stimulation of midline occipital. Such distant effects likely reflect connectivity along the well-known pathways between occipital visual and prefrontal regions thought to be critical for visual attention (Giampiccolo et al., 2025; Harnett et al., 2025; Squire et al., 2013; Clark et al., 2015).

Given these findings, we performed simultaneous HD-tDCS of the prefrontal and primary occipital cortices in the current study. These two regions are considered integral to Gf, but are located at opposite ends of the network serving Gf. Both of these regions have been stimulated individually in many previous HD-tDCS studies (e.g., Arif et al., 2021, 2022), but only one study to date has simultaneously stimulated both regions and this work focused on working memory processing (Huang, Arif, Son, et al., 2025). To identify the effects of prefrontal-occipital HD-tDCS on the neural regions serving abstract reasoning, we collected high-density magnetoencephalography (MEG) data as participants performed a validated abstract reasoning task. This abstract reasoning task was adapted from Raven’s Progressive Matrices and has been linked to classic measures of Gf (Raven and Raven, 2003; Taylor et al., 2020, 2022). Given the laterality effects noted above, all participants completed three stimulation conditions, including two active conditions that varied by laterality of the prefrontal stimulation (i.e., left prefrontal-midline occipital and right prefrontal-midline occipital) and a sham condition. Based on previous studies, we hypothesized that right prefrontal stimulation would lead to compensatory beta oscillations in the left prefrontal cortices during performance of the abstract reasoning task, and that both active stimulation conditions would be associated with increased gamma oscillations in prefrontal and parietal cortices relative to sham.

Methods

Participants

Forty-five right-handed, healthy adults (24 females) who were 19–35 years-old (mean: 25.9 years) were enrolled from the local community for this study. Exclusionary criteria included history of head trauma, current substance use, neurological or psychiatric disorders, medical illnesses affecting CNS function (e.g. HIV/AIDS), and the standard exclusion criteria for MEG/MRI studies (e.g., ferromagnetic implants). The study protocol (BTNRH-20–26-XP) was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Boys Town National Research Hospital. All experimental procedures conformed to the standard set by the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki, except for registration in a database. All participants received a full description of the study prior to informed written consent, which adhered to the guidelines set forth by the Boys Town National Research Hospital IRB.

High-definition transcranial electrical stimulation

The high-density MxN-65 transcranial electrical stimulation system (Soterix Medical, New York, NY, USA) was used to deliver dual montage HD-tDCS. Each 4 × 1 montage was configured with a central anode surrounded by four cathodes. Participants completed three separate visits following a randomized crossover design, with an interval of at least five days between visits (mean = 11.5 days, SD = 7.7; Fig. 1) and all visits occurring at the same time of day (Wilson, Heinrichs-Graham, et al., 2014). During the two active stimulation visits, 20 min of 2.0 mA anodal HD-tDCS was administered to the midline occipital cortices simultaneously with 20 min of 2.0 mA anodal HD-tDCS of either the left or right DLPFC. Each 20-minute stimulation window began with a 30-s ramp-up period. This custom dual montage approach was designed to deliver relatively focal stimulation to both the primary visual cortices in the calcarine fissure and the DLPFC. During sham visits, no stimulation was delivered outside of the ramping period, and the placement of the DLPFC montage (left versus right) was counterbalanced and pseudorandomized across participants. Across all visits, participants performed a multi-source interference task (MSIT) during stimulation to ensure cognitive engagement (Arif et al., 2023; Arif, Son, et al., 2024; Son et al., 2024; Wiesman and Wilson, 2020; Wiesman et al., 2020). Following stimulation, the HD-tDCS arrays were removed and participants were moved to the MEG recording chamber. The interval between the end of HD-tDCS and the beginning of the MEG abstract reasoning task was approximately 44–49 min (Fig. 1), which aligns with the timeline considered appropriate to ensure offline tDCS effects (Kuo et al., 2013; Reinhart et al., 2017). Following each MEG session, participants completed a questionnaire about sensations (i.e., itching, pain, tingling, burning, warmth, pinching, metallic taste, and/or fatigue) they may have experienced during the stimulation. About half of participants reported experiencing one or more of these sensation side effects, with the overwhelming majority ranking the effect(s) as mild. Importantly, there were no statistical differences in the frequency or severity of such side effects by HD-tDCS condition, meaning they were reported equally often following sham. Participants and the research team involved in data processing and statistical analyses were blinded to the stimulation condition of each visit and the success of this blinding was supported by the questionnaire data.

Each electrode had a diameter of 12 mm, was comprised of Ag/AgCl, and was positioned using the international 10/20 system (Jasper, 1958; Klem et al., 1999). Based on published transformations of the scalp-based international 10/20 system onto cortical points in MNI space, the central anodes were placed on F3 (with the surrounding cathodes at FC3, F5, F1, and AF3), F4 (with the surrounding cathodes at FC4, F6, F2, and AF4), and OZ (with the surrounding cathodes at PO3, PO7, PO4, and PO8). These locations corresponded to the left DLPFC, right DLPFC, and midline occipital near the calcarine fissure, respectively (Okamoto et al., 2004; Okamoto and Dan, 2005). Note that these transformations have been used in many transcranial studies (Arif et al., 2020, 2021, 2022; Arif, Song, et al., 2024; Erker et al., 2025;Koshy et al., 2020; Son et al., 2025;Spooner et al., 2020; Wilson, Kurz, et al., 2014). To estimate the intensity and focality of these configurations, current density modelling was performed using the ROAST software implemented in MATLAB (Fig. 1), with tissue conductivities based on the literature (in S/m units, grey matter = 0.276, white matter = 0.126, CSF = 1.65, skull = 0.01, skin = 0.465, air = 1 × 10−7, gel = 0.3, electrodes = 5.8 × 107; Datta et al., 2009; Huang et al., 2013, 2017, 2019). This modeling utilized a standardized template brain and showed peak stimulation within the targeted DLPFC and occipital areas (Huang et al., 2019).

MEG experimental protocol

Participants sat in a magnetically shielded room with their heads positioned within the MEG helmet-shaped sensor array and completed a non-progressive abstract reasoning task (Fig. 1; Erker et al., 2024; Heinrichs-Graham et al., 2022; Penhale et al., 2024; Dietz et al., 2023; Wilson et al., 2026) adapted from the classic Raven’s Progressive Matrices (Raven and Raven, 2003). Participants were instructed to fixate on a crosshair, which appeared in the center of a 2 × 2 grid for a jittered period of 2750 ± 250 ms. Within the grid, either the bottom left or bottom right box was highlighted. An array of four complex figures then appeared for 4000 ms and participants responded as to whether the highlighted figure accurately completed the pattern displayed in the other non-highlighted boxes based on the pattern of shapes, colors, and/or orientations. Note that the patterns were allowed to vary along the three parameters to minimize learning and strategy adaptation effects. Participants were instructed to respond as fast as possible by pressing a button with their right index finger if they determined the highlighted figure correctly completed the pattern or with their right middle finger if it did not. The 120 total trials were split equally and pseudorandomized between correct and incorrect pattern completions, with the task lasting approximately 14 min. Before completing the full task, each participant completed a practice session per visit that consisted of about 15 trials to ensure that they understood the instructions. This practice session also helped minimize practice effects and/or strategy optimizations during the MEG recordings, as such practice and adaptation effects are most likely to occur in the first few trials.

MEG data acquisition

All recordings were conducted in a two-layer magnetically shielded VACOSHIELD room (Vacuumschmelze, Hanau, Germany). Neuro-magnetic responses were sampled continuously at 1 kHz, with an acquisition bandwidth of 0.1–330 Hz using a MEGIN Neo MEG system (Helsinki, Finland) with 306 sensors, including 204 planar gradiometers and 102 magnetometers. Participants were monitored via real-time audio-visual feeds from inside the shielded room. Each dataset was individually corrected for head motion and subjected to noise reduction using the signal space separation method with a temporal extension (Taulu and Simola, 2006).

Structural MRI processing and MEG coregistration

Before MEG recording, a 3D digitizer (Fastrak, Polhemus Navigator Sciences, Colchester, VT, USA) was used to localize five head position indicator (HPI) coils that were attached to the participant’s head relative to three fiducial points and their scalp surface. During the MEG recording, an electric current with a unique frequency label (e.g., 322 Hz) was delivered to each coil, which induced a measurable magnetic field enabling the coils to be localized relative to the MEG sensors. Since the coil locations were also known relative to the fiducials and the scalp surface, MEG measurements could be transformed into a common coordinate system and coregistered with the participant’s structural T1-weighted MRI. All MRI data were acquired with a Siemens Prisma 3T scanner using a 32-channel head coil and a 3D MP-RAGE sequence (TR = 2400 ms, TE = 2.05 ms; flip angle = 8°; FOV = 256 mm; slice thickness = 1 mm; voxel size = 1 mm3; 192 slices). The structural MRI volumes were aligned parallel to the anterior and posterior commissures and transformed into standardized space. Following source analysis (i.e., beamforming), each participant’s MEG functional images were also transformed into standardized space using the same transformation matrix that was previously applied to the structural MRI volume and spatially resampled.

MEG pre-processing, time-frequency transformation, and sensor-level statistics

The continuous MEG time series was segmented into epochs (duration: 6500 ms), which began 2500 ms prior to the onset of each stimulus and extended for 4000 ms after the stimulus. The baseline period was defined as −1800 to −800 ms prior to stimulus onset to minimize potential anticipatory effects, although no such effects were identified in the final analysis. Cardiac and ocular artifacts were removed from the data using signal space projection (SSP), which was accounted for during source reconstruction (Uusitalo and Ilmoniemi, 1997). Epochs containing remaining artifacts were removed based on a fixed threshold method supplemented with visual inspection. Participant-specific amplitude and gradient distributions across all trials were used to establish thresholds for rejecting individual trials with high amplitude and/or gradient values (see below). This was done to minimize the impact of individual differences in head size and sensor proximity, which strongly affect the signal amplitude in MEG. Across all conditions and participants, the average amplitude threshold was 1232.0 fT/cm (SD = 452.48) and the average gradient threshold was 406.21 fT/(cm*ms) (SD = 220.34) and these values did not differ by stimulation condition. Trials where participants responded inaccurately to the abstract reasoning task were also removed (i.e., only correct responses were used for final analysis). Following the removal of inaccurate and artifactual trials, an average of 87.18 (SD = 12.40) out of 120 trials per participant and stimulation condition remained and were used for further analysis. A 1 × 3 repeated measures ANOVA showed that the number of trials per participant did not significantly differ by stimulation condition (F(2,86) = 2.190, p = .118). MEG pre-processing and imaging used the BESA Research (Version 7.1) software following established pipelines (Wiesman and Wilson, 2020).

Artifact-free epochs were transformed into the time-frequency domain using complex demodulation (Kovach and Gander, 2016), with the spectral power estimations per sensor being averaged to generate time-frequency plots of mean spectral density. We used a frequency step of 1 Hz and time step of 50 ms for frequencies between 2 and 30 Hz to examine lower frequency activity, and a 2 Hz by 25 ms resolution above 30 Hz to estimate higher frequency activity. The sensor-level data were normalized using the baseline power, which was calculated as the mean power per respective frequency bin during the −1800 to −800 ms time-period. The time-frequency windows used for source construction were determined using a data-driven method based on the entire array of 204 gradiometers across all participants and stimulation conditions. Briefly, paired-samples t-tests against the baseline were conducted for each data point and the resulting spectrogram of t-values was thresholded at p < .05 to identify potentially significant oscillatory deviations across all participants and conditions. The time-frequency bins above the significance threshold were then clustered with temporally and/or spectrally neighboring bins that were also above the threshold, and cluster t-values were derived by summing the t-values of all data points within each cluster. To derive a distribution of cluster values, non-parametric permutation testing using 1000 permutations was utilized. The significance level of the observed clusters was then tested directly using this distribution (p < .05; Ernst, 2004; Maris and Oostenveld, 2007).

MEG beamformer imaging

The dynamic imaging of coherent sources (DICS) beamformer (Gross et al., 2001; Van Veen et al., 1997) was used to image cortical neural responses. This method employs spatial filters in the time-frequency domain to calculate the source power per voxel for the entire brain volume. The single images are derived from the cross-spectral densities of all combinations of MEG gradiometers calculated over the time-frequency window of interest and the solution of the forward problem for each location on a grid specified by the input voxel space. Following convention, we computed noise normalized source power in each voxel using active (i.e., task) and passive (i.e., baseline) periods of equal duration and bandwidth per oscillatory response at 4.0 × 4.0 × 4.0 mm resolution (Hillebrand et al., 2005). Such images are referred to as pseudo-t maps, with units (i.e., pseudo-t) that reflect noise-normalized power differences per voxel. All source imaging used the BESA version 7.1 software.

Whole-brain statistics and behavioral analysis

To identify stimulation-related changes in neural oscillatory power, the whole-brain maps per oscillatory response were submitted to voxel-wise 1 × 3 repeated measures ANOVAs, with stimulation condition as the factor. A cluster forming threshold of p < .001 and cluster-extent threshold of k > 4 (i.e., at least 250 mm3 of brain tissue), based on Gaussian random fields theory (Poline et al., 1995; Worsley et al., 1996), was used to account for multiple comparisons. Pairwise post-hoc t-tests were conducted on the peak voxels in the F-maps to determine the direction of the effect for each significant cluster. Values that were three standard deviations above or below their respective conditional means were considered outliers and removed from the analysis. To determine the effects of HD-tDCS on behavioral performance, separate 1 × 3 ANOVAs were run for accuracy and reaction time, with stimulation condition as the factor. The strength of neural oscillations within significant clusters was also correlated with behavioral performance (i.e., reaction time and accuracy) after averaging behavior and neural responses across all three stimulation conditions.

Results

All participants successfully completed the study, but one was excluded due to technical issues. The remaining 44 participants were 19–35 years old, with a mean age of 25.9 years. Separate 1 × 3 repeated measure ANOVAs were conducted for accuracy and reaction time, with stimulation condition as a within-subjects factor. These indicated that participants were numerically more accurate in the active stimulation conditions (left DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation: 92.43%; right DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation: 91.58%; sham: 91.12%), but this effect was only trending (F = 2.22, p = .11). The reaction time data were similar among the three conditions (F = 0.13, p = .88; left DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation: mean = 1555 ms, range = 965–2266 ms; right DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation: mean = 1559 ms, range = 987–2375 ms; sham: mean = 1544 ms, range = 968–2431 ms).

Sensor-level analysis

Statistical evaluation of the time-frequency spectrograms revealed five windows of interest (p < .05, corrected; Fig. 2). These windows included theta (θ; 3–6 Hz) from 50 to 400 ms, alpha (α; 8–14 Hz) from 300 to 1300 ms, beta (β; 14–20 Hz) from 200 to 1200 ms, early gamma (γ1; 72–98 Hz) from 125 to 325 ms, and late gamma (γ2; 66–82 Hz) from 425 to 925 ms following grid onset. Each of these windows were imaged separately per participant using a beamformer and a baseline interval of equal duration and bandwidth. The resulting images were grand averaged per response across participants and stimulation conditions to assess data quality and identify the brain regions exhibiting the strongest responses (Fig. 2), which unsurprisingly were centered in cell-dense regions of the early occipital visual cortices. Of note, the time window of the late gamma response ended about 40 ms before the earliest behavioral motor response (see the range of reaction times above). Many studies have suggested that the motor gamma response signal emerges about 50 ms before movement (e.g., Arif et al., 2026). Thus, in the fastest participants, it is possible that the final ~10 ms of the late gamma response included aspects of the motor signal. Since this constitutes only about 2% of the total window duration, was similar across stimulation conditions, and would only have been present in select trials of the fastest participants, we feel it is unlikely to have contributed significantly to our primary results.

Whole-brain analyses and neurobehavioral correlations

Whole-brain, voxel-wise 1 × 3 repeated measures ANOVAs on each of the five oscillatory response windows revealed significant clusters in the beta and late gamma windows (Fig. 3). Specifically, stimulation significantly affected beta oscillations in the right temporoparietal junction (TPJ; F = 10.94, p = .00005, corrected) and left DLPFC (F = 8.38, p = .0004, corrected), as well as gamma oscillations in the left inferior parietal cortices (IPC; F = 10.21, p = .0001, corrected), left supplementary motor area (SMA; F = 9.50, p = .0002, corrected), and left primary somatosensory cortices (S1; F = 9.44, p = .0002, corrected). To determine the directionality, we conducted pairwise t-tests on the oscillatory power at the peak voxel of each significant cluster. For the beta clusters, neural oscillations were stronger (i.e., more negative relative to baseline) during both left (t = 3.08, p = .004) and right (t = 4.05, p = .0002) DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation conditions relative to sham in the right TPJ, while beta oscillations in the left DLPFC were stronger during right DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation relative to both left DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation (t = 2.21, p = .03) and sham (t = 4.24, p = .0002). Regarding the gamma oscillations, these were stronger during both active stimulation conditions relative to sham in the left IPC (left DLPFC-occipital: t = 3.71, p = .0008; right DLPFC-occipital: t = 3.29, p = .003), left SMA (left DLPFC-occipital: t = 3.49, p = .002; right DLPFC-occipital: t = 3.49, p = .002), and left S1 (left DLPFC-occipital: t = 3.39, p = .002; right DLPFC-occipital: t = 3.61, p = .001; Fig. 3). No other comparisons were significant.

Finally, we conducted exploratory analyses to examine whether accuracy and/or reaction time were significantly correlated with neural oscillatory responses in each significant cluster. Note that the goal of these analyses was only to determine whether neural activity in these regions was related to task performance and not whether the relationship between performance and neural activity varied by stimulation condition. To this end, we found that reaction time and the strength of gamma oscillations were significantly correlated across all stimulation conditions in each region where significant stimulation effects were observed (Fig. 4; left IPC: r = −0.38, p = .032; left SMA: r = −0.53, p = .002; and left S1: r = −0.50, p = .005). No other oscillatory responses were significantly related to behavioral performance.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the offline effects of simultaneous left/right DLPFC-midline occipital HD-tDCS on the neural oscillatory activity serving abstract reasoning in healthy adults. We identified robust oscillatory activity during performance of the abstract reasoning task, with multispectral responses in the frontal, parietal, and occipital cortices, which is broadly consistent with prior work that used this same abstract reasoning task in healthy participants (Arif et al., 2021; Dietz et al., 2023; Erker et al., 2024; Penhale et al., 2024; Taylor et al., 2020). Regarding stimulation effects, our voxel-wise, whole-brain analyses indicated that active HD-tDCS modulated both beta and late gamma oscillations across a collection of brain regions supporting abstract reasoning, while having no effect on theta, alpha, or early gamma responses. Interestingly, all of our significant results indicated that the dual-montage, anodal stimulation increased the strength of oscillatory activity during task performance. Specifically, we found that beta oscillatory responses were significantly stronger (i.e., more negative relative to baseline) in the right TPJ following active stimulation with either montage relative to sham, while beta oscillations in the left DLPFC were stronger following right DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation relative to both sham and left DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation. Further, in the gamma range, we found that active stimulation in general (i.e., both montages) increased the strength of gamma activity in the left IPC, left SMA, and the left S1 cortices relative to sham. Such gamma responses were also negatively correlated with reaction time across all stimulation conditions, suggesting that the gamma increases during active stimulation conditions were associated with faster reaction times. Below, we discuss the implications of these findings for understanding the impact of left/right DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation on abstract reasoning performance, including the evidence for laterality effects.

Our most exciting findings were likely the altered gamma oscillations following both types of active stimulation, which across all conditions were correlated with behavioral performance. Specifically, we found stronger gamma oscillations in the left IPC, left SMA, and left S1 cortices following active left/right DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation relative to sham. First, the original P-FIT model suggests that both the SMA and left IPC are critical for fluid intelligence (Jung and Haier, 2007), and while left S1 cortices were not part of the original P-FIT model, the left postcentral gyrus has been proposed in the more recent extended P-FIT model (Gur et al., 2020). Thus, neural changes in these regions during abstract reasoning was not surprising. Regarding the direction of changes (i.e., increases), both active stimulation conditions included two (i.e., left or right prefrontal and midline occipital) anodal, center-surround arrays that were simultaneously activated, and past studies using anodal stimulation have broadly reported increases in local (i.e., near the simulating electrode) and/or distant gamma oscillations (Huang, Arif, Rempe, et al., 2025; Arif et al., 2022; Ikeda et al., 2019; Wilson et al., 2018; Wiesman et al., 2018). Such findings extend across both conventional (i.e., sponge electrodes) and HD-tDCS arrays, with increases in gamma being particularly common following anodal occipital tDCS (Wilson et al., 2018; Wiesman et al., 2018; Arif et al., 2022). However, unlike these previous studies which reported gamma changes in both local occipital cortices and distant brain regions, all of our significant findings were in areas relatively distant from the two arrays. While we can only speculate on the origin(s) of this disparity, several major differences in the stimulation protocol could have contributed. First, the current study was the first to our knowledge to examine gamma oscillations using a dual montage approach and the second montage may have caused the effects to be smaller and/or more distributed, which could have attenuated the occipital changes. In other words, the additional stimulation site may have changed the overall modulatory pattern impending on the brain network serving abstract reasoning. Second, we used a HD-tDCS array at both stimulation sites, while most of the work showing local occipital gamma increases with anodal stimulation used conventional sponge electrodes over the occipital cortex (e.g., Wilson et al., 2018; Wiesman et al., 2018). Finally, the total amount of current applied to the brain was 4 mA in the current study, which is twice that used in the previous works cited above. While we would be surprised if this attenuated gamma increases in the occipital cortex (since the local current would have been approximately the same; i.e., 2 mA), it is certainly possible.

As per the nature of these effects, we propose that these increases in gamma oscillatory activity reflect mild enhancements of the system. This interpretation is based on the fact that gamma oscillations were stronger in all three regions (i.e., left IPC, SMA, and S1) during the active stimulation conditions relative to sham, and the observation that stronger gamma activity across all three stimulation conditions was associated with faster reaction times during performance of the abstract reasoning task. Further, performance in terms of accuracy was numerically better during the active stimulation conditions relative to sham, although the overall ANOVA for accuracy was only trending. Regarding the mechanisms, magnetic resonance spectroscopy studies have suggested that anodal stimulation decreases local GABA-ergic activity (Stagg et al., 2009a, 2011, 2014; Kim et al., 2014; Bachtiar et al., 2015) and numerous cellular electrophysiology studies have shown that cortical gamma oscillations are critically dependent upon local GABA-gated interneuronal networks (Bartos et al., 2007; Uhlhaas et al., 2009, 2012; Buzsaki and Wang 2012; Vinck et al., 2013; Fries 2009, 2015; Salkoff et al., 2015). Thus, based on this framework, the stronger gamma oscillations likely reflect decreased local GABA activity. However, given our findings are distant from the sites of stimulation, such gamma alterations may primarily reflect indirect or network-level changes during abstract reasoning performance. Future work integrating multivoxel MRS and dual-montage tDCS could help distinguish between these possible interpretations.

Beyond gamma, we also found that HD-tDCS modulated beta oscillations in two brain regions known to be critical for abstract reasoning and recognized in the P-FIT model. Specifically, stronger beta oscillations (i.e., more negative relative to baseline) were detected in the right TPJ following both active stimulation conditions relative to sham, while beta oscillations in the left DLPFC were stronger following right DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation relative both sham and left DLPFC-midline occipital stimulation. Thus, unlike gamma, we observed stimulation laterality effects in the beta range, with the montage involving the right DLPFC more strongly modulating left DLPFC activity than the montage that included the left DLPFC and sham. Interestingly, a previous study using the same abstract reasoning task and a single stimulation array showed superior behavioral performance and reduced connectivity between the left prefrontal and parietal cortices following right DLPFC stimulation relative to sham (Arif et al., 2021). However, it should be noted that left DLPFC stimulation in that study was associated with a similar reduction in connectivity between left prefrontal and inferior parietal relative to sham, but no behavioral reaction time effect (Arif et al., 2021). Likewise, a more recent study using the same abstract reasoning task and a single array parietal montage also found attenuated connectivity between left prefrontal and left parietal cortices following HD-tDCS of either left or right parietal cortices relative to sham (Erker et al., 2024). Thus, the strength of oscillatory beta activity and connectivity involving the left DLPFC during abstract reasoning appears to be sensitive to HD-tDCS at multiple P-FIT nodes, with such stimulation also affecting task performance (i.e., reaction time) depending on the specific target of stimulation. Thus, our findings are broadly consistent with these past works and suggest that the additional midline occipital stimulation may sharpen the laterality effect whereby only right DLPFC stimulation leads to stronger beta oscillations. As noted above, we also observed stronger beta oscillations in the right TPJ during both active stimulation conditions relative to sham. The right TPJ area was implicated in the original P-FIT model and significant oscillatory responses in this region have been observed in multiple studies using the current abstract reasoning task (Schantell et al., 2024; Penhale et al., 2024; Taylor et al., 2020), although occasionally these effects have been left lateralized, at least in pediatric studies (Ward et al., 2024). Thus, the current findings confirm this region’s involvement in abstract reasoning and extend the field’s understanding by showing that HD-tDCS at other sites leads to stronger beta oscillations within this region during task performance.

Before closing, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. First, we did not collect MEG responses during abstract reasoning prior to each stimulation session, and thus there could have been baseline differences across the separate visits due to learning, strategy adaptation, and/or random differences in brain responses due to mood, time of day, sleep variation, and/or other variables. Our rationale for not collecting such data was that multiple studies have shown MEG maps to be stable in the same participant over many months (Lew et al., 2021; McCusker et al., 2021), with some variation based on the time of day of the recording (Wilson, Heinrichs-Graham, et al., 2014). Thus, we controlled for time-of-day effects by ensuring that each participant’s appointments occurred at the same time of day. While some day-to-day variability would still exist (e.g., mood variation, poor sleep the night before), we felt this was unlikely to systematically vary with tDCS condition across our sample given our overall study design. In other words, the order of visits in our randomized crossover design was balanced across the sample so that each of the three stimulation conditions occurred an equal number of times as visit one, two, or three across the full sample. Our rationale for adopting this approach was based on the study goals, which were to determine the impact of the different stimulation conditions on behavioral and neural measures during abstract reasoning. Thus, by randomizing the order, we were able to largely ensure that any practice or repetition effects did not systematically differ by stimulation condition, as any such systematic effect could have biased the results. While we think this design feature and our pre-task practice session before each MEG session (see methods) largely ensured that such practice effects did not differ by stimulation condition, we do want to acknowledge that abstract reasoning tasks are known to be susceptible to both learning and strategy adaptation effects. Given this, quantifying these effects irrespective of stimulation condition would still be highly valuable, and future work should consider either administering the task both before and after stimulation at each study visit or having a second group of participants who complete the abstract reasoning task three times over the same time period with no stimulation. Second, while our dual-montage design was novel and enabled us to simultaneously stimulate different P-FIT regions, including additional conditions in the same experiment that involved one or more single region montages would have enabled stronger conclusions. While increasing the number of HD-tDCS montage conditions prolongs the setup and leads to greater participant fatigue, the benefits may outweigh the costs in some experimental designs. Third, our primary analyses focused on whether the stimulation conditions differentially modulated oscillatory power. Several recent MEG studies have shown that tDCS can affect connectivity estimates (e.g., Arif et al., 2026) and future work should consider computing connectivity to ascertain whether the effect of stimulation varies by pathway. Methods that enable causal conclusions such as effective connectivity and/or Granger causality could be particularly informative in this context. Fourth, our functional mapping approach relied exclusively on MEG, which is a strong technique for studying neural oscillations, but does have the weakness of decreased sensitivity to deep sources (Hillebrand and Barnes, 2002). This is a particularly important consideration in the current context as some P-FIT regions are more distant from the MEG sensors and sensitivity to these areas could be reduced. Of note, several recent MEG studies have reported robust responses in hippocampal regions and other deep areas (Hall et al., 2026; Petro et al., 2025; Rempe et al., 2025; Meehan et al., 2021; Wilson et al., 2017), which may suggest that the limiting factor is neural response strength and thus such P-FIT regions may be within the sensitivity range of modern MEG systems, although additional studies are needed. Finally, we did not observe significant behavioral differences across the stimulation conditions, which is a common finding in tDCS studies involving healthy participants. This may have been attributable to task parameters, non-optimal montage configuration, insufficient stimulation intensity, or more simply that healthy young adults can often compensate for temporary changes in arousal and other factors that affect performance. Thus, future work may benefit from using more strenuous cognitive tasks in the context of tDCS experiments, or from using a more personalized approach that leverages functional mapping to identify the specific brain regions for tDCS targeting in each person (e.g., Radecke et al., 2025). Integrating high-resolution structural data and/or a priori knowledge on the dominant rhythms within the underlying cortical areas may also improve rigor and effect sizes (Picci et al., 2026; Rempe et al., 2022, 2023). Of note, we did find neurobehavioral correlations between reaction time during task performance and the strength of gamma oscillations across all conditions in brain regions modulated by the active stimulation (i.e., left IPC, left SMA, and left S1). Further, there was a trending difference in accuracy with the active stimulation conditions having better performance. Nonetheless, despite these limitations, our results show robust neurophysiological effects in the context of dual-montage stimulation and contribute significantly to our understanding of how tDCS affects the neural dynamics underlying abstract reasoning.

In summary, we leveraged a novel dual-montage DLPFC-midline occipital HD-tDCS design to examine the impact of stimulation on the neural dynamics underlying abstract reasoning. Our key findings were that the strength of beta and gamma oscillations increased across brain areas known to be critical for abstract reasoning following stimulation with the dual anodal montages. Finally, the laterality of DLPFC stimulation in our dual montage framework only affected beta oscillations in the left DLPFC (i.e., stronger responses following right DLPFC-occipital stimulation), with the other significant areas showing relatively equal changes following either configuration of active stimulation. These findings advance the field’s understanding of how dual-montage HD-tDCS affects the neural dynamics serving abstract reasoning and continues the progress toward the development of clinical HD-tDCS applications.
