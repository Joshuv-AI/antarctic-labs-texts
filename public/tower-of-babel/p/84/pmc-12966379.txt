Analysis of different methods to calculate tertiary regulation reserves for renewable energy in Japan
Authors: Joao G S Fonseca, Tatsuma Hori, Kazuhiko Ogimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegulation reserves designed to compensate for renewable energy power generation unexpected shortfalls are being implemented around the world. In Japan, since 2021, the balancing market has a specific type of tertiary power regulation reserve to mitigate the effects of day-ahead forecasting errors of renewable energy systems. However, the cost of these reserves is quite high, and it is not clear how effective they are. In this study, we provide one of the first comprehensive analysis of the efficacy of a regulation reserve designed to compensate for renewable power generation shortfalls, that is currently in use in national scale. To do that, we used three years of real data provided by one of the largest TSOs in Japan. Moreover, through the study we identified issues with the current reserve calculation methodology, and proposed two modifications to it, to mitigate these issues. The use of the modifications resulted in more effective reserves. For example, for 2021, the proposed modifications yielded reserves that covered shortfalls of renewable based power generation 78.7% of the time while reaching annually 2.3 TWh. This was approximately 7% higher coverage than the one achieved by the method currently in use, while requiring almost 30% less reserves than what it prescribed. Furthermore, the proposed modifications resulted in reductions in the largest values of hourly deficits of reserves in some cases. As such, they constitute good options to properly estimate the RR-FIT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-37441-x.Keywords: Balancing market, Regulation reserves, Renewable energy, Reserve calculation methods, Power generation uncertaintySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Variable renewable energy systems (photovoltaic, PV, and wind power-based systems), VRES, are becoming essential parts of many power grids around the world. As their contribution to the total power generation increases, finding ways to manage unexpected variations in the power output due to weather changes becomes crucial. In Japan, since 2021, a new regulation reserve in the balancing market was created to allow power utilities to compensate for shortfalls in the expected power generation from VRES. Particularly, this reserve address overestimation of VRES power generation in day-ahead forecasts. The reserve is called the replacement reserve for the feed-in tariff based VRES, RR-FIT, and transmission system operators, TSOs, must procure it in the market daily. The introduction of the RR-FIT is expected to bring transparency regarding the cost of sudden shortfalls of VRES based generation. Moreover, it should provide an incentive to improve VRES power generation forecasts.

Currently, to calculate the RR-FIT in Japan, the error of hour-ahead forecasts are excluded from the error of day-ahead forecasts using a methodology proposed by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators, Japan, OCCTO. Despite the objectives of the RR-FIT, information from the Japanese balancing market published since 2022, show that it not only has a high cost, but it also does not provide the expected coverage of VRES forecasting errors. As a result, the RR-FIT currently is not achieving completely its objective of mitigating shortfalls of expected VRES power generation. Although this might be seen as an issue exclusive to Japan, it is not, as many countries that are increasing their reliance on renewables, also want to manage renewables’ weather related variability.

The topic of estimating uncertainty of forecasts of VRES power generation is not new. Important studies were done in this area with probabilistic and empirical models to estimate uncertainty of PV and wind power generation forecasts in different temporal and spatial scales, and different locations around the world. For example, a variety of studies can be found in review such as in Prema et al.1. or Singla et al.2. We also investigated prediction intervals for day-ahead forecasts of a fleet of PV systems in Japan, as far back as 20183. More recently, attention has been given to find prediction intervals for forecasts done with deep learning models such as the LSTM model4,5. Regarding wind power forecasting, its uncertainty has been investigated for longer than for PV. For example, Lange M. using data of onshore wind farms in Denmark showed that the statistical distribution of wind speed prediction error follows a different shape than the one for the power prediction error6. Reviews of a variety of studies regarding the uncertainty of wind power forecasts can also be found7,8.

Uncertainty studies regarding forecasts of PV and wind power forecast are important, but they approach the problem from the side of the forecasting models. On the other side there are power system operators, who need to understand and apply uncertainty information about VRES forecasts to estimate the necessary reserves to prevent imbalances between supply and demand of power. Studies from the power system side often involve simulation of VRES penetration scenarios, and analyses of the impact of VRES growth on operating reserves. For example, Ela and Milligan, presented a thorough review of studies trying to evaluate the impact of the growth of renewable on the operating reserves of power systems in the U.S. and Europe9.

Analysis of impacts of VRES power generation on different types of operational reserves, according to different potential scenarios have been done in studies such those by Krad et al.10, or by Klansupar and Chaitusaney11. A review of common approaches recommended in literature to plan and operate reserves for VRES, according to probability indices in different scenarios, was presented by Garcia et al.12. Beneficial policies to integrate VRES variability in operating reserves planning have also been investigated13. More recently, Wu et al. investigated the use of hydropower as energy storage to compensate for wind and PV power systems power generation shortage and surplus in China14. They proposed a Gaussian mixture model, to describe the probability of power generation deviation, and use it as a restriction to regulate the operation of hydropower stations connected to the same grid. Xiang et al. proposed a method to determine regulation reserves for renewables and their corresponding cost allocation15. Their method uses ensemble learning to model different types of errors involved in power and supply deviations and applies chance-constrained optimization to compensate for them. Focusing on ramping reserves estimation now, Li et al. focused on solar forecasts, trying to estimate necessary ramping reserves in the U.S. by using a k-nearest-neighbor based method and weather data16. Haugen et al. reviewed the different types of uncertainty of renewables, as well as available power market tools that can incorporate such uncertainty, in the context of power market models for operational planning17. Their study focused mostly on the modeling problem, and identified a lack of power market models that can represent different aspects of uncertainty for large power systems in a short time.

When the focus is on currently used VRES dedicated reserves’ performance, or analyses using real data of power systems currently under operation, the number of studies is scarce. For example, using historical data of Italy, Guizzi et al., developed an evaluation method of reserve requirements that takes into consideration VRES variability for the Italian grid18. Pierro et al., proposed a method to calculate prediction intervals for net load forecasts, and tested their method using real data of a system operator that controls an area of 80 km219. In the U.S. Andrade et al., presented an analysis of the required reserves of ERCOT to deal with short variability of VRES generation. The authors showed that it is possible to introduce more VRES without a significant increase of operational reserves, by revising inter-dispatch time periods and by using sub-hourly generation scheduling20.

In this study we execute a comprehensive analysis of the current method used in Japan to procure reserves for unrealized VRES power generation. We discuss issues with the current methodology and evaluate the performance of the reserves calculated with it. In addition, we propose and evaluate modifications to the current methodology, to improve the estimation of such reserves as well as their effectiveness. The study is carried out using three years of real data used in the operation of one of the largest power systems in Japan. These data are usually not available, and their use provides a clear picture regarding ongoing issues on the use of reserves to support large-scale integration of VRES in power systems. The main contributions of the study are as follows. Firstly, by using real data, the study provides a realistic analysis of an in-use method for estimating regulation reserves for VRES. Thus, the study contributes to mitigating the problem of limited amount of information regarding the performance of reserves for VRES, used by under-operation power systems. Besides the analysis, we also identify issues with the current method, propose modifications on it, and verify their validity in quantitative manner. Additionally, we also show that issues with the reserve estimation method in Japan are related with market design. The effects of such issues, although significant in cost, and efficiency, sometimes are hidden by the current market design. Thus, by sharing issues Japanese TSOs are facing regarding the estimation and effectiveness of regulation reserves for renewables, the study provides a valuable reference for stakeholders and policymakers in markets around the world transitioning to power grids with high penetration of VRES.

In section “RR-FIT calculation methods”, we provide a description of the current method used to calculate the RR-FIT and discuss some issues it presents. Additionally, we present two modifications aimed at improving this method. In section “Area and data”, details about the data used and the balancing area it belongs are presented. The results of the study are presented and discussed in section “Results”. The main conclusions of the study are presented in section “Conclusion”.

RR-FIT calculation methods

In Japan, TSOs need to schedule the operation of power generators one day ahead of the target day. To do that, besides power demand forecasts for the target day, operators also need to know the expected power generation from VRES. As the dissemination of these systems increases, even not so large overestimations for these systems’ power generation can have a significant impact on power system operation. For example, unrealized renewable power generation might cause strong imbalances between power supply and demand, causing frequency issues (or ultimately even black-outs), or force TSOs to purchase large amounts of power in short-notice, at high costs.

To prevent such situations, the RR-FIT was created. In Table 1, we present its characteristics compared with all the regulation reserves that the TSOs in Japan must have (the source of the data in Table 1, was initially presented in21).

As Table 1 shows, the RR-FIT is a tertiary regulation reserve. Each TSO in Japan must allocate it, when scheduling the operation of its power system. Its role is to compensate for any unexpected unrealized VRES power generation. Thus, it is a reserve meant to supply potential deficits of the expected power generation by VRES. This reserve must reflect the current state of the day-ahead forecasting accuracy of VRES in Japan. It should be large enough to cover any forecasting error overestimating power generation, as well as to be as small as possible to avoid a costly power system operation. The RR-FIT creation also aimed to provide an incentive to improve forecasting techniques, as well as transparency regarding the effects of large-scale introduction of weather dependent renewable energy. Its proper estimation is, then, important to achieve both objectives.

Regarding the sources for the procurement of reserves in Table 1, they are diverse, including conventional power generators, battery storage and demand response. For a resource to be allowed to participate in the RR-FIT market, it is checked if it satisfies a series of requirements regarding RR-FIT. These checks are done before and after a bidder participation. Therefore, the type of source of procured reserves has no impact on the power system stability.

In this section, we present the current method to calculate the RR-FIT, and two types of modifications to improve it. To evaluate the effectiveness of the modifications, we will apply the current method first and use its results as reference to which to compare the results achieved with the proposed modifications.

Current method

The current method to calculate the RR-FIT is recommended by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators, Japan, OCCTO. For each day-ahead forecast value of VRES (photovoltaic and wind power), an associated value for the RR-FIT is estimated. The estimation is made daily, one day ahead of the target day, based on the day-ahead forecasts of VRES power generation for such day. The day-ahead forecasts have a 30-minute temporal resolution and are made for the balancing area of each TSO in Japan. Likewise, the RR-FIT has the same temporal and spatial resolutions as the forecasts.

To estimate the RR-FIT for a given forecast value, a monthly table is calculated before each target month. The table is built based on the two previous years of day-ahead forecasting errors. Specifically, from this past data, data of the same month as the target one, of the month preceding it, and of the month succeeding it are used.

Once the data for the target month are selected, the corresponding forecasting errors are divided into three-hour blocks and on renewable based power generation blocks, (these blocks are in tenths of the maximum value expected for each three-hour block and are called power output blocks). After this division process, the hour-ahead forecasting errors are excluded from the day-ahead forecasting errors, per hour-power generation blocks using Eq. (1) (based on documents published by OCCTO18).

In Eq. (1), RR-FIThB, pB is the RR-FIT value for hour block hB and for power output block pB. The variable Qe(dF)hB, pB represents the quantile of the positive (overestimations) day-ahead forecasting errors, and Qe(hF)hB, pB, is the corresponding value for the positive hour-ahead forecasting errors of VRES power generation. Once the RR-FIT calculation is done for each combination of hB and pB blocks, the monthly table is built in fashion presented in Table 2.

In principle, once a monthly table is complete, all that is necessary to use it is to have a day-ahead forecast value, and its respective time. With these two values, the corresponding hour block and power output block are identified and the corresponding RR-FIT value for this day-ahead forecast is identified. In practice though, there are some problems with calculating the RR-FIT with this method.

Firstly, many blocks might end up with values equal to 0. This might happen, for example, when Qe(hF)hB, pB is equal to or greater than Qe(dF)hB, pB in Eq. (1). As negative reserve values do not have meaning in the context of the RR-FIT, they are zeroed. Another case in which 0 might occur is when there are no data available at a given combination of hour-power blocks. This happens because, as VRES power generation is not uniformly distributed from 0 to 100% throughout the day, there is no uniform distribution of data points within power output blocks. Additionally, the division of hour blocks and power output blocks is somewhat artificial, sometimes yielding strong variation between adjacent blocks. For example, a common situation is represented in Table 1, hour block 12 h to 15 h, power output blocks 60 to 70, 70 to 80, and 80 to 90. Such strong variations are mostly caused by a lack of enough data in a given block. All RR-FIT values arising from these cases (including zeros and negative values) are regarded as anomalous values. Because of their frequency, OCCTO recommends a series of adjustments on the table to compensate for them22.

Even with these modifications, annual data published by power utilities in Japan, have shown that the use of RR-FIT tables in its current form, yields large RR-FIT values that provide forecast error coverage lower than expected. For example, in Fig. 1, we present data published by Chubu Electric Power Grid, regarding the balancing area of Chubu in Japan, in 202223.

Observed coverage of the day-ahead forecasting error of PV and Wind power generation in Chubu region in 2022 19 (translated to English with permission from Chubu Electric Power Grid).

In this figure, one can see that, in the Chubu region, although reserves are calculated using 0.99865 quantiles of day-ahead forecasting errors, annually, the reserves covered the difference between the day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors (meaning that they were greater than or equal this quantity) only 70.2% of the time in 2022. Furthermore, reserve shortfalls of more than 2 GW were observed in the same year, indicating that the reserves for some specific hours were significantly smaller than the part of the forecasting errors that they were supposed to compensate.

It is important to notice that this is not an isolated case for the Chubu region, or for the year 2022. All other TSOs in Japan reported similar coverage values, and occurrence of discrepancy between the RR-FIT and the forecasting errors they were supposed to compensate.

The calculation method of RR-FIT based on Eq. (1) was decided based on discussions with stakeholders. Its present form is due to the fact that intra-hour forecasting errors are covered with different types of reserves. These reserves are calculated using the 0.99865 quantiles of the hour-ahead forecasting errors. As they are also available to compensate for VRES power output deficits, they are added to the RR-FIT to become the total reserve available for deficits of VRES power output at any given hour. Because of that, it was decided to exclude the hour-ahead forecasting errors from the RR-FIT calculation, via Eq. (1).

To illustrate the reasoning behind this choice and the problems related to it, Fig. 2 presents characteristics of the distributions of one year of observed forecast errors and their difference for the Chubu region. Considering only overestimations of the observed VRES power generation, Fig. 2(I) shows that, yearly, the largest hour-ahead forecasts’ errors cover most of the day-ahead forecasts’ errors. In Fig. 2(II), the 0.99865 quantiles of each distribution are plotted and the corresponding value for the hour-ahead error is greater than 97.9% of the day-ahead forecasts’ errors for that year. The dashed purple line shows the difference between both forecasts (calculated for the same target day and time). In principle, this is the quantity that should be used to cover only errors that happen before the hour-ahead horizon. As the example shows, the yearly quantile for this distribution is even higher than the one of the hour-ahead distribution (it is greater than 99.25% of the day-ahead forecasts’ errors).

II: Boxplots and 0.99865 quantiles of the positive part of the curves in I (vertical dashed lines indicate the 0.99865 quantiles of the positive values of each distribution in I).

One should note that these are annual values, which are not used (as a single reserve value is not used for all hours of the year). However, the same trend happens when one verifies the distribution of these quantities per hour of the year (not shown here). Due to these characteristics, stakeholders concluded that if the RR-FIT were calculated as the 0.99865 quantile of the difference between the day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors, under the current market rules, the total reserves would be considerably greater than the one actually needed (as the value of C would have to be added to the reserves already covering B, in Fig. 2(I) and (II)).

The solution found was to remove the quantile of the intra-hour forecast errors from the RR-FIT using Eq. (1). In other words, the RR-FIT became something equivalent to subtract from the 0.99865 quantile of A, the 0.99865 quantile of B in Fig. 2(I) and (II).

To account for the seasonal characteristics, the data are divided into the following categories: month, blocks of 3 h, and % of maximum day-ahead forecast value for month and hour. Then, conditioned to all these categories together, past day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors are retrieved, and their quantiles calculated. By conditioning past hour-ahead forecasting errors to, month, hours, and day-ahead forecast power output, it is not known if the characteristics presented in Fig. 2 will persist. Moreover, there is no guarantee that the resulting RR-FIT is adequate to cover the difference between the day-ahead and the hour-ahead forecasting error, as, now, its value is not directly based on this difference.

In other words, in general, the difference of quantiles of day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors expressed in Eq. (1) is not equivalent to quantiles of the difference of the errors of both types of the forecasts. The differences between the day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasts arise for each target hour, conditioned to the weather conditions of this hour. By calculating day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors’ quantiles independently, this characteristic of the problem is lost. This loss should affect the performance of the reserves.

RR-FIT calculation via the distribution of the difference between past day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors (modification I)

Due to the problems described in section “Current method”, transparency is partially lost regarding the value of the RR-FIT. With the current methodology, it is not clear how much of the difference between the day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors the RR-FIT should cover. This loss of transparency ends up affecting also discussions and evaluations about the cost and the effectiveness of the RR-FIT.

To verify the suitability of the current methodology for the RR-FIT calculation, we propose first to calculate the RR-FIT using the quantile of the distribution of the forecasts’ differences as shown in Eq. (2) instead of Eq. (1). This modification is heretofore referred to as modification I. In Eq. (2), Qe(dF − hF), hB, pB (0.99865) is the 0.99865 quantile of the differences between both types of forecasts.

As the quantity the RR-FIT is meant to cover comes from the distribution of the differences between the day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors, we believe the use of the quantiles over this distribution can provide insights into the performance of the current method. Moreover, it can serve as reference to which it can be compared to. By using it we can directly verify in which conditions that the reasoning used in the first place for using Eq. (1) holds, and to quantify the trade-offs involved.

RR-FIT calculation via quantiles’ interpolation (modification II)

Another problem with the current method is that the use of hour-power output blocks generates discrete quantiles for periods of 3 h and ranges of renewable power output that can extend to several megawatts (since the power output blocks are divided into tenths of the maximum power output for each three-hour block). For each three-hour block, this results in a piecewise function outputting the same RR-FIT values for completely different power output values, just because they happen to be in the same power output block.

To mitigate this issue, we investigated the conversion of discrete quantiles to continuous ones. We will do that by fitting the discrete quantiles using a smoothing spline model over the whole power output range for each three-hour block. This is called modification II.

The description of the smoothing spline theory can be found in James et al.24. Here only a brief description of the method is provided. A smoothing spline fits a smooth curve to a set of points, by using a constrained set of piece-wise cubic polynomial g(x). This piece-wise function has knots at each unique value of the points to which it is fitted to. Moreover, it also has continuous first and second derivatives at each knot. The fitting procedure consists in finding a g(x) that minimizes the cost function in Eq. (3).

In Eq. (3) (xi, yi) are the points to which the function g(x) will be fitted too. The parameter λ controls the smoothness of the function. The equation provides a trade-off between fitting all the points as well as possible (the first term of Eq. 3) and having as little variance (or high smoothness) as possible (the integration term in the same equation). To perform the optimization, λ must be set, which can be done via a cross-validation procedure, or indirectly by setting the number of degrees of freedom of the model. In this study the smoothing spline model library implemented in R language in the package stats was used. This implementation was developed by Ripley and Maechler25. Different values of degrees of freedom were tested with spare data, and it was set to 3 as it provided smooth curves that followed well the distribution of hour-block and power output blocks quantiles for a variety of different samples. Figure 3 shows an example of the application of smooth splines to provide continuous quantiles in the problem of the RR-FIT calculation.

The blue line is the fitted smoothing spline. Circles are the 0.99865 quantiles of the forecasting errors yielded by methodology currently used in Japan to calculate the RR-FIT.

One advantage of modification II is that it can be built directly on top of the current calculation method. Thus, it is quite straightforward for power systems operators to implement it and to visualize the resulting functions. Another advantage of this modification is that it uses the data more efficiently. The reason for that is that within each three-hour block, the resulting fitted function will have used all the discrete quantile values of all the power output blocks. Thus, the RR-FIT estimation for each power output block will also be affected by the overall behavior of the forecasting errors with the forecasting values. This should cause a reduction of strong RR-FIT variations between blocks. Moreover, instead of generating a table, now functions for each three-hour block, that estimate 0.99865 quantiles once given the forecast of renewable power generation, are generated.

Area and data

To analyze the efficacy of the RR-FIT and of the modifications proposed, 5 years of real operation and RR-FIT data from the balancing area of Chubu in Japan were used. The location of Chubu is shown on Fig. 4. Currently, in Chubu region there is an installed capacity of PV and wind power of 12.02 GW and 360 MW respectively26. Of these systems the equivalent to approximately 10.8 GW is installed capacity subject to day-ahead and hour-ahead regulation reserves. For reference, the balancing area of Chubu had a maximum load of 25.2 GW in 202427.

Map of administrative areas of Japan (map data from: https://www.gadm.org) with Chubu Electric Power balancing area in orange.

The data on the VRES installed capacity of the Chubu region, subjected to regulation reserves, were provided by Chubu Electric Power Grid. The data are organized in 30-minute values, in MW, and it contains information about PV power forecasts (day-ahead and hour-ahead), and wind power forecasts (day-ahead and hour-ahead). In addition to that, observed values of power generation for wind and PV systems, as well as installed capacity of these types of systems per month of period studied were also provided.

The target period analyzed comprises the years from April of 2019 to March of 2024. To calculate the RR-FIT the current method employs two years of past data. Thus, data from the years of 2019 and 2020 were used to calculate reserve values from the year 2021; data from 2020 to 2021 were used to 2022 reserves and so on.

As mentioned in section “RR-FIT calculation via the distribution of the difference between past day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors (modification I)”, to calculate the RR-FIT for a target month and year, first day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors, from the same month, the month preceding, and the month succeeding the target month from the two years preceding the target year are selected. For example, to calculate a table with RR-FIT values as shown in Table 1, for May 2021, data from April, May, and June of the years of 2020 and 2019 are used. Once the data are selected, they are scaled to correspond to the installed capacity of the target month. Then, the categorization of the data as explained in section “RR-FIT calculation via the distribution of the difference between past day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors (modification I)” is carried out, and the calculations of the RR-FIT using Eq. (1), for the current method, or Eq. (2) for the proposed modifications are done.

For the current method, once a table in the form of Table 2 is ready, the RR-FIT for each 30 min of power system operation of 24 h a day is calculated by using the time and the day-ahead forecasts of VRES power generation for that time. For the proposed modification II, a function is used instead of the table. However, input values are also the same ones as those used in the current method.

Results

To provide a comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy of the modifications described in section “RR-FIT calculation methods”, we evaluated the reference method and the modified ones according to five quantiles, the currently used, the 0.99865 quantile, and the 0.70, 0.80, 0.90, and 0.95 quantiles. Moreover, since reserves for the hour-ahead forecasting errors are also partially used to cover the day-ahead, the size of these reserves as well as their utilization must be considered together with the evaluation of the RR-FIT. Thus, we evaluate the coverage of the day-ahead forecasting error considering both types of reserves, those meant for the hour-ahead forecasting error, and those for the day-ahead forecasting errors.

Regarding performance parameters, we focused on four variables representing different quantities relevant to the problem. The first one was the annual forecasting error coverage provided with the two types of reserves. Coverage here means the hours in which the reserves were greater or at least equal to the part of the forecasting error they are supposed to compensate for. The second quantity is the total reserves, in MWh, required annually to achieve different levels of coverage. The third and four quantities are the highest deficit and highest excess of reserves, at any given time, in MW, when compared with the corresponding realized renewable power generation. Reduction of the highest annual reserve deficit values is of particular concern for power system operators. This is because high deficit values imply in operation hours with high amounts of expected VRES power generation not realized and unmet by the reserves available destined for this function. Compared with that, power excesses of reserves are less of a concern, but when converted into total energy, MWh, throughout the year they provide an indication of inefficient use of energy. This is because reserves’ excesses for long periods mean paid for and unused allocation of generators that could be supplying power to the grid.

Performance of the reserves calculated with different methods

In Fig. 5, the annual coverages of the difference between day-ahead and hour-ahead forecast errors, referred to as [D.A. Error - H.A. Error], by the RR-FIT, achieved with different methods, are presented. To show the importance of removing anomalous values in the current method, its results before (w anomalous values) and after removing such values (w/o anomalous values) are also presented. Anomalous values in this study were regarded as negative reserve values, that happened in hours in which the quantiles of the H.A. errors were larger than the corresponding quantiles of the D.A. errors. When such values appear, OCCTO guidelines for their processing were adopted. Namely, they were replaced by the average of the positive values in adjacent hB and pB blocks when processed. One can visualize the blocks by referring to Table 2. For the results of the case in which they were not processed, a flag indicating their occurrence was used. The reserve then for corresponding hours was regarded as 0 (no RR-FIT).

Starting with anomalous values, they appear due to the use of Eq. (1) in the current method, as explained in section “Current method”. As Fig. 5 shows, the coverage of the [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] is strongly affected by anomalous values. Without correction for these values, increasing the reserves (through higher quantiles in Eq. 1) has little effect on providing better coverage of the quantity the RR-FIT is expected to cover. Once the anomalous values are processed, the black lines in Fig. 6 show how the current methodology-based RR-FIT improves with higher quantiles and higher reserve values. Although now there is a monotonic relation between [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] coverage and quantile/reserves, the achieved coverage with the current method at the 0.99865 quantile, heretofore referred to as the reference case, is quite low regardless of the year. The best result was achieved in 2023, with a coverage slightly higher than 80%. It is interesting to observe also that with the current method, changing the quantile used from 0.95 to 0.99865 quantile does cause an increase in the annual RR-FIT. However, these higher reserves do not increase the [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] coverage achieved. These results indicate a saturation or limiting values that the RR-FIT reaches, when calculated with the current method.

Compared with that, using modification I, the relation between the quantile used in the RR-FIT and the achieved [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] coverage had a good correspondence, particularly for values higher than 90%. Moreover, another advantage of modification I is that it does not present anomalous values, as the quantile of the difference between the day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasting errors are calculated directly from the distribution of such difference. Thus, an anomalous value processing step is not needed.

Looking now at the results achieved with modification I and modifications I and II, the difference between the blue and purple curves in Fig. 5 indicates that slightly higher [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] coverage was achieved for the same reserve values with modification I and II. This indicates that modification II, resulted in small but meaningful improvements compared with implementing only modification I. When comparing with the reference case, there is a meaningful improvement. For example, for 2021, the reference case required 3.3 GWh of reserves that covered the [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] only 73.1% of the time. By using the 0.70 quantile with modifications I and II, 2.4 GWh were required to cover 78.7% of the same [D.A. Error - H.A. Error].

On the other hand, with modifications I and II, the value of the total reserves increased considerably when compared with the current method value at the 0.99865 quantile. When using this quantile, the annual reserves increased more than two times, regardless of the year. The higher [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] coverage achieved by the modifications was due to the fact that they resulted in higher reserve values. Still, the dot-dashed vertical gray line in Fig. 5, shows that modifications I and II implemented with the 0.70 and 0.80 quantiles required the same annual reserves as reference case, but achieved higher [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] coverage. This suggests that the modifications yield more effective reserves.

In Fig. 6, we show the highest deficits (positive values, top figures) and highest excesses of reserve (negative values, bottom figures) achieved in the three years studied for each method, and for each quantile used.

The results in Fig. 6 show that anomalous values processing had negligible effects in the reduction of the extreme deficits of reserves when the 0.99865 quantile was used. Focusing only on the current method, after processing anomalous values, Fig. 6 indicates that higher [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] coverage had an effect reducing the value of the largest deficit of RR-FIT (around 14.3% reduction, from 2555 MW to 2190 MW). However, it also increased considerably the highest excess of reserve (around 94% increase, from 1388 MW to 2702 MW).

Looking now at the methods with modification I and modifications I and II, one can see that increasing the quantile used in the calculations resulted in higher [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] coverage, Fig. 5, and consistently reduced the highest deficit of RR-FIT. In the case of excess of RR-FIT, modifications I and II were particularly effective in reducing them when the quantiles used were lower than 0.95. In terms of annual RR-FIT, for the same quantile used in the calculations, both modifications resulted in considerably more reserve than the one yielded by the current method. However, again here, one can see that using lower quantiles with modifications I and II, yields higher [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] coverage, and deficit and excess reduction of the RR-FIT. For example, in 2021, the method with modifications I and II, when achieving a coverage of 85% of the difference between the day-ahead and hour-ahead errors, in Fig. 5, had as the highest RR-FIT deficit, a value of 1877 MW. The highest excess of reserve was 1527 MW. These values were 14% and 42% lower than the respective values achieved in the reference case.

Ultimately, the results in Figs. 5 and 6 show that even with just modification I, there is a better relation between the quantile used in the calculations of the reserves and the achieved [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] coverage. Using high quantile values increases the required RR-FIT. However, the results show that for lower quantile values, the estimation method with modifications I and II resulted in the same or less annual reserve than the one yielded by the reference case. Moreover, it also reduced lower extreme deficits and excesses of RR-FIT. These results are clear indications of more efficient estimation of procured reserves.

Why is the performance of the current method not a problem in the Japanese market?

In principle, the results in section “Performance of the reserves calculated with different methods”, show that the current method used to calculate the RR-FIT does not yield good results. It provides much lower-than-expected coverage of the part of the forecasting errors it should cover. It covers the [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] 73% of the time, using the 0.99865 quantile of the past values of this quantity. Moreover, extreme deficits are higher than they would be if methods, such as those proposed, were implemented. However, this apparently low performance, in the current system, is not causing power system instability problems for TSOs in Japan. The reason for that is related to the obligation to procure hour-ahead reserves and on the way this is done, as explained in section “Current method”. The use of such reserves with the RR-FIT, to compensate for the total day-ahead forecasting error, masks the real performance of the RR-FIT (as the function of the RR-FIT ends up being exerted also by hour-ahead error destined reserves).

Despite the current method for procuring RR-FIT not being a problem operationally, it does have some side effects. For example, TSOs would like to reduce the RR-FIT, or at least its cost. As its coverage is already low, and it is weakly related to the nominal quantile used in its calculation, it is not clear by how much the RR-FIT can be reduced, nor how should the reduction be implemented.

To provide a comprehensive evaluation of the RR-FIT and its role covering the part of the day-ahead forecasting errors it covers, it is necessary to identify the exact role of the hour-ahead reserves in the total forecast error coverage. To do that, we propose to classify such reserves in two categories. The first type, heretofore referred as “used for hour-ahead error”, is the amount of hour-ahead reserve actually used to cover the forecast error in the hour-ahead horizon. The second category represents the excess (when it occurs) from the difference between the total hour-ahead reserve and the hour-ahead error. This second category is referred as “not used for hour-ahead error”. The amount not used for the hour-ahead error is then what we will regard as available to be used with the RR-FIT to cover [D.A. Error - H.A. Error]. It should be noted that this division between RR-FIT, hour-ahead used, and hour-ahead unused is for market purposes only, as they are all added to the reserves available to the TSO to compensate for VRES power generation forecasting errors.

To evaluate then the effective coverage of the difference between the day-ahead and hour-ahead errors, we consider first the RR-FIT and the hour-ahead reserve not used for the hour-ahead error. The resulting reserve performance is presented in Figs. 7 and 8. As the importance of processing anomalous values has already been established, the results associated with the current method before such processing will be excluded from the ensuing analyses for brevity.

Annual RR-FIT plus H.A. reserves not used for H.A. error versus coverage of the difference between D.A and H.A. forecasts’ errors (gray dotted line indicates 0.99865 quantile; gray dot-dashed lines indicate reference case results). Left (a): 2021, center (b): 2022, right (c): 2023.

Annual RR-FIT + H.A. reserves not used for H.A. error versus highest deficit (positive values) and excess (negative values) of reserve throughout the year (gray dotted line indicates 0; gray dot-dashed lines indicate current method performance). Left (a): 2021, center (b): 2022, right (c): 2023.

The impact of hour-ahead reserves in compensating for the difference between day-ahead and hour-ahead errors is clear. Once considered, RR-FIT at all quantiles studied had little effect on the annual coverage of the [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] (Fig. 7, left graph). Even with the lowest quantile used, 0.70, the RR-FIT plus the hour-ahead reserve covered more than 95% of the [D.A. Error - H.A. Error], regardless of the year studied. For the reference case, the values increased to more than 98%. When one looks at the total reserves in Fig. 7, one sees that this small gain of the coverage is achieved with the current method, by increasing total reserves by 1.3 to 1.4 times, depending on the year, (considering the values at the 0.70 quantile and those of the reference cases).

In this scenario the modifications proposed have added little benefit, as the hour-ahead reserves are doing most of the work. Still, even in this case, one can see that the RR-FIT calculated with modifications I or with modifications I and II resulted in better use of the reserves. For example, in 2021, with modifications I and II, 6.76 TWh of total reserves were required to achieve 98.5% of error coverage. For the reference case the 7.1 TWh annually were required to cover 98.2% of the [D.A. Error - H.A. Error].

Regarding the extreme deficits and excesses of reserves, Fig. 8, shows that the use of hour-ahead reserves reduced the value of the extreme deficit of reserves in the three years studied. For example, for the reference case in 2022, the extreme deficit of reserves was reduced by around 31%, from 2572 MW (in Fig. 7) to 1781 MW (in Fig. 8). On the other hand, the highest excess of reserve increased by approximately 52% (from 2518 MW to 3835 MW). This variation of both quantities was achieved by adding to the reference case RR-FIT, 3.9 TWh, which was the contribution of the hour-ahead reserves not used for the hour-ahead forecasting error. This value is 1.2 times greater than the RR-FIT value of the reference case, in 2022.

Comparing the highest deficits of reserve achieved with the method implementing modifications I and II with those of the current method, the trend noticed in Fig. 6 is kept. Modification I and II, generally, reduced the highest deficit of reserve at annual reserve values like those of the reference case. However, this happened only when lower quantiles values were used. For the method implementing only modification I, the result is less pronounced and dependent of the year.

Looking at the variation of the highest excess of reserves in Fig. 8, one can see that considering hour-ahead reserves with the current method RR-FIT, resulted in a non-linear behavior. In the case of the methods implemented with modifications I and II, the behavior was like that noticed in Fig. 7. Changing the quantiles used in the calculations changes the date/time of occurrence of the extreme deficits/excess. Moreover, excess of reserves occurs when there was underestimation of VRES power generation, at the day-ahead forecast horizon, and reserves go completely unused. With the current method, the variability of the RR-FIT values is much stronger, because the quantities in Eq. 1 are drawn from different distributions. This variability, associated with the variability of the hour-ahead reserves, results in more possibility of non-monotonic behavior of the highest excesses of reserves with the variation of their annual values. With the modifications I and II the increase of quantiles causes a uniform increase in the reserves, as the reserves are calculated from a single distribution, Eq. (2). We believe this allows for less possibility of non-monotonic behavior of the excess of reserves.

From the results in Figs. 7 and 8, one can see that the implementation of modifications I and II, with the 0.99865 quantile, would increase the procurement of RR-FIT considerably. This would happen in spite of the fact that, with the current system, the annual coverage of the [D.A. Error – H.A. Error] is already high (due to the latent contribution of the hour-ahead reserves). The fact that hour-ahead reserves are used to cover most of the forecast errors that the RR-FIT should cover makes it difficult to estimate adequate values for the RR-FIT. It also makes it difficult to verify their suitability for each hour of operation of the power system. One potential solution in this case would be to implement modifications I and II with lower quantiles, to have better correspondence between the quantile used in the calculation of the reserves, and the achieved forecasting error coverage.

How good are the reserves to mitigate the day-ahead forecasting error?

Once the role of the hour-ahead reserves compensating the difference between day-ahead and hour-ahead errors was clarified in section “Why is the performance of the current method not a problem in the Japanese market?”, important questions that must be answered are: how to evaluate the total reserves used for the day-ahead forecasting error, and how effective are they to compensate for the VRES unrealized power generation. These questions must be answered, because what the TSOs want in the end is to have sufficient and cost-effective reserves to compensate for an unrealized VRES generation. Moreover, regardless of how and what reserves are used, they should reflect the forecasting errors characteristics, as well as accuracy improvements of related forecasts.

To answer these questions, we propose to evaluate the ability of the total day-ahead and the hour-ahead reserves to cover the total day-ahead forecasting errors, when different methods are used to calculate the RR-FIT. This should provide a clear view of the importance and effectiveness of each method used to calculate RR-FIT as well as the role of each reserve used. The proposed evaluation methodology provides the results in Fig. 9 for the three years of data of Chubu balancing area.

Left (a): 2021, center (b): 2022, right (c): 2023. The vertical gray dashed line in each bar graph indicates the reference case reserves for each respective year.

The results in Fig. 9 indicate that in all three years, the reserve procured for the hour-ahead forecasting error is high enough to cover more than 93% of the day-ahead forecasting errors. This indicates that there is little space for the RR-FIT to act in covering the day-ahead forecasting errors. For the three years, the addition of RR-FIT reserves, at values greater than the total hour-ahead reserves, provided marginal gains in improving the coverage of the day-ahead forecasting error. This characteristic was the same, regardless of the method used to calculate the RR-FIT.

Because of that, the evaluations also show that there is considerable space for RR-FIT reductions, while keeping similar level of error coverage. This is true even with the current calculation method. For example, for the 2021 the RR-FIT of the reference case could be reduced from 3.3 TWh to 1.3TWh, less than half its reference value, if a reduction of the coverage of the day-ahead forecasting error from 99.06% to 97.76% is allowed. Reductions of the same order can be achieved in 2022 and 2023. If an error coverage like the one currently achieved is desired, according to the results in Fig. 9, for all three years this can be achieved with modifications I and II, using the 0.70 quantile of the past [D.A. Error - H.A. Error] in the RR-FIT calculations.

Finally, to single out the effectiveness of the RR-FIT calculated with current and modified methods, in Fig. 10, we excluded the excess of hour-ahead reserves (those not used to cover than hour-ahead forecasting errors). Once this is done, it is clear how the current calculation method is inefficient to cover the day-ahead forecasting error. Coverage levels drop to less than 90% in any of the three years. Moreover, using the same amount of energy with modification I, or with modifications I and II, yield higher day-ahead error coverage. Here too, the main effect of modification II was to require slightly lower reserves than when only modification I is implemented, while achieving similar levels of error coverage than the latter.

Conclusion

The objective of this study was to provide a first-hand analysis of the use of specific regulation reserves to compensate for unrealized VRES power generation in Japan. In it, we also discussed issues with the current methodology and evaluated the effectiveness of the reserves calculated with it to cover day-ahead overestimation errors. Furthermore, the study evaluated the use of two modifications on the current method, aimed at improving the effectiveness of the reserves calculated with it. Five years of real data of the TSO of the Chubu region in Japan were used in the analyses.

Regarding the current methodology to calculate the RR-FIT, the results show that processing of anomalous values is vital for it to provide reasonable values. Still, even with that, the calculation method severely overestimates the effective coverage these reserves provide. This is not a problem currently, because hour-ahead reserves are used to compensate for the day-ahead forecasting errors. However, once these reserves are disregarded in the analysis, the low performance of the current methodology becomes evident.

Modification I provided less overestimation of the coverage of the reserves than the one provided with the current method. Modification II improved the effectiveness of the reserves, as, in many cases, it yielded reserves that were lower than those presented by the current method, and that achieved higher error coverage. In other words, both modifications yielded reserves with better correspondence between forecasting errors and quantile setting. Moreover, for the same amount of reserves of the current method, they provided higher forecasting error coverage, indicating a more efficient use of the reserves. It should be noted also that, both modifications are on the calculation stage of the reserves’ estimation, and use information available at the time of the calculations. Therefore, regarding the calculation method of the RR-FIT itself, there are no new data requirements or extra costs for the operators. Moreover, it does not alter the current way to procure such reserves.

Regarding limitations of the modifications, they are effective in markets where day-ahead and hour-ahead forecasts of VRES power generation are available, and where intraday regulation reserves to mitigate their variations are required. Moreover, both modifications proposed are highly dependent on the ability of the data available to properly describe the characteristics of the forecasts. The spline method also requires the setting of the degree of freedom parameter. In this study, extensive tests with spare data showed that using a value higher than 3 for this parameter often caused overfitting, and degrees of freedom of 2 or less resulted in curves without enough flexibility to describe the behavior of the forecasts errors with the power capacity and hour of the day. Regions with different climate than Japan, may require a different setting for this parameter. Thus, we recommend its customization. Once this is done, we believe that the method can be easily applied to other locations with similar needs regarding regulation reserves.

The results also showed that a market design with no excessive allocation of reserves for their respective time-horizons, and where reserves allocated for specific time-horizon are mainly used in that time-horizon, would increase the merit of the proposed modifications. In this sense, another contribution of this study was to show that to achieve high gains in efficient reserve allocation, adequate estimations methods for reserves should be followed by market designs and mechanisms that allow such gains to have positive effects on the power system.

Regarding the quantification of cost reductions, regulation reserves are procured via an auction system in Japan. Their cost is highly dependent on the market conditions and bidding prices at the moment each auction occurs. Thus, it is difficult to estimate the exact cost reductions that can be achieved by the modifications proposed. Nevertheless, reductions of the RR-FIT should result in considerable cost savings for two reasons. Firstly, fewer reserves need to be procured by the TSO, reducing its cost. Secondly, by having less RR-FIT procurement bidders will redirect their offers to markets for other types of reserves, which should cause a reduction of the overall cost of regulation reserves in those markets.

Currently, both modifications still rely on the division of data in time slots and power output slots (as the current methodology). As such, issues associated with scarcity of data, and efficient use of data remain. Therefore, even with current market rules in Japan, there is still a margin for improvements in the calculation of the RR-FIT, so that they can be reduced while providing better coverage of the day-ahead forecast errors. In further studies, we will keep exploring new ways to achieve such improvements.
