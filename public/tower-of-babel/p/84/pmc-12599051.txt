Association between extreme temperature events and multimorbidity among older adults: evidence from the CHARLS
Authors: Jingyi Tang, Wangyang Gu, Meng Wang, Jue Liu, Yang Chen, Xiaoli Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe older adults are a high-risk group for multimorbidity. However, a possible multimorbidity link with extreme temperature events is unknown. We aimed to explore the association of cold spells and heatwaves and risk of multimorbidity among older people.MethodsThis cohort included 9475 adults aged 45 and over from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Heatwaves and cold spells were defined according to the average temperature, with the combination of six thresholds (≥ 97.5%, 97.5%, 92.5%, 7.5%, 5%, and 2.5%) and three durations (≥ 2, 3, and 4 days). Our outcome variable was multimorbidity, defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic diseases in individuals. Cox proportional hazard models with time-dependent covariates were employed to assess effects of extreme temperature events on the risk of multimorbidity. We also conducted subgroup analyses to assess possible modifying effects among various populations.ResultsHeatwaves were positively associated with the risk of multimorbidity, with HRs ranging from 1.008 (95% CI: 1.002–1.013) to 1.021 (95% CI: 1.007–1.035). Besides, each additional day of cold spell exposure was correlated with an increased risk of multimorbidity by between 1.8% (HR: 1.018, 95% CI: 1.012–1.024) and 2.4% (HR: 1.024, 95% CI: 1.016–1.032) among older adults. The subgroup analyses revealed that males, adults with insufficient sleep, or living in southern areas were more susceptible to the extreme temperature events.ConclusionsBoth cold spells and heatwaves exposure increase the risk of multimorbidity in older adults. Our findings highlight the necessity of preventing harmful effects of extreme temperature events.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04456-6.

Background

As the population is aging and the incidence of chronic disease is rising, multimorbidity has become an increasingly severe global health concern [1]. Currently, no consensus exists regarding the concept of multimorbidity. It is typically defined as an individual suffering from at least two chronic diseases simultaneously, which is proposed by the World Health Organization (WHO) [2]. The global prevalence of multimorbidity stood at 37.2%, with obvious variations across continents. South America showed the highest prevalence rate at 47.5%, followed by North America (43.1%), Europe (39.2%), and Asia (35%) [3]. In China, a nationwide cohort study demonstrated that multimorbidity prevalence among adults aged ≥ 50 years had reached 42.4% [4]. Among older adults (≥ 70 years), the prevalence rate climbed to 61% [5]. Multimorbidity brings a heavy burden on individuals, caregivers, and society [6, 7]. For instance, a meta-analysis reported annual multimorbidity costs per person ranging from I$800 to I$150,000, imposing a large economic pressure on the health system and society [8].

Environmental factors have emerged as pivotal drivers in the development of multimorbidity. However, much research focuses on air or water pollution [9, 10], while the epidemiological implications of meteorological factors on the development of multimorbidity remain critically understudied. Cold spells and heatwaves are typical extreme temperature events, which are defined as prolonged periods (consecutive days) of abnormally low or high temperatures [11]. Global climate change has amplified temperature fluctuations, causing a constant growth of the frequency, duration, and severity of extreme temperature events [12–15]. From 1980 to 2019, the average annual occurrence frequency of heatwaves in major land regions worldwide increased by 37–52%, and their duration prolonged by 17–31% [16]. A systematic review reported the cold spell-related and heatwave-related risk of asthma exacerbation and increased mortality [17]. A national Chinese study indicated that exposure to cold spells and heatwaves increased the risk of pneumonia by 69% [18]. Furthermore, multinational researches consistently identified extreme temperature events as a vital risk factor for cardiovascular diseases [19–21]. Nevertheless, existing research has focused on individual chronic diseases, without considering that nearly a quarter of adults have multimorbidity [22].

To date, the intricate impact of extreme heat and cold exposure on multimorbidity outcomes has not been unexamined. To fill this gap, we designed a prospective cohort study using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) from 2011 to 2020, a nationwide survey of more than 17,000 participants. Incorporating various definitions of heatwaves and cold spells, we investigated whether there were associations between extreme temperature events and the risk of multimorbidity among Chinese adults aged 45 and older. In addition, our subgroup analysis assessed whether these relationships were influenced by age, gender, region, residence, and sleep duration. We aimed to highlight the impact of extreme climate on older people and provide guidance for formulating public health policies for the prevention and treatment of multimorbidity.

Methods

Study population

The participants in this cohort were from the CHARLS [23, 24]. CHARLS is a nationally representative survey that recruited approximately 17,000 older adults from 10,000 households using a multistage stratified sampling approach, covering 150 county-level and 450 village-level units. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011 and 2012, followed by four waves of follow-up surveys (2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020). All respondents received a face-to-face interview and filled out a structured questionnaire, providing data on basic characteristics, family structure, economic level, health status, etc. The CHARLS study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Peking University (approval number: IRB00001052-11015). All participants have signed informed consent forms.

As shown in Fig. 1, our study included 17,708 older people recruited in the baseline (2011–2012) survey. Considering the study objectives, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) no age information or age < 45 years old (N = 648), (2) no multimorbidity information in the baseline (N = 841), (3) participants were diagnosed with multimorbidity in the baseline (N = 6206), and (4) no information on multimorbidity during follow-up period (N = 538). Ultimately, 9475 older people were included in the current cohort study. Fig. 1Flow chart of the selection criteria for analyses

Definitions for extreme temperature events

We gathered daily average temperature data from the China Meteorological Administration Land Data Assimilation System (version 3.0). Considering the participants’ residential privacy, we matched the temperature with individuals based on the city and calculated the number of heatwave or cold spell days that the individuals were exposed to 1 year before the follow-up survey. In other words, we used a 1-year exposure time window to reflect the time-varying variable of extreme temperature event exposure.

According to previous studies, we determined 18 definitions for extreme temperature events (9 each for heatwaves and cold spells) by combining temperature thresholds and duration [25]. The temperature thresholds for heatwaves and cold spells were defined as the 92.5, 95, and 97.5 percentiles and the 7.5, 5, and 2.5 percentiles, respectively, of the average temperature in all cities. The exposure durations of extreme temperature events were determined by the number of consecutive days: ≥ 2 days, ≥ 3 days, and ≥ 4 days. Therefore, heatwaves were defined as daily average temperature at or above thresholds for at least 2, 3, or 4 consecutive days; cold spells were defined as daily average temperature at or below thresholds for at least 2, 3, or 4 consecutive days (Additional file 1: Table S1).

Assessment of multimorbidity

The WHO defines multimorbidity as the coexistence of two or more chronic diseases in the same patient [2]. In the CHARLS, data on chronic diseases were gathered using questionnaires, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, digestive disease, psychiatric disease, memory-related disease, arthritis, and asthma [26]. The participants were asked whether they had been diagnosed with the disease by a doctor. If participants answered “yes,” they were considered to have this disease. In the present study, multimorbidity means that participants had two or more of these 14 chronic diseases. In the CHARLS, the four waves of follow-up surveys (2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020) all provided data on chronic diseases. The outcome of interest was the first occurrence of multimorbidity.

Covariates

The following covariates were considered: age, gender, BMI, education level (below primary school, primary school, middle school, and high school), residence (urban and rural), region (north and south), socioeconomic status (poor, fair, and good), smoking status (yes and no), drinking status (yes and no), physical activity (no, low, middle and high intensity), sleep duration (< 6 h and ≥ 6 h), heating fuels (clean and solid), and cooking fuels (clean and solid). All above information was obtained at baseline using a structured questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

We first conducted descriptive analyses for characteristics at baseline. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and proportions, and the group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were described as the medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) owing to the skew distribution, and the Mann–Whitney U-test was applied to group comparisons.

Then, we used Cox proportional hazards regression models with time-dependent covariates to examine the association between exposure to extreme temperature events and multimorbidity among older people. Heatwaves and cold spells were analyzed as time-dependent variables, and the remaining variables were set as time-fixed covariates. We calculated the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) to evaluate the risk effects. Models were adjusted based on previous studies and a causal directed acyclic graph (DAG, Additional file 1: Fig. S1) [20, 27]. In brief, model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, BMI, residence, and region; and model 3 was further adjusted for education, smoking status, drinking status, sleep duration, heating fuels, and cooking fuels. Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analyses by age, gender, residence, region, and sleep duration to assess possible modifying effects among various populations, applying interaction terms to test subgroup differences. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.0). P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

MICE allows conducting separate conditional distributions for different types of variables [28]. Thus, logistic regression models were used as the imputation models for the binary variables, linear regression models were used for the continuous variables, and polynomial regression models were used for the multi-class variables. We used the “mice” package to generate 5 imputed datasets, each with 10 iterations, combined with convergence graphs to ensure model convergence. Then, we performed Cox proportional hazards regression in five datasets and combined the estimated values using Rubin’s rules.

Exposure to extreme temperature events

We calculated the number of cold spell and heatwave days for each city using 18 kinds of definitions of extreme temperature events and matched them to each participant. We describe the average number of cold spell or heatwave days in the year preceding follow-up survey (Additional file 1: Table S3). During the year prior to the first follow-up survey, the average exposure period per old adult ranged from 2.0 to 24.5 days for heatwaves and from 2.1 to 25.2 days for cold spells among participants. In 2019, the average number of days of heatwave and cold spell exposure was 2.1–23.5 and 1.2–24.7, respectively.

Discussion

This nationwide cohort study investigated the effect of extreme temperature events on multimorbidity. The results indicate that exposure to cold spells and heatwaves increases the risk of multimorbidity among the Chinese people over 45 years old. This risk was significantly elevated by the intensity and duration of cold spell and heatwave. Notably, subgroup analysis revealed potential heterogeneity of the association between extreme temperature events and multimorbidity, indicating that males, adults with insufficient sleep, or living in southern areas are more vulnerable to extreme temperature events.

Extreme temperature events exhibit escalating frequency and intensity, bringing health challenges for aging populations. Epidemiological evidence has established positive associations between extremely high temperatures and cardiometabolic comorbidities, with hazard ratios ranging from 1.028 to 1.102 for heatwaves and 1.046 to 1.187 for cold spells [20]. A meta-analysis revealed increased cardiovascular vulnerability during thermal extremes, particularly among females, adults aged ≥ 65 years, and people living in low- and middle-income countries [29]. Notably, extreme temperature events exhibit strong associations with endocrine-metabolic disorders. A nationwide Chinese cohort study demonstrated temperature-dependent mortality amplification in diabetic patients, increasing 22.3% during extreme cold and 81.1% during intense heat events [30]. Cold exposure induces lipid alterations in elderly populations, including elevated erythrocyte count, plasma cholesterol, and fibrinogen levels, potentially explaining the increased hyperlipidemia risk [31]. Cerebrovascular risks also escalate during cold spells, evidenced by 14.15% increases in stroke hospitalization rates [32] and statistically significant odds ratios (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.09–1.53) [33]. Beyond physiological impacts, extreme temperature harms mental health. Per 1 °C temperature increase, morbidity rises occur across multiple psychiatric disorders, including mood disorders, organic mental disorders, schizophrenia, and neurotic and anxiety disorders [34, 35]. However, existing studies predominantly focus on a single chronic disease rather than multimorbidity. In contrast, our study concurrently evaluated the health effects of extreme heat and cold temperatures while focusing on multimorbidity. Among nine definitions, high-standard temperature thresholds (lower for cold spells, higher for heatwaves), combined with longer exposure durations, showed more robust hazard risk estimates, providing compelling statistical evidence for the association.

Some evidence has suggested potential biological mechanisms linking extreme temperature events to the development of multimorbidity. Heatwaves and cold spells exert harmful effects on multiple physiological systems in older people, accelerating chronic disease progression and increasing risk of multimorbidity. When facing heatwaves, thermoregulatory-driven peripheral vasodilation induces blood redistribution that may cause myocardial ischemia [36]. Concurrently, excessive sweating under heatwaves leads to hemoconcentration and significantly elevates thrombosis risk [37]. Besides, cold exposure activates the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and renin-angiotensin system (RAS), resulting in hypertension and myocardial infarction [37–39]. Reviews have reported the detrimental impacts of extreme temperatures on respiratory. Extreme temperature can induce airway constriction, enhance mucus production, exacerbate airway inflammation, and impair pulmonary function, thereby exacerbating asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [40, 41]. Furthermore, heat stress directly compromises immune system in the elderly population, playing a vital role in promoting inflammatory disease progression [42]. Notably, heatwaves induce dysregulation across endocrine systems, including the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, autonomic nervous system, thyroid axis, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), and white adipose tissue metabolism [43]. Cold exposure has been shown to induce intestinal barrier dysfunction and oxidative stress through the SIRT1/Nrf2 signaling pathway [44]. Importantly, both cold spells and heatwaves adversely affect cognitive performance and emotional regulation in older adults, increasing vulnerability to anxiety and depressive disorders [45, 46]. These mental disorders may further induce subsequent physical disorders, increasing multimorbidity risk [47].

Subgroup analyses revealed that the positive associations between exposure to extreme temperature events and multimorbidity among older adults were markedly amplified in individuals with insufficient sleep. This finding aligns with previous findings from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), which demonstrated that shorter sleep duration (> 7 h) was linked to a higher prevalence of multimorbidity [48]. Moreover, our study identified that males exhibited greater vulnerability to cold spells and heatwaves. This disparity mainly stems from the combined effect of biological factors (e.g., hormonal regulation, metabolic characteristics) and sociobehavioral factors (e.g., outdoor exposure, lifestyle). The physiological decline in homeostatic regulation and thermoregulatory capacity associated with aging processes increases susceptibility in older populations, explaining the age-dependent escalation in multimorbidity prevalence [49, 50]. However, the present study did not identify a significant exacerbating effect of age on the association between extreme temperature events and multimorbidity. Furthermore, older adults living in southern areas were more susceptible to the impacts of heatwaves and cold spells. The lack of climate-adaptive facilities in southern regions, such as centralized heating systems, may exacerbate the adverse impacts of cold waves. Additionally, the high humidity in these areas is likely to have a synergistic effect with high temperatures, strengthening the link between heatwave exposure and multimorbidity [51].

This study holds significant public health implications. By preventing and reducing the risk of multimorbidity, such initiatives could effectively alleviate burdens on individuals, families, and society.

Second, governments must heighten awareness regarding the health impacts of extreme temperature events. While pursuing urban planning objectives and economic development, local governments should proactively adopt strategies to reduce the frequency of extreme temperature events and adverse health impacts.

Our study has several strengths. Crucially, our investigation delves into a previously unexplored area, providing the first epidemiological evidence for an association between the number of cold spell and heatwave days and the risk of multimorbidity. A previous study investigated the association between extreme temperature events, functional dependency, and cardiometabolic multimorbidity [20]. Compared to this, we focused on multimorbidity related to several physiological systems rather than just cardiovascular multimorbidity. While both examine heatwave and cold spell exposure, Dong et al. focused on the frequency of exposure events, whereas we investigate the number of exposure days. Exposure frequency reflects how often heatwaves or cold spells occur, indicating the ability of the elderly population to adapt to a repeated “impact-recovery” stress pattern. In our study, exposure day is essentially a composite metric that combines exposure intensity and duration. It captures the cumulative burden imposed by extreme temperatures on older adults. This persistent and chronic stress increases the risk of multimorbidity among the elderly. Considering that extreme temperature events usually occur continuously for multiple days in a relatively short period, the number of exposure days might be more accurate to estimate the impacts of heatwaves and cold spells on older adults, compared to exposure frequency. Second, the large sample size from a nationally population-representative cohort enhances the robustness and generalizability of our findings. Third, we explored whether several characteristics and behavior factors modified the association between extreme temperature events with multimorbidity outcomes, finding the susceptible people for cold spells and heatwaves. Finally, sensitivity analysis confirmed a robust correlation between extreme temperature events and multimorbidity among older adults.

Nevertheless, some limitations merit attention. First, assessing multimorbidity outcomes relying on self-reported data may cause some bias, even if doctors have diagnosed it. Besides, the lack of individual-level information on extreme temperature events limits the accuracy of exposure assessment. Due to privacy protection, we did not have participants’ precise geographical location data. Therefore, city-level cold spell and heatwave data were used for matching. In addition, some potential confounding factors not taken into account in the models, such as working environment, might influence the risk estimation. Furthermore, factors such as sleep and drink status might simultaneously act as potential mediating pathways through which extreme temperatures affect health and independent predictors of the outcome (multimorbidity). In our study, the number of exposure days to heatwaves and cold spells was calculated based on data from the year prior to each follow-up, while the variables, such as sleep duration, were collected via questionnaires in the baseline. In terms of temporal ordering, covariates such as sleep duration occurred before exposure to extreme temperature events. Thus, considering these variables as covariates to adjust the Cox models is reasonable.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this nationwide cohort study discovered the link between heatwave and cold spell exposure and the risk of multimorbidity among people aged 45 and over. It is necessary to adopt adaptive strategies during extremely high and low temperatures to ensure the health of older people and prevent the occurrence of multimorbidity. This measure is particularly necessary among males, adults with insufficient sleep, or living in southern areas.
