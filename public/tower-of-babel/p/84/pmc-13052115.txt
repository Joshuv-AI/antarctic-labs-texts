Neutrophil‐to‐Albumin Ratio Mediates the Association Between Life's Crucial 9 and Sarcopenia: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Qiqi He, Yingying Liu, Yanbin Li, Yingji Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEmerging evidence links cardiovascular health, inflammation, and sarcopenia (SP), but the mediating mechanisms remain unclear. Life's Crucial 9 (LC9), a novel metric integrating mental health with cardiovascular risk factors, and neutrophil‐to‐albumin ratio (NPAR), a cost‐effective inflammatory biomarker, may jointly influence SP risk. This investigation seeks to examine the connection between LC9 and sarcopenia, assessing whether NPAR acts as a mediator in this relationship.MethodsMultivariable logistic regression, smooth curve fitting, subgroup analysis, and other methods were employed to investigate the relationship between LC9 and the incidence of SP. Additionally, mediation analysis was conducted to examine the potential relationship between NPAR levels, LC9, and SP.ResultsThis analysis involved 7,761 participants, of whom 648 reported experiencing SP events. After adjusting for all covariates using multivariable logistic regression, a 10‐point increase in LC9 was associated with a 37% reduction in the prevalence of sarcopenia (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.59, 0.67). Additionally, sarcopenia increased by 1.17‐fold for each unit increase in NPAR (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.11, 1.24). Conversely, the NPAR level and the highest quantile of SP prevalence increased by 2.26 times, respectively (p < 0.001). Smooth curve fitting indicated a non‐linear relationship between LC9 and SP risk (p for overall < 0.001, p for non‐linearity = 0.001). Subsequent threshold analysis identified a turning point at an LC9 score of 73.33; the risk of SP decreased gradually before this point, whereas a more pronounced and accelerated decline was observed once the score exceeded 73.33.ConclusionLC9 inversely correlates with SP, partially mediated by NPAR‐driven inflammation, suggesting anti‐inflammatory interventions may mitigate SP risk.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is characterized as a systemic and progressive disorder of skeletal muscle, marked by an age‐related reduction in muscle mass, alongside decreased muscle strength and/or reduced physical performance [1]. To address this condition, the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2025 consensus introduces a novel conceptual framework centered on “muscle health promotion,” shifting the focus toward life‐course management. This update establishes diagnostic thresholds for the middle‐aged population for the first time, streamlines the diagnostic criteria to the coexistence of low muscle mass and low muscle strength, and reclassifies physical performance as an outcome measure [2]. Such a pivot is essential as sarcopenia has surfaced as a notable contributor to global morbidity and disability; research suggests its prevalence lies between 5.5% and 25.7%, exhibiting a marked predominance in males (5.1%–21.0% in men vs. 4.1%–16.3% in women) [1]. Therefore, recognizing and addressing modifiable risk factors is critical for preventing muscle decline and alleviating its related complications.

Inflammation associated with sarcopenia, aging, and lipid metabolism is marked by elevated systemic levels of dysfunctional inflammatory molecules, often termed “chronic low‐grade inflammation,” which play a role in the onset of sarcopenia [3]. Key inflammatory molecules involved in this process include TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐1, and various chemokines; these promote the recruitment of inflammatory cells via the NF‐κB pathway, thereby worsening muscle degradation [4]. A meta‐analysis revealed that people with sarcopenia exhibited significantly higher CRP levels than those without elevated CRP (SMD = 0.51; 95% CI 0.26, 0.77; p < 0.0001) [5], although the precise mechanisms are still unclear. Inflammation is also acknowledged as a risk factor for numerous conditions, including cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes, osteoporosis, sarcopenia, cancer, anemia, and Alzheimer's disease [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Moreover, increased levels of inflammatory markers in the bloodstream correlate with greater muscle mass and strength loss, a quicker decline in mobility, diminished lower limb performance and physical activity, as well as heightened rates of depression among older adults. Viewed from this angle, inflammation may significantly influence the aging process and the development of sarcopenia.

Life's Crucial 9 (LC9) denotes a thorough evaluation of cardiovascular health proposed by Circulation magazine in 2024. This system serves as an indicator to assist individuals in enhancing cardiovascular health through healthier lifestyle choices [13]. LC9 builds on Life's Essential 8 (LE8), which was launched in 2022, by integrating mental health into its model. The elements of LC9 comprise sleep, smoking cessation, daily physical activity, dietary habits, body mass index (BMI), non‐HDL cholesterol, blood glucose levels, blood pressure, and mental well‐being [14]. Nonetheless, inflammation, recognized as a significant modifiable risk factor, could hinder the advantages of LC9 concerning sarcopenia. The neutrophil percentage‐to‐albumin ratio (NPAR), a biomarker that denotes the body's inflammatory condition and nutritional health, has attracted considerable focus in recent years. NPAR not only signifies inflammation but also demonstrates the influence of inflammatory elements on cardiovascular diseases [15]. This research posits that NPAR is independently linked to the risk of sarcopenia and serves a mediating function in the connection between LC9 and sarcopenia. Through this mediating influence, NPAR may clarify how inflammation impacts the health benefits derived from LC9. By performing mediation analyses, this study intends to validate this hypothesis and shed light on the possible pathways that connect LC9, NPAR, and sarcopenia. These results could provide crucial evidence for creating prevention and intervention strategies aimed at sarcopenia.

Methods

Study Participants

This study, which employs a cross‐sectional design, draws upon data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). This survey gathers demographic data related to the health and nutritional habits of individuals in the United States. All protocols related to the NHANES study received approval from the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. Our secondary analyses followed the STROBE guidelines specifically applicable to cross‐sectional studies [16].

Among 39,156 participants across four NHANES cycles from 2011 to 2018, 22,369 participants were aged 20 years or older and not pregnant. We excluded those with LC9 indicators (n = 7880), NPAR data (n = 95), and SP data for individuals with incomplete information (n = 6,633). Ultimately, 7761 participants were included in the final analysis (Figure S1).

Definition of SP

In the 2011‐2018 NHANES study, participants underwent evaluation through dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA). The combined lean mass of the limbs, which includes both arms and legs, is termed appendicular lean mass (ALM). To determine the muscle loss index, ALM (expressed in kilograms) is divided by body mass index (kg/m²). The National Institutes of Health (FNIH) defines individuals as sarcopenic if their muscle loss index is less than 0.789 for men and 0.512 for women [17].

Definition of NPAR

Data on the participant neutrophil percentage to albumin ratio (NPAR) were obtained from the NHANES database. NPAR was calculated by dividing the neutrophil percentage (numerator) by albumin, utilizing the same blood sample, according to the following formula: (neutrophil percentage (%) * 100/albumin (g/dL)) [18].

Definition of Life's Crucial 9

The LC9 model consists of nine indicators, which include four health behaviors (such as a balanced diet, regular physical activity, quitting smoking, and sufficient sleep) and five health factors (including weight management, cholesterol levels, blood sugar levels, blood pressure control, and mental well‐being). Table S1 outlines the detailed procedures for calculating each participant's LC9 score using the NHANES database. To summarize, every one of the nine indicators contributes a score that ranges between 0 and 100. The aggregate LC9 score is computed as the average score of the nine individual indicators. The evaluation of a healthy diet was based on the Healthy Eating Index 2015 (HEI‐2015) [19], with scoring criteria and components explained in Table S2. Information regarding sleep health, smoking habits, physical activity, and mental health was acquired through established questionnaires, whereas data for BMI, blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol were gathered from the NHANES database (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm) by professionals trained in data collection.

Covariables

Covariates in this study include age, gender, race, marital status, education, and PIR (household poverty–income ratio). See Table S3 for details on these covariates.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical terms and symbols used in this study include the Odds Ratio (OR), representing the strength of association, and the 95% Confidence Interval (CI), indicating the precision of estimates. This research employed R (version 4.3.1) to conduct statistical analyses, utilizing sampling weights to ensure that the estimated data accurately reflected the national population. For our analysis, the weight variable “WTMEC2YR” was incorporated, with a new weight for the 2011–2018 period determined by calculating 1/4 × WTMEC2YR [20]. Continuous variables are represented as mean ± standard deviation, while p‐values were derived from the t‐test. We calculated percentages of categorical variables (weighted N, %) along with their associated p‐values using the weighted chi‐square test.

The following analyses were pre‐specified as primary: To examine the associations between LC9 and SP, as well as NPAR and SP, multivariable logistic regression models were utilized. Three separate models were developed: (1) a simple model without adjustments for covariates; (2) a model that accounted for age, gender, and race; and (3) a model adjusted for age, gender, education level, marital status, PIR (household poverty–income ratio), and race. Smooth curve fitting techniques were employed to analyze both linear and nonlinear relationships involving LC9 and SP. Mediation analysis was also pre‐specified: to assess the indirect, direct, and overall effects of LC9 on SP mediated by NPAR, with the mediation ratio calculated as indirect effect/(indirect effect + direct effect) × 100%. The “mediation” package in R software was used to evaluate mediating effects.

Exploratory analyses, including subgroup analyses, were performed to delve deeper into the connections between LC9 and SP across different demographic strata. A two‐sided p‐value threshold of less than 0.05 was set a priori as indicative of statistical significance. The statistical analysis and reporting follow the recommendations in the ‘Guidelines for reporting of statistics for clinical research in urology’ and the SAMPL guidelines [21, 22].

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The study included 7761 participants aged 20 and older, representing approximately 95,787,201 adults in the United States. The prevalence of sarcopenia was found to be 7%, which corresponds to approximately 6,672,094 individuals. Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed between patients with sarcopenia and those without regarding age, sex, race, marital status, education level, income level, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Additionally, the LC9 level in the sarcopenia group was lower than that in the non‐sarcopenia group, while the NPAR level in the sarcopenia group was higher than that in the non‐sarcopenia group. Association between LC9, NPAR, and SP

According to Table 2, three distinct models were utilized to assess the relationship between LC9 and SP, with all models revealing a negative correlation between LC9 levels and the occurrence of SP (all p < 0.001). In the third model, when controlling for various covariates, a 10‐point rise in LC9 correlated with a 37% reduction in the prevalence of SP [odds ratio: 0.63 (95% confidence interval: 0.59, 0.67)]. Moreover, analysis based on tertiles showed that the cohort with the highest LC9 levels (T3) had an 83% lower prevalence of SP compared to the lowest LC9 cohort (T1) [odds ratio: 0.17 (95% confidence interval: 0.12, 0.23)]. In addition, we explored the connection between NPAR and SP, where the findings illustrated that across all three models, NPAR was positively correlated with SP prevalence (all p < 0.001); notably, individuals with higher NPAR scores exhibited a greater prevalence of SP. Statistically significant differences were recorded (all p < 0.05).

Subgroup Analyses

Subgroup analyses were carried out considering factors such as age, gender, race, marital status, education level, and poverty income ratio (PIR) (Figure 2). The findings demonstrated a notable negative association between the LC9 score and SP across all subgroups. Moreover, an important interaction between LC9 and gender was detected (p < 0.05). Within the NPAR subgroup analysis, a significant positive association between NPAR and SP was found. In addition, significant interactions were identified involving NPAR with both gender and education (p < 0.05).

Rcs of the LC9 and Sp

To further explore the dose‐response relationship between LC9 scores and the risk of sarcopenia, we employed a Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) model. The analysis confirmed a significant non‐linear inverse association (P for Overall < 0.001, P for non‐linearity = 0.001; Figure 1). Subsequent threshold analysis identified a turning point at an LC9 score of 73.33. Specifically, the risk of sarcopenia decreased gradually at lower LC9 scores, whereas a more pronounced and accelerated decline was observed once the score exceeded 73.33.

Mediation Effect

The mediation model is illustrated in Figure 3, with LC9, SP, and NPAR serving as independent variables, dependent variables, and mediating variables, respectively. Table 3 indicates that LC9 exhibits a significant correlation with NPAR after controlling for other covariates (β = −0.27, 95% CI: −0.33, −0.22). Upon adjusting for all covariates, we observe a mediating effect of NPAR (indirect effect = −7.46 × 10⁻³, p < 0.001; direct effect = −9.51×10⁻², p <0.001), resulting in a mediation ratio of 7.18% (p <0.001). Consequently, NPAR can be considered a mediating factor in the relationship between LC9 and SP.

Discussion

The research examined a total of 7761 participants from the NHANES dataset covering the years 2011 to 2018, shedding light on the mediating influence of NPAR in the relationship between LC9 and SP. Results showed a negative association between LC9 and the incidence of SP, whereas NPAR demonstrated a positive relationship with SP. Moreover, mediation analysis suggested that NPAR served as a partial mediator in the connection between LC9 and SP. Notable interactions were found between LC9 and gender, along with interactions between NPAR and both gender and educational factors. Additionally, mechanisms that have anti‐inflammatory effects might reduce the impact of NPAR on SP.

To the best of our understanding, this research represents the initial examination of the association between LC9 and SP mediated by NPAR. Earlier investigations have indicated that cardiovascular health (CVH), as measured by the LE8 score, correlates negatively with SP prevalence [23]. Although this observation is consistent with our findings, it overlooked the influence of mental health on SP and failed to identify NPAR's mediating effect. Our research not only validates the mediating function of NPAR but also uncovers a positive association between NPAR and SP. Furthermore, we investigated the relationship between LC9 and SP, emphasizing the importance of mental well‐being in the context of cardiovascular health. Mental health includes various aspects, such as depression, anxiety, chronic stress, and trauma, all of which are linked to a heightened risk of cardiovascular ailments.

Sarcopenia and cardiovascular disease (CVD) are both common conditions associated with aging and have recently become important issues for public health. Shared risk factors for both sarcopenia and CVD encompass a lack of physical activity [24, 25], inadequate blood flow to muscles [26], increased levels of inflammatory markers [27], oxidative stress [28], and hormonal imbalances [26]. A growing body of research has clarified the connections between sarcopenia and a variety of cardiovascular diseases, such as heart failure [29], acute myocardial infarction [30], atrial fibrillation [31], and coronary artery disease [32]. The occurrence of sarcopenia in individuals suffering from CVD varies from 10.1% to 68.9%, in contrast to the significantly lower rates found in the general population, which range from 2.9% to 28.6% [33]. Sarcopenia can lead to issues like obesity, insulin resistance, and increased chronic inflammation [34], thus making adults more susceptible to cardiovascular incidents. On the other hand, the persistent inflammatory conditions, malnutrition, and decreased physical activity seen in cardiac patients serve as catabolic factors, resulting in quicker muscle depletion and the development of sarcopenia [35]. Therefore, there is a reciprocal relationship between sarcopenia and CVD.

The elements of LC9 have a significant impact on sarcopenia (SP) via several mechanisms. The health habits linked to LC9, especially engaging in physical exercise and quitting smoking, could indirectly influence SP by decreasing inflammation and promoting metabolic well‐being [36, 37]. Engaging in physical activity serves as a vital intervention for alleviating symptoms of SP by enhancing blood circulation to essential muscles, boosting muscle mass and tone, lowering age‐related risks, and positively affecting parameters related to sarcopenia and physical capabilities in older adults [38]. Additionally, regular physical exercise fosters improved blood flow and decreases the likelihood of developing cardiovascular issues [39]. It aids in managing weight, enhances fat distribution, and diminishes fat buildup, which contributes to reducing inflammation and lessening the onset of sarcopenia [37]. Sleep duration plays a critical role in SP; a meta‐analysis indicated that minimal sleep duration (under 6 h) is correlated with an elevated risk of sarcopenia compared to the ideal sleep range (6–8 h). Furthermore, an excessive sleep duration (over 8 h) was notably associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia (OR: 1.52, 95% CI, 1.23, 1.88) [40]. Sufficient sleep is crucial for the body's restoration and recovery processes. During sleep, various growth functions are carried out most effectively, including protein synthesis, tissue regeneration, muscle repair, and hormone secretion, which all contribute to enhancing or rebuilding muscle fibers, function, and strength [38, 40]. Furthermore, quality sleep can help alleviate symptoms by promoting restorative rest and reducing anxiety or depression related to SP. Moreover, enhancements in health behaviors suggested by LC9, such as increased physical activity and dietary changes, may assist in lowering abdominal fat [41]. This, in turn, could indirectly decrease NPAR and improve metabolic health, thereby diminishing the risk of SP.

Interaction analysis revealed a significant interplay between LC9 and gender, with CVH scores exerting a more substantial impact on males than females (p < 0.001). This finding is consistent with recent research by You and Chen [42], which demonstrated that testosterone partially mediates the LC9‐sarcopenia association. The more pronounced effect in males may be attributed to their higher baseline testosterone levels and androgen receptor density, which are pivotal for activating muscle protein synthesis. Previous studies have established a significant correlation between muscle mass, serum free testosterone, and IGF1 in male subjects [43]. In contrast, lower basal testosterone levels in females may lead to a diminished anabolic effect, contributing to the age‐related decline in maximal neuromuscular performance [44]. Furthermore, as aging—a primary risk factor for sarcopenia—induces anatomical and functional changes, including reduced muscle mass and compliance [45], the decline in hormonal support may further exacerbate these processes.

There were notable interactions identified among NPAR, gender, and education levels. A particular investigation revealed that androgens are recognized for their role in reducing inflammation, whereas estrogens tend to promote inflammatory responses [46]. Additionally, socioeconomic status (SES), generally measured by factors like income, education, or job type, has been shown to associate with levels of C‐reactive protein (CRP) (Z = 0.12; 95% CI, 0.09–0.16) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) (Z = 0.15; 95% CI, 0.12–0.18). Those with lower SES tend to exhibit elevated systemic inflammation, and analyses of subgroups indicate that an increase in SES correlates with lower CRP and IL‐6 levels. As a result, pro‐inflammatory pathways could play a vital role in how socioeconomic disparities are reflected in differences in both physical and mental well‐being [47].

In this study, we utilized NPAR as a unified immuno‐nutritional biomarker rather than analyzing its components—neutrophil percentage and albumin—independently. This approach is predicated on the growing consensus that NPAR offers superior predictive value and stability across various clinical settings compared to either inflammatory or nutritional markers alone [48, 49]. By integrating the systemic inflammatory response with the nutritional status, NPAR provides a more holistic reflection of the ‘immuno‐nutritional’ landscape associated with musculoskeletal aging. While we acknowledge that parsing the independent contributions of neutrophils and albumin could offer additional mechanistic depth, the primary strength of NPAR lies in its synergistic ability to identify high‐risk individuals for sarcopenia. Future research employing specialized designs is encouraged to further elucidate the distinct biological driving factors within this mediating effect.

The potential mechanism through which NPAR influences the association between LC9 and Sarcopenia can be elaborated as follows: An elevation in NPAR reflects a state of systemic inflammation and nutritional depletion, which are closely linked to insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and hormonal fluctuations. Critically, elevated NPAR is associated with the systemic release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) [50]. These cytokines can activate the nuclear factor‐kappa B (NF‐κB) signaling pathway within skeletal muscle, which in turn upregulates the ubiquitin‐proteasome system and leads to accelerated muscle protein degradation. Furthermore, chronic exposure to IL‐6 and TNF‐α has been shown to impair myogenesis by inhibiting the activation of satellite cells. While previous studies have primarily utilized markers like CRP to study this link, our research is the first to identify NPAR as a robust ‘immuno‐nutritional’ mediator. We observed that NPAR partially mediated the relationship between LC9 and SP, with its impact being further modulated by significant interactions with gender and education, suggesting that the inflammatory burden on muscle mass may vary across different demographic and socioeconomic strata.

This study presents several benefits: (1) It marks the first effort to examine the relationship between LC9 and the occurrence of sarcopenia within the US population, implying that LC9 may serve as a valuable tool for diagnosing and assessing SP. This introduces fresh perspectives for SP diagnosis and evaluation. (2) As a new inflammatory marker, NPAR provides an economical approach for detecting SP in medical environments. (3) The research employs a comparatively large sample size, integrating NHANES data spanning from 2011 to 2018, which improves its representation of national population traits. (4) By constructing diverse models and conducting subgroup analyses while controlling for confounding variables, the findings illustrate a robust negative correlation between LC9 and SP, affirming both reliability and strength. (5) The subgroup analysis uncovers noteworthy interactions between LC9 and gender, along with NPAR's relationship to both gender and education level. This indicates that gender and educational background affect the link between cardiovascular health (CVH) and the prevalence of SP, highlighting the necessity for additional studies to validate these relationships. In summary, these advantages improve the study's integrity and reliability, laying a strong groundwork for comprehending the role of LC9 in sarcopenia prevention and management.

While this research is of great significance, it is crucial to recognize several limitations: (1) As a cross‐sectional study, it cannot determine a causal link between LC9 and sarcopenia (SP); larger prospective studies are necessary to elucidate this connection. (2) Although LC9 includes a “Physical Activity” metric, it does not distinguish between resistance and aerobic exercise, which have distinct impacts on muscle mass and strength. (3) The use of a single NPAR measurement may introduce random measurement error, potentially leading to an underestimation of the observed associations. (4) The data from NHANES (2011–2018) means results are primarily generalizable to U.S. adults; relevance to other geographic or ethnic cohorts may be restricted. (5) Specific nutritional drivers (e.g., individual protein intake) and alcohol consumption—which can inhibit muscle protein synthesis—were not adjusted for and may act as unmeasured confounders. (6) Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) and specific comorbidities like hypertension or diabetes were not included as covariates. We reasoned that these conditions are either downstream mediators of LC9 or integrated within its framework; adjusting for them could introduce multicollinearity or underestimate the total effect of LC9. (7) Despite adjusting for numerous variables, the influence of unidentified factors cannot be overlooked. Thus, while offering initial evidence, these findings require careful interpretation and further corroboration through robust longitudinal designs.

Conclusion

Ultimately, our research reveals a noteworthy negative correlation between LC9 and SP, with NPAR acting as a partial mediator in this connection. This investigation provides fresh perspectives on the prevention and treatment of SP, underscoring the importance of a holistic strategy aimed at improving cardiovascular health and addressing inflammation, which is highly valuable in reducing the likelihood of SP.
