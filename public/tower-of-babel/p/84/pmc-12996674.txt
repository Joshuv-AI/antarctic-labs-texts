Sangyod rice extract attenuates oleic acid–induced hepatic steatosis by modulating apoptotic, inflammatory, and lipid metabolic pathways
Authors: Furoida Moolsup, Wiwit Suttithumsatid, Wanwipha Woonnoi, Jirawat Saetan, Dusida Noothong, Supita Tanasawet, Wanida Sukketsiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFood-derived bioactive compounds have long been studied for their safety, cost-effectiveness, and potential industrial applicability. In this study, Sangyod rice extract from a traditional Thai rice variety was investigated using cell-based models to evaluate its antioxidant capacity and its effects on cellular oxidative status, lipid accumulation, and lipid metabolism–related signaling pathways. The extract was further assessed for its influence on inflammatory response, apoptosis-related markers, and proteins associated with lipid metabolism. The results demonstrated that treatment with the extract significantly reduced reactive oxygen species production and lipid accumulation in oleic acid–induced HepG2 cells. Furthermore, Sangyod rice extract modulated key regulators of lipid metabolism, including sterol regulatory element binding protein 1c (SREBP-1c), acetyl–CoA carboxylase (ACC), carnitine palmitoyltransferase I (CPT-1), fatty acid synthase (FASN), microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTTP), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) γ coactivator 1α (PGC-1α), and PPARα. In addition, the extract influenced Akt and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways. These findings suggest that Sangyod rice extract is a promising source of bioactive compounds with measurable functional activity in vitro and may have potential for further development in functional food and nutraceutical applications.Keywords: Oryza sativa, Ethanolic extract, SREBP-1c, PPAR-α pathway, Hepatocytes, Mitogen-activated protein kinase

Introduction

Rice is a major staple crop that sustains more than half of the global population (Fukagawa & Ziska, 2019). Beyond its role as a major energy source, certain rice varieties-particularly pigmented types-have attracted increasing attention due to their distinctive phytochemical composition and antioxidant properties (Zhao et al., 2025). In contrast to non-pigmented rice (e.g., white or light brown), pigmented varieties such as black, purple, and red rice contain higher concentrations of phenolic compounds in the bran layer. These include total phenolics, flavonoids, anthocyanins, and proanthocyanidins (Kaur et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2025). These compounds are predominantly present in bound forms and contribute to differences in antioxidant capacity observed among rice types. Phenolic acids such as trans-ferulic acid and p-coumaric acid are frequently reported as major constituents in pigmented rice, and extracts from these varieties generally exhibit stronger radical-scavenging activity in chemical assays (e.g., DPPH and FRAP) than non-pigmented counterparts (Avinash et al., 2024).

Sangyod rice (Oryza sativa L. var. indica) is a traditional red-pigmented cultivar indigenous to the Songkhla Lake Basin of southern Thailand. Compositional studies have identified γ-oryzanol, tocopherols, tocotrienols, phenolic acids, flavonoids, proanthocyanidins, lignans, B-complex vitamins, selenium, and phytic acid as major constituents (Hanchang et al., 2024; Itharat et al., 2016; Woonnoi et al., 2023). Compared with non-pigmented rice varieties, Sangyod rice has been reported to contain high levels of total phenolics and flavonoids, which are associated with enhanced antioxidant capacity in chemical assays (Hansakul et al., 2011). In addition to compositional characterization, Sangyod rice has been incorporated into various food products, particularly bakery formulations, where it enhances nutritional value and functional properties while maintaining consumer acceptability (Punfujinda et al., 2025; Yampuang et al., 2022). However, although its application in food systems has been explored at the product level, mechanistic studies linking its phenolic-rich composition to specific cellular signaling pathways remain limited.

Previous studies evaluating extracts from pigmented rice, including Sangyod rice, have reported modulation of inflammatory markers, oxidative status, and lipid accumulation–related parameters in both cellular and animal models (Hanchang et al., 2024; Min et al., 2010; Sompong et al., 2011; Woonnoi et al., 2023). However, many reports describe overall biological responses without critically linking specific compositional characteristics to defined intracellular signaling mechanisms. In particular, limited information is available regarding how phenolic-rich extracts from Sangyod rice influence lipid metabolism– and redox-related signaling at the molecular level under controlled in vitro conditions.

The oleic acid (OA)–induced HepG2 model is widely used to investigate lipid accumulation and associated signaling responses in hepatocyte-derived cells (Cui et al., 2010; Ma et al., 2023). Although this model does not replicate whole-food matrices or dietary exposure, it provides a controlled system for examining pathway-level modulation. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of Sangyod rice extract on intracellular lipid accumulation, oxidative status, apoptosis-related markers, and inflammatory signaling in this model, with emphasis on compound-associated modulation at the cellular and molecular levels.

Materials and methods

Sangyod rice extract and phytochemical analysis

The extraction of Sangyod rice was performed according to the method described by Woonnoi et al. (2023). Sangyod rice powder was macerated in 70% ethanol at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v). The mixture was shaken at 150 rpm at room temperature for 72 h using a shaker, then filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The solvent was evaporated under reduced pressure at 50 °C using a Büchi rotary evaporator. After preparation, the Sangyod rice extract was aliquoted into light-protected containers and stored at −20 °C until use to prevent repeated freeze–thaw cycles and minimize degradation of bioactive compounds.

The chemical composition of the Sangyod rice extract was analyzed using UHPLC-ESI-Q-TOF-MS/MS, as described in the supplementary methods. Total phenolic content was quantified using a modified Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric assay. Briefly, a 10 mg/mL extract was mixed with 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 7.5% sodium carbonate, incubated at room temperature for 1 h, and the absorbance was measured at 765 nm. Phenolic content was calculated from a gallic acid calibration curve and expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per g of dry extract. Total flavonoid content was determined using an aluminum chloride–based colorimetric assay. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm, and results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalents (QE) per g of dry weight based on a quercetin standard curve. However, the dosing concentrations used in this study were not calculated based on the quantified levels of individual compounds identified by UHPLC-MS. Instead, treatment concentrations were selected according to relevant literature and preliminary experiments evaluating cytotoxicity and biological activity to ensure cellular safety and experimental relevance.

Proximate composition

The extracts were subjected to proximate analysis to determine moisture, ash, fiber, fat, protein, and carbohydrate contents. Moisture content was determined by oven-drying the samples at 105 °C for 16 h, and dry matter content was calculated based on weight loss, following AOAC method 934.01 (AOAC, 2000). Total ash content was determined by incineration in a furnace at 550–600 °C until a constant weight was achieved, in accordance with AOAC method 942.05 (AOAC, 2000). Crude fiber content was measured using gravimetric analysis after chemical digestion, and the residue was quantified according to AOAC method 962.09 (AOAC, 2005). Fat content was determined using the Soxhlet extraction method as described in AOAC method 948.15 (AOAC, 2019). Crude protein content was analyzed using the Kjeldahl method following AOAC method 981.10 (AOAC, 2019). Carbohydrate content was calculated by difference from the measured proximate components (AOAC, 2000). All analyses were conducted in biological triplicate. The energy value was calculated using the Atwater general conversion factors of 4 kcal/g for protein, 9 kcal/g for fat, and 4 kcal/g for carbohydrates.

Cell culture

The HepG2 human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line was procured from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) and nurtured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), and 1% l-glutamine (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). The cells were maintained in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cultures were routinely monitored for morphology and growth characteristics, and mycoplasma contamination was assessed using a PCR-based detection method. All experiments were conducted using mycoplasma-free cells. For experimental procedures, cells were seeded into different plate formats (96-, 24-, or 6-well plates) depending on the specific requirements of each assay. Seeding densities were adjusted proportionally to the surface area of each well to obtain comparable cell confluence (approximately 70–80%) at the time of treatment. OA and extract treatments were prepared in the same culture medium across all plate formats, and treatments were initiated once cells reached similar confluence to minimize variability related to cell density.

Cell viability

HepG2 cells were plated in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well and exposed to Sangyod rice extract at concentrations ranging from 1 to 1000 μg/mL. The cells were then cultured for 24, 48, and 72 h to evaluate the cytotoxic impact of the extract at different time intervals. Each concentration and time point were assessed in technical triplicate across four independent biological experiments. Following treatment, the cells were incubated with a 0.5 mg/mL solution of 3(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) for 2 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the formazan product was dissolved, and the absorbance was gauged at 570 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., VT, USA).

Model of OA-induced HepG2 cells

An in vitro model was generated by exposing HepG2 cells to 0.4 mM OA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cells were incubated with OA for 24 h, a duration selected based on preliminary cell viability assessments to ensure sub-cytotoxic conditions (>80% cell viability). This time point was subsequently used for mechanistic studies to investigate early metabolic and molecular alterations associated with steatosis.

Cell viability of Sangyod rice extract in OA-induced HepG2 cells

HepG2 cells were plated at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in a 96-well plate. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to 0.4 mM OA with or without 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL of Sangyod rice extract for 24 h. After that, the MTT assay was employed to evaluate the cell viability of Sangyod rice in combating OA-induced steatosis in HepG2 cells. Each treatment group was conducted in technical triplicate across four independent biological experiments. Cells were incubated with 0.5 mg/mL MTT solution for 2 h at 37 °C, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., VT, USA).

Lipid accumulation by oil red O staining

HepG2 cells were cultured in a 24-well plate with 5 × 104 cells/well. Subsequently, the cells were treated with 0.4 mM OA in the presence or absence of 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL of Sangyod rice extract for 24 h. Each treatment was performed in technical triplicate across four independent biological experiments. The Oil Red O (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) staining procedure was performed with minor modifications based on our previous study (Woonnoi et al., 2023). Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. After fixation, the cells were rinsed three times with deionized (DI) water, incubated with 60% isopropanol for 10 min, and subsequently stained with 1% (w/v) Oil Red O solution for 30 min at room temperature. The stained cells were then washed five times with DI water to remove excess dye. Lipid droplets were visualized using a phase-contrast microscope (Olympus IX70 with DP50, Tokyo, Japan) to confirm intracellular lipid accumulation morphologically, and the oil red O content in the cells was extracted with 100% isopropanol. Lipid accumulation was quantified using Oil Red O staining followed by dye extraction and spectrophotometric measurement at 500 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., VT, USA). In the present study, absorbance values were not normalized to total protein content or cell number. Instead, lipid accumulation data were interpreted in conjunction with parallel cell viability results to ensure that the differences were not attributed to cytotoxicity or variations in cell density.

Triglyceride (TG) content

HepG2 cells were cultivated in a 6-well plate with 1 × 105 cells/well. Subsequently, the cells were treated with 0.4 mM OA with or without 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL of Sangyod rice extract for 24 h. Each treatment was performed in technical triplicate across four independent biological experiments. Following incubation, TG levels were assessed utilizing the TG Quantification Colorimetric/Fluorometric Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). Total protein content for each sample was determined, and TG levels were normalized to total protein to account for variations in cell number. The absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., VT, USA).

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) level by DCFH-DA assay

HepG2 cells were grown in a 96-well white plate with 5 × 104 cells/well and exposed to 0.4 mM OA with or without Sangyod rice extract (10, 50, and 100 μg/mL) for 24 h. The cells were then rinsed with PBS in each well. Each treatment was performed in technical triplicate across four independent biological experiments. Following treatment, cells were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and intracellular ROS levels were determined using the DCFH-DA oxidation assay. Cells were incubated with 50 μM DCFH-DA for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. After incubation, the fluorescence intensity of dichlorofluorescein (DCF) was immediately measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., VT, USA) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 nm and 530 nm, respectively. To account for variations in cell number and viability, fluorescence intensity values were normalized to relative cell viability obtained from parallel MTT assays. Normalized ROS levels were expressed relative to the untreated control group.

Cell apoptosis by Hoechst 33342 staining

HepG2 cells were incubated in a 24-well plate with 5 × 104 cells/well and treated with 0.4 mM OA with or without Sangyod rice extract (10, 50, and 100 μg/mL) for 24 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, rinsed with PBS, and subsequently stained with Hoechst 33342 (10 μg/mL) for 30 min at room temperature. Images were obtained from five randomly selected fields per well using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX70 with a DP50 camera; Tokyo, Japan). Apoptotic cells were quantified as the percentage of condensed or fragmented nuclei relative to the total number of nuclei per field using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) across four independent biological experiments.

mRNA expression by real time RT-PCR analysis

HepG2 cells were cultured in a P100 culture plate with 1 × 106 cells/well and treated with 0.4 mM OA with or without Sangyod rice extract (10, 50, and 100 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, RNA was extracted from the HepG2 cells using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at 260 and 280 nm. Samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered to have acceptable purity. RNA integrity was further confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis, demonstrating intact rRNA bands. One microgram of total RNA was treated with DNase I (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to remove genomic DNA contamination and subsequently reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA at 42 °C for 1 h using the SuperScript™ VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the SensiFAST™ SYBR® No-ROX Kit (Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in a real-time PCR detection system (BIOER, Hangzhou Bioer Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). Primer sequences are provided in the Supplementary Material (Table S1). Primer specificity was verified by melt curve analysis showing a single amplification peak, and amplification efficiency for each primer pair was confirmed to be within the acceptable range (90–110%). β-Actin was used as the internal reference gene, and its expression stability across treatment groups was confirmed by comparable Ct values. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method (ΔCt = Ct_target – Ct_reference; ΔΔCt = ΔCt_treated – ΔCt_control). All experiments were performed in four independent biological replicates.

Protein expression by Western blot analysis

HepG2 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well. The cells were then treated with 0.4 mM OA with or without 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL of Sangyod rice extract for 24 h. After the 24-h treatment, the cells were washed with PBS, lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail on ice for 30 min. Subsequently, cell lysates were harvested, and protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford protein assay. Briefly, 50 μg proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were then blocked for 4 h at room temperature with either 5% (w/v) non-fat dry milk or 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) prepared in 1× TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20). The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Bcl-2 (#3498), Bax (#14796), pro-caspase-3 (#9662), cleaved caspase-3 (#9661), ERK (ab36991), phospho-ERK (SC-81492), p38 (cat. no. 8690S), phospho-p38 (cat. no. 4511S), Akt (SC-81434), phospho-Akt (SC-514032), NF-κB (SC-8008), and β-actin (MA5–15452). All primary antibodies were diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer. After washing with 1× TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted 1:10,000 for 2 h at room temperature. Protein signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA), normalized to β-actin as the internal control from four independent biological experiments.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed using four independent biological replicates conducted on different days with independently cultured cells. Within each biological experiment, treatments were applied in technical triplicate (three wells per condition), and the averaged value from each experiment was used for statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) of the four biological replicates. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which is appropriate for small sample sizes. Homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene's test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Phytochemical content of Sangyod rice extract

The chemical constituents of the Sangyod rice extract were characterized using UHPLC-QTOF-MS analysis, and the identified compounds are listed in the Supplementary Material (Tables S2 and S3). The extract contained 84.01 ± 7.59 mg GAE/g of total phenolics and 30.05 ± 0.99 mg QE/g of total flavonoids (Table 1), with an extraction yield of 9.56% (w/w).

Proximate composition of Sangyod rice extract

The proximate composition of the Sangyod rice extract is summarized in Table 1. The moisture, ash, and crude fiber contents were 7.86 ± 0.76, 95.31 ± 0.11, and 8.16 ± 0.98% (w/w), respectively. The protein and fat contents of the extract were 6.10 ± 0.10 and 56.32 ± 0.27% (w/w), respectively. Carbohydrates were not detected in the Sangyod rice extract.

Sangyod rice extract increased cell viability and reduced ROS levels in OA-induced HepG2 cells

The MTT assay was performed to assess cytotoxicity and to establish appropriate concentrations of the Sangyod rice extract for subsequent functional assays. As shown in Fig. 1A, cell viability progressively decreased at higher concentrations (250–1000 μg/mL) in a time-dependent manner. The calculated IC₅₀ values at 24, 48, and 72 h were 480.50 ± 17.67, 342.35 ± 6.29, and 327.01 ± 10.22 μg/mL, respectively. Notably, concentrations below 100 μg/mL-maintained cell viability above 80% at all time points and did not promote abnormal proliferation in HepG2 cells. Based on these results, non-cytotoxic concentrations of 10, 50, and 100 μg/mL were selected for subsequent functional assays to ensure that the observed biological effects were not attributable to reduced cell viability. In Fig. 1B, a significant reduction in cell viability was observed in the OA-treated group; however, cell viability remained above 80%, indicating that OA induced the cells under sub-cytotoxic conditions. Treatment with 50 and 100 μg/mL of Sangyod rice extract for 24 h led to a notable increase in cell viability. Furthermore, the ROS levels in the OA group were markedly higher than those in the control group. Remarkably, treatment with Sangyod rice extract at concentrations of 50 and 100 μg/mL resulted in a significant reduction in ROS levels compared to the model group, as depicted in Fig. 1C. Based on these results, treatment with Sangyod rice extract increased cell viability and decreased ROS production in OA-induced HepG2 cells, and these effects were not dose-dependent. These findings suggest that Sangyod rice extract may be beneficial in improving cell viability and reducing ROS levels in HepG2 cells.

Sangyod rice extract inhibited apoptosis in OA-induced HepG2 cells by suppressing the Bcl-2–associated X protein (Bax) and caspase 3 pathway

Apoptotic nuclear morphology was evaluated using Hoechst 33342 staining to visualize chromatin condensation and nuclear fragmentation. Fig. 2A and B show that the percentage of apoptotic cells was significantly higher in the OA group than in the control group. In contrast, treatment of the OA group with Sangyod rice extract at 50 and 100 μg/mL significantly reduced the percentage of apoptotic cells compared to the untreated OA group. As Hoechst staining provides morphological and semi-quantitative evidence of apoptosis, the findings were further validated by assessing apoptosis-related protein expression using Western blot analysis. Fig. 2C-E demonstrate that protein levels of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 were significantly elevated in the OA group relative to the control group, whereas procaspase-3 expression was markedly reduced. Bcl-2 expression remained unaffected by OA treatment. Sangyod rice extract treatment decreased Bax and cleaved caspase-3 protein levels in OA-induced HepG2 cells and significantly increased procaspase-3 expression. All concentrations of Sangyod rice extract had no effect on Bcl-2 expression (Fig. 2C-D). These results indicate that Sangyod rice extract attenuates OA-induced apoptosis by modulating the Bax/caspase-3 pathway; however, these effects were not dose-dependent.

Sangyod rice extract reduced the inflammatory response in OA-induced HepG2 cells by suppressing the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway

In Fig. 3A-D, the gene expression levels of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL) 1beta (IL-1β), and IL-6 in the OA group increased approximately fivefold, while the expression of IL-10 decreased compared to the control group. Conversely, treatment with Sangyod rice extract at 50 and 100 μg/mL significantly reduced the gene expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, and increased IL-10 expression compared to the OA group. The expression of NF-κB protein in the OA group rose approximately twofold compared to the control group. However, NF-κB protein expression was significantly decreased in the OA group treated with Sangyod rice extract compared to the untreated OA group (Fig. 3E-F). These findings suggest that Sangyod rice extract suppresses inflammation in OA-induced HepG2 cells in a dose-independent manner by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway.

Sangyod rice extract reduced the lipid accumulation in OA-induced HepG2 cells

Oil Red O staining revealed a significant increase in the number of lipid droplets in the OA group compared to the control group (Fig. 4A and B). Conversely, treatment with 50 and 100 μg/mL of Sangyod rice extract for 24 h led to a reduction in the number of lipid droplets compared to the OA group, as depicted in Fig. 4A and B. Furthermore, the TG levels in the OA group were notably higher than those in the control group. Conversely, the TG levels in the OA group treated with Sangyod rice extract were significantly decreased compared to the untreated OA group, as illustrated in Fig. 4C.

Effect of Sangyod rice extract on lipid metabolism in OA-induced HepG2 cells

To confirm the association between OA-induced lipid accumulation in HepG2 cells and alterations in key lipid metabolism enzymes, mRNA levels of these enzymes were assessed using RT-qPCR. Fig. 5 demonstrates significant differences in the mRNA levels of these key enzymes between the OA and control groups. Specifically, the mRNA levels of SREBP-1c, ACC, and FASN were notably reduced in the Sangyod rice extract-treated group compared to the OA group (Fig. 5A-C). Conversely, the mRNA levels of CPT-1A and MTTP were significantly increased in the Sangyod rice extract-treated group compared to the OA group (Fig. 5D and F). Sangyod rice extract did not alter stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) mRNA levels compared with the OA group (Fig. 5E). The gene expression of lipoprotein lipase (LPL)-1 and LPL-2 were significantly increased, while PGC-1α and PPARα were decreased in the OA group compared to the control group (Fig. 6A-D). However, Sangyod rice extract led to an upregulation in the expression of PGC-1α and PPARα without affecting LPL-1 and LPL-2 levels compared to the OA group, as illustrated in Fig. 6A-D. These findings highlight the effects of Sangyod rice extract on key enzymes involved in lipid metabolism in a dose-independent manner, suggesting its role in modulating lipid metabolism in the context of steatosis.

Sangyod rice extract regulates lipid metabolism through the Akt and MAPK signaling pathways

Western blot assays revealed a significant upregulation in the phosphorylation of Akt, ERK, and p38 MAPK in the OA group (Fig. 7A-D). Treatment of OA-induced HepG2 cells with Sangyod rice extract notably decreased the phosphorylation of Akt, ERK, and p38 MAPK compared to the untreated OA group, as depicted in Fig. 7A-D. These results indicate that Sangyod rice extract reduces oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and lipid accumulation by modulating the Akt and MAPK signaling pathways in a dose-independent manner.

Discussion

Sangyod rice is a carbohydrate-rich staple that provides essential dietary energy, and its constituent compounds support its potential as a source of functional ingredients for food and nutraceutical applications (Punfujinda et al., 2025; Yampuang et al., 2022). Ethanolic extracts of Sangyod rice cultivated in the Songkhla Lake Basin of southern Thailand have demonstrated strong free radical–scavenging activity, as evidenced by high antioxidant capacity in FRAP and DPPH assays. This antioxidant activity is closely associated with its total phenolic content, flavonoids, and proanthocyanidins, highlighting its value as a natural source of antioxidants (Hanchang et al., 2024; Hansakul et al., 2011; Itharat et al., 2016; Punfujinda et al., 2025; Woonnoi et al., 2023). In the present study, the total phenolic and flavonoid contents were higher than those previously reported by Woonnoi et al. (2023). The moisture content observed in this study was lower than that reported for whole Sangyod rice in previous studies. Specifically, Sompong et al. (2011) reported a moisture content of 9.28% (w/w), whereas Chakkaravarthi et al. (2025) documented a higher value of 12.14% (w/w). Conversely, the ash and fiber contents of the Sangyod rice extract in the present study were substantially higher than those reported for whole grain samples. Sompong et al. (2011) found ash and fiber contents of 1.42% (w/w) and 4.51% (w/w), respectively, while Chakkaravarthi et al. (2025) reported corresponding values of 1.35% and 1.42% (w/w). The protein content of the extract in this study was lower than that of whole Sangyod rice, which is approximately 10% (10.36% reported by Sompong et al., 2011, and 10.11% by Chakkaravarthi et al., 2025). In contrast, the fat content was higher and the carbohydrate content was lower in the Sangyod rice extract compared with the values reported in previous studies (Chakkaravarthi et al., 2025; Sompong et al., 2011). These differences may be attributed to variations in the extraction methods used.

The present study employed HepG2 cells as an in vitro model to investigate lipid accumulation and associated signaling pathways. Although these cells are widely used due to their human origin, reproducibility, and ease of culture, they are derived from hepatocellular carcinoma and therefore do not fully represent the metabolic phenotype of normal hepatocytes (Blidisel et al., 2021). Compared with primary hepatocytes, they exhibit altered lipid and glucose metabolism, differences in mitochondrial function, and modified hormonal responsiveness. Moreover, this monoculture system lacks the complex multicellular interactions present in liver tissue (Huggett et al., 2022). However, HepG2 cells remain widely used to investigate lipid metabolism in in vitro models (Cui et al., 2010; Luo et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2023). Accordingly, future studies should validate these findings in more physiologically relevant models, such as primary hepatocytes or co-culture systems. Preliminary cytotoxicity screening showed that Sangyod rice extract at concentrations of 10–100 μg/mL did not adversely affect HepG2 cell viability, indicating its suitability for subsequent in vitro analyses. This observation is consistent with previous findings in adipocytes, hepatocytes and macrophages (Hanchang et al., 2024; Woonnoi et al., 2023; Woonnoi et al., 2025). OA, a predominant free fatty acid in dietary triglycerides, is commonly used to induce lipid accumulation and oxidative stress in hepatocyte models (Cui et al., 2010; Luo et al., 2022). In the present study, OA exposure reduced cell viability, increased ROS levels, and promoted intracellular triglyceride accumulation. Treatment with Sangyod rice extract improved cell viability and reduced ROS levels compared with the OA-exposed group, which may be attributed to the high phenolic content of the extract. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that the high phenolic and flavonoid contents of Sangyod rice extract may contribute to its antioxidant activity through mechanisms such as free radical scavenging and ROS neutralization (Hanchang et al., 2024; Srisawat et al., 2010; Woonnoi et al., 2023). Although normalization to total protein content or cell number would enhance the quantitative robustness of the assay, several previous studies using similar HepG2 steatosis models have employed absorbance-based Oil Red O quantification without protein normalization. Therefore, the present approach is consistent with commonly accepted methodologies in preliminary in vitro lipid accumulation studies (Cui et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings support the characterization of Sangyod rice extract as a food-derived source of phenolic-rich compounds with measurable antioxidant capacity under in vitro conditions. Although UHPLC-MS profiling confirmed the presence of phenolic and flavonoid compounds, the biological effects observed in this study reflect the activity of the whole extract rather than isolated constituents.

Lipid accumulation and increased ROS production are associated with enhanced apoptotic signaling in hepatocyte-based steatosis models (Geng et al., 2020; Hanchang et al., 2024). In the present study, apoptosis was initially evaluated by Hoechst 33342 staining to visualize the nuclear morphological changes. To strengthen the reliability of apoptosis assessment, the expression of key apoptotic regulators, including Bax, Bcl-2, pro-caspase-3, and cleaved caspase-3, was further examined by Western blot analysis. Although additional assays, such as Annexin V/PI staining with flow cytometry analysis, would provide more quantitative confirmation, these experiments were beyond the scope of the present study. OA exposure increased the proportion of apoptotic cells and altered the expression of proteins involved in apoptosis-related pathways. Treatment with Sangyod rice extract reduced the percentage of apoptotic cells compared with the OA-treated group and modulated the expression of key apoptosis-associated proteins, including Bax and caspase-3. These molecular alterations are consistent with the morphological observations obtained from Hoechst staining and collectively support the protective effect of the extract against OA-induced apoptosis. These observations are consistent with previous reports describing the modulation of apoptosis-related markers—such as ER stress markers (CHOP, GRP78, ATF4, ERN1) and apoptosis indicators (Bax/Bcl-2 and pro-caspase-3/cleaved caspase-3 ratios)—following exposure to Sangyod rice extract in experimental models (Hanchang et al., 2024; Woonnoi et al., 2025). Caspase-3 is a central effector in the apoptotic process, while Bax and Bcl-2 are regulatory proteins that influence mitochondrial membrane integrity and apoptotic signaling; the relative balance between these proteins is commonly used as an indicator of apoptosis status (He et al., 2018). Collectively, the findings from this study demonstrate that Sangyod rice extract modulates apoptosis-related protein expression in OA-treated HepG2 cells under in vitro conditions, supporting its characterization as a phenolic-rich food-derived extract with measurable bioactivity at the cellular level.

Inflammation plays a significant role in the initiation and advancement of hepatic steatosis. The presence of inflammatory cytokines can worsen liver damage and escalate hepatic steatosis to more severe liver conditions (Hanchang et al., 2024; Rafaqat et al., 2024). In the present study, OA–treated HepG2 cells exhibited increased gene expression of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, along with reduced expression of IL-10 compared with the control group. Following treatment with Sangyod rice extract, the gene expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in OA-induced HepG2 cells showed a decreasing trend, while IL-10 expression increased relative to the OA-treated group. Similar modulation of inflammatory markers has been reported in previous experimental studies evaluating Sangyod rice extract (Hanchang et al., 2024). In addition, NF-κB, a family of inducible transcription factors that regulates genes involved in immune and inflammatory responses (Liu et al., 2017; Sukketsiri et al., 2019), was downregulated in the extract-treated group compared with the OA group. Previous studies have demonstrated that Sangyod rice and its bran contain phenolic and flavonoid compounds (Aenglong et al., 2024; Hanchang et al., 2024; Woonnoi et al., 2023), and phenolic- and flavonoid-rich extracts have been associated with modulation of inflammatory and oxidative stress–related pathways (Chusongdam et al., 2024; Sukketsiri et al., 2019; Woonnoi et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings indicate that Sangyod rice extract modulates cytokine gene expression and NF-κB–related signaling in OA-treated HepG2 cells under in vitro conditions, consistent with its phenolic- and flavonoid-rich chemical composition and antioxidant capacity.

Lipid accumulation, particularly the buildup of intracellular TG, is a defining feature of OA–induced steatosis models in hepatocytes (Esler & Bence, 2019; Hanchang et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2023). In the present study, Sangyod rice extract reduced lipid accumulation in OA-treated HepG2 cells, as evidenced by a lower intensity of red–orange lipid droplets compared with the untreated OA group. In addition, intracellular TG levels were significantly decreased in the extract-treated group relative to the OA group. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing reduced TG levels and lipid accumulation following exposure to Sangyod rice extract in experimental models (Hanchang et al., 2024; Woonnoi et al., 2023). Key enzymes involved in lipid metabolism include SREBP-1c, ACC, CPT-1, FASN, SCD1, and MTTP, each of which plays a distinct role in fatty acid synthesis, oxidation, and lipoprotein assembly (Gowda et al., 2023). SREBP-1c regulates genes associated with fatty acid and triglyceride synthesis (Xu et al., 2013). ACC catalyzes the conversion of acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA, a critical step in fatty acid biosynthesis (Ma et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022). CPT-1 is a rate-limiting enzyme in long-chain fatty acid oxidation (Ma et al., 2023), whereas FASN and SCD1 contribute to fatty acid synthesis and modification (Fhu & Ali, 2020; Kotronen et al., 2009). MTTP is involved in very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) assembly and secretion (Heeren & Scheja, 2021). Coordinated regulation of these enzymes is essential for maintaining lipid metabolic balance at the cellular level. In this study, OA treatment altered the mRNA expression of these lipid metabolism–related genes, whereas Sangyod rice extract treatment modulated their expression profiles. Specifically, the mRNA levels of SREBP-1c, ACC, and FASN were reduced, while CPT-1 A and MTTP expression levels were elevated in the extract-treated group compared with the OA group. These gene expression patterns are consistent with reduced intracellular TG accumulation observed under the same conditions. Similar modulation of lipid metabolism–related genes has been reported in previous studies evaluating Sangyod rice extract (Hanchang et al., 2024; Woonnoi et al., 2023). Sangyod rice extract has been reported to contain high levels of phenolic and flavonoid compounds (Hanchang et al., 2024; Woonnoi et al., 2023), which are known to interact with lipid metabolism–related pathways in cellular systems. Collectively, these findings characterize Sangyod rice extract as a phenolic- and flavonoid-rich food-derived material with measurable effects on lipid metabolism–associated gene expression and triglyceride accumulation in an in vitro hepatocyte model. However, as the present results are primarily based on mRNA analyses, further studies at the protein level (e.g., Western blotting or proteomic approaches) are necessary to confirm corresponding changes in protein expression and activity.

In addition, the expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis, including LPL-1, LPL-2, PGC-1α, and PPARα, was evaluated. LPL plays a key role in hydrolyzing circulating triglycerides into free fatty acids (Heeren & Scheja, 2021; Rodríguez-Hernández et al., 2023). Alterations in LPL gene expression may influence fatty acid flux and intracellular lipid handling in hepatocytes. PGC-1α and PPARα are transcriptional regulators that coordinate pathways related to mitochondrial function and fatty acid oxidation (Cheng et al., 2018; Todisco et al., 2022), contributing to the maintenance of cellular energy balance. In the present study, the OA-treated group exhibited increased mRNA expression of LPL-1 and LPL-2 and a decreasing trend in PGC-1α and PPARα expression compared with the control group. Treatment with Sangyod rice extract upregulated PGC-1α and PPARα expression relative to the OA group, while LPL-1 and LPL-2 levels were not significantly altered. These findings further demonstrate that Sangyod rice extract modulates the expression of genes associated with lipid metabolism and energy regulation in an in vitro hepatocyte model. The observed upregulation of PGC-1α and PPARα is consistent with coordinated changes in lipid metabolism–related pathways and aligns with the phenolic- and flavonoid-rich composition previously reported for Sangyod rice extract (Hanchang et al., 2024; Woonnoi et al., 2023).

The Akt and MAPK signaling pathways are important regulators of cellular lipid metabolism and are frequently examined in hepatocyte-based lipid accumulation models. Dysregulation of Akt, ERK, and p38 MAPK signaling has been associated with altered expression of genes involved in fatty acid synthesis and oxidation, contributing to triglyceride accumulation under in vitro conditions (Pan et al., 2023). Akt is a central mediator of glucose and lipid metabolism and can influence the expression of lipogenic enzymes such as ACC and FASN (Götting et al., 2020). ERK signaling has been linked to the regulation of SREBP-1c expression (Li et al., 2023), while p38 MAPK is responsive to cellular stress and has been reported to interact with lipid metabolism–related pathways, including those involving SREBP-1c and PPARs (Lawan & Bennett, 2017). In the present study, Western blot analysis showed increased phosphorylation of Akt, ERK, and p38 MAPK proteins in OA-treated cells compared with the control group. Treatment with Sangyod rice extract reduced the phosphorylation levels of these signaling proteins relative to the OA group. Similar modulation of Akt and MAPK pathway activation has been reported in previous studies evaluating Sangyod rice extract (Hanchang et al., 2024; Woonnoi et al., 2023). Considering its reported phenolic- and flavonoid-rich composition of Sangyod rice extract (Aenglong et al., 2024; Hanchang et al., 2024; Woonnoi et al., 2023), the observed changes in signaling protein phosphorylation may be associated with its antioxidant properties and interactions with lipid metabolism–related pathways. Overall, these findings further support the characterization of Sangyod rice extract as a food-derived source of bioactive compounds capable of modulating cellular signaling events related to lipid metabolism in an in vitro model.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Sangyod rice extract modulates the expression of key genes and proteins associated with lipid metabolism, including SREBP-1c, ACC, CPT-1, FASN, SCD1, MTTP, PGC-1α, and PPARα, as well as Akt- and MAPK-related signaling pathways in an oleic acid–induced HepG2 cell model (Fig. 8). These findings provide molecular-level evidence of the extract's bioactivity and support its characterization as a phenolic- and flavonoid-rich food-derived material with measurable antioxidant capacity and effects on lipid metabolism–related pathways in vitro. However, the present study was conducted using an in vitro oleic acid–induced HepG2 cell model, which represents a simplified system for examining cellular responses to lipid overload. While this model is useful for mechanistic evaluation at the molecular level, it does not replicate the complexity of whole-food matrices, gastrointestinal digestion, metabolism, or realistic dietary exposure conditions. Therefore, the findings cannot be directly extrapolated to efficacy in a dietary context. In addition, the present work does not include application-based assessments in food systems. No experiments were performed to evaluate the incorporation of Sangyod rice extract into specific food products, nor were processing stability, storage stability, sensory impact, matrix interactions, or dose feasibility under practical consumption scenarios examined. These factors are critical for determining functional applicability in real food formulations. Furthermore, although gene and protein expression analyses provide insight into pathway modulation, the study does not include comprehensive compound isolation, structural characterization of individual active constituents, or evaluation of their stability and bioaccessibility after simulated digestion. Future research should therefore focus on (i) detailed chemical profiling and identification of key active compounds, (ii) assessment of stability during food processing and storage, (iii) evaluation of bioaccessibility and bioavailability using in vitro digestion models, and (iv) validation within actual food matrices to determine practical functionality and formulation feasibility.
