The association between dietary insulin index and load with depression, anxiety and stress in university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hossein Nassaji-Jahromi, Sanaz Joekar, Akram Yazdani, Azadeh Aminianfar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mental disorders are considered as one of the most important concerns among university students, potentially impacting their quality of life and academic performance. Limited data are available regards the association between dietary insulin index (DII) and dietary insulin load (DIL) and mental disorders especially in university students. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between DII and DIL with depression, anxiety, and stress among students affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences. This cross-sectional study included 330 university students from Kashan University of Medical Sciences in 2023. A validated Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21) was applied to assess the mental health of participants. Dietary intakes were obtained using a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ-117). Multivariable analyses were performed to adjust for potential confounding factors. P < 0.05 was considered as significant. Participants’ mean age and BMI were 21.4 years and 22.8 kg/m2, respectively. In this study, after adjusting for a wide range of possible confounding variables, individuals who were in the highest quartile of DIL had 45% significantly lower odds for depression than those in the lowest quartile (95%CI: 0.31, 0.99). However, there was no statistically significant association between DII and depression (95%CI: 0.4, 1.28). No other statistically significant relationship was observed between DII, DIL, and anxiety and stress in university students (P < 0.05). In conclusion, DIL may be associated with depression in university students. Higher DIL is associated with higher accessibility for tryptophan in brain cells, which could be related to a better mood. Further studies with a larger sample size and stronger designs, such as cohort studies or clinical trials, are needed to confirm our results.

Introduction

Mental disorders are one of the most important health problems that could affect cognitive functions, emotions, and behavior regulation1. Depression, anxiety, and stress represent the most important mental health challenges2. Depression is classified as a mood disorder characterized by persistent sadness, disinterest, feelings of emptiness or irritability, and alterations in appetite and sleep patterns3,4. Anxiety often co-occurs with depression, amplifying its detrimental effects5. It is characterized by anticipating a real or perceived threat or danger in the future5. Stress is a condition of mental or emotional pressure caused by difficult or challenging situations6. These mental disorders rank among the top ten causes of disease burden globally7, which are linked to disability, early mortality, and a higher risk of chronic diseases, such as metabolic syndrome and Alzheimer’s disease8–11. There are various factors such as being away from home, entering a new environment, and financial problems which could affect the mental situation of students12. The pooled global prevalence of anxiety and depression was estimated at 45% and 48%, respectively in medical students in 202413. In Iran, the prevalence of these conditions among medical students was similarly high, with 44% experiencing anxiety and 43% reporting depression, in 202414.

Several risk factors contribute to the development of mental disorders, including genetic predispositions, sedentary lifestyles, poor health status, and diet15,16. Diet is one of the most modifiable risk factors for chronic diseases, including mental disorders17. The dietary insulin index (DII) and dietary insulin load (DIL) are commonly used indicators to examine the linkage between diet and chronic diseases. The food insulin index (FII) classifies foods based on their insulin response, providing a practical tool for evaluating the dietary impact on insulin homeostasis and the risk of diseases associated with insulin resistance in nutritional epidemiology18. The FII is calculated as the insulin response to the test food compared to an equivalent caloric amount of a reference food (glucose or white bread). This index accounts not only for carbohydrates but also for the quantity and quality of protein and fat consumed. Unlike the glycemic index (GI), it also considers foods with low or negligible carbohydrate content. DIL is computed by summing the product of FII, energy content, and frequency of consumption of food items18. Finally, DII is obtained by dividing DIL by total daily energy intake18. Limited data are available on the association between DII and DIL and mental disorders, particularly in university students. Their results are also very inconsistent. A study conducted by Anjom-Shoae et al. in 2020 demonstrated a significant positive association between DII and DIL and the likelihood of depression in adult women. However, no significant relationship was observed between DII and DIL and depression in men. Additionally, neither DII nor DIL showed a significant association with anxiety among the study participants19. In contrast, findings by Darand et al. indicated that adults in the highest quartile of DIL had 31% lower odds of stress compared to those in the lowest quartile. No other significant associations were found between DII, DIL, and depression or anxiety2.

To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to investigate the association between the insulin index and insulin load of the whole diet and mental health in university students. There is very limited and controversial data regarding the linkage of DII and DIL with mental health in other populations. University students are typically at a higher risk of experiencing psychological stress. In addition to this, the prevalence of mental disorders in Iran is rising20. Thus, investigating the relationship between modifiable risk factors associated with this health issue is imperative. Accordingly, this study aims to investigate the relationships of DII and DIL with depression, anxiety, and stress among university students in Iran.

Methods

Participants

In 2023, we conducted a cross-sectional study among university students at Kashan University of Medical Sciences in Kashan, Iran. To determine the required sample size for this study, we used the formula for logistic regression analysis as following.

The calculation was based on the findings of the study by Anjom-Shoae et al.19, assuming an odds ratio (OR) of 1.54, a type I error rate (α) of 0.05, and a type II error rate (β) of 0.10 (corresponding to 90% statistical power). Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 300 participants. The calculation was performed using PASS software. To account for an anticipated dropout rate of 10%, the final target sample size was increased to 330 participants.

University students were selected using a stratified cluster sampling method. Subsequently, the required number of individuals from each faculty was randomly selected using proportional allocation. Participants were included if they were: (1) students affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences, (2) being at age 18 to 65 years, (3) not being at exams periods, (4) not have any chronic diseases such as mental disorders, heart disorders, diabetes, thyroid disorders, etc., (5) not taking medications related to chronic diseases, particularly psychiatric medications, (6) no experiences of grief, emotional failure, or divorce in the preceding three months, (7) not adherence to any special diet in the last three months, (8) being a non-smoker, and (9) not being pregnant or breastfeeding. The exclusion criteria were: (1) failure to cooperate with the researcher until the end of the study and (2) under-reporting (less than 800 kcal per day) or over-reporting (more than 4200 kilocalories per day) of daily energy intake. All participants provided written informed consent. This study was approved by the Research Council of the Faculty of Medicine, Kashan University of Medical Sciences in Kashan, Iran (ethical code: IR.KAUMS.MEDNT.REC.1402.115 ). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Assessment of dietary intakes

Dietary information was collected using a 117-item validated and reliable semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)21. This FFQ included a comprehensive list of commonly consumed foods in Iran, along with standardized portion sizes. Participants were instructed to report the frequency and quantity of their consumption for each food item, using options ranging from “never” or “less than once a month” to “6 times or more per day.” The reported frequencies were subsequently converted into daily consumption values in grams. The total intake of energy, macronutrients, and micronutrients was calculated using USDA databases22 and the Nutritionist IV software, which was specifically adapted to include Iranian foods.

Calculation of dietary insulin index and load

The FII for various foods was derived from prior research publications18. To calculate the DIL, we first computed the insulin load for each food item using the formula: Food Insulin Load = FII × Energy Content of Food (kcal/day). Next, the DIL was determined by summing the insulin loads for all food items. The average DII was then calculated by dividing the DIL by the total daily energy intake.

Assessment of depression, anxiety, and stress

An Iranian-validated abbreviated version of the self-report questionnaire for depression, anxiety, and stress (DASS 21), was used to assay mental health23. This questionnaire includes seven questions for each subscale (depression, anxiety, and stress). Participants responded using a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Does not apply to me at all) to 3 (Applies to me very much or most of the time). The total score was calculated by summing the scores of each subscale. Based on the documents, since the full version of the DASS contains 42 items, the final score should be multiplied by two24. Then the scores of depression, anxiety, and stress ranged from 0 to 42. The total score was calculated by summing the scores for each subscale. In addition, the DASS-21 scoring provides cutoff scores for defining normal (0–9 for depression, 0–7 for anxiety, and 0–14 for stress), mild (10–13 for depression, 8–9 for anxiety, and 15–18 for stress), moderate (14–20 for depression, 10–14 for anxiety, and 19–25 for stress), severe (21–27 for depression, 15–19 for anxiety, and 26–33 for stress), and very severe (> 28 for depression, > 20 for anxiety, > 34 for stress)25.

Evaluation of other variables

Demographic and medical information of all participants was collected through a general questionnaire and face-to-face interviews. This questionnaire assessed demographic details such as age, college, university semester, home ownership status, marital status, and place of residence (dormitory, native, or living alone). It also assessed medical history, including family history of chronic and psychological disorders, as well as drug usage and dietary supplement intake. Weight was measured without shoes and in light clothing using a Seca digital scale, with an accuracy of 0.1 kg. To prevent errors, the scale was calibrated daily using standard weights of 1 kg and 2 kg. Height was measured with a wall-mounted stadiometer while the participants stood barefoot, with their heels against the wall and they were looking straight ahead. Height measurements were recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. Physical activity levels were assessed using the validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire - Short Form (IPAQ-SF)26. This questionnaire measures the duration and frequency of light, moderate, and vigorous activities lasting more than 10 min over a week, as well as the time spent sitting. Vigorous and moderate physical activities were defined as those that raise heart rate and induce sweating. The metabolic equivalents (METs) values assigned were 3.3 for low, 4.0 for moderate, and 8.0 for vigorous activities. Each participant’s activity score was calculated by multiplying the MET value by the activity’s duration (in minutes) and frequency (per day). Finally, participants’ physical activity levels were categorized into three groups based on their weekly activity scores: low (< 600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–1500 MET-min/week), and high (> 1500 MET-min/week).

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. To eliminate the effect of energy on the DII and DIL, adjusted values were calculated using the residual method27. To investigate the exact associations between DII and DIL and psychological disorders, these indices were categorized into quartiles using SPSS. The chi-square test was applied to evaluate relationships between qualitative variables and DII and DIL, while ANOVA (Analysis of variance) was used to examine associations with quantitative variables. To adjust for confounding variables we used ANCOVA (Analysis of covariance). Ordinal regression analysis was performed to explore the relationship between DII and DIL and the odds of having stress, anxiety, and depression. Three models were constructed for adjustment: Model 1 controlled for age and sex. Model 2 included additional adjustments for marital status, family history of psychological illness, family history of chronic illness, medication use, and physical activity (measured in METs). Finally, Model 3, the fully adjusted model, incorporated BMI. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

The mean ± SD values of DII and DIL among the study participants were 41.9 ± 5.73 and 92,075 ± 31,923, respectively. The participants had a mean age of 21.4 years; 31.5% were male, and 68.5% were female. Their mean BMI was 22.8 kg/m². Compared to participants in the lowest DIL, those in the highest quartile of DIL reported lower drug usage. Similarly, for DIL, students in the highest quartile of DII reported lower drug usage compared to those in the lowest quartile. No other significant associations were observed between DII or DIL and other variables such as age, weight, height, BMI, number of children in the family, physical activity situation, gender, college, academic term, marital status, place of residence, family history of psychological or chronic diseases, history of taking nutritional supplements, home ownership status, PAL (Physical Activity Level), and depression, anxiety, and stress classifications among participants.

Table 2 indicates the dietary intake of participants across quartiles of DII and DIL. After adjusting for age, gender, and daily energy intake (energy intake adjusted only for age and gender), participants in the highest quartile of DII had significantly higher intakes of carbohydrates, protein, fiber, alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), iron, calcium, zinc, magnesium, and vitamins A, E, K, C, B1, B2, B3, B6, and B9 compared to those in the lowest quartile (P < 0.05). Additionally, these participants consumed significantly less fat, saturated fatty acids, monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) and linoleic acid (P < 0.05). No other significant relationships were observed between DII and energy, cholesterol, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), selenium, vitamin D, or vitamin B12 (P > 0.05).

Discussion

In the present cross-sectional study, after adjusting for potential confounders, university students with higher DIL had significant reduced odds for depression compared to those with lower DIL. However, no other significant associations were observed between DII and depression. In addition, anxiety and stress revealed no significant association between DII and DIL.

In addition, in our study, both the lowest and the highest quartiles of DII and DIL exhibited higher mean energy intakes compared with the middle quartiles, producing a U-shaped pattern. This may be attributable to differences in the sources of dietary energy in these quartiles. Participants in the lowest quartile of DII and DIL may have consumed energy-dense foods eliciting a low insulin response, such as those rich in fats and proteins (e.g., nuts, oils, and animal products), which could explain their relatively high caloric intake despite low insulin index and load. Conversely, those in the highest quartile likely consumed diets high in refined carbohydrates and added sugars, leading to both elevated caloric intake and high insulin index and load.

We also observed that DII and DIL were generally inversely associated with depression, anxiety, and stress scores across quartiles, with the lowest values occurring in Q2 or Q3. Only the inverse association between DIL and stress was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Slight increases in scores in Q4 compared to Q3, although still lower than those in Q1, are likely due to normal random fluctuations in the data and the limited sample size in each quartile, rather than indicating an actual reversal of the overall trend.

To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to examine the relationship of DII and DIL with mental disorders in university students. An increasing body of evidence suggests that mental disorders are associated with disability, premature mortality, and increased risk of chronic diseases, including metabolic syndrome, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and Alzheimer’s disease8–11. Both DII and DIL have previously been linked to various chronic diseases28,29, but studies exploring their relationship with mental disorders are limited. In our study, university students with higher DIL had 45% significant lower odds of depression, while DII showed no significant association with depression. Only two previous studies have addressed this association, which yielded conflicting findings. For example, a cross-sectional study by Anjom-Shoae et al. found a significant positive association between DIL and DII and the odds of depression in adult women, whereas no significant association was found in men19. In contrast, in the study by Darand et al., no significant association was observed between DII and DIL with depression2. There are some differences between this study and previous ones. Anjom-Shoae et al. used the validated Iranian version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) for mental disorders assessment19, while the DASS-21 questionnaire was applied in our study. Furthermore, we applied our investigation on university students whose are at risk of mental disorders, which was different from healthy adults in previous studies.

In case of anxiety and stress, neither DII nor DIL was significantly related to these mental disorders. Similarly, in a study by Anjom-Shoae et al., neither DII nor DIL was significantly associated with anxiety or psychological distress in men or women19. In addition, individuals the highest quartile of DIL had a non-significant 12% reduced odds for stress than those in the lowest quartile; in another study. Furthermore, no significant associations were found between either DII or DIL and the risk of anxiety2. In contrast with our results, in the study conducted by Darand et al., adults in the highest quartile of DII were 61% less likely to experience stress compared to those in the lowest quartile2.

Several biological mechanisms may explain the observed association between DIL and depression. Insulin increases the plasma tryptophan-to-large neutral amino acid (LNAA) ratio by promoting the uptake of LNAA (excluding tryptophan) into skeletal muscles. This mechanism facilitates increased tryptophan transport across the blood-brain barrier, enhancing serotonin synthesis30,31. Serotonin, a key neurotransmitter, is involved in regulating mood, psychological states, and sleep patterns32. Furthermore, insulin can stimulate the activity of tryptophan hydroxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme in serotonin biosynthesis, particularly in the pineal gland and other brain tissues33. Additionally, insulin may suppress monoamine oxidase, the enzyme responsible for degrading serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. This inhibition may prolong the availability of these key mood-related neurotransmitters and enhance affective stability33,34. Also, the consumption of foods rich in carbohydrates and sugars has been shown to attenuate hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity and reduce cortisol secretion, thereby potentially alleviating perceived stress, which is commonly reported among university students35,36. Finally, serotonin also serves as a biochemical precursor to melatonin, the primary hormone involved in circadian rhythm regulation37. By increasing brain serotonin levels, insulin may indirectly improve sleep quality, an important factor for mental health in university students38.

Inflammation and oxidative stress have been reported to negatively impact mental health by disrupting the metabolism and signaling of neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine39,40. Some evidence suggests that insulin may have anti-inflammatory effects primarily through inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway, resulting in the suppression of intranuclear NF-κB activity in peripheral blood mononuclear cells and a subsequent decrease in the expression and plasma concentrations of pro-inflammatory chemokines such as MCP-1 (monocyte chemoattractant protein-1) and RANTES (regulated on activation normal T-cell expressed and secreted)41. Also, insulin activates the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) signaling pathway, which plays a key role in attenuating inflammatory responses42. In addition, certain insulinogenic foods, particularly those containing slowly digestible starches and fermentable carbohydrates and fibers, may influence mental health through modulation of the gut microbiome43. Such dietary components can promote the growth of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing gut bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium, Roseburia, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii43–45. SCFAs, especially butyrate, are known to exert anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and mood-stabilizing effects through gut–brain axis signaling, enhancement of blood–brain barrier integrity, and upregulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)46,47.

Our study has several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, it was the first to investigate the association between DII and DIL and mental disorders in university students. We utilized validated questionnaires to assess mental health and dietary intake, minimizing measurement errors. Confounding factors, including age, sex, marital status, family history of psychological or chronic illnesses, medication usage, physical activity, and body mass index, were extensively controlled. Data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviews by trained nutritionists to reduce recall bias. In this study, we utilized ordinal regression to evaluate the relationship between the DII and DIL and the severity of mental disorders. Notably, this type of analysis has not been conducted in previous similar studies. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. In addition, a FFQ was used for dietary assessments, which may usually have recall bias48, although, in this study, a trained expert was used to complete the questionnaires to reduce recall bias. Also, despite using a validated FFQ for dietary intake evaluation, the potential for misclassification remains. However, energy-adjusted DII and DIL were utilized to diminish misclassification of study subjects. Although, a wide range of confounding variables were adjusted in this study, there may other confounding variables which could affect the associations.

According to the findings of this study, it seems that consuming a diet with a higher insulin load may be associated with improving depression in university students. However, DII was not significantly associated with depression. Neither DII nor DIL showed significant associations with anxiety or stress. Given the limited available evidence, further studies with stronger designs should be conducted in this field to confirm our findings. It is also suggested that inflammatory and oxidative stress indices could be measured along with the DII and DIL to determine the more precise relationship between the DII and DIL and mental health.
