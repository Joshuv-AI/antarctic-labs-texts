Factors associated with low short-term memory development among pupils in public primary schools: evidence from Benin republic
Authors: Omonlola K Elvire Assogba, Isaac Luginaah, Yann Eméric Madodé, Anelia Merveille Ebilola Francisco, Jahdiel Kossou, Jaurès Lokonon, Fifali Sam Ulrich Bodjrenou, Waliou Amoussa Hounkpatin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShort-term memory, the ability to temporarily hold and manipulate information, plays a critical role in learning and academic performance. This study aims to assess factors associated with low short-term memory in 524 children aged 8–14 years who attended public government schools in the Benin.MethodologySixteen randomly selected schools located in peri-urban areas in northern and southern Benin participated in this cross-sectional study. Short-term memory was assessed via the WISC digit span tool. Food insecurity was evaluated with the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS). Socioeconomic and health data were collected through a digitalized questionnaire. Nutritional status was determined through anthropometric measurements and a hemoglobin test.ResultsThe prevalence of below average short-term memory was 53.47% in the Northern and 28.81% in the southern Benin. In the northern region, 5.56%, 8.68%, and 13.19% of the pupils were, mildly, moderately, and severely food-insecure, respectively. In the southern region, 22.46%, 6.36%, and 3.38% of the pupils fell into the same categories, respectively. Multivariate logistic regression revealed that severe food insecurity (OR = 3.461, p < 0.05), moderate and severe thinness (OR = 1.680, p < 0.005), poverty (OR = 2.916, p < 0.001), caregivers’ illiteracy (OR = 1.89, p < 0.05), pupils’ age (OR = 0.864, p < 0.05) and being from the northern regions (OR = 2.263, p < 0.05) were significant predictors of low short-term memory ability.ConclusionEven with access to a school canteen program, pupils from northern regions, malnourished, food-insecure and from socioeconomically disadvantaged households are more likely to exhibit low short-term memory skills. Policymakers should prioritize the implementation of evidence-based interventions and policies aimed at alleviating food insecurity, malnutrition, and poverty, alongside initiatives to enhance infrastructure and essential services, including access to electricity in underserved and remote areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01182-3.Keywords: Primary pupils, Food insecurity, Short-term memory, School feeding programme, Nutritional status

Introduction

Memory plays a pivotal role in human cognition, serving as the foundation for learning and reasoning. In educational contexts, memory is critical for retaining information, solving problems, and fostering intellectual growth. Short-term memory (STM), often referred to as working memory, acts as a temporary mental workspace where information is held and processed for immediate use. In contrast, long-term memory (LTM) stores information over extended periods, allowing for the accumulation of knowledge and skills. The interplay between STM and LTM is essential for academic achievement, as information from STM is often consolidated into LTM through effective learning processes [1]. This foundational role of memory highlights its importance as a key component of human cognition, essential for driving sustainable development, particularly in contexts where education catalyzes broader social and economic progress.

Learning processes are the foundation for human capital development, an indispensable driver for sustainable development, particularly in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) where education serves as a catalyst for social and economic development [2]. Despite global efforts, such as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), aimed at ensuring inclusive and equitable quality education (SDG 4), many countries, including Benin [3], continue to face substantial challenges in their educational systems. The Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) of 2015 revealed a narrow focus on access to education while neglecting quality, prompting the inclusion of comprehensive targets in the SDGs. However, in Benin, approximately 60% of the population remains uneducated, with alarming school dropout rates and low literacy levels among primary school pupils [4, 5]. Factors such as poverty, malnutrition, and food insecurity may significantly contribute to these challenges [5–7].

Benin's socio-economic profile underscores its vulnerability to food insecurity and malnutrition, which have direct implications for cognitive development. With an economy reliant on agriculture and trade, compounded by gender dynamics and a political system that is more focused on urban areas, rural and peri-urban regions face heightened vulnerability to climatic shocks, unstable incomes, and limited access to essential services, including electricity [8–10]. Food insecurity, defined as insufficient access to adequate food for an active and healthy life, often leads to nutrient deficiencies among vulnerable populations such as schoolchildren resulting in malnutrition. Chronic malnutrition, affecting 36.5% of children under five in Benin, often extends into adolescence, exerting profound impacts on linear growth and cognitive development [8]. This exacerbates the cycle of poverty, food insecurity, and poor educational outcomes [9].

The National Integrated School Feeding Program (NISFP), launched in Benin in 2017, represents a critical intervention aimed at addressing hunger and supporting education [11]. By improving school enrollment, attendance, and retention, the program seeks to enhance academic performance and contribute to SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) [11]. Unlike earlier initiatives, the NISFP incorporates community participation to ensure sustainability. However, limited research has explored its impact on cognitive abilities, particularly memory development, which is a key determinant of learning outcomes [11]. Existing studies in Ghana, South Africa, Malawi [11, 12], and Ethiopia [13] have demonstrated a positive influence of adequate nutrition on children’s cognitive capacities, suggesting that a change in feeding practices could yield significant effects on schoolchildren’s cognition.

On the other hand, educational disparities in Benin are also influenced by sociocultural and environmental factors. Approximately 70% of children in Benin are unable to understand a short written text by the end of primary school [4]. Urban areas exhibit unique challenges, including multilingual education environments, which contribute to performance disparities between urban and rural schools. Additionally, early-life malnutrition has been consistently linked to deficits in cognitive development [14], with evidence highlighting its detrimental effects on school performance and intellectual skills. Despite these findings, dedicated research focusing on the neurocognitive dimensions of education and nutrition in Beninese primary school pupils remains scarce.

This study aims to assess factors associated with low STM ability. We hypothesize that severity of food security and household poverty are associated with low STM ability. By exploring these dynamics, the study seeks to inform interventions and policies that address both nutritional deficiencies and cognitive challenges, ultimately contributing to improved human capital development in Benin.

Theoretical framework

This study adopts an integrative theoretical framework that combines the World Health Organization’s (WHO) Social Determinants of Health Framework (2008) [15] and Pollitt’s [16] frameworks on undernutrition and cognitive development. Cognitive development in the context of food insecurity is a multidimensional process influenced by both biological and environmental factors. Pollitt’s framework explores how inadequate nutritional intake, socioeconomic stressors, and limited cognitive stimulation interact to shape developmental outcomes [15, 16]. Food insecurity not only leads to nutritional deficiencies but also creates an environment of chronic stress that affects brain development and cognitive functions, including memory, attention, and learning ability. The WHO Social Determinants of Health Framework provides a holistic lens through which the interplay of social, economic, and environmental factors influencing health and cognitive development can be analyzed [16]. It identifies structural determinants such as food security, parental education, household wealth, energy resources and living conditions as pivotal in shaping health inequities. Several studies, such as Berkman, et al. [17]; and Walker et al. [18], have used this framework to study associations between malnutrition and environmental health (key social determinants) with cognitive impairments in Peru and, broadly, in LMIC countries. This framework emphasizes the broad influences of socioeconomic and environmental conditions, offering valuable insights into the systemic barriers affecting child development in resource-restricted contexts such as Benin [16]. Pollitt’s [16] framework complements the WHO model by focusing on the direct impacts of nutritional deficiencies on cognitive functions. This highlights the role of nutritional status and key micronutrients status including iron, zinc, and iodine, in cognitive impairments, such as deficits in memory and attention [15]. According to Pollitt, undernutrition adversely affects children’s physical activity, curiosity, and social interactions, thereby limiting their exposure to essential cognitive stimuli. These developmental deficits, if unaddressed, can persist into adolescence and adulthood, resulting in long-term cognitive impairments. In such contexts, the interplay between nutritional health and cognitive development becomes critical. Key mechanisms include deficits in essential nutrients such as iron, zinc, and iodine, which disrupt neural processes critical for memory and learning [17]. Several studies have used this framework to underscore the importance of addressing nutritional deficiencies to mitigate their impact on cognitive development [19, 20] (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Study area

The Republic of Benin is a West African country bordered by Togo to the west, Nigeria to the east, Burkina Faso and Niger to the north, and the Atlantic Ocean to the south. Benin spans 114,763 km2 (Fig. 2) and has a population of 13,712,828 [21]. Urban areas in Benin are characterized by rapid growth and increasing modernization, whereas rural areas remain predominantly agricultural, facing challenges in infrastructure and access to public services. Peri-urban areas exhibit a unique blend of these characteristics, where modern infrastructure coexists with traditional practices and agricultural activities.

Benin map showing location of the study areas (created by: geography and environment department, Western University, 2024). Legend: N: North, km: kilometer

Two urban cities, Cotonou in the south and Natitingou in the north serve as central urban cities. Surrounding these urban centers are peri-urban municipalities such as Sèmè-Kpodji and Abomey-Calavi in the southern Benin, and Toukountouna and Boukoumbé in the northern Benin. Sèmè-Kpodji has a diverse population engaged in trade and agriculture. Its proximity to Nigeria and its role as a gateway for cross-border trade make it an ideal location for economic activities and cultural exchanges. While Abomey-Calavi is undergoing rapid urbanization, with agriculture, fishing, and trade as key economic activities. Despite their agricultural potential and crop diversity, 28% of households in the region remain food insecure, as reported by Dembélé et al. [20]. These dynamics of urban sprawl guided the focus of this study on the outskirts of Abomey-Calavi and Sèmè-Kpodji, which hold both urban and rural characteristics.

Boukoumbé and Toukountouna: Located in the Atakora department in northwest Benin, are municipalities renowned for their cultural richness and traditional lifestyles. Most of the population is engaged in agriculture and crafts. However, they face significant infrastructural deficits and limited access to public services. Notably, these areas have some of the highest rates of household food insecurity in the country [22].

Research design, sample size and sampling method

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional data collection process conducted in the peri-urban areas of northern and southern Benin. Public governmental schools in four municipalities were selected for participation. The schools were randomly sampled to ensure representativeness of the regional characteristics in both the northern and southern regions. Figure 2 shows the map of Benin Republic and the municipalities included in the study.

Sampling and participants

A systematic random sampling method was applied to select four governmental schools per municipality, following a 4 × 4 design.

Charan and Biswas (2013) [23] formula for quantitative cross-sectional studies was used to calculate the sample size at 95% confidence interval. N = (Z1 – α/22 P(1 – P))/d2. Where α = 5%, Z1 – α/22 = 1.96 for α = 5%, P: prevalence of moderate food insecurity in Atacora North P = 20.6% and P = 26.7% in South [22]. With a 15% allowance for dropout, a sample size of 289 and 346 was calculated for the North and south respectively. A total of 288 pupils were enrolled in Atacora in Toukountouna and Boukoumbé municipalities in northern Benin, and 236 pupils from Abomey-Calavi and Sèmè-Kpodji in southern Benin. The final sample size was 524 pupils.

The number of pupils selected per schools was obtained by dividing the sample size calculated in each region per 8 (number of schools to be selected). The number of boys and girls selected was proportional to gender distribution in each class and number of pupils in grade 4, 5 in each selected school. It is important to note that a significant percentage of dropouts occurred in the southern regions, due to a lack of awareness regarding cognitive tests, hemoglobin test and cultural factors that led to reluctance within those communities’. Parents were reluctant to involve their children in the study. Even after the explanation of the study and cautions implement to mitigate risks, many parents still withheld consent for their children to participate in the study.

Only Pupils from grade 4 and 5 were enrolled because the test was conducted in French and they were more susceptible at this stage to be fluent in French, since it is the main official language learned at school. Pupils were eligible if they: (1) provided signed informed consent from a parent or guardian and signed an assent form, (2) were apparently in good health on the data collection day, had no contraindications regarding meals served in the school canteen, (3) had no physical limitations, and (4) were 8–14 years of age and in grade 4 or 5. Children were excluded if they: (1) were found sick the data collection day, (2) were taking medications (3) did not provide signed informed consent from their legal guardians or did not consent to participate in the study.

The southern municipalities were surveyed in March 2023, whereas the northern municipalities were surveyed in September 2024.

Data collection

Dependent variable

STM was evaluated using the forward Wechsler Digit Span task from the Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC) [12]. The Digit Span assessed how many items a pupil could recall immediately, and in the order in which the items were shown in the test. The performance on this test provides valuable information about an individual's short-term memory span and their ability to process and temporarily store information [13, 24]. The Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children Digit Span Revised tasks have been proven reliable and enable valid measures of short-term memory capacity in several studies. The test was administered according to Wechsler guidelines. Based on the methodological review and user’s guide for memory span tasks [25], and the mean of score in the current study (5.88 rounded to 6), we categorized the pupils’ scores into two groups: “low scores” = 0 to 5 lines correct, and “high scores” = 6 to 16 lines correct.

Explanatory variables

Food security was measured using the nine-item Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) developed by Coates et al. [26]. Pupils responded to questions regarding the frequency of specific food insecurity situations, using a Likert scale (0 = rarely, 1 = sometimes, 2 = often). On the basis of the combination of responses to these nine questions, the respondents were categorized into four groups: food secure, mildly insecure, moderately insecure, and severely insecure (0 = food secure, 1 = mildly insecure, 2 = moderately insecure, 3 = severely insecure). The HFIAS tool had been widely applied in studies across Africa, including in Benin [26, 27].

The nutritional status of the pupils was assessed using their body mass index for age (BMI-for-age), according to the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines. Z-scores were calculated via WHO’s Anthro Plus software, following standard operating procedures (2006). The classification of nutritional status was based on WHO growth charts (BMI-for-age and sex) [28], which categorize individuals as severe thinness, moderate thinness, mild thinness, normal, or overweight. In addition, hemoglobin levels were measured on the field using the Hemocue Hb 201 + device, following standardized procedures [28]. Hemoglobin levels were categorized into normal, mild, moderate and severe according to WHO guidelines [28]. These indicators are globally recognized and have been widely used in studies assessing children’s nutritional status [23].

To conceptualize family wealth, pupils were asked to report their household assets, including ownership of a bicycle, cell phone, radio, television, stove, access to electricity, and access to running water. Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to reduce the socioeconomic variable dataset into two categories: pupils from “poor households” and those from “non-poor households.” Based on the PCA, the poverty line was set at −0.04177, representing the intermediate value that separates poor and non-poor groups. This approach provides a pragmatic alternative to collecting data on income, occupation, or expenditures, especially in households where money flow, membership, and seasonal variations are volatile and difficult to track. Empirical evidence supports this method as a reliable predictor of household wealth [29]. Socioeconomic and demographic data were collected via a digitized questionnaire. The demographic variables included the pupil’s gender, age, the formal education status of the caregivers, and household size. Gender was coded as (1 = male, 2 = female), formal education was coded as 0 = formal education, 1 = no formal education, and both age and household size were recorded as continuous variables. In this study, caregivers’ literacy levels were categorized into two groups: those with formal education, defined as individuals who attended structured institutional education systems, and those without formal education (coded as 0 = formal education, 1 = no formal education).

Given Benin’s status as a tropical country endemic to malaria, information was collected on whether pupils had experienced malaria in the past month or other illnesses during the current school term. Pupils who reported attending a hospital during the school term were asked, with their parents' permission, to bring their medical records to school for verification. All illnesses, infections, or pains were recorded by the enumerators via a digitized questionnaire.

Data were collected electronically by enumerators after training and pre-testing using the Kobo collect software a reliable tool widely used in fieldwork and various African studies [30]. The enumerators were those fluent in selected regions mother tongues, local languages and had at least bachelor’s degree. The team comprised three types of enumerators: sociologists for administering cognitive tests, nutritionists for conducting questionnaire interviews (anthropometric measurements), and fieldwork laboratory technicians for hemoglobin level testing.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0. The data analysis involved a three-stage approach (univariate, bivariate, and multivariate). The univariate approach examined the descriptive statistics to understand characteristics and distribution of each variable before conducting further analysis. It provided valuable insights into the individual variables, which helped identify outliers, understand the central tendency, and assess the overall shape of the data distribution. We used bivariate analysis in the second stages to assess association between explanatory variables and the dependent variable. In the third stage we used the Binary logistic regression to predict low short-term memory. We used a likelihood estimation method to calculate the odds ratios for pupils having low short-term memory [30]. The odds ratios exceeding one (OR > 1) indicate a higher likelihood of low short-term memory, while those below one (OR < 1) demonstrate a lower likelihood. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The reliability of the model was assessed using R-squared, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). It should be noted that no-gender-based analysis was conducted in this study.

Factors associated with short-term memory

The bivariate analysis identified several significant predictors of short-term memory ability among pupils (See Table 3). Pupils from poor households had increased odds of exhibiting below average short-term memory ability (OR = 1.834, p < 0.05). Similarly, pupils from households without electricity demonstrated higher odds (OR = 1.959, p < 0.05), suggesting a greater likelihood of low short-term memory ability. Furthermore, pupils whose caregivers or guardians had no formal education were significantly more likely to score below average on the short-term memory test (OR = 1.959, p < 0.001). Pupils from North Benin in Atacora department had increased odds of scoring low short-term memory compared to their counterparts from southern region (OR = 1.972, p < 0.001).

Table 3 also presents the results of the multivariate analysis (See Table 3). Pupils facing severe food insecurity were significantly more likely to exhibit low short-term memory ability (OR = 2.297, p < 0.05) compared to their counterparts. Pupils suffering from severe and moderate thinness were significantly more likely to score low on short-term memory test (OR = 1.680, p < 0.05). Similarly, pupils from poor households were two times more likely to score poorly on the short-term memory test (OR = 2.510, p < 0.001) than their peers from nonpoor households were. Age was also a significant factor, with older pupils having lower odds of having low short-term memory ability (OR = 0.830, p < 0.05). Additionally, pupils whose mothers had no formal education were more likely to have low short-term memory scores (OR = 1.668, p < 0.05). A significant predictor was location, pupils from northern region had higher odds of scoring low on short-term memory test (OR = 2.263, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Food insecurity remains a persistent challenge in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and socioeconomically disadvantaged nations such as Benin. By identifying its relationship with STM and key factors associated with low STM, this study expands current understanding of the biological mechanisms that can impair memory ability. Despite the implementation of the National Integrated School Feeding Program (NISFP), factors such as severity of food insecurity, nutritional status, household wealth, access to electricity, location and caregivers' literacy significantly influence STM.

These results emphasize the importance of food security which predicts nutritional status in promoting better cognitive abilities in children [30]. Although this study assessed individual food insecurity over the past four (4) weeks, this condition significantly negatively influenced short-term memory ability. Individual food insecurity, as assessed through anxiety about food, reduced dietary quality, and insufficient food quantity, may lead to insufficient provision of macronutrients and micronutrients essential to the maturation of body and brain [30, 31]. This may explain the fact that nutritional status is also linked to short-term memory. These results are consistent with those of prior studies conducted in African countries, including Ghana [30], and Ethiopia [31], which similarly highlight the detrimental effects of food insecurity and nutritional status [32–34] on children's cognitive abilities. Conversely, evidence from Beckman et al. [32] highlights the nuanced relationship between food insecurity and cognitive abilities, revealing associations only among girls. This gender-specific finding could be explained by differences in household coping strategies during periods of food insecurity, which are often shaped by gender inequities and societal expectations. In many contexts, girls may bear additional household responsibilities, face early marriage, or experience limited educational opportunities compared with boys, which may negatively affect their cognitive abilities. Furthermore, such disparities could also be linked to gender-specific developmental trajectories in cognitive, physical, and psychomotor growth [33]. It is also worth noting that Beckman et al.’s study focused on children aged 5—12 years and used the Flanker task, a cognitive test that measures information processing and inhibitory control rather than memory span [34]. This methodological difference may partially explain the difference in findings. These evidences highlights the urgency of addressing food insecurity to fight malnutrition and support healthy cognitive development.

Consistent with our hypothesis, household wealth is associated to short-term memory. Our findings indicate that household poverty may exert significant stress on short-term memory development. The negative associations among poverty, cognitive abilities, and short-term memory aligns with evidence from multiple African studies that highlighted the crucial impact of socioeconomic status on cognitive development. Evidence from South Africa [35] and Ethiopia [41] confirm our results, emphasizing the detrimental effects of poverty on cognitive performance. This may stem from the fact that poor household lack substantial environment conducive to memory development. However, some studies have reported no association between socioeconomics characteristics and STM and other related cognitive abilities overtime. For example, Kohler et al (2023) research reported no overtime associations between socioeconomic characteristics and cognition [42]. This discrepancy could be attributed to the use of deferent tools to assess short-term memory (the California Verbal Learning Test) and the homogeneity of study population. It is plausible that household poverty, within the context of food insecurity, hinders optimal cognitive development.

Our findings also suggest that older pupils were less likely to exhibit "low short-term memory ability." Older students consistently outperformed their younger counterparts in memory tests, indicating that cognitive development improves with age. This relationship is consistent with developmental theories, which posit that cognitive functions, including memory, mature as children grow older. The observed positive association between age and STM ability aligns with findings from other studies in Africa and beyond. For example, research in South Africa by Abrahams [36] and in Kenya by Kamau [37], similarly demonstrated that cognitive abilities, including short-term memory, improve with age among primary school pupils.

This study highlights location as a powerful determinant of short-term memory. This magnitude is plausible in light of well-documented north–south gradients in West Africa. Northern (often Sahelian) areas face higher food insecurity and child undernutrition, which are mechanistically linked to poorer cognitive performance—particularly working/short-term memory [26, 38]. At the country level, UNICEF’s Situation Analysis for Benin highlights that children in the northern departments (Alibori, Donga, Borgou) experience higher multidimensional deprivation compared to those in coastal departments, reflecting deeper poverty and constrained access to services that underpin nutrition and learning (e.g., diversified diets, WASH, health, and schooling) [38, 39].

Overall, these findings have important policy implications for improving the memory development of school-aged children, as food insecurity may significantly impair memory ability—even though memory naturally matures with age. Strategies to improve school feeding programs by improving the micronutrients density (through bio fortification and dietary diversity) of dishes served could serve as effective buffers against food insecurity in vulnerable communities. Additionally, creating employment opportunities by effectively supporting local purchases for the school canteen program in peri-urban areas could empower them to provide nutritious food for their children, and stimulate the local economy.

Limitations

This study investigates associations between variables but does not explore causal relationships, which limits our ability to infer direct causality. While hemoglobin assessment was conducted via precise medical measurements, many other variables rely on self-reported data, which could introduce recall bias. Furthermore, the study employed the digit span test as the sole cognitive assessment tool. Although widely used, this test is often administered alongside other cognitive tests to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of cognitive abilities.

The digit span test was conducted in French, the official national language of Benin. However, the majority of the participants primarily spoke traditional mother tongues such as Fon. This language discrepancy could have contributed to lower scores on the short-term memory test. To mitigate this, the study included only Grade 4 and 5 pupils, who are more likely by this stage to have achieved fluency in French.

Food insecurity was assessed in this cross-sectional study, using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS), reflecting experiences of food insecurity in the four weeks preceding data collection. This method does not account for temporal fluctuations in food insecurity. Consequently, the interpretation of its effect on short-term memory development should be made with caution, as longer or cumulative exposure may be necessary for food insecurity to exert a measurable impact on cognitive outcomes. These may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader school populations. Future research should address these limitations by employing longitudinal designs, incorporating additional cognitive assessments, and exploring the impact of school feeding programs in diverse settings.

Conclusion

In the peri-urban areas in the Republic of Benin, there is a significant prevalence of low short-term memory. We found that food insecurity, malnutrition, sociodemographic patterns such as age and location, caregiver’s education, and household wealth are key factors that are associated with short-term memory abilities. To address this problem, we suggest that government policies support interventions to reduce food insecurity, malnutrition and electrification in peri-urban and rural areas, and promote local purchases for the school canteen programme to stimulate community economies.
