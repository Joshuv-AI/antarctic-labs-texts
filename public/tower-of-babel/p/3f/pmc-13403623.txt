Shared and distinct features between interpersonal and self-directed violent behaviors: a simulated intervention network study
Authors: Wenjie Luo, Yahong Shi, Tianxiao Li, Junmei Hu, Xiacan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterpersonal and self-directed violence share many common risk factors, yet most studies examined the two forms of violence separately. To date, no research has investigated the mutually interactions between these shared and distinct features of the two forms of violence.MethodsA total of 2,223 men aged 18–34 were recruited via community-based random sampling in Chengdu, China. Assessments covered sociodemographic factors, psychiatric symptoms (depression, anxiety, psychosis), child maltreatment, adulthood stress, impulsivity, substance use, interpersonal and self-directed violent behaviors. Ising model was used to construct a violence network. Community detection was used to identify clusters of violence related factors. Simulated interventions were used to determine potential intervention targets of interpersonal and self-directed violence.ResultsA stable socio-psychological network was established among the interpersonal and self-directed violence, which encompassed externalizing, internalizing and social isolation communities. Externalizing community included interpersonal violence, adulthood stress, impulsivity, child maltreatment and victim of stalking, while internalizing community encompassed self-directed violence, chronic illness/disability, anxiety, depression, and psychosis. Psychosis exhibited the highest bridge centrality. Child maltreatment presented the highest direct and indirect expected influence of the network. Child maltreatment and anxiety were identified as the most effective statistical leverage points of interpersonal and self-directed violence, respectively.ConclusionThis study highlights that interpersonal violence and self-directed violence tends to gather with stress factors and mental disorders, respectively. Psychosis is the vital bridge, while child maltreatment is a critical covariant of the network.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27910-3.

Introduction

According to victim type, the World Health Organization (WHO) classifies violent behavior into interpersonal and self-directed violence, the latter encompassing suicidal self-injury (SSI) and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) [1, 2]. Traditionally, interpersonal violence and self-directed violence have been treated as distinct types because of separate etiologies [1]. Distinct risk factors of interpersonal violence and self-directed violence were consistently reported [3–5], but recent studies have revealed some shared associated factors of these two types of violent behavior, such as impulsivity, child maltreatment [6]. However, these studies were conducted in interpersonal violence and self-directed violence samples, respectively [3–5]. A study based on a combined sample of interpersonal and self-directed violence found 18.1% of general population reported violent behavior, but only 2.8% reported both interpersonal and self-directed violence [7]. Therefore, why do some of these impulsive individuals shared associated factors choose to harm themselves, while others choose to harm others? We speculate that there are distinct interactive associations between these associated factors and the two types of violence, which has not been reported.

Furthermore, violence is always accompanied by addictive behavior [8, 9]. Both violent and addictive behaviors were associated with impulsivity: substantial evidence from genetic, electrophysiological, and epidemiological studies links impulsivity closely to interpersonal violence [10, 11]. Evren reported that impulsivity along with drug addiction were risk factors of self-directed violence [12], while a cross-sectional study using magnetic resonance imaging found impulsivity was a therapeutic target of cocaine addiction [13]. Our recent studies showed impulsivity partially mediated the relation from child maltreatment to NSSI, while alcohol addiction mediated the relation from child maltreatment to NSSI comorbid SSI [14]. Meanwhile, we also found the addictive feature in the development of repeated NSSI [15]. A network study revealed that different drugs addictions were associated with various impulsivity patterns [16]. Therefore, whether the addictive behavior and violent behavior share the similar social-psychological characteristics networks? To our knowledge, it has not been reported.

Violent and addictive behavior have many shared and distinct associated factors, which might imply a complex associative network. However, traditional analytical approaches could only provide limited insight into how these factors interact with different forms of violent behavior [17–19]. Network analysis, an emerging methodological approach, could offer a system-level perspective by elucidating how a component is interconnected in a network structure based on Markov random fields [20]. It is structured with symptoms as nodes and the edges as their interactions, while the latent class could also be clustered by community detection [21, 22], which has gained significant attention in behavioral science and psychology [23, 24]. A network study has revealed strong interconnections between internet addiction and depressive symptoms, with “avoidance” and “guilt” emerging as both central and bridging symptoms [25]. Network analysis was also used to identify the key brain region in the transition from social interaction to aggression [26], as well as expose systematic associations and gender-specific patterns, linking perpetrators and victims in terms of technology-facilitated sexual violence [27]. Among these various models, the Ising model has become a common choice for constructing network analysis models due to its analytical suitability for binary data [28, 29]. The Ising model was originally proposed as a theoretical framework for the alignment of atomic spins in positive and negative states, and it is now widely used to model pairwise interactions among binary variables [30]. Overall, network analysis could enrich our understanding of violent behavior by uncovering interactive relations between all related factors. Nevertheless, network research encompassing interpersonal and self-directed violent behavior has not been reported.

Within the symptom network, simulated interventions can explore the expected promoting effects of specific interventions on a single node, highlighting the specificity and clinical intervention strategies under cross-sectional study design [31]. Furthermore, simulated intervention analysis can assess how changes in each feature affect the entire network by observing the changes in the activation rate of outcome feature, thereby providing the most potential statistical intervention target for the outcome [32, 33]. Previously, the effectiveness of intervention could be assessed by clinical trials, which could assess one intervention target in each time. By contrast, simulating symptom-specific interventions assesses the impact of each factor on other factors within the network by either mitigating or exacerbating each, which could compare the efficiency evaluation of all the factors simultaneously to identify the most effective one as the potential intervention target, which could provide more information for the following clinical trial studies [34]. For example, regulating the stress factor could affect the vulnerability of the Borderline Personality Disorder symptom network and the household mental illness, being the first bridge factor, was the critical marker for early detection of depression risk in middle-aged and older adults [34, 35].

In terms of violent behaviors research, sex dimorphism is presented with notably higher rates of involvement in various types of violent behaviors among young men in epidemiological studies [1]. Among confirmed murderers, the proportion of men is significantly higher than that of women [36]. Much research has been conducted on incarcerated men in judicial and clinical settings, which could not control the selection bias. Therefore, based on community random male sample to explore the interactions of risk factors of violent behavior could provide more novel evidence for the understanding of violence among young men.

Since interpersonal and self-directed violent behavior is often accompanied with addictive behaviors, is there homogeneity between them? What’s the differences of these shared and distinct associative factors in their interactive relations with these negative behaviors? Do these negative behaviors share similar potential preventing target?

Methods

Sample

The sample came from the Men’s Health and Modern Lifestyles Survey conducted in Chengdu, Sichuan Province, China, during 2012–2013. In this survey, a total of 4,238 participants aged between 18 and 34 years were randomly selected from three economic circle, including urban, suburban and rural areas, of Chengdu city which is in the southwest of China. Data was collected using paper-based questionnaires administered by trained interviewers through household visits. The survey was conducted in an anonymous, self-administered manner. Participants who completed the survey were provided with a gift valued at 50 yuan. More details of sampling method were presented in our previous research [36]. According to the demand of network analysis, these data were then preprocessed to exclude cases with any missing values in the corresponding variables (2015 cases), resulting in a final sample size of 2,223 participants to analyze.

Clinical assessment

Demographic variables

Patients completed a self-administered questionnaire to gather demographic data, including age, education level, employment status, living alone and no close relationships. Ethnic Han was identified as majority, and other ethnicities were identified as minority. Education level was assessed using the question: “At what age did you complete your full-time education?”.

Violent behavior assessment

In this study, self-directed violence included NSSI and SSI [37]. In the present study, two items from the Clinical Interview Schedule-Revised (CIS-R) were employed to differentiate between NSSI and SSI [38]. “Have you ever tried to kill yourself or made a suicidal self-injury?” was used to identify SSI. “Have you ever deliberately injured yourself without the intention of killing yourself?” was used to assess NSSI.

Interpersonal violence was evaluated using six self-report items embedded within the questionnaire, each targeting a specific form of violence directed toward others: “Have you ever hit or thrown things at your partner?”, “Since you were 15, have you ever hit a child, yours or someone else’s, so hard that they had bruises or had to stay in bed or see a doctor?”, “Since you were 15, have you ever been in any fights?”, “You have used a weapon like a stick, knife, or gun in a fight.” “I have used violence in the past 5 years to get what I wanted (e.g., money, drugs, sex).”, “I have been involved in gang fights in the past 5 years.” When participants answered “yes” for any one of the six items, they would be identified as having interpersonal violence.

Addictive behavior assessment

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT), with a high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.93), was employed to evaluate patterns, frequency, and consequences of alcohol consumption [39, 40]. A score exceeding 20 is indicative of alcohol abuse according to standard diagnostic criteria. The Drug Use Disorders Identification Test (DUDIT), with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80, was used to identify drug abuse with a cutoff score of above 6 [41, 42]. Pathological gambling behavior and pornographic behaviors were also assessed, detail of which have been reported in supplemental materials.

Psychiatric symptom

Anxious and depressive symptoms were measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), a self-reported instrument for detecting anxiety and depression in outpatient populations. Greater than 11 was considered as the presence of clinically significant symptoms [43, 44]. Psychotic symptoms were evaluated by the Psychosis Screening Questionnaire (PSQ), a brief and reliable binary-response instrument widely applied in global epidemiological surveys, with a positive criterion greater than 3 [45]. The PSQ had a sensitivity of 96.9%, a specificity of 95.3%, a positive predictive value of 91.2%, and a negative predictive value of 98.4% [46].

Other variables

To measure impulsivity, we utilized a specific item, “Now thinking of the time, since you were 15, do you often do things on the spur of the moment without thinking about how it will affect you or other people?”, from the DSM-IV Structured Clinical Interview for Personality Disorders (SCID-II). A “yes” response to this question was categorized as indicative of impulsivity.

Child maltreatment were identified by asking “Have you ever suffered from any of these problems or events before the age of 16 (e.g., bullying, witnessing violence at home, witnessing parents/careers fighting, sexual abuse/assault, physical abuse, neglect, serious/life threatening injury)?”. Endorsing at least one of these events resulted in classification as having experienced child maltreatment.

Adulthood stress events were identified by asking: “Have you ever suffered from any of these problems or events since the age of 16 (e.g., being injured as a result of physical attack, violence at workplace, domestic violence at home, sexual assault, serious/life threatening injury, separation due to marital difficulties, death of your husband/wife or partner or child, being fired, being homeless, and etc.)?” Endorsement of at least one of these events resulted in classification as having experienced adulthood stress events.

The presence of chronic disability among respondents was assessed by asking, “Do you have any long-term (lasting for 1 year or more) physical illness(s) or physical disabilities?” A “yes” response was considered indicative of a chronic disability.

Victim of stalking was determined by asking: “Have you ever experienced any of these problems or events since the age of 16?”. A positive response of “A victim of stalking” was classified as victim of stalking.

Statistical methods

All data analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2 within the RStudio environment.

Network structure

The Ising models were used to estimate network structures for binary variables within the estimateNetwork function of the R package bootnet [47]. This method achieves network sparsity by combining L1-regularized logistic regression with model selection based on the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) [48]. Additional penalization was imposed on models with a higher number of variables and adjacent nodes (γ = 0.25) using EBIC, yielding a more parsimonious and interpretable network structure [49]. To maintain sensitivity in network estimation, the OR-rule was further applied to enhance edge detection [50]. The plot function in R package igraph used to draw a network structure, arranging the nodes within a network by simulating physical forces [51].

Community detection

The Spinglass algorithm from the igraph R package was employed to struct communities within the network, by maximizing internal connectivity and minimizing connections between communities [52]. Each node is uniquely assigned to a single community. The algorithm employs simulated annealing to iteratively optimize modularity, ultimately converging on a stable partition characterized by dense intra-community and sparse inter-community connections [53]. Modularity (Q), a parameter used to evaluate the stability of the detected community structure, being greater than 0.3 is generally considered indicative of a stable and non-random community partition [54]. Additionally, we performed 100 iterations of the Spinglass algorithm to assess the stability and reliability of the community partitions.

Network robustness test

To assess the robustness of the constructed network model, the R package bootnet was employed to perform both non-parametric bootstrapping and case-dropping bootstrapping procedures [47]. The former estimates the 95% confidence intervals of edge weights by repeatedly resampling the original data 1000 times, where narrower intervals indicate greater stability of the edge estimates. The latter assesses the stability of node strength and bridge centrality by evaluating the correlation between recalculated and original centrality indices with the correlation stability (CS) coefficient. According to previous studies, a CS above 0.25 indicating an acceptable stability is the network selection criteria [47].

Bridge centrality and expected influence

Bridge strength centrality was calculated by the R package networktools to identify bridge nodes that serve as connectors between different communities within the symptom network [55]. It measures the total sum of the connection strengths between a node and the nodes in other communities, making it a valuable metric for identifying nodes that serve bridging functions within the network [56]. It also implies how this node could influence different symptom groups of the network. One-step expected influence (EI1) and two-step expected influence (EI2) was employed as centrality measures to identify influential nodes within the network structure. EI1 is defined as the sum of all edges extending directly from a given node to other nodes. EI2 extends this by capturing both direct and indirect (neighbor-mediated) effects, thereby offering a more comprehensive assessment of each node’s cumulative influence in the network [57]. Expected influence quantifies the potential of a node to spread effects throughout the network, thus facilitating the identification of key driving nodes within the network structure.

Simulation intervention

The NodeIdentifyR algorithm (NIRA), implementing different interventions on the generated simulated population, was used to evaluate the changes in the activation rates of target nodes to identify the most efficient intervention targets of interpersonal violence and self-directed violence, respectively [31]. Within NIRA, the IsingSampler package in R employs the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm to generate datasets of 5,000 virtual participants based on the original network parameters. During network estimation, each node produces its own threshold parameter. In this study, interpersonal violence and self-directed violence were selected as the target nodes. Different intervention conditions were simulated by adjusting the magnitude of the threshold parameters, which were expressed as multiples of the standard deviation of the threshold values [31]. Specifically, different adjustment magnitudes were implemented by decreasing or increasing the thresholds of non-target nodes by 2SD and 3SD. Activation rate of the two target nodes were got to assess the impact of the simulated interventions.

Overall, the relatively sample size in this study was sufficient to meet the requirements of network analysis [58].

Networks selection and robustness test

The networks of violence including 17 nodes was structured into four communities, including externalization, internalization, social isolation and addiction, with a modularity value of 0.37 (Fig. S1.). The bridge strength CS and the EI CS of the four-community networks are 0.128 and 0.672, respectively (Fig.S1c). The bridge strength CS has not reached the standard of 0.25. The bridge strength CS and EI CS of externalization-internalization-social isolation networks were 0.361 and 0.750, respectively (Fig. 1c), and the bridge strength CS and EI CS of externalization-internalization-addiction network were 0.128 and 0.517, respectively (Fig. S2c.). Therefore, given that CS value being above 0.25 is the criteria of a network’s robustness, the present study finally adopted the 13 nodes externalization-internalization-social isolation network as the violence behavior analysis network (Fig. 1a). Bridge centrality and expected influence of nodes

Figure 1d presents the bridge strength of each node within the networks. Within the externalizing community, impulsivity exhibited the highest bridging centrality (Bridge strength = 1.28), indicating a key role in linking to external communities. Similarly, psychosis (bridge strength = 1.92) showed the highest bridging centrality within the internalizing community. In contrast, the three nodes of social isolation showed low bridge strength values, indicating fewer connections extending to other parts of the networks. Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. 1a, Anxiety demonstrated the highest edge weight with self-directed violence, indicating the strongest association, whereas Child maltreatment had the highest edge weight with interpersonal violence.

The standardized bridge EI1 and EI2 are presented in Fig. 1e and f, respectively. In the one-step analysis, child maltreatment (EI1=5.99), anxiety (EI1=4.79) and psychosis (EI1=3.91) exhibited the strongest positive direct influences in the networks. In contrast, the two-step analysis revealed that child maltreatment (EI2=42.23), psychosis (EI2=19.74) and impulsivity (EI2=19.19) exhibited relatively higher EI2 values.

Discussion

This study is the first to apply network analysis and simulated intervention to examine the complex interactions between different forms of violence and socio-psychological factors among young men. Our first question is answered with the finding that addictive behavior could not form a stable network with violent behavior, which implies heterogeneity between addictive behavior and violent behavior. The second question was also answered by highlighting the novel finding that interpersonal violence clustered with impulsivity, child maltreatment, and stress to form an externalizing community, whereas self-directed violence clustered with psychotic symptoms to form an internalizing community. Psychosis serves as the bridge factor of the different communities of the network, while child maltreatment might be a covariation of the externalizing and internalizing communities in the network by being a vital statistical intervention target for both international and self-directed violence, which answered our third question to some extent.

We found that externalizing and internalizing were not isolated communities but were mainly bridged through psychosis and impulsivity, which is consistent with the new psychopathological nosological systems containing externalizing, internalizing and psychotic experience domains [59]. The psychopathological nosological system classified anxious and depressive symptoms as internalizing, and aggressive, delinquent and hyperactive-impulsive symptoms as externalizing [59]. This is further supported by this network study with the community cluster findings that anxiety, depression and self-directed violence were classified as internalizing and interpersonal violence and impulsivity were classified as externalizing. By contrast, alcohol and substance misuse could not be clustered with these symptoms to build a stable network. This could be contributed to the alcohol and substance misuse being rarely reported in this study, which is consistent with previous studies of China [60]. Substance use is illegal and strictly managed by Chinese law, so the prevalence of substance use is much lower than many other countries and the negative effect of substance use is also controlled effectively [61].

Meanwhile, we found psychosis is not only a member of internalizing community, but also the most important bridge in the network, connecting most features of the internalizing and externalizing communities directly, which is consistent with the conceptional framework that psychopathology is dimensional rather than categorical [62, 63]. Although the psychopathological nosological system identifies psychosis as one of the three main dimensions, the correlated factor model suggests the mutual correlations between the three dimensions. The community detection findings present psychosis is more likely to gather with self-directed violence in internalizing community among Chinese young men. Lambe found that psychotic symptoms, such as hallucinations and delusions, not only directly promote violent behavior by increasing psychological stress and feelings of threat, but can also indirectly trigger violence by eliciting intense emotions [64]. Ullrich has reported the mediating role of anger in the pathway from psychotic symptoms towards interpersonal violence [65]. Thus, the psychosis-anger-violence pathway should be further paid attention in future studies. Meanwhile, Cates found persecutory ideas, auditory hallucinations were associated with suicidal thoughts and attempts [66]. Especially, persecutory idea was the only risk factor of later suicidal thoughts in cohort study. However, this association was mediated by mood instability, which seemed to be the most potential target of suicidal behavior rather than impulsivity [66]. Although our previous study found anxiety and impulsivity played a mediating role in the pathway from child maltreatment towards self-directed violence [67], the community detection of this study further demonstrates a closer relation between mood instability, such as depression and anxiety and self-directed violence.

We also found child maltreatment, clustered in externalizing community, is not only the most important factor to directly affect other factors or indirectly influence the whole network, but also the most important potential intervention target in the simulated interventions. This finding might support the covariation hypothesis that there might be a substantive factor that contributes to meaningful variance across the main dimensions of psychopathology in some degree [59]. Child maltreatment, being a long-distance stress factor, along with the short-distance stress factor, adulthood stress events, gathered with interpersonal violence. This is consistent with the reporting that it is the mediator from adulthood stress events towards interpersonal violence in some degree [68–70]. Furthermore, we also found child maltreatment was a vital effective statistical target in alleviating both interpersonal and self-directed violence. This is consistent with cohort evidence that adverse childhood experiences are strong predictors of suicidal behavior [71]. The importance of child maltreatment on suicidal behavior was persistently existing across different cultural backgrounds [14]. Up to now, the neural mechanisms through which child maltreatment influence self-directed violence remain unclear, and further research is needed to clarify these pathways.

By contrast, self-directed violence was closely embedded in mental disorders, a pattern that differs from findings in the 1990s [1]. At that time, impulsivity, was found to be the most critical feature of suicidal behavior among Chinese, which was different from western countries reporting mental disorders being the most risk factors [72]. However, along with the financial development of China, the overall suicide rate has declined in the 21st century [73]. Meanwhile, our recent cross-cultural study demonstrated a more complex relation between mental disorders and impulsivity by finding that the impulsivity and mental disorder subtypes were both existing among young men attempted suicide across eastern and western countries [67]. Furthermore, social isolation emerged as another distinct community in the network, which is consistent with our previous finding that the no psychosis subtype of suicide attempts in China is primarily characterized by social isolation [67]. This study further revealed that social isolation community is positively associated with self-directed violence through “no close relationship.” Simulation interventions also showed that targeting “no close relationship” could, to some extent, reduce self-directed violence.

Furthermore, anxiety is a predominant intervention target of self-directed violence in the statistical results of simulated interventions. A surprising finding was that depression showed only a weak association with self-directed violence, which contradicts studies consistently reporting depression as a major risk factor [74], but consistent with others which found depression has no relation to the incidence of self-directed violence [14, 75]. This inconsistency may stem from the fact that many previous studies did not control for anxiety when assessing depression’s effect on self-directed violence, despite the high comorbidity between the two [76]. Moreover, not only did the present study find that anxiety plays an important role in self-directed violence, but this was also observed in our previous research [14]. The network study provides us with stronger evidence to reconsider the relation between depression, anxiety and self-directed violence. Notably, compared with the effect of depression, the present study suggests that anxiety seemed to be the most effective intervention target of self-directed violence in terms of statistical level, which should be further tested by experiment.

By contrast, impulsivity, clustered with externalizing violence, was the third most important bridge in the network, connecting most features of the internalizing and externalizing communities directly. This finding aligns with previous research, which has widely recognized as a key contributor to both interpersonal violence and self-directed violence [77, 78]. The Cognitive-Emotional Model of Dual-Harm proposes that impulsivity, as a distal personality factor, underlies both self-directed and interpersonal violence [79]. Our previous study found anxiety, impulsivity and psychosis were mediators between child maltreatment and self-directed violence [14]. Meanwhile, we discovered the impulsivity subtype existed among both interpersonal and self-directed violent population [67, 80]. However, these previous studies rarely explored the relation between impulsivity and interpersonal, self-directed violent behavior at the same time. Therefore, the present network study controlling other factors at the same time, reveals a novel finding that although impulsivity shows its effect in both interpersonal and self-directed violence, it seems to play a more important role in interpersonal violence than self-directed.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inference, although the simulated intervention analysis helps partially address this issue. All the identified intervention targets are statistical leverage points only, not validated causal pathways and experimentally validated treatment targets. Therefore, these statistical leverage points should be tested experimentally in future studies. Future studies may collect longitudinal data to further examine causal inferences among factors related to violent behavior. Secondly, the sample predominantly consists of young men, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to female population, given the well-documented sex differences in both interpersonal and self-directed aggression research [63]. Future studies recruiting female participants would allow for the construction of gender-specific networks and help reveal gender differences in the presentation of violent behavior. Thirdly, although the data were collected ten years ago, which does not compromise the validity of the findings, significant socio-cultural changes in China over the past decade, such as widespread internet and short-video use were not captured. Fourthly, the present study only used a single question to assess NSSI and SSI, respectively. Although these questions have a reliable source (from the Clinical Interview Schedule-Revised) and have been reported in previous studies [81, 82], this also limited assessing the dimension of these features and behaviors and could not recover important variation of frequency, severity, intent and so on. Fifthly, impulsivity is captured by a single yes/no question despite being a well-established multidimensional construct. Sixthly, this study aggregated child maltreatment, highly heterogeneous experiences (sexual abuse, neglect, bullying, witnessing violence), into one composite, could not reveal the relation between different child maltreatment experiences and different violent behavior [83]. Finally, the exclusion of addiction community might also contribute to its low incidence of alcohol use disorders (3.0%), drug abuse (1.3%). This is a primary exploration and should be retested by future studies.

Conclusions

Interpersonal and self-directed violence shared a similar psychological network, emerging specific characteristics that interpersonal violence more tends to gather with stress factors across different stages of life to form externalizing, while self-directed violence is imbedded by mental disorders to form internalizing. Our findings challenge traditional isolated conceptualizations of these two forms of violence, revealing their overlapping dimension by finding high-centrality bridging symptom psychosis of the network. These critical bridging features suggest shared underlying psychological mechanisms between the self-directed and interpersonal violence. Notably, we further present the covariant of the interpersonal and self-directed violence by finding child maltreatment as potential intervention target of self-directed violence and interpersonal violence in statistical level, which should be tested by clinical research in the future. The neural mechanisms for how child maltreatment affects both interpersonal and self-directed violence should also be explored in the future.
