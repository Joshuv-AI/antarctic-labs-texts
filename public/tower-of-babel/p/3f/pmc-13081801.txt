Complex Patterns of Hitchhiking Mutation Load Among Stickleback Populations
Authors: Jana Nickel, Jan Laine, Andrew D Foote
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPositive selection causes beneficial alleles to rise to high frequency in a population. This can cause linked genetic variation to “hitchhike,” and thereby also rise in frequency. This linked variation may include deleterious recessive alleles, previously neutrally harbored at low frequency and in heterozygous genotypes. Modeling studies have shown that local effective population size and reduced recombination rate should contribute to the probability of deleterious mutations being swept to high frequency by being linked to beneficial alleles in selective sweeps or selection on inversion karyotypes. Marine sticklebacks have repeatedly adapted to thousands of freshwater habitats that became available after the last ice age, resulting in the formation of distinct marine and freshwater ecotypes. Selection acts on ancient standing genetic variation present in marine populations, causing freshwater-adaptive alleles to increase rapidly over tens of generations. These genomic regions play a key role in the repeated freshwater adaptation of morphological, physiological, and behavioral traits, and evolve under strong selection. Thus, threespine stickleback is an ideal system for investigating the impact of hitchhiking mutation load. We estimate the mutation load in regions of low recombination, including inversions and the Eda haplotype. We find some evidence for increased accumulation of deleterious alleles in one inversion, while this is not the case for two other inversions. Inversions deviated from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium in several populations due to an excess of homozygotes.

Hitchhiking is a ubiquitous force in population genetics, shaping genetic diversity across the genome. Threespine stickleback is a model system for studying parallel adaptation to ecological variation, with low-recombination genomic regions known to harbor ecologically adapted alleles that drive these adaptations. A key question is whether adaptation in this system is associated with a deleterious mutation load through hitchhiking. We find that hitchhiking mutation load patterns are complex and do not conform to simple model predictions, despite some widespread patterns shared among populations.

Introduction

Felsenstein (1974) elegantly argued that the evolutionary advantage of recombination was to disassociate linkage between beneficial and deleterious alleles, and to link adaptive alleles on the same haplotype. The relationship between recombination and linkage of beneficial and deleterious alleles on a haplotype predicts that regions of low recombination rate in the genome may have a reduced capacity to disassociate linked beneficial and deleterious mutations. Low recombination regions include those evolving under strong linked selection, such as selective sweeps or inversions (Hoffmann and Rieseberg 2008; Burri et al. 2015).

The importance of structural variants within genomes in determining phenotypic variation and underpinning adaptations associated with divergent ecological or sexual selection within wild populations is of key interest to evolutionary biologists (Kirkpatrick and Barton 2006; Berdan et al. 2023). Inversions in a heterozygous state suffer from recombination suppression and therefore a reduction in crossovers between the two forms of the inversion. This can have an additional effect of linking many haploblocks into a single so-called supergene, in which many ecology- or phenotype-associated alleles across multiple loci can become linked on an inverted haplotype (Thompson and Jiggins 2014). Inversions have been linked to trait variation, including the striking phenotype differences of mating morphs of ruff (Philomachus pugnax) (Lamichhaney et al. 2016); tail length and coat color differences between prairie and forest ecotypes of deer mice (Peromyscus maniculatus) (Hager et al. 2022), and seed size and flowering time variation between non-dune and dune ecotypes of sunflowers (Helianthus spp.) (Huang et al. 2020; Todesco et al. 2020). In the latter examples, ecological selection has repeatedly acted on the genetic variation within inversions, resulting in parallel evolution of ecotypes and driving genetic divergence between them (Hager et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2025). The role of inversions in behavioral, ecological, and phenotypic divergence is thought to maintain inversion polymorphisms (Wellenreuther and Bernatchez 2018).

Deleterious mutation load has been found to be associated with inversions in some taxa (Heliconius numata (Jay et al. 2021); Acropora kenti (Zhang et al. 2024)). However, few empirical studies have focused on deleterious recessive mutation load associated with selective sweeps, despite both theoretical expectations (Hartfield and Otto 2011) and empirical evidence (Di et al. 2021) that mutation load can interfere with and slow the progress of adaptation, especially during domestication (Marsden et al. 2016). In this study, we look for empirical support for these model-based hypotheses (Hartfield and Otto 2011) in natural populations of threespine stickleback with different demographic histories and, consequently, effective population sizes. We compare the mutation load in genomic regions with reduced recombination rate to putatively neutrally evolving regions.

Threespine stickleback provide an excellent system for understanding parallel adaptation to ecological variation (Reid et al. 2021). Marine stickleback have independently adapted to thousands of freshwater habitats that became available after the Last Glacial Maximum, resulting in the formation of distinct marine and freshwater ecotypes. Upon colonization of new freshwater habitats, selection acts on ancient standing genetic variation present in marine populations, causing freshwater-adaptive alleles to increase rapidly over tens of generations (Fig. 1a) (Colosimo et al. 2005; Barrett and Schluter 2008; Nelson and Cresko 2018). Quantitative trait loci (QTL) within some of these divergent genomic regions have been characterized and found to drive the behavioral, morphological, and physiological differences between ecotypes, such as the number of bony plates and body shape (Peichel and Marques 2017).

These QTL include the Ectodysplasin (Eda) haplotype on chromosome IV, a highly pleiotropic locus linked to multiple traits that characterize differences between marine and freshwater sticklebacks, including bony plate architecture (Colosimo et al. 2005), lateral line patterning (Archambeault et al. 2020), and schooling behavior (Greenwood et al. 2016). The Eda gene exhibits a large selection coefficient and a large fitness effect (Schluter et al. 2021). Consequently, freshwater ancestry rapidly increases at the Eda haplotype within a few generations in transplant experiments (Barrett et al. 2008; Schluter et al. 2021), and after recent colonization events in natural lakes (Roberts Kingman et al. 2021b). Strong selection results in the 16 kb long genomic region of the Eda haplotype being shared by freshwater populations (Colosimo et al. 2005). This is contrasted with the high divergence observed in the flanking regions upstream and downstream of the haplotype, as alternative haplotypes are fixed in different lakes (Roesti et al. 2014; Kirch et al. 2021), consistent with a selective sweep. Given the characteristics and strength of selection acting on the Eda locus during the colonization of freshwater, we predict that deleterious recessive alleles could hitchhike upon the Eda locus during the resultant sweep (Fig. 1b). Accumulation of deleterious variation near Eda is a conditional prediction, expected when selection on the freshwater haplotype is strong relative to recombination and gene flow. Repeated selection on a low-frequency haplotype, such as the freshwater Eda haplotype in threespine sticklebacks, provides conditions under which elevated linked deleterious variation is plausible and testable.

The Eda locus is one of the 242 genomic loci, including inversions, that are known to be associated with parallel marine-freshwater divergence (Jones et al. 2012). These marine-freshwater-adaptive loci typically display reduced recombination rates, thus maintaining strong linkage among loci on the same chromosome and creating “adaptive cassettes” that are under selection (Venu et al. 2024). Thus, we can compare the accumulation of deleterious alleles between inversions and within the selective sweep at the Eda haplotype to better understand the interaction between genomic architecture and mutation load.

Results

We sequenced 241 stickleback from two assumed anadromous populations from marine fjords carrying predominantly marine-adaptive alleles at marine-freshwater divergent regions of the genome (see Jones et al. 2012), six lacustrine populations carrying predominantly freshwater-adaptive alleles, and one riverine population with mixed marine and freshwater ancestry (Laine et al. 2025), yielding an average genome coverage of 14.45 ± 6.23× (Table S1). The six lakes represent habitats that are geographically isolated from the marine habitats, with limited potential for gene flow (Fig. S1).

Demographic History

We reconstructed the demographic history of the marine or freshwater populations, as effective population size (Ne) determines the efficacy of selection. As expected, the highest effective population sizes are found in the marine and river populations, while the freshwater populations generally show lower values (Table S2).

To identify further differences, we inferred Ne over time with SMC++. Assuming a generation time of 2 years (Liu et al. 2016), the maximum effective population size was reached 100,000 years before present (BP) (Fig. S2). The two marine populations, Oslofjord and Altafjord, remained relatively stable over time. Freshwater populations experienced a decline in Ne around 10,000 years BP, particularly in the four oldest lakes (Pernislavatnet, Langevatnet, Klubbvatnet, and Jossavannet), and exhibit lower effective population sizes. This demographic history would be consistent with the freshwater populations being isolated after the last ice age (Liu et al. 2016; Kirch et al. 2021). Due to the limited number of recent coalescent events, estimates of Ne closer to the present should be interpreted with caution (Terhorst et al. 2017).

Shared Eda Haplotypes Among Lakes

We first focused on the selective sweep at the region containing the Eda gene and the flanking regions on chromosome IV to compare genomic differentiation and divergence between marine and freshwater populations. The defined flanking regions, spanning 80 kb on either side of the Eda gene, exhibit a lower recombination rate than the Eda gene and the genome-wide average (Fig. S3). In addition, the flanking regions are surrounded by recombination hotspots upstream and downstream. The recombination rate in this region was found to be below the genome-wide average and decreased compared to ninespine stickleback and other fishes, suggesting this lower recombination rate is a derived trait in threespine stickleback (Roberts Kingman et al. 2021a). Therefore, we expect a stronger linkage within this region.

Additionally, since this recombination rate is derived from the marine Rabbit Slough population and to assess if this is consistent across populations, we estimated the recombination rate for freshwater and marine populations, finding a broad pattern of reduced recombination in the Eda region (Fig. S4).

Overall, genome scans comparing freshwater populations and marine populations showed a similar pattern for all pairwise comparisons, with differentiation (FST) and divergence (dxy) peaks at the Eda gene and the trailing ∼40 kb genomic region, followed by a smaller peak in the flanking region (Fig. 2a and b, Fig. S5). Although the two marine populations are approximately 1,350 km apart, they show very little genomic differentiation (FST = 0.01) and divergence (dxy = 0.004) across the genome.

For most freshwater–freshwater population comparisons, we find reduced divergence at the Eda locus with peaks in the flanking regions, i.e. differentiation is reduced directly at the Eda locus but is relatively high in adjacent regions of chromosome IV (Fig. S5). This pattern more broadly supports the previously reported findings of reduced FST at the Eda locus and high FST at the flanking regions in comparisons between freshwater populations from Vancouver Island (Roesti et al. 2014) and between the Jossavannet and Klubbvatnet populations (Kirch et al. 2021). Comparisons between marine or freshwater populations reveal genetic differentiation and divergence peaks at the Eda gene, while comparisons between freshwater populations show reduced differentiation and divergence. This suggests that the core Eda haplotype is shared by these six isolated lake populations.

We visualized the average FST and dxy as a heatmap in the upstream and downstream flanking regions of the Eda gene, comparing all populations (Fig. 2c and d, Fig. S6). In the upstream and downstream flanking regions, the differentiation and divergence are lowest between the marine populations, and are most elevated in comparisons that include the Jossavannet population, which is known to be highly inbred, with over 60% of the genome being found in long (>300 kb) runs of homozygosity (Kirch et al. 2021). These patterns are consistent with linked haplotypes adjacent to Eda approaching fixation in at least some freshwater populations, i.e., a hard selective sweep. The majority of other comparisons between freshwater populations show slightly increased differentiation and divergence in the flanking regions. The nucleotide diversity is markedly reduced in most freshwater populations, while populations Altafjord, Oslofjord, Trondheim, and Hoksvatnet show peaks at the Eda gene and in the downstream flanking region (Fig. S7). Samples for the Trondheim population were collected approximately 1 km upstream of the Nidelva River mouth. These stickleback had a mixture of marine and freshwater ancestry and traits and are therefore not categorized as either a marine or freshwater population in subsequent analyses.

Inversion Karyotype Frequencies Within and Among Populations

We used principal component analysis (PCA) and heterozygosity to identify the karyotype of each individual. We classified the three distinct clusters separated along the genome as heterozygous (A/B) or homozygous (A/A or B/B) (Figs. S8 to S10).

The standard karyotype, i.e., the most common form in the marine populations and therefore the assumed ancestral state, is expected to be the cluster we assigned as A/A (Fig. 3). The inversion karyotype is known to segregate between marine and freshwater populations and is likely the cluster we assigned as B/B, which only appears in freshwater populations. In the marine populations, most individuals are homozygous for the assumed standard karyotype for all inversions. The highest inversion frequencies in freshwater populations are found for the inversion on chromosome I (70% to 100%), with fixation in populations from the oldest lakes, Pernislavatnet, Langevatnet, Klubbvatnet, and Jossavannet. The majority of freshwater populations exhibit varying levels of polymorphism for all three inversions, which could point to differences in the evolutionary history of inversions or ecological differences among populations.

Populations varied in the degree of polymorphism and the karyotypes they carried at each inversion. The majority of marine or freshwater populations deviate significantly from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) or are completely fixed at the inversions on chromosomes I, XI, and XXI (Fig. 3). The excess of homozygotes at inversions in many of the marine and freshwater populations indicates strong spatially divergent selection.

Mutation Load in Low Recombination Genomic Regions and Differences Between Populations

Our goal was to identify whether there is evidence for deleterious mutations segregating in low recombination regions, resulting in an increased mutation load. To test this, we compared the proportion of different categories of putative impact of variants in the selective sweep at Eda and the inversions on chromosomes I, XI, and XXI to the proportions of the genome-wide SNP data set (Fig. 4a). The three inversions are associated with parallel marine-freshwater divergence (Jones et al. 2012), but the fitness effects of inversion karyotypes were found not to be strongly associated with salinity (Rodríguez-Fuentes et al. 2025), suggesting other sources of selection may drive karyotype frequencies.

No observed values are significantly different from the null distribution. b) Ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous sites (pN/pS) for the inversions on chromosomes I, XI, and XXI and genome-wide (excluding the regions of interest) for each population computed in 100 kb windows. For the whole genome, 0.1% of the SNPs were categorized as highly deleterious, which likely disrupts the gene function, 7.2% were categorized as moderate, and 11.9% as low impact. The inversion on chromosome I has a slightly higher proportion for each impact category compared to the whole genome. This is also the case for the Eda region for high and low impact. In contrast, the other two inversions show a lower or similar proportion of deleterious mutations relative to the whole genome. The regions of interest are not significantly increased or decreased compared to the null distribution based on 1,000 randomly selected genomic regions for all impact categories (Fig. 4a). Thus, the relationship between inversions and mutation load is complex in threespine stickleback, in that it varies among loci.

To characterize the deleterious variation of each inversion, we calculated the ratio of segregating nonsynonymous to synonymous mutations (pN/pS) in each region of interest of each population (Fig. 4b) and for all marine or freshwater populations combined (Fig. S11) and compared it to the genomic background. A higher ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous mutations indicates potentially increased accumulation of deleterious alleles in a genomic region.

The two-sided t-test showed that the deleterious variation, estimated as pN/pS, was significantly increased (P < 0.05) for the inversion on chromosome I between the genome-wide pN/pS and populations Vaagevannet and Pernilsavatnet (Fig. 4b). The other comparisons between a population within an inversion and the genome-wide pN/pS for that population were non-significant. However, only one comparison remained significant after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Several additional comparisons show consistent but non-significant trends toward elevated pN/pS. Overall, these results provide limited but suggestive evidence for increased accumulation of deleterious alleles in the inversion on chromosome I in some freshwater populations, while highlighting that statistical support is sensitive to multiple testing correction and may be constrained by limited power. This result is also reflected in the marine and freshwater populations combined (Fig. S11).

The 300 kb region surrounding the Eda haplotype is relatively small and therefore does not contain many SNPs that are categorized as nonsynonymous or synonymous, making a meaningful statistical comparison challenging. The pN/pS calculated for the upstream and downstream flanking regions of the Eda gene are low compared to the genome-wide pN/pS distribution, falling close to the lower 5th percentile (Fig. S12).

For the distribution of fitness effects (DFE) in each population, we expect purifying selection to act more efficiently against highly deleterious mutations in large populations and be relaxed in smaller populations. In all populations, most SNPs are estimated to be deleterious, with most of them in the highest bin of population-scaled selection coefficient, indicating strongly deleterious mutations (Fig. 4c). This pattern is similar to those found in other species (Gutiérrez-Valencia et al. 2024). The proportion of sites with neutrally, weakly, moderately, and strongly deleterious fitness effects is similar across all marine and freshwater populations.

Discussion

Hitchhiking involves changes in allele frequencies linked to selective sweeps or to purged deleterious mutations (background selection), and is a ubiquitous force in shaping genetic variation in populations (Charlesworth and Jensen 2021). The Eda locus is under strong selection in freshwater threespine sticklebacks, with a large selection coefficient (Schluter et al. 2021) and effects on multiple traits (Archambeault et al. 2020). We found that the core Eda haplotype is shared by all six freshwater populations, regardless of lake age, as expected from strong selection on ancient genomic variation present in the marine populations that colonized freshwater habitats (Schluter and Conte 2009). Due to the reduced recombination rate in selective sweeps, such as found at the Eda locus, we hypothesized that this region could have an increased hitchhiking mutation load. The freshwater threespine stickleback populations included in this study had distinct demographic histories due to being formed at different times following the Last Glacial Maximum. Colonization by marine stickleback was estimated to have occurred between 1,000 and 12,900 years ago for the different lakes sampled here. Demographic reconstructions show a decline in Ne around 10,000 years BP found in Atlantic stickleback (Liu et al. 2016; Kirch et al. 2021), and a larger effective population size in marine populations than in freshwater populations (Table S2, Fig. S7). Theory predicts that small populations should accumulate more deleterious mutations due to the stronger effect of genetic drift and relaxed purifying selection (Lynch et al. 1995), a phenomenon that has been observed in natural populations (Grossen et al. 2020; Leroy et al. 2021; Ramasamy et al. 2023). In addition, gene flow has the potential to reduce the accumulation of deleterious alleles by introducing new alleles that are beneficial or mask recessive deleterious mutations, especially in small populations (Robinson et al. 2023). However, gene flow may also introduce new deleterious alleles present in large populations (Kyriazis et al. 2021). As these freshwater stickleback populations are geographically isolated, gene flow is expected to be limited, mostly occurring from freshwater habitats to marine habitats downstream.

As expected, we found evidence for positive selection on the freshwater Eda haplotype in the lake populations. Accordingly, pN/pS was generally low in the flanking 80 kb regions of the Eda haplotype for all populations, compared to the genome-wide pN/pS distribution, suggesting purifying selection (Fig. S12). However, we were unable to test for significance due to a lack of power caused by the relatively small size of this genomic region and the limited number of SNPs carrying potential deleterious mutations. Quantifying pN/pS is only possible in coding regions of the genome and is more challenging in small genomic regions that contain only a limited number of synonymous or nonsynonymous mutations. Venu et al. (2024) found that low recombination regions (or coldspots) are not limited to the flanking regions of the Eda haplotype, but also encompass a much larger region of linked genomic loci inherited together as a “linked adaptive cassette”. These regions shape marine-freshwater divergence and could be a target for investigating increased hitchhiking mutation load in low recombination genomic regions.

Inversions suppress recombination in their heterozygous form, thus resulting in increased linkage between alleles within inversions (Kirkpatrick and Barton 2006; Berdan et al. 2023). Inversions may also be affected by associative overdominance, where an inversion captures a karyotype carrying weakly deleterious, partially recessive, or recessive mutations at several loci that are masked in inversion heterozygotes (Berdan et al. 2023). This has been predicted to reduce purging of deleterious mutations from inversions, and to potentially promote associative overdominance, thereby limiting ecological adaptation (Roesti et al. 2022).

Focusing specifically on three large inversions with some known association between marine and freshwater stickleback populations (Jones et al. 2012), we found inconsistent patterns of inferred deleterious mutation load in inversions. The inversion on chromosome I carries a slightly higher percentage of high, low, and moderate impact SNPs compared to the genome-wide background and the other inversions (Fig. 4a). In addition, the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous sites revealed that this inversion exhibited significantly increased accumulation of deleterious alleles compared to the genome-wide background in two freshwater populations (Fig. 4b). The other two inversions on chromosomes XI and XXI show little evidence of increased accumulation of deleterious alleles. However, the limited number of nonsynonymous or synonymous sites present in the inversions in certain populations could potentially influence these results. The lineage sorting of marine and freshwater populations at the inversion on chromosome XXI is less clear relative to the inversions on chromosomes I and XI (Jones et al. 2012). Thus, this inversion may have a limited role in marine-freshwater divergence (Rodríguez-Fuentes et al. 2025), which is reflected in the inversion karyotype frequencies (Fig. 3). The patterns of limited deleterious variation in these two inversions are consistent with the absence of deleterious fitness effects being observed for the homozygous inversion karyotypes in response to freshwater or marine salinity conditions (Rodríguez-Fuentes et al. 2025). A slight effect was observed for the inversion on chromosome I, where heterozygous individuals had a lower body condition in the freshwater salinity treatment. However, the increased accumulation of deleterious alleles that we identified for the inversion on chromosome I for some populations could have a negative impact on the fitness related to several other ecological factors that shape freshwater and marine habitats, such as temperature or predators.

Inversion frequency is shaped by balancing and divergent selection (Faria et al. 2019), and the tightly linked alleles are known to be associated with ecotype differentiation in fish (Berg et al. 2017; Arostegui et al. 2019; Meyer et al. 2024), including stickleback (Jones et al. 2012). Inversion frequencies vary across the populations for chromosomes I, XI, and XXI, which could indicate differences in ecological association, evolutionary history, and inversion age (Fig. 3) (Berdan et al. 2023). All three inversions are highly polymorphic, but marine populations were monomorphic for karyotype A/A on the inversion on chromosome XXI, and the two oldest freshwater populations were mostly monomorphic. In freshwater populations, the inversion karyotype (B/B) is carried by the majority of individuals. In addition, both the marine and freshwater populations deviated from HWE with an excess of homozygotes, potentially indicating positive selection on the karyotypes (Lachance 2016).

Theory predicts that reduced recombination rate in a genomic region increases the likelihood of deleterious hitchhiking alleles becoming fixed (Hartfield and Otto 2011). However, this phenomenon appears to be more complex in natural populations and is influenced by several factors. Studies have identified an increased mutation load in inversions or supergenes, which also exhibit suppressed recombination. In the butterfly Heliconius numata, for example, three inversions involved in wing-pattern polymorphism were shown to have accumulated substantial deleterious mutations, but are maintained as polymorphic due to the reduced viability of homozygotes (Jay et al. 2021). Elevated mutation load has also been identified for multiple inversions in the coral Acropora kenti (Zhang et al. 2024). In contrast, large inversions across multiple species of Helianthus exhibited a lower deleterious mutation load than expected. However, populations that were polymorphic for inversions exhibited a higher mutation load than monomorphic populations, likely due to the recombination suppression in inversion heterozygotes (Huang et al. 2022). Inversions play a role in the genetic differentiation and local adaptation of Atlantic herring, but do not carry increased genetic load. The authors suggest that this is due to the large population size, which allows for effective purifying selection (Jamsandekar et al. 2024). This is also the case for deer mice, where inversion homozygotes are common, and large effective population sizes could facilitate purifying selection (Harringmeyer and Hoekstra 2022).

Our study shares many of these key findings with the studies on deer mice (Harringmeyer and Hoekstra 2022) and Helianthus (Huang et al. 2022), whereby assortative mating or selection can result in deviation from HWE, with an excess of homozygote karyotypes. Deviation from HWE is common at inversions in the two marine populations and the younger freshwater populations. We also find fixation of inversion karyotypes in the four oldest lakes (the lack of within-population polymorphism thus precluding tests for deviation from HWE). In contrast, the fixation of the karyotype A/A (common in marine populations) at chromosomes XI and XXI in the most inbred freshwater population (Jossavannet) is consistent with the idea that inversions can constrain optimal ecological adaptation (Roesti et al. 2022). Our findings of mutation load in two inversions showing limited differences to genome-wide patterns, suggest that recombination between homozygous inversion karyotypes can purge at least some of the excessive deleterious mutation load. However, it is also possible that deleterious mutations never accumulated to strongly elevated levels in these inversions, for example, due to limited time since origin, effective population size, or efficient purifying selection.

In a conservation context, mutation load in a population and its potential negative effect on fitness is often used to estimate the threat to small populations (Dussex et al. 2023). In the Japanese stickleback, it has been shown that isolated freshwater populations accumulate more deleterious mutations than marine populations (Yoshida et al. 2020). Similarly, in nine-spined stickleback, smaller inbred freshwater populations have higher deleterious load (Chen et al. 2025). Our study is among emerging studies that consider the potential impact of increased mutation load in low recombination genomic regions caused by deleterious alleles hitchhiking on positively selected alleles. Understanding the real-life fitness effects of such mutation load requires future experimental validation.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

Adult threespine sticklebacks were collected using unbaited minnow traps or shore-based seine netting from two marine populations, six freshwater populations, and one river population in Norway (Kirch et al. 2021; Laine et al. 2024, 2025). The pectoral or caudal fin was clipped and preserved in 95% ethanol, and the fish were released back, or sticklebacks were euthanized and frozen. The lakes ranged in age from 1,000 to 12,900 years old, and further information on all populations is provided in Table S3.

DNA Isolation, Library Preparation, and Sequencing

DNA was extracted from the fin tissue using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit following the manufacturer's protocol. Libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II FS DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina and sequenced using 150 bp paired-end reads on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 at the Norwegian Sequencing Centre.

Read Alignment and Variant Calling

The quality of the reads was assessed with FastQC v0.12.1 (Andrews 2010). We trimmed adapters, Ns, and low-quality bases using AdapterRemoval v2.3.1 (Schubert et al. 2016). Where necessary, poly-G tails were trimmed with fastp v0.23.4 (Chen et al. 2018). The trimmed reads were mapped to the stickleback reference genome gasAcu1-4 (available at https://datadryad.org/stash/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.547d7wm6t, Roberts Kingman et al. 2021b) using the BWA-MEM algorithm in BWA v0.7.17 (Li and Durbin 2009). This reference genome originates from a freshwater population and includes improvements for the mitochondrial genome and the genomic region of chromosome VII containing the Pitx1 gene. The BAM files were sorted, duplicates were marked, and files originating from the same samples were merged using samtools v1.16.1 (Li et al. 2009).

Genotype calling was performed using the BCFtools v.1.20 (Li 2011) mpileup and call commands with the multiallelic calling model. We removed indels and multiallelic sites with BCFtools v.1.20 to produce an “all sites” data set including 444,582,088 sites. This “all sites” data set was used to calculate the total number of genotyped sites (variant and invariant) within a genomic window for the summary statistics.

Subsequently, we filtered for SNPs and removed sites for which genotypes were missing for more than 30% of the individuals, a Phred quality score of <30, a minimum genotype depth of 10×, and a maximum genotype depth of 100× using VCFtools v0.1.16 (Danecek et al. 2011). The final SNP data set included 1,470,232 sites across 241 samples.

Genome Scan

We estimated nucleotide diversity (π), genetic differentiation (FST), and divergence (dxy) from the “all sites” data set using the Python script popgenWindows.py (github.com/simonhmartin/genomics_general release 0.4, last accessed May 2024) with a sliding 5 kb window and a step size of 1 kb, comparing all possible freshwater–marine, freshwater–freshwater, and marine–marine population pairs. We defined the upstream and downstream flanking regions of the Eda gene as spanning 80 kb on either side, which corresponds with the presence of divergence and differentiation peaks and a low recombination rate (Fig. S3).

Deleterious Variation

To define ancestral and derived alleles, we used whole-genome data from one individual each of outgroup species Gasterosteus nipponicus (NCBI accession: DRR072169), Gasterosteus wheatlandi (NCBI accession: DRR013347), and Pungitius pungitius (NCBI accession: SRR11611426). Mapping and genotype calling were performed as described previously, and the VCF files were filtered using the same parameters. To annotate the ancestral alleles in the VCF file, we used fastDFE v1.1.8 with the maximum likelihood model, specifying the outgroups and requiring at least 15 ingroups at a site (Sendrowski and Bataillon 2024). Based on this, we generated a polarized SNP file in which the reference alleles were replaced with the ancestral annotation and excluded sites that were no longer variants with BCFtools v.1.20.

To identify increased accumulation of deleterious alleles in genomic regions of interest, we used the stickleback reference genome gasAcu1-4 annotation to build a custom database for SnpEff v5.2c (Cingolani et al. 2012). We then annotated the polarized SNP file with SnpEff to predict the putative impact of variants in the entire genome and the regions of interest. The SNPs were classified into the categories high (e.g. stop-gain or frameshift variant), low (e.g. missense variant or inframe deletion), moderate (e.g. synonymous variant), and modifier (non-coding variants or variants affecting non-coding genes).

To assess whether the number of high, moderate, and low impact variants deviates from genome-wide expectations, we used a bootstrap approach. First, we randomly generated 1,000 regions from the rest of the genome that were the same size as the Eda region and the three inversions. We classified the SNPs for each genomic window with SnpEff, as described above, yielding an empirical null distribution of proportions for that region and impact category (Fig. S13). Then, we compared the observed proportions in the regions of interest to this null distribution and computed two-sided empirical P-values to test whether they were significantly higher or lower than expected for each impact category.

To test for increased accumulation of deleterious alleles in inversions compared to the genome-wide background, we calculated the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous variants (pN/pS) using the SnpEff annotation. We filtered the SNP file to include only SNPs where at least one was present as an alternative allele for the respective population using BCFtools v.1.20 and calculated pN/pS in 100 kb nonoverlapping genomic windows for each population for the genome-wide background (excluding the regions of interest) and for the three inversions. The same approach was used to calculate pN/pS for all marine, freshwater, and river populations combined. We performed a two-sided t-test in R v4.3.2 to test for significant differences in pN/pS between the inversions for each population or ecotype and the genome-wide background for that group. Additionally, we corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. For the Eda flanking regions, pN/pS was calculated for the respective populations for the combined upstream and downstream flanking regions (160 kb).

We used a PCA approach to identify the inversion karyotype of each individual. The polarized SNP data set was phased with Beagle v5.4 with 1,000 bp step size and 100 bp overlapping sliding windows (Browning and Browning 2009). Windowed principal components and heterozygosity were calculated using WinPCA v1.2 with a window size of 1 Mb, a step size of 10 kb, and a minor allele frequency of 0.01 along the phased inversion regions (Blumer et al. 2025). To analyze whether the inversion karyotype frequencies in each population were deviated from HWE, which could indicate a deleterious effect of homozygotes, we identified the heterozygous and the two homozygous karyotypes in all individuals using the k-means clustering algorithm in R with K = 3. Based on this, we assigned the cluster in the center of the PCA plot and with the highest heterozygosity as the heterozygous karyotype (A/B), and the other two clusters as the homozygous karyotype (A/A and B/B) (Figs. S9, S10). We then tested for HWE with the HWE.chisq function from the genetics package in R.

Demographic Analysis

We calculated the effective population size for each population with the formula Ne = π/4 μ, using the mean π we estimated and a mutation rate of 5.11 × 10−9 (Zhang et al. 2025).

To estimate the demographic history of each population, we used SMC++ v1.15.2, which uses a sequential Markov coalescent model to infer population size history from unphased genomic data (Terhorst et al. 2017). The SNP file without ancestral alleles was converted to the format used by SMC++ with the vcf2smc command. The model for the population size history was fitted for each chromosome using the SMC++ estimate command with a mutation rate of 5.11 × 10−9 (Zhang et al. 2025) and plotted with a generation time of 2 years.

Recombination Rate Estimation

To infer fine-scale recombination rates from patterns of linkage disequilibrium along the genome for each population, we used pyrho v0.1.7 (Spence and Song 2019). We built lookup tables for each population with the make_table command, using the number of haplotypes as the population size, the Moran approximation method with a sample size 50% larger than the population size, a mutation rate of 5.11 × 10−9, and the demographic history computed by SMC++. Then, pyrho hyperparam was used to determine the most suitable settings, and we selected a window size of 40 and a block penalty of 20. These settings were used with the optimize command to infer recombination maps for chromosome IV.

Distribution of Fitness Effects

A key part of understanding natural selection is the distribution of fitness effects (DFE), which describes the proportion of new mutations that are expected to be deleterious, neutral, and beneficial (Eyre-Walker and Keightley 2007). This can be achieved by contrasting the SFS of putatively neutral (synonymous) and selected (nonsynonymous) sites (Sendrowski and Bataillon 2024). The DFE were estimated using the SNP file polarized with ancestral alleles for the whole genome for each stickleback population using the GammaExpParametrization model with 10 iterations and 100 bootstrap replicates with fastDFE v1.1.8. The DFE results were summarized into four bins of population-scaled selection coefficients: neutral (0 > Nes > −1), weak (−1 > Nes > −10), moderate (−10 > Nes > −100), and strong (−100 > Nes).
