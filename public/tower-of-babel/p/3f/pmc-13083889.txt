Sub-micron-resolution temperature mapping of Zn negative electrode for flow batteries
Authors: Shengnan Wang, Yao Gao, Shixun Wang, Mingzhong Ai, Yihui Guo, Xingjun Liu, Yiqiao Wang, Zhiquan Wei, Jiaxiong Zhu, Qingshun Nian, Cuili Zhang, Lang Wang, Shengbo Lu, Tracy Chenmin Liu, Quan Li, Chunyi Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZinc-based flow batteries are gaining attention as safe, cost-effective, and sustainable energy storage solutions amid global energy transition challenges. However, their practical application is hindered by poor reversibility and dendrite formation of Zn negative electrode, particularly under high state-of-charge conditions. Despite extensive research on Zn side, the relationship between localized temperature distribution and dendrites remains underexplored, primarily due to limited microscopic observation techniques. Here, we present a non-invasive optically detected magnetic resonance with nanodiamond quantum sensors to monitor temperature variations during Zn deposition, achieving a sub-micron spatial resolution ( ~ 300 nm) and a temperature sensitivity of ~2 K/Hz0.5. Our findings suggest that spatial temperature non-uniformity may play a critical role in accelerating dendrite growth and potentially leading to more severe short circuits. Simulations revealed that higher substrate thermal conductivity improves Zn deposition uniformity. Herein, we introduced a flowable gallium-indium liquid metal electrode, which disperses localized heat and lowers interfacial temperature gradients, thereby suppressing hotspot-driven dendrite growth and enabling in situ formation of a liquid Zn alloy. The zinc-bromine flow battery with the liquid metal electrode demonstrated enhanced cycling stability over 2400 hours at a high state-of-charge of 90%, achieving a cumulative discharge capacity of 46.2 Ah cm−2 at 40 mA cm−2.Subject terms: Batteries, Chemical engineering, Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

In addressing the challenges of global energy transition and climate change, flow batteries (FBs) are emerging as a highly efficient and scalable energy storage solution1,2. Their distinctive design allows independent regulation of energy and power, making them particularly suitable for large-scale storage applications, especially for integrating intermittent renewable energy sources such as solar and wind3,4. Zinc-based flow batteries (Zn-FBs) stand out among the various FB technologies due to their notable safety, cost-effectiveness, and environmental sustainability5. The abundant and economical nature of Zn offers a distinct advantage in reducing material costs and advancing the widespread deployment of renewable energy systems6.

However, despite the numerous advantages of Zn-FBs, their practical application is still limited by the poor reversibility of the Zn negative electrode7. During the charging process, Zn deposition often exhibits inhomogeneity due to the inherent nature of liquid-solid phase transformation, which can easily lead to the formation of dendrites and exacerbate undesirable side reactions, as well as the generation of dead Zn8. Moreover, this inhomogeneity intensifies with the increase in state of charge (SOC) and areal capacity, thereby increasing the risk of short circuits and battery failure9. Various strategies have been proposed to tackle this issue, including electrode modification and the addition of electrolyte additives10. Despite these efforts, the improvements remain inadequate under practical testing conditions, such as high current density and high SOC, where the performance still falls short of expectations.

Despite acknowledging the correlation between metal negative electrode behavior and temperature, there has been insufficient exploration of the temperature changes at the microscopic scale during the deposition process in Zn-based batteries11. Additionally, existing temperature monitoring technologies mainly operate at the macroscopic level, failing to capture temperature fluctuations in the nanoscale environment or the impact of localized temperature “hotspots” on dendrite growth12,13. Therefore, effective technology is urgently needed to monitor real-time temperature changes at the microscopic level throughout the Zn deposition and its evolution process. This is beneficial for enhancing our understanding of the mechanisms governing dendrite growth and for improving thermal management in battery systems.

In this work, we demonstrated spatially resolved temperature monitoring during Zn deposition by utilizing optically detected magnetic resonance (ODMR) with nanodiamond quantum sensors to analyze microscale temperature changes. The developed platform achieves sub-micron spatial resolution ( ~ 300 nm) and satisfactory temperature sensitivity ( ~ 2 K/Hz0.5), enabling temperature mapping and concurrent morphological tracking over areas up to 200 μm × 200 μm. We employed nanodiamond sensors in a wide-field configuration to map the spatially resolved temperature variations in a working Zn electrochemical cell, illustrating the spatial non-uniformity of temperature at the onset of internal short circuit, which may play a critical role in accelerating Zn dendrite formation. COMSOL simulations further revealed that increasing the substrate’s thermal conductivity markedly reduced interfacial temperature gradients and improved Zn deposition uniformity, although this benefit diminished as the Zn coverage lowered the effective conductivity. To tackle this problem, we introduced a flowable eutectic gallium-indium (EGaIn) liquid metal (LM) electrode with high thermal conductivity into Zn-FBs. Forced convection within the LM promotes efficient heat transfer and facilitates rapid dissipation of hotspots, thereby reducing temperature gradients that accelerate dendrite growth. In parallel, the in situ alloying of Zn with LM suppresses dendrite formation and mitigates localized heat accumulation, resulting in a highly reversible Zn negative electrode. With enhanced thermal management and dendrite suppression, the zinc-bromine flow battery (ZBFB) demonstrated stable cycling performance, being charged to a SOC of 90% at a current density of 40 mA cm−2 and subsequently delivering a cumulative discharge capacity of 46.2 Ah cm−2.

Results

Spatial temperature distribution of Zn negative electrode

Temperature, as a critical factor influencing Zn morphology, significantly affects the reaction kinetics of the Zn2+/Zn couple and further impacts the plating-stripping process of Zn in Zn-FBs14. The heat generated by electrochemical reactions and Joule heating at high current densities can rapidly form local temperature “hotspots”, further exacerbating the inhomogeneity of Zn deposition15. To investigate the thermal dynamics during Zn deposition with greater precision, we developed a custom-designed real-time in situ ODMR platform capable of mapping the spatial temperature distribution within electrochemical cells. A schematic of the device is presented in Fig. 1a, and the complete wide-field microscopy configuration for spatially resolved temperature sensing is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The cell configurations employed in the ODMR measurements are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Nanodiamonds with nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers (Fig. 1b) are used as temperature sensors for spatially resolved nanoscale temperature measurements in operating electrochemical devices. Their long spin coherence and high thermal conductivity make them ideal materials for sensitive thermal sensing16. A 532 nm laser was focused onto a total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) objective to excite NV centers in nanodiamonds, with the resulting fluorescence imaged via an electron-multiplying charge-coupled device (EMCCD) camera. Since the sensing relies on ODMR, the non-invasive optical characteristics of the measurement protocol ensure minimal disturbance to the testing system during the ongoing electrochemical process16. During the testing process, the spin states of NV centers are manipulated by applying microwaves. The temperature of nanodiamonds containing NV centers is determined by analyzing the zero-field splitting (D) temperature dependence in their ODMR spectra (Fig. 1c). The D, which describes the energy difference between the ms=0 and ms = ±1 state of ground state NV electrons, is highly sensitive to thermal variations17. To quantify the D shift, the ODMR spectrum is fitted to a double-peak Lorentzian function, and the two resonance frequencies are averaged. A clear linear dependence of D on temperature is observed, with a slope of dD/dT = −74 kHz/K derived from ensemble measurements of 170 nanodiamond sensors (Supplementary Fig. 3), reflecting a stable and well-defined temperature sensitivity. On our measurement platform, the sensor updates the temperature in real time at intervals shorter than 1 s (Supplementary Fig. 4). By leveraging these features, our developed platform achieves sub-micron spatial resolution ( ~ 300 nm) and high temperature sensitivity ( ~ 2 K/Hz0.5), enabling wide-field temperature mapping and concurrent morphological tracking over areas of up to 200 μm × 200 μm.

During the constant-current charging of a Zn | |Zn electrochemical cell, time-resolved thermal variations were monitored. Using a wide-field ODMR setup, we first recorded the temporal temperature profile of individual nanodiamond sensors during Zn plating, as exemplified in the upper panel of Fig. 1d (arrow marking the sensor’s position; see also Supplementary Figs. 5–8 for additional examples). By compiling the time‑dependent temperature data from all nanodiamonds within the spatial range, we constructed the spatially resolved map of temperature evolution shown in Fig. 1e, which highlights the temperature non-uniformity across the electrode surface. To analyze the evolution of local temperature rise more clearly, we divided the ODMR temperature maps into four regions (labelled 1, 2, 3, and 4). At the early stage of Zn deposition, approximately 61 s after initiation, the voltage curve displayed a notable drop, indicating that the rapid growth of Zn dendrites had caused a short circuit in the battery. Shortly thereafter, at 66 s, nanodiamond sensors in region 1 detected a sharp and localized temperature surge, exceeding 55 °C, marking the formation of an initial hotspot. As the deposition progressed, a second voltage drop appeared in the charging curve at 130 s, indicating the continued expansion of dendritic Zn regions. This corresponded closely to the substantial enlargement of heated areas observed between 150 s and 280 s in the nanodiamond temperature maps (Supplementary Fig. 9). Further spatial analysis of the dynamic spatiotemporal evolution revealed that, in region 1, high-temperature points expanded progressively from 66 s to 220 s, while in region 2, a similar expansion pattern emerged later, between 100 s and 220 s. This sequential growth of hotspot areas suggests that the initial temperature-rise locations act as preferential nucleation sites for subsequent thermal intensification and dendrite proliferation18. Consequently, early-formed hotspots not only mark the onset of localized heating but also appear to play a pivotal role in driving more extensive and intense thermal events as deposition continues. Analysis of the average temperature in each of the four regions showed that region 1 exhibited a decline after 220 s (Supplementary Fig. 10). This phenomenon may reflect the migration of dendritic growth and its associated Joule heating to other parts of the electrode, rather than intrinsic local cooling. These results indicate that the internal short circuit in batteries caused by dendrite growth may initially occur at very localized sites (on the nanoscale), and the heat generated can trigger further chemical reactions in the vicinity. This implies that even small-scale dendritic formations can lead to significant thermal effects, potentially exacerbating the failure within the battery. Therefore, effective thermal management at the microscale is essential for extending battery lifespan and ensuring operational reliability.

To mitigate the adverse effects of non-uniform temperature distribution on battery performance, we systematically investigated the influence of higher substrate thermal conductivity on Zn deposition behavior. Four substrates, carbon felt (CF), highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG), Zn, and silver, were selected to represent typical conductivity levels in carbon-based and metallic materials (Supplementary Table 1). In the model shown in Fig. 2a, a heat source was applied to the bottom of the module to establish a vertical temperature gradient, allowing for the examination of the effect of thermal non-uniformity on the deposition process. CF, Zn, Ag, and HOPG correspond to the idealized condition in which the intrinsic thermal conductivity of the substrate remains constant and is unaffected by Zn coverage, while a HOPG-Zn Gradient Substrate (HZGS) represents the case where the thermal conductivity gradually transitions from that of HOPG to that of Zn with increasing Zn deposition. The results in Fig. 2b indicate that increasing the substrate thermal conductivity reduces the temperature gradient along the Y direction at the electrode/electrolyte interface. As confirmed in Fig. 2c–f, the temperature difference between regions near and far from the heat source decreases from 20.9 °C for CF to 0.4 °C for HOPG, while the height variation of the deposited layer decreases from 1.2 µm for CF to 0.6 µm for HOPG, leading to markedly improved deposition uniformity. In this scenario, where the influence of deposited Zn on the substrate’s thermal conductivity is neglected, higher substrate thermal conductivity facilitates a more uniform surface temperature distribution over time, thereby promoting homogeneous Zn deposition.

However, when the effect of Zn coverage on substrate thermal conductivity is considered, the advantage of high-conductivity substrates is progressively diminished as deposition proceeds. Using HOPG as an example, which has the highest thermal conductivity among the four substrates, we find that when its thermal conductivity decreases linearly from that of HOPG to that of Zn, its thermal advantage is markedly reduced, although it still outperforms pure CF or Zn substrates (Fig. 2b–f). This indicates that in liquid-solid phase transition processes, simply increasing the initial substrate thermal conductivity cannot sustain its beneficial effect at high areal capacities. With continued Zn accumulation on the substrate, the temperature distribution ultimately becomes governed by the thermal conductivity of Zn. These findings suggest that stable thermal management during prolonged deposition requires more than a fixed high-conductivity substrate; it demands a mechanism for continuous heat transfer and removal.

In flow batteries, electrolyte circulation enables persistent heat exchange at the reaction interface, mitigating temperature non-uniformity. Incorporating LM as a flowable electrode, with thermal conductivity exceeding that of the electrolyte, may combine high heat-transfer efficiency with active heat removal through circulation, offering a potential means of maintaining a uniform temperature field even at high areal capacities. As illustrated in Fig. 2g and h, COMSOL simulations show that the static cell with an LM electrode exhibits a substantially more homogeneous temperature distribution compared to conventional solid substrates (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, relative to the other substrate configurations shown in Fig. 2e, the LM-based cell reaches thermal equilibrium within a markedly shorter timescale ( ~ 0.025 h) and displays a lower temperature gradient after 1 h (1.56 °C for LM versus 5.22 °C for HZSG), underscoring its enhanced thermal management performance. This improvement arises not only from the intrinsic thermal conductivity of the LM, but also from its extensive contact area and large volume fraction, which facilitate bulk heat conduction and promote more homogeneous heat dissipation across the electrode region.

Thermal uniformity of the LM electrode

LMs are a category of materials characterized by distinct physicochemical properties, notably their high thermal conductivity, metallic conductivity, and fluidity19,20. These properties render them particularly suitable for electrochemical energy storage devices, such as FBs, which necessitate the movement of electrolytes. High thermal conductivity facilitates rapid heat dissipation, minimizing temperature fluctuations and enhancing safety. High metallic conductivity enhances energy conversion efficiency, while high fluidity mitigates dendrite growth on the electrode surface, thereby prolonging battery lifespan21.

For LM to be utilized in Zn-FBs, they must exhibit high thermal conductivity, appropriate Zn content, and a low melting point to maintain a liquid state at or near room temperature. Gallium-based LMs, as representatives of room-temperature liquid metals, not only retain the general advantages of LMs but also offer environmentally friendly, recyclable, safe, and self-healing properties22. Three gallium-based LMs fulfilling these criteria were selected, and their compositions and physical properties are presented in Supplementary Table 2. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, the binary EGaIn LM exhibits the highest thermal conductivity, which is 46 times greater than that of the electrolyte. This indicates a high capacity for maintaining thermal balance at the electrolyte/electrode reaction interface. Figure 3b shows the saturation solubility of Zn metal in the three LMs at room temperature, with EGaIn achieving the highest affinity for Zn, reaching a mass fraction of 3.1%, corresponding to a theoretical capacity of ~160 Ah L(LM)−1. Given this high solubility, we further investigated the thermodynamic stability of the Zn-EGaIn system by calculating its phase diagram using the CALPHAD (CALculation of PHAse Diagrams) methodology. As shown in Fig. 3c, the system comprises six phases: Liquid, α (HCP_Zn), η (Tetragonal_In), γ (Orthorhombic_Ga) and their coexistence. The Zn content in EGaIn increases rapidly with rising temperature, leading to a high theoretical Zn storage capacity. In addition, EGaIn demonstrates high stability in the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 11). Overall, this binary LM offers a higher Zn accommodation capacity, improved thermal conductivity, and reduced viscosity. These improvements are critical for promoting uniform Zn deposition, reducing localized heating, and facilitating hydraulic transport of the LM, thereby improving negative electrode reversibility under high areal capacity and high SOC conditions. Under identical 50% SOC testing, the binary EGaIn also delivered a higher CE than the ternary EGaInSn (Supplementary Fig. 12), further confirming its effectiveness. Based on these merits, EGaIn was selected for investigations in this work.

To investigate the influence of a highly thermally conductive LM interface on the Zn deposition process, we integrated EGaIn alloy LM with a Zn negative electrode to assemble a Zn | |LM cell. When charged at a constant current of 50 mA cm−2 for 200 s, no short circuit occurred (Fig. 3d). In contrast, the cell without LM failed due to a short circuit after 61 s (Fig. 1d). The in situ ODMR images corresponding to the deposition process indicated that the temperature of the nanodiamonds across the entire test area remained stable and did not differ significantly from the initial state over time (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 13). Owing to the high spatial resolution and temperature sensitivity of ODMR, each nanodiamond serves as an independent nanoscale thermometer, enabling a direct mapping of local thermal fluctuations during Zn deposition in the LM environment. To further illustrate the microscopic temperature variation process, six randomly selected nanodiamonds were analyzed to show their specific temperature changes over 200 s. As shown in Fig. 3f, Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15, the temperature variation values are centered around 0 °C, confirming the absence of any measurable temperature rise during the Zn2+ ion deposition process in LM. To isolate the influence of the thermal conductivity of flowable LM electrode on FB, we developed a two-dimensional transient model and employed COMSOL to simulate the temperature gradient distribution induced by localized heat generation at the electrode/membrane interface (mimicking a hotspot during a short-circuit) under flow conditions, while excluding electroplating processes. The temperature varies significantly, mainly between the inflow and outflow of the electrolyte, primarily influenced by mass transfer (Fig. 3g–l)14. In comparison to Zn-FBs assembled without LM, batteries using LM demonstrate faster temperature diffusion in the negative electrode region when subjected to heat sources (Fig. 3g and j). The external flow of LM in the Zn-FBs can enhance its rapid thermal conductivity characteristics, further uniformizing potential temperature hotspots on the electrode, stabilizing the plating/stripping process, and contributing to the battery cycle life. As shown in Fig. 3h, i, and Supplementary Figs. 16–18, the temperature variation curves in the X and Y directions of the negative electrode demonstrate that the high thermal conductivity of LM rapidly equilibrates heat distribution, minimizing the impact of heat sources on other regions of the negative electrode. In the absence of LM, the local temperature of the negative electrode rapidly spikes, complicating heat dissipation (Fig. 3k and l). An increased rate of temperature change at the electrode can lead to a more uneven plating/stripping process, further exacerbating the formation of Zn dendrites.

LM-mediated Zn plating and stripping

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were utilized to analyze the morphology and microstructure of the EGaIn LM at three different stages: prior to charging, after charging, and following discharge. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 19, the morphology of the LM remained predominantly unchanged throughout these processes. EDS assessments revealed a consistent elemental distribution of Ga and In across the various states of the EGaIn LM. Notably, after Zn plating, the detected Zn was evenly distributed rather than forming clusters, indicating the formation of the EGaInZn alloy23. Such uniform alloying in the liquid phase effectively avoids the formation of solid dendrites, thereby preventing localized hotspots and associated interfacial temperature fluctuations. Furthermore, no Zn was observed in the elemental maps of the LM after discharge, suggesting that the EGaInZn alloy could completely revert back to EGaIn, and that the LM can serve as an electrode in Zn-FBs. These observations were further validated by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analyses performed on the LM after multiple cycles (Fig. 4a). The quantity of charge injected correlated with the increase in Zn content, and the composition of the LM after discharge matched its initial state, demonstrating that the deposition and dissolution processes of Zn, specifically, the alloying and dealloying reactions within the LM, were entirely reversible. This liquid-liquid phase transition minimizes the mechanical and thermal stress typically observed in solid-state Zn deposition, contributing to a more stable temperature profile during cycling, as evidenced in Fig. 3e. Additionally, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted to investigate the structural characteristics of the LM before and after 60 cycles. The XRD patterns of the Zn-deposited LM displayed a distinctive broad peak around 35.3°, indicative of a liquid alloy phase and suggesting the presence of the EGaInZn alloy (Fig. 4b)24. These findings indicate that, within a fully assembled cell, the EGaIn LM can function as a reservoir for a significant amount of Zn2+ ions during charging and revert to EGaIn upon discharging, thereby enabling reversible liquid-liquid phase transitions at the Zn negative electrode.

To further understand the deposition process of Zn in LM, we analyzed the surface elemental composition and chemical states of LM after charging using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). This analysis confirmed the presence of Ga, In, and Zn (Supplementary Fig. 20). Figure 4c illustrates the Zn 2p spectrum of Zn deposited on both the EGaIn LM and CF. Compared to the spectrum obtained from CF, the positions of the Zn 2p1/2 and Zn 2p3/2 peaks in the LM alloy shift to higher binding energies, indicating the formation of the EGaInZn alloy25,26. Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) further validated these findings. Figure 4d presents signals for ZnIn2GaO5−, ZnGaO2−, and ZnIn2O4−, suggesting that the Zn deposited in LM exists as the EGaInZn alloy27. In contrast to solid-state-deposited Zn, the alloy phase Zn, encapsulated by Ga and In metal in LM, reduces direct contact with the electrolyte, thereby mitigating side reactions, such as electrochemical corrosion of Zn. In Tafel polarization tests, the LM electrode with in situ alloyed Zn (EGaInZn) exhibited a significantly lower corrosion current density than Zn, together with a positively shifted corrosion potential, indicating suppressed corrosion kinetics and enhanced electrochemical stability (Supplementary Fig. 21). Supplementary Fig. 22 presents the differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curve of the LM following Zn deposition, which exhibits notable supercooled and non-solidified characteristics. This indicates that the LM electrode is suitable for use across a broad temperature range. Moreover, the intrinsic broad thermal tolerance of the LM negative electrode enhances the thermal management capability of Zn-FBs, enabling rapid dissipation of transient heat accumulation under high current density conditions. These features emphasize the promise of LMs for creating room-temperature Zn-ion-driven LM batteries with liquid-liquid electrochemical interfaces. Furthermore, the Zn-saturated LM, produced by the natural dissolution of Zn foil at room temperature, demonstrates alloying characteristics (Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24). This suggests that the LM can dissolve the detached dead Zn in the Zn-FB, thereby restoring its capacity.

In FBs, energy density is positively correlated with the concentration of redox species, the number of electron transfers per molecule, and the actual SOC of the electrolyte28. Enhancing SOC of the electrolyte is a straightforward approach to improving the battery’s output energy density in established systems. Furthermore, a higher SOC can decrease the volume of electrolyte required to achieve the rated energy output of the FB, thus reducing operational costs. In Zn-FBs, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 25, an increased SOC in the negolyte not only leads to a greater areal capacity for Zn deposition but also alters the morphology of Zn deposit to shift from a dense, layered structure to a loose, moss-like porous form. XRD results further reveal that as the SOC rises, the (101) crystal plane of electrochemically deposited Zn becomes dominant, surpassing the (002) crystal plane, indicating the inevitability of inhomogeneous deposits (Supplementary Fig. 26)29. At high SOC, loosely deposited Zn that is partially in contact with the electrode, along with non-conductive byproducts, exacerbates the uneven distribution of electrochemical reactions and Joule heating at the interface, thereby intensifying local temperature fluctuations and leading to more severe dendrite growth30. Additionally, these loose deposits are likely to lose contact with the electrode during discharge, allowing them to be carried away by the flowing electrolyte. This results in dead Zn accumulating at the bottom of the tank and causes irreversible capacity loss (Supplementary Fig. 27). Moreover, the variation in deposition morphology at different SOC levels can substantially affect the battery’s performance and cycling stability (Supplementary Fig. 28). These findings indicate that in the traditional liquid-solid conversion method of Zn, high SOC amplifies the unevenness of deposition and temperature distribution at the interface, considerably reducing the reversibility of the Zn negative electrode.

To further investigate the effect of Zn deposition at high SOC on the internal structure of the battery, we employed SEM to analyze the morphology of the electrodes and membranes after 80 cycles. Electrodeposition was performed at 50% SOC with a current density of 40 mA cm−2. In the CF electrode of the Zn-FB without LM, the deposited Zn exhibited a loose structure characterized by larger Zn aggregates and dendritic crystals (Fig. 4e). These dendritic crystals displayed highly irregular shapes and rough surfaces, resulting in increased Zn shedding. Furthermore, XRD analysis indicated that as the number of cycles increased, the formation of the non-conductive byproduct Zn5(OH)8Cl2·H2O on the CF became more pronounced (Supplementary Fig. 29). Such byproduct layers not only hinder electron transport but also act as thermal insulators, aggravating localized heating under high current density. Figure 4f and Supplementary Fig. 30 display the surface morphology of the membrane after cycling. EDS analysis (Fig. 4g) confirmed the presence of Zn deposits and their associated byproducts (chlorine elements) on the membrane surface of the Zn-FB without LM. The growth of the dendritic Zn on the membrane impeded ionic transport efficiency and may contribute to a decline in battery performance through both electrochemical and thermal instability.

In the LM-based Zn-FBs, the LM replaced the CF to serve as the electrode. Consequently, we analyzed the morphology and elemental distribution of the graphite plate (GP) collector in direct contact with the LM. As depicted in Fig. 4h, Supplementary Figs. 30c and 31, no solid-phase Zn deposits appeared on the surface of the collector after cycling, and no Ga or In elements were detected, indicating that there was no formation of solid Zn dendrites or decomposition of the LM. In comparison to the base battery utilizing CF, the membrane of the Zn-FB assembled with LM remained intact and undamaged after undergoing the same cycling conditions (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 32), indicating that all Zn deposits had transformed into a liquid-phase eutectic alloy. These results align with our previous observations, confirming that the EGaIn accepted Zn2+ ions during the charging process, forming a liquid EGaInZn alloy.

Figure 4j schematically illustrates the operating principle of the LM electrode in the Zn-FB system. The LM and electrolyte are simultaneously delivered into the battery reaction chamber, where forced convection within the LM enhances internal heat transfer. Within the reactor, the LM acts as the electrode in contact with the current collector. At the liquid-liquid interface between the electrolyte and LM, Zn2+ ions are reduced to Zn atoms that immediately alloy with Ga and In, forming a liquid eutectic negative electrode in situ. This alloying suppresses dendrite growth and stabilizes deposition, thereby reducing the probability of hotspot generation, while the high thermal conductivity of the LM rapidly dissipates any residual heat. The synergistic effects of dendrite suppression and efficient heat spreading enable a highly reversible Zn negative electrode. The resulting Zn-rich EGaInZn alloy subsequently flows into an external electrolyte reservoir for recirculation. This approach converts the conventional liquid-solid Zn deposition/dissolution process into a liquid-liquid reaction mode, enabling the operation of a fully flow-type battery with enhanced stability, dendrite-free morphology, and high areal capacity.

Zn||Br flow battery performance

Considering the promising results of the dendrite-free Zn alloying process in the flowable LM with high thermal conductivity, we further investigated the overall performance of Zn-FBs utilizing the LM negative electrode. Thus, we assess the feasibility of the design in the ZBFBs, where the positive electrode facilitates the conversion between highly active Br− ions and Br231. First, we investigated the corrosion and side reactions of the Zn negative electrode by analyzing the self-discharge performance of ZBFBs at 50% SOC, with and without LM. After a 12-hour rest period, the charged LM-based ZBFB retained 85.84% of its capacity, while the ZBFB without LM retained only 75.30% (Fig. 5a). Furthermore, under low current density (10 mA cm−2), the LM-based ZBFB delivered higher average Coulombic efficiency (CE) (97.8% for 1 h cycles and 96.6% for 2 h cycles) compared to the conventional ZBFB (94.1% and 93.4%, respectively), confirming that the LM effectively mitigates corrosion-related side reactions (Supplementary Fig. 33). These results can be attributed to the in situ alloying capability of Zn atoms encapsulated in EGaInZn, which allows for their continuous movement out of the reactor. This process mitigates the self-corrosion of Zn due to direct contact with the negolyte and reduces self-discharge caused by electrolyte interpenetration.

Under practical operating conditions, particularly at high SOC and elevated current density, we separately tested the performance of ZBFBs with and without LM. Battery performance was evaluated based on CE and energy efficiency (EE). The cycling performance of the ZBFB with LM was first examined at 50% SOC of the negolyte (Fig. 5b). The developed ZBFB operated for over 800 cycles at a current density of 40 mA cm−2, which is approximately 12 times longer than that of the ZBFB without LM. During this period, the average CE stabilized at 98.5%, while the average EE reached 83.2%. In contrast, the CE of the ZBFB without LM rapidly decreased to 39.8% within 86 cycles, and the EE decreased to 19.8%. To further enhance energy output, we increased the SOC to 90%. The robust LM-based ZBFB operated for more than 500 cycles at an areal capacity of 84.4 mAh cm−2 without significant decay in CE, maintaining an average of 97.8%, as shown in Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 34. Benefiting from this notable cycling stability and optimized SOC, it delivered an energy density of 78.6 Wh L−1 when calculated based on the negolyte volume excluding that of the LM, and 45.9 Wh L−1 when the LM volume was included, both exceeding most previously reported values for Zn-FBs (Supplementary Fig. 35). These findings highlight the full reversibility of Zn alloying and dealloying processes enabled by liquid-liquid phase transitions within the high thermal conductivity LM, which promotes uniform heat dissipation and stable interfacial temperature profiles.

Figure 5d shows the voltage-time profiles of the two ZBFBs at 84.4 mAh cm−2. The fluctuations in the voltage curve of the battery without LM correspond to the formation of dendrites, and the rapid increase in polarisation also leads to an increase in byproducts (Supplementary Fig. 36). Conversely, the ZBFB with LM exhibited stable charge-discharge performance for over 2400 h. Notably, after undergoing hundreds of Zn deposition-dissolution cycles, the LM remained in a liquid form, visually confirming its stability as an electrode material (Supplementary Fig. 37). In addition, stability tests of the battery with lower Zn2+ ion concentration further confirmed the high reversibility of the LM negative electrode (Supplementary Fig. 38). To further demonstrate the broad applicability of LM in Zn-FBs, the ZBFB with LM exhibited high performance across a wide range of current densities from 20 to 60 mA cm−2 (Fig. 5e) and stable CE and EE were observed under different working currents (Supplementary Fig. 39). The ZBFBs assembled with LM were evaluated under practical conditions considering lifetime, cumulative discharge capacity, SOC, and energy density, with the results indicating stable performance (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Table 3)10,32–40.

Although gallium-based LMs have a higher price than conventional Zn electrodes, cycling tests demonstrated that the LM composition remained essentially unchanged after use (Supplementary Fig. 40), enabling direct recovery and reuse in subsequent assemblies without performance degradation (Supplementary Fig. 41). This recyclability substantially reduces the amortized material cost over the system’s lifetime. In addition, the LM configuration eliminates the need for a negative-side CF and achieves >90% electrolyte utilization, thereby lowering both electrode cost and electrolyte consumption per unit of energy compared with conventional ZBFBs. Furthermore, for integrating the LM electrode into a commercial ZBFB stack, only a simple adjustment, operating the stack in a horizontal orientation, was required to prevent gravitational redistribution of the LM, while retaining the original electrolyte flow channels and manifolds (Supplementary Fig. 42). In summary, the notable electrochemical performance, demonstrated recyclability, and setup feasibility highlight the strong potential of LM electrode for practical ZBFB applications.

Discussion

This study demonstrated an approach to addressing the critical challenges associated with Zn-FBs, particularly concerning dendrite formation and thermal management during Zn deposition. We established a custom-built ODMR platform with nanodiamond quantum sensors to effectively monitor temperature fluctuations at the microscopic scale during Zn deposition. This approach achieved sub-micron spatial resolution ( ~ 300 nm) and demonstrated high temperature sensitivity ( ~ 2 K/Hz0.5). Our results highlighted that localized temperature non-uniformity at internal short circuit sites may accelerate dendrite growth, leading to more severe short circuits. COMSOL simulations showed that higher substrate thermal conductivity reduces interfacial temperature gradients and improves deposition uniformity, though this effect diminishes as Zn coverage lowers effective conductivity. Incorporating a high-thermal-conductivity EGaIn LM electrode efficiently dissipates hotspot heat, and forced convection further suppresses temperature-driven deposition instabilities. This innovation also enables the in situ formation of a liquid Zn eutectic alloy, which prevents the growth of solid dendrites, mitigates undesirable side reactions, and alleviates the dead Zn issue. The performance of the assembled ZBFB has been notably enhanced, achieving stable operation for over 2400 h at a high SOC of 90%, along with a high cumulative discharge capacity of 46.2 Ah cm−2 at 40 mA cm−2. These findings highlight the effectiveness of our approach in improving the cycling stability and efficiency of Zn-FBs, thereby supporting their viability for large-scale energy storage applications and contributing to the development of more sustainable energy systems.

Methods

Materials

Zinc bromide (ZnBr2, ≥99.9%), potassium chloride (KCl, ≥99.5%), and 1-ethyl-1-methylpyrrolidinium bromide (MEP, ≥99%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd and used without further purification. All electrolytes were prepared using deionized water. Carbon felt (CF, 4.6 mm, carbon ≥99 %, bulk density 0.12-0.14 g·cm−2) was purchased from Liaoyang J-Carbon Materials Co., Ltd. (China) and used as received. Graphite plate (GP, 2 mm) was purchased from Shanghai Hongjun New Energy Materials Co., Ltd. (China). Polyolefin-based porous membrane was purchased from Daramic® HP (0.1 µm average pore size, 58% ± 7.5% porosity, 900 µm thickness). The liquid metals (LMs) were purchased from HuaTai Metal Materials Co., Ltd. (China) and used as received.

Material characterization

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL-7001F) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were employed to analyze the microstructure and elemental distribution of the electrode, membrane, and liquid metal (LM). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the LM and electrodes were obtained using a Rigaku SmartLab 9 kW diffractometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were gathered with a Thermo Fisher Scientific K-Alpha instrument. Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) analysis was performed using an IONTOF M6 instrument (IONTOF GmbH). Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 720ES) was utilized to determine the elemental concentrations in the LM. The saturation solubility of Zn at 25 °C ± 1 °C was evaluated through experiments by adding excess Zn foil in the LMs and keeping the samples in a vacuum chamber for over 30 days. After this period, the Zn content in the homogeneous liquid phase of the LM was analyzed using ICP-MS. At this time, the LM is referred to as Zn-saturated LM. For all other tests of various LMs (pristine, charged, and discharged), unless otherwise specified, measurements were conducted at 25 °C ± 1 °C without the use of a vacuum chamber. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, Netzsch DSC 200 F3) was conducted to measure the freezing point of the LMs, with a cooling rate of 1 °C min−1 over a temperature range of −40 °C to 40 °C.

Nanodiamond-based temperature sensing

The Zn | |Zn cell consists of two pieces of Zn foil (20 µm, 2 mm × 5 mm) immersed in the electrolyte and attached to the substrate. The Zn | |LM cell was assembled in a similar manner to the Zn | |Zn cell, with one Zn foil replaced by an LM electrode, both immersed in the electrolyte and attached to the substrate. The two electrodes were placed diagonally to ensure that the short-circuit location falls within the temperature-monitoring range, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2. The temperature measurement area is 100 μm × 100 μm. Temperature sensing based on nanodiamonds was performed using continuous-wave (cw) optically detected magnetic resonance (ODMR). Nanodiamonds with a high density of nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers ( > 1000 NVs, FND Biotech) were dispersed onto a cover glass and randomly distributed across the measurement area. A microwave field was applied using a copper (Cu) wire ( ~ 25 μm in diameter) positioned between two electrodes. The local temperature during the electrochemical process was monitored at the nanoscale by simultaneously measuring multiple nanodiamonds sensors using a wide-field fluorescence microscope. A 532 nm laser was employed to optically pump the NV center spins within the nanodiamonds, while the microwave field, delivered via the Cu wire, was used to manipulate the spin states of the NV centers. The resulting fluorescence emitted by the nanodiamonds was collected using an electron-multiplying charge-coupled device (EMCCD; Evolve 512, Photometrics). Experimental parameters, including laser power, microwave amplitude, and acquisition time, were rigorously optimized to achieve high thermal sensitivity. The ODMR spectrum of the nanodiamond sensors was normalized to the fluorescence counts obtained off-resonance. The normalized spectrum was subsequently fitted using a nonlinear least-squares method with a double-peak Lorentzian function. The resonant frequencies of the two peaks were extracted from the fitting results, and the zero-field splitting (D) was calculated as the average of the two resonant frequencies. To enable local temperature determination in real-time experiments, the D0 at room temperature (T0) was calibrated prior to the electrochemical measurements by a standard thermometer. Then the calibration of the temperature dependence of the D (dD/dT) was performed using a controlled heating process in a water bath. ODMR spectra were acquired by sampling 31 microwave-frequency points. For each time point, one temperature readout was obtained from an integrated ODMR spectrum generated by accumulating 12 consecutive spectra (acquisition time per spectrum ∼0.83 s; fixed total integration time tint = 10 s for all time points). The resonance shift ΔD was extracted from the integrated ODMR spectrum and converted to temperature change ΔT using the calibrated dD/dT. In Figs. 1d and 3f, Supplementary Figs. 5 and 7, the data points show ΔT. Error bars indicate the estimated temperature uncertainty ( ± σ) for the integration time tint. The uncertainty was calculated from the individually determined temperature sensitivity ηT for each ND, with units of K/Hz0.5 (typical values around 2 K/Hz0.5), according to σ = ηT/tint0.5.

Electrochemical characterization

A zinc-based flow battery (Zn-FB) was assembled by placing a Daramic® HP membrane (3 × 3 cm2) between two carbon felt (CF, 2 × 2 cm2) electrodes, which were secured by two graphite plates (GP). The effective reaction area of the electrodes in the Zn-FB is 2 × 2 cm2. For the construction of a Zn | |Br flow battery (ZBFB), an negolyte containing 1 mol L−1 ZnBr2 and 3 mol L−1 KCl was utilized, while a posolyte with a bromine complexing agent was employed to reduce the diffusion of corrosive bromine, incorporating 0.4 mol L−1 MEP. In the LM-based battery, no CF was used on the negative side; instead, LM was introduced into the negolyte tank and circulated through the reactor along with the negolyte. Various electrolytes were circulated throughout the batteries using peristaltic pumps, maintaining a constant flow rate of 50 mL min−1, without any additional flow channel design. To ensure optimal contact between the LM and the current collector, the battery reactor is maintained in a horizontal orientation during testing, with the negative electrode positioned at the bottom and the positive electrode at the top. During long-cycle testing of the ZBFBs at different states of charge (SOC), the volumes used were 5 ml for the LM, 7 ml for the negolyte, and 15 ml for the posolyte. The SOC of the ZBFB is calculated as the actual charged capacity divided by the theoretical capacity of the negolyte. For 7 ml negolyte, 100% SOC corresponds to a charge capacity of 375.2 mAh. The batteries were operated at a constant current density, and the charging process was limited by capacity. The discharge process ceased when a cutoff voltage of 0.1 V was reached. The reported energy density was based solely on the negative electrode side of the ZBFB. All battery performance tests were conducted using a CT3001A LAND Battery Testing System.

The Zn | |GP flow cell was constructed using a Zn plate (2 cm × 2 cm × 0.05 cm) attached to CF (2 cm × 2 cm × 0.42 cm) as the positive electrode, and GP as the negative electrode, separated by a Daramic® HP membrane positioned between the two electrodes. No CF was employed on the negative side, allowing Zn2+ ions to directly deposit onto the GP substrate. Both the negolyte and posolyte were identical, consisting of 1 mol L−1 ZnBr2 and 3 mol L−1 KCl. The test involved plating a specified amount of Zn metal onto the GP substrate, followed by stripping the Zn metal from the GP substrate using a cut-off voltage. All these electrochemical tests were carried out at 25 °C ± 1 °C, no climatic/environmental chamber was used.

Phase diagram calculation

The phase diagram of the Zn-EGaIn system was calculated using the CALPHAD (CALculation of PHAse Diagrams) methodology41–43. The sub-regular solution model was applied to describe the liquid phase and terminal solid solution phases, with the excess Gibbs energy expressed using the Redlich-Kister polynomial. The thermodynamic parameters for the constituent binary systems, Ga-In, Ga-Zn, and In-Zn, were taken from critically assessed databases and literature. The Ga-In, Ga-Zn, and In-Zn binary systems are entirely composed of liquid phase and solid solution phases. The thermodynamic parameters were based on previously optimised parameters that reproduced the experimental phase equilibria and thermodynamic properties reliably. Subsequently, the ternary Ga-In-Zn system was modeled by introducing suitable ternary interaction terms. Phase parameters were refined using the PARROT module to align the calculations with available phase equilibria and thermodynamic measurements. Reported experimental datasets were used as initial inputs, with weights assigned based on their reliability. The parameters were iteratively modified until the results agreed with most experimental observations within acceptable error limits.

Heat transfer in static Zn half‑cell: A two-dimensional transient multiphysics model was developed in COMSOL Multiphysics by coupling the Heat Transfer in Solids and Fluids module with the Tertiary Current Distribution interface to investigate the influence of substrate thermal conductivity on Zn deposition under continuous heat flux.
