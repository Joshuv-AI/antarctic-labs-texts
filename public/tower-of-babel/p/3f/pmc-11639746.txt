QF-LCA dataset: Quantum Field Lens Coding Algorithm for system state simulation and strong predictions
Authors: Philip Baback Alipour, Thomas Aaron Gulliver
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantum field lens coding algorithm (QF-LCA) dataset is useful for simulating systems and predict system events with high probability. This is achieved by computing QF lens distance-based variables associated to event probabilities from the dataset produced by field lenses that encode system states on a quantum level. The probability of a state transition (ST), doubles in prediction values at the decoding step, e.g., ST probabilities of P≥1/3 into P≥2/3 based on a single field (SF) transform into a quantum double-field (QDF). This transformation doubles the ST probability space via the field's scalar κ, in a published QDF method article. A QDF, as a double-field computation (DFC) model, simulates thermodynamic systems in predicting events by producing datasets from a QF-LCA, using laser cooling methods relative to high energy systems. The dataset can be used to, e.g., train quantum algorithms via quantum artificial intelligence (QAI) for a QF-LCA user. The user then decides after the algorithm predicts and suggests an efficient energy path for the system to choose. For example, an N-particle system simulated as a heat engine, by QAI classifier(s), predicts and reroutes particle energy paths on a logarithmic input-output scale. To determine system's energy change, the QDF lens code (DFC) measures entanglement entropy (EE). The algorithm's classification of EE values distinguishes entangled states in the system. The QF-LCA program employs the dataset to achieve an automated prediction and classification method, rather than dataset's manual use and analysis by the user. As the system evolves in its distribution of states, those particles not reaching a desired energy state (a target state or TS), i.e., the probability of observing a ground or excited state (GS or ES) outcome at the decoding step, can be rerouted by the heat engine to satisfy a TS outcome. This establishes a GS or ES energy profile to access and classify states by a classifier. The data points (qubits) in this profile are inverse distance-based and labelled for a specific class. After learning the profile, the classifier decodes and predicts the next system state. A QDF AI game “Alice & Bob's Quantum Doubles,” is developed to validate the dataset as the P’s map for a classical/quantum prediction where the P’s of states and the user's P’s correlate in their value difference, ΔP. Dataset validation results are mapped to an intelligent decision simulator (IDS) as a QAI map. This maximizes system efficiency on a TS by EE of energy states (distributed in the system). Future additions to the dataset from the QAI map program can improve quantum algorithms to determine which particles of the system participate in a phase transition after state prediction. QF-LCA applications are in data science, security, forensics, particle physics, etc., such as retrieving or reconstructing information by distinguishing particle states from an evidence sample. Examples are, reconstruct damaged DNA strands of cells to predict a virus's TS, or cancer cell, its spread and growth against healthy cells, identify forged documents from genuine based on QDF's P values, and so on.Keywords: Transition probability, QF-LCA dataset, Qubit, Quantum double-field (QDF) computation, QDF transform, Quantum circuit, Quantum artificial intelligence (QAI), QDF AI game

QAI probability map as a cheat sheet for the user

The QAI probability P map is developed based on QAI classifiers presented in [1]. Classifiers are coded in the game, Listing 5, to address limitations on an event prediction outcome [5, Section 2]. As the game conditions, e.g., if-else statements, compared to Listing 5, expand and get more complex on the weighted values w, lens distances from Eq. (2b), etc., are profiled according to [1, pp. 25-28]. This requires more coding and debugging on the conditions part for the upgraded version of this game. The map enables the user to make strong predictions bidirectionally: 1- starting from the range of 2/3 upwards vs. complementary values, and 2- from a 1.0 hit probability downwards in a successful hit gaining the perfect weight of w=1. The greater the weight, the farther the probability of being a classical event (e.g., n=1 of Table 2), and thus being a strong quantum prediction (e.g., n=2 of Table 2). Classically, the event outcome is uncertain to be a 0 or 1 as a quantum event, and can be both as in superposition between qubit states, see row n=4 Table 2, which obeys the entropy bounds of the rightmost column of ([1], Table 2). For example, a probability of P(In)=0.71 from the user as Bob, with the help of Eve in the game, is combined with the dataset as Bob's cheat sheet, see Fig. 9 (cont.). The 〈Psuccess〉 from Eq. (7) is determined to be high or ≈1 when w approaches 1, and is <1 as the weight increases showing a greater distance from a classical prediction. In the latter, the expected P|ij〉 or 〈M(P,ψij)〉 is a strong prediction of the quantum event, or a weak prediction of a classical event outcome. QDF hybrid security for future work

With the advent of post-quantum cryptography (PQC) [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]], PQC replaces elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) and Rivest-Shamir-Adleman (RSA) cryptosystems [20,23], from smart phones to blockchains that are affected by potential threats. Examples of threats by quantum algorithms, and quantum-resistant Alice and Bob's key exchange protocols (KEP) to resist attacks, are given by [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. Section 4.4.3 exemplified a fault-tolerant environment as a solution to resist fault-based attacks from quantum servers (see demo files from [2]).

Quantum computer attacks can be efficiently resisted by employing a hybrid quantum system and security. A hybrid QDF circuit can employ Shor's algorithm [5, pp. 9-10] as the QFT-to-QFT−1 circuit coupled with a classical processor [1]. This hybrid architecture is comparable to hybridized simulators such as Q-Kit [25] for large scale quantum simulations using 20-40 qubits, with a logical time cost. The QDF encoding-decoding method obeys QF-LCA protocols for its data transmission [1, pp. 10-13]. This is presented as matter condensation (BEC) to store qubits (q-info), Eq. (5), compressing data at BEC temperatures for encoding qubit pairs in the heat engine, Section 4.1. At the decoding layer of ED in Fig. 4, QDF data are decompressed in the engine, and then decoded as classical from the QDF circuit [1, Algorithm 2], Eq. (5). This data operation is maintained in a closed system with (-out) entanglement prior to decoherence with environmental variables that cause information loss, errors, and attacks. Quantum attacks might be an issue when the heat engine measuring events of a thermodynamic system is exposed to larger (open) systems [1,3,4], including KEP in a QDF key distribution of that system [4, Sections 1–2.2.2, Appx. A].

A quantum computer with a QDF circuit can work with a classical computer as a hybrid to implement a quantum safe environment [5,26]. This is done by employing the QAI map and QF-LCC, Section 4.4. The classical output on the classical computer comes from a QDF, inverse from an SF input, based on Eqs. (2) and (5)’s QDF operations. In this model, the classical output is validated as algorithm's classical bit count performance based on Table 2’s b‾(|ij〉) results. The QDF circuit produces a QDF from SF in the system while running quantum processors connected to classical processors as parallel threads, sharing the same memory space in this hybrid process.

This hybrid model requires the QAI map to play the middle role in predicting results as qubit pairs, then decode them as classical bits, and feed them to one of the classical algorithms to encode data in parallel. This is possible via QFT encoding-decoding [1,5], or ED from Fig. 4, with Table 2’s classical b‾(|ij〉) results. This is proposed as a hybrid computer in Section 4.2, which can operate as a secured I/O data operations transition stack (DOTS), or a QDF-PQC hybrid process:

The product of this process reiterates within DOTS, which flows from either the PQC classical side or QDF side. Thus, the pre-fetching of classical bits during parallel operations (threads) of the upper part in Fig. 11 via the QAI algorithm, is within the middle mapping role of (qu-)bit pair P’s at Eve's point. This makes HQDF/PQC secured on both I/O ends i.e., at the “top bottom” of the stack in Eq. (8), the “QFT-to-classical,” over the channel. The top of the stack becomes trivial in terms of noise. This is where quantum events occur, such as entanglement and superposition within the uncertainty range (∞), or see line #2531 of Listing 5, to extract information prior to Eve and Bob without having specific encryption-decryption key exchanged in the QDF circuit space as Alice's information. Alice holds a specific measurement point made at 〈M(P,ψij)〉 in Table 2, from the system via BEC traps [1]. Alice as a photoprobe [1, p. 10] encodes 2n≤N states (or n qubits) as entangled qubit pairs. The is Alice's key which Eve can decode by knowing the BEC trap qubits [1, pp. 7-15], Section 4.2. Eve can share this key with Bob linked via BEC traps. Qubit pairs are registered in quantum memory addresses as qubit registers [1, pp. 10-13]. Here, classical security protocols can place security barriers over KEP, e.g., side-channel security against attacks implemented in a high-performance architecture [22]. Such potential attacks can occur during this combined set of operations (parallel, bi-directional, and predictive), only when the QDF information is shared at Eve's point having Alice's QDF message or key to decode, and/or the DF on the recipient side as Bob. Either way, the system is secured against attacks in this ED hybrid operation.
