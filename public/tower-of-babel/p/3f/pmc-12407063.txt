Doping-tunable charge ordering in semiconducting single-layer Cr2Se3
Authors: Sisheng Duan, Jian Gou, Zhihao Cai, Jingcong Hu, Yihe Wang, Hui Ru Tan, Ming Lin, Jialin Zhang, Baojie Feng, Kian Ping Loh, Feipeng Zheng, Andrew T. S. Wee, Wei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The charge density wave (CDW), a charge ordering phase, offers a valuable framework for exploring electron-electron interactions, electron-phonon coupling, and quantum phase transitions. In CDW materials, carrier density substantially influences the ground state, typically altered through foreign ion doping and investigated at macro- or mesoscopic scales via photoemission or transport techniques. However, atomic-scale visualization, particularly in doped CDW systems without foreign ions, remains rare. Here, we present real-space observation of doping-tunable granular charge ordering using scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) in semiconducting single-layer Cr2Se3, a group VIB transition metal chalcogenide (TMC). Observations of lattice distortion, bandgap modulation at EF, and STM contrast inversion at low temperatures indicate a charge ordering origin. Cr2Se3’s semiconducting nature enables charge ordering modulation through doping: Hole doping suppresses it, whereas electron doping alters the pattern, yielding a periodic 3√3 × 3√3 CDW phase. This tunable charge ordering in a group VIB TMC advances the understanding of charge doping and ordering interactions in two-dimensional materials.

INTRODUCTION

Charge ordering is a broken-symmetry ground state characterized by spatially modulated electronic charge density (1). As a charge ordering phase, the charge density wave (CDW) in metallic crystals often coexists with emergent phenomena such as unconventional superconductivity (2, 3), antiferromagnetism (4, 5), Mott insulators (3), and excitonic insulators (6). This interplay has sparked notable research interest in CDW due to their potential to unveil insights into the fundamental physics of these electron-correlated phases. Originally, the concept of CDW emerged from one-dimensional (1D) and quasi-1D systems and has since been extended to other low-dimensional systems, including transition metal chalcogenides (TMCs; MX2, M = Ti/Zr/Hf/V/Nb/Ta, X = S/Se/Te) (6–11), kagome lattice materials (AV3Sb5, A = K/Rb/Cs) (12–14), oxide-layered materials (15–17), and more. However, theoretical mechanisms behind CDW emergence, such as Fermi surface nesting, electron-phonon coupling, exciton condensation, and saddle point singularity theory (18–21), remain subjects of ongoing debate. Furthermore, most reported TMCs exhibiting CDW belong to group IVB and group VB elements (6–11); thus, developing new TMCs materials with CDW is still a challenge. Advances in the synthesis of new TMCs materials with multiple quantum phases are anticipated to lead to exciting discoveries and offer deeper insights into the physical mechanisms governing CDW.

Beyond exploring CDW phenomena in intrinsic materials, various methods have been developed to tune the CDW phase and its associated electronic properties, which include doping, applying high pressure, using gate voltage, and modifying layer thickness (3, 6, 8, 22–24). Among these, doping stands out for its simplicity and practicality as it does not require extreme physical conditions or intricate external controls (6). Now, doping is predominantly accomplished through intercalation or atomic substitution. Although effective, these approaches may introduce impurities or defects that complicate the understanding of the underlying mechanisms (22, 25). Alternatively, surface charge transfer doping, performed by introducing free carriers from substrates during growth, offers a straightforward, nondestructive, and efficient approach to tuning CDW properties (6, 26). Despite its advantages, direct atomic-scale visualization of the CDW tuning process via surface charge transfer doping remains rarely reported.

Here, we present real-space observation of a doping-tunable charge ordering phase in semiconducting single-layer Cr2Se3, a group VIB TMC, prepared on single-crystal graphite. Using STM/scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS), we identified the charge ordering characterized by granular patterns at low temperatures, which exhibits contrast inversion between opposite-bias STM images. The presence of a small bandgap at the Fermi level (EF), with its edges modulated by the charge ordering, provides compelling evidence of the CDW origin of this phenomenon. Furthermore, temperature-dependent STM measurements revealed signatures of a CDW phase transition, and noncontact atomic force microscopy (nc-AFM) verified in-plane lattice distortions associated with charge ordering. Crucially, we found that the semiconducting nature of Cr2Se3 allows charge ordering to be tuned via doping. Hole doping, facilitated by localized defect aggregation, suppresses the charge ordering, whereas electron doping, achieved by using a series of graphene substrates with progressively lower work functions, modifies the charge ordering and finally results in a regular 3√3 × 3√3 CDW pattern.

RESULTS

MBE growth and crystal structure of single-layer Cr2Se3

Unlike conventional transition metal dichalcogenides with a layered van der Waals (vdW) structure, chromium selenide ordinarily tends to adopt a non-vdW growth mode that resembles a bulk structure (27, 28), where chromium and selenium atomic layers are alternately stacked with valence bonds. Nevertheless, using molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), high-quality single-layer Cr2Se3 with a five-atomic-layer thickness can be successfully synthesized (27, 29). Figure 1A shows a large-scale STM topography of single-layer Cr2Se3 grown on single-crystalline graphite, revealing submonolayer coverage. The measured island thickness of 1.03 ± 0.02 nm (Fig. 1A, inset), consistent with a five-atomic-layer Se-Cr-Se-Cr-Se stacking (Fig. 1E), was confirmed by high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images (fig. S1). Atomic-resolution nc-AFM and STM images (Figs. 1B and 2E) reveal that surface Se atoms form a close-packed hexagonal lattice with a lattice constant of 0.369 ± 0.01 nm, aligning with the underlying graphite substrate (lattice constant: 0.246 nm). This establishes a 2 × 2 Cr2Se3 supercell commensurate with the 3 × 3 graphite unit cell, verified by low-energy electron diffraction (LEED; Fig. 1C). Hence, the five-atomic-layer structure of Cr2Se3 (Fig. 1, D and E) has been confirmed by systematic STM, nc-AFM, HAADF-STEM, and LEED analyses.

To model the effects of electron correlation in the established crystal structure, we performed density functional theory (DFT) + U simulations, considering both ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic ground states (fig. S2). The DFT + U band structure for an antiferromagnetic ground state with U = 2 eV (Fig. 1F) closely matches published angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) data of Cr2Se3 grown on bilayer graphene (BLG)/4H-SiC(0001) (fig. S4 for the bandgap determination (30)]. The positioning of EF near the conduction band edge indicates n-type doping induced by the electron transfer from the graphite substrate (31), which is also verified with the field-emission resonance spectroscopy measurement (fig. S5). The agreement between the calculated electronic properties and experimental results further validates the proposed crystal structure and magnetic configuration.

Charge ordering in single-layer Cr2Se3

In the charge-ordered phase, the charge redistribution forms alternating regions of charge accumulation and depletion. In negative-bias STM images, these regions exhibit enhanced and reduced intensities, respectively, with the inverse observed under positive bias. This behavior, known as contrast inversion, serves as compelling evidence of charge ordering (7). In addition, the charge ordering is characterized by a bandgap at EF, with the bandgap edge spatially modulated by the charge-order pattern (32, 33). Furthermore, the formation of charge ordering is associated with lattice distortions via electron-phonon interactions (34).

In our experiment, we observed a temperature-dependent electronic ground state of single-layer Cr2Se3 using STM. At 4.3 K, the STM image in Fig. 1A shows a granular state. However, as the temperature increases from 4.3 to 77 K (Fig. 2, A to E), the granular patterns gradually diminish, and a close-packed hexagonal lattice at 77 K finally dominates the surface topography (Fig. 2E). This temperature dependence of the surface granular states suggests a signature of charge ordering, with the phase transition temperature identified between 30 and 77 K. Bias-dependent dI/dV maps (figs. S6, S7, and S24) reveal that the grain size is independent of the bias voltage ranging from −300 to 200 meV, ruling out the explanation of the granular pattern as quasiparticle interference (QPI) induced by impurities or island edges.

Systematic STM and nc-AFM studies were performed on Cr2Se3 to confirm the charge ordering origin of the granular pattern. First, in Fig. 2 (A and F), contrast inversion is observed in STM images with opposite biases at 4.3 K. For comparison, the STM images with opposite biases at 77 K (Fig. 2, E and J) do not exhibit contrast inversion. This reveals that the STM contrast inversion is an intrinsic property of the observed granular pattern, indicating that the phase transition is primarily of electronic character. In addition, the spatial charge redistribution, being the origin of STM contrast inversion, can be demonstrated in the local contact potential difference (LCPD) grid map (fig. S17). Furthermore, a bandgap of ~30 meV is observed at EF (Fig. 2G and fig. S18) at 4.3 K, which also gradually diminishes with increasing temperatures. Spatially resolved dI/dV spectra (Fig. 2, H and I) demonstrates the modulation of bandgap edges by the granular pattern. To further validate the lattice distortions associated with charge ordering, nc-AFM and STM images were taken from the same region (Fig. 2K and fig. S8). The atomic-resolution nc-AFM image, showing surface Se atom positions, was analyzed using the Lawler-Fujita algorithm (35–37) to reveal the in-plane lattice distortion (Fig. 2, L and M). The consistency between the lattice distortion (Fig. 2, L and M) and STM topography (fig. S8A) further confirms the structural distortion accompanied by the charge ordering formation (figs. S8 and S23). In addition, considering the nonzero magnetic momentum of the chromium ions, we performed magnetic field–dependent STM measurements on the granular pattern to examine whether the granular pattern responds to an applied magnetic field. If the granular pattern were due to noncolinear magnetic structures—which, in some cases, can be detected even with a non–spin-polarized tip (38)—then applying a sufficiently strong magnetic field could tilt the magnetic moments, resulting in observable changes in the STM contrast. The absence of contrast inversion for STM images with the opposite magnetic fields (Fig. 2, N and O, and fig. S19) indicates the charge ordering rather than spin ordering origin of the granular pattern.

Doping tunability of charge ordering

The semiconducting nature of single-layer Cr2Se3 allows for high tunability of the charge ordering through doping. We noticed that the doping of Cr2Se3 can be achieved both intrinsically by aggregation of defects and externally by using substrates with different work functions. Near some edges of the Cr2Se3 island, we found that the aggregation of p-type defects (fig. S9A) shifts the Fermi level into the bandgap, which quenches the charge ordering in Cr2Se3. Figure 3 (A and B) presents the STM and nc-AFM images of Cr2Se3/graphite from the same scanning window at the boundary (fig. S9A). The constant-current STM image (Fig. 3A and fig. S9B; VS = −100 mV) reveals two distinct regions with the lower left and upper right areas designated as region I and region II, respectively. In the STM image, region I appears elevated compared to region II, where the charge ordering is suppressed. However, the nc-AFM image (Fig. 3B and fig. S9C) indicates that the physical heights of both regions are identical (39, 40), suggesting an electronic rather than structural origin for the height difference observed in the STM topography.

To further understand these phenomena, spatially resolved LCPD (41, 42) (Fig. 3D and fig. S10) and dI/dV spectra (Fig. 3E) measurements were performed along the red arrow in Fig. 3A. As shown in Fig. 3D, the LCPD in region II is ~0.2 eV higher than in region I, indicating hole doping in region II. The spatially resolved dI/dV spectra in Fig. 3E also demonstrates an upward band bending in region II, where the Fermi level shifts toward the center of the bandgap, rendering this region semiconducting and suppressing the granular charge ordering pattern. Instead, a 2 × 2 moiré pattern originating from the interface states (43) between Cr2Se3 and the graphite substrate (Fig. 3C) is easily observed in the STM image at a bias voltage within the bandgap.

To investigate the impact of electron doping, we examined the behavior of charge ordering in single-layer Cr2Se3 grown on substrates with lower work function than graphite. Epitaxial graphene on 4H-SiC(0001) with varying layer numbers—including tetralayer graphene, trilayer graphene (TLG), bilayer graphene (BLG), and monolayer (MLG) graphene—were used in this study. Systematic structural analyses (figs. S20 and S21 and table S2) demonstrate that Cr2Se3 on different substrates exhibits consistent crystal structures. The work function of epitaxial graphene surface is lower than that of graphite and decreases with layer number deduction (44). Consequently, the extent of electron doping increases as the number of graphene layers decreases. Figure 3 (F to I) depicts typical STM images (VS = −200 mV) of single-layer Cr2Se3 grown on tetralayer graphene, TLG, BLG, and MLG, respectively. Cr2Se3 grown on all these substrates exhibits granular charge ordering, with the grain size progressively decreasing (Fig. 3, F to I) and grain density increasing (Fig. 3K) as the substrate work function decreases. Notably, the aperiodic granular pattern of Cr2Se3 on thicker graphene evolves into a periodic charge-ordered pattern for Cr2Se3/MLG, which serves as fingerprint evidence for a CDW. The Fourier transform of the STM image in Fig. 3I, shown in Fig. 3L, highlights Bragg peaks (blue circles), CDW peaks (red circles), and CDW satellite peaks (figs. S15 and S16). A quantitative analysis confirms that the periodic charge ordering corresponds to a 3√3 × 3√3 CDW phase (Fig. 3M and fig. S11).

Moreover, contrast inversion in the STM image with a positive bias voltage (VS = 100 mV; Fig. 3J) further supports the CDW origin of the 3√3 × 3√3 periodicity. To confirm this, we applied the analysis method by Spera et al. (7), showing that the contrast difference between the STM images in Fig. 3 (I and J) is mostly due to an inversion of the periodic 3√3 × 3√3 pattern. First, Fourier filtering is used to isolate the CDW component from the rest of the image. This CDW component was then inverted and combined with the remaining part of the original image to generate Fig. 3N. The agreement between Fig. 3 (M and J) provides strong evidence that the observed contrast inversion originates from the CDW.

Formation mechanism of CDW

To elucidate the formation mechanism of CDW in Cr2Se3, we conducted DFT calculations to investigate its electronic properties. The computed band structure of the intrinsic freestanding Cr2Se3, depicted in Fig. 4A, indicates its semiconducting nature, with the valence band maximum and conduction band minimum (CBM) located at the Γ and K points, respectively. To explore electron doping effects that happened in the experiment, we calculated the band structure of electron-doped single-layer Cr2Se3. As seen in Fig. 4B, the Fermi level shifts upward to the CBM with a doping level of around 0.1 electron (e−) per unit cell, comparable to the doping level resulting from the work function disparity between Cr2Se3 and epitaxial MLG.

We computed the phonon dispersion for electron-doped single-layer Cr2Se3 in a (1 × 1) structure, presented in Fig. 4C. The presence of imaginary frequencies near the Γ point suggests a tendency for CDW phase transition in Cr2Se3. Considering the 3√3 × 3√3 periodicity of the CDW pattern in Cr2Se3/MLG, we begin with Cr2Se3/MLG to elucidate the CDW formation mechanism. The Brillouin zone of the 3√3 × 3√3 CDW supercell is shown in Fig. 4D, compared to the 1 × 1 normal phase unit cell. The K points of the 1 × 1 unit cell fold to the Γ point of the supercell. In addition, the Fermi energy equienergy contour at this doping level is displayed in Fig. 4F, with the vector of the 3√3 × 3√3 superlattice charge modulation connecting the states on the electron pockets. Consequently, the nested Fermi level eigenstates stabilize the system by forming a 3√3 × 3√3 CDW phase.

We further investigate how electron concentration affects the formation of charge ordering. With the work function of the substrate increasing with additional graphene layers, the extent of electron doping decreases, leading to shrinkage of the electron pocket at the Fermi level (Fig. 4E). This contraction reduces the magnitude of potential nesting vectors, ultimately decreasing the density of charge ordering grains. To elucidate the transition of Cr2Se3 from a granular charge ordering phase to a regular CDW phase as the number of graphene layers decreases (Fig. 3, F to I), we computed the phonon dispersion for Cr2Se3 at varying electron concentrations. Our findings indicate that the intensity of charge order increases with higher electron doping (fig. S12). Consequently, the transition from an aperiodic to a periodic pattern observed in Fig. 3 (F to I) is attributed to the enhanced charge order intensity in Cr2Se3 with elevated electron concentrations (fig. S22).

DISCUSSION

The observation of granular charge ordering phase in single-layer Cr2Se3 represents a significant advancement in the field of 2D CDW materials, particularly in group VIB TMCs. The study demonstrates that electron doping of Cr2Se3 effectively induces a transition from an intrinsic semiconductor to a metallic state, with a notable reduction in charge ordering grain size and a transition to a 3√3 × 3√3 CDW phase in Cr2Se3/MLG, likely due to Fermi surface nesting. This tunability of charge ordering phase through electron concentration, and its elimination via hole doping, underscores the complex interplay between charge doping and CDW phases. These findings not only broaden our understanding of CDW phenomena in 2D semiconductors but also open avenues for research into their potential applications in electronic and quantum devices.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

The 2D Cr2Se3 was synthesized in an ultrahigh-vacuum MBE chamber, which maintained a base pressure of 1 × 10−10 mbar. This chamber was integrated with a commercial Omicron LT-STM/nc-AFM system.

Substrates used in this study included cleaved single-crystalline graphite (NGS Naturgraphit GmbH) and epitaxial graphene-terminated n-type 4H-SiC(0001) (Tankeblue Semiconductor Co. Ltd.). The graphite substrate was cleaved in ambient air and immediately introduced into the MBE chamber to degas surface contaminations. The preparation of the epitaxial graphene followed the protocol outlined in ref. (45). The thickness of epitaxial graphene is determined using a combination of STM and STS performed on the substrates adjacent to the Cr2Se3 thin films, with the details discussed in Supplementary Text S2 and figs. S13 and S14.

For the evaporation process, high-purity selenium (Se, 99.999%) and chromium (Cr, 99.95%) were used. Selenium was evaporated from a standard Knudson-style thermal effusion cell, and chromium was evaporated using an electron-beam evaporator. The Se/Cr flux ratio was maintained at greater than 10/1, and the substrate temperature was ~450°C.

STM/STS and nc-AFM measurements

For scanning probe microscopy characterizations, the as-grown Cr2Se3 samples were transferred in situ into the Omicron LT-STM/nc-AFM system without breaking vacuum. Experiments were conducted at a temperature of 4.3 K using a qPlus AFM sensor (spring constant k0 ≈ 1800 N m−1, resonance frequency f0 ≈ 27.4 kHz, and quality factor Q ≈ 30,000) equipped with a tungsten tip.

A clean, atomically sharp tip apex was prepared through repeated voltage pulses and controllable crashes on an Au(111) substrate. The tip state was calibrated on the Au(111) surface before STS characterization. STS spectra and dI/dV maps were acquired using a standard lock-in technique with a bias modulation of 1 mV at 963 Hz. All dI/dV maps were collected in constant height mode. An oscillation amplitude of 40 pm was used for all nc-AFM imaging. The STM and nc-AFM images were processed using the WSxM software (46).

First-principles calculations

Our first-principles calculations were conducted using DFT as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) (47) and Quantum Espresso (QE) (48). The QE package was used to calculate the phonon dispersions of the systems on the basis of the density functional perturbation theory, whereas the remaining calculations were performed using the VASP. The calculations use the projector augmented-wave pseudopotential method. The generalized gradient approximation with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof realization was used for the exchange-correlation functional (49). The DFT + U method was taken into account to deal with the Coulomb interaction between 3d orbitals of Cr atoms (50). The Kohn-Sham valence states were expanded using plane waves, with energy cutoffs set at 50 and 500 rydberg (Ry) for wave functions and charge densities, respectively. For Brillouin zone sampling, an 18 × 18 × 1 k-grid was used to calculate the electronic properties, whereas a 6 × 6 × 1 q-point mesh was used for phonon calculations. The crystal structure was optimized with fixed experimental lattice constants until the forces on the atoms were less than 0.001 eV/Å. The Fermi surfaces were calculated using a 120 × 120 × 1 k-grid, based on tight-binding Hamiltonians. These Hamiltonians, expressed in the Wannier basis, were derived using the Wannier90 package (51), based on the DFT-calculated ground-state wave functions.
