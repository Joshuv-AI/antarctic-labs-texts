Effectiveness of pharmacogenomics-guided dosing in improving treatment outcomes
Authors: L. V. Karthikeyan, A. Subamathi, P. Pragathi, S. Vaishnavi, V. Sanjana, R. Sangamithra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The pharmacogenomics (PGx) discipline has played the pivotal role in the precision medicine by enabling the tailoring drug therapies to the genomic characteristics on an individual patient. This study reviewed PGx-guided dosing in the treatment of major diseases, such as cardiovascular, psychiatric, transplant, and oncologic. Data from randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, and system reviews provided important insights. Several case reports indicated that genotype-guided drug prescription, indeed, helped to better select therapies for patients. For cardiovascular care specifically, warfarin and clopidogrel dosing, which were guided by PGx, was shown to work. Psychiatric cases supported better rates of remission and a lower number of side effects. The field of oncology demonstrates a marked reduction in the incidence of toxic reactions in chemotherapy regimens, whereas the field of transplant medicine had improved the management of immunosuppressants and the survival of grafts. Economic analyses further supported PGx testing, showing reduced hospitalizations and fewer treatment failures, thereby lowering healthcare costs. Although the clinical benefits of PGX-guided therapy were evident, several challenges limited its widespread adoption. These include inadequate clinical clinician training in consistent testing standards and gaps in infrastructure. Overcoming these barriers depended on advancements in decision support systems, machine learning applications, and the integration of multiomic data. Growing evidence from recent research has highlighted this shift, demonstrating how the PGx approach continues to evolve. Ultimately, PGF-guided dosing emerged not simply as a new technique but a promising pathway to improving patient outcomes, reducing healthcare costs, and shaping the future direction of personalized medicine.

Introduction

Over the last years, medicine as a branch of science has been on exponential growth, moving into what is known as personalized health care. Advancement of pharmacogenomics (PGx) is more noticeable now. It uses genetic data to tailor drug treatment and predict therapeutic effects. In the prescription of medicines, the traditional “one-size-fits-all” model has always been too limited due to the large deviation in how the patients metabolize and respond to medicines. PGx-informed dosing takes this diversity into consideration. In a wide range of conditions,[12] genetic polymorphisms lie at the core of PGx. Naturally defective or impaired allelic variants, especially those encoding enzymes such as CYP2D6, are central to this variability. CYP2C19 and VKORC1 are critical determinants of drug metabolism and therapy and may affect medication efficacy and toxicity.[345] The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) has published clinical practice guidelines for over 100 gene-drug pairs to guide therapy.[6]

In cardiovascular medicine, genotype-directed warfarin dosing enhances time in the therapeutic range (TTR) and optimises anticoagulation. Zhu et al. confirmed that PGx-guided therapy for cardiovascular diseases is cost-effective among high-risk patients.[1] In psychiatry, a large randomized controlled study found that patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) who received PGx-guided antidepressant treatment had higher rates of remission and fewer side effects than those receiving standard treatment.[7] Wang et al. corroborated these findings using a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), whereas Peterson et al. demonstrated the cost-effectiveness of PGx by reducing trial-and-error prescribing and health care use.[28] In oncology, PGx allows clinicians to weigh efficacy versus toxicity. Aminkeng et al. recommended genetic screening for anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity in paediatric cancer patients to reduce cardiac complications.[9]

PGx also enables individualized chemotherapy dosing, improving tolerance and mitigating resistance, enhancing cancer care.[10] In transplantation, polymorphisms in CYP3A5 affect the pharmacokinetics of immunosuppressants such as cyclosporine and tacrolimus. Girardin et al. found that PGx-guided dosing may improve graft outcomes in renal transplantation populations; however, these findings are context-specific and require research.[11]

Advances in sequencing technology and bioinformatic have enabled multigene panel testing. AI and machine learning support the interpretation of complex PGx data. Equitable access to PGx testing across populations remains essential, and disparities in genomic representation and research diversity cannot be overlooked.[12] An overview of the key concepts discussed in pharmacogenomics is presented in Table 1.

Materials and Methods

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library was conducted to identify studies published between January 2000 and June 2024. These databases were used to refine and filter the literature relevant to various pharmacological interventions. The PubMed search strategy combined previously established keywords with controlled vocabulary (MeSH).

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they were peer-reviewed publications, English-language publications, PGx-related RCTs, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, economic assessments, and practice guidelines or consensus recommendations. English-language restriction was applied to ensure accurate interpretation of pharmacogenomic terminology and clinical recommendation.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they were non-English-language publications, conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, or case reports. English abstracts of non-English studies were reviewed for relevance when available, but full-text inclusion was limited to English language publication.

The screening and selection process included:

Two reviewers screened records independently in two stages: (a) title/abstract review and (b) full-text review. Disagreements during the review process were resolved by consensus.

Reference management was performed using Zotero, and thematic synthesis was applied to organise the findings across therapy areas. This approach also guaranteed an in-depth, focused analysis of the existing evidence for PGx-guided dosing and its effect on the endpoints of treatment.

Quality assessment

While this review was narrative in nature, study quality was assessed qualitatively. RCTs were appraised based on study design, sample size, outcome reporting, and consistency of findings. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses were evaluated for methodological transparency and risk of publication bias. Observational and cohort studies were interpreted with caution, particularly where confounding factors or selection bias were likely. This approach was adopted to contextualize findings and avoid overinterpretation of clinical outcomes.

Results

During the period of January 2000 to June 2024, we found 1,236 articles through database search (PubMed, Embase, Scopus and Cochrane Library). After removing duplicates (n = 312), 924 records were screened by title and abstract. Out of these, 168 publications were reviewed in full text. The selection process was further guided by the review, which identified 36 studies for this study. A further search identified seven guidelines/consensus statements for inclusion. Although 36 studies and seven guidelines informed the narrative synthesis, only the most relevant and representative sources were cited explicitly in the reference list.

The included studies embraced a wide range of PGx applications, and among them were the pharmacogenomic applications of antidepressant prescribing, cardiovascular therapy, oncology drug toxicity prediction, and the usage of immunosuppressants in transplantation.

Cardiovascular section

In cardiovascular cohorts, genotype-guided warfarin doses led to faster stabilization of anticoagulation compared to standard clinical protocols. Patients with CYP2C9 and VKORC1 genotypes achieved therapeutic international normalized ratio values in 4.8 days, compared to 6.7 days in the standard treatment arm, resulting in a significant reduction in time to stable anticoagulation (P < 0.001).[13] Accelerating treatment control led to a significant decrease in haemorrhagic risk. In the first 3 months, severe bleeding events occurred in 5.5% of genotype-guided patients compared to 8.1% of controls (P = 0.02).[13] Importantly, thromboembolic event rates remained low and comparable between groups (1.2% in the guided arm compared to 1.4% in the control arm; P = 0.68), indicating that safety was improved without losing efficacy.[13]

Clopidogrel metabolism, which is regulated by the activity of CYP2C19, benefited considerably from the development of pharmacogenomic stratification. Switching to prasugrel or ticagrelor from clopidogrel reduced high on-treatment platelet reactivity by one-third compared to unscreened controls on clopidogrel (P < 0.001).[3] At 1-year follow-up, the composite endpoint of nonfatal myocardial infarction or no hemorrhagic stroke occurred in 6.8% and 8.9% of patients assigned to genotype-guided therapy and standard care controls, respectively, with a hazard ratio of 0.75 (95% confidence interval 0.61–0.92; P = 0.005).[3] More recent analyses (Mega et al., 2019; Pereira et al., 2020) reinforced these findings in broader cardiovascular cohorts, supporting routine CYP2C19-guided therapy for PCI patients.

Emerging pharmacogenomic markers in cholesterol management and heart failure support these benefits. SLCO1B1 polymorphisms, which decrease hepatic statin absorption, were linked to a twofold increased risk of statin-induced myopathy when normal doses were used. In a population stratified by SLCO1B1 genotype, personalized statin selection and dose lowered myopathy incidence from 1.8% to 0.7% while maintaining lipid-lowering efficacy.[14] Similarly, ADRB1 and ADRB2 receptor variants have had moderate but consistent effects on beta-blocker responsiveness in heart failure, implying that multigene panels may help to improve therapeutic options beyond monogenic techniques.[15]

Psychiatric section

The GUIDED study and subsequent meta-analyses of genotype-informed prescribing in patients with MDD have shed light on the significance of CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 genotyping. The PRIME care trial of 1,944 veterans demonstrated that PGx-guided therapy resulted in higher remission rates at 24 weeks (28% vs. 22%, P = 0.01) and fewer medication changes compared with usual care. Adverse medication reactions, primarily gastrointestinal and neurologic, occurred in only 9% of the guided cohort versus 14% of controls (P = 0.04).

Economic analysis in depressive disorders demonstrated that the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) for pharmacogenomic-guided therapy varied between €20,000 and €45,000 in a quality-adjusted life year (QALY), driven primarily by decreased outpatient visits, shorter time to remission, and lower hospitalization rates.[2] Over 6 months, the guided group experienced a €480 reduction in healthcare expenses due to fewer drug switches and adverse event-related visits.[16]

In addition to depression, preliminary antipsychotic management research suggests that the CYP2D6 and HTR2A genotypes have the potential to identify sensitivities to extrapyramidal symptoms and metabolic side effects; nevertheless, more extensive RCTs are necessary. Preliminary investigations suggest that genotype-directed dosing for risperidone will reduce extrapyramidal side effects by 20% or less while maintaining antipsychotic efficacy, with potential applications in psychiatry.[4]

Oncology section

Pre-emptive DPYD genotyping before fluoropyrimidine treatment has shown paradigm-shifting safety results in cancer. In a study of 1,234 patients, DPYD variant carriers who got dose-reduced fluoropyrimidines had an 8% grade 3–4 toxicity rate, but untested people on full-dose therapy had 23% (P < 0.001).[5]

Screening for HLA-B 57:01 before starting abacavir medication is the most effective level of pharmacogenomic impact for preventing idiosyncratic responses. In a multicenter investigation among 2 736 screened in patients, no hypersensitivity syndrome occurred, unlike the previous occurrence among 5–8% unscreened controls (P < 0.0001).[17] The removal of this deadly adverse event gives the best evidence yet for the importance of routine HLA screening as part of HIV therapy.

Research on childhood anthracycline cardiotoxicity has also identified RARG and UGT1A6 polymorphisms as risk factors for late-onset cardiac insufficiency. After 10 years of longitudinal follow-up with 462 long-term survivors of pediatric cancer, genotype-adapted dosage reduced the incidence of cardiomyopathy from 14% to 7%, with no negative influence on recurrence rates or event-free survival.[9]

Transplant section

Transplantation end-points tacrolimus dose based on CYP3A5 genotype achieved target trough levels much faster than empiric techniques. In a prospective, randomized series of 218 renal allograft recipients, CYP3A5 expressers achieved goal values (5–12 ng/mL) in a mean of 7.1 days, compared to 10.3 days for nonexpressers using normal weight-based regimens (P < 0.001).[18]

Discussion

It is important to emphasize that the clinical significance of PGx varies across therapeutic areas and must be examined in terms of both the magnitude of effect and level of treatment necessity. In high-risk therapeutic domains such as anticoagulation, cancer, and transplantation, PGx-guided therapy has demonstrated a significant and clinically immediate benefit by reducing potentially fatal side events. In contrast, the effect in psychiatric practice is relatively modest and context-specific, with an emphasis on increasing tolerability, therapy selection, and response time rather than life-threatening occurrences. Thus, evidence from diverse therapy domains must not be assessed in the same manner, but rather in the context of clinical urgency, effect size, and the availability of alternative therapies.

Integration across therapeutic domains

PGx is a key element of precision medicine, enabling drug therapy to be tailored to a patient’s genetic profile. A large body of evidence in many areas of therapeutic activity supports the phenomenon that PGx-guided dosing improves outcomes, including reducing the incidence of adverse events. Through the systematic adoption of genotype data in prescribing decisions, clinicians can more quickly achieve optimal exposure to and efficacy of medications when compared to empiric trial and error. Clopidogrel also has polymorphisms in CYP2C19 that correlate with the degree of platelet inhibition, and individuals with impaired function alleles have a greater risk of stent thrombosis and cardiovascular complications. PGx-directed approaches that take CYP2C19 genotypes into consideration enable early identification of patients who require alternative P2Y12 inhibitors, thus reducing cardiovascular morbidity.[7] Pharmacodynamic testing improves cardiovascular results and establishes a precision prescription paradigm that may be used in other treatment areas. The incorporation of genetic data into therapeutic decision-making promotes a proactive strategy in which risk prediction, therapy optimization, and adverse event prevention are accomplished before harm occurring. This shift from “reactive” to “predictive” care does not only help to avoid uncertain patient outcomes and lost charges but also assists the doctors to gain trust in personalized prescribing approaches at higher levels of collaboration, as well as with the help of digital health solutions. These findings indicate that cardiovascular PGx represents a high-impact application where clinical benefit is immediate and effect sizes are consistently strong.

Collectively, these findings emphasize the evolving importance of PGx in achieving safer, more predictable, and individualized cardiovascular outcomes [Table 1].[11516] In terms of public health, the increased safety and efficacy could also lead to cost savings in higher-income settings that already have established PGx infrastructure, given the potential for fewer hospitalizations and secondary events [Table 2]. The cost-effectiveness in resource-limited settings, however, is still uncertain. Progress in PGx of psychiatric disorders is also slow and gradual. Psychiatric conditions, including major depression (MDD) and bipolar disorder, may require protracted pharmaceutical therapy. However, recalcitrance to treatment and side effects are widespread. Studies on CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 polymorphisms suggest that they influence how tricyclic antidepressants and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors are metabolized. Genotype-guided therapy appears to improve tolerability and reduce the number of medication changes, which could enhance long-term outcomes. In contrast, psychiatric pharmacogenomic applications offer incremental but clinically meaningful benefits, particularly in reducing trial-and-error prescribing and improving tolerability rather than producing large effect sizes.

Not all pharmacogenomic trials have demonstrated clear superiority over standard care. A landmark randomized trial published in 2013 reported that genotype-guided warfarin dosing did not significantly outperform a well-validated clinical dosing algorithm in achieving TTR. These findings suggest that the incremental benefit of PGx may depend on comparator quality, population characteristics, and healthcare system context.

The GUIDED trial, one of the largest PGx trials in psychiatry, found that genotype-guided medication resulted in fewer transitions to alternative medicines and improved results for both physicians and patients.[8] These findings suggest that PGx can shorten the long process of adjusting medications in psychiatric care. This results in enhanced patient compliance and superior long-term outcomes. Immunosuppressive regimens in transplant medicine need to be optimally balanced to avoid graft rejection and minimize drug toxicity. Tacrolimus is a calcineurin inhibitor of a narrow therapeutic index. It is primarily metabolized by CYP3A5. Genotype-guided dosing strategies considering CYP3A5 polymorphisms have been linked to earlier attainment of therapeutic levels.[9] This initial establishment of therapeutic control was associated with a definite 3.1% reduction in biopsy-confirmed acute rejection at 3 months (12.5% in guided vs. 15.6% in controls; P = 0.045).[18] During the 1st year after transplant, genotype-directed therapy resulted in an overall savings of €1,150 per recipient due to fewer rejection therapies and rehospitalizations. In the same manner, thiopurines such as azathioprine and mercaptopurine are metabolized by TPMT and NUDT15. Individuals with low-activity alleles for these genes are at increased risk of myelosuppression, a severe toxicity. Preemptive screening prior to the initiation of thiopurines is now the standard of care in many nations. Preemptive TPMT and NUDT15 genotyping reduced severe myelosuppression from 18% to 5% in haematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, allowing for better immunosuppressive management while reducing graft-versus-host disease.[6] This approach not only avoids unnecessary adverse effects but also facilitates long-term transplant success and optimal patient outcomes [Table 3].[10]

Oncology is among the most active therapeutic fields in which PGx has obvious and direct implications. Fluoropyrimidines, commonly employed in gastrointestinal cancer, can induce severe and occasionally lethal toxicities in DPYD gene variant carriers. Pharmacodynamics has had a profound impact on oncology, particularly tailoring chemotherapy to minimise toxicity and optimise outcomes. Preemptive DPYD genotyping allows clinicians to identify patients at high risk for severe fluoropyrimidine toxicity and adjust doses. Accordingly, studies PGx has relevance for balancing efficacy and toxicity, with evidence that genotype-directed dosing lowers treatment related toxicity, hospitalization, and treatment interruption Table 3.[511] The change not only prevented deadly mucositis, neutropenia, and cardiotoxicity, but it also maintained antitumor activity, with overall response rates and progression-free survival statistically equal between the genotype-directed and full-dose arms. Subsequent large-scale implementation trials in Europe (Henricks et al., 2018; Meulendijks et al., 2019) confirmed that preemptive DPYD screening significantly reduced hospitalization costs and chemotherapy delays, with no loss of oncologic efficacy. For anthracyclines, PGx research has identified associations with RARG and UGT1A6 polymorphisms that increase the risk of cardiotoxicity, guiding dose modifications or alternative regimens.[16] They also underscore the fact that germline screening is a must when handling genetic drugs to make sure of their efficacy in the long run. The embrace of such personalized therapies on a genomics basis in day-to-day medical practice can significantly increase a patient’s life expectancy and quality of life while decreasing treatment issues and complications, as well as improving therapeutic outcomes. In addition to the application of PGx for toxicity prevention measures, oncology has also advanced into the field of targeted therapy using companion diagnostics, which are currently playing a very significant role in identifying patients who are more likely to benefit from molecularly tailored treatments. In general, therefore, the results show that PGx-guided dosing has the potential for effectiveness and safety enhancement in a wide range of diseases and may be employed in different therapeutic areas and health care environments. Nevertheless, the value proposition remains somewhat variable across therapeutic areas and healthcare systems, affected by allele frequencies as well as health system capacity. There is no doubt that with more evidence being generated on PGx, it is increasingly becoming clear that this will be the mainstay for individualized therapeutic strategies in routine clinical practice. In oncology and transplantation, pharmacogenomic testing addresses high therapeutic risk, where even moderate effect sizes translate into substantial clinical benefit by preventing severe toxicity or graft failure.

Clinical and economic implications

The economic aspect of PGx therapy is promising. Van der Wouden et al. developed a decision analytic model that cost effectiveness in avoiding gene-drug-related mortality and adverse drug reactions.[16] Plumpton et al. evaluation of economic studies PGx-guided prescribing, economically favourable.[19] Samwald et al. noted that repurposing prescriptions for PGx-adherent patients could reduce cost.[20]

At its core, the clinical benefits of PGx should not be separated from its economic implications. Health systems all around the world are under growing pressure to deliver cost-effective care and improve patient outcomes. PGx has been demonstrated to have an impressive economic value by preventing hospitalizations and treatment failures, as well as adverse events.

An example comes from pharmacoeconomic studies of warfarin PGx in Europe and North America, suggest that genotype-guided anticoagulation may represent a cost-effective strategy in high income healthcare settings.[1] In psychiatric care, pharmacogenomic testing for antidepressant therapy has been associated with low treatment failures and psychiatric hospitalizations, with overall cost offsets surpassing the price of genetic testing.[2] Additionally, the savings realized are not limited to the health systems only since there is also a reduction in the indirect cost lost productivity and caregiver burden.

At the population level, the preemptive multi-gene panel testing has the potential to generate long term economic benefits when implemented at scale.[19] Similar trends have been reported in oncology, where genotype-guided dosing strategies may reduce toxicity-related healthcare utilization and treatment disruptions, supporting more efficient use of limited oncology resources.[16]

Overall, these findings seem to indicate that PGx does not only raise outcomes at an individual level but also accelerates efficiency within entire health care systems. With the price of genetic testing continuing its downward spiral, the economic value proposition of it will likely further develop, positioning PGx as an essential investment for health systems worldwide. While most data supporting the use of PGx originates from industrialized countries, its relevance in the Indian setting must be viewed with caution. The absence of genetic infrastructure, unequal access to testing, and cost concerns all impede PGx application in India. However, the present burden of adverse medication reactions and out-of-pocket healthcare costs suggests that focused pharmacogenomic testing for high-risk drug-gene combinations may be useful.[121619]

Although economic studies on PGx are typically positive, they should be interpreted with caution in the context of low-and middle-income nations. Most cost-effectiveness models rely on high-income healthcare systems that have already established genomic infrastructure and electronic health records. In low-and middle-income nations, the cost of initial testing, combined with competing public health objectives, may reduce the immediate economic viability of widespread PGx deployment. As a result, the optimistic economic estimates provided in the literature may not be easily generalized. The clinical applications and related findings are summarized in Table 2.

Challenges to clinical adoption

Despite these advantages, integration of PGx into clinical practice remains challenging. Robust electronic health record (EHR) systems, clinical decision support (CDS) systems, and standardized reporting formats are needed to support clinicians. Hall et al. and Devine et al. showed that PGx can help reduce prescribing errors, while Lemke et al.[212223] emphasized the role of computer-based alerts in enhancing prescriptions and decreasing ADRs. However, many clinicians still require ongoing education to improve knowledge and confidence, as emphasized by Giri et al.[24]

There is a significant amount of evidence that even so, PGx is not used in clinical practice extensively. There are various hindrances, such as knowledge gaps, technical variations, and infrastructure issues, that prevent the technology from being applied universally. One big hindrance is clinician education; this under preparedness results in indecisiveness and undertreatment, especially in critical decisions like chemotherapy dosing or transplant immunosuppression.[2021] Technical disparities, absence of standardization, and infrastructural constraints complicate the integration even more due to such a specific use within a clinical workflow. Improvements in decision support tools, CPIC guidelines, and education programs help to fill up the knowledge gaps and to enhance adoption of innovations, yet there are variances in trial outcomes, which highlight the necessity for further evidence generation and interpretative caution.[23] While barriers to implementation include regulatory challenges, data integration issues. Furthermore, variable naming conventions for alleles and metabolic traits lead to confusion and errors in clinical judgement.

Infrastructural constraints are yet another factor. Real-time decision-support mechanisms or prescribing alerts based on genotype are absent in most EHR systems. Even in cases where they are available, inadequate interface design might cause “alert fatigue.” As a result, providers are often more likely to disregard the warnings than abide by them.[2526] Unintegrated systems dilute the potential impact of PGx otherwise.

Economic analyses across all treatment categories consistently support the value proposition of pharmacogenomic advice. Pharmacogenomic dosage for warfarin indicated an ICER of €18 400/QALY due to fewer bleeding hospitalizations and emergency department visits.[13] Genotype-guided clopidogrel medication had an ICER of €24 200/QALY due to fewer ischemic events and lower long-term cardiovascular therapy expenditures.[3] In oncology, DPYD genotyping had an ICER of €26 500 per severe toxicity prevented, while HLA-B*57:01 screening for abacavir had an ICER of €14 800, both comfortably below the limits for high-impact safety interventions.[517]

According to a budget impact estimate, investing $3–5 million for every 100,000 patients in multi-gene panel deployment will result in net savings of $2–4 million over the next 5 years. Savings are generated by lower expenses for controlling adverse events, shorter hospital stays, and simpler drug regimens.[19] Health system simulation further supports pharmacogenomic testing’s integration with value-based models of care by providing evidence for both clinical and economic benefits.

Real-world usage patterns despite good randomized data and a positive economic prognosis, utilization in daily practice is unsatisfactory. Retrospective analysis of on-treatment EMR/molecular data in a tertiary care tertiary-care institution patient populations indicate that 54% of patients are prescribed at least one drug with level I practice influencing PGs, while just 12% are genotyped for the relevant PGs.[20] Physician surveys reveal several significant barriers to adoption. 67% admit to a lack of training for interpreting results, and 74% are unsure of how to include genotypic information in the art of prescribing.[24] Lack of uniform laboratory reporting templates, as well as the absence of nomenclatural standardization to identify specific pharmacogenomic tests, are fundamental challenges that contribute to complicating the smooth integration of pharmacogenomic information into the EHRs and clinical workflows [Table 2].

Advances in decision support and infrastructure

The past few years have seen significant progress in overcoming these challenges through improved decision support tools, shared networks, and specific education efforts. The development of specific CDS modules is a particular improvement. One case in point is the Genomics Module by Epic that permits integration of results from multi-gene panels into the prescribing workflow. The system gives alerts with priority in connection with high-risk gene-drug interactions. The studies show that those systems are observed to bring about clinician adherence levels above 70% and lower rates of alert overriding.[25] The providers show how the conscientious design and inclusion into workflows can transform genomics data into helpful clinical knowledge.

It is becoming more obvious that these groups of people are significant. Ten years ago, CPIC was established and indicated the way in which standard nomenclature for alleles and evidence-based dosing guidelines should be developed.[27] These instruments resulted in unifying different institutions on a single prescribing pathway and translating complicated genetic data into clear guidance directives. Nowadays, CPIC guidelines include many crucial drug-gene interactions and are therefore indispensable references not only for clinicians but also for labs.

The integration of training courses in all levels of education, from postgraduate to undergraduate, is a must to guarantee the continuity of this science. A well-structured medical curriculum has made significant progress in the understanding of PGx.[20] Moreover, reintroduction of a continuing education program among clinical practitioners has been found to be associated with an increased test ordering frequency and compliance with current guidelines. The program enhances knowledge of today’s gaps as well as creates a culture towards precision medicine in clinical practice. Recent CPIC guideline updates[34] and DPWG harmonization efforts (2021–2024) have strengthened the translational impact of PGx by providing consistent, actionable recommendations across diverse clinical domains. However, variability in outcomes across different trials (e.g., modest improvements in some depression trials) underscores the need for continued evidence generation and cautious interpretation. Ethical and equity considerations

PGx, as well as any other new medical development, brings forth ethical issues and equity-related challenges. Genetic variations are highly heterogeneous across global populations. Unless there is fair implementation, PGx may widen existing disparities in health. The examples are variants determining warfarin doses, VKORC1 and CYP2C9, which are more common among Asian and European populations than among African populations.[11] The population in urban areas has been the focus and the beneficiary of the PM. Through programs like the NIH’s dbGaP and major biobanking initiatives, there have been some attempts to increase the representation of minority groups in genomic research. Nevertheless, underrepresentation remains a challenge. To address this, it is necessary that we actively involve diverse populations in genomic studies, reduce test costs, and provide culturally specific educational outreach.[24] If these are not considered, then PGx can unintentionally perpetuate inequalities in healthcare access and outcomes systemwide.

Limitations and future directions

It is crucial to remember that some pharmacogenomic examples may lose their clinical usefulness over time. For example, direct oral anticoagulants are gradually replacing warfarin and clopidogrel in many healthcare settings, and certain pharmacogenomically challenging antidepressants or chemotherapeutic drugs may be avoided if safer alternatives are available. Pharmacogenomic testing is expected to be less necessary in healthcare settings with fewer resources because healthcare professionals are more likely to utilize medicines with larger therapeutic margins. These instances are still helpful as proof-of-concept studies, though, and PGx is still effective in high-risk groups, pharmacogenomic pairings, and medical settings without access to alternative medicines.

The evidence for PGx is strong, although there are still limitations that need to be researched. Economic models often make assumptions on testing cost and the health care pathways, which in turn may not apply to different health systems.[1019] Heterogeneity in study design, outcome measures, and patient Evolving cultural frameworks amongst diverse populations make it harder to conduct meta-analytic synthesis. Thus, making it hard to apply the findings universally.[21]

The next step in research should not simply be further single-gene associations. It should go deeper, into polygenic and multiomic frameworks. For example, drug responses do not only depend on genetic polymorphism systems. However, it is possible for the predictive accuracy to be undermined not only by drug-drug interactions and phenoconversion but also by these influencing factors that can change metabolic pathways regardless of genotype. Incorporating these factors into predictive models will increase clinical effectiveness. Machine learning methodologies that use big data over time, often biobank data, are the foundation for building dynamic predictive models. These models will be based on genetic, metabolic, and clinical information.[1426]

It is possible to consider the combination of PGx and metabolomic and proteomic data as one of the most prospective approaches in this context. This can be helpful for the development of a multi-omic precision medicine framework. From this perspective, these approaches may, at least partially, provide support for truly individualized therapy selection and dosing based on genomics only.

In the current global healthcare setup veering towards value-based care, pharmacodynamics has demonstrated clinical utility for selected drug-gene pairs. The wider application will depend not only on the readiness of technology but also on the growing cases of patients who do not fill or refill their prescriptions, referred to as “medication non-adherence,” which impact the efficacy of medical interventions among the patients. What might be called the soft-power side of personalized medicine may, however, prevent this from being a luxury restricted to an elite few regardless of how it is perceived by referring providers and insurers. This narrative review was not intended to be an exhaustive mapping of all gene–drug combinations but rather a focused synthesis of clinically impactful and well-studied pharmacogenomic applications. Conclusion

While this review highlights established and emerging benefits of PGx -guided dosing, the evidence base remains heterogeneous, with variable effect sizes across therapeutic areas and several trials demonstrating modest or context-dependent benefits. Conflicting findings, particularly in cardiovascular and psychiatric settings, underscore the need for cautious interpretation and avoid overgeneralization of clinical and economic outcomes. The information from cardiovascular, psychiatric, transplant, and oncology medicine makes it obvious. PGx-guided prescribing is a way to improve clinical outcomes and to decrease serious side effects. It also is in line with widely accepted standards on cost-effectiveness. Yet further steps will have to be taken for the wider use of PGx in practice. These measures are educating clinicians, standardizing tests, and shaping health system infrastructure for better application of personalized medicine into practice. It is also important that equity issues are addressed so that everyone could benefit from personalized medicine.

In the future, by adding genomics to machine learning and multi-omic approaches, as well as with strong decision support systems where one can get information from different variables, precision prescribing will be improved. PGx has moved from its initial stages of research to a clinically validated practice. This is the current situation. This issue poses a problem: How can we ensure that these developments will be applied in an equitable and sustainable way in various health systems.
