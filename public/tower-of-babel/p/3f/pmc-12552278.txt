PNH clones prevalence study in ph-negative myeloproliferative neoplasms: a multicenter Italian study
Authors: Alessandra D’Addio, Michela Rondoni, Marzia Salvucci, Giovanni Marconi, Massimiliano Bonifacio, Ilaria Tanasi, Omar Perbellini, Giuseppe Carli, Patrizia Tosi, Simona Tomassetti, Giovanni Poletti, Evita Massari, Marco Rosetti, Elisabetta Fabbri, Chiara Zingaretti, Alessandro Lucchesi, Maria Teresa Bochicchio, Giorgia Simonetti, Mauro Krampera, Francesco Lanza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The prevalence of paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) clones is little investigated in myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) patients. The aim of this multicenter study was to evaluate the prevalence of PNH clones (glycosyl-phosphatidyl-inositol lacking) in 119 Ph- negative MPN patients having anemia, LDH elevation, asthenia and history of thrombosis. All the participating centers performed the standardized diagnostic test by using a single lyophilized template for granulocytes, monocytes, and erythrocytes. Next generation sequencing (NGS) was performed in 2 PNH-positive MPN cases and 13 PNH-negative MPN. The prevalence of PNH positive clones was 3.23% (n. 3 patients). All three patients had splenomegaly; none of them had thrombosis. One patient affected by CALR mutated essential thrombocytopenia, had a small clone (0.52%), clinically irrelevant; one patient affected by JAK2V617F primary myelofibrosis (PMF) showed a PNH clone of 89.8%, severe anemia and hemoglobinuria and started eculizumab therapy; the third patient affected by CALR mutated PMF showed a PNH clone of 92.6% but without severe anemia and breakthrough hemolysis and eculizumab therapy was not undertaken. PIGA deletion was detected in PNH-positive cases along with mutations of myeloid-related genes. These data seem to suggest an association of CALR mutation and JAK2V617F mutation with PNH positive clones suggesting that the worsening of malignant process may be associated with the acquisition of multiple genetic mutations.Clinical Trial Registration: NCT06159816.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06556-y.

Introduction

Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria (PNH) is a rare disease characterized by intravascular hemolysis, predominantly venous thrombosis and in some cases bone marrow failure [1–5].

In 1993, the synthesis defect of the glycosyl-phosphatidyl-inositol (GPI) anchoring protein was documented in patients suffering from PNH, through which numerous proteins bind to the cell membrane of blood cells [6]. This defect was then correlated to the presence of somatic mutations in the PIGA gene, which normally intervenes in the biosynthesis phase of GPI [7]. From an immunophenotypical point of view, PNH is characterized by the development of clones characterized by defects in GPI proteins and consequently by the lack of a series of proteins bound to the membrane via these in leukocytes, red blood cells and platelets, including CD59 and CD55. The affected cells are therefore much more sensitive to complement-mediated lysis and the clinical consequence is a condition of persistent chronic intravascular hemolysis, with acute episodes (hemolytic crises). The search for GPI-anchored membrane antigens using monoclonal antibodies (moAbs) and flow cytometry allows the best diagnosis for PNH and allows quantification of the extension of the clone involved [8]. In the erythrocyte population, the flow cytometric assessment of PNH patients have shown that the proportion of cells with the PNH phenotype is significantly lower than that in granulocytes and monocytes [8–10]. A PNH clone can be found in patients diagnosed with aplastic anemia in percentages up to 26%, and up to 5–8% in patients diagnosed with myelodysplasia [10–12].

Complement-induced intravascular hemolysis contributes to the greater propensity of patients with PNH to develop thrombotic events [13–15]. Thrombotic events have also been highlighted in patients with only a small PNH clone and in the absence of hemolysis [13, 16, 17]. Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) are diseases that originate from the neoplastic transformation of the pluripotent stem cell, characterized by the clonal proliferation of one or more hematopoietic progenitors in the bone marrow and in extra-medullary sites. There are three main forms of Philadelphia-negative (Ph-) MPN, represented by polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET) and primary myelofibrosis (PMF), which were the subject of this study.

All these neoplasms can present an increased thrombotic risk: approximately one third of patients with PV and ET present a major thrombotic event at diagnosis or during follow up [18–21]. A greater tendency to develop thrombotic events has been demonstrated in patients with MPN and JAK2 mutation.

Few data are available in the literature on the presence of PNH clones in the context of MPN [22–25]. Highly sensitive molecular biology methods (Next Generation Sequencing, NGS), hypothesizes that the general principles of clonal dynamics derived from the study of leukemia can also be explored in non-neoplastic pathologies such as PNH [26].

The results of this author’s study led to the concept that PNH has clonal dynamism and is subjected to selection forces similar to those observed in hematopoietic neoplasms. As a consequence, clonal and subclonal mutations are associated with PIGA genetic alterations, responsible for the prevalent phenotype of the cells [3, 4, 27, 28].

Methods

Patients and study design

This is a prospective multicenter study of the prevalence of the PNH clone in Ph- MPN showing signs of ongoing hemolysis or particular clinical conditions.

The study was carried out in the 3 hematology centers of the ​​Romagna network (Ravenna and Rimini – AUSL Romagna, and IRST IRCCS of Meldola), and in the clinical hematology departments of 2 other Italian centers (Verona and Vicenza). The coordinating center of the study is the Hematology Unit of Ravenna. The presence of the PNH clone was assessed. Samples were obtained from patients with a confirmed diagnosis of Ph-MPN afferent at the centers participating in the study who fall within the inclusion criteria and who have given written informed consent to the study (NCT06159816), according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

The clones were identified on neutrophil granulocytes and monocytes, applying a screening test at the reference laboratories of the individual centers. On positive cases, a second confirmatory test was carried out at a local level, also including the study of red blood cells.

The clinical and laboratory characteristics of Ph- MPN (the presence of thrombotic phenomena, the disease state, the Dynamic International Prognostic Scoring System (DIPSS) prognostic score index, and the mutational status) were correlated with the characteristics of the PNH clone.

Assessment of PNH clones by flow cytometry

In order to identify GPI-deficient microclones in granulocyte, monocyte and erythrocyte cells, the following parameters were evaluated: limit of detection (LOD) and lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of the flow cytometric method.

The aforementioned parameters are defined by the total number of events acquired in the various gates of interest and considering as positive a homogeneous cluster of at least 30 events.

The participating centers carried out the diagnostic test with a single lyophilized template for granulocytes and monocytes consisting of FLAER-FITC/CD157-PE/CD64-PC7/CD15-APC/CD45-5 fluorescence channel and a single lyophilized template for erythrocytes consisting of CD235a-FITC/CD59-PE/CD45-APC. To harmonize the method regardless of the instrumentation used (Beckman Coulter and Becton Dickinson), specific calibration beads will be provided.

The screening test was carried out only on the leukocyte population.

In cases testing positive in the screening test (PNH clone > = 0.1%) with the maximum sensitivity available, the analysis was repeated and also extended to the erythrocyte population.

Sample preparation: erythrocyte population

Dispense 100 µl of peripheral blood suitably diluted so as to have an erythrocyte concentration between 3 and 5.5 × 106/µl into a test tube and add 3 ml of physiological solution and centrifuge for 5 min at 500xg. Incubate the red blood cell pellet with the antibodies in the dark. Wash with 3 ml of physiological solution twice by centrifuging for 5 min at 500xg. Acquire on the flow cytometer (at least 500,000 events). Analyze after having positioned an appropriate series of sequential gates that best identify the erythrocyte population.

Granulocyte and monocyte population

Lyse 200 µl of peripheral blood suitably diluted to have a leukocyte concentration that allows the acquisition of an adequate number of events (in any case not exceeding 10,000/µl) using ammonium chloride. Incubate the sample obtained with the specific template for 30 min at room temperature in the dark and wash once with Phosphate-buffered saline. Acquire at least 300,000 events using the flow cytometer, both in the monocytic and granulocytic population (highly sensitive technique); in the case of insufficient cellularity, acquire at least 30,000 events in both the monocytic and granulocytic populations (intermediate sensitivity technique). Analyze after having positioned an appropriate series of sequential gates that best identify the monocyte and granulocyte population.

Targeted next generation sequencing (NGS)

Samples were obtained from 15 patients (2 PNH-positive and 13 PNH-negative MPN).

DNA was isolated from whole blood using the Maxwell® RSC Whole Blood DNA Kit (Promega Corporation) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mutational profile of patients was determined using the Myeloid Extended Solution panel (SOPHiA GENETICS). Libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s recommendations and quantified using the Qubit® dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay on a Qubit® Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). The mean size of the amplicons were determined by capillary electrophoresis using Agilent High Sensitivity DNA kit (Agilent Technologies, Inc.). The pooled libraries were sequenced on a NextSeq™ 550 instrument (Illumina, Inc.), as described by the manufacturer. FASTQ sequencing files were analyzed by SOPHiA DDM™ platform (Pipeline ILL1XG1S5_CNV_NextSeq_1, v5.5.80). The Human Genome Build 19 (Hg19) was used as the reference for sequence alignment. Downstream analysis was performed through an in-house algorithm developed in R (version 4.1.3). We first excluded from the variants annotated by Sophia DDM the synonymous and intronic variants and those falling within the 3’ UTR and 5’ UTR. The remaining exonic and splice site variants were further filtered by coverage (≥ 100), minor allele frequency (MAF < 1.0%) in the G1000 (v5.20130502), GnomAD (r2.1), ESP5400 (v.6500) databases, and variant allele frequency (VAF ≥ 2.5%).

The variants were annotated according to the following databases: https://www.cbioportal.org/, https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic (v.6.0.16), https://franklin.genoox.com/clinical-db/home (v.76), https://genebe.net (v0.01/d5607ff) and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/ (v.20220416).

Benign or likely benign variants were discarded.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics of all collected variables were calculated in terms of mean, standard deviation, median, range, interquartile range, absolute frequency and percentage, depending on the nature of the variable under study.

Regarding the secondary objective, the relationship between the presence of the characteristic under study and the patient’s other conditions was studied through the chi-square test and the Student’s t-test, depending on the variable considered. The significance threshold for all tests was 0.05; all analyzes were performed with STATA 17.0 software.

Prevalence of PNH clones

The prevalence of PNH positive clones was 2.5% (three patients out of 119). All three patients had splenomegaly at the time of study enrolment; none of them had thrombosis at the time of PNH suspicion. One patient (#2205) affected by CALR mutates ET and he was receiving hydroxyurea plus acetylsalicylic acid for MPN. Due to onset of anemia, he was tested for PNH and had a small clone (0.52%) was found. Hydroxyurea therapy was reduced and anemia recovered; therefore, PNH therapy was ever started yet. One patient (07–020) affected by JAK2V617F- positive PMF was tested for PNH clone due to persistent pancytopenia, consistent not only with PMF. The patient showed a PNH clone of 89.8%, severe anemia and hemoglobinuria and started eculizumab therapy; this therapy was interrupted for inefficacy; the patient refused allogeneic transplant and a few months later deceased for myocardial infarction. The third patient (#2204) affected by CALR mutated PMF was treated with hydroxyurea for MPN; he showed a PNH clone of 92.6% but without severe anemia and breakthrough hemolysis and eculizumab therapy hasn’t started yet. In later stages, it was treated with ruxolitinib and then with cytarabine. Death occurred a few months later.

Molecular profile of PNH positive patients

Primary material was available for two PNH-positive patients. We therefore analyzed their molecular profile by targeted NGS. In order to have an internal reference cohort, we also performed the analysis on a subset of PNH-negative patients affected by either ET or MPF, thus representing the PNH-positive cohort. Given the descriptive nature this analysis, due to the low number of PNH-positive cases that did not allow statistical comparison, we randomly selected a small reference cohort of PHN-negative patients carrying mutation of JAK-2, Cal-R, and MPL in two cases(n = 6 ET, and n = 7 PMF).

The patient #2204 having a high GPI-deficient clone displayed a deletion of the PIGA gene mapping on X chromosome and encompassing all exons (1–6), that was not present in PNH-negative MPN patients (Table 4). In the patient #2205, with a small PNH clone (0.52%) no PIGA alteration was detectable by NGS.

Regarding the mutational profile, NGS analysis confirmed that both PNH-positive patients were CALR-mutated (#2204 had a variant allele frequency [VAF] of 35.5% and #2205 of 28.9%) and each of them displayed some myeloid-related alterations, targeting the ATM, STAT5B, SH2B3 (#2204), and the TET2, BCOR and KDM6A genes (#2205, Fig. 1).

Conclusions

This is a multicentre Italian study evaluating the prevalence of PNH clones in Ph-negative myeloproliferative disorders. PNH has been described in the context of bone marrow failure syndromes (aplastic anemia and myelodysplastic syndrome). However, with the development of more sensitive cytofluorimetric techniques, PNH clones of various sizes are increasingly being detected in various onco-hematologic disorders [29]. The coexistence of PNH and MPNs has been reported, but its clinical/prognostic significance and therapeutic management are still poorly known. Moreover, these two conditions share an overlapping clinical and laboratory presentation, represented by thrombotic events, pancytopenia, anemia and LDH elevation.

Additionally, in the MPN setting, the differential diagnosis of haemolytic anemia may be hampered by several confounders: LDH is elevated as a consequence of disease burden [30], reticulocytosis can be observed in case of myeloid metaplasia [31] and haptoglobin can be decreased due to ineffective erythropoiesis [32]. Furthermore, the appearance of anemia, which was the most common sign for PNH suspicion in our study, should prompt the exclusion of fibrotic evolution [33]. Patients with MPN are more likely to develop venous thrombosis if they have another thrombophilic condition, such as factor Leiden or prothrombin G20210A [34–36].

Three meta-analyses have demonstrated that patients with JAK2V617F have an increased thrombotic risk [37–39]. Furthermore, patients who have JAK2 mutations and an inherited thrombophilic condition have an additional thrombotic risk [37, 40]. Based on the evidence reported above, it can be hypothesized that the PNH clone may coexist in some forms of Ph- MPN. It is likely that the early identification of a PNH clone could play a role in the etiopathogenesis of thrombotic events and could provide additional elements in the pathogenesis of thrombotic events, and in the clinical planning of the therapeutic path of these patients.

Our data show a prevalence of PNH clones in PH negative MPN patients around 3%, similar to that reported in MDS, and lower than in aplastic anemia.

Despite the small numbers, we found association between CALR mutation or JAK2V617F mutation and PNH positive clones suggesting that the worsening of malignant process may be bridged by the acquisition of multiple genetic mutations. Moreover, NGS analysis provided additional information. PIGA deletion was associated with the PNH clone in one he two tested cases. No alteration was detected in the other patient, likely due to the presence of a very small clone, that did not reach the limit of detection of the NGS analysis. Firstly, clonal and subclonal mutations are associated with PIGA deletion, responsible for the prevalent phenotype of the cells. An important consequence of this new concept is that the nature and composition of somatic clonal mutations can modify the expression of the PIGA-deleted clone and have the potential to explain various distinct clinical courses seen in individual patients affected by PNH. The mutational events that we identified in our PNH-positive patients, targeting the ATM, CALR, SH2B, STAT5B, BCOR, KDM6A, and TET2 genes are not unique to PNH, but largely overlap with the spectrum of mutations seen in MDS/MPN. Various theoretical hypotheses were reported to explain the clonal architecture of PNH: (a) PIGA gene defects are ancestral initiating events and other mutations are acquired subsequently in a similar way to myelodysplastic syndrome, (b) other somatic initiating events are followed by PIGA alterations contained within and completely overlapping the PNH clone, suggesting that the initial non-PIGA events are passing mutations, (c) secondary PIGA lesions arise in the context of other clonogenic mutations that are present in cells carrying either wild-type cells or mutated PIGA, and (d) the myelodysplastic and PNH clones are independent [26]. Fraiman described a case report relating to a patient with MPN with a positive CALR mutation and negative JAK2, in this context he hypothesizes that the CALR mutation confers a survival benefit, necessary for clonal expansion within the bone marrow stem cell compartment with PIGA alteration [24].

During PNH, the mutation of the PIGA gene may not be the only initial event of the pathology, but instead be accompanied by other somatic mutations in a large number of cases, in which subclonal events common to hematological malignancies may occur. In some patients, mutations with leukemogenic effects such as JAK2, TET2 or STAC3 may arise as a primary event, providing an initial proliferative advantage; genetic alterations of PIGA would provide a further boost to subclonal growth [26]. JAK2 (V617F) mutations are very frequent in Ph- MPNs, with a frequency reported in 97% of cases of PV, in 57% of cases of ET and in 50% of cases of PMF. Recently, other variants of gene mutations in MPN Ph- have been identified, such as those affecting exon 12 of the JAK2 gene, the MPL gene (W515L and W515K), and the CALR (at the level of exon 9). Sporadic cases of PNH in the context of myeloproliferative neoplasms are reported in the literature, some of which with coexistence of the JAK2 mutation [24, 41–45].

The evolving nature of myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative conditions support the hypothesis that multiple genomic “hits” may occur in order to develop clinically significant PNH. While not fully elucidated, the CALR mutation has been shown to lead to excessive cell proliferation [46]. The CALR mutation as well as the JAK2V617F mutation may confer the survival benefit needed for clonal expansion and survival benefit within the bone marrow stem cell compartment of PIGA mutant cells, thus cooperating to generate the PNH phenotype [47].

MPN diagnosis preceded very often that of PNH and Shen et al. demonstrated that PIGA mutation was subclonal to JAK2-mutated clone in their report. Furthermore, the concurrence of PIGA mutations with genetic mutations such as JAK2 and CALR may lead to more accurate diagnosis, pathogenic understanding of disease process and development of targeted therapies [26, 48, 49].

In conclusion, the association of MPN and PNH deserves attention because of the unpredictable clinical course. MPN patients showing unexplained anemia with/without frank hemolysis, recurrent thrombosis and/or atypical bone marrow morphologic findings may be tested for PNH in order to better assess the thrombotic risk in this disease subcategory.
