Squaring the circle: Is triangulation of child abuse reports helpful?☆
Authors: Nehal Eldeeb, Andrew Grogan-Kaylor, Lijian Zhao, Sunghyun Hong, Olivia D. Chang, Todd I. Herrenkohl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Child maltreatment measurement has been a longstanding issue, with discrepancies across administrative records, parent-reports, and self-reports. One proposed solution is “triangulation,” or integrating data from multiple reporters and sources. However, it remains unclear how best to operationalize this concept.Objective:This study examines the concept of “triangulation” by employing different analytic methods to determine whether these methods reveal a common underlying construct of physical abuse and whether they predict adult depression.Participants and setting:Data come from the Lehigh Longitudinal Study, a 40+ year prospective study that began in the 1970s with children ages 18 months to 6 years of age. Data were collected in early childhood, middle childhood, adolescence, and adulthood (ages 36 and 46, on average).Methods:We applied five analytic approaches - network analysis, ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, structural equation modeling (SEM), latent profile analysis (LPA), and a cumulative index regression - to assess the relationships among multiple reporters of childhood physical abuse and adult depression.Results:SEM best modeled the latent construct of physical abuse and significantly predicted adult depression, with adult self-reports playing a particularly strong role. Network analysis also highlighted strong intercorrelations among self-reports and meaningful links with depression.Conclusion:SEM and network analysis were the most informative for triangulation and prediction of adult depression. Adult self-reports of abuse were most related and most predictive of adult depression.

Introduction

Child physical abuse has long been a social concern and focus of scientific research. Less often recognized, however, is how or whether different data sources (i.e., parent reports, self-reports, administrative records) can be meaningfully integrated to identify physical abuse, despite each having its own strengths and limitations.

Although the use of physically abusive discipline continues to be viewed by many as an acceptable practice in the United States (Gershoff et al., 2024), a growing number of child welfare advocates and researchers now conceptualize physically abusive discipline as encompassing “actual or potential physical harm resulting from an act or failure to act that is reasonably within the control of a parent or other person in a position of responsibility, power, or trust” (World Health Organization, 1999, p.15). Accordingly, in this paper, we use the terms physical abuse and abusive physical discipline interchangeably as both conceptualize forms of caregiver-perpetrated physical harm toward a child.

When multiple data sources are available in a single data set, researchers face a key methodological dilemma: should they try to integrate these sources or work with them independently? This question is of particular concern to researchers who study developmental outcomes of abuse. Triangulation across multiple data sources has been recommended as the “gold standard” (Fluke et al., 2021); however, it remains unclear whether sources should be combined (e.g., self- and agency-reports; Bull, Trott, & Kisely, 2025) or compared across indicators (Gilbert et al., 2012). Thus, while triangulation sounds appealing, it lacks concrete methodological guidance. Decisions regarding what sources to use, how to resolve discrepancies between them, and what analytic approaches to employ are often left to the researcher.

In this study, we explore the issue of triangulation and predictive validity of physical abuse measures derived from multiple reporters. We focus on physical abuse – a construct well established in the literature with data available from multiple reporters – and on adult depression, a widely-documented consequence of child physical abuse. Using data from the Lehigh Longitudinal Study, one of the longest-running prospective studies in the United States on the correlates and consequences of child abuse and neglect, we employ several strategies to examine the concurrence of these data sources and their capacity to predict adult depression. We evaluate the relative advantages and limitations of the different strategies and conclude with a discussion about optimal approaches to triangulating data and implications for predictive modeling vis-à-vis child physical abuse outcomes.

Background: literature review

Child maltreatment (CM) is a complex social phenomenon that is hard to measure. CM is inherently multidimensional, encompassing aspects such as frequency, chronicity, severity of harm, developmental timing, and perpetrator identity (Fallon et al., 2010; Gabrielli & Jackson, 2019). These dimensions not only influence the long-term impacts of maltreatment on individual development but also result in inconsistencies in how the construct is operationalized across studies (Gabrielli & Jackson, 2019). Moreover, potential discrepancies in reports from different data sources, including official records, self-reports, and caregiver (parent) reports, further complicate the identification and comparison of maltreatment experiences (Baldwin, Reuben, Newbury, & Danese, 2019; Coleman et al., 2024a). Together, the multidimensionality of maltreatment and the variability in data sources present major obstacles to achieving consistent and comprehensive measurement of CM.

Retrospective vs. prospective reports: definitions, strengths, and discrepancies

In CM research, there are two commonly used data sources: prospective and retrospective reports. Prospective reports are collected contemporaneously or shortly after the occurrence of maltreatment, often through surveys or interviews with parents, teacher assessments, or official records. These data tend to reflect observer or informant accounts and are typically documented during childhood (Coleman et al., 2024a; Danese, 2020). Retrospective data are generally gathered during adolescence or adulthood and rely on individuals’ self-reports to capture first-person experiences of abuse and neglect that occurred earlier in life (Coleman et al., 2024a).

Prospective reports are often considered more objective because they are less affected by recall bias and other memory-related distortions. They provide opportunities to document the timing and frequency of maltreatment during childhood and often use information from multiple reporters and sources. However, these measures are typically limited to cases that are visible or reportable by third parties; and therefore, tend to underestimate the true prevalence of child maltreatment (Mathews, Pacella, Dunne, Simunovic, & Marston, 2020; Shaffer et al., 2008). Moreover, marginalized groups are more likely to appear in administrative or case-record prospective reports, potentially reflecting differences in surveillance and reporting, whereas this pattern is less evident in prospective parent reports. As such, these data may not fully capture the hardships children endure and record-based prospective reports may introduce additional variability due to differences in surveillance systems, reporting thresholds, or organizational procedures following the disclosure of abuse (Cooley & Jackson, 2022).

Retrospective reports, in contrast, provide first-person accounts of maltreatment experiences that may have been missed by third-party informants. They are particularly valuable for identifying less externally observable forms of abuse that may remain hidden until later in a person’s life (Cooley & Jackson, 2022; Francis, Tsaligopoulou, Stock, Pingault, & Baldwin, 2023). Studies have shown that retrospective reports are more consistently associated with mental health outcomes in later life, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress (Danese & Widom, 2020; Francis et al., 2023; Reuben et al., 2016). At the same time, retrospective reports are vulnerable to recall bias and motivational influences, as individuals may underreport or reinterpret earlier experiences, due to feelings of shame, family loyalty, or social desirability leading to reports that are intentionally or unintentionally distorted (Coleman et al., 2024a; Hardt & Rutter, 2004). In some cases, particularly when parents reflect on their own past behaviors, retrospective reports can be biased by attempts to normalize or reframe the abusive behaviors or deflect responsibility (Seng & Prinz, 2008).

Given the strengths and limitations of each approach, some have proposed that they be used together (Bull et al., 2025). Yet, discrepancies between prospective and retrospective measures remain a consistent and a well-documented challenge. In a meta-analysis of 16 studies, Baldwin et al. (2019) found very limited agreement in sources used to determine whether maltreatment occurred suggesting that retrospective and prospective approaches should not be treated as interchangeable. Instead, their divergence offers insight into distinct ways maltreatment is experienced, remembered, and recorded, calling for analytic strategies that account for their complementary contributions.

Beyond retrospective and prospective: objective and subjective experiences

Recent research has emphasized that, beyond the timing of data collection, it is also important to consider how reports capture experiences of maltreatment, whether they reflect externally verifiable events or individuals’ internal perceptions and recollections. Building on the retrospective and prospective distinction, scholars have further differentiated between objective and subjective experiences of childhood maltreatment (Danese & Widom, 2020, 2023, 2024).

Specifically, objective experiences refer to externally verified events, such as court-substantiated records of abuse or neglect (Danese & Widom, 2020), whereas subjective experiences reflect individuals’ internal appraisals and recollections, typically reported retrospectively in adulthood (Danese & Widom, 2023). Although these two reporting approaches often overlap in timing, such as objective measures are usually assessed prospectively and subjective measures retrospectively, they capture distinct psychological processes. Objective reports focus on observable events documented by social systems, while subjective reports reflect how individuals perceive, interpret, and internalize those experiences.

Longitudinal findings from Danese and Widom’s series of studies consistently show that objective and subjective measures identify largely non-overlapping groups, each capturing different facets of maltreatment (Danese & Widom, 2020, 2023, 2024). Overall, subjective reports more strongly predict psychopathological outcomes in adulthood, likely through processes of perceived harm (Danese & Widom, 2020, 2023), whereas the associations with cognitive deficits are specific to objective indicators of neglect and did not generalize to physical or sexual abuse (Danese & Widom, 2024).

This divergence suggests that different data sources may represent distinct risk pathways, and that integrating both the objective-subjective and retrospective-prospective perspectives provides a more comprehensive understanding of how maltreatment is recorded, experienced, and linked to later developmental outcomes.

Understanding discrepancies in child maltreatment reporting

To explain the discrepancies between retrospective and prospective reports of CM, Coleman et al. (2024a) identified three key domains: methodological inconsistencies, memory processes, and motivational factors. Such discrepancies reflect not only differences in timing and information sources but also the distinction between observed events and experienced events (Danese & Widom, 2020, 2023, 2024). Methodological inconsistencies involve variation in definitions, informants, timing, and data collection procedures across studies and sources. Memory processes reflect how individuals encode and recall maltreatment experiences, influenced by age, dissociation, or reinterpretation over time. Motivational factors are described in the literature as personal or social reasons behind the choice to disclose or conceal past maltreatment (Coleman et al., 2024a). While motivational factors primarily focus on personal or social influences that affect disclosure, these motivations may also reflect developmental processes that influence whether and when a disclosure is made. Individuals’ appraisals of whether an experience constitutes maltreatment may interact with these motivations, particularly as self-understanding and awareness evolve over time (Coleman et al., 2024a; Coleman et al., 2024b).

In a follow-up qualitative study, Coleman et al. (2024b) found that individuals often reinterpreted childhood experiences as maltreatment only later in life, during adolescence or early adulthood. This “meaning-making process” may help explain why retrospective reports are more strongly associated with some developmental consequences of maltreatment, including internalizing behaviors, such as depression and anxiety (Park, 2010; Reuben et al., 2016).

Beyond the previously mentioned factors, informant identity also plays a central role in shaping discrepancies across maltreatment reports. Differences in informant perspective can systematically influence what forms of maltreatment are detected and recorded. For example, prospective data often derive from teachers’, medical professionals’, or caseworkers’ observations, which are constrained by what can be externally verifiable or meets legal thresholds for reporting (Coleman et al., 2024b; Danese, 2020). In contrast, retrospective self-reports can reveal private or subjective experiences such as emotional humiliation or psychological control, that rarely come to the attention of third parties (Coleman et al., 2024a; Cooley & Jackson, 2022).

Triangulation as a strategy for integrating multiple sources of maltreatment data

Triangulation, originally adapted from navigation and land surveying, refers to the use of multiple points of measurement to locate a single target (Padgett, 2016). Denzin (1978) first introduced this concept into the social sciences as a strategy to enhance the validity and credibility of research findings. He proposed four main types of triangulation: data triangulation (using multiple sources of data), methodological triangulation (employing multiple research methods), observer triangulation (involving multiple raters or observers), and theoretical triangulation (drawing on multiple theoretical perspectives; Carter, 2014; Denzin, 2017; Padgett, 2016). Later, Janesick (2000) added interdisciplinary triangulation, which emphasizes integrating insights from different academic fields to enrich interpretation. The core logic across all types is that if findings from different methods or sources converge, the results are more credible.

In recent years, triangulation has been increasingly invoked in CM research as a way to integrate diverse data sources to obtain a more complete understanding of complex experiences. Yet, clear guidance on how to apply this concept, particularly in quantitative research, remains limited. Patton (1999) defined triangulation as “the use of multiple methods or data sources to develop a comprehensive understanding of phenomena,” (p.1192) but most applications remain qualitative. Flick (2004) further argued that triangulation can enrich interpretive depth, while others highlight that inconsistencies across data sources may be analytically and theoretically meaningful rather than problematic (Bloor, 1997; Riessman, 1990). Baldwin and Degli Esposti (2021) extended the application of triangulation to child maltreatment research, proposing it as a methodological framework for integrating evidence from studies with different sources of bias to strengthen causal inference. By reviewing three complementary approaches, measurement comparisons, within-family comparisons, and cross-context comparisons, they demonstrated how triangulation can help clarify the distinct contributions of subjective and objective experiences of maltreatment to later outcomes, with perceived harm potentially serving as the mechanism linking childhood adversity to psychopathology.

Current study

Building on these perspectives, the present study conceptualizes triangulation as the quantitative integration and comparison of multiple data sources. To do so, we employ a multi-method analytical approach, including network analysis, ordinary least squares (OLS) regression (in which reports from each reporter are entered individually as independent variables), structural equation modeling (SEM), latent profile analysis (LPA), and a regression using a cumulative index. These methods are used to examine how different sources of physical abuse data from the Lehigh Longitudinal Study relate to one another and to adult depression. Our goal is to triangulate across multiple reporters of physical abuse (parent, self, and administrative records) to assess the extent to which they reflect a shared underlying construct and explore whether integrating these sources improves predictive validity.

Depression, measured using the validated PHQ-8 assessment (Kroenke et al., 2009), was selected as the outcome due to its well-established association with childhood physical abuse (Herrenkohl et al., 2013, 2021a). While not exhaustive of all possible outcomes, depression serves as an illustrative example to evaluate the utility of multiple reporter measurement approaches.

This study addresses two primary research questions: To what extent do physical abuse measures from different reporters (parent, self, and administrative) reflect a common underlying construct?Does integrating data from multiple sources (i.e., triangulating across reporters) improve the prediction of adult depression compared to relying on single-source measures?

Methods

Sample

As cited in Herrenkohl et al. (1991, 2013, 2021b), data for this study were drawn from a longstanding, prospective investigation of the correlates and consequences of child maltreatment. The study began in the 1970s with a gender-balanced sample of 457 children, aged 18 months to 6 years, recruited from child welfare caseloads and group settings (e.g., Head Start and daycare centers) in a two-county area in eastern Pennsylvania, USA. The sample was socioeconomically diverse, with over 60 % of families living below the federal poverty level at baseline, and was predominantly White (80.7 %), with smaller proportions identifying as multiracial (11.2 %) or Black/African American, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, or other/unknown (6.8 %).

Participants were followed over five decades, with assessments conducted during preschool (1976–1977), elementary school (1980–1982), adolescence (1990–1992), and adulthood (2008–2010 and 2019–2020). Extensive data on child abuse and neglect were obtained at all waves of the study, including from parents and child welfare agencies at the start of the study in the 1970s and 1980s. In the adult waves of the study, CM was assessed very generally, as the focus during these waves was primarily on the long-term consequences and continuation of abuse across generations. All data collection and procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Boards at Lehigh University, the University of Washington, and the University of Michigan.

The most recent wave of data collection (conducted in 2019–2020) included 303 participants, who were on average 46 years old at the time. Among them, 52.5 % were male, and 83.8 % identified as White. Eighty percent had graduated from high school or earned a General Education Development (GED); 44 % had obtained at least one higher education degree; and 76 % were employed at least part-time. In terms of socio-economic status, the median household income fell between $60,000 and $70,000, 23 % of households were receiving food assistance, and 36 % were receiving medical assistance due to low income.

Measures

Prospective reports of physical child abuse

As reported in Herrenkohl and colleagues (2021b), physical child abuse was assessed prospectively using both official records and parent self-reports. Official child welfare records, which captured incidents of abuse from birth to the start of the study (when the children were between 18 months and 6 years old), were obtained at study onset in the 1970s and used in the initial recruitment of families. At the time of recruitment, 54 % of participating children had documented involvement with child welfare agencies for abuse or neglect. This official record data is represented in the analyses by the variable labeled administrative.

Parent self-reports of physical discipline were collected during both the preschool and school-age assessments. In the preschool assessment (M age = 4 years), primary caregivers (mostly mothers) reported on their own and others’ use of disciplinary practices in two timeframes: (a) the past 3 months and (b) prior to the past 3 months. In the school-age assessment (M age = 8 years), parents reported on disciplinary practices used over the past year. These responses were used to create two parent-reported abuse variables: (1) Parent Report 1 (PR1) reflects the preschool period and was calculated by summing the severity scores of abusive practices reported across both preschool timeframes, and (2) Parent Report 2 (PR2) reflects the school-age period and captures abusive practices reported for the past year.

Across both assessments, parents reported on 12 physical discipline practices considered severely punitive or abusive (e.g., burning, biting, or hitting a child with a hard object resulting in bruising). Each practice received a severity rating of 4 or 5 on a scale ranging from 1 (rewarding) to 5 (abusive). These severity ratings were summed to produce a total abuse score for each child. Scores of 4–5 indicate exposure to one abusive practice; scores of 8–10 indicate exposure to two abusive practices; scores of 12–15 indicate exposure to three abusive practices; and scores above 16 reflect exposure to four or more abusive practices. A score of 0 indicates no exposure to abusive practices.

Retrospective reports of physical child abuse

Participants retrospectively reported on physical abuse during adolescence and adulthood. In adolescence (M age = 18 years), participants reported whether they had experienced any of the same 12 abusive physical discipline practices previously reported by their caregivers. These questions referred to experiences from birth through age 12 (e.g., “Has your [mother], [father], or [anyone else] ever used the following methods to influence your behavior – spanking you; slapping your face?”). Severity scores were again assigned based on established ratings and summed to yield a total abuse severity score. This measure is represented in the analyses as Self-Report 1 (SR1).

In adulthood, participants completed a single-item retrospective measure: “As a child, were you ever badly beaten up by your parents or the people who raised you?” (yes/no). This question was asked in two waves (Wave 1: 2010, M age = 36 years; Wave 2: 2020, M age = 46 years), and represented as: (1) Self-Report 2 (SR2) for the 2010 wave, and (2) Self-Report 3 (SR3) for the 2020 wave.

Self-reported adult depression

During the 2020 adult assessment, participants completed the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8), a validated and widely used measure of adult clinical depression (Kroenke et al., 2009). Each item assessed the frequency of depressive symptoms over the last 12 weeks using a 4-point scale: 0 (not at all), 1 (several days), 2 (more than half the days), and 3 (nearly every day). A composite depression score was calculated by summing responses across the eight items (range: 0–24), with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. Internal consistency of the scale was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.90).

Demographics

Covariates were added from the adult wave of 2020 data and included age, ethnicity/race, gender, marital status, and annual household income.

Data transformation

Several data transformations were applied to facilitate the analyses, as described below.

Standardization:

Continuous variables from different reporters (PR1, PR2, SR1) were measured on different scales. To allow for meaningful comparisons and to roughly align these variables more closely with dichotomous variables (SR2, SR3), we standardized all continuous variables using z-scores (PR1, PR2, SR1).

Binary conversion:

For the “additive model”, we created binary versions of each continuous reporter variable. Original values of 0 remained 0, while any non-zero value was recoded as 1, indicating the presence of physical abuse. This approach aligns with prior work in additive/cumulative risk frameworks (e.g., Evans, Li, & Whipple, 2013). These binary indicators were then summed to create a composite index variable (reportercount), representing the total number of reporters (ranging from 0 to 6) indicating physical abuse.

Missing data:

The network analysis was estimated using pairwise correlations, allowing each correlation to be calculated based on all available data for each pair of variables. All other analyses (OLS, LPA, SEM, additive model) relied on complete case data. Minor variations in sample sizes across models reflect the slightly different sets of equations used in each model (network analysis, n = 211; OLS, n = 186; SEM, n = 208; LPA, n = 273; and additive regression, n = 243).

Analytic approaches

We used five analytic approaches to examine the relationships between multiple reporters of child physical abuse and adult depression. All analyses were conducted using Stata 18 or 19 (StataCorp., 2023, 2025), with additional network analyses conducted in R (R Core Team, 2025). Initial drafts of the paper were prepared in Quarto (Allaire et al., 2025) using the Statamarkdown package developed by Hemken (2023).

Conceptually, we envisioned each statistical approach as taking a unique approach. Network analysis could be considered to be a more exploratory technique for examining the relationship of different indicators of child physical abuse. OLS regression suggests that the association of different reports of child physical abuse with the outcome of depression is linear and additive. SEM tries to estimate an underlying latent construct of physical abuse that might plausibly give rise to the different observed reports of physical abuse. While closely related to SEM approaches, LPA attempts to find underlying latent subgroups of individuals (rather than an underlying statistical construct) who share similar patterns of reported maltreatment. Lastly, the additive model uses a simple count of the number of reporters who reported child physical abuse as an indicator of abuse exposure. We outline the particulars of each statistical approach in more detail below.

Network analysis

We began by examining the structure of associations among physical abuse indicators and their relationship to adult depression using network analysis. A pairwise correlation matrix was computed for all the abuse indicators and the depression outcome. We then converted this matrix into a network object using the igraph package (Csárdi et al., 2025) in R, where each node represented a variable (i.e., an abuse indicator or the depression outcome) and each edge represented the strength of the correlation between two variables. Next, we visualized the network and applied a “community detection” algorithm (Csárdi et al., 2025) to identify clusters of strongly correlated variables. Then, we calculated the centrality of each network node using procedures in igraph. This allowed us to identify which indicators were most strongly connected to other variables in the network, including depressive symptoms. We note that, given the small number of nodes, our network analysis is used primarily as a visual and descriptive tool to illustrate the dimensionality and relationships among reports. This approach helps highlight the need for triangulation across informants, offering a more holistic view of the relationships among measures that may not be as apparent from a traditional correlation table.

Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression

A standard OLS regression model (Stock & Watson, 2003) was used to estimate the association between each individual abuse report (administrative, PR1, PR2, SR1, SR2, SR3) and adult depression, adjusting for covariates. This approach served as a statistical baseline for comparison with more advanced quantitative modeling techniques. Given that the depression outcome is continuous, OLS was the appropriate analytic strategy; for categorical outcomes, logistic regression would have been used (Long & Freese, 2014).

Structural equation modeling (SEM)

We used SEM (StataCorp, 2025) to model a latent construct of child physical abuse. Each reporter-specific abuse indicator was allowed to load onto this latent construct, enabling us to assess their relative contributions. We also estimated the direct association between the latent child physical abuse construct and adult depression. Model fit was assessed using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI).

Latent profile analysis (LPA)

LPA (StataCorp, 2025) was employed to identify distinct subgroups (or profiles) of physical abuse exposure across reporters. We estimated models specifying between one and four profiles. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating a better fit. The Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR) compared the fit of a k-class model to a k-1 class model, with a significant p-value (p < .05) indicating better fit. We examined entropy (ranging from 0 to 1) to assess classification quality, with higher values indicating clearer separation between profiles. Finally, we used class membership as a predictor of adult depression in a linear regression model in Stata, specifying class membership as a categorical variable using factor notation (i.class).

Additive regression model

Using the composite index reporter count, which represented the number of reporter sources that indicated physical abuse, we used OLS regression to examine the association between this additive index and adult depression, adjusting for covariates.

Results

Network analysis

The network analysis (see Figs. 2 and 3) showed that the strongest correlations emerged within reporter types. The three self-report indicators (SR1, SR2, and SR3) were significantly correlated with each other (ranging from r = 0.41 to 0.66, p < .05), indicating moderate to strong consistency in retrospective self-reports of childhood physical abuse. Similarly, parent-report measures (PR1 and PR2) were significantly correlated (r = 0.34, p < .05), suggesting moderate consistency in parent-reported child physical abuse over time. In contrast, correlations across reporter types (e.g., between parent- and self-report, or administrative records) were generally less correlated but still significant, ranging from r = 0.13 to 0.27, p < .05. The strongest cross-reporter correlation was between administrative records and adult self-report wave 1 (SR2), with a significant correlation of r = 0.30, p < .05.

When examining associations between physical abuse indicators and the outcome of depression, adult self-report wave 2 (SR3) showed the strongest significant correlation (r = 0.26, p < .05), followed by administrative reports (r = 0.21, p < .05) and adult self-report wave 1 (SR2; r = 0.14, p < .05). In contrast, parent-report indicators were not statistically significant and uncorrelated with adult depression (r = 0.04 to 0.07). Overall, these results (see Table 1) highlight moderate consistency within reporter types, weaker agreement across reporter types, and a modest association between some abuse indicators and adult depressive symptoms.

Finally, we employed two network analytic strategies: A “walktrap” community detection and a measure of centrality. A “walktrap” community detection algorithm (Csárdi et al., 2025), which identifies communities or clusters of nodes, suggested that there were two underlying communities of highly correlated nodes within the network (see Fig. 4). The first community consisted of self-reports, administrative records, and the outcome of depression. The second separate community was confined to the two nodes representing the parental reports. Centrality scores, which identify how strongly connected a node is to its neighbors, indicated that the self-report and second parental report nodes were among the most highly connected nodes in the network, though they formed parts of separate communities within the network.

Ordinary least squares regression (OLS)

Results from the OLS regression model (see Fig. 5, Eqn 2) indicated that adult self-reported childhood physical abuse at wave 2 (SR3) was the only unique predictor of depression. Specifically, a one-unit increase in SR3 was associated with a 4.41-point increase in depression scores (p < .01), controlling for all other reporter indicators and covariates (see Table 2). None of the other physical abuse indicators, including parent reports (PR1, PR2), adolescent self-report (SR1), adult self-report wave 1 (SR2), or administrative records, significantly predicted depression once SR3 was included in the model. Among the covariates, marital status and annual household income were also significantly associated with depression scores. Compared to those who were married, individuals who were separated or never married had significantly higher depression scores (β = 3.90, p < .05 and 2.14, p < .05, respectively). In addition, higher household income was significantly associated with lower depression, with a one-unit increase in income linked to a 0.20-point decrease in depression scores (p < .05). Overall, these findings support a direct link between adult self-reports of past physical abuse and adult depression. In contrast, the influence of other reporter perspectives may be indirect or disrupted by intervening experiences that change the developmental pathway to adult depression.

Structural equation modeling (SEM)

In the measurement model (see Fig. 6, Eqn 3 and 4), all indicators loaded significantly onto the latent construct of physical abuse (X), with loading strengths generally increasing across developmental time points (see Table 3). The overall model demonstrated acceptable but slightly below conventional cutoffs for good fit (CFI = 0.89, TLI = 0.83). Early childhood parent report (PR1) had the smallest loading (β = 0.26, p < .01), followed by middle childhood parent report (PR2; β = 0.29, p < .01). The adolescent self-report (SR1) contributed more strongly (β = 0.53, p < .01), and the adult self-reports demonstrated the strongest associations with the latent construct. Specifically, the first wave of adult self-report (SR2) had a loading of β = 0.75 (p < .01), and the second wave (SR3) had the highest loading overall (β = 0.78, p < .01). Administrative records also significantly contributed to the latent construct (β = 0.43, p < .01), though to a lesser extent than the adult self-reports.

In the structural portion of the model, the latent construct (X) representing physical abuse was significantly associated with adult depression. A one-unit increase in the physical abuse latent construct was associated with a 0.22-point increase in depression scores (p < .01), indicating a moderate and statistically significant relationship. Additionally, gender (β = −0.18, p < .01) and annual household income (β = −0.20, p < .01) were both significant predictors of depression, with males and individuals with higher income reporting lower depression scores. Overall, although multiple reporter perspectives shape the latent construct of physical abuse, the construct is most strongly defined by adult self-reports. This dominant contribution likely drives the observed association between physical abuse and adult depression.

Latent profile analysis (LPA)

To explore whether underlying latent profiles could be identified based on multiple reporter sources of physical abuse (see Fig. 7, Eqn 5) we began by estimating models with two and three latent profiles using all six abuse measures. However, neither the two- nor three-class models converged, indicating poor model fit and an inability to extract distinct profiles from the combined measures.

We then conducted separate LPAs for the single-item abuse indicators (adult self-reports: SR2 and SR3) and the multi-item abuse indicators (PR1, PR2, SR1). The model using only the single-item self-report measures also failed to converge. In contrast, the model based on multi-item measures successfully converged, and a three-class solution provided the best fit (see Table 4). Specifically, the 3-class model demonstrated significantly improved fit over the 2-class model (BIC = 6472.12; LMR = 28.99, p = .035), while the 4-class model did not further improve fit. Entropy for the 3-class model was high (0.835), indicating that the classes are well separated.

The first and largest class (n = 210; 77 %) was labeled concordant low exposure and was characterized by consistently low (i.e., below average) scores across early childhood parent report, middle childhood parent report, and adolescent self-report (PR1 = −0.374, PR2 = −0.328, SR1 = −0.195). The second class (n = 44; 16 %) was labeled discordant higher early childhood exposure and showed elevated early childhood parent report but only moderate scores (i.e., above average) in middle childhood parent report and adolescent self-report (PR1 = 1.614, PR2 = 0.442, SR1 = 0.489). The third and smallest class (n = 19; 7 %) was labeled concordant elevated middle childhood and adolescent exposure, with the highest middle childhood parent report and high adolescent self-report compared to the two other groups, alongside moderate (i.e., above average) early childhood parent report (PR1 = 0.297, PR2 = 2.351, SR1 = 1.029) (see Fig. 8).

We then examined whether these latent classes predicted adult depression. Class membership in the three profiles did not significantly predict depression scores after adjusting for covariates (gender, age, ethnicity/race, marital status, and annual income). Overall, although potentially meaningful patterns of concordance and discordance in abuse exposure were identified across reporters and time, the current latent class memberships were not related to adult depression.

Additive regression model

To examine whether the accumulation of physical abuse indicators across multiple reporters enhances the prediction of adult depression, we conducted an OLS regression using the composite index variable, reporter count. We found that a one-unit increase in reporter count was associated with a 0.43 point increase in depression scores, though this association did not reach statistical significance (see Table 5). Among the covariates, gender and annual household income were significantly related to depression. Males reported significantly lower depression scores than females (β = −1.69, p < .01) and higher annual household income was associated with lower depression scores (β = −0.25, p < .01). Overall, although there was a modest, non-significant trend suggesting that depression scores increase as more sources indicate physical abuse, this additive approach did not significantly improve the prediction of depression.

Discussion

This study explored the utility of multiple reporting sources in measuring childhood physical abuse and predicting adult depression. Specifically, we examined whether measures from different reporters reflect a common underlying construct of physical abuse, and whether triangulating data from multiple sources improves the prediction of adult depression. Using five analytic approaches – network analysis, OLS, SEM, LPA, and a regression using a cumulative index – we derived several important insights that we elaborate on below (see Table 6).

First, while OLS regression – where each reporting source is an independent variable – might appear to be the most straightforward technique, its interpretive utility is limited. In our model, the OLS identified only the third self-report (SR3) as a statistically significant predictor of depression, paralleling the important role assigned to SR3 by the other analyses. However, a disadvantage of the OLS approach is that mathematically, it estimates the association of each variable with the outcome ceteris paribus. That is to say, OLS estimates the unique contribution of each variable to the outcome, controlling for all others, and while accounting for the interrelationship of predictors. However, it does not model the interrelationships among predictors. This limits its ability to capture the overlapping or shared variance among reporters.

In contrast, the most informative approaches appear to be SEM and network analysis. SEM, a well-established technique (Bollen, 1989; Tarka, 2018), allowed us to model a latent underlying construct of physical abuse and estimate the relationship from each individual reporter with that latent construct. As noted, findings from this model highlight a particularly strong role for the SR3, but also indicate the correlation of the other self-reports with the underlying construct of physical abuse, and the weaker correlation of the parental reports with this construct. Further, the SEM approach found that the underlying construct of physical abuse significantly predicted adult depression. However, it is likely that the influence of the self-reports is primarily driving the prediction of depression.

The network analysis produced findings that closely mirrored those of the SEM. Despite several decades of methodological work (Csárdi et al., 2025; Csárdi & Nepusz, 2006; Florean, Dobrean, & Roman, 2022), network analysis remains an “emerging” approach, but also shows promise for analyzing patterns of correlated indicators. SEM assumes that there is a latent underlying construct measured by the observed indicators, in this case, reports from different reporters. Epistemologically, or perhaps ontologically, network analysis treats variables as a system of interacting sets of indicators, without the assumption of an underlying latent construct. It is important to emphasize here that we do not adopt this conceptual stance of network analysis (we do believe that there is an underlying construct of physical abuse), but simply employ network analysis as a complementary, sophisticated method to examine patterns of correlated indicators.

The initial graph from the network analysis revealed strong intercorrelations among the self-reports, and appreciable correlations between the self-reports and the depression outcome. Again, SR3 showed the strongest correlation with depression, likely due to the “proximity” of SR3 to the outcome, and that both were measured contemporaneously and from the same source. Notably, administrative records emerged as the second most strongly correlated with depression. This may reflect the severity of abuse captured by administrative records, with more extreme forms potentially leaving a more enduring impact on mental health. The network analysis also showed lesser correlations among the parental reports, and relatedly, weaker correlations between the parental reports and the outcomes of depression.

The “walktrap” community detection algorithm provided further insight by identifying a correlated “community” of nodes comprising the self-reports, administrative records, and depression outcome. This structure, not as clearly visible in the SEM, highlights the role of the administrative records as aligned with both the self-reports and the outcome. This finding is echoed by the calculation of the network quantity of importance (Csárdi et al., 2025), which highlights the role of SR3, while also drawing attention to the importance of SR2 and SR1.

Taken together, the SEM and network analysis emphasize that self-reports relate more strongly with one another and predict the outcome. In the SEM, the self-report indicators loaded strongly onto the estimated latent construct of physical abuse. Similarly, in the network model, the walktrap community detection algorithm identified self-reports as some of the indicators most strongly correlated with both administrative records and the outcome of depression.]

Overall, while triangulation and prediction are conceptually distinct, this study demonstrates that certain analytic approaches can support both goals. Both SEM and network analysis appear to afford the possibility of triangulating across multiple reports of physical abuse, as well as assessing the degree to which these multiple reports are predictive of depression. Although simpler methods such as OLS regression may be appealing because of their simplicity, more advanced methods should be employed when information from multiple sources is available.

Limitations

Although this study offers valuable insights into the use of different data sources in the prediction of adult outcomes of physical child abuse, it is not without limitations. A primary limitation is the variation in how physical abuse was measured across reporters. The parent report and adolescent self-report measures are aggregated from 12 specific physical abuse items (e.g., hitting, biting, burning), whereas the adult self-reports consist of a single item regarding being (“badly beaten up”). These discrepancies raise the possibility that the measures capture related but distinct constructs. Moreover, the wording of the single-item measure may have limited variability, potentially capturing only the most severe cases of physical abuse, which could partially explain the stronger correlation between administrative and self-reports than between administrative and parent reports. However, the parent-report measure also included an item reflecting similar severe discipline (“hit or paddle child such that it left a bruise”), which similarly captures more extreme behavior. Future studies should consider using the same or comparable measures across all reporters.

In our exploration of these various analytic methods, we also acknowledge that there may be shared method variance between some measures, particularly between self-reported measures of abuse and self-reported measures of depression. Future research could leverage longitudinal repeated measures data to examine such possibilities. For example, in panel data with relatively frequent measures of both self-reported abuse and depression, methods such as fixed effects regression could be employed to attempt to rule out possible shared variance. With repeated measures data, fixed effects regression methods are able to control for all time-invariant characteristics of study participants (e.g., unobserved factors that might contribute to both self-reports of abuse and depression), whether or not they are explicitly included in the model (Allison, 2009; Ma, Grogan-Kaylor, & Lee, 2020; Wooldridge, 2010).

The study’s focus on physical abuse, selected because data were available across reporters and the construct is well-established in the literature, also represents a limitation. Convergence patterns and predictive associations may differ for other forms of maltreatment. Future research should examine whether current findings generalize across different abuse types.

Another limitation is that the number of indicators varied by reporter (one administrative, two parent-report, and three self-report), which may have influenced the latent construct models (e.g., SEM, LPA) and the additive regression model by unevenly weighting some reporters. However, the OLS regression and network analysis provide complementary perspectives that are less sensitive to this imbalance. Future studies should include a similar number of indicators across reporter types to improve the comparability.

Additionally, dichotomizing continuous reporter variables for the additive model may have removed nuance. However, this step was necessary to construct an interpretable composite index, as mixing continuous and binary measures would have complicated interpretation, and this approach aligns with cumulative risk frameworks.

Further, although we employed advanced analytic techniques, the analyses remain correlational in nature and do not imply causality. Establishing causal relationships was beyond the scope of this study, but future research might build on our findings to explore triangulation approaches in causal frameworks, leveraging the longitudinal nature of this data set or other data sets, along with appropriately advanced quantitative methods. Finally, the sample is relatively modest in size, and the sample is racially and ethnically homogeneous, which may limit generalizability. Nonetheless, the dataset is notable for its long-term follow-up (over four decades) and diversity in socio-economic status and gender.

Conclusions and implications for research

Our findings have several important implications for research, practice, and policy. A central implication for research is the demonstrated validity and reliability of self-reports. Despite common concerns about recall bias, self-reports in this study were the most highly intercorrelated, loaded strongly onto the latent construct of physical abuse, were most predictive of adult depression, and aligned closely with administrative records. These results suggest the promise of self-reports for measuring childhood physical abuse, but concerns about recall bias remain. The particularly strong association between SR3 (adult self-report measured concurrently with depression) and adult depression may be partly driven by shared method variance or timing. Given that both SR3 and depression were reported at the same time, even though SR3 retrospectively asks about past experiences, we cannot establish temporal ordering. This highlights the importance of using prospective measures when examining long-term outcomes, as the temporal proximity of measurement makes it difficult to determine whether childhood physical abuse led to depression or whether current depression influenced reports of past abuse, thereby limiting causal interpretation. Future studies should aim to disentangle the effects of source and timing by, for example, using time-lagged designs or multi-method data collection approaches. Additionally, our findings support further investigation into patterns of agreement and discrepancy across prospective and retrospective reports. Understanding when and how different sources align or differ could clarify which discrepancies are meaningful for predicting outcomes and informing interventions. We also recommend reconsidering terminology around administrative records. While often treated as an objective source, administrative records (e.g., child welfare reports) are produced by individuals and are subject to human judgment. Researchers and practitioners might instead refer to them as “caseworker reports” to more accurately reflect their nature as one perspective among others.

In summary, our findings add to the longstanding discussion about how to leverage different data sources to improve the prediction of child and adult outcomes from abuse. We determined that adult self-reports of physical abuse are dominant in the prediction of adult depression for reasons previously stated. However, questions remain as to how to work productively and efficiently with child abuse data drawn from different sources and what to make of differences that emerge in predictive models. It is critical that researchers remain cognizant of the challenges that exist in this space and interpret their findings with some degree of caution, particularly when working with a single data source. Realizing that the prediction of later outcomes is somewhat determined by the data sources employed, it is important not to overstate or become too wedded to the findings that emerge. We encourage other researchers to contribute to this discussion by conducting similar analyses with different datasets and engaging in conversation about what their findings reveal.
