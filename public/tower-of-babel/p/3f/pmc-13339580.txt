MDSGene Systematic Review of Common Forms of Dominant Hereditary Spastic Paraplegia: Novel Insights

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive spasticity and lower limb weakness. The most common forms of autosomal dominant HSP are caused by pathogenic variants in SPAST (SPG4 or HSP‐SPAST), ATL1 (SPG3A or HSP‐ATL1), and REEP1 (SPG31 or HSP‐REEP1).ObjectivesWe performed an MDSGene Systematic Review to determine in‐depth genotype–phenotype associations and to estimate longitudinal progression, to inform prognostication and clinical trial stratification.MethodsWe systematically collected demographic, phenotypic, and genetic data from published reports of individuals affected by these forms of HSP using the MDSGene protocol.ResultsWe reviewed 2177 affected individuals, including 1670 individuals with HSP‐SPAST, 356 with HSP‐ATL1 and 151 with HSP‐REEP1. HSP‐ATL1 was associated with an earlier age at onset compared to HSP‐SPAST and HSP‐REEP1. Toe‐walking was more frequently reported in HSP‐ATL1 (10.4%) and HSP‐REEP1 (3.3%) than HSP‐SPAST (0.3%). Upper limb hyperreflexia and abnormalities of bladder function were more frequent in HSP‐SPAST than HSP‐ATL1 or HSP‐REEP1. Sufficient data was available to estimate disease progression for HSP‐SPAST; this showed that Spastic Paraplegia Rating Scale (SPRS) scores increased with increasing age at examination after the age of 40 years. Truncating variants were more frequent in HSP‐SPAST and HSP‐REEP1 than HSP‐ATL1.ConclusionOverall, HSP‐ATL1, HSP‐SPAST and HSP‐REEP1 demonstrated differences in clinical phenotypes. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to model longitudinal progression using SPRS scores in HSP. Missing data is a limiting factor in all these comparisons, highlighting the need for uniform data collection. Online resources can be found at https://www.mdsgene.org/.

Hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) refers to a group of inherited neurodegenerative disorders that are characterized by progressive spasticity and weakness of the lower extremities. Clinically, HSP is classified into either pure (uncomplicated) or complex (complicated) forms. 1 Progressive lower extremity spastic weakness, bladder dysfunction, and a mild impairment of lower extremity vibration sensation are features of pure HSP. Complex HSPs are characterised by additional abnormalities such as amyotrophy, ataxia, intellectual disability, and peripheral neuropathy. 2

HSP demonstrates marked genetic heterogeneity, with over 92 distinct genetic loci and 79 genes identified to date. 2 , 3 These genes encode proteins with a wide variety of neuronal functions, such as membrane and axonal transport, endoplasmic reticulum morphology, mitochondrial function, DNA repair, autophagy, lipid metabolism, and myelination processes. 4 Despite this genetic diversity, all forms of HSP share a common disease pathway—the progressive degeneration of the corticospinal tract in a length‐dependent manner.

HSP can be inherited in an autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X‐linked or mitochondrial pattern. 5 Amongst the autosomal dominant HSPs, the most common forms are due to heterozygous, disease‐causing variants in SPAST (SPG4 or HSP‐SPAST), ATL1 (SPG3A or HSP‐ATL1), and REEP1 (SPG31 or HSP‐REEP1), accounting for an estimated 40%, 10–15%, and 5% of all affected individuals, respectively. 6

To our knowledge, only two other systematic reviews of the clinicogenetic features of HSP have been published. 7 , 8 The strength of our review is the use of a stringent and rigorous method to gather all affected individuals of the three most common dominant HSPs (HSP‐SPAST, HSP‐ATL1 and HSP‐REEP1) reported in the literature with sufficient data. We abstract, harmonize and curate available clinical and genetic data, classify reported variants using a standardized approach, and explore genotype–phenotype correlations. The results are presented in an easily accessible online database, the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society Genetic database (MDSGene, https://www.mdsgene.org/). Additionally, to our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to longitudinal model spastic paraplegia rating scale (SPRS) data in HSP, which is critical information for prognostication and planning of clinical trials.

Methods

This systematic review follows MDSGene's standardized protocol as described previously 9 and is compatible with the PRISMA guideline. 10 The literature search and data extraction protocol have been developed for the generation and maintenance of MDSGene, a database that summarizes and quantifies demographic, genetic, and phenotypic data from the literature for monogenic movement disorders. Data collected for this review are accessible on the MDSGene website (available at http://www.mdsgene.org). 10

Literature Search and Eligibility Criteria

We performed a systematic literature search for publications including patients with HSP‐ATL1, HSP‐SPAST, and HSP‐REEP1 using the NCBI's PubMed database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed), and standardized search terms (Table S1).

Titles, abstracts, and full texts of peer‐reviewed articles published in English until January 2024 were screened for inclusion. Articles that demonstrated at least one patient with a disease‐relevant variant in ATL1, SPAST, or REEP1 were selected. References of these original articles were also screened for potentially eligible other articles and additional data. Figure 1 demonstrates an overview of the literature search and the filtering procedure.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria for Patients and Genetic Variants

This review included articles in which patients were both clinically affected and genetically confirmed to carry a disease‐relevant variant in ATL1, SPAST, or REEP1. Patients were defined as “affected” if they had either spasticity, weakness, or hyperreflexia of the lower limbs. Variants were not considered “disease‐relevant” if they had a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 1% based on ethnicity with the maximal MAF in the gnomAD Browser (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), dbSNP (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp), and/or in at least 100 unaffected control individuals screened for the variant of interest in the respective publication. Further variants that were interpreted as benign (see below) were also excluded from genotype–phenotype analyses. We also included rare instances of biallelic disease‐relevant variants in these genes. Please see article screening log for details on individual articles screened (Article Screening Log, Supporting Information).

Data Collection Process

We applied the MDSGene data extraction protocol as previously reported 9 , 11 , 12 to all eligible articles. For each article, data on demographics, genetics, and clinical variables, as well as features of the study, were extracted (see Supporting Information S1 for a list of all extracted variables).

Pathogenicity Scoring

The pathogenicity of a genetic variant was assessed according to co‐segregation with disease in affected family members and its recurrence among HSP patients, frequency in controls, Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) scoring, 13 as well as functional evidence extracted from published in vitro and in vivo studies. Based on these categories, a pathogenicity score was devised, and variants were classified as definitely pathogenic (score > 14), probably pathogenic (score 10–14), possibly pathogenic (score 5–9), or benign (score <5) based on predefined criteria as described previously 9 (see also https://www.mdsgene.org/methods).

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS. Comparison of continuous and demographic data between groups of patients were performed using Kruskal‐Wallis and Wilcoxon rank sum tests. Categorical data were analyzed between groups using the Chi‐square tests. Here, we also tested for correlation between the SPRS, a scale to rate functional impairment occurring in pure HSP 14 , with age at exam and disease duration using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Respective figures were produced using Prism 10. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons when appropriate.

Results

Overview of Literature Data

The PubMed literature search and articles obtained through additional sources yielded a total of 239 articles for HSP‐ATL1, of which 70 were eligible, 531 articles for HSP‐SPAST, for which 187 were eligible, and 334 articles for HSP‐REEP1, of which 31 were eligible (Fig. 1, Supplementary File_HSP Article Screening Log).

By classifying these articles according to study type, we found that the most common were genetic screening studies (36.3%), family studies (30.4%), followed by, case reports/series (23.2%), and other/mixed‐type studies (10.0%). While genetic screening studies were the most common study type for HSP‐SPAST and HSP‐REEP1, family studies were the most common study type for HSP‐ATL1 (Table S2).

Our literature search included 2177 variant carriers and 709 potentially distinct disease‐causing variants. There were 356 HSP‐ATL1 patients with 83 different variants, 1670 HSP‐SPAST patients with 571 variants, and 151 HSP‐REEP1 with 53 variants.

Demographic Data

We found that more males than females were reported for all three genes: 57.4% for HSP‐ATL1, 54.7% for HSP‐SPAST, and 65.3% for HSP‐REEP1 (Table 1). Most patients carrying disease‐relevant variants, in any of the three genes, had a positive family history (85.8% in HSP‐ATL1, 83.5% in HSP‐SPAST, and 96.3% in HSP‐REEP1, Table 1).

Overall, 49.6% affected individuals had European/White ancestry, 40.7% had Asian ancestry, and 1.7% had African ancestry. Most HSP‐ATL1 patients were of European/White ethnicity (57.7%), followed by Asians (25.9%) and Africans (6.3%). ATL1 carriers originated from 33 countries; most patients were from China (14.6%), Italy (7.0%), or France (7.0%). HSP‐SPAST affected individuals were most commonly of European/White ethnicity (48.4%), followed by Asians (43.3%). SPAST carriers originated from 36 countries; most reported countries of origin were China (17.4%), Italy (15.5%), and South Korea (6.8%). In contrast, most of the patients with REEP1 variants were of Asian ethnicity (70.6%), followed by European/Whites (29.4%). Notably, for REEP1, there was a large proportion of affected individuals with missing data, with ethnicity unreported in 100 affected individuals (66.2% of variant carriers), and the most common countries of origin were the United States (19.2%), the Netherlands (16.6%), and France (14.6%).

Characteristics of Disease‐Onset

Both HSP‐ATL1 and HSP‐REEP1 patients had a significantly earlier age at onset (median 3 years old and 10 years old respectively), compared to HSP‐SPAST (median 30 years old, Table 1). Whilst the onset of HSP‐ATL1 was most frequently below 9 years of age (n = 206, consistent with HSP‐ATL1 as a frequent cause of HSP in this age group), SPAST variants also accounted for a comparable number of reported affected individuals with AAO <9 years (n = 226). Our data also suggested a bimodal pattern of age at onset for HSP‐SPAST with peaks at 0–9 years and 30–39 years (Fig. 2).

Distribution of age at onset; (A) HSP‐ATL1, (B) HSP‐SPAST, and (C) HSP‐REEP1.

The most common presenting sign at onset was lower limb spasticity for HSP‐ATL1 (n = 233/356, 65.4% of available data, 28.9% missing data), HSP‐SPAST (n = 884/1670, 52.9%, 44.3% missing), and HSP‐REEP1 (n = 130/151, 86.1% of available data, 13.9% missing).

Clinical Features

A summary of the clinical findings can be found in Table S3 (see “Yes,” “No” and “Missing” columns). This highlights phenotypic differences between the common autosomal dominant forms of HSP.

Most patients in each of the three common dominant forms had a pure HSP phenotype although this was less frequently reported for HSP‐SPAST (HSP‐ATL1 78.7%, HSP‐SPAST 56.8%, and HSP‐REEP1 80.1%). When reported, toe‐walking (tip‐toe gait) was more frequently described for HSP‐ATL1 (10.4%, n = 37/356) and HSP‐REEP1 (3.3%, n = 5/151) compared to 0.3% (n = 5/1670) in HSP‐SPAST. There was also a higher prevalence of impaired proprioception in HSP‐ATL1 [HSP‐ATL1: 3.7% (n = 13/356) compared to HSP‐SPAST: 0.6% (n = 10/1670), and HSP‐REEP1: 0.7% (n = 1/151)]. HSP‐SPAST had a higher frequency of upper limb hyperreflexia (14.7%, n = 245/1670), bladder abnormalities (16.6%, n = 277/1670), and use of a wheelchair (5.9%, n = 99/1670, versus 3.7%, n = 13/356 for HSP‐ATL1 and 2.0%, n = 3/151 for HSP‐REEP1). However, there was a lower frequency of lower limb signs including amyotrophy, spasticity and weakness for HSP‐SPAST. Patients with HSP‐REEP1 more commonly had upper limb weakness (12.6%, n = 19/151), abnormalities of the skeletal system (such as scoliosis; 6.6%, n = 10/151) and higher frequency of lower limb signs (spasticity, weakness, hyperreflexia). Radiologically, in HSP‐ATL1, more patients were reported to have had a hypoplastic corpus callosum (3.9%, n = 14/356) compared to HSP‐SPAST (0.3%, n = 5/1670) and HSP‐REEP1 (0.7%, n = 1/151).

A major caveat is that all comparisons were compromised by missing data, with the percentage of missing data higher for HSP‐SPAST for all clinical parameter compared to HSP‐ATL1 and HSP‐REEP1.

Disease Severity and Longitudinal Data

HSP‐ATL1 patients had higher SPRS scores (18.3 ± 10.8, n = 56) compared to HSP‐SPAST and HSP‐REEP1 (16.5 ± 12.7, n = 161 and 7.8 ± 4.1, n = 5 respectively; P = 0.013; Table 1), however, this may be influenced by differences in disease durations for the different genetic forms eg, 18 (7.5–37.0 IQR) years disease duration for HSP‐ATL1 and 21 (12.0–36.0) years for HSP‐REEP1 compared to HSP‐SPAST [13 years (6.3–23.0)].

We also correlated the age at exam and disease duration with SPRS scores to evaluate the rate of disease progression. For patients with HSP‐SPAST, as age at exam increased, SPRS increased – consistent with the progressive nature of HSP‐SPAST (Fig. 3A). This fits a sigmoid curve with a sharp rise during approximately ages 50 to 80 years. For HSP‐ATL1 and HSP‐REEP1, SPRS data was insufficient to quantify longitudinal changes. Correlation of SPRS scores with disease duration produced a negative parabolic model; we assessed disease duration as an unreliable measure given it is typically reliant on the patient recognizing their symptoms to determine disease onset.

In terms of functional disability, individuals with HSP‐SPAST were more likely to require a wheelchair compared to HSP‐REEP1 and HSP‐ATL1 (HSP‐SPAST: 99/1670, 5.9%; HSP‐ATL1: 13/356, 3.7%; HSP‐REEP1: 3/151, 2.0%, P < 0.001, Table S3), although this is not controlled for age at onset, age at examination, or disease duration. HSP‐SPAST patients requiring a wheelchair had an older median age at examination when compared to HSP‐ATL1 (47.5 versus 17, Fig. 3B, Table S4A). Furthermore, HSP‐SPAST patients requiring a wheelchair had a longer median disease duration when compared to HSP‐ATL1 (21.0 versus 16.0 years, Fig. 3C, Table S4B).

Spectrum of Genetic Variants

There were marked differences in the distribution of variant types across the three genes (Fig. 4). Missense variants predominantly occurred in the HSP‐ATL1 group, whereas truncating variants were most frequent in HSP‐SPAST and HSP‐REEP1. A considerable percentage (approximately 10%) of the SPAST variants were copy number variants that may be missed by sequencing approaches.

Frequency of missense and truncating variants in affected patients, (A) HSP‐ATL1, (B) HSP‐SPAST, (C) HSP‐REEP1.

ATL1 Variants

A total of 83 disease causing variants were reported in the ATL1 gene (Fig. S1A). Amongst these variants, missense variants were the most common variant type (85.5%, n = 71). The most common pathogenic variant was c.715C>T (p.Arg239Cys), observed in 24.7% (n = 43 of 174 index affected individuals). Of the 83 variants, 11 (13.3%) were classified as definitely pathogenic, 59 (71.1%) were probably pathogenic, 13 (15.7%) were possibly pathogenic, using the MDSGene pathogenicity scoring classification.

SPAST Variants

A total of 571 variants were reported in the SPAST gene (Fig. S1B). Amongst these variants, missense variants were the most common variant type (34.9%, n = 199), followed by frameshift variants (23.3%, n = 133), splice site variants (13.7%, n = 78), and exon deletions/duplications (8.6%, n = 49). The most common variant for index patients was c.1684C>T (p.Arg562*) (2.8%, n = 32/1146). Of the 571 variants, 25 (4.4%) were classified as definitely pathogenic, 436 (76.4%) were probably pathogenic and 110 (19.3%) were possibly pathogenic.

REEP1 Variants

A total of 53 variants were reported in the REEP1 gene (Fig. S1C). Amongst these variants, missense variants were also the most common variant type (30.2%, n = 16), followed by frameshift variants (20.8%, n = 11), and splice site variants (13.2%, n = 7). The most common variants were c.337C>T (p.Arg113*) and c.417 + 1G>T (6.2%, n = 4 of 65 index patients). Of the 53 variants, 8 (15.1%) were classified as definitely pathogenic, 37 (69.8%) were probably pathogenic and 8 (15.1%) were possibly pathogenic.

Summary of Genetic Variants

In summary, missense variants predominated in HSP‐ATL1 (88.5%), whereas they only accounted for 35.7% of affected individuals in HSP‐SPAST and 19.2% in HSP‐REEP1. Truncating variants/loss‐of‐function variants as a group overall (including nonsense, frameshift, splice site variants and exon deletions) were found in most affected individuals with HSP‐SPAST (54.2%) and HSP‐REEP1 (68.2%). Included in the “Other” variants category were start‐loss, silent, and other structural variants (eg, duplications).

Genotype–Phenotype Correlation

We investigated differences in signs and symptoms between missense and truncating variants – that is, nonsense, splice site, frameshift variants and exon deletions (Fig. S2A–C, Table S5). For all genes (HSP‐ATL1, HSP‐SPAST and HSP‐REEP1), missense variants had a significantly lower age at onset and a lower age at exam. For HSP‐ATL1, age at onset (median) for missense variants was 3.0 years, for truncating variants it was 10.0 years. For HSP‐SPAST, age at onset for missense variants was 27.0 years, for truncating it was 34.0 years. For HSP‐REEP1, age at onset for missense it was 5.0 years, for truncating it was 9.5 years. Regarding age at exam, for HSP‐ATL1, median age was 32.0 years for missense variants, and 44.5 years for truncating variants; for HSP‐SPAST, median age was 39.0 years for missense variants, and 50.0 years for truncating variants; for HSP‐REEP1, median age at exam was 32.0 years for missense variants, and 40.5 years for truncating ones.

For SPRS scores, sufficient data was available for HSP‐SPAST for a comparison. Here, SPRS scores were higher for truncating variants (median of 14.0) compared to 13.0 for missense variants (Fig. S2C).

Data on treatments

Limited data were available for treatments and responses. One patient with HSP‐ATL1 and five patients with HSP‐SPAST were treated with oral antispasmodics, none were treated with intrathecal pumps, and two affected individuals with HSP‐SPAST were treated with botulinum toxin injections. No treatment data were reported for HSP‐REEP1.

Missingness of Data

For ATL1, data missingness ranged from 8.7% to 85.1%, for variables associated with the core phenotype (ie, “pure” form of HSP), and data missingness ranged from 12.1% to 96.3% for complicated symptoms. For SPAST, data missingness ranged from 31.6% to 98.9% for pure symptoms/signs and from 33.2% to 97.7% for complicated symptoms. Finally, in HSP‐REEP1, data missingness ranged from 6.0% to 95.4% and from 26.5% to 92.1% for pure and complicated symptoms/signs, respectively.

Conclusion

We performed a comprehensive review of all published affected individuals of HSP due to variants in SPAST, ATL1, and REEP1 using the MDSGene protocol with a standardised method for classifying variants, allowing us to generate novel insights into genotype–phenotype correlations. Our study includes 2177 affected individuals with 709 distinct disease‐causing variants. While other systematic reviews have been performed for HSP, 7 , 8 our review is more up to date by including recently published individuals and uses a highly structured approach with a standardized method for classifying detected genetic variants, allowing detailed genotype–phenotype correlations with data collected in a systematic and comprehensive manner. Furthermore, our data has been uploaded and is accessible online through the MDSGene database (https://www.mdsgene.org): a user‐friendly platform allowing for the efficient interrogation and mining of the data. 15 This allowed us to confirm previously reported findings and provide novel insights, as described below.

We demonstrate a bimodal pattern of age at onset for SPAST which has previously been identified for carriers of pathogenic SPAST variants and was attributed to an earlier age at onset in carriers of missense variants. 16 Consistent with this, we did identify that missense variants contributed to the first peak (Fig. S3). An age at onset consistent with a bimodal pattern has also been reported in HSP‐REEP1 in the literature, 17 but has yet to be definitively replicated in our study (a minor peak at 30–39 years was observed). Notably, our systematic review is based on a considerably larger number of patients than the previous publication 17 (151 vs. 16 patients). Also, it is important to note that we did not include the data from this paper 17 due to a lack of individual data. A possible reason for conflicting findings is that exact age at onset can be difficult to accurately determine due to recall bias and the frequently insidious nature of symptom onset in HSP.

A previous report suggested an excess of males over females with HSP due to pathogenic variants in SPAST. 18 Furthermore, a previous study suggested higher penetrance in males and that females and males had subtle phenotypic differences (eg, females showed greater upper limb reflexes). 16 Including a larger number of individuals with SPAST disease‐causing variants than previously published 16 (1670 versus 842), we were unable to replicate these sex‐related findings; however we did show a non‐significant excess of affected males over females. It is again worth noting that we could not include the data published in the previous study observing sex differences 16 in our systematic review because individual level data was not provided.

Most patients for all three genes had a positive family history (>85% in all three genes). However, over 10% of HSP‐ATL1 and HSP‐SPAST patients had no known family history, reinforcing that these disorders should also be considered in sporadic affected individuals. Sporadic occurrences may be due to de novo variants [our study suggested that these account for 6.8% affected individuals (n = 22) of HSP‐ATL1 and 3.0% of affected individuals (n = 41) of HSP‐SPAST, according to testing of parents in the original report etc.], as well as other factors such as reduced penetrance and non‐paternity.

Amongst the affected individuals with ATL1 and SPAST variants included in our review, the majority were of European/White ethnicity (approximately two‐thirds). In contrast, amongst the smaller group with REEP1 variants, most individuals were of Asian ethnicity. The predominance of European/White reported affected individuals likely in part reflects increased access to genetic testing and scientific publications for this ethnic group and emphasizes the need for a more globally representative picture of HSP.

Our data reinforced known findings, including an earlier onset in carriers with disease‐causing ATL1 variants, 19 followed by REEP1 and then SPAST. Most patients with common dominant forms of HSP have pure phenotypes, and therefore, complicated symptoms and signs or specific clinical features may not be the best discriminating features. However, we did find subtle distinguishing phenotypic features, or a “phenotypic signature,” which to our knowledge have not been clearly delineated in the literature to date (Table S3). For example, in HSP‐ATL1, there is a higher prevalence of toe‐walking. However, it should be noted that toe‐walking is likely to be underreported in clinical practice. Moreover, many clinical features of HSP are strongly age dependent. Differences in reported phenotypes across genotypes may therefore reflect differences in age at evaluation or disease stage, rather than true biological divergence. This limits the ability to distinguish subtle phenotype distinctions. Additionally, several statistically significant findings are based on small absolute numbers or highly imbalanced denominators. This could lead to the overinterpretation of modest effect sizes with limited clinical discriminability. This should be considered before applying the results of this study to routine clinical decision‐making. Furthermore, it should be said that missing data (included as a separate column in Table S3) is a major limiting factor in all these comparisons, and that data missingness was more pronounced for the most common autosomal dominant form (HSP‐SPAST). The extent of missing phenotypic data, especially for SPAST, is substantial and non‐random. The absence of a reported feature in the literature should not be interpreted as evidence of its true absence. This is particularly relevant for features such as toe walking, upper limb involvement, bladder dysfunction, sensory abnormalities, and cognitive features, which are often underreported or inconsistently assessed. This limitation is a major source of bias that weakens cross‐genotype phenotypic comparisons.

In relation to variant types, missense changes were the most frequent variant type in all three genes, with 88.5% of variants in ATL1, 35.7% in SPAST, and 19.2% in REEP1. However, when taking all different types of variants that lead to a predicted premature protein truncating (nonsense, splice site, frameshift variants and exon deletions) together, the group of truncating variants was the most prominent one in HSP‐SPAST, and HSP‐REEP1.

The affected genes encode proteins (atlastin‐1, spastin, REEP1) that bind one another and play a critical role in maintaining endoplasmic reticulum shape via interactions with the microtubules. 20 , 21 The preponderance of missense variants in HSP‐ATL1 could be consistent with a dominant negative mechanism of action rather than haploinsufficiency, as previously discussed. 4 , 22 There is an ongoing debate on the underlying disease mechanism in HSP‐SPAST; loss of function or haploinsufficiency is thought most plausible, although a dominant‐negative mechanism may be associated with missense variants. 16 Additionally, we demonstrated that for HSP‐SPAST, individuals with truncating variants had a more severe phenotype as measured by the SPRS compared to those with missense variants. Conversely, our data suggest that missense variants result in an earlier age at onset and age at exam for all three common forms of autosomal dominant HSP. These results provide critical prognostic information when counseling patients in the clinic. In addition, the high frequency of SPAST and REEP1 exonic deletions, previously described, 23 highlights the need to assess for copy number variants in these disorders, which may require an additional test such as multiplex‐ligation dependent probe amplification (MLPA) for accurate detection.

To our knowledge, this is the only systematic review to assess longitudinal deterioration in clinical phenotype over time in HSP‐SPAST by analyzing SPRS against age at examination. We show a deterioration over an 82‐year timeframe (lowest and highest age at exam 8 and 90 years, respectively). Notably, analysis of longitudinal progression was not possible for HSP‐ATL1 and HSP‐REEP1 due to limited SPRS data. While individuals with HSP‐SPAST are more likely to be reported as wheelchair‐users, this occurs at a later age and longer disease duration than in HSP‐ATL1. The longitudinal data is helpful for providing prognostic information to patients. Additionally, it is helpful for clinical trial planning, which will need to be conducted over several years to demonstrate a meaningful improvement or reduced rate of clinical deterioration for this relatively slowly progressive neurodegenerative disorder. Furthermore, it highlights the need for a more accurate and sensitive biomarker to monitor disease progression in HSP. We acknowledge that actual longitudinal progression evaluation would require data from actual longitudinal studies and not simply correlate SPRS with age at exam or duration of disease. Here, we used age‐at‐exam or reported disease duration as proxies for disease progression. It should be noted that correlating SPRS scores with age at examination represents cross‐sectional modeling rather than true longitudinal progression.

As in earlier MDSGene systematic reviews, the degree of missingness of phenotypic data remains a major challenge. Furthermore, data on treatment response was virtually non‐existent and this needs to be rectified in future reports. Additionally, only one database (PubMed), was used for the literature search, potentially overlooking relevant articles. As this study is constrained by the nature of published case reports and series feeding into MDSGene, it cannot define true prevalence, penetrance, or natural history. Its primary strength lies in systematic aggregation and hypothesis generation, rather than definitive clinical stratification.

Despite these limitations, this comprehensive analysis of all individual‐level published data provides novel insights with implications for clinical diagnosis, with subtle clinical phenotypic signatures delineated for the common forms of autosomal dominant HSP requiring further confirmation. Key information is provided to estimate prognosis according to gene and variant type. Furthermore, we highlight the need for more uniform collection of data for these rare disorders to confirm the clinical differences we have identified and reveal others that are as yet unknown.
