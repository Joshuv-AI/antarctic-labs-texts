Bone targeted microenvironment actuated engineered exosomes for precision therapy of IBD associated bone loss
Authors: Jiang Liu, Cong Wang, Taojin Feng, Siyu Chen, Siliang Ge, Yue Zhao, Ming Chen, Zhongqi Wang, Weier Bao, Pincong Fu, Zhichao Wang, Simiao Kang, Pengbin Yin, Ying Tian, Yi Li, Haile Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic bone loss is a frequent extraintestinal complication of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and can continue even when intestinal inflammation is clinically controlled. The steps linking gut inflammation to bone deterioration at distant sites are not fully defined. We found that interleukin-18 (IL-18) links intestinal inflammation to bone loss. In the gut, IL-18 supports mucosal repair and barrier maintenance. When circulating IL-18 remains elevated, it accumulates in bone-associated niches, shifts bone remodeling, and ultimately leads to bone loss. While IL-18 is a protective factor essential for intestinal repair and barrier homeostasis, its sustained systemic elevation leads to pathological accumulation in bone-associated niches, driving aberrant bone remodeling and bone loss. To address this challenge, we developed ExoBIP, a bone-targeted and microenvironment-actuated engineered exosome system. This platform enables site-specific IL-18 neutralization within inflamed bone tissues while preserving the physiological function of IL-18 in the gut. ExoBIP integrates the intrinsic pro-osteogenic properties of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell derived exosomes with spatiotemporally controlled cytokine blockade, thereby simultaneously suppressing inflammation-driven bone resorption and supporting bone regeneration. In a chronic IBD-associated bone loss model, ExoBIP effectively restores bone mass, improves trabecular microarchitecture, and single-cell RNA sequencing showed that ExoBIP restores the marrow niche by increasing osteogenic cells and reducing inflammatory, cytotoxic CD8+ T cell programs. Collectively, this work establishes IL-18 as an actionable driver of IBD-induced bone loss and introduces a precision nanotherapeutic strategy for treating inflammation-induced skeletal complications, highlighting a generalizable paradigm for distal organ protection in chronic inflammatory diseases.Keywords: Inflammatory bowel disease, Inflammatory bone loss, Interleukin-18 (IL-18), Engineered exosomes, Exosome-based drug delivery

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is now recognized as a systemic immune-mediated disorder with effects that extend beyond the gastrointestinal tract [[1], [2], [3]]. Among its extraintestinal manifestations, bone loss is one of the most common yet often overlooked complications. Epidemiological studies show that 30-50% of patients with IBD have reduced bone mass [4,5], and up to 38% progress to osteoporosis with an increased risk of fragility fractures [[6], [7], [8]]. This skeletal complication can lead to long-term disability and increased healthcare burden. Despite this clinical relevance, current IBD management focuses primarily on controlling intestinal inflammation and achieving mucosal healing, whereas bone health is frequently underassessed [9]. As a result, skeletal complications are commonly treated using strategies developed for primary osteoporosis, without consideration of the immune-driven mechanisms specific to IBD[10]. However, most existing studies have focused on suppressing intestinal inflammation or promoting bone formation, whereas the persistence of bone loss during remission has received much less attention. The molecular drivers underlying this sustained skeletal deterioration therefore remain poorly defined. This gap motivated our study, in which we sought not only to develop a targeted therapeutic strategy but also to identify a key gut-derived mediator that may sustain bone loss after intestinal inflammation is controlled.

Current management of IBD-associated bone loss still relies largely on conventional osteoporosis-directed strategies, including calcium and vitamin D supplementation, anti-resorptive agents, and, in selected contexts, denosumab. However, calcium plus vitamin D alone shows limited efficacy in improving BMD and may be associated with adverse effects [10,11]. Meanwhile, biomaterial-based and regenerative approaches are emerging in IBD treatment. Zhang Z et al. constructed olsalazine into a nanofibrous self delivery system and combined it with an inulin hydrogel capable of modulating the gut microecology. Through the strategy of “drug structuralization and local gel retention,” they achieved colon-targeted anti-inflammatory and reparative therapy, thereby promoting barrier repair, alleviating inflammation, and improving microbial homeostasis [12]. Lei et al. developed an oral co-delivery system based on a pH responsive sodium alginate hydrogel-coated nanoemulsion (NE@SA), which enabled the synergistic delivery and controlled release of curcumin and emodin to the colon, thereby targeting the inflammatory microenvironment and promoting mucosal repair to ameliorate the colitis phenotype [13]. And bone directed strategies, including Wnt agonist-loaded bone-targeted exosomes [14] and IL-6 inhibition combined with gene therapy [15], have shown encouraging effects on bone formation in experimental models.

Traditionally, IBD related bone loss has been attributed mainly to acute intestinal inflammation such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 that spills over into the systemic circulation [16]. This process involves a surge of pro-inflammatory cytokines, gut microbiota dysbiosis [17,18], and prolonged steroid exposure [19]. However, recent clinical data indicate that bone loss frequently persists or even progresses after patients achieve intestinal remission [7,20]. At the same time, classical inflammatory cytokines tend to normalize once gut inflammation is controlled [21], yet skeletal deterioration can continue [22,23]. This suggests that the post-treatment mucosal repair phase is not a period of systemic stability. Instead, delayed intestinal barrier recovery may allow specific gut-derived factors to continuously enter the circulation, and these lingering signals may disrupt crosstalk between the gut and the skeletal system.

Accordingly, beyond simply suppressing intestinal inflammation or enhancing bone formation, it is essential to identify the persistent molecular mediators that sustain bone loss during remission; however, the specific links remain largely unknown. This gap motivated our study to identify these signals, we performed an integrated analysis using Mendelian randomization, single-cell RNA sequencing, and animal models, providing an evidence chain across the serum, gut, and bone compartments. Taken together, our results suggest that interleukin-18 (IL-18) may be a relatively key mediator linking IBD to bone loss. In the gut, IL-18 supports epithelial restitution and helps maintain mucosal barrier integrity during the post-treatment repair phase [[24], [25], [26]]; accordingly, it can remain elevated even after intestinal inflammation is controlled and mucosal repair is initiated. Therefore, we speculate that this persistent IL-18 could contribute to ongoing bone loss after intestinal inflammation is controlled. Mechanistically, IL-18 activates T cells to trigger a pathological signaling cascade [27,28]. This immune response inhibits osteogenic differentiation and promotes bone resorption [29]. These findings present a clear therapeutic dilemma. Systemic inhibition of IL-18 might reduce skeletal damage but could also impair intestinal healing. Conversely, ignoring this persistent signal may allow bone loss to progress.

Therefore, a delivery strategy that is both organ-selective and inflammation-responsive is needed. Such a platform should decouple the protective functions of IL-18 in the gut from its pathological actions within the skeletal niche. Ideally, it would enable localized immunomodulation by releasing the therapeutic payload only in inflamed microenvironments, while remaining inert in noninflamed tissues. As inflammation resolves, drug release would cease, thereby limiting unnecessary exposure at sites where inflammation has been cleared and minimizing systemic side effects.

To address these challenges, we developed an engineered exosome platform termed ExoBIP (Fig. 1). This system utilizes bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) exosomes as a bioactive carrier to leverage their intrinsic osteogenic potential. To enable precise bone targeting and inflammation-triggered release, IL-18 binding protein (IL-18BP) was first coupled to the SDSSD bone-homing peptide, and this IL-18BP-SDSSD conjugate was then grafted onto the exosome surface through an MMP9-responsive linker. This design ensures that IL-18BP is released selectively within the inflammatory skeletal microenvironment. Such a target and trigger logic enable localized neutralization of pathological IL-18 while preserving its physiological function in the gut. In a chronic IBD model, ExoBIP effectively restored bone mass and improved the dysregulated marrow immune-osteogenic microenvironment. Collectively, this work introduces a precision nanomedicine strategy for managing inflammation-driven systemic complications.

Schematic illustration of the design, bone targeting, and therapeutic mechanism of ExoBIP in IBD-associated bone loss.

BMSC-derived exosomes were engineered to generate ExoBIP for bone-targeted, inflammation-triggered IL-18 neutralization: IL-18BP was first coupled to the SDSSD bone-homing peptide, and the resulting IL-18BP-SDSSD conjugate was then grafted onto the exosome surface via an MMP9-responsive cleavable linker, enabling selective IL-18BP release within the inflammatory skeletal microenvironment while preserving IL-18's gut physiology. Neutralization of IL-18 suppresses T cell activation and IFN-γ–mediated inhibition of osteogenic activity, reduces RANKL-mediated osteoclastogenesis, and restores the balance between osteoclast and osteoblast activity, thereby alleviating IBD-induced bone loss.

Result

After intestinal inflammation improved, DSS-induced bone loss was not reversed

To test whether controlling intestinal inflammation and promoting mucosal repair is sufficient to reverse IBD-associated bone loss, we compared a healthy control group (Healthy), a DSS-induced colitis group (UC), and a treatment group (UC + Treatment). UC mice were 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice that received two cycles of 2% DSS for 7 days followed by regular water for 7 days, administered via drinking water, a commonly used regimen to model chronic colitis and its systemic effects [30]. UC mice received PBS by oral gavage once daily for 2 weeks after the first DSS cycle to match the treatment procedure, while UC + Treatment mice received sulfasalazine (SASP, 100 mg/kg) by oral gavage once daily for 2 weeks from D14 to D28. The main reason for selecting SASP as the intestinal therapeutic agent is that SASP is one of the most classical and commonly used anti-inflammatory drugs in clinical IBD treatment, particularly for UC [31]. Its use allowed us to model a clinically relevant scenario in which intestinal inflammation is controlled, whereas extraintestinal complications still require management [32]. This design enabled us to specifically investigate the downstream effects of persistently elevated IL-18 during the recovery phase on the bone marrow niche. Considering that the UC group had already developed a stable disease phenotype after the second DSS cycle and would not receive any further intervention, mice in this group were euthanized and analyzed on day 28 in accordance with animal welfare and ethical considerations. UC + Treatment mice were euthanized and tissues were collected at the end of treatment (day 28) and at an extended endpoint (day 56). Healthy controls were harvested on day 56 to provide a time-matched reference for the longitudinal gut-repair and bone-phenotype readouts (Fig. 2A).

In terms of intestinal phenotypes and systemic inflammatory burden, UC mice showed typical colitis features compared with Healthy controls, including body weight loss, colon shortening, and marked histological damage with inflammatory cell infiltration. In the UC + Treatment group, these changes were clearly improved after SASP administration and continued to recover from day 28 to day 56: body weight increased, colon length was restored, colonic architecture became more intact, and histology scores decreased (Fig. 2B–D). Systemic inflammation also declined over time: UC mice developed pronounced splenomegaly, whereas spleen weight was significantly reduced in UC + Treatment mice, and serum MMP9 levels (ELISA), which were strongly elevated in UC mice, gradually decreased and approached Healthy levels by day 56 (Fig. 2E–F). Consistent with these improvements, inflammatory cytokines in the colon were reduced after treatment. Compared with the UC group, UC + Treatment mice showed significantly lower protein levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (ELISA), and these remained low after day 28. In contrast, IL-10 and ZO-1 were increased, suggesting a shift toward an anti-inflammatory, repair-associated mucosal state after treatment (Supporting Fig. 1A). In addition, we examined other commonly affected extraintestinal organs, including the liver, spleen, and eyes, and found that inflammatory cytokine levels in these tissues also declined toward baseline (Supporting Fig. 1B–D). Together, these time-course data indicate that intestinal inflammation transitioned toward tissue repair and was accompanied by a reduction in systemic inflammatory burden. In contrast to the intestinal recovery, bone loss did not improve. Micro-CT analysis showed substantial trabecular bone loss in UC mice compared with Healthy controls. Notably, in UC + Treatment mice, bone mass and trabecular microarchitecture did not recover even with extended follow-up to day 56 (Fig. 2G–H). Immunofluorescence staining showed that osteogenic markers (OPN and OSX) were significantly lower in both UC and UC + Treatment mice (day 28 and day 56) than in Healthy controls, indicating sustained suppression of osteogenic activity (Supporting Fig. 1E). tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining revealed increased numbers of TRAP-positive osteoclasts in UC and UC + Treatment mice, suggesting persistent elevation of osteoclastic activity after treatment (Supporting Fig. 1F). Dynamic double labeling further showed that bone formation indices did not rebound over time in UC + Treatment mice and that mineral apposition remained low (Supporting Fig. 1G). Overall, these results agree with the micro-CT findings and indicate that the skeletal phenotype reflects ongoing remodeling imbalance rather than a simple delay in recovery. In addition, MMP9 immunofluorescence in bone showed enhanced MMP9 signals in the marrow cavity of both UC and UC + Treatment mice (Fig. 2I), suggesting that bone remains under long-lasting inflammatory pressure even after intestinal inflammation is controlled. Prior work has suggested that IBD-associated bone loss is often attributed to gut-derived classical pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α during acute inflammatory phase [33,34]. However, we found that, compared with the UC group, these cytokines in bone tissue were already significantly reduced by day 28 in UC + Treatment mice and remained at low levels thereafter (Fig. 2J).

Therefore, we observed a key mismatch: despite clear improvement of intestinal inflammation, enhanced mucosal barrier repair, reduced systemic inflammatory burden, and normalization of classical pro-inflammatory cytokines in both colon and bone, bone mass and remodeling indices continued to worsen and were accompanied by persistently elevated MMP9 in bone marrow. These findings suggest that the ongoing bone loss after treatment may be driven by other signals that persist during intestinal repair and act on the bone marrow niche, thereby promoting an imbalance in bone remodeling. At the same time, we recognize that SASP may introduce potential confounding factors, such as reduced systemic pro-osteoclastogenic signaling secondary to inflammation control, improvement in body weight, food intake, and nutritional status, altered mineral and vitamin absorption following restoration of the intestinal barrier, as well as possible metabolic or microbiota-related effects of the drug itself. Therefore, throughout the study, all UC + Treatment comparison groups were maintained under the same SASP background.

Multi-omics analyses identify gut-derived IL-18 as a candidate mediator of bone loss in IBD

To explore molecular factors linking intestinal inflammation to bone loss, we applied a multi-omics strategy that combined genetic analyses, single-cell transcriptomics, and bulk RNA sequencing (Fig. 2K). We first used LD score regression (LDSC) to estimate SNP-based heritability and pairwise genetic correlations among osteoporosis (OP), IBD, ulcerative colitis, and Crohn's disease (CD) (Supporting Tables 1–6). IBD was included to represent the shared genetic basis of inflammatory bowel disease as a whole, whereas UC and CD were analyzed separately to capture subtype-specific genetic heterogeneity. OP showed a significant positive genetic correlation with IBD (rg = 0.113) and with UC (rg = 0.210), suggesting shared genetic architecture (Supporting Fig. 2A). We then performed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) to test whether genetically predicted IBD risk is associated with OP susceptibility. Using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, we found that genetic liability to IBD was associated with higher OP risk (OR = 1.11, p = 0.0014). A similar association was observed for CD (OR = 1.07, p = 0.043) (Supporting Fig. 2B). Within the MR framework, we further prioritized candidate circulating cytokines (Supporting Fig. 2C). Notably, IL-18 and its receptor IL-18R1 consistently ranked among the top signals across IBD, CD, UC, and OP (Supporting Fig. 2D), and showed the most concordant pattern among the candidates (Fig. 2L). To examine their expression in the human gut, we analyzed a public single-cell RNA-seq dataset from colonic biopsies of IBD patients (GSE214695) [35] (Supporting Fig. 2E). Il18 transcripts were significantly increased in inflamed colonic tissue (p = 0.006) and were detected across multiple cell types, including mast cells, M1-like macrophages, and secretory epithelial progenitors (Fig. 2M). In contrast, Il18r1 did not show an obvious difference between inflamed IBD samples and controls (Fig. 2N). We next performed bulk RNA-seq on femoral tissue from IBD mice. The bone transcriptome showed broad changes, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) indicated downregulation of osteogenesis-related programs, including extracellular matrix organization, BMP signaling, biomineral tissue development, and bone mineralization (Supporting Fig. 2F). Importantly, Il18 mRNA in bone did not increase and instead showed a trend toward lower expression (p = 0.053), while Il18r1 expression was unchanged (p = 0.81) (Supporting Fig. 2G).

It is not unexpected that our multi-omics analyses revealed elevated IL-18 expression in the intestine during IBD. IL-18 belongs to the IL-1 family [36], in healthy individuals its intracellular precursor (pro-IL-18) can be maintained as a preformed reservoir in multiple barrier cell types, with broad distribution across the gastrointestinal epithelium [24]. During IBD, the barrier- and repair-related functions of IL-18 become particularly prominent: IL-18 has been proposed to act as an epithelial effector downstream of the IL-22-STAT3 axis and to participate in a feedback loop that supports epithelial defense and repair [26]. More recently, immunological studies further suggest that intestinal IL-18 may contribute not only to acute repair but also to a more durable state of tissue tolerance through metabolic reprogramming. For example, Il18 and other repair-associated factors remain upregulated during the recovery window after DSS-induced injury; exogenous IL-18 pretreatment attenuates subsequent high-dose DSS colitis; and enhanced endogenous IL-18 activity, such as in the absence of IL-18BP, confers more sustained protection [25].

Based on this, we hypothesized that IL-18, which remains persistently expressed during intestinal repair, may enter the circulation and accumulate in the bone marrow microenvironment, thereby contributing to IBD-associated bone loss. We therefore next evaluated IL-18 expression in the intestine and its levels in the gut, blood, and bone marrow during disease progression.

Systemic accumulation of IL-18 is associated with disrupted bone homeostasis in IBD-associated bone loss

To examine IL-18 in the gut and bone during IBD, we used the same experimental design as above (Fig. 3A). After model establishment, we first assessed IL-18 expression in the colon. Compared with healthy controls, Il18 mRNA was significantly increased in both the UC and UC + Treatment groups (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence further showed stronger IL-18 signals in the mucosal region in UC and UC + Treatment mice (Fig. 3C). Consistently, IL-18 protein levels in colonic homogenates were also elevated (Supporting Fig. 3A). Serum IL-18 followed the same pattern. It increased progressively during disease development and remained high at later time points (Fig. 3D). In bone, we also detected stronger IL-18 staining within the bone marrow (Fig. 3F), and higher IL-18 protein levels in bone homogenates (Supporting Fig. 3B). Notably, this increase was not accompanied by higher Il18 mRNA in bone tissue (Fig. 3E), which was consistent with our femoral bulk RNA-seq results. At D56, correlation analysis showed that bone IL-18 IF intensity was positively correlated with serum IL-18 concentration and bone marrow fluid IL-18 concentration (serum: r = 0.932, p = 0.0068; bone marrow fluid: r = 0.944, p = 0.0046), further supporting an association between bone IL-18 accumulation and circulating and local marrow IL-18 levels (Supporting Fig. 3C). These findings suggest that IL-18 in bone is more likely driven by systemic input rather than local transcriptional induction. We next tested whether neutralizing IL-18 could improve the bone phenotype in this model. IL-18 is naturally inhibited by IL-18BP [36,37], a high-affinity antagonist that blocks IL-18 from engaging the IL-18Rα/β receptor complex, thereby shifting IL-18 from a signaling-competent free form to a receptor-inactive complex [38,39]. After the second DSS/water cycle, mice received IL-18BP by tail-vein injection at 0.5 mg/kg daily, administered concurrently with SASP. the IL-18BP dose was selected with reference to previously reported effective dosing regimens in murine colitis models [40,41]. At day 56, Micro-CT analysis of the femur showed that, compared with the UC group, IL-18BP treatment significantly increased bone mineral density (BMD) and trabecular bone volume fraction (BV/TV), and also improved trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) and trabecular number (Tb.N). Although trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) was not significantly reduced, it showed a downward trend. (Fig. 3H–I and Supporting Fig. 3D). On the osteogenic side, immunofluorescence staining showed that OPN and OSX signals were significantly reduced in the UC group, whereas IL-18BP treatment significantly increased the OPN and OSX positive areas (Fig. 3J–K), indicating improved osteogenic activity. On the osteoclast side, TRAP staining was strongly increased in the UC group, and IL-18BP treatment significantly lowered the osteoclast number (N.Oc/B.Pm) (Fig. 3L–M), suggesting reduced bone resorption. Dynamic labeling further supported these findings: compared with the UC group, the IL-18BP group showed higher mineral apposition rate (MAR) and bone formation rate (BFR/BS), although the change in mineralizing surface (MS/BS) was limited (Fig. 3N–O). While these indices did not fully return to healthy levels, overall IL-18BP both reduced osteoclast activity and improved osteogenesis, shifting bone remodeling toward a more bone-forming state. These results support our idea that IL-18 may be a gut-derived signal that remains elevated and accumulates in the bone marrow, where it contributes to pathological changes. To understand how IL-18 drives this imbalance, we first tested whether IL-18 acts directly on bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) or on osteoclast precursors (Fig. 3G). Adding recombinant IL-18 directly to osteogenic cultures of BMSCs or to osteoclast differentiation cultures of monocytes did not significantly change Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP), Alizarin Red S (ARS), or TRAP readouts (Supporting Fig. 3E–G). This suggests that IL-18 does not directly suppress osteogenesis or promote osteoclastogenesis, and points to an indirect pathway mediated by immune cells.

Based on reports that IL-18 can enhance CD8+ T cell effector function and promote secretion of factors such as IFN-γ and RANKL [36,[42], [43], [44]], we established a T cell conditioned medium-bone cell differentiation in vitro system. CD8+ T cells were treated as indicated, and their supernatants were collected as CM and then applied to BMSC osteogenic cultures or to monocyte osteoclast cultures (Fig. 3P). In osteogenic assays, CM from IL-18-treated T cells (G2) significantly inhibited BMSC differentiation compared with control CM (G1), as shown by reduced ALP staining and fewer ARS mineralized nodules. Neutralizing IL-18 with IL-18BP (G3) restored both ALP and ARS signals, indicating that the osteogenic inhibition in G2 was mainly driven by IL-18-related signals (Fig. 3Q–S). In osteoclast assays, the same CM showed the opposite effect: G2 increased TRAP-positive area, while IL-18BP (G3) significantly reduced TRAP staining, indicating that IL-18 neutralization weakened the pro-osteoclast effect (Fig. 3R–S). Consistent with these phenotypes, osteogenic genes (Runx2 and Bglap) were downregulated and osteoclast-related genes (Nfatc1 and Ctsk) were upregulated under G2, and these changes were partially reversed by IL-18BP (Supporting Fig. 3H). We then examined the PI3K-Akt pathway in activated T cells [43]. Western blotting showed that IL-18 increased p-PI3K and p-Akt levels, and IL-18BP reduced this phosphorylation (Fig. 3T). In the IL-18-T cell CM system, adding the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (G4) clearly improved osteogenic differentiation and also weakened osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 3Q–S), suggesting that PI3K-Akt signaling is important for these effects. Consistent with these phenotypic changes, qPCR analysis further showed that LY294002 (G4) restored the expression of the osteogenic genes Runx2 and Bglap, and reduced the expression of the osteoclast-related genes Nfatc1 and Ctsk. Moreover, IFN-γ blockade with an IFN-γ antagonist (G6) or JAK1/2 inhibition with ruxolitinib (G7) similarly rescued Runx2 and Bglap expression, whereas RANKL neutralization with an anti-RANKL antibody (G9) or inhibition of NF-κB signaling with BAY 11-7821 (G10) reduced Nfatc1 and Ctsk expression, further supporting that IFN-γ and RANKL act as downstream effector branches of IL-18-activated T cells in regulating osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis, respectively (Supporting Fig. 3I).

Finally, we tested downstream effector factors. Adding IFN-γ alone (G5) or RANKL alone (G8) reproduced the inhibitory effect on osteogenesis seen in G2. In contrast, blocking IFN-γ with an IFN-γ antagonist (G6) or with the JAK1/2 inhibitor Ruxolitinib (G7) similarly rescued the suppression of osteogenic differentiation, indicating that IFN-γ-mediated JAK/STAT signaling is required for the anti-osteogenic effect (Fig. 3Q–S). Likewise, neutralizing RANKL with an anti-RANKL antibody (G9) or inhibiting NF-κB signaling with BAY 11-7821 (G10) comparably reduced osteoclast differentiation, supporting that RANKL-induced NF-κB activation mediates the pro-osteoclast effect (Fig. 3R–S). Together, these data support a model in which IL-18 activates CD8+ T cells through PI3K-Akt signaling, increases IFN-γ and RANKL output, and thereby shifts bone remodeling toward reduced osteogenesis and enhanced bone resorption. To explore a possible microbiota-related contribution to persistent IL-18 in IBD, we performed an exploratory 16S rRNA sequencing analysis in a small human cohort. Patients with IBD showed altered gut microbial composition and reduced abundance of representative butyrate-related genera, including Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, and Anaerostipes (Supporting Fig. 3J and K). A butyrate-producer index, calculated as the sum of the relative abundances of these genera, was positively correlated with serum IL-18 levels and BMD T-score within the patient cohort (Supporting Fig. 3L). Taken together, these exploratory data suggest that the relationship among gut microbiota, IL-18, and bone phenotypes is likely complex and context-dependent, and support the possibility that altered gut microbial ecology may contribute, at least in part, to the persistent IL-18 signal observed in IBD. Further investigation will be needed to define the mechanistic basis of this microbiota–IL-18 association and its relevance to IBD-associated bone loss.

Construction and characterization of an MMP-responsive, bone-targeted exosome system (ExoBIP)

To achieve selective neutralization of IL-18 in bone while providing a delivery vehicle that also supports bone repair, we engineered an exosome-based therapeutic system termed ExoBIP using bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC)-derived exosomes. Here, “Exo” refers to the exosome carrier, “B” denotes bone-targeting modification, and “IP” indicates that the therapeutic cargo is IL-18BP. BMSC exosomes have good biocompatibility and are readily engineered, and they have been widely used for delivering biomacromolecules such as proteins and nucleic acids. Based on this platform, we integrated bone-targeted enrichment and lesion microenvironment-responsive release into one system to increase effective exposure in bone while reducing systemic exposure. Because we observed sustained upregulation of MMP9 in the bone marrow, we selected an MMP9-cleavable linker (PLGVR) as a microenvironment switch to enable on-demand release of IL-18BP in the inflammatory bone niche. As shown in Fig. 4A, ExoBIP was prepared using thiol-maleimide chemistry. The bone-targeting peptide SDSSD was conjugated to IL-18BP to enhance bone accumulation. In parallel, IL-18BP was anchored to the exosome surface through the cleavable PLGVR linker. This design gives ExoBIP two key features: SDSSD improves delivery to bone tissue, and in an MMP9-enriched inflammatory bone microenvironment, the linker is selectively cleaved to trigger local IL-18BP release, enabling on-demand IL-18 neutralization within bone. Quantitative analysis further showed that the average conjugation density was approximately 5 IL-18BP molecules per exosome.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that ExoBIP exhibited the typical cup-shaped morphology of exosomes. The successful surface conjugation of IL-18BP on ExoBIP was further demonstrated by immunogold labeling with secondary antibody-linked gold nanoparticles (Fig. 4B). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and ζ-potential measurements further showed a right-shifted size distribution and a slightly reduced surface charge for ExoBIP, indicating successful surface functionalization and altered interfacial properties (Fig. 4C). Western blot analysis confirmed that ExoBIP retained canonical exosome markers (CD9, CD63, CD81, and TSG101) and also showed an IL-18BP signal, supporting successful assembly of IL-18BP onto the engineered exosomes (Fig. 4D). To confirm the spatial localization of IL-18BP, we performed super-resolution confocal imaging at the single-vesicle level. IL-18BP (red) showed strong co-localization with the Cy5-labeled exosome membrane (green) (Fig. 4E). Line-scan profiles showed highly overlapping fluorescence peaks (Fig. 4F), supporting a relatively uniform distribution of IL-18BP on the vesicle surface rather than random aggregation or nonspecific adsorption. We next evaluated the microenvironment-responsive release behavior of ExoBIP. In the presence of recombinant MMP9, IL-18BP was gradually released from the ExoBIP surface over time. This release was almost completely blocked by the broad-spectrum MMP inhibitor GM6001 (Fig. 4G), indicating that release depends on MMP-mediated cleavage of the linker and shows clear protease specificity. To rule out loss of activity caused by the engineering process, we tested the function of released IL-18BP. ELISA showed no significant difference in IL-18 binding/capture capacity between recombinant IL-18BP and IL-18BP released after MMP9 cleavage (Fig. 4H), suggesting that conjugation and cleavage did not compromise key functional sites of IL-18BP. For formulation stability, ExoBIP maintained stable particle size and ζ potential after storage in PBS at 4 °C for 7 days (Fig. 4I), indicating good short-term stability. ExoBIP could also be lyophilized into a powder and rapidly reconstituted in PBS, producing a clear suspension with a visible Tyndall effect (Fig. 4J). DLS analysis showed no obvious changes in size distribution or ζ potential before and after lyophilization/reconstitution (Fig. 4K), indicating preserved colloidal properties and physicochemical integrity. Finally, because IL-18BP binds IL-18 with high affinity, we tested whether ExoBIP might consume circulating free IL-18 soon after injection and thereby reduce IL-18 availability at bone sites. Serum IL-18 measured 1 h after ExoBIP injection in DSS-induced colitis mice showed no significant decrease compared with PBS controls (Supporting Fig. 4). This suggests that ExoBIP does not cause a detectable rapid systemic reduction of IL-18 during early circulation, consistent with the intended design of release mainly triggered by the lesion microenvironment.

Bone-targeting efficiency and in vivo biodistribution of ExoBIP

To test whether the engineered vesicles can target bone, we first performed an ex vivo femur-binding assay. Femurs isolated from IBD-induced osteoporotic mice were incubated with three Cy5-labeled vesicle preparations: unmodified BMSC exosomes (Exo), IL-18BP-conjugated exosomes (ExoIP), and bone-targeted IL-18BP-conjugated exosomes (ExoBIP). To further verify whether the enhanced bone association of ExoBIP was specifically mediated by the SDSSD targeting peptide, we additionally included a competition group in which free SDSSD peptide was co-incubated with ExoBIP. After 12 h, ExoBIP showed a much stronger fluorescence signal on the bone surface than Exo or ExoIP (Fig. 5A). Notably, the fluorescence signal was significantly reduced in the ExoBIP + SDSSD (competition) group compared with ExoBIP alone. Quantification confirmed that ExoBIP had significantly higher binding to femurs (Fig. 5B), whereas free SDSSD peptide significantly attenuated this enhanced binding, indicating that the bone-targeting peptide increased bone association. We then examined in vivo biodistribution. Cy5-labeled vesicles were injected intravenously into IBD-induced osteoporotic mice, and in vivo imaging was performed using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) for up to 96 h (Fig. 5C). In the Exo and ExoIP groups, the femur-region signal was weak and faded quickly within 24-48 h. In contrast, ExoBIP showed clear femur enrichment at 6 h, reached a peak at 24 h, and remained detectable beyond 72 h (Fig. 5D). Consistently, area-under-the-curve (AUC) analysis of the femur signal showed higher overall bone exposure in the ExoBIP group (Supporting Fig. 5). support the in vivo imaging results, major organs and representative skeletal tissues, including the femur and spine, were collected at 24 h for ex vivo fluorescence imaging. ExoBIP showed preferential accumulation in bone, with consistent enrichment observed in both the femur and spine, while relatively low signals were detected in non-target organs (Fig. 5E). On bone sections, Cy3 labeling showed stronger vesicle-associated red fluorescence in the ExoBIP group than in the Exo or ExoIP groups (Fig. 5F), and quantification confirmed higher vesicle distribution in bone tissue (Fig. 5G). In addition, immunofluorescence staining showed a stronger IL-18BP signal in bone after ExoBIP administration (Fig. 5H), and quantification supported this observation (Fig. 5I).

In the broader field, targeted and environment-responsive exosomes are becoming a main direction in EV therapy [45]. Targeted and environment-responsive exosomes are now a main direction in EV therapy. Reviews describe common targeting tools, including peptides, aptamers, antibodies, and small molecules, added by lipid insertion or chemical conjugation to improve tissue enrichment and reduce off-target exposure. For bone, reported strategies include alendronate coupling and bone-binding peptides to guide EVs to skeletal tissue and improve outcomes in osteoporosis or fracture models [46]. Therapeutic efficacy of ExoBIP in a mouse model of IBD-associated bone loss

To evaluate ExoBIP in IBD-associated bone loss, we used the same chronic DSS regimen described above to generate colitis-associated osteoporosis in 8-week-old male mice. Briefly, mice were exposed to two cycles of 2% DSS with intervening periods of regular water. From day 14, sulfasalazine (SASP, 100 mg/kg, once daily by gavage) was administered to facilitate intestinal recovery. Healthy mice served as baseline controls and received no injections. DSS-challenged mice were randomized into four groups (PBS, Exo, ExoIP, and ExoBIP). Starting on day 29, animals received tail-vein injections every 3 days for a total of 10 treatments, and bone outcomes were assessed at the study endpoint (Fig. 6A).

Micro-CT 3D reconstruction showed severe trabecular bone destruction in the UC group and the PBS-treated group, whereas ExoBIP treatment significantly improved the trabecular network and produced a structure closer to that of healthy controls (Fig. 6B). Compared with other groups, ExoBIP significantly increased BMD and BV/TV, and also improved trabecular microarchitecture, including Tb.N and Tb.Th (Fig. 6C). Notably, these improvements were more pronounced with ExoBIP than with Exo (vehicle only), free IL-18BP, IL-18BP + SDSSD peptide, or ExoIP (IL-18BP-conjugated but non-bone-targeted). At the histological level, we further examined how ExoBIP affected bone remodeling. Immunofluorescence staining of osteogenic markers (OPN and OSX) showed very low signals in the PBS, free IL-18BP and Exo groups, a modest increase in the IL-18BP + SDSSD and ExoIP groups, and a clear increase in the ExoBIP group, indicating improved osteogenic activity (Fig. 6D–H). Toluidine blue staining showed more osteoblasts in the ExoBIP group (Fig. 6E–H), while TRAP staining showed fewer osteoclasts (Fig. 6F–H), supporting a shift toward a more balanced osteoblast-osteoclast state. Dynamic double labeling further showed a larger calcein-alizarin distance in the ExoBIP group, consistent with a significant recovery of mineral apposition rate (MAR) (Fig. 6G–H). Taken together, these findings suggest that the superior therapeutic efficacy of ExoBIP cannot be simply attributed to the targeting peptide alone, but is more likely due to the delivery advantage provided by the exosome carrier and further enhanced by the targeting modification.

qPCR analysis of femoral tissue showed partial restoration of osteogenic and osteoclast related gene expression in the ExoBIP group (Supporting Fig. 6A). We also evaluated the biosafety of ExoBIP. No obvious damage was observed in major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) (Supporting Fig. 6B), and serum biochemistry and hematological parameters remained within the normal range (Supporting Fig. 6C). A hemolysis assay showed no visible hemolysis even at a high dose of 1000 μg, with absorbance comparable to the PBS control, indicating good blood compatibility (Supporting Fig. 6D and E).

Overall, these results indicate that ExoBIP effectively alleviates persistent bone loss in IBD by enhancing osteogenesis and suppressing osteoclast activity. Its efficacy is greater than that of unmodified exosomes or non-bone-targeted IL-18BP exosomes, and it does not cause detectable systemic toxicity.

ExoBIP suppresses the IFN-γ and RANKL effector phenotype of bone marrow CD8+ T cells and helps restore bone remodeling balance

To examine ExoBIP's therapeutic efficacy at the cellular level, we used the same CD8+ T cell-conditioned medium system. CD8+ T cells were first stimulated with IL-18 and treated with PBS (G1), Exo (G2), ExoBIP (G3), recombinant MMP9 (G4), or ExoBIP plus recombinant MMP9 (G5). Supernatants from each group were then collected as CM and applied to osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs (ALP, ARS) or osteoclast differentiation of monocytes (TRAP) (Fig. 7A). On the osteogenic side, CM from IL-18-treated T cells significantly suppressed osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Compared with PBS, Exo provided only limited relief, mainly reflected by a modest improvement at the mineralization stage (ARS), with little effect on early ALP staining. In contrast, ExoBIP alone produced a clearer recovery trend in osteogenesis. We speculate that this may be due to endogenous protease activity in the system: under inflammatory stimulation, T cells can generate a certain level of MMP activity, which may partially cleave the linker on ExoBIP, release a small amount of IL-18BP, and contribute to IL-18 neutralization. When ExoBIP was combined with recombinant MMP9, ALP and ARS signals increased further, suggesting that ExoBIP acts more effectively in the presence of protease activity (Fig. 7B–C). On the osteoclast side, CM from IL-18-stimulated T cells strongly promoted monocyte-to-osteoclast differentiation and increased the TRAP-positive area. Exo showed little inhibitory effect on TRAP-positive osteoclast formation. By contrast, ExoBIP significantly reduced the TRAP-positive area even without exogenous MMP9, which may relate to the high level of MMP activity generated during osteoclast differentiation, facilitating linker cleavage and IL-18BP release. Under the ExoBIP plus recombinant MMP9 condition, the TRAP-positive area was also significantly reduced, further supporting that ExoBIP suppresses osteoclast activity by neutralizing upstream IL-18-related signals (Fig. 7D). To exclude the possibility that adding MMP9 itself alters differentiation, we included a recombinant MMP9-only control (G4), which did not show obvious improvement in either ALP, ARS or TRAP readouts. We then performed immunofluorescence staining of the femoral bone marrow region to examine how treatment affected the effector phenotype of bone marrow CD8+ T cells in vivo. Compared with the UC group, ExoBIP treatment significantly reduced IFN-γ and RANKL fluorescence signals around CD8 staining in the marrow, and quantification of the positive area (or integrated density) confirmed significantly lower IFN-γ and RANKL levels (Fig. 7E). Collectively, results from cellular assays and the IBD mouse model support ExoBIP as an effective strategy to restrain CD8+ T cell linked marrow inflammation, normalize dysregulated bone remodeling, and restore bone mass. To evaluate whether different IL-18-targeting strategies affected intestinal barrier repair, we examined ZO-1 expression in colon tissues by immunofluorescence staining. Compared with the healthy group, ZO-1 staining was significantly reduced in the UC group. After treatment of SASP, among groups, IL-18BP without bone target showed a lower ZO-1-positive area, whereas ExoBIP preserved ZO-1 expression to a greater extent. These results indicate that non-targeted systemic IL-18BP intervention may interfere with intestinal barrier recovery, whereas the ExoBIP strategy exhibits less impact on colonic tight junction integrity (Supporting Fig. 7A and B). To further clarify the local mode of action of ExoBIP in bone tissue, we performed additional immunofluorescence analyses at the treatment endpoint. Exo associated fluorescence showed partial colocalization with OCN positive osteoblast-lineage cells in bone, suggesting that the exosome carrier can be distributed to, and at least partly taken up by, osteogenic lineage cells within the bone microenvironment. In parallel, IL-18BP fluorescence exhibited spatial overlap with IL-18 signal in the marrow cavity, consistent with local retention of the released payload in the bone marrow niche (Supporting Fig. 7C and D). Free IL-18 levels in serum, colon, and femur were measured in UC colitis mice before and after ExoBIP treatment. ExoBIP treatment did not significantly alter free IL-18 levels in the serum or colon, whereas in bone tissue free IL-18 was significantly decreased after treatment (Supporting Fig. 7E). We further analyzed bone marrow Th17 and Treg populations at the treatment endpoint. Compared with the Healthy group, the Th17/Treg ratio was increased in the UC and PBS groups, and ExoBIP treatment did not restore the overall Th17/Treg ratio (Supporting Fig. 7F and G). Thus, unlike its suppression of the CD8+T cell IFN-γ/RANKL effector phenotype, ExoBIP did not significantly remodel the Th17/Treg axis during the repair phase, suggesting that its bone protective effect is mainly mediated through selective interruption of the persistent IL-18 driven CD8+T cell program.

Single-Cell Transcriptomic Profiling Reveals ExoBIP-Mediated remodeling of the bone Immune–Osteogenic landscape in IBD-associated osteoporosis

To further elucidate the mechanism by which ExoBIP mitigates IBD-induced bone loss, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on femoral bone marrow from UC mice and ExoBIP-treated mice. UMAP analysis of more than 30,000 cells resolved six major populations, including BMSCs, osteoblasts, myeloid cells, T cells, B cells, and erythroid cells (Fig. 8A). Relative to the UC group, ExoBIP treatment was associated with an increased representation of osteogenic populations, particularly BMSCs and osteoblasts, alongside a reduction in myeloid cells (Fig. 8A–C). These shifts suggest that ExoBIP influences not only bone structure but also the cellular makeup of the marrow niche in a manner consistent with tissue recovery. Cell proportion analysis further reflected these trends, with osteogenic compartments expanding and inflammatory myeloid populations declining after ExoBIP treatment (Fig. 8C). To further capture treatment-associated changes beyond the major lineage shifts, we reanalyzed non-lymphoid immune compartments and Tregs in the marrow (Supporting Fig. 8A and B). Proportionally, inflammatory monocytes, neutrophils, and macrophage-related subsets remained among the dominant immune populations and showed coordinated remodeling after ExoBIP treatment (Supporting Fig. 8A). At the transcriptional level, inflammatory monocytes displayed ExoBIP-associated upregulation of immune activation programs together with a concomitant downregulation of motility-related signatures, including myeloid leukocyte migration, chemotaxis, and cell chemotaxis (Supporting Fig. 8B), suggesting an attenuation of recruitment and trafficking programs that could otherwise sustain inflammatory propagation within the marrow niche. Neutrophils exhibited increased expression of translation-related and bioenergetic programs, whereas pathways linked to innate immune response and immune receptor signaling, as well as RNA splicing regulation, were comparatively reduced (Supporting Fig. 8B), consistent with a shift away from a highly receptor-driven inflammatory state toward metabolic and translational reprogramming. Macrophage-related populations showed enrichment of reactive oxygen species metabolic processes together with signatures associated with IL-1 production, while genes involved in cell killing, chemotaxis and migration, and extrinsic apoptotic signaling via death receptors were downregulated following ExoBIP (Supporting Fig. 8B), overall indicating functional reprogramming accompanied by reduced cytotoxic and migratory inflammatory programs. In parallel, Tregs demonstrated upregulation of RNA processing and splicing programs, whereas translation and proliferation associated terms were decreased (Supporting Fig. 8B), which may reflect a treatment linked normalization of their activation and proliferative state in a less inflammatory marrow environment. Within the T cell compartment, subcluster analysis showed enrichment of effector-like CD8 positive T cells in UC mice. This bias was reduced following ExoBIP administration, and the overall T cell composition appeared more balanced (Fig. 8D–E).

We next examined whether ExoBIP altered global marrow communication patterns. In UC mice, CellChat analysis revealed an interactive network linking immune and stromal compartments. Multiple immune populations, including inflammatory monocytes, neutrophils, and macrophage-related subsets, showed active connectivity and potential interactions with osteoblast-lineage and osteoclast-lineage cells (Fig. 8G). This was consistent with the incoming versus outgoing signaling landscape, where several immune compartments exhibited elevated communication activity (Fig. 8F). Notably, within this globally activated network, CD8+ T cells emerged as one of the major interaction hubs, displaying high centrality and connectivity and indicating strong reception and propagation of intercellular cues (Fig. 8F and G). After ExoBIP treatment, overall communication intensity was attenuated: the connectivity between immune and stromal compartments weakened (Fig. 8G). Importantly, CD8+ T cells showed a marked reduction in interaction strength in the incoming–outgoing landscape, and CD8+ T cell associated connections were diminished in the circle plots (Fig. 8F and G). Together, these results suggest that ExoBIP is associated with reduced CD8+ T cell-centered intercellular communication, accompanied by changes in myeloid and stromal interactions, consistent with a bone marrow microenvironment that may be more supportive of osteogenic recovery.

Collectively, these single-cell analyses demonstrate that ExoBIP reprograms the bone immune–osteogenic landscape by suppressing inflammatory cell subsets, expanding osteoprogenitor populations, and enhancing pro-regenerative communication networks. By linking IL-18 neutralization with niche-level remodeling, these findings position ExoBIP as a mechanistically informed therapeutic approach for restoring skeletal integrity in IBD-associated osteoporosis.

Conclusion

In the chronic IBD model, we observed a divergence between intestinal and skeletal outcomes. After treatment, intestinal inflammation improved and classical inflammatory cytokines shifted toward baseline. In contrast, bone mass and indices of bone remodeling continued to deteriorate. These results indicate that mucosal healing alone does not halt skeletal loss, and that pathogenic signals in the marrow may need to be controlled without broadly blocking pathways that support intestinal repair. We therefore developed an engineered exosome platform, ExoBIP. We used BMSC-derived exosomes as the carrier to provide pro-osteogenic support. To direct the particles to bone, we introduced an SDSSD peptide. We also displayed IL-18BP on the exosome surface through an MMP9-responsive linker, so that IL-18BP is released primarily in inflamed skeletal microenvironments where MMP9 is enriched. This configuration concentrates IL-18 neutralization in bone and limits systemic exposure, reducing the likelihood of disrupting IL-18 function in the gut. Our cellular and animal data indicate that IL-18 drives an immune-mediated shift in bone remodeling. In the marrow, IL-18 activated CD8+ T cell effector programs and increased downstream mediators that suppress osteogenesis and promote osteoclastogenesis. ExoBIP blunted these changes by blocking IL-18 locally, reducing CD8+ T cell–associated inflammatory outputs, and moving the marrow environment toward a state that supports bone formation. Consistent with these effects, ExoBIP improved trabecular microarchitecture and bone mass, increased osteogenic readouts, and reduced osteoclast-related burden. In the measurements we collected, we did not detect obvious safety signals.

Together, these data show that bone-targeted, inflammation-triggered cytokine neutralization can decouple skeletal protection from intestinal repair in chronic inflammation. By combining bone targeting, microenvironment-dependent release, and the regenerative properties of exosomes, ExoBIP reduced inflammation-associated bone loss in this model. Beyond IBD-associated bone loss, ExoBIP may also be applicable to other inflammatory bone diseases. Its platform potential lies in two modular features: bone-targeted enrichment and protease-triggered release within inflammatory microenvironments, whereas the therapeutic payload can be replaced according to disease context. In disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis, inflammatory bone lesions are often accompanied by increased MMP activity, supporting the biological plausibility of this design in these settings [47,48]. Nevertheless, because bone pathology differs substantially across diseases, the efficacy of this strategy and the optimal payload will require validation in disease-specific models.

Limitations of this study are that we did not employ intestinal specific IL-18 knockout mice to provide direct genetic evidence that gut derived IL-18 is causally required for IBD associated bone loss, nor did we perform immune cell specific depletion or conditional knockout experiments (e.g., targeting macrophages, neutrophils, or Treg cells) to define cell type contributions to the IL-18 associated bone loss phenotype in vivo. While our data support a model in which circulating IL-18 accumulates in the bone marrow niche and drives bone loss, definitive source attribution and cell resolved mechanistic validation will require these genetic and depletion approaches. Future studies will therefore prioritize IEC specific IL-18 knockout models alongside immune cell targeted conditional knockout strategies in the same IBD setting to directly test the causal contribution of gut-derived IL-18 and to delineate which immune compartments mediate IL-18 driven skeletal pathology.

Experimental section

Animals. C57BL/6J mice (6-8 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Animals were housed in individually ventilated cages (five mice per cage) under controlled environmental conditions (temperature ≈ 25 °C, humidity 40-70%) with a 12-h light and dark cycle. Standard laboratory chow and water were provided ad libitum by specialized breeders.

Study approval. All animal experiments adhered to relevant laws and were approved by the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, Harbin Medical University (Approval No. YJSDW2024-210).

Preparation of DSS-induced OP models. 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were used to establish IBD-associated bone loss. Chronic colitis was induced by voluntary oral intake of dextran sulfate sodium (DSS; MP Biomedicals) in drinking water. Mice received 2% DSS for 7 days followed by normal drinking water for 7 days; this cycle was repeated twice to induce chronic intestinal inflammation. Control mice were provided with regular drinking water throughout the experiment.

Estimation of heritability. We applied univariate linkage disequilibrium (LD) score regression (LDSC, version 1.0.1) to estimate the genome-wide SNP-based heritability (h2 SNP, the proportion of phenotypic variance that can be explained by common genetic variants restricted to HapMap3, which is recognized as well imputed in most studies to minimize bias due to low imputation quality) for each trait, as well as the prevalence-corrected heritability (i.e., liability scale) for several diseases. Briefly, LDSC estimates heritability for a polygenic trait using the fact that SNPs in a high LD region would have higher chi-square statistics than that in a low LD region.

Global-level genetic correlation analysis. To evaluate the genome-wide genetic correlation between OP and gastrointestinal diseases, we further utilised LDSC software to decipher the shared genetic architecture. Briefly, it quantifies polygenic effects by examining the relationship between LD scores and GWAS summary statistics, yielding genetic correlation from deviations in chi square values from the null hypothesis.

Mendelian Randomization Analysis. Summary-level GWAS data for in IBD, CD, UC, and OP were obtained from the IIBDGC, GEFOS Consortium, UK Biobank, and Finn Gen, and circulating cytokine data were sourced from large-scale proteomic GWASs, all in European-ancestry cohorts. Genome-wide significant SNPs of IBD subtype (p < 5 × 10−8) were clumped at r2 < 0.001 within 10,000 kb, excluding palindromic variants and weak instruments (F < 10). Genome-wide Proteome Quantitative Trait Loci analyses were executed on 91 CIPs across 11 cohorts encompassing a total of 14,824 participants. The criteria for the genetic instrumental variables (IVs) were as follows: p < 1 × 10−5, r2 < 0.001, MAF >0.01 and kb < 10,000. The F statistic, F = R2 (n−2)/(1−R2), was used to measure the strength of each genetic tool, where N was the effective sample size for GWAS for SNP association. Typically, IVs with low F statistics (<10) were removed from our analyses. Harmonization aligned effect alleles across datasets. Two-sample MR was conducted using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method, with MR Egger and weighted median as sensitivity analyses. Heterogeneity was assessed by Cochran's Q, horizontal pleiotropy by the MR Egger intercept, and robustness by leave-one-out analysis. Analyses were performed in R (v4.2.1) with the Two Sample MR and MRPRESSO packages, and p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis (GSE214695). Publicly available scRNA-seq data from colonic biopsies (GSE214695; Healthy donors n = 6, UC donors n = 6, CD donors n = 6) were analyzed using Seurat (v4.1.1) in R. Cells were filtered using standard quality-control criteria (e.g., removal of cells with <200 detected genes or > 20% mitochondrial transcripts). Counts were log-normalized (Seurat LogNormalize), scaled, and subjected to PCA; clustering was performed using the shared nearest neighbor graph with commonly used default settings (e.g., PCA dimensions 1-30, resolution around 0.5), and UMAP was used for visualization. Cell clusters were annotated based on canonical marker genes. Il18 and Il18r1 expression was visualized using violin and UMAP feature plots. For group comparisons (Healthy vs IBD), to reduce cell-level pseudoreplication, expression was summarized at the donor level within each cell type (pseudo-bulk/aggregate), followed by statistical testing between the two donor groups (two-sided, non-parametric testing when appropriate). The R package CellChat was employed to infer and evaluate cell-cell communication networks across various cell types, excluding those containing fewer than 10 cells. Cells were grouped according to curated cell-type annotations generated by integrating refined T cell and myeloid annotations into the original labels. The full mouse ligand–receptor reference database was used without signaling category filtering. After subsetting the dataset to signaling genes, overexpressed genes and ligand–receptor interactions were identified using CellChat. In the combined dataset, intercellular communication probabilities were computed using the triMean method Pathway-level communication probabilities were then calculated and aggregated to construct the global communication network, and signaling roles were characterized by pathway-level centrality analysis. All other parameters were kept at their default settings.

Bulk RNA-Sequencing Analysis. Total RNA was extracted from femoral bone tissues of DSS-induced colitis mice and healthy controls using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse transcription was carried out using SuperScript III reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen) to synthesize cDNA. Sequencing libraries were constructed with a library preparation kit (BGI-Shenzhen, China) and subjected to paired-end sequencing on the T7 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China). Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes between groups were identified using the R package DEGseq (R-3.6.2), with |log2 fold change| > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler (v4.0.5) package, with pathways from the Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG databases. Normalized enrichment scores (NES) and permutation-based p values were reported, with adjusted p < 0.05 deemed significant.

Histological Staining. Colonic and bone tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution for 1 month, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning at 4 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on colonic sections. Toluidine blue staining was used to quantify osteoblast number (G3665, Solarbio, China), while TRAP staining, performed using the Amizona staining kit (Hangzhou Yangming Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), was employed to assess osteoclast number. Toluidine blue positive osteoblasts lining the trabecular bone surface were identified based on their typical morphology and location (cuboidal cells on bone surfaces and bone lining cells flattened cells lining the trabecular surface). Quantification was performed using ImageJ. For each section, the trabecular bone region of interest (ROI) was defined consistently across samples. The number of toluidine blue positive osteoblasts along the trabecular bone surface was counted and normalized to the measured bone surface length, and reported as cells per bone surface length. TRAP-positive multinucleated cells located on the trabecular bone surface were identified as osteoclasts. Osteoclast parameters were quantified in a predefined trabecular region of interest (ROI) under blinded conditions. Histomorphometric measurements were performed using ImageJ after setting the spatial scale. Trabecular bone surfaces were traced using the freehand line tool to obtain the bone perimeter (B.Pm). TRAP-positive osteoclast coverage along the trabecular bone surface was measured accordingly. Osteoclast number per bone perimeter was reported as N.Oc/B.Pm.

Single-Cell Transcriptomic Profiling of Mouse Bone Marrow. Femoral tissues from IBD and ExoBIP-treated mice were subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform. Raw sequencing reads were processed with Cell Ranger (v6.1.2) for alignment to the mouse reference genome (mm10). Downstream analysis was conducted in Seurat (v5.3.0). Cells with <200 or >6,000 detected genes or >5% mitochondrial content were excluded. After normalization, scaling, and PCA, UMAP was used for clustering. Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes. Subclustering of myeloid and T cell populations was performed to resolve immune subsets. Proportional changes across groups were assessed. CellPhoneDB (v5.0.1) was used for analyzing cell communications.

Immunofluorescence staining. Paraffin-embedded sections of intestinal or femoral tissues underwent antigen retrieval by microwave heating in citrate-sodium citrate buffer, followed by cooling to room temperature. Sections were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5% serum with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against MMP9 (1:300 AF5234, Beyotime), OPN (1:300 22952-1-AP, Proteintech), OSX (1:300 sc-393325, Santa Cruz), IL-18 (1:300 AF7266, Beyotime), IL-18BP (1:200 ab214229, Abcam), IFN-γ (1:200 ab224197, Abcam), Rankl (1:200 AG4340, Beyotime), CD8 (1:300 AG1411, Beyotime), ZO-1 (33-9100, Invitrogen), OCN (GB11233, Servicebio). After washing, sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L Alexa 555 (1:200 ab150078, Abcam) or 647 (1:200 ab150079, Abcam), Goat anti-mouse IgG H&L Alexa 488 (1:300 ab150113, Abcam) or 555 (1:200 ab150114, Abcam), Goat anti-rat IgG H&L Alexa 647 (1:200 ab150167, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were mounted using an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI (P0131, Beyotime) and imaged on a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (A1, version 5.20.00, Nikon) with the associated acquisition software. All images were acquired under identical microscope settings (laser power, gain, pinhole, and exposure time) for all groups within the same experiment. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ (NIH, USA). For each section, the region of interest (ROI) was defined consistently across samples (intestinal mucosa excluding the lumen, or bone marrow area within the femoral section). Background was corrected using a rolling-ball algorithm, and fluorescence channels were split. For each marker, a uniform threshold was set based on the healthy group and then applied identically to all images from the same batch to segment positive signals. The positive area fraction was calculated as the percentage of marker-positive pixels within the ROI (positive area/ROI area × 100%); Image acquisition and quantification were performed in a blinded manner with respect to group allocation.

Bone histomorphometry analysis. To assess dynamic bone formation, mice received intraperitoneal injections of calcein (20 mg/kg) and alizarin red (30 mg/kg) on days 18 and 25, respectively, prior to sacrifice. Femurs were collected, fixed, dehydrated, and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. Longitudinal sections (∼10 μm) were prepared using a cryostat (Leica, Germany) and imaged with a CLSM (A1, version 5.20.00, Nikon). Mineral apposition rate (MAR) was quantified using BioQuant OSTEO software (version 20.8.60, BioQuant).

ELISA. Intestinal tissue, serum, and femoral bone samples were collected from experimental and control mice. After pulverization of bone and intestinal tissues in liquid nitrogen, samples were homogenized in ice-cold lysis buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5% Triton X-100) and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. IL-18 (SEKM-0019 Solarbio), IL-18BP (USEH821Mu, USCN), IL-1β (YJ106733A, mlbio), IL-6 (YJ063159A, mlbio), IL-10 (EK0417, Boster), TNF-α (YJ002095A, mlbio), ZO-1 (ml037693, mlbio), concentrations in all samples were determined using a commercially available ELISA kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

RT-qPCR Assay. Total RNA was isolated from femoral bone tissues using RNAiso Plus Total RNA Extraction Reagent. For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed with HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme) following the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative PCR was conducted in a final volume of 20 μL containing 5 ng of cDNA template, ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme), and specific primers. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and average values were used for statistical evaluation. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 7.

Radiological Analysis. Mice were anesthetized and femurs were scanned using a high-resolution micro-CT system (IMAGING 100, Raycision Medical Technology Co., Ltd) with the following parameters: voxel size = 10 μm, tube voltage = 90 kV, tube current = 88 μA, and scan time = 3 min. The 3D reconstruction and quantitative analysis of trabecular bone parameters, including BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Th, and Tb.Sp, were performed using the manufacturer's standard analysis software. BMD was determined using a dual-energy X-ray system (XPERT 40, KUBTEC).

T cell Sorting and conditioned medium Preparation. Mouse spleens were mechanically dissociated, and red blood cells were lysed to prepare a single-cell suspension. CD8+ T cells were isolated by magnetic bead-based separation using a CD8+ T cell selection kit (IMMOCELL, IMP-MK021). Cells were activated with plate-bound anti-CD3ε (1 μg/mL) and soluble anti-CD28 (1 μg/mL) in the presence of 0.5 ng/mL IL-2 for 24 h. Groups included a PBS control; 20 ng/mL recombinant IL-18 (RP02521, ABclonal); 20 ng/mL recombinant IL-18BP (50206-M08H, Sino Biological); 10 μM LY294002, a PI3K pathway inhibitor (S1737, Beyotime); 25 ng/mL IFN-γ (P6137, Beyotime); 25 ng/mL RANKL; 1 μg/mL IFN-γ Antagonist (HY-P4717, MCE); and 1 μg/mL Anti-Mouse RANKL Antibody (HY-P990165, MCE); 1 μM Ruxolitinib (HY-50856, MCE), a JAK1/2 pathway inhibitor; 5 μM BAY 11-7821 (HY-13453, MCE), an NF-κB pathway inhibitor. For treatment conditions, cells were stimulated with IL-18 and co-treated with PBS, recombinant MMP9, Exo, ExoIP, or ExoBIP. Exosomes were quantified by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and added to cultures at 1 × 109 particles/mL. After 24-48 h of treatment, supernatants were collected and centrifuged to remove debris, and the resulting conditioned media (CM) were used for subsequent co-culture induction assays with BMSCs and mononuclear cells.

Isolation of BMSCs and assessment of osteogenic differentiation. C57BL/6J mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Femurs and tibias were harvested under sterile conditions, and the bone marrow cavities were flushed with α-MEM (Gibco, C12571500BT) containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) to collect cells. The harvested cells were cultured in complete α-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% P/S. After 3 days, the medium was replaced to remove non-adherent cells. When BMSCs reached 80-90% confluence, the culture medium was switched to osteogenic induction medium. The induction medium was mixed 1:1 with conditioned medium from each CD8+ T cell group to investigate the regulatory mechanism and evaluate the efficacy of ExoBIP. On day 14 of induction, ALP activity was assessed using a BCIP/NBT kit (C3206, Beyotime). On day 21, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with ARS (C0148S, Beyotime) to assess calcium deposition and mineralized nodule formation.

Isolation of Monocytes and Osteoclastic Differentiation. Bone marrow was flushed from mouse long bones to obtain a single-cell suspension and cultured at 37 °C for 12-14 h to allow stromal cells to adhere. The non-adherent mononuclear cells were then collected, treated with ACK lysis buffer to remove red blood cells, counted, and seeded. For osteoclast induction, cells were first stimulated with M-CSF (30 ng/mL) for 12-14 h to promote precursor attachment. Cells were then cultured in induction medium containing RANKL (50 ng/mL) and treated as indicated by mixing the induction medium 1:1 with conditioned medium from each CD8+ T-cell group to investigate the regulatory mechanism and evaluate the efficacy of ExoBIP. After 5-7 days of induction, cells were stained for tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) (AMIZONA, AMK2001-24T), and osteoclast differentiation was quantified using an image analysis system.

Gut microbiota analysis and construction of a butyrate-producer index. Fecal microbiota from healthy controls and patients with IBD were profiled by 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. After standard sequence processing and taxonomic annotation, genus and family level relative abundance data were obtained for downstream analyses. Overall microbial compositional differences between groups were assessed using Bray–Curtis distances, followed by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) in R with the vegan package. Group separation was evaluated by PERMANOVA. For targeted taxonomic analysis, the relative abundances of the genera and families shown in the figure were compared between healthy controls and patients using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg method and are reported as q values. At the genus level, Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, and Anaerostipes were selected as representative butyrate-related taxa. A butyrate-producer index was calculated by summing the relative abundances of these three genera in each sample. Correlation analyses were performed within the patient cohort only. Correlation coefficients and two-sided p values were reported. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Preparation of exosomes. Culture supernatants from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were sequentially centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min and 2,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cells and cell debris. The clarified supernatant was further centrifuged at 10,000×g for 30 min to eliminate larger vesicles. Exosomes were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g for 70 min twice, washed in PBS between spins, and finally resuspended in PBS. Protein concentration of exosome suspensions was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Preparation of ExoBIP. The MMP-cleavable linker peptide (sequence: PLGVR; 0.25 mg ml−1) was synthesized with an NHS group at the N-terminus and a 6-maleimide group at the C-terminus. The linker was reacted with IL-18 binding protein (IL-18BP, 1 mg ml−1) at a molar ratio of 10:1 (linker-to-IL-18BP) at 4 °C for 12–18 h. Based on the same NHS-ester chemistry targeting surface amino groups, the bone-targeting peptide (SDSSD) was also conjugated to IL-18BP using NHS-modified SDSSD. Unreacted linker was removed using a 10 kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tube at 4,000×g for 15 min, yielding the purified maleimide-functionalized cL–IL-18BP–SDSSD complex. Separately, to generate reactive sites on the carrier. BMSC-derived Exo were pre-treated with 1 mM tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) at 37 °C for 30 min to reduce surface protein disulfide bonds and expose free sulfhydryl groups (-SH). The cL–IL-18BP–SDSSD complex (carrying reactive maleimide groups) was then incubated with the thiol-activated exosomes in PBS (pH 7.4) at 25 °C for 1 h, enabling specific maleimide–thiol conjugation. The resulting Exo-cL-IL-18BP-SDSSD (termed ExoBIP) was washed and purified by ultracentrifugation (100,000×g, 70 min, 4 °C).

Quantification of IL-18BP conjugation on ExoBIP. After completion of the conjugation reaction, ExoBIP was purified by ultracentrifugation, and the supernatants and wash fractions were collected. The amount of unbound IL-18BP in these fractions was quantified using an IL-18BP ELISA kit (USEH821Mu, USCN) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The amount of conjugated IL-18BP was then estimated by subtracting the unbound fraction from the initial input. Total exosomal protein was measured by BCA assay, and the conjugation amount was expressed as ng IL-18BP per μg exosomal protein. Exosome particle concentration was determined by NTA. To estimate the average conjugation density, the mass of conjugated IL-18BP was converted into molecule number based on its molecular weight and normalized to the total exosome particle count, yielding the estimated number of IL-18BP molecules per exosome.

Morphology of ExoBIP. Morphological characterization of exosomes was performed on a JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan) operated at 120 kV. For size distribution and surface charge measurements, samples were analyzed using a Particle Metrix nanoparticle tracking analysis system according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Western blot analysis. The total protein content of exosomes was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PI3K (1:1000 ab302958, Abcam), p-PI3K (1:1000 4228T, CST), Akt (1:1000 9272S, CST), p-Akt (1:1000 4060T, CST), β-actin (1:1000 ab8227, Abcam), CD9 (1:1000 ab236630, Abcam), TSG101 (1:1000 ab125011, Abcam), CD63 (1:1000 ab217345, Abcam), CD81 (1:1000 10037T, CST), and IL-18BP (1:1000 14153-1-AP, Proteintech). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Beyotime) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5200).

Colocalization of ExoBIP. Exo and IL-18BP were labeled with Cy5 and Alexa Fluor 555, respectively. For fluorescent imaging of ExoBIP, the double-labeled complexes were immobilized on a confocal dish and visualized using CLSM (A1, version 5.20.00, Nikon) and STED (SP8, Leica). Fluorescence colocalization and intensity were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

In vitro measurement of release titre of IL-18BP from ExoBIP. According to experimental requirements, ExoBIP was incubated with different reagents. After incubation, the reaction mixture was processed using a 50 kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tube to separate cleaved, free IL-18BP from the intact, uncleaved ExoBIP. The filtrate containing the released IL-18BP was then collected and quantified by ELISA.

Tail vein injection. After mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 0.5% pentobarbital sodium (0.1 mL per 10 g body weight), the indicated treatments, including PBS, Exo, IL-18BP, ExoIP, and ExoBIP, were administered via tail vein injection. For all groups, the dose was standardized to 200 μg of the respective preparation dissolved in 150 μL sterile PBS. Injections were performed slowly over approximately 30 s to ensure proper delivery and minimize vascular injury.

In Vivo and Ex Vivo Fluorescence Imaging of ExoBIP Distribution. In Vivo and Ex Vivo Fluorescence Imaging of ExoBIP Distribution. Cy5-labeled exosomes (Exo, ExoIP, or ExoBIP) were intravenously injected into DSS-induced IBD-associated osteoporosis model mice. Whole-body and organ fluorescence signals were acquired at predetermined time points using the IVIS Spectrum Imaging System (PerkinElmer) equipped with Living Image software. For ex vivo imaging, mice were euthanized at 24 h post-injection, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) and femurs were harvested, rinsed with PBS, fluorescence imaging.

Targeting ability and spatiotemporally controlled release. After mice received Cy3-labeled ExoBIP via tail vein injection for 24 h, femurs were harvested and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, then stored at −20 °C. Tissue cryosections (10 μm thick) were prepared using a cryostat (Leica) in combination with an adhesive cryofilm (Section Lab, Japan), which facilitated stable section handling and preserved structural integrity. The presence and distribution of IL-18BP were further visualized by immunofluorescence staining. Imaging was performed by CLSM (A1, version 5.20.00, Nikon) to assess the spatial distribution of exosomes and IL-18BP within the tissue.

Measurement and calculation of free IL-18. Total IL-18 and IL-18BP levels in serum and tissue samples were measured separately using commercially available assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions, IL-18 (SEKM-0019 Solarbio), IL-18BP (USEH821Mu, USCN). Free IL-18 was not measured directly, but was inferred from total IL-18 and IL-18BP concentrations based on the law of mass action, assuming a 1:1 stoichiometric binding model between IL-18 and IL-18BP, as commonly described in previous studies. This approach was used to estimate the biologically active fraction of IL-18 under the current murine experimental conditions. Notably, this calculation-based approach has been widely adopted in previous studies to estimate biologically active free IL-18 [[49], [50], [51]].

Flow cytometric analysis of bone marrow Th17 and Treg cells. Bone marrow single cell suspensions were prepared from femurs collected at the treatment endpoint. Briefly, bilateral femurs were aseptically isolated. Bone marrow cells were flushed out with sterile PBS, passed through a 70 μm cell strainer, and subjected to red blood cell lysis (420301, BioLegend). After washing, cells were resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer. For Th17 analysis, bone marrow cells were stimulated for 5 h at 37 °C with Leukocyte Activation Cocktail in the presence of brefeldin A. The cells were then stained with Zombie Aqua Fixable Viability Kit (423102, BioLegend) to exclude dead cells and with FITC anti-mouse CD4 (100406, BioLegend) antibody for surface staining. After fixation and permeabilization, intracellular staining was performed using Brilliant Violet 421™ anti-mouse IL-17A (506926, BioLegend); For the assessment of Treg cells, freshly isolated bone marrow cells were processed in parallel without ex vivo stimulation. Cells were stained with Zombie Aqua Fixable Viability Kit (423102, BioLegend) to exclude dead cells and with FITC anti-mouse CD4 (100406, BioLegend) antibody for surface staining, followed by fixation, permeabilization, and intracellular staining with PE anti-mouse Foxp3 (560408, BD Biosciences) antibody.

Safety assessment in vivo. For the biosafety evaluation, C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into five groups (PBS, IL-18BP, Exo, ExoIP, and ExoBIP; n = 6 per group). Mice were administered the respective formulations via tail vein injection every three days for a total 10 injections (200 μg per dose dissolved in 150 μL PBS). At the study endpoint, blood samples were collected for routine hematology and biochemical analyses using a fully automated analyzer (Hitachi 7080, Hitachi Ltd., Japan). Major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and subjected to hematoxylin-eosin staining for histopathological evaluation. Hemolysis assessment was performed by incubating mouse red blood cells with different concentrations of ExoBIP, and absorbance at 540 nm was measured to evaluate hemolytic activity.

Statistical analysis. data are presented as mean ± s.d. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0. Two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple-comparisons test was used for multiple groups. No data points were excluded from the analyses. Outliers were not removed unless attributable to predefined technical or experimental failure criteria, which did not occur in this study. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
