Nonthyroidal Illness Syndrome in Clostridium Difficile Infections Predict Mortality
Authors: Aleksandra Młodożeniec, Martyna Mendyka, Anna Watras, Karol Skoczylas, Krzysztof Gargasz, Agnieszka Gala-Błądzińska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Clostridium difficile infections (CDIs) are among the most common causes of hospital diarrhea and are associated with prolonged hospitalization and increased mortality.Aim:To evaluate the prevalence of nonthyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS) in hospitalized patients with CDI and its impact on the length of hospitalization and patient survival.Methods:A retrospective single-center study at a hospital in the Subcarpathian region of Europe. We reviewed the medical data of 370 patients with confirmed CDI who stayed at the hospital between January 1, 2015, and April 1, 2024. Demographic data, laboratory values, comorbidities, treatments, and clinical outcomes were also collected. We independently compared the data of patients with NTIS and euthyroidism and independently between survivors and nonsurvivors. The statistical test used were Mann–Whitney U-test and Chi-square test (with Pearson’s or Yates’ correction).Results:Of 370 patients, 113 (30%) were included in the study. Eighty-one (71.7%) patients presented with CDI accompanied by NTIS. The median age in both the CDI and NTIS groups was 82 years, and 51.8% of patients were female. NTIS was significantly associated with 3.7-fold higher odds of death compared to euthyroidism (odds ratio = 3.70; 95% confidence interval: 1.02–13.38; P = 0.036). Lower serum free triiodothyronine (FT3) levels were also significantly associated with increased mortality (P = 0.035).Conclusions:Low serum FT3 levels appear to be an independent marker of clinical deterioration and a predictor of mortality in patients with CDI and could be incorporated into the prognostic assessment of these patients.

INTRODUCTION

Clostridium difficile (CD) is an anaerobic bacillus found in the gastrointestinal tract. Its uncontrolled proliferation is often the result of the eradication of normal physiological flora by broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. Other important risk factors include old age, hospital stay, and nursing home stay.[12] CD infection (CDI) is the most common cause of in-hospital diarrhea, with clinical presentations such as asymptomatic carriage, mild or moderate diarrhea, and fulminant enteritis.[3] Gastrointestinal infections caused by CD are associated with higher hospitalization and mortality rates as well as longer hospital stays.[45]

CDI is categorized as a healthcare-related infection (HAI) and is often transmitted through hospital staff or the hospital environment. An HAI is an infection that occurs in a patient during the process of care in a hospital or other healthcare facility and is not present or incubated at the time of admission.[6] It is widely known that the relationship between cleanliness procedures and reductions in infections, including CDI, noncompliance with healthcare tools, correlated with higher rates of gastroenteritis.[7] HAIs are a consequence of poor-quality care as well as increasingly advanced care, as highlighted by the World Health Organization.[8]

Thyroid function is controlled by the hypothalamus–pituitary–thyroid axis (HPT), which regulates the secretion of thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) and thyrotropin (TSH). The peripheral availability of triiodothyronine (T3) is controlled by deiodination, carried out by deiodinase enzymes in the kidney, liver, and muscles. Nonthyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS), also known as low free T3 (FT3) syndrome, is the temporal biochemical changes of thyroid hormone concentrations, such as normal or decreased levels of TSH, low FT3, and low or normal free thyroxine (FT4). This syndrome is accompanied by an increase in reverse T3 levels. However, its diagnostic tests are not routinely performed in hospitals.[9] The pathogenesis of NTIS is complicated and includes (1) the suppression of the HPT axis, as confirmed by the observation of reduced TRH gene expression in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus in autopsy samples;[10] (2) the decreased activity of deiodinase enzymes and expression of thyroid hormone receptors;[11] and (3) the participation of proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor, which reduce the expression of deiodinases and could act on the HPT.[12]

This study aimed to determine the incidence of NTIS in hospitalized patients with CDI and to determine the connection between thyroid hormones and the length of hospitalization and mortality in these patients.

METHODS

Study design

This retrospective analysis included the medical records of 370 patients with CDI treated in the internal medicine department of a regional hospital in Poland (Clinical Hospital No. 2 in Rzeszow) between January 1, 2015, and April 1, 2024. The department is a 37-bed internal medicine unit operating within a large regional referral hospital with nearly 1000 beds in total. The hospital provides care for a city of approximately 200,000 inhabitants and hosts the only emergency department in the city. The internal medicine department is characterized by a very high bed occupancy rate, reaching approximately 99%.

The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (1) aged 18 years who tested positive for CDI; CD toxin tests were performed using a chemiluminescent immunoassay; the test detected CD A and/or B toxins in the stool samples; and rectal swabs were collected before treatment initiation; (2) hospitalized between January 1, 2015, and April 1, 2024; and (3) admitted to the general internal medicine wards. Patients were excluded if they had (1) a diagnosis of any thyroid disease, such as hypothyroidism, subclinical hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, or thyroid tumors; (2) a diagnosis of pituitary disease; (3) a history of thyroidectomy; (4) a history of taking medications which can interfere with the HPT axis, such as dopamine and glucocorticoid, or thyroid hormone therapy; or (5) no thyroid hormonal test in the medical history.

After applying the exclusion criteria, data from 113 (30%) patients with CDI were analyzed. Information was collected from electronic medical records. Age, sex, comorbidities, laboratory results, FT3, FT4, TSH, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), white blood cell count (WBC), creatinine, glomerular filtration rate, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were assessed.

Among the studied group of patients, heart diseases included coronary artery disease, condition after myocardial infarction or coronary angiography, heart failure, arrhythmias, and condition after cardiac surgery. Patients with concomitant lung diseases in the study were those suffering from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or bronchial asthma.

The presence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) was classified in accordance with the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes 2024 guidelines.[13] Categorical variables included gender, comorbidities, and the presence of death.

To incorporate categorical variables, the present/absent criterion were used.

Laboratory tests were performed in the hospital’s laboratory. Serum FT3, FT4, and TSH levels were measured using chemiluminescent immunoassays. The reference values in the laboratory were as follows: FT3, 2.3–4.2 pg/ml; FT4, 0.89–1.76 ng/dl; and TSH, 0.55–4.78 μIU/ml. If a patient underwent several similar tests, the earliest results were used to collect data on the prognostic value of the risk of death and disease severity. The patients were divided into appropriate groups: (1) CDI and euthyreosis: patients with normal TSH, FT3, and FT4 levels and (2) CDI and NTIS. The diagnostic criteria for NTIS were as follows: FT3, <2.3 pg/ml; FT4, <0.89 ng/dl or normal, and TSH, <0.55 μIU/ml or normal.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and inferential statistical methods were used to evaluate the differences between patients with NTIS and those with euthyreosis. Because continuous variables did not follow a normal distribution (as verified by the Shapiro–Wilk test), they were summarized as medians with minimum and maximum values. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Comorbidities were treated as binary categorical variables and coded as present or absent based on medical records. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used to assess the differences between the two groups for continuous variables, whereas the Chi-square test (with Pearson’s or Yates’ correction) was used to evaluate the associations between categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The odds ratio (OR) was calculated to assess the association between NTIS and mortality, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) providing a measure of precision. All analyses were performed using Statistica version 13 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA).

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Regional Medical Chamber in Rzeszów, Poland (approval no.: 30/2024/B). The procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2000. As only a retrospective study was performed, according to Polish law, patient consent was not required. We collated data mainly from electronic patient histories. Although personal identification numbers were used to match the datasets, these were subsequently anonymized.

RESULTS

A total of 370 adult patients were identified based on the inclusion criteria. We excluded 257 patients who were diagnosed with thyroid disease, underwent thyroidectomy, received medications that altered the thyroid function test results or thyroid hormone therapy, or had missing thyroid hormone data. A total of 113 adult patients were ultimately enrolled in this study. The patients were divided into NTIS (n = 81) and euthyreosis (n = 32) groups. The baseline characteristics and laboratory data of the 113 patients in the study population are shown in Table 2. Our analysis showed the presence of NTIS (serum FT3 levels: <2.3 pg/mL; FT4 and TSH levels within or below the normal reference ranges) in 81 (72%) patients, 23 of whom died. The median age of the NTIS and euthyreosis groups was 82 (40–101) and 69 (25–95) years, respectively (P = 0.001). Patients with NTIS were significantly older than those with euthyroidism. Forty-two (51.8%) patients in the NTIS and 15 (46.9%) in the euthyreosis groups were women (P = 0.633). Among the two groups, patients with NTIS were more likely to have comorbidities, the most common being heart disease (i.e., coronary artery disease, prior myocardial infarction, or arrhythmias) (54.9%, P = 0.033), hypertension (54.9%, P = 0.393), CKD (41.6%, P = 0.170), and diabetes (25.6%, P = 0.646). There were no statistically significant differences in the length of hospital stay between the two groups (P = 0.861).

Compared with the euthyreosis group, there were higher plasma concentrations of creatinine (P = 0.273), ALT (P = 0.589), CRP (P = 0.491), and PCT (P = 0.160) in the NTIS group. DISCUSSION

The literature suggests that there are deviations in thyroid hormone concentrations in critically ill patients without any organic disease of the thyroid gland itself. Disturbances in the hormonal axes during infections occur due to the influence of stress factors, hypercortisolemia, increased cytokines, and oxidative stress. A greater extent of deviation has been correlated with a worse overall prognosis.[1415] The reviewed literature did not include any studies, confirming the relationship between NTIS and death in patients with CDI.

The study revealed that compared with patients in the euthyreosis group, those in the NTIS group had a significantly higher chance of death. There was also a statistically significant correlation between FT3 concentration and mortality among patients with CDI. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that FT3 concentration may be used for better risk stratification of mortality and as a prognostic predictor tool in patients with CDI.

The authors are aware that the research group was relatively small, and additional research is required to confirm these findings.

The fact that low FT3 concentration could be a prognostic factor for mortality is supported by the literature. To date, most studies on NTIS as a potential marker of mortality involve patients with sepsis. Zeng et al. analyzed NTIS in patients with sepsis and concluded that FT3 could be a potential biomarker for predicting death.[16] In addition, low FT3 levels have been described as the most powerful independent predictor of ICU mortality[17] and have been described as a prognostic tool for patients with cardiovascular diseases.[18]

The NTIS score on admission in patients with COVID-19 has been described as a predictor of disease severity.[1920] El-Nawawy et al. found a significant negative correlation between the mortality scores of patients with shock and thyroid hormone levels, including FT3.[21]

Drobnik et al.[22] identified hypothyroidism as a risk factor for mortality in patients with CDI and explained this relationship as a slowing of gastrointestinal peristalsis and weakening of gastric acid secretion, which may occur during hypothyroidism. However, in the aforementioned study, there were no data regarding the possible NTIS.

Notably, low concentrations of thyroid hormones may cause intestinal peristalsis to slow, suggesting that NTIS can be considered a condition leading to CDI. Adamski et al. concluded that myxedema, a severe form of hypothyroidism, may increase the probability of treatment-resistant CDI.[23] The functioning of the digestive tract in myxedema can predispose the small intestine to bacterial growth. Antibiotics can decrease intestinal bacterial diversity, favoring CD overgrowth.

In this study, patients with NTIS were significantly older than those with euthyreosis. The results showed a decrease in FT3 levels with age in patients with CDI, which was a confounding factor in our analysis. This finding aligns with the literature, which suggests that most patients requiring hospitalization for CDI constitute the geriatric population.[24] Advanced patient age is a risk factor for CDI and poor outcomes, including severity and mortality, as documented in many studies.[252627]

Furthermore, patients with CDI often present with eating disorders, which could have had some influence on the frequency of NTIS among them.[28]

This study revealed that compared to the euthyreosis group, there were slightly higher plasma concentrations of CRP and PCT in the NTIS group, which is also reflected in the available literature.[15] CRP and PCT levels are indicators of inflammation, and the inflammatory factors released during the disease are among the main determinants of NTIS development.

Surprisingly, the analysis of survivors and nonsurvivors revealed lower CRP and PCT concentrations in the nonsurvivor group than in the survivor group. Serum CRP level is an independent risk factor for death in patients with CDI.[29] The discrepancy between our results and those of Bednarska et al.[29] may be owing to the small size of our group. To correctly interpret the NTIS, strict exclusion criteria were applied, which limited our study group.

According to the literature, poor prognosis of CDI is associated with high leukocytosis and creatinine levels.[30] In the deceased group, higher WBC counts and creatinine levels were observed; however, these differences were not statistically significant. Patients with NTIS had slightly higher creatinine levels than those with euthyreosis. Acute renal failure is often observed in patients with CDI and is associated with high mortality rates. This is largely due to dehydration in patients with CDI, caused by diarrhea, reduced fluid intake, and fever, which impairs renal function.[31] However, it should be noted that the patients in the NTIS group were more often older adults with concomitant CKD, in whom kidney damage found on admission was not always associated with CDI.

The mechanism of NTIS remains unclear; therefore, there is no obvious treatment for this condition with hormone replacement therapy. Interestingly, Kovacevic et al. conducted two pilot placebo-controlled randomized trials.[32] The trial showed that T3 supplementation in patients with septic shock and NTIS was associated with a low mortality rate in patients with low T3 and T4 levels but with increased mortality in patients with low T3 levels. However, long-term studies have suggested that NTIS is an adaptive response to disease; therefore, TH substitution therapy is not required.[3334]

Identifying simple prognostic factors for CDI could aid clinicians in their everyday work with patients with CDI and reduce the risk of relapse. This study may be used as a starting point for future prospective studies to investigate further the effects of changes in TH metabolism in patients with CDI. In particular, further research on the detailed pathogenesis and role of NTIS in patients with CDI may improve patient care and outcomes.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective study and was therefore unplanned; consequently, FT3 assays were missing in some patients, and most of the data were obtained from electronic patient histories. Second, the size of the study population was relatively small and varied with age. Therefore, some tests had low statistical power. The results should be confirmed on a larger sample. Third, patients with comorbidities have a high incidence of NTIS, and it is not always possible to determine whether NTIS was caused by CDI alone. Fourth, the study lacked reverse T3 serum determination, which would confirm NTIS and the adaptation mechanism of the body.

CONCLUSIONS

Identifying the risk factors for CDI-related mortality helps clinicians classify patients for more intensive monitoring and aggressive treatment. Our study provides data that validate the prognostic role of low FT3 levels. A low serum concentration of FT3 may be a valuable predictor of the risk of mortality in patients with CDI and could be a useful tool for clinicians.
