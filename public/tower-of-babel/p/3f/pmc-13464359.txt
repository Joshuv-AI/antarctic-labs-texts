Pancreatic Collision Tumor Composed of Solid Pseudopapillary Neoplasm and Well-Differentiated Neuroendocrine Tumor: Diagnostic Challenges and Classification Considerations
Authors: Jose Valentin Lopez, Nian Jiang, David Cartwright, Khalid Amin, Diana Oramas Mogrovejo, Ismail Elbaz Younes, Robben Schat, Eric Jensen, Byoung Uk Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Collision tumors are uncommon neoplasms composed of two histologically distinct tumor populations occurring within the same anatomic site while maintaining separate boundaries. Pancreatic collision tumors composed of solid pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN) and well-differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor (PanNET) are rare and may be diagnostically challenging because these entities can show overlapping morphologic and immunophenotypic features and may radiographically present as a single lesion. A 41-year-old woman presented with left upper quadrant abdominal pain and was found to have a pancreatic head mass. Fine-needle aspiration favored SPN, and the patient underwent extended pancreaticoduodenectomy. Histologic examination demonstrated a 3.7 cm SPN and an adjacent 0.6 cm World Health Organization grade 1 PanNET, separated by an abrupt interface without appreciable intermingling. Immunohistochemistry showed nuclear β-catenin and cyclin D1 expression in the SPN, while the PanNET showed diffuse chromogranin and INSM1 expression with retained membranous E-cadherin. Synaptophysin was positive in both tumors, highlighting a diagnostic pitfall. Eighteen lymph nodes were negative for metastatic disease, and both tumors were confined to the pancreas. This case represents the fourth reported pancreatic SPN-WDNET collision tumor and highlights important diagnostic and classification challenges in pancreatic neoplasms that demonstrate dual differentiation. Because SPNs may exhibit partial neuroendocrine differentiation, neuroendocrine marker positivity alone should not be used to establish a diagnosis of WDNET in the setting of SPN. Instead, accurate classification requires careful integration of histomorphology and complementary immunophenotypic findings.

Introduction

Collision tumors are uncommon neoplastic entities characterized by the coexistence of two or more histologically and immunophenotypically distinct neoplasms within the same anatomic site, with each component maintaining separate borders and lacking histologic admixture or an intermediate transition zone [1]. They are distinct from combined or composite tumors, which show intermingling of different cellular populations, and from mixed neuroendocrine-non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs), which represent a single neoplasm with both neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine differentiation, typically arising from a common clonal origin [2,3]. Collision tumors have been described in multiple organs, including the skin, gastrointestinal tract, kidney, thyroid, and central nervous system [4-7]. In the pancreas, collision tumors are rare and diagnostically challenging because different pancreatic neoplasms may show overlapping morphologic or immunophenotypic features and may radiographically appear as a single heterogeneous lesion. Reported pancreatic collision tumors include combinations of ductal adenocarcinoma, neuroendocrine tumor, lymphoma, and periampullary malignancies, while collision tumors composed of solid pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN) and pancreatic well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor (WDNET) have been described only rarely [8-14].

SPNs are uncommon low-grade malignant neoplasms that account for approximately 1-3% of pancreatic tumors and predominantly affect young women [15]. Histologically, they are characterized by solid and pseudopapillary architecture, delicate fibrovascular cores, hemorrhagic and pseudocystic degeneration, and discohesive monomorphic cells. Activating CTNNB1 mutations that lead to Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation are considered a defining molecular feature. Immunohistochemically, SPNs typically demonstrate nuclear β-catenin accumulation and are positive for cyclin D1, LEF1, CD10, progesterone receptor, and vimentin, with loss of membranous E-cadherin expression [15].

WDNETs account for approximately 1-5% of pancreatic neoplasms and typically present in the fifth and sixth decades of life [16]. Histologically, they are composed of organoid nests, trabeculae, or gland-like structures separated by delicate fibrovascular septa and demonstrate diffuse expression of neuroendocrine markers, including chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and INSM1 [17]. In contrast to SPNs, WDNETs frequently harbor alterations involving MEN1, DAXX, ATRX, and genes involved in the mTOR signaling pathway [16].

The coexistence of SPN and WDNET presents a particularly challenging diagnostic scenario because SPNs may exhibit partial neuroendocrine differentiation, including synaptophysin expression, which can obscure the presence of a second neoplasm. Consequently, distinction among collision tumors, combined tumors, and MiNENs requires careful integration of morphologic and immunophenotypic findings rather than reliance on individual immunohistochemical markers alone. Herein, we describe a pancreatic collision tumor composed of SPN and WDNET and highlight the diagnostic challenges in distinguishing true collision tumors from other pancreatic neoplasms that demonstrate dual differentiation.

Case presentation

A 41-year-old woman with a five-year history of chronic constipation presented with left upper quadrant abdominal pain. Her constipation had been managed with linaclotide and over-the-counter medications, including bisacodyl and magnesium citrate. Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated a well-circumscribed enhancing mass centered in the pancreatic head/neck, measuring up to 3.9 cm, with partial encasement of the superior mesenteric vein near its confluence with the splenic vein (Fig. 1A-1C). Residual normal pancreatic head parenchyma was present posterior to the mass. Marked to complete fatty atrophy of the pancreatic body and tail was also identified, with no visible residual distal pancreatic parenchyma at the expected anatomic location (Fig. 1D). Subsequent MRI of the abdomen performed using a dedicated pancreatic protocol showed preservation of the ventral pancreas and findings suggestive of partial dorsal pancreatic agenesis, without main pancreatic duct dilation.

One month later, endoscopic ultrasound demonstrated a well-circumscribed, irregular pancreatic neck mass measuring 3.7 × 2.4 cm (Figure 2). The lesion abutted the portal venous confluence and hepatic artery without vascular encasement, luminal irregularity, or stenosis. Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) showed a neoplasm immunoreactive for β-catenin and CD10, with focal synaptophysin and chromogranin expression and negative mucicarmine staining, supporting a diagnosis of solid pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN). Based on these findings, surgical resection was pursued (Figure 3).

Three months after initial imaging, the patient underwent an extended pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure) with en bloc portal vein resection and resection of the extrahepatic bile duct. Intraoperatively, there was no evidence of peritoneal dissemination or serosal involvement. Significant fibrosis surrounded the lesion, and extensive fatty tissue was present in the expected location of the residual distal pancreas.

Gross examination of the extended pancreaticoduodenectomy specimen revealed a 3.7 × 2.6 × 1.8 cm tan-red, focally hemorrhagic solid mass centered within the pancreatic head and closely associated with the pancreatic duct.

Histologic examination demonstrated two distinct neoplastic populations readily identifiable at low magnification on H&E examination (Fig. 4A). At the interface between the tumors, both populations maintained sharp boundaries without appreciable intermingling or transitional morphology, supporting a collision tumor (Fig. 4B). One population consisted of nests and trabeculae of cells separated by delicate fibrous septa (Fig. 4C). At higher magnification, the cells exhibited eosinophilic cytoplasm and round nuclei with finely stippled chromatin, consistent with a well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor (WDNET) (Fig. 4D). The second population demonstrated sheets of discohesive monomorphic polygonal cells associated with areas of hemorrhage (Fig. 4E). Higher magnification revealed eosinophilic-to-clear cytoplasm and characteristic SPN morphology (Fig. 4F).

Immunohistochemical studies further established the distinction between the two tumors. They were most striking at their interface (Fig. 5A). The WDNET retained membranous E-cadherin expression (Fig. 5B) and showed diffuse nuclear INSM1 positivity (Fig. 5C) and diffuse cytoplasmic chromogranin expression (Fig. 5D), all of which were absent in the SPN component. Conversely, the SPN demonstrated diffuse nuclear expression of cyclin D1 (Fig. 5E) and β-catenin (Fig. 5F), whereas the WDNET lacked cyclin D1 expression and retained only membranous/cytoplasmic β-catenin staining. Synaptophysin was positive in both neoplasms, highlighting the potential diagnostic pitfall of relying on a single neuroendocrine marker in this setting (Fig. 6). Additional immunophenotypic differences included strong OSCAR expression in the WDNET and blush positivity only in the SPN. Ki-67 labeling was less than 1% in both tumor populations.

Overall, the resection specimen demonstrated a 3.7-cm SPN and an adjacent 0.6-cm World Health Organization grade 1 WDNET (Ki-67 <1%), both confined to the pancreas. All surgical margins were negative, with the closest margin measuring 0.3 cm (uncinate). Perineural invasion was identified in the SPN component, whereas lymphovascular invasion was absent. Eighteen regional lymph nodes (17 peripancreatic and 1 portal) were negative for metastatic disease (0/18).

The postoperative course was notable for abdominal pain, tenderness, and bloating, managed with multimodal analgesia. She was monitored with serial drain amylase testing and glucose surveillance, with endocrinology consultation for assessment of postoperative pancreatic endocrine function. C-peptide and blood glucose levels were within acceptable ranges, and she did not require exogenous insulin therapy. She was discharged home on postoperative day 5. After discharge, she reported intermittent abdominal cramping and loose yellow stools but was tolerating small amounts of food and nutritional supplements. By the most recent follow-up visit on postoperative day 20, her pain had significantly improved, she was tolerating small frequent meals with adequate protein and fluid intake, her constipation was controlled on a bowel regimen, and her surgical incision was well healed. Given the unusual combination of SPN and well-differentiated PanNET, referral to medical oncology was recommended to discuss surveillance strategies.

Discussion

The principal contribution of the present case extends beyond its rarity and instead highlights the diagnostic challenges involved in distinguishing true collision tumors from other pancreatic neoplasms demonstrating dual differentiation. The central question was whether the lesion represented two independent neoplasms or a single neoplasm with divergent differentiation. Several morphologic features supported classification as a true collision tumor. Histologically, the lesion demonstrated two architecturally distinct neoplasms separated by an abrupt interface, with no appreciable intermingling or transitional morphology. The WDNET exhibited classic organoid growth with nested and trabecular architecture, whereas the SPN demonstrated discohesive monomorphic cells associated with hemorrhagic change. Together, these findings strongly support classification as a true collision tumor [1].

This distinction is particularly important because SPNs frequently exhibit partial neuroendocrine differentiation and may express neuroendocrine markers, including synaptophysin and CD56, which can lead to overdiagnosis of a neuroendocrine neoplasm [15]. Indeed, synaptophysin was positive in both neoplasms in our case. Consequently, neuroendocrine marker positivity alone should not be used to establish a diagnosis of WDNET in the setting of SPN. Rather, accurate classification requires integration of morphologic architecture and complementary immunophenotypic markers. This distinction becomes increasingly important given the diagnostic spectrum of pancreatic tumors with dual differentiation, which includes collision tumors, combined/composite tumors, mixed neuroendocrine-non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs), and amphicrine carcinomas (Table 1). Unlike combined/composite tumors and MiNENs, which arise from a common precursor with divergent differentiation, collision tumors represent independent neoplasms that remain spatially segregated [2,3]. In our case, reciprocal staining patterns between β-catenin, E-cadherin, cyclin D1, INSM1, and chromogranin provided compelling evidence supporting two distinct neoplasms.

Including the present report, only four SPN-WDNET collision tumors have been described [12-14] (Table 2). The reported cases demonstrate substantial clinicopathologic heterogeneity without a consistent demographic, clinical, or radiographic pattern. Interestingly, all three previously reported tumors arose in the pancreatic tail, whereas our case originated in the pancreatic head. Moreover, each prior report emphasized distinct aspects of this entity, including histogenesis, imaging features, and molecular profiling, further underscoring the heterogeneity of these tumors. Collectively, these observations suggest that SPN-WDNET collision tumors cannot be reliably predicted based on demographics, imaging findings, or tumor location and are most often recognized only after complete histopathologic examination.

An additional noteworthy feature of this case was the absence of visible pancreatic body and tail parenchyma and ductal structures on CT and MRI, together with extensive fatty replacement of the expected distal pancreas noted intraoperatively. These findings raise two principal considerations: severe distal pancreatic atrophy with fatty replacement and dorsal pancreatic agenesis. In the absence of definitive ductal characterization or histologic confirmation of the distal pancreatic bed, the underlying mechanism cannot be established with certainty. Dorsal pancreatic agenesis has been sporadically associated with pancreatic neoplasms, including neuroendocrine tumors [18-20]; however, whether the pancreatic anatomy in this case represents a congenital anomaly, acquired atrophy, or an incidental finding remains uncertain.

The pathogenesis of collision tumors remains incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include coincidental synchronous tumorigenesis, shared environmental or microenvironmental influences, paracrine signaling between adjacent neoplasms, and, less likely, differentiation from a common progenitor cell [1]. Earlier reports have favored a multipotent primordial cell hypothesis to explain the coexistence of SPN and WDNET [12]. However, such a mechanism conceptually overlaps with combined or composite tumors. Although definitive conclusions regarding the pathogenesis of SPN-WDNET collision tumors cannot be drawn from a single case, the abrupt morphologic and immunophenotypic segregation observed in our tumor is more supportive of independent tumorigenesis and classification as a true collision tumor. Ultimately, this case emphasizes that accurate diagnosis and classification depend upon careful integration of morphologic architecture and complementary immunophenotypic findings.

Conclusions

We describe a pancreatic collision tumor composed of SPN and WDNET, representing the fourth reported case in the literature. This case highlights important diagnostic challenges associated with pancreatic neoplasms, demonstrating overlapping morphologic and immunophenotypic features. In particular, the propensity of SPNs to exhibit partial neuroendocrine differentiation and express neuroendocrine markers may obscure recognition of a coexisting WDNET and complicate distinction from other pancreatic neoplasms with dual differentiation. Consequently, neuroendocrine marker positivity alone should not be used to establish a diagnosis of WDNET in the setting of SPN. Instead, accurate classification requires careful integration of histomorphology and complementary immunohistochemical findings. As additional cases are reported, a more comprehensive understanding of the clinicopathologic spectrum of SPN-WDNET collision tumors may further refine their classification and biological significance.
