Virtual Overlay Staining With Plasmonic Oligomer Metasurface
Authors: Doeun Kim, Juhwan Kim, Jiyeong Ma, Gyurin Kim, JuHyeong Lee, So Young Yoon, Youngsoo Jun, Young Min Song, Hyeon‐Ho Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe here present a label‐free virtual staining technique using plasmonic oligomer metasurfaces fabricated through a scalable, lithography‐free method. This approach enables morphological analysis of cells with enhanced colorful contrast based on refractive index differences between the cells and their surrounding medium. Unlike conventional methods that rely on molecular dyes and regularly patterned plasmonic structures, our technique employs electrostatic nanoparticle assembly to rapidly create oligomeric plasmonic arrays across an entire wafer. These oligomers provide far‐reaching optical near‐field enhancement, exceeding 30 nm, approximately 10 times greater than that provided by monomeric nanoparticles, allowing for the visualization of cellular components located above the cellular membrane. This advancement not only simplifies the imaging process but also opens new avenues for label‐free cellular imaging, offering significant potential for broader applications in biological imaging and diagnostics.

Introduction

Cells are the basic structural units of life, encapsulated within a membrane‐bound cytoplasm [1]. Within this cytoplasm, a variety of molecules ranging from microscale proteins, DNA, and RNA to nanoscale nutrients and metabolites work in harmony to perform essential functions such as cell growth and apoptosis [2, 3]. Understanding the distribution of these cellular components and their local functions is crucial for elucidating the physiological roles of living organisms and identifying ongoing and potential threats from diseases. For instance, histological analysis of cellular life and death is a key approach in understanding various cancers, often requiring the observation of cellular components within body fluids, which are mostly water [2]. However, a significant challenge arises from the fact that the refractive index of these fluids (n = 1.33) is very similar to that of cellular components [4], rendering cells nearly invisible in this medium.

To overcome this challenge and visualize cellular components against their surrounding medium, various staining methods have been developed using molecular dyes (e.g., fluorescent dye) [5], quantum dots [6], and other agents [7, 8, 9]. Among these, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining is considered the gold standard for cellular diagnosis and is the most widely used technique because it operates under visible light [10]. Hematoxylin stains cell nuclei blue, while eosin stains the extracellular matrix and cytoplasm pink, allowing for clear differentiation of the cell's nuclear and cytoplasmic regions. This method readily facilitates the examination of diverse tissues, such as mammary intraepithelial neoplasia [11], lung adenocarcinoma [5], and bone [12]. However, since staining involves the intentional introduction of foreign substances into the cell, it can potentially distort or lose critical biological components, leading to errors in data analysis [13]. Furthermore, the preparation of H&E‐stained samples typically requires 7–26 h when using formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissues [14], which are necessary to maintain tissue morphology with minimal damage, but cannot capture dynamic processes or preserve native cellular states in living samples [15].

An alternative approach is to utilize plasmonic metasurfaces to visualize the cell with the colorful refractive index contrast while keeping the simple optical system [11]. In contrast to surface plasmon resonance (SPR) [16], which is primarily designed for ensemble‐averaged bulk sensing and typically requires specialized optical configurations such as prism coupling, plasmonic metasurfaces, based on localized surface plasmon resonances (LSPR) [17, 18], enable spatially resolved, wide‐field imaging. These localized modes are highly sensitive to the refractive index in the immediate vicinity of the nanostructures, allowing direct visualization of cellular heterogeneity without complex instrumentation [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. For example, cell invasion of Au nanoparticles enables the virtual color staining of cells without the need for labeling [11, 24, 25]. Similarly, chip‐based plasmonic scattering microscopy visualizes nanoparticles and cellular interfaces by exciting omnidirectional surface plasmons on a gold‐coated substrate [26]. However, the mass production of plasmonic metasurfaces is limited by nanolithography, and additional contrast agents are often required to achieve vivid color contrast, due to the inherently short penetration depth of plasmonic near‐field enhancement, typically below 10 nm [27].

We here present a rapid and simple optical method for visualizing cells and their components using a virtual staining approach with a plasmonic oligomer metasurface. The wafer‐level metasurface is produced through scalable, rapid coating methods that eliminate the need for traditional lithography. In particular, we demonstrate that a metasurface formed with plasmonic oligomers enables far‐reaching optical near‐field enhancement exceeding 30 nm, allowing for sensitive color changes in response to variations in the surrounding refractive index. As a result, this metasurface provides distinct color visualization of cultured animal cells in water, which is otherwise difficult to achieve with existing plasmonic imaging methods.

Results and Discussions

Concept of Virtual Overlay Staining

To illustrate the principle of this virtual staining platform, we describe how a wafer‐scale plasmonic oligomer metasurface generates high spatial resolution with strong contrast from living cells (Figure 1a). Upon white‐light illumination, the metasurface emits strongly enhanced scattering signals, originating from optical near‐field enhancements. Remarkably, this enhanced scattering can be directly captured with a conventional optical microscope, without the need for fluorescence labels or complex optical instrumentation. Because the scattering intensity and spectral position vary sensitively with local refractive index, the recorded color distribution encodes information about the chemical and structural heterogeneity across different subcellular regions (Figure 1b). Consequently, simple color readouts obtained from the camera can be mapped to refractive index variations, providing an optical basis for the virtual identification of cellular components.

Concept of virtual overlay staining. (a) Schematic of cellular imaging using a plasmonic oligomer metasurface. (b) Visualization of four representative cellular components.

The plasmonic oligomer metasurface here consists of metallic nanoparticles (NPs) sitting on a metallic mirror, separated by a dielectric nanospacer (Figure 2a). Gold is chosen as the material for both the NPs and the mirror due to its strong chemical stability and resistance to oxidation in liquids, making it suitable for contact with biomaterials [28]. A key feature of this plasmonic oligomer metasurface is its ability to trap the light within the dielectric nanogap between Au NP and the mirror (Figure 2b‐i), creating strong optical near‐field enhancements, termed ‘hotspots’ [29, 30]. This results in a significant scattering signal, which is sensitive to changes in the refractive indices of both the gap between the Au NP and the mirror, and the environment above the Au NP [20]. However, this near‐field decays rapidly, within ∼3 nm from the Au NP surface (Figure 2c), making it ineffective for probing cellular structures located beyond the ∼10 nm‐thick cellular membrane [31]. Thus, this conventional plasmonic geometry is difficult to reliably detect refractive‐index variations originating from components inside the cell.

To overcome this limitation, we introduce aggregated Au NPs, termed plasmonic oligomers, formed on the mirror (Figure 2b‐ii). The oligomeric geometry introduces additional interparticle hotspots between laterally coupled Au nanoparticles, giving rise to plasmonic modes beyond the vertically confined nanogap resonance. While the nanogap mode is dominated by capacitive coupling between the nanoparticle and the mirror and is largely confined within the sub‐5 nm dielectric spacer beneath the particle, the oligomeric configuration supports additional modes arising from dipole–dipole interactions between neighboring nanoparticles. These lateral coupling effects redistribute the electromagnetic field beyond the confined nanogap region, allowing the enhanced optical near‐field to extend into the surrounding medium above the nanoparticles. As a result, the field is no longer strictly localized beneath the particle but spans a larger interaction volume, contributing to an extended near‐field sensitivity compared to conventional monomeric configurations (Figure 2c). While thinner dielectric gaps further increase optical near‐field penetration (Figure S1), they compromise fabrication robustness. To balance this trade‐off, we adopt a 5 nm spacer, compatible with wafer‐scale production. Importantly, plasmonic oligomers with a uniform solid nanogap exhibit highly consistent visible colors irrespective of their geometric arrangement [32, 33], changing only in response to refractive index variations within the optical near‐field penetration depth of the metasurface.

The plasmonic oligomer metasurface therefore exhibits pronounced and systematic color shifts as a function of the local refractive index (Figure 2d and Figure S2). To mimic the refractive index range of cellular components (1.36 < n < 1.6), we model the metasurface response for media spanning from 1.33 to 1.41, while assuming a cellular membrane (n = 1.45 [34], thickness: 10 nm [31]) covering the surface. The simulated spectra reveal two dominant resonances, i.e., a visible resonance (570–600 nm) arising from nanogap‐dominant coupling between Au NPs and the mirror, and a NIR resonance primarily governed by interparticle coupling within oligomers. Although the NIR mode can exhibit higher refractive index sensitivity, its response is susceptible to variations in oligomer geometry (Figure S3). Thus, we focus on the visible resonance as a robust and reproducible signal for quantitative analysis.

Regardless of the introduction of the artificial membrane, the plasmonic oligomer metasurface exhibits a redshift in the visible resonance, particularly spanning from 576 nm to 581 nm with the artificial cellular membrane, yielding a refractive index sensitivity of 60 nm/RIU (64 nm/RIU for the case without membrane). To complement wavelength shift analysis, we quantify intensity‐based sensitivity using the relative scattering intensity change, given as [35], (1) dIrel=dII0=IRI−I1.33I1.33 where IRI is the scattering intensity at a given refractive index and I 1.33 is the reference intensity in water (n = 1.33). Plotting dIrel vs. refractive index reveals a linear relationship across the biologically relevant refractive index range (Figure 2e). The intensity sensitivity, determined from the slope of the linear fit, reaches 0.65 per RIU at 630 nm, located in the red spectral region. This demonstrates that monitoring changes in red channel scattering intensity alone is sufficient to infer refractive index variations from cellular components positioned beyond the cellular membrane. Such intensity‐based analysis allows more sensitive and nuanced discrimination of subtle refractive index differences across intracellular structures.

Fabrication of Wafer‐Scale Plasmonic Oligomer Metasurface

The key structural concept here is the formation of plasmonic oligomers on the mirror. To achieve this, we introduce a proton‐assisted nanoparticle assembly method, which controls the structural form of the transferred nanoparticles, ranging from monomers to oligomers (Figure 3a) [33]. Chemically synthesized colloidal Au NPs dispersed in a solution containing 0.25 mM trisodium citrate, with proton concentrations ranging from 0 to 2.5 mM HCl, are drop cast onto an HfO2‐coated Au mirror surface and removed after the desired coating time, typically ranging from 1 s to a maximum of 200 s. As proton concentration increases, the transfer rate of the Au NPs onto the surface accelerates, resulting in a proportional increase in surface coverage at a given coating time (Figure 3b). Simultaneously, as the Au NPs become densely arranged (i.e., high surface coverage), the number of monomeric Au NPs decreases rapidly, leading to the formation of various oligomeric Au NP structures (Figure 3c). This rapid coating process, while forming oligomers, is driven by two primary functions of the protons during assembly. Firstly, protons dissociate negatively charged OH groups formed on the surface in water [36], thereby exposing the surface charge of the HfO2, which typically presents a positive surface potential. This positively charged surface then electrostatically attracts the negatively charged Au NPs in solution, leading to rapid nanoparticle coating on the surface. Secondly, as the proton concentration increases (and/or decreases the citrate concentration, i.e., surfactant), the ionic strength also increases. This increased ionic strength reduces the mean interparticle spacing between Au NPs on the surface [37], driving the formation of an array of oligomers across the entire wafer. We therefore use 2.5 mM HCl (i.e., 0.25 mM ionic strength) to enable efficient oligomer assembly within 200 s, facilitating the fabrication of a wafer‐scale plasmonic oligomer metasurface for imaging.

Thanks to the statistically uniform 29% surface coverage (with a 74% oligomer fraction), confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis, the metasurface exhibits highly consistent scattering characteristics across the entire wafer. Using a 2D raster‐scanning method at 0.5 mm intervals, we collect 7845 spectra from the 2‐inch wafer, revealing uniform peak colors at all sampled positions (Figure 3d). The plasmonic resonance peaks appear at 615 nm for the nanogap‐dominant coupling mode and above 810 nm for the interparticle‐dominant coupling mode, showing overall consistency with the simulated spectral features. Minor deviations in peak position arise from the geometric variability of the randomly assembled oligomeric structures and the use of literature‐based optical constants in the simulation. Because the nanogap‐dominant mode produces a smooth and spatially uniform spectral response across the visible range, including the imaging‐relevant red region, it serves as a stable optical readout for quantitative analysis based on relative intensity changes rather than absolute peak positions. Notably, the peak distribution in the red channel (600–630 nm, Figure 3e), extracted from the spatially scanned spectra and fitted with a Gaussian function, exhibits exceptional uniformity (Figure 3f), confirming that the metasurface provides the spectral reproducibility required for reliable imaging applications. In contrast, although the interparticle‐dominant NIR mode offers higher intrinsic refractive‐index sensitivity [17], its strong dependence on local oligomeric forms results in poor spectral reproducibility (Figure S4), limiting its utility for imaging. Given these characteristics, we split the RGB channels of the scattering images and primarily use the red channel, which corresponds to the nanogap‐dominant resonance, as the basis for refractive index imaging across the entire wafer (Figure 3g). This wafer‐scale color uniformity in the visible regime enables the metasurface to reliably sense liquids with varying refractive indices and visualize cellular components without requiring spatial calibration. The high consistency of the red channel response ensures robust, quantitative imaging performance across the full wafer area.

Refractive Index Imaging

The primary function of plasmonic sensing is to recognize and distinguish variations in the refractive index of the surrounding medium [20]. To evaluate this capability under biologically relevant conditions, we sweep the refractive index from 1.33 to 1.391 using urea–water mixtures (8–38 wt.%) [38]. While both resonance‐wavelength shifts and intensity changes can serve as refractive index sensing metrics, intensity‐based detection is more robust and accessible. Wavelength tracking requires a spectrometer and precise alignment, whereas intensity monitoring only requires a dark‐field microscope with a color CMOS camera, allowing instantaneous wide‐field imaging (exposure < 0.03 s, Figure 4a).

For quantitative calibration, scattering images are binned (10 × 10 pixels) to suppress noise while maintaining spatial resolution (Figure S5). Because the metasurface exhibits maximal scattering and sensitivity in the red spectral region, the red channel is isolated for analysis (Figure 4b). To assess sensing robustness, measurements are conducted on both the bare plasmonic oligomer metasurface and a metasurface capped with a 10 nm SiO2 layer mimicking a cellular membrane (Figure 4c and Figure S6). A crucial result is that the metasurface retains its refractive index sensitivity even after membrane capping. Despite the presence of the dielectric overlayer, the scattering spectra exhibit clear resonance shifts relative to water (Figure 4d and Figure S7), and the red channel intensity increases linearly from 1.7% to 2.1% as the refractive index rises (Figure 4e). This confirms that the enhanced optical near‐field generated by plasmonic oligomers penetrates well beyond the membrane layer, enabling the detection of refractive index variations originating from regions above the membrane. The metasurface capped with SiO2 shows a linear increase in normalized red channel intensity (ΔI/I0 ) as a function of refractive index, yielding a sensitivity of ca. 7.9 /RIU (left panel of Figure 4f). Numerical simulations corroborate these findings, demonstrating that the thin membrane layer does not diminish sensing fidelity, instead, the locally elevated refractive index slightly enhances scattering. Because the red channel intensity varies linearly with refractive index, a single‐color image can be directly converted into a quantitative refractive index map of objects on the metasurface. For example, mitochondria (n ≈ 1.40–1.42) correspond to ΔI/I0 ∼ 0.71, enabling selective visualization without labeling or spectral scanning (right panel of Figure 4f).

To experimentally evaluate the effective optical near‐field probing range enabled by the plasmonic oligomer geometry, we deposit SiO2 overlayers with stepwise thicknesses ranging from 30 to 90 nm on the metasurface (Figure 4g and Figure S8) and measure the corresponding refractive‐index sensitivity (Figure 4h and Figure S9). The sensitivity exhibits an exponential decay with increasing SiO2 thickness, decreasing from 8.35/RIU at 10 nm to 1.82 /RIU at 90 nm (Figure 4i), consistent with the evanescent nature of the plasmonic near field. By defining the effective probing depth as the thickness at which the sensitivity drops to 1/e of its initial value (at 10 nm), we obtain a characteristic decay length of approximately 36 nm (Figure 4j). This experimentally derived value is smaller than the near‐field extension (>100 nm) predicted by simulation, which can be attributed to the difference between idealized near‐field calculations and realistic far‐field measurements from non‐ideal nanostructures. Nevertheless, the result confirms that the plasmonic oligomer metasurface maintains measurable sensitivity well beyond the immediate nanogap region, indicating an extended interaction volume compared to conventional monomer‐dominated configurations.

Such an extended probing capability enables refractive index sensing even in the presence of an intervening dielectric layer, supporting its applicability to cellular environments where intracellular features are separated from the surface by a membrane barrier. While the present calibration is based on homogeneous dielectric layers and does not fully capture the complexity of intracellular heterogeneity, the plasmonic response is governed by localized near‐field interactions that primarily probe the effective refractive index in the immediate vicinity of the metasurface. Therefore, rather than assigning absolute refractive index values to specific intracellular components, our analysis focuses on relative variations within a biologically relevant refractive index range, which enables robust visualization and segmentation of intracellular features. Consistent with prior refractive index‐based bioimaging approaches, [1, 39] this strategy avoids overinterpretation while preserving meaningful contrast for cellular imaging.

Living Cell Imaging

Having established that the plasmonic oligomer metasurface provides a linear and membrane–permeable refractive index response, we next apply this capability to visualize living cells without labels. COS‐7 cells [40, 41], derived from African green monkey kidney fibroblasts, are imaged under low‐ and high‐magnification objectives to evaluate how refractive index variations within a cell translate into spatial contrast on the metasurface. At low magnification, only cell boundaries are readily distinguished due to the large refractive index discontinuity at the membrane–medium interface (Figure S10). Internal contrast remains limited. Under high magnification, however, lateral illumination couples more efficiently into the metasurface, enabling finer sampling of intracellular refractive index variations (left panel of Figure 5a). This produces markedly enhanced spatial contrast, particularly in the red channel (right panel of Figure 5a), which serves as a quantitative readout for mapping refractive index across the cell (Figure 5b). Using the previously calibrated relationship of relative intensity change (ΔI/I0 ) vs. refractive index (illustrated in Figure 4f), the red channel intensity distribution can be converted into a pixel‐wise refractive index map, allowing intracellular components to be segmented based on their characteristic refractive indices (Figure 5c). A representative COS‐7 cell can thus be divided into (1) nucleus (n∼1.345–1.36), (2) cytosol (1.36–1.38), which occupies most of the intracellular volume, (3) mitochondria‐rich regions (1.40–1.42), (4) lipid droplets (1.42–1.50), and (5) surrounding aqueous medium (1.33), with all values assigned based on literature‐reported ranges [34, 40].

We further validate the mitochondrial segmentation by statistically comparing our label‐free scattering results with fluorescence‐stained control samples (Figure 5d and Figure S11). Because fluorescent dyes can modify the optical properties of cellular components, including their refractive index [42], direct comparison of the same cell using both scattering and fluorescence imaging is not strictly equivalent. Therefore, mitochondrial area fractions are compared statistically across independently prepared samples (N = 35 cells for scattering and N = 52 cells for fluorescence imaging). In scattering images, mitochondria are identified by their characteristic refractive index range (n ≈ 1.40–1.42), whereas fluorescence images define mitochondria by signal area. Fluorescence microscopy intrinsically broadens apparent organelle size due to diffraction and the three‐dimensional point‐spread function of emitted light [43], which spreads the signal beyond true mitochondrial boundaries. The fluorescence‐derived areas are therefore calibrated using known mitochondrial dimensions (Figure S12) [44]. In contrast, the scattering signal originates from plasmonic hotspots on the metasurface and reflects refractive index modulation rather than volumetric light emission, yielding a more localized, surface‐weighted contrast that is less prone to systematic area overestimation [26]. After calibration, the mitochondrial area fractions measured by scattering imaging and fluorescence microscopy show good agreement, with average values of 8.0 ± 7.6% (N = 35 cells) and 7.6 ± 1.8% (N = 52 cells), respectively (Figure 5e). This close correspondence confirms that the plasmonic scattering approach reliably captures mitochondrial distributions at the single‐cell level while avoiding the volumetric blurring inherent to fluorescence imaging.

Dynamic intracellular processes are also resolved in our system (Figure 5f). Within the cytoplasm, we observe the motion of high‐index particles (n∼1.52) with characteristic sizes of 0.8 µm and an estimated diffusion coefficient of ∼0.135 µm2/s, exhibiting trajectories that are consistent with confined Brownian or anomalous sub‐diffusive motion typically associated with organelle–cytoskeleton interactions (Figure 5g). The elevated refractive index and size range observed here are consistent with those of lipid droplets [40, 45], which are known to accumulate in metabolically active cells. Their continuous displacement within the cytoplasmic matrix, captured without exogenous labeling, demonstrates the capability of this metasurface‐enabled imaging approach to resolve dynamic subcellular events in real time. This observation not only confirms the sensitivity of the platform to nanoscale refractive index heterogeneities but also highlights its potential utility for probing metabolic flux [46], lipid storage dynamics [47], and intracellular transport phenomena in living cells [48].

While further investigation across diverse cell types needs to be warranted, our method provides clear advantages in both imaging depth and operational simplicity, particularly in visualizing structures located beyond the plasma membrane, a capability that remains difficult to achieve using conventional plasmonic imaging approaches. Moreover, this virtual staining strategy holds the potential to streamline, or even bypass, several labor‐intensive steps in the standard H&E workflow, including hydration and dehydration cycles, double staining, differentiation, bluing, and multiple washing steps, thereby offering a substantially simplified and reagent‐free alternative for routine cellular analysis.

Conclusions

We demonstrate a facile approach to virtually staining biological cells by using plasmonic oligomer metasurfaces. This method leverages electrostatic coating to rapidly assemble oligomeric nanoparticles across an entire wafer, providing a scalable and efficient alternative to traditional nanolithography techniques. The resulting plasmonic oligomer metasurfaces offer significant optical near‐field enhancement, extending beyond 30 nm, which allows for the detailed visualization of cellular components, including nuclei, cytoplasmic compartments, and high‐index lipid inclusions within COS‐7 cells, surpassing the penetration limits of conventional plasmonic probes.

This approach substantially reduces experimental complexity as it eliminates molecular dyes, bypasses multi‐step sample processing, and requires only a standard dark‐field optical microscope to achieve high contrast, quantitative refractive index imaging. The capability to visualize subcellular architecture and dynamic intracellular processes in real time further highlights the broad utility of this platform. Given its simplicity, scalability, and compatibility with living cell environments, this virtual staining method holds significant promise for diverse biological and diagnostic applications, including label‐free pathology, live‐cell phenotyping, metabolic activity mapping, drug‐response profiling, high‐throughput cytometry, and early disease detection [49, 50]. While this approach does not provide molecular‐level specificity (e.g., vibrational fingerprints), such imaging can guide targeted Raman measurements for subsequent molecular‐level analysis, establishing a synergistic workflow for multiscale cellular characterization [51]. Moreover, integration with machine‐learning‐based image analysis could enable automated classification, virtual re‐staining, and predictive modeling [52], positioning this technology as a versatile foundation for next‐generation, non‐invasive cellular imaging.

Experimental Section

Numerical Simulation

The optical response of the plasmonic oligomer metasurface was simulated using finite‐difference time‐domain (FDTD) calculation software (Lumerical Solutions). The dielectric functions of gold [53] and HfO2 were adopted from the literature [54]. A random distribution of 50 nm Au NPs with 38% surface coverage (30 particles in 0.25 µm2 square) was modeled on a gold film, separated by a 5 nm thick dielectric HfO2 layer. A linearly‐polarized, scattered‐field white light (ranging from 400 nm to 1000 nm) was used to illuminate the film, with the propagation direction parallel to the film surface. For sensing performance, a 10 nm thick SiO2 layer was added as an artificial cellular membrane on top of the Au NPs while varying the medium refractive index from 1.33 to 1.41.

Fabrication of Plasmonic Oligomer Metasurface

The plasmonic oligomer metasurface was fabricated starting with the growth of a 150 nm thick gold mirror on a 2‐inch silicon wafer using an electron beam evaporator, operating at a base pressure of 4 × 10−5 Torr and a deposition rate of 0.05 nm/s. Subsequently, a 5 nm thick HfO2 layer was deposited on the Au mirror using the same electron beam evaporator at a growth rate of 0.01 nm/s. Lastly, the Au NPs were transferred onto the mirror via proton‐assisted nanoparticle assembly [33]. Prior to the coating, a colloidal solution of 50 nm Au NPs was synthesized by the Turkevich method [55]. Briefly, 100 mL of 0.25 mM sodium tetrachloroaurate (III) dihydrate (NaAuCl4, Sigma‐Aldrich) was heated to 100°C. Then, 0.45 mL of 4.85 mM sodium citrate (Na3C6H5O7, Sigma‐Aldrich) was added and stirred for 20 min, followed by slow cooling to room temperature. The resulting colloidal Au NPs were concentrated to 0.1 vol.% by using a centrifuge (model 5425R, Eppendorf) and dispersed in 0.25 mM trisodium citrate solution, with proton concentration controlled by adding 0–2.5 mM HCl. For nanoparticle coating, the colloidal solution was drop‐cast onto the HfO2 surface and removed after a specified processing time (1–200 s) using an air gun. The number densities (surface coverages) and structural forms of the assembled Au NPs were quantified from scanning electron microscopic (SEM, JSM‐7500F, JEOL) images acquired from three representative positions of each sample.

Optical measurement and imaging

Scattered light from the plasmonic metasurface was measured using an optical microscope (Olympus BX53) integrated with a fiber optic spectrometer (Ocean Optics QE Pro) and a true‐color CMOS camera (Sentech, STC‐MCS500U3V). A halogen lamp (PHILIPS 7724, 100 W) served as a white light source throughout the entire measurement. The plasmonic scattering signals were measured using a 5× objective lens across the 2‐inch plasmonic oligomer metasurface with a 2D raster scanning method at 0.5 mm intervals, resulting in a total of 7845 scanned spots. The peak wavelengths for each position were then determined by assigning the highest local maximum values of the filtered signals within the range of 600 nm to 800 nm using MATLAB's findpeaks function. Refractive index imaging of the plasmonic metasurface with urea‐water mixture or COS‐7 cells was conducted using a 50× objective (Olympus MPLFLN‐BD, NA 0.8). Original image size was 2048 × 2448 pixels, and only the central region with 300 × 300 pixels was cropped for uniformity, which corresponds to about 34 × 34 µm2 . Cropped images were binned by 10 pixels to a pixel, resulting in 30 × 30 pixels. The red channel was then separated from these images for calibration of the refractive index value from the red channel intensity. Fluorescence imaging was conducted with an inverted optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti‐U) integrated with a CMOS camera (Nikon DS‐Qi2), equipped with a Nikon Plan Apo 40×, NA 0.8 objective.

Cell Culture and Preparation

COS‐7 cells (African green monkey kidney fibroblast–derived) were maintained in standard culture conditions at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, using Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, high glucose) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. For experiments involving the plasmonic oligomer metasurfaces, cells were dissociated with 0.05% Trypsin–EDTA and resuspended in complete medium before seeding. These cells were seeded directly onto the substrates (∼1 × 104 cells per substrate) immediately upon retrieval by the user. In addition, we prepared pre‐growth cells, defined as cells plated immediately after thawing. For these samples, freshly thawed COS‐7 cells were washed, resuspended in complete medium, and directly seeded onto the metasurface without an intermediate expansion step. During live‐cell imaging, the culture medium was replaced with HEPES‐buffered PBS (20 mM HEPES‐KOH, pH 7.2) to maintain physiological pH in the absence of CO2 buffering. Cells were maintained in this buffer throughout the imaging period to preserve viability. To facilitate live‐cell imaging, metasurface wafers with attached cells were mounted on a standard slide glass. Double‐sided tape was applied around the cell‐attached area to form a simple, sealed imaging chamber. Care was taken to avoid leakage and to maintain a medium volume sufficient to sustain cells during the imaging period. For the staining experiment, cells were mixed with MitoTracker dyes (Thermo Fisher, M7512) at a concentration of 50 nM for 30 min and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min.
