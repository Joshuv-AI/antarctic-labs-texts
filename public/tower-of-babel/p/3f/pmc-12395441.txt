Introduction

Cecal volvulus is a rare cause of intestinal obstruction, accounting for only 1–2 % of all large bowel obstructions. It has an average incidence of 2.8–7.1 per million people per year [1,2]. Based on the underlying pathophysiological mechanism, cecal volvulus is classified into three established types: the axial torsion type (type 1), the loop type (type 2), and the cecal bascule type (type 3). Types 1 and 2 account for 80 % of all cases, while the cecal bascule (type 3), the rarest type, comprises about 5–20 % of the cases. Cecal bascules typically occur in patients aged 40–70 years, with a slight male predominance [2].

Cecal bascules occur when the cecum folds upward and anteriorly over the horizontal axis, resembling a seesaw motion. The term ‘bascule’ means ‘seesaw’ in French. The folded lumen creates a potentially obstructing site for the passage of colonic contents [3,4]. If an ileocecal valve is competent, cecal bascule can evolve to a closed-loop obstruction. The bowel then distends with metabolic gas from colonic bacteria and becomes ischemic due to compromised arterial supply and venous congestion and may become perforated or gangrenous [5].

The diagnosis of cecal bascule is not straightforward due to its rarity and nonspecific presentation. It can understandably be misdiagnosed as cecal volvulus; however, emphasis is placed on the differing physical mechanisms of the two distinct surgical conditions [3]. In this case report, we will discuss the diagnosis and management challenge of a 40-year-old male patient who presented with a distal small bowel obstruction secondary to cecal bascules. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of a cecal bascule from Africa. A thorough literature search was conducted using PubMed, Google Scholar, major open-access case report journals, and available local academic repositories, but no previous cases from Africa were identified. We have reported this case report in line with the SCARE criteria [17].

Case presentation

A 40-year-old male presented with a 3-day history of crampy periumbilical abdominal pain, followed by five episodes of bilious vomiting, inability to pass feces and flatus, and progressive abdominal distention. He also complained of intermittent, crampy abdominal pain over the last few years. He had no history of a previous abdominal surgery.

On physical examination, his vital signs were stable except for the pulse rate: blood pressure (BP) = 110/72 mmHg, pulse rate = 112 beats per minute, respiratory rate = 20 breaths/min, and temperature = 37.3 degrees Celsius (°C). The abdomen was distended with hyperactive bowel sounds, hypertympanic on percussion, and showed deep tenderness in the right lower quadrant. Digital rectal examination revealed an empty rectum without mass or blood.

Laboratory investigations showed WBC of 9753/μL, hemoglobin of 14 g/dL, and platelet count of 189,000/μL. An abdominal X-ray revealed distended small bowel loops and multiple air-fluid levels with a dilated cecum in the right lumbar region of the abdomen (Fig. 1). Nasogastric decompression drained 400 mL of feculent gastrointestinal (GI) content. Based on clinical and radiologic findings, a preoperative diagnosis of acute abdomen due to distal small bowel obstruction was made, and the patient was prepared for exploratory laparotomy. Computed tomography (CT) scan was not considered due to limited availability, and the diagnosis of acute mechanical small bowel obstruction is primarily based on clinical evaluation and abdominal X-ray findings in our setting.

Management

Following informed consent, the patient underwent a laparotomy. Intraoperatively, approximately 250 mL of reactive peritoneal fluid was noted. The cecum was found to be distended but viable. It was folded superiorly, with its whole anterior surface—including the tinea coli—extensively adhered to the anterior part of the ascending colon (Fig. 2). Additionally, the small bowel was dilated above the bascule, and the colon below collapsed.

The left image shows a distended but viable cecal bascule, whereas the right image demonstrates the cecum following the release of adhesions.

Surgical procedures performed include aspiration of reactive fluid, release of cecal adhesion (bascule), right hemicolectomy, and ileotransverse anastomosis. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged in stable condition on the seventh postoperative day following clinical improvement. The follow-ups after discharge were also uneventful.

Discussion

Treves initially described cecal bascule in 1899, and later, in 1938, Weinstein provided a more detailed clinical and radiographic explanation [2,5,6]. A cecal bascule is characterized by the bending of the mobile cecum anteriorly and superiorly over the distal ascending colon (Fig. 3). Although the cecum may become gangrenous, there is no major vessel occlusion. Some authors exclude this condition from being a cecal volvulus, as it is not a true volvulus [7].

Bascule typically arises due to mobile cecum, which results from congenital incomplete or absent fixation of the right colic mesocolon to the retroperitoneum or from abnormally long mesenteric attachment. Approximately 25 % of the general population have cecal mobility [2,10]. It may also be acquired, occurring following abdominal surgery such as after right colon mobilization [8]. Furthermore, most authors identify congenital or acquired adhesion as a key etiological factor, as it can cause abnormal attachment of the anterior surface of the cecum to the anterior ascending colon. Additional coexisting predisposing factors that may contribute to cecal distention and/or upward displacement—leading to cecal bascule—comprise high dietary fiber intake, chronic constipation, pregnancy, colonoscopy, paralytic ileus, distal colonic obstruction, or Ogilvie syndrome [9]. The risk factor identified in our patient included a mobile cecum, which was not fixed to the retroperitoneum, as well as adhesion between the anterior cecum and the anterior ascending colon.

The anterior rotation of the posterior portion of the cecum creates a flap valve mechanism that hinders the cecum from emptying its contents. In cases where the ileocecal valve is competent, cecal bascule may progress to a closed-loop obstruction, as the cecum is unable to decompress into the ascending colon. The bowel then distends with metabolic gas from colonic bacteria and becomes ischemic due to compromised arterial supply and venous congestion and may become perforated or gangrenous [11].

Cecal bascule is a challenging condition to diagnose. Its clinical manifestations can resemble those of bowel obstruction and have a broad differential diagnosis. Therefore, a high index of suspicion is important in order to avoid delays in diagnosis and prevent potential complications. The presenting symptoms often include periumbilical abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, abdominal distention, and constipation [4]. These patients may experience intermittent symptoms, as the cecum can flop back into anatomical position and temporarily relieve the obstruction. Abdominal distension with tympanism and initially increased bowel sounds are common findings. As the clinical condition progresses, the bowel sounds may diminish as a result of impaired peristalsis. In addition, other signs of peritoneal irritation may appear when there is vascular compromise, gangrene, or perforation. However, unlike in cecal volvulus, these ischemic complications are rare in cecal bascule, as there is no associated vascular torsion [11,12].

Leukocytosis with left shift and elevated acute-phase reactants can be observed. An abdominal X-ray is the first-line diagnostic modality and may show the presence of air-fluid levels with dilation of the small bowel loops. Marked distension of the cecum, which often appears as an “inverted teardrop”, is usually visible and displaced towards the right hypochondrium [2,13]. CT scans are diagnostic in 60 to 90 % of the patients [2,5]. Classically, findings include upward kinking of the cecum without torsion, along with displacement of an ileocecal valve and terminal ileum into the right upper quadrant [8,11]. CT also allows identification of complications such as pneumatosis intestinalis, pneumoperitoneum, and mesenteric stranding, which may indicate perforation or strangulation. Contrast enemas demonstrate a diagnostic accuracy approaching 90 % and may also provide therapeutic benefit by relieving the obstruction. A cecal bascule appears as a gas-filled cecum projecting anterior to the contrast-filled ascending colon, often displaying an identifiable fold [5,12].

In our clinical case, the large bowel dilation noted preoperatively on x-ray was not initially interpreted as cecal dilation, as cecal bascule was a rare case. The presence of dilated small bowel loops and centrally located multiple air-fluid levels on radiography, along with symptoms of small intestine obstruction, led us to make the diagnosis of small bowel obstruction—most likely secondary to small bowel volvulus, based on regional epidemiological patterns. Furthermore, various literature demonstrated that X-ray findings only enable diagnosis in less than 20 % of cases, with a definitive diagnosis confirmed during an exploratory laparotomy in a significant number of cases [2,13].

Surgical intervention is the preferred treatment for cecal bascule, as conservative treatment has a failure rate that approaches 95 % [5,14]. In some reported cases, nonoperative management that involves nasogastric, contrast enema, and/or colonoscopic decompression has been successfully employed. It may be tried for temporary measures and in patients who are unfit for surgery [15].

Surgical treatment options comprise ileocolectomy, release of the cecum with cecopexy, and decompressive cecostomy tube. The gold standard is to perform a right hemicolectomy or ileocecal resection, as these have an almost zero recurrence rate due to the removal of the mobile cecum [5,11,13]. There is a lack of studies that directly compare ileocecal resection with right hemicolectomy in the context of cecal bascule, which highlights an important gap in the literature and a potential area for future studies. In this case report, the authors propose that ileocecal resection might be considered when only the cecum and terminal ileum are involved and when preservation of bowel length is crucial. Nevertheless, right hemicolectomy with ileotransverse anastomosis is the preferred technique in most literature, with acceptable morbidity and mortality. If the patient's general condition does not allow an anastomosis, an ileostomy is recommended. The morbidity and mortality rates are lower with right hemicolectomy than with other techniques, although the mortality rate can be as high as 22 % in some literature [2,7,12,13]. Some authors even suggest laparoscopic cecopexy [2,9]. However, cecopexy presents high recurrence and mortality rates. Cecostomy presents fewer recurrences but has a morbidity and mortality rate of 40 % [5,11,16].

Conclusions

Cecal bascule is a rare form of cecal volvulus, and its etiology is multifactorial. Preoperative diagnosis is challenging. Enhancing awareness and promoting familiarity among surgical health professionals is essential to make early diagnoses and improve clinical outcomes. Delay in diagnosis or management of cecal bascule may lead to serious complications, such as colonic ischemia, perforation, and fulminant sepsis. An ectopic cecal dilation on imaging should prompt consideration of this diagnosis. A CT scan is the imaging modality of choice for suspected cases of cecal bascule. Timely intervention is crucial to prevent complications. The best surgical treatment of cecal bascule depends on bowel viability, the concomitant presence of a mobile ascending colon, patient hemodynamic stability, risk of recurrence, surgeon experience, and institutional guidelines. However, Ileocolectomy is generally considered the preferred surgical approach.
