Authorship and citation trends in dementia research: A path to equitable career development for scientists

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWomen are increasingly entering the dementia research workforce, but they frequently fail to attain senior leadership positions in academia. Discrepancies in academic research outputs were investigated to guide equity‐focused policy recommendations.METHODSBibliometrics was conducted on 400,482 original research articles (2003–2022) followed by surveying the AD/ADRD research community, to quantitatively and qualitatively evaluate discrepancies in publication and authorship trends. In addition, an unsupervised learning algorithm was developed to analyze underlying relationships between author groups.RESULTSMale author gender was consistently predictive of better publication and citation metrics across all bibliometrics outcomes, with senior male authors outperforming all other subgroups and fewer senior female authors in most research areas. Female survey respondents reported more barriers, but comparable productivity and citation strategies as male authors.DISCUSSIONMulti‐pronged approaches by stakeholders across institutions, funders, and journals are necessary to provide career support for early‐career scientists working to transition to senior research roles.

BACKGROUND

Science and technology have advanced at a rapidly accelerating pace in recent decades, yet progress toward achieving gender equity in academia has been markedly slower. 1 Within dementia research, disparities in representation and recognition remain evident despite growing awareness of these inequities. 1 Although women make up a substantial proportion of the dementia workforce, particularly in clinical, nursing, and caregiving domains, their visibility and participation decline at more senior career stages and in certain subfields. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Evidence indicates that gender bias in authorship, funding allocation, and leadership positions continues to shape the structure and culture of dementia research, influencing who gains recognition and access to advancement opportunities. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 For instance, despite comparable or even greater female participation in major conferences such as the Alzheimer's Association International Conference (AAIC), publication and authorship patterns remain skewed toward male researchers. 3 Collectively, these findings suggest that gender disparities in dementia research extend beyond mere differences in participation rates, reflecting deeper structural inequities in authorship credit, visibility, and career progression.

Gender disparities in citation patterns remain a persistent and underrecognized form of structural bias in scientific publishing. Papers authored by women receive fewer citations than those authored by men, even after adjusting for factors such as journal impact factor, publication year, topic, and collaboration size. 2 , 6 This disparity contributes to selective and cumulative disadvantages in visibility, funding, and career advancement for women scientists. In the dementia field, articles with female first or last authors are less cited than expected given equivalent publication characteristics (such as impact factor of journal), and this bias has widened over time. 2 This imbalance may be driven by many factors, such as differential citation practices among male authors. 7 , 8 Given that citation metrics are routinely used to evaluate research impact and career progress, these disparities may risk women researchers’ recognition, retention, and advancement in academic careers.

Beyond general patterns of gender biases in academic productivity, nuanced disparities emerge when examining how representation and recognition vary across research fields and career stages. One contributing factor is the career stage–dependent declines in the proportion of female scientists, which often reflect barriers to advancement such as unequal access to mentorship, networking opportunities, and leadership positions. 9 Structural factors—including implicit bias in hiring and promotion practices, sexism, uneven distribution of caregiving responsibilities, and limited institutional support for work–life balance—further exacerbate attrition among women as they progress through the academic pipeline. 4 , 10 To foster more equitable recognition and visibility in the field, recent proposals advocate for inclusive citation practices, such as authors and journals auditing reference lists for gender, publishing citation diversity statements, increasing gender diversity among editorial boards and reviewers, and reforming funding policies. 11 , 12 , 13 Incorporating such practices into Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD) research could represent a concrete step toward a fair and accurate evaluation of scientific contributions.

Addressing these imbalances requires a systematic examination of how institutional practices, disciplinary cultures, and evaluation criteria shape gendered trajectories across the dementia research landscape. This study aimed to elucidate authorship and citation gender biases in AD/ADRD research subfields through quantitative bibliometrics, machine learning methods, and to explore the factors underpinning these via qualitative methods. Based on prior research, we anticipated fewer female authors publishing in mechanistic, translational, and clinical research and higher numbers of female authors publishing in psychosocial research focused on caregiving, lifestyle factors, and prevention. 2 We expected that male authors hold more senior author positions and also have higher publication and citation counts compared to female authors. 3 , 6 We also expected that women face disproportionally more systemic and structural barriers, such as difficulties in obtaining grant funding, lack of institutional support and mentorship that hinder productivity. 9 , 10 A clearer understanding of the persistence and potential amplification of gender imbalances in publication and citation behavior is vital to encourage the research community to implement meaningful corrective measures.

METHODS

To study the publication and citation trends in the AD/ADRD research landscape, we adopted a mixed methods approach where we combined complementary qualitative and quantitative methods to comprehensively characterize the complex relationships between gender, author seniority, research area, and research output metrics (specifically as measured by publications and citations). Critically, we have used an explanatory sequential approach where we first extracted bibliometric data for quantitative analysis and then used qualitative approaches to add context to the interpretation. 14 As recommended for such methodological approaches, we have adhered to the standards for each individual component and integrated findings from all individual components as best as our methods allowed. 14 The overall workflow for our approach is illustrated in Figure 1.

Data extraction & disease ontology classification

Members of the AWARE Women Authorship and Citation Trends workgroup surveyed the publication landscape using Web of Science (WoS) (A Clarivate product). 15 The Common Alzheimer's and Related Dementias Research Ontology (CADRO, version 2.0), 16 a three‐tiered classification system developed by the National Institute on Aging and the Alzheimer's Association was the framework used to organize and compare basic, translational and clinical AD/ADRD research publications and to generate keyword searches (Figure 2 and Table 1). We focused on comparisons between CADRO categories for our analysis, but data were extracted and organized using the full three‐tiered schema of CADRO ontology (shown in Supplementary Material S1).

For each research area, peer‐reviewed original research articles were surveyed using keyword searches such as “Alzheimer's dementia AND tau”. To capture original research outputs, searches were restricted to the following types of articles: ‘original research’, ‘data articles’, ‘conference proceedings’, and ‘early access original research’. Book chapters, reviews, commentaries, and editorials were excluded. Articles published within a span of 20 years, between January 1, 2003, and December 31, 2022, were selected to reflect the growing global investment in dementia research. Key data about selected articles for each research area (complete author list, article title, journal, author affiliations, year of publication, PubMed ID, doi, number of citations, keywords, corresponding author details, and funding) were selected for export. Once consolidated, the data were processed to remove articles with missing/incomplete data, and articles where authors’ first names were not available (e.g., author first names listed as initials) were also excluded using Python's Groupby Method. 17 , 18 The search algorithms for all the CADRO categories are listed in Table 1.

Gender classification and accuracy thresholding

The processed datasets for the five broad CADRO categories (Figure 2 and Table 1) were then fed into the Gender‐API (Application Programming Interface) algorithm. 19 Gender API was selected due to its high classification accuracy. 20 , 21 Powered by artificial intelligence (AI), Gender‐API classifies gender (male, female, or unknown) as well as providing a confidence/ accuracy score for that classification. A score of 50 indicates that the name is equally likely to be used by a man or a woman, while a score of 100 signals that the algorithm is certain of the gender classification of that name. A second metric of confidence was the number of samples of data entries that the API's database used to determine the gender. A small percentage of names (e.g., first names that were hyphenated) were not classified by the algorithm and these articles were excluded from further analysis. These data and classifications were obtained for both the first author and the last author (proxy for author seniority) for all the publications in each research category.

Once gender was obtained for all the publications, two separate files were generated for each CADRO category to perform publication and citation analyses for both first and last author (author seniority). We performed our initial analyses in three stages. First, using all the names that were profiled regardless of the accuracy of the gender classification (no threshold). Next, we applied a high threshold of 90, to only include publications where the algorithm was certain of the gender classification. However, names that the AI algorithm has not been previously exposed to, usually names of individuals from minority communities, typically receive a lower confidence score. By using a high threshold, we biased the datasets towards primarily Anglo‐Saxon authors. Therefore, we used the variance of the whole dataset, as measured by our unbiased approach (see section 2.4), to determine a fair threshold that was inclusive of names but removed those classifications that were low accuracy/confidence from the dataset. This threshold was set at 74.

Bibliometric analysis

Citation profiling

Publication, citation, and normalized citation trends for gender classified publications were extracted using National Institute of Health's (NIH's) iCite tool. 22 , 23 iCite provides a dashboard of bibliometrics for papers associated with a portfolio, in this case as dictated by the CADRO categories. PubMed ID (PMIDs) for the articles in both first and last author datasets for all CADRO categories were entered into the iCite platform and group level metrics were obtained. These metrics included total publication and citation counts, publications/year, and standard errors for summary metrics. Group level citation and publication data were extracted from iCite to compare relative citation ratios (RCRs). 24 RCR is calculated by using each article's co‐citation network, to field‐ and time‐ normalize the number of citations it has received. By doing so, RCR incorporates a customizable benchmarking feature that relates field‐ and time‐ normalized citations from one group to the performance of a comparison group, for example, citations for articles in different research portfolios. iCite also ignores repeats of PMIDs, so cross‐comparisons across CADRO categories are not biased by duplication.

Statistical plan

Statistical tests were performed using JMP (Version 17.0.0). Various total and normalized publication and citation metrics were compared based on gender of the author (male × female), by author seniority (first × last author) and by CADRO Categories (A, B, C, D, E). Summary statistics and analysis of variance (ANOVA) are reported for interaction effects, and whenever statistical significance was found, post‐hoc power was assessed.

Sources of data loss

From the initial WoS extraction to the citation profiling through iCite, several lines of data were excluded due to the following reasons: (i) first name reported as an initial; (ii) duplications of articles were removed, for example, an article that reports on mechanism of amyloid beta and tau might have been pulled for both CADRO A.1 and A.2, therefore, duplicates were removed; (iii) article PMIDs unavailable for the iCite analysis pipeline, and (iv) missing publication information such as year of publication.

To mitigate the effects of data loss, as well as to deeply investigate our dataset, in addition to investigating our a priori hypotheses, we employed unbiased methods to surface relationships within our dataset.

Unbiased approach

Optimized model selection

An unsupervised clustering approach, Mapper‐Topological Data Analysis (TDA) was applied to the full dataset. 25 For each CADRO category, there were two datasets, one for the first author gender information, and another with the last author gender information. Labels used to train the model were: the CADRO category (A, B, C, D & E), times cited (WoS core), publication year, gender, and gender classification accuracy across all authors (first and last). We tried different overlap parameters (0.25, 0.3, 0.4) within Mapper for selecting an optimized model to fit the input data. The goal of the model was to classify the data into “clusters” of related data points, with each data point indicating a row of information related to an author (first or last), a specific publication, publication year, gender, gender classification accuracy, and citations. After conducting a sanity check to assess cluster variation, we selected a model with an overlap parameter of 0.3 for further analysis due to its stable variance. We denoted this selected model as TDA0.3. Once the clusters were reported by the model, the best and worst performing clusters were analyzed by gender to investigate the differences in citations.

Working principle

The input data were first normalized to ensure that all features contributed equally to the clustering process. This normalization is standard in unsupervised learning workflows and is essential when dealing with raw datasets containing features on different scales. The normalized data matrix was then indexed appropriately to preserve interpretability across downstream analysis. In constructing the Mapper graph, the original normalized feature space directly guided the clustering process, without dimensionality reduction or transformation. As a result, the colors assigned to clusters in the Mapper visualization reflect the overall magnitude of feature values in the original data space. Further details about this approach can be found in Supplementary Materials S.2.

Mathematical formulation of accuracy threshold

To calculate the variance‐based threshold for bibliometrics (described in section 2.2), we used the stratification of data to calculate the variance of each cluster. The proportion was then calculated as cluster's size/total size.

Stratified Weighted Sum (SWS) = Σ (j = 1: n) ((cluster j accuracy/ sqrt (cluster j variance)) * cluster j proportion), where j represents individual clusters and n represents total number of clusters.

The value of the SWS, 73.95, established a novel data‐driven approach to set the accuracy threshold for inclusion of gender classifications.

Input from the dementia research community: Intermissions

Input was solicited periodically from the larger dementia community on the key challenges and opportunities for gender equity. This was done by hosting two in‐person intermission sessions during the annual AAIC held in 2023 (in Amsterdam, Netherlands) and 2024 (in Philadelphia, PA, USA), with participation open to all conference attendees. The preliminary results of the working group were shared with the attendees of both intermission sessions, followed by discussions led by the intermission leaders (representatives of this working group). These discussions were documented by the intermission leaders and collected as field notes to inform subsequent analyses.

Input from the dementia research community: Survey

Survey Rationale and Description: A short, 5‐minute voluntary and anonymous survey was conducted to understand the reasons behind the disparities in publications and citations in AD/ADRD research areas. To develop the survey, members of the Working Group (WG) who collected field notes from the intermissions worked with working group chairs and developed a survey sub‐group. The finalized version of the survey was developed by this subgroup (which included experts in survey development). The survey was then submitted for approval from the ISTAART committee and for ethics approval. In the survey, demographic information was collected (including gender, country of birth and work location, career stage and area of research), publication and citation productivity, personal strategies for publication, and institutional support for research outputs. A copy of the full survey can be found as Supplementary Material S.3. The survey was conducted in the English language via Qualtrics, and promoted widely through the ISTAART newsletter, emails to known networks of the working group, and on social media (X, LinkedIn) from February to May 2025. This research was exempted from ethical review by the Western Sydney University Human Research Ethics Committee (EX H16355). Survey data were then exported to Excel and incomplete responses were removed. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the survey responses. Parametric and non‐parametric tests were used to assess differences in the distributions of responses between men and women.

Methodological gaps that limit interpretability

While we have used a mixed methods approach to understand the relationship between gender and research “success” as measured by publications and citations, methods have limitations. These are summarized below.

Disclaimer on mixed terminology

This report employs mixed terminology related to gender due to differences in data sources and methodological approaches. Quantitative analyses relied on gender classification using Gender API, which categorized authors as “male”, “female”, or “unknown” based on first names. These categories reflect algorithmic classifications and do not correspond to individuals’ genders. In contrast, the qualitative components of the study used self‐reported gender identity categories (“man”, “woman”, “non‐binary”), which align more closely with current best practices for inclusive and person‐centered gender measurement. 26 As a result, the terminology used in this manuscript varies by methodological context. Findings should be interpreted with an understanding of these distinctions.

Using gender API

In a previous study, the overall accuracy of Gender API was shown to be 96.1% with 2.7% incorrect gender predictions. 21 By using a threshold of 74, our dataset still preferentially excluded non–Anglo‐Saxon names, and by setting a less stringent threshold incorrect gender classifications are possibly included in our dataset.

Using RCR

Articles do not receive an RCR value until 2 years after publication. 24 Since this analysis was conducted in 2023, all the results reported in the RCR analysis (Figure 3D) are for articles published between 2002 and 2021 for this analysis.

Using raw citations in TDA unsupervised method

The “citations” used for our unsupervised clustering approach were raw citations extracted from WoS and were not time‐ or field‐normalized. Older articles, therefore, have had more time to accumulate citations than more recent articles, and subcategories of research topics may have had much higher citations across all researchers. 24

Survey limitations

Respondents of our survey were overwhelmingly women which limited our ability to make more nuanced comparisons across gender, investigate the gender × seniority responses, and draw generalizable conclusions.

Other gaps in this report

Recommendations of the dementia community (collected during the AAIC intermissions, Sections 2.7 & 3.4) were used to craft the survey. Items (i)–(iv) were used in the design of the survey (Supplementary Item 3). However, most of the suggestions listed in (v) fell outside the scope of the current manuscript; these remain important questions to address the core need for improving equity in research environments for minority researchers, and some would be worthy questions to ask using deeper analyses of our dataset or other similar ones.

RESULTS

Data extraction and bibliometrics analytics

Summary of data extraction and bibliometrics are reported in Table 1. After publication and citation profiling, bibliometric “success” outcomes were analyzed by gender (Figure 3A–C).

Publication and citation trends from iCite profiling

Mean RCR was analyzed as the main output of these analyses, as a time‐ and field‐ normalized metric of research impact. 22 , 24 Mean RCR values for CADRO datasets (with publications excluding gender categorizations lower than an accuracy of 74) are shown in Figure 3D.

Factorial ANOVAs were conducted to evaluate the effects of author seniority (first, last), CADRO category (A, B, C, D, E), and gender (male, female), as well as relevant interactions on the dependent variables below (total publication counts, total citation counts, and mean RCR). Statistical analyses were performed on the datasets with the Gender API accuracy threshold of 74.

Total publication counts

The model was statistically significant (F(11, 8) = 20.72, p = 0.0001) with R2 (adjusted) = 0.92 and root mean square error (RMSE) = 4092.5 for a mean of 19,084.5. Gender was a significant predictor (F(1, 8) = 22.70, p = 0.0014), with male authors (LS Means = 23,444.4) publishing significantly more than female author (LS Means = 14,724.5) across all the CADRO categories and author seniority levels (mean difference = 8,719.9 (95% confidence interval [CI] [4,499.5, 12,940.4])). CADRO category was also significant (F(4, 8) = 43.87, p < 0.0001), with post‐hoc Tukey's tests showing that CADRO category A had significantly higher publications (LS Mean = 40,916) compared to CADRO categories C (LS Mean = 10,951.5) and D (LS Mean = 7,155). While author seniority was not statistically significant (F(1, 8) = 0.0023, p = 0.9630), a significant interaction effect between author seniority and gender was found (F(1, 8) = 10.82, p = 0.0110), with male last authors publishing the most (LS Mean = 26,411), and female last authors publishing the least (LS Mean = 11,670.4). Additionally, the CADRO × Gender interaction was significant (F(4, 8) = 4.74, p = 0.0296) with CADRO A males publishing the most (LS Mean = 52,101) and CADRO D females publishing the least (LS Mean = 6,946.5). Post‐hoc power analyses indicated that the model was well powered to detect gender and CADRO effects (power = 0.99 and 1.00 respectively).

Total citation counts

The model was statistically significant (F(11, 8) = 4.0717, p = 0.028), with an R2 (adjusted) = 0.64 and RMSE = 297,046.6 and a mean of 620,197.4. Author gender was significantly associated with citation count (F(1, 8) = 9.79, p = .014), with male (LS Mean = 827,970.7) authors being cited significantly more than female (LS Mean = 412,424.1) authors (mean difference = 415,546.6 citations (95% CI [109,209.4, 721,883.8]). CADRO category also predicted differences in citation counts (F(4, 8) = 6.3493, p < 0.0133). Tukey's post hoc tests also indicated that CADRO A had the highest citations (LS Mean = 1,145,219.5) and CADRO D had the lowest (LS Mean = 280,825.5). Author seniority (F(1, 8) = 1.9344, p = 0.20) and author seniority × gender interaction (F(1, 8) = 1.7742, p = 0.23) were not significant. The CADRO × gender interaction was also not significant (F(4, 8) = 1.4744, p = 0.29). Post‐hoc power analyses indicated that the model was robustly powered for detecting gender (power = 0.78) and CADRO (power = 0.87) effects.

Mean RCR

Overall, the model was statistically significant F(11, 8) = 3.87, p = 0.0325, with R2 (adjusted) = 0.62 and RMSE = 0.1012 for a mean of 1.9184. Gender was significantly associated with mean RCR (F(1, 8) = 29.09, p = 0.0007), with male (LS Mean = 2.04) authors demonstrating significantly higher RCR than females (LS Mean = 1.79), with a mean difference of 0.2442 (95% CI [0.1398, 0.3486]). Post‐hoc power analysis indicated high power for the gender effect (0.9967). CADRO category (F(4, 8) = 3.08, p = 0.08), author seniority (F(1, 8) = 0.0845, p = 0.78), author seniority × gender interaction (F(1, 8) = 0.036, p = 0.85), and the CADRO × gender interaction (F(4, 8) = 0.26, p = 0.89) were not significant.

Overall

Excluding low confidence names (less than 90% Gender API accuracy threshold), affected the male/ female distribution of articles in some research areas (such as CADRO category E) more strongly than other categories (such as CADRO category A). In statistical analyses of total citations, total publications, and mean RCR, author gender consistently emerged as a significant predictor of research output. Our statistical analysis of bibliometrics spanned articles published in dementia research areas over a span of 20 years (2002–2022). The overall gender differences observed in total publication and citation rates are large over this cumulative period. However, our RCR analysis, which is a time‐ and field normalized metric, also showed a significant difference in publication impact by gender. While the main effect of author seniority was not always a significant predictor, the gender × seniority effect emerged in some cases, where senior male authors were more successful than all female authors.

In‐person intermissions

The dementia research community (average attendance of 30 researchers at the AAIC intermission sessions each year) was supportive of the bibliometrics and data‐driven analyses performed by the working group as well as conducting a survey to elicit reasons for inequity. The discussion of the intermissions broadly covered the following topics:

i. Improving research practices at the laboratory and institution levels: These may include improving transparency in hiring, promotion, contribution criteria for publication author order, developing institutional equity plans, and ensuring equitable access to resources.

ii. Improving funding opportunities for women and minority researchers: These may include developing specific funding schemes for women, reporting requirements for funding agencies on demographics of funded researchers and implementing double blind review processes to decrease identity‐based bias (race and gender) in grant schemes.

iii. Improving publishing and editorial practices towards equity: These may include providing opportunities (such as fee‐waivers) for scientists (including women and minority researchers) to publish in open‐access and high‐impact journals, implementing double‐blind review processes for publication, improving representation of women in editorial boards, and setting editorial policies that prioritize equity.

iv. Improving research environments and culture towards equity: These may include encouraging women to scientific careers through early education, improving citation practices for publications led by female authors to reflect actual scientific output, and addressing implicit biases discouraging women in scientific endeavors.

v. Future directions for this research: Recommendations for additional analyses that fell outside the scope of the current manuscript, but important questions to address in future manuscripts were discussed. These include temporal analyses of areas in which female authors have grown over time, geographical analyses of similar bibliometrics across high‐ and low‐ income countries, improved methods for capturing disparities for non–Anglo‐Saxon named authors, tracking outputs across impact factor of journals, and developing methods to track gender identity of authors as opposed to assuming gender based on names.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we analyzed the relationship between author gender and bibliometrics, as well as the influence of career stage and AD/ADRD research area on this relationship. In addition, we explored the modifiable factors that contribute to the observed disparities. To achieve this, we pursued a mixed methods approach where we used quantitative bibliometrics followed by qualitative techniques to understand the causes for disparities observed.

Male authors outperform female authors across all CADRO categories

Our bibliometric analyses revealed consistent gender differences across publication and citation metrics, with male authors outperforming female authors across all CADRO categories. While looking across the AD/ADRD research areas, a more nuanced pattern was reported. Across the technical core of dementia research inclusive of CADRO categories A (Molecular Pathogenesis & Physiology), B (Diagnosis, Assessment & Disease Monitoring), and C (Translational Research & Clinical Interventions), a consistent pattern emerged: female first authorship approached parity, yet senior (last) authorship remained persistently male‐dominated, reflecting a broad trend where the pool of junior female researchers has grown but face a sharp decline in senior roles. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 In category A, where overall productivity and citation impact are highest, male senior authors achieve the strongest performance metrics. Categories B and C further underline this pattern, with male senior authors producing substantially higher publication counts and achieving higher RCRs than female authors. These biomarker and translational categories are particularly sensitive to the female authorship gap, further evidenced by women filing vastly fewer patent applications than men. 32 The path from discovery to clinical validation is inherently marked by roadblocks. 33 Overcoming these requires team science efforts, creative problem solving, securing large‐scale funding, navigating complex regulatory pipelines, and establishing collaborations. These crucial processes are often limited by lack of diversity in scientific teams. Our findings illustrate that, in the most technically intensive and high‐impact areas of dementia research, senior leadership remains disproportionately male, signaling a structural bottleneck that limits women's advancement precisely at the stage where influence, resources, and recognition consolidate.

Interestingly, the gender divide in senior‐authored publication percentages was less pronounced in CADRO category D (Population Studies) and E (Dementia Care and Impact of disease). In category D, gender disparities were also highly sensitive to gender‐classification certainty thresholds, suggesting that an ethnically diverse authorship pool may mask or mitigate gender differentials. It needs to be highlighted that categories D and E were the least prolific and least cited domains, with substantially fewer publications and citations than other categories, despite the vital importance of disparities, care, and quality of life to the dementia experience. These findings indicate that both topic (care‐ and impact‐focused research) and gender (women as lead/senior authors) are structurally undervalued in current academic reward systems. As a result, the evidence base that informs guidelines and policy is disproportionately driven by male senior investigators in domains where fewer women are advancing, while “feminized” areas such as care, psychosocial interventions, and equity remain underfunded and under‐cited. This perpetuates gender inequities in career progression, risks skewing the field away from patient‐ and caregiver‐centered priorities, and limits innovation in dementia care models, caregiver support, and real‐world implementation. Correcting these imbalances will require targeted investment in categories D and E and explicit recognition of care and equity outcomes in funding.

Our main findings were corroborated by our analyses using unsupervised machine learning methods, which demonstrated that the “best” male author clusters vastly outperform the “best” female author clusters in citations. Consistent with our findings, prior studies show that composition of author groups reflect specific patterns of connectivity, influence, and bias, 34 , 35 , 36 and that scientific authorship networks exhibit homophily and high clustering. 37 , 38 , 39 Successful author groups may be less likely to challenge the structures that support their visibility. 37 , 40 Increasing awareness, challenging assumptions, and incentivizing scientists to expand and diversify their scientific networks will aid in mobilizing resources, fostering collaborations across clusters, and supporting innovation. 41 , 42

Women report greater structural barriers to success

Our qualitative data collection was focused on further understanding the reasons behind these disparities and identifying modifiable factors to improve equity in career progression for dementia researchers, via AAIC intermissions and a survey. Survey respondents indicated that although men and women reported employing similar personal strategies to enhance the visibility of their publications and overall publication productivity appeared comparable across genders, women reported facing more barriers related to editorial rejections, heavier time commitments (such as from participation in committees and mentoring responsibilities), as well as limitations in research resources compared to men.

Interpreting the connected results between our qualitative and quantitative data demonstrate that while surface‐level productivity indicators may be similar across genders, women face more structural constraints. Structural inequities in dementia research pose a risk to the field's efficacy, since loss of female perspectives and leadership may result in a research ecosystem that overlooks sex and gender‐specific variables that are essential for treating a disease that disproportionately affects women. 43 , 44 , 45 Furthermore, applying the Health Disparities Research Framework to analyze aging research studies highlights the complexity of addressing disparities and underscores the need for diverse research teams to eliminate structural barriers to health equity in AD/ADRD. 46

Several studies report that women leave academia (“the Leaky Pipeline”) 47 due to structural barriers such as unsafe or unsupportive workplaces (facing sexual harassment and discrimination), 48 , 49 disproportionate caregiving/ personal life demands, 50 , 51 and reduced training, mentorship and promotion opportunities. 52 , 53 , 54 Social and structural systems favor men, with female scientists facing unequal pay and funding disparities. 55 , 56 While more women are entering academia, nuanced analyses have consistently demonstrated that they are disproportionately hired into positions with limited opportunities for career advancement and face more barriers to research productivity. 57 , 58

Other factors that influence publication visibility are related to journal and editorial practices, resource and funding opportunities, as well as regional and national policies. While our analyses did not directly quantify these factors, they are discussed based on our intermission outcomes to add context and propose impactful solutions. Data from journals show that double‐blind review favors increased female authorship in journals. 59 Similar results were obtained for mitigation of racial bias when grant outcomes were examined after blinding for author identity. 60 In addition, the median amount of time that female‐authored research articles spend in review is 7.4–14.6% longer than male authored articles. 61 The citation gap is also driven by the impact factor of the journal, with high impact journals including disproportionately more articles with men as both first and last authors. 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 Another study suggested that promoting gender diversity in editorial teams and reviewers has a positive impact on review outcomes. 66 These studies demonstrate that female authored publications incur higher criticism and scrutiny, which can be addressed using a double‐blind review process masking or obscuring the gender and identity of authors. Given their central roles in academic careers, journals are analyzing their publications and editorial board compositions, encouraging citation diversity statements, and presenting female authors with platforms to publish their research via special issues and collections. 67 , 68 , 69 Funding plays a critical role in career progression. To reduce the undue influence of a researcher's reputation, the US NIH have moved towards a binary classification of “sufficient”/“insufficient” to evaluate investigators’ expertise and resources in the simplified review framework implemented in 2025. 70 Using multi‐pronged approaches to improve research environments and laboratory cultures, provide systems to support equitable career advancement, make institutions safer for women, and re‐evaluate funding, promotion and tenure criteria to be more inclusive can therefore help decrease this gender gap in bibliometrics. 71

CONCLUSIONS

Our results highlight that gender asymmetries in publications and citations depend on the area of research and career stage. Structural factors preferentially favor visibility for male senior authors. However, our working group identified that several of these present as modifiable factors and recommended reforms to increase retention of female researchers in academia. Key factors required to promote gender equity in career progression include a multi‐pronged approach, with buy‐in from stakeholders across academia, funders, journals, and policy‐makers. Implementing these policy shifts is a necessary step toward the retention of women in dementia research, deploying team science approaches with diverse perspectives, and ultimately finding paths to prevent and treat AD/ADRD.
