miR156-StSPL9 regulating the biosynthesis of flavonoids and secondary metabolites in potato tubers
Authors: Nan Li, Hushuai Nie, Xiaojuan Wu, Peijie Wang, Yu Ma, Enze Yang, Juan Wu, Zhicheng Zhang, Rui Xie, Dan Wang, Yanhong Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The colored potatoes exhibit enhanced nutritional value due to their rich flavonoid content. Flavonoids are a class of compounds that confer a wide range of colors to plants, significantly influencing the color and quality of various plant species. Elucidating the mechanisms underlying flavonoid biosynthesis will provide important insights applicable to enhancing flavonoid accumulation in potato varieties. Through transient expression assays, we confirmed that StSPL9 is a target of miR156a. Functional studies revealed that miR156a overexpression reduces flavonoid content, a phenotype reversed by STTM-miR156a. Expanding on this, integrated multi-omics analyses demonstrated that the miR156a-StSPL9 module plays a systematic regulatory role in the biosynthesis pathways of flavonoids and anthocyanins in potato tubers. These results suggest that key downstream flavonoid pathway genes (e.g., CHS2, F3H, HST, CHS1A, BEATH, CHIL2, F3′5'H, FGGT1, and U79B6) may be regulated by miR156a-StSPL9 module, potentially contributing to the increased accumulation of hesperetin, kaempferol, and quercetin in potato tubers. By elucidating miR156a-StSPL9 regulatory module, this work provides novel insights into the metabolic networks of Solanum species and thereby establishes a foundation for the enhancement of nutritional quality in potatoes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08897-w.

Introduction

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) is the third most cultivated food crop globally. It provides essential nutrients, including carbohydrates, vitamins, and minerals, such as potassium, magnesium, and iron, which enhance the body's immunity. Potatoes contain diverse bioactive compounds, including flavones, phenolic acids, alkaloids, and terpenoids [1, 2]. Colored potatoes are rich in flavonoids, a class of secondary metabolites ubiquitously distributed in nature. Flavonoids are one of the primary plant pigments, contributing to a wide range of colors in plants, including anthocyanins (red, orange, blue, and purple), chalcones and aurones (yellow), and flavonols and flavones (white and yellowish) [3]. These compounds play crucial roles in plant growth and development, such as regulating pollen fertility, seed germination, and stress responses [4]. Additionally, flavonoids exhibit significant antioxidant, anti- inflammatory, and anticancer activities, making them beneficial for human health. In recent years, advances in metabolomics and transcriptomics have greatly enhanced our understanding of flavonoid biosynthesis and its regulatory mechanisms. Flavones (apigenin and luteolin) and flavonols (quercetin, galangin, kaempferol, and myricetin) are key branch products in the flavonoid metabolic pathway. Flavones are synthesized from dihydroflavones catalyzed by flavone synthase (FNS), while flavonols are produced from dihydroflavonols through the action of flavonol synthase (FLS). Beyond their role as co-pigments of anthocyanins, flavonols also function in ultraviolet (UV) light absorption and pollinator attraction. Anthocyanins are phenolic water-soluble compounds in certain fruits, red or purple-blue vegetables, grains, and tubers. Although anthocyanins are present in small amounts in the human diet, numerous studies have demonstrated their health advantages, suggesting they may help prevent various chronic diseases [5]. Research indicates that anthocyanins can be effective in preventing metabolic disorders and microbial infections, including cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes [6]. Additionally, they may improve conditions such as obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and blood glucose abnormalities [7].

Color changes are readily observable during plant growth and development, particularly during the ripening of fruits and the senescence of leaves. These alterations in color significantly impact the nutritional and quality attributes of crops, especially those that produce tubers and fruits. Flavonoids and anthocyanin-rich colored potatoes play an important role in enhancing health and helping to prevent cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. With the improvement of living standards, the nutritional demands for food are gradually increasing. Up to now, although certain transcription regulators, plant hormones, and radiation have been shown to influence pigment accumulation in potato tubers [8, 9], our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms governing flavonoid and anthocyanin metabolism in colored potato tubers remains limited [10–13]. Since the potato is a homologous tetraploid crop capable of asexual reproduction and is sensitive to genetic homogeneity, it is essential to investigate the mechanisms regulating anthocyanin production through the genetic improvement of colored potato varieties. The biosynthetic genes and regulatory factors associated with anthocyanin production have been extensively studied in the context of fruit color change [14].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of endogenous, non-coding small RNAs (19-23nt) that primarily regulate the expression of target genes at the post-transcriptional level, playing a crucial role in plant development [15]. Among these, miR156 is one of the most highly conserved miRNA families in plants. Research has shown that the miR156/SPL module plays a vital role in plant freezing tolerance [16], drought tolerance [17], the accumulation of reactive oxygen species, and the salicylic acid signaling pathway [18]. Furthermore, miR156 delays the vegetative phase transition of plants by controlling its target gene SPL (squamous promoter binding protein-like) [19]. It also influences leaf growth and number [20], inhibits tillering and branching [21], affects fruit ripening [22], regulates the number of lateral roots [23], and impacts floral organ development, inflorescence development, and plant fertility [24].

The miR156/SPL regulatory network governing flavonoids and anthocyanin accumulation has been recognized in multiple species, including Arabidopsis [25, 26]. Overexpression of miR156 was found to decrease the expression of its target SPL genes, resulting in delayed flowering in plants overexpressing miR156. The MBW complex is widely involved in the regulation of secondary metabolite synthesis in plants, especially flavonoids and anthocyanin biosynthesis. bHLH (Transparent testa 8, TT8), MYB (Production of anthocyanin pigment 1, PAP1) and, WD40 repeat protein (Transparent testa glabra 1, TTG1) are the main components of the MBW complex. Among them, bHLH is mainly involved in the biosynthesis of flavonoids and anthocyanins, and MYB transcription factors mainly activate the expression of flavonoid and anthocyanin biosynthesis related genes [27]. The miR156 targeted SPL9 transcription factor competes with TT8 for binding to PAP1, thereby interfering with the MBW complex transcription complex and inhibiting the expression of dihydroflavonol-4-reductase (DFR), a key gene in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, and thus suppressing anthocyanin synthesis [27]. Additionally, Csn-miR156d regulates flowering and anthocyanin accumulation in tea plants by suppressing the expression of CsSPL1 [28]. In 'Suri' pear (Pyrus pyrifolia Nakai), the miR156/SPL module inhibits anthocyanin accumulation by suppressing the expression of PpMYB10 [29]. In grapes, miR156/SPLs are involved in forming anthocyanin biosynthesis by responding to phytohormone signals such as ABA, MeJA, and GA [30]. In peach (Prunus persica), PpSPL1 can reduce the expression level of PpMYB10.1 [31].

In addition, miR156 is involved in the regulation of flavonoid and anthocyanin accumulation in Arabidopsis, blueberry, and other plants [32–35]. Although the molecular mechanism by which miR156/SPL regulates anthocyanin accumulation has been widely studied, the synthesis of flavonoids and phenolic acids regulated by miR156/SPL in potato tubers remains poorly understood. The miR156a and its target gene StSPL9were previously identified through small RNA sequencing, degradome sequencing, and transient expression assays [36]. In this study, we functionally characterized miR156a and StSPL9 in potato. Our findings indicate that the miR156a and StSPL9 regulate the pigmentation of potato tubers. Notably, our study revealed that the miR156a/StSPL9 module serves as a crucial upstream regulatory factor, potentially influencing several candidate genes in the flavonoids and anthocyanins biosynthesis pathways, including CHS2, F3H, CHS1A, CHIL2, F3′5'H, FGGT1, among others. These results reveal a novel regulatory network mediated by the miR156a-StSPL9 module, which plays a crucial role in the synchronized regulation of flavonoids and anthocyanins accumulation in potato tubers.

Results

In vitro validation of miR156a and its target gene StSPL9

In our previous research, we performed small RNA sequencing, degradome sequencing, and transcriptome sequencing on purple potato variety ‘Huasong66’ during different developmental stages. The expression of miR156a increased during the growth period, and the target gene was predicted to be StSPL9, with the binding site located between the 1000th and 1500th base of the target gene (Fig. 1A, Wu et al. 2022). To further verify that miR156a targets StSPL9, the transient expression assay via Agrobacterium tumefaciens in tobacco leaves was performed. We injected OE-StSPL9-mGFP5 and the combination of OE-StSPL9-mGFP5 + OE-miR156a- mGFP5 into the leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana respectively (Fig. 1B). Confocal microscopy showed the strongest fluorescence in tobacco leaves injected with only OE-StSPL9-mGFP5, while weaker fluorescence was observed in leaves co-injected with OE-StSPL9-mGFP5 and OE-miR156a-mGFP5 (Fig. 1C). The expression of mGFP5 in OE-StSPL9 was significantly higher than in those leaves co-injected with OE-StSPL9-mGFP5 + OE-miR156a-mGFP5(Fig. 1C). miR156a regulatory controls flavonoid biosynthesis in potato

To investigate the biological function of miR156a, we constructed two genetic vectors: a P35S promoter-driven miR156a silencing vector (pBWA(V)KS-miR156a) using STTM (short tandem target mimic) technology, and a 35S CaMV promoter-driven overexpression vector (pCAMBIA1302-miR156a) (Fig S1A). Through Agrobacterium-mediated genetic transformation, we obtained 12 independent STTM-miR156a transgenic lines and 13 OE-miR156a lines in potato. PCR identification was conducted using the STTM-35seq-F/NOSseq-R and pCAMBIA1302-miR156a-F/R specific primers, respectively (Table S1). In the STTM-miR156a transgenic line, an amplification product of approximately 238 bp was observed, while a product of about 423 bp was amplified in the OE-miR156a transgenic line. No amplification product was detected in the non-transgenic control line or the blank control (Fig S1B-C).

We harvested transgenic potato tubers, including WT, STTM-miR156a (10, 12, 13), and OE-miR156a (7, 10, 12) lines. At the morphological level, the STTM-miR156a transgenic tubers exhibited significantly lighter pigmentation, and the tubers of OE-miR156a transgenic lines exhibited a distinct purple hue (Fig. 2A), The OE-miR156a plants showed significantly darker leaf color compared to the STTM-miR156a plants (Fig. 2B). At the physiological level, compared with the WT, the flavonoid content in STTM-miR156a transgenic potato tubers increased by 1.9–2.8 folds, while that in OE-miR156a tubers decreased to 0.7–0.9 folds of the WT level (Fig. 2C). The anthocyanin content in STTM-miR156a transgenic potato tubers was significantly reduced to 0.5–0.7 folds, while that in OE-miR156a tubers was significantly increased to 1.7–2.0 folds (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that miR156a plays a regulatory role in flavonoid and anthocyanin accumulation in potato. The relative expression of miR156a in STTM-miR156a and OE-miR156a transgenic lines exhibited opposite patterns. In STTM-miR156a transgenic potato leaves miR156a expression decreased to 0.45–0.62 folds, and in tubers, in decreased to 0.2–0.5 folds of the WT level (Fig. 2E). In STTM-miR156a leaves, StSPL9 expression was upregulated 3.0–4.3 folds compared with WT, whereas in OE-miR156a leaves, it decreased 1.8–3.0 folds (Fig. 2F). In STTM-miR156a tubers, StSPL9 expression increased 1.4–3.6 folds, whereas in OE-miR156a tubers, it decreased 1.4–12.2 folds (Fig. 2F). Based on phenotypic and physiological indicators, significant differences were observed between the STTM-miR156a and OE-miR156a transgenic potato tubers. Moreover, the trends in flavonoid and anthocyanin content were consistent in the campatisons of WT vs OE-StSPL9 and WT vs STTM-miR156a, indicating that miR156a negatively regulates its target gene StSPL9, thereby suppressing flavonoid accumulation while promoting anthocyanin biosynthesis.

Expression profiling of differentially expressed genes following miR156a silencing and overexpression

Compared with WT, RNA-seq analysis of transgenic potato plants expressing STTM-miR156a and OE-miR156a identified 154 and 4,693 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), respectively. Of these, 103 and 2,458 DEGs were up-regulated, and 51 and 2,235 DEGs were down-regulated in the STTM-miR156a and OE-miR156a transgenic potato plants, respectively, suggesting that more genes were up-regulated than down-regulated in both lines (Fig. 3A). The DEGs from the comparisons WT vs STTM-miR156a and WT vs OE-miR156a were classified into 44 and 138 KEGG terms, repectively (Fig S2 A-B). Most of the pathways enriched in the WT vs STTM-miR156a included Metabolic pathways (ko01100), Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110), Flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941), Phenyl propanoid biosynthesis (ko00940) and Starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500) (Fig. 3B). In the KEGG pathway analysis of WT vs OE-miR156a, DEGs were mainly enriched in Metabolic pathways (ko01100), Biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110), Plant-pathogen interaction (ko04626), Plant hormone signal transduction (ko04075), MAPK signaling pathway-plant (ko04016), Ribosome (ko03010), Carbon metabolism (ko01200), and Starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500) (Fig. 3B). Differentially abundant metabolomic following miR156a silencing

Given the conserved function of miR156a and its interaction with flavonoid biosynthesis and metabolism in potatoes, we selected WT and STTM-miR156a samples for further metabolomics analysis. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), accounting for 42.7% and 21.12% of the total variance, respectively, clearly separated the six biological replicates into distinct WT and STTM-miR156a clusters. The metabolites were categorized into two groups, which exhibited a clear separation and notable differences between the two genotypes. The samples within each group exhibited good reproducibility (Fig. 4A). The results derived from the OPLS-DA analysis (Fig. 4B) were used for subsequent model testing according to the differential metabolite screening criteria established in this study. In total, 152 differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were observed in the transgenic plants, comprising 62 upregulated and 90 downregulated DAMs (Fig. 4C). KEGG and MetMap analysis of the DAMs in STTM-miR156a transgenic potatoes indicated that these metabolites were predominantly enriched in various metabolic pathways, ABC transporters, and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, in contrast to the WT (Fig. 4D). Through integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis, eight DEGs regulating these metabolites were identified: CHS2, F3H, HST, BEATH, CHIL2, F3′5'H, FGGT1, and LOC102600944 (U79B6, UDP-glycosyltransferase 79B6). Using JASPAR, the binding motif of StSPL9 was predicted to be GTAC, and each of the eight DEGs was found to contain 8 to 18 GTAC core sequences (Fig. 5D and Table S4). Based on this bioinformatic prediction, we hypothesize that StSPL9 may be involved in regulating flavonoid accumulation; however, direct experimental evidence (e.g., promoter binding assays) is required to confirm this hypothesis.

Discussion

Colored potato varieties, with their skins and tubers rich in flavonoids and anthocyanins, offer humans protection against oxidants, free radicals, and LDL cholesterol. The antioxidant activity of potato tubers primarily comes from flavonoids, anthocyanins, and phenolic acids [42].

miR156 negatively regulates flavonoid biosynthesis in potato

miR156 is a highly conserved microRNA family that plays a critical role in regulating plant growth, development, and stress responses by targeting SPL transcription factors [43]. To date, miR156 has been identified and functionally characterized in a wide range of plant species, including tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) [44], maize (Zea mays) [45], and apple (Malus domestica) [46]. The functional roles of miR156 family members display considerable variation among different species. miR156 has been shown to play important roles in regulating a wide range of biological processes, such as drought stress responses [47], leaf and lateral branch development [48], floral transition [49], and anthocyanin biosynthesis [28]. The conserved yet species-specific regulatory mechanisms of miR156a highlight its crucial role in plant growth, development, and environmental adaptation. In this study, we successfully generated STTM-miR156a and OE-miR156a transgenic potato tubers. Phenotypic observations revealed that STTM-miR156a tubers exhibited a distinct light purple coloration, whereas OE-miR156a tubers displayed a pronounced dark purple hue. Physiological analyses of the transgenic tubers demonstrated that STTM-miR156a plants had significantly higher flavonoid content but lower anthocyanin content compared to the WT controls. In contrast, OE-miR156a plants showed the opposite trend, with elevated flavonoid content and reduced anthocyanin accumulation (Fig. 2C-2D). Previous studies indicated that the miR156 is widely involved in the accumulation of plant flavonoids and anthocyanins. Guo demonstrated that the overexpression of miR156b in grape cells increased anthocyanin content, while STTM-miR156b produced the opposite phenotype [33]. The reports indicate that miR156 is involved in the regulation of anthocyanin, flavone, and flavonol accumulation [34]. Xia performed integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses on three differently colored cultivars of R. pulchrum Sweet, namely 'Baihe', 'Fenhe', and 'Zihe', and found that high levels of kaempferol and quercetin derivatives accumulated in 'Baihe', resulting in the formation of white petals [50]. Xiao conducted widely targeted metabolomic analyses on fruits of four cultivars, including Green ‘Gaoligongshan’ (group G), yellow–red ‘Fujian No. 2’ (group R), pink ‘Qiu Hong’ (group BH), and red ‘Xia Zi’ (group CH) and demonstrated that delphinidin-3-O-glucoside is the key anthocyanin contributing to anthocyanin accumulation [51]. Qin performed transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses on safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.) with three distinct flower colors (red, yellow, and white), and revealed that white flowers accumulated high levels of colorless flavonoids such as apigenin, kaempferol, and naringenin [52]. In the present study, STTM-miR156a transgenic potato tubers showed significantly increased contents of flavonoid-related metabolites including hesperetin, kaempferol, and quercetin, and exhibited an obvious white appearance. By contrast, no significant changes were detected in the contents of anthocyanin-related metabolites. These results indicate that miR156a positively regulates flavonoid biosynthesis but negatively regulates anthocyanin accumulation in potato tubers.

StSPL9 positively regulates flavonoid biosynthesis in potato

SPL transcription factors are a family of proteins with a highly conserved SBP-box domain unique to photosynthetic organisms [53] and play an important role in plant growth, development, and stress tolerance [54]. Up to now, the SPL gene family members have been identified in various plants, including potato, blueberry, Vaccinium corymbosum, Nicotiana tabacum, Salvia miltiorrhiza, Camellia sinensis [55–57]. It is well known that genes are regulated by miRNA targeting, and in potato transgenic plants overexpressing miR156, StSPL9 expression levels were downregulated, and it was confirmed that the miR156/SPLs module is involved in the regulation of potato plant height and root growth [55]. In this study, we presents several lines of evidence indicating that miR156a was a critical gene that negatively regulates flavonoid accumulation in potatoes by repressing its target gene, StSPL9. Firstly, our results showed that overexpression and silencing of miR156a resulted in decreased and increased flavonoid content of potato tubers, respectively (Fig. 2). Second, miR156a primarily influences flavonoid accumulation through the regulation of its target gene StSPL9. After overexpression or silencing of miR156a, the expression of StSPL9 was downregulated or upregulated respectively (Fig. 2F). It has been established that the target gene of miR156 is SPL in Arabidopsis, rice, and other plants species [58, 59]. For instance, in apple fruit and callus, the overexpression of MdSPL2-like and MdSPL33 promotes anthocyanin accumulation, while silencing these genes yields opposite results [60]. Similarly, in Salvia miltiorrhiza, overexpression of SmSPL7 also enhances anthocyanin accumulation [57]. In summary, the miR156/SPL9 plays a crucial role in regulating flavonoid biosynthesis in potato tubers. The expression levels of miR156a and StSPL9 correlate with the changes in potato flavonoids-related gene expression, thereby influencing related metabolites in potato tubers. This provides a valuable reference for the molecular breeding of colored potatoes.

Analysis of flavonoid related structural gene expression in miR156a transgenic potato

Flavonoids, as essential secondary metabolites in plants, play a critical role in determining fruit quality and market value owing to their significant effects on fruit coloration, nutritional content, aroma, and antioxidant capacity. Early flavonoid biosynthesis pathway genes (EBGs), including PAL (phenylalanine ammonia-lyase), CHS (chalcone synthase), CHI (chalcone isomerase), and F3H (flavanone 3'-hydroxylase), collectively contribute to the synthesis of common precursors for flavonoid production. PAL is the first structural gene involved in flavonoid biosynthesis, as reported by Wang, who demonstrating that PAL activity can enhances flavonoids formation [61]. Ma revealed that, in apples, the expression level of St4CL is positively correlated with the accumulation [62]. Shi discovered that CHI and CHS interact to regulate the biosynthesis of flavonols [63]. F3H is involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis, and its high expression can result in reddish-brown petals in dahlias [64]. The downstream genes, termed late biosynthesis genes (LBGs), are specifically involved in the production of diverse flavonoid derivatives. For instance, DFR (dihydroflavonol 4-reductase), ANS (anthocyanin synthase), and UFGT (UDP-glucose:flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase) function as LBGs in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway, while DFR, ANS, LAR (leucoanthocyanidin reductase), and ANR (anthocyanin reductase) are associated with the biosynthesis of proanthocyanidins (PAs). Regarding DFR, the up-regulation of DFR upstream facilitates the movement of substrates towards the flavonol pathway [65]. Yan reported that the up-regulation of ANS increased anthocyanin content in poplar [66]. Transcriptome analysis of STTM-miR156a transgenic potatoes revealed that the expression levels of most flavonoid-related structural genes were significantly up-regulated, consistent with the increased flavonoid content observed in potato tubers. In contrast, the expression levels of most flavonoid-related structural genes were significantly down-regulated in OE-miR156a transgenic plants, resullting in decrease flavonoid content. Notably, the expression levels of four genes PAL-2, 4CLL9, 4CL1, and CHSB were up-regulated in OE-miR156a plants. We hypothesize that these genes may promote anthocyanin accumulation in transgenic potato tubers by enhancing the supply of substrates required for anthocyanin biosynthesis.

Flavonoids and anthocyanins are two major subclasses of plant, flavonoids, and they share a common biosynthetic pathway and regulatory mechanism. Their biosynthesis begins with the phenylpropanoid pathway, in which early biosynthetic genes (BEGs) common universal precursors that branch into different flavonoid subclasses, including flavonols, flavanones, and anthocyanins. Since flavonol and anthocyanin synthesis share common precursors and similar biosynthetic pathways, changes in flavonol content may influence anthocyanin synthesis due to competition for shared substrates and intermediates. For example, the up-regulation of flavonol synthesis mediated by enzymes such as FLS (flavonol synthase) may lead to a reduction in metabolic flux toward anthocyanin production, resulting in lower anthocyanin levels [67]. Conversely, the inhibition of flavonol synthesis could enhance anthocyanin accumulation by redirecting precursors to the anthocyanin branch of the pathway [68]. This metabolic interplay between anthocyanin and flavonol biosynthesis is further regulated by the MYB family of transcription factors [69]. For instance, AtMYBL2 interacts with TRANSPARENT TESTA 8 (TT8) to suppress anthocyanin biosynthesis in Arabidopsis [27]. In the context of flavonoid biosynthesis, AtMYB12 has been proposed to regulate flavonol production by modulating the expression of genes such as CHS (chalcone synthase), CHI (chalcone isomerase), F3H (flavanone 3-hydroxylase), and FLS (flavonol synthase) [70]. Thus, the MYB family of transcription factors plays a pivotal role in coordinating the synthesis of these secondary metabolites. In conclusion, the dynamic interactions between flavonoids and anthocyanins represent a crucial aspect of plant secondary metabolism, and this study provides a foundation for further exploration of these complex regulatory mechanisms.

Functional analysis of miR156a-StSPL9 in regulating flavonoid metabolism in potato

Flavonoids are an important class of secondary metabolites widely distributed in plants, playing diverse regulatory roles in plant growth and development. Based on their chemical structures, flavonoids are primarily classified into 12 subclasses: chalcones, stilbenes, aurones, flavanones, flavones, isoflavones, phlobaphenes, dihydroflavonols, flavonols, leucoanthocyanidins, proanthocyanidins, and anthocyanins [71, 72]. To date, more than 9,000 flavonoids have been isolated and identified [73]. Among these, dihydroflavonols serve as key intermediates in the biosynthesis of both colored anthocyanins and colorless flavonols, representing a critical regulatory node in the downstream branching of the flavonoid metabolic pathway [59]. Flavones, on the other hand, are major colorless pigments synthesized through a branch of the flavonoid pathway. Studies have demonstrated that overexpression of the CmFNS and CmFLS genes in chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum morifolium) significantly increased the accumulation of flavones and flavonols, respectively, while markedly reducing anthocyanin content [74]. Flavonols, which typically exhibit pale yellow or colorless pigmentation, function as co-pigments for anthocyanins. In tobacco, overexpression of RrMYB12 and RrMYB111 genes led to a significant elevation in flavonol levels accompanied by a reduction in anthocyanin accumulation [75]. Similarly, in Gerbera, overexpression of GhMYB1a upregulated structural genes involved in flavonol biosynthesis, resulting in decreased anthocyanin accumulation and increased flavonol production [76].

Research on STTM-miR156a transgenic potatoes has provided new insights into the regulatory mechanisms of flavonoid and anthocyanin metabolism. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that STTM-miR156a significantly modulates the accumulation of metabolites by altering the expression patterns of flavonoid and anthocyanin related genes. In the isoflavonoid biosynthesis pathway, metabolite profiling identified significant upregulation of flavonoid-related metabolites, including the dihydroflavonoid derivatives hesperetin (3.0–5.2 folds) and quercetin (6.1 folds), as well as the flavonol kaempferol (2.6 folds) (Fig. 2). Furthermore, we observed an upward trend in five downstream flavonoid-related metabolites that are loacted downstream of common precursors but not directly derived from cyanidin and delphinidin, including epicatechin derivatives (Epicatechin, MWSHY0174; Epicatechin-6-C-β-D-glucopyranoside, Zmdn002353; Epicatechin gallate, mws1397) and epigallocatechin derivatives (Epigallocatechin, MWSHY0098; Epigallocatechin-3-O-gallate, MWSHC2016). We hypothesize that the observed reduction in anthocyanin contents may result from the metabolic flux diversion towards flavonoid biosynthesis, wherein the precursor consumption for flavonoid production potentially compromises anthocyanin accumulation.

These findings suggest a competitive relationship between the flavonoid and anthocyanin biosynthetic pathways, with the redistribution of metabolic flux playing a pivotal role in determining metabolite content. STTM-miR156a indirectly regulates downstream flavonoid and anthocyanin biosynthesis pathways by inhibiting the expression of miR156a, resulting in the up-regulation of its target gene StSPL9. Further functional analysis of StSPL9 revealed that the promoters of several DEGs associated with contain putative sites for StSPL9,, including CHS2, F3H, HST, CHS1A, BEATH, CHIL2, F3'5'H, FGGT1, and U79B6.

Although we have identified candidate direct targets of StSPL9 through bioinformatic predictions, experimental validation via promoter binding and transcriptional activation assays is required to confirm these interactions, and such experiments are currently underway in our laboratory. These findings provide a useful tool for predicting StSPL9 binding sites in potato and offer initial insights into the regulatory network governing flavonoid and anthocyanin metabolism. Future studies are needed to clarify the interactions between the miR156-StSPL9 module and its downstream regulators. Such efforts will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of metabolic regulation in potatoes.

miR156a regulates phenolic acids biosynthesis in potato

Current literature provides substantial evidence that pigmented tissues exhibit approximately twice the phenolic acid content of their non-pigmented counterparts [77]. In Solanum tuberosum subsp, phenolic acids have chlorogenic acid (CGA) as the predominant constituent, followed by anthocyanins and flavan-3-ols [78]. Studies have shown that HST is involved in chlorogenic acid biosynthesis [79]. Ferulic acid, caffeic acid, and gallic acid increased the colour intensity of anthocyanins [80]. Research by Qian has shown that the expression of HST in yellow tubers potatoes is significantly lower than in colored potatoes, and the accumulation patterns of chlorogenic acid are highly correlated with those of 4-caffeoylquinic acid [80]. A decrease in HST reduces the content of caffeoylquinic acids (CQAs) [79]. Notably, transcriptomic analysis showed a significant decrease in the abundance of HST transcripts in STTM-miR156a plants (Fig. 3).

In this study, the transcriptional abundance of BGL47, and ACCH3 was significantly reduced in STTM-miR156a. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that downregulation of BGL47, and ACCH3 expression led to decreased accumulation of five phenolic acid-related DAMs, including methyl caffeate, 3-O-caffeoyl-4-O-sinapoylquinic acid, 1-O-Gentisoyl-β-D-glucoside*3-O, protocatechuic acid xylosyl xyloside, and 2,5-Dihydroxybenzoic acid-5-O-glucoside (Fig. 6 and S4).These results demonstrate that miR156a negatively regulates StSPL9, and that StSPL9 might promotes the expression of candidate genes including BGL47 and ACCH3, which in turn could negatively modulates the biosynthesis of phenolic acid metabolites. Furthermore, phenolic acids were found to exert regulatory effects on anthocyanin biosynthesis. In STTM-miR156a transgenic potatoes tubers, the contents of most flavonoid metabolites have increased, whereas the contents of most phenolic acids have decreased. Phenolic acids contribute to the stability of anthocyanins enhance color intensity [78]. Reduced expression of phenolic acid metabolism-related genes may be another factor contributing to the lightening of potato tuber colour.

Material and moehods

Plant materials and the growth conditions

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) cultivar ‘Huasong 66’ (a mid-late maturity fresh-consumption variety, provided by Inner Mongolia Huasong Agricultural Technology Co, Ltd, Jining, China) was maintained as sterile tissue-cultured plantlets. All transgenic potato materials were generated in the ‘Huasong 66’ background. The growth conditions were set at an illumination of 20000 lx, a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle, 25 ± 2 °C temperature, and 60% relative humidity. On July 2, 2024, OE-miR156a and WT potato seedlings were transplanted into nutrient pots (30.5 cm in diameter × 30 cm in height) and were harvested on December 30, 2024. The plants were maintained in controlled environment growth chambers at the College of Agriculture, Inner Mongolia Agricultural University (Hohhot, China). Subsequently, field trials with WT and STTM-miR156a were conducted, the plants were planted on May 10, 2024 at the experimental station of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University (located at 40°51′44.21″N, 111°45′38.72″E, altitude 1063 m, Hohhot, China). Tubers of WT and STTM-miR156a were harvested on October 19, 2024. The tubers were collected at the maturity stage of potato, and each tuber was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored in a refrigerator at −80 °C for subsequent experiments.

The interaction between miR156a and StSPL9 was validated using Nicotiana benthamiana L.

Production of the transgenic potato plants

In our previous work, multi-omics analyses were performed on potato tubers of the purple-fleshed cultivar ‘Huasong 66’ at different developmental stages, including small RNA sequencing (PRJNA743178), degradome sequencing (PRJNA743184), and transcriptome sequencing (SRR15021473-SRR15021481). These analyses revealed that miR156a was consistently upregulated throughout the growth period, and the sequences of its target gene StSPL9 were screened from degradome sequencing [36].

The miR156a silencing vector pBWA(V)KS-8601 was constructed using homologous recombination and Golden Gate assembly. The plasmid was digested with Bsa I/Eco31 I and ligated with T4 DNA ligase (TaKaRa, 2011 A, Beijing, China). The ligation products were transformed into Escherichia coli TOP10 competent cells (TOP10 Chemically Competent Cell, DL1010, Nanjing, China). Positive clones were sequenced by Sangon (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). To verify plasmid integrity, the recombinant vectors pBWA(V)KS-miR156a were digested with EcoR V (New England Biolabs, R0195VVIAL, Beijing, China), and detected by agarose gel electrophoresis. Positive transformants were selected on LB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin and cultured overnight in liquid LB medium. Subsequently, the plasmid was extracted using the TIANprep Mini Plasmid Kit (TianGen, DP103, Beijing, China). The plasmid was then into Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404 (LBA4404 Chemically Competent Cell, AC1030, Shanghai, China).

The miR156a sequence (flanked by 15–20 bp homology arms) was amplified by PCR and subsequently inserted into the 35S CaMV promoter-driven expression vector pCAMBIA1302 using homologous recombination. The ligation product was transformed into Escherichia coli TOP10. Positive clones were identified by PCR, and the PCR products of miR156a were sequenced by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Sequence alignment was performed the reference sequence. To verify plasmid integrity, the recombinant vector pCAMBIA1302-miR156a were digested with Mlu I (TaKaRa, 1071 A, Beijing, China), and the products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Then, the recombinant plasmid was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404.

The stem segments (8–10 mm in length, excluding axillary buds) of ‘Huasong 66’ were excised from 3–4 weeks in potato plants, and pre-cultured on solid MS medium for 2 days. The explants were then transformed via Agrobacterium mediated transformation. After 2 days of co-cultivation, the infected explants were transferred to callus induction medium. Following callus expansion, tissues were subcultured onto shoot regeneration medium to induce adventitious shoot formation. Putative transgenic plants were selected using media with either 50 mg/L kanamycin or 4 mg/L hygromycin.

Verification of the interaction between miR156a and its target gene StSPL9

The in vitro validation experiments for miR156 and its target gene StSPL9 were conducted via Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression in tobacco leaves, following the method described by Ke [37]. The recombinant positive control plasmids pCAMBIA1302-StSPL9 and pCAMBIA1302-miR156a were activated and resuspended. The Agrobacterium suspensions of OE-StSPL9-mGFP5 and OE-miR156a-mGFP5 were mixed in equal volumes to form a composite bacterial suspension. The OE-StSPL9-mGFP5 suspension alone and the mixture of OE-miR156a-mGFP5 + OE-StSPL9-mGFP5 were injected separately into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. After injection, the tobacco plants were cultured in the dark for 24 h and then under light for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted from the injected leaf regions, and the relative expression levels of StSPL9 were detected using qRT-PCR. Additionally, temporary leaf sections were prepared, and the fluorescence phenomena in the leaves were observed and photographed under a fluorescence confocal microscope.

Expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from ‘Huasong 66’ potato tubers using the TRIzol reagent (TIANGEN, GDP424, Beijing, China). cDNA was synthesized using the miRcute Plus miRNA First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TIANGEN, LOTY2109, Beijing, China) for miRNA, and the FastKing RT Kit (TIANGEN GKR116-02, Beijing, China) for other genes. The 10 × dilution cDNA was amplified using the miRcute Plus miRNA qPCR Detection Kit (TIANGEN FP411, Beijing, China) for miRNA, and the MonAmpTM SYBR® Green qPCR Mix kit (Monad Biotechnology, MQ10101S, Shanghai, China) for other genes on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). U6 (for small regulatory RNAs) and Actin (for other genes) were used as reference genes. Three biological replicates and three technical replicates were performed. The relative expression levels of each sample were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method [38]. Sequences of all primers used for qRT-PCR assays are listed in Table S5.

Transcriptome sequencing of transgenic potato

Transcriptome analysis was performed on WT, STTM-miR156a, and OE-miR156a transgenic potato tubers, with three biological replicates per sample. The total RNA of tested potato tubers was extracted using RNAprep Pure Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (Trelief ® RNAprep Pure Plant Plus Kit, DP441, TIANGEN, Beijing, China). The concentration of RNA was measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer and an MD microplate reader. RNA integrity was assessed with a Qsep400 Biological Analyzer. High-quality RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. The sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Metware Biotechnology Ltd in Wuhan, China). The raw data were filtered, and the sequencing error rate and GC content distribution were checked using fastp to obtain clean reads [39].

DESeq2 was used to analyze differential gene expression between sample groups [40, 41].The Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to correct for multiple hypothesis testing, adjusting P-values to obtain the false discovery rate (FDR). Differential genes were identified based on the criteria of |log2Fold Change |≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. Annotation of these differential genes was carried out using the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg) and the GO database (http://www.geneontology.org). Enrichment analysis was conducted using the hypergeometric test. For KEGG, pathways served as the units of analysis, wheres for GO, the analysis was based on GO terms.

Metabolomics sequencing of transgenic potato

The samples from WT and STTM-miR156a tubers underwent freeze-drying using a freeze-dryer (Scientz-100F, Zhejiang, China), were pulverized with a grinder (MM400, Retsch) operating at 30 Hz, and were centrifuged in a centrifuge (5424R, Hamburg, Germany). The resulting supernatant was collected and filtered through a microporous membrane with a pore size of 0.22μm for subsequent UPLC-MS/MS analysis. An ExionLC™ AD ultra-performance liquid chromatography system was employed in conjunction with tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS).

The QQQ scan operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, with the collision gas (nitrogen) set to a medium level. MRM mode, acquired via triple quadrupole mass spectrometry and guided by the metabolite database (MWDB), facilitated the detection of metabolites within the analyzed samples. To ensure the reliability of both qualitative and quantitative assessments of the metabolites, the mass spectrum peaks from each identified metabolite across the samples were comprehensively adjusted. Throughout the process of instrumental analysis, quality control (QC) samples were incorporated to evaluate the technical reproducibility of metabolite extraction and detection.

Joint analysis of transcriptome and metabolome

The molecular mechanisms underlying flavonoid and anthocyanin accumulation in potato tubers were investigated through integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses. Differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were correlated with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using Pearson correlation analysis (|r|> 0.8, P < 0.05). Significant gene-metabolite pairs were visualized through hierarchical clustering heatmaps and correlation networks. For comprehensive data integration, a bidirectional orthogonal partial least squares (O2PLS) model was applied to evaluate the systemic covariance patterns between metabolic fluxes and transcriptional regulation.

To investigate the regulatory role of StSPL9 and its potential interactions with eight DEGs, the binding motifs of StSPL9 were predicted using JASPAR (https://jaspar.genereg.net/). The GTAC core sequence was identified as the binding motif for StSPL9, and the promoter regions of the eight DEGs were scanned to determine the presence and frequency of GTAC motifs.

Statistical analysis

Statistically significant differences were determined using one-way ANOVA. The mean and standed error (SE) of each data point were plotted. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and significant differences between groups and denoted with different lowercase letters.
