Elastic stockings for venous thromboembolism prevention, time to go
Authors: Lara N. Roberts, Emma Gee
Published: 2026

Hospitalization and surgery are major risk factors for venous thromboembolism (VTE), associated with an estimated 10 million events globally each year [1]. VTE prevention is thus integral to optimizing patient safety in these settings. Elastic stockings or antiembolism stockings (AES) have been a mainstay of VTE prevention. Randomized controlled trials in the 1970s and 1980s demonstrated a clear reduction in asymptomatic VTE in postoperative patients using AES [2]. VTE prevention guidance recommend AES as an adjunct to pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis in surgical subgroups or in isolation for inpatients with both high VTE and high bleeding risk, with some also recommending them for medical or obstetric patients with bleeding risk factors [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. In England, despite national guidance making no recommendation regarding AES in medical or obstetric patients due to a lack of direct evidence, use remains widespread. A national survey of VTE prevention practice revealed AES were considered indicated for 22% and 76% of medical (n = 2538) and obstetric (n = 1632) patients, surveyed respectively [7]. The perceived ease of use, low cost, and lack of bleeding risk, and perhaps fear of litigation in cases for which pharmacologic prophylaxis is not possible, have likely contributed to continued AES use in these groups. Advances in medical care, enabling earlier discharge home (or to ambulatory care), and surgical care, including minimally invasive approaches, day surgery, regional anesthesia, and enhanced recovery programs, have led to earlier mobilization and shorter hospital stays. Contemporary randomized controlled trials have called the continued role of AES in VTE prevention into question.

The seminal CLOTS1 trial comparing AES with routine care without AES in stroke patients (n = 2518) demonstrated a lack of benefit in reducing VTE (absolute risk reduction, 0.5%; 95% CI, −1.9% to 2.9%) and highlighted a significant 5% risk of skin damage (odds ratio [OR], 4.2; 95% CI, 2.4-7.3) [8]. The GAPS trial in the UK found the addition of AES to low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was no better than LMWH alone in surgical patients, with an absolute risk difference of 0.3% (95% CI, −0.7% to 1.3%) [9]. This was confirmed in a meta-analysis with no reduction in deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), or VTE mortality associated with use of AES in addition to pharmacologic prophylaxis in surgical inpatients [10]. The PETS trial investigating AES compared with no prophylaxis in patients with low VTE risk undergoing day surgery terminated early [11], presumably due to futility, with results still awaited. An ongoing randomized controlled trial is investigating the role of AES in addition to LMWH in surgical patients with high VTE risk [12]. Although there is evidence that intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) can reduce VTE in stroke patients (OR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.51-0.84) [13], there is little robust evidence supporting their role as an adjunct or in isolation in other patient groups. The American Society of Hematology evidence review of VTE prevention in surgical patients reported a reduction in symptomatic proximal DVT with IPC compared with AES in surgical patients not receiving pharmacologic prophylaxis (relative risk, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.25-0.93, with very low certainty of evidence of effect) but with no impact on PE or VTE-related mortality.[5] ASH also reported combined mechanical (either AES or IPC) and pharmacologic prophylaxis in surgical patients reduced PE but with no impact on symptomatic proximal DVT or mortality (with very low certainty in the evidence of effect). A meta-analysis of high VTE risk in orthopedic/trauma patients demonstrated a benefit of combined IPC with LMWH over IPC alone (OR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.21-0.70) [14]. Further study is required to ascertain whether the addition of IPC to pharmacologic prophylaxis is superior to pharmacologic prophylaxis alone. Simply replacing AES with IPC at scale should be avoided due to uncertainty of the evidence of benefit, higher cost, and the known risk of harm with increased skin damage (3.1%) reported in stroke patients using IPC (OR, 2.2; 95% CI, 1.3-3.8) [13].

Within the above trials, correct application and use of AES was suboptimal (15%-31%), and real-world studies suggest many patients (60%) have incorrectly applied and/or used stockings [8,15,16]. Such low levels of adherence will further undermine any small potential prophylactic benefit [8].

The significant risk of stocking-related skin damage (3%-5%), including blisters, ulcers, skin breaks, and necrosis, not only diminishes patient experience through direct harm, but also introduces additional clinical workload and potential downstream costs [8]. Additionally, the soft fabric of AES may contribute to the risk of falls when standing or walking. These risks continue after discharge from the hospital, with instructions to continue use until ‘normal mobility returns’ being common, particularly after orthopedic surgery [17]. The risk of skin damage/inappropriate application may be further compounded by difficulties donning and doffing the garments in the home environment. Of note, the AES pressure gradient is insufficient to counteract normal hydrostatic pressures during standing or walking [18].

Delivering high-value care, in both economic and patient-centered terms, requires clinicians to ensure that prescribed interventions provide meaningful benefit relative to the resources used [19]. Although AES are individually inexpensive, their widespread use represents a substantial cumulative expenditure, estimated at over £63 million annually in England for surgical patients alone [20]. This investment extends beyond the purchase price: it includes nursing time for measurement, application, monitoring, patient education, and the broader supply-chain effort in procurement and distribution. Any assessment of value must therefore consider not only clinical effectiveness but also the operational costs associated with routine use.

The environmental impact of AES should not be overlooked; they are typically made from nonrecyclable synthetic materials, and contamination with body fluids often necessitates disposal through clinical waste streams, including incineration, adding to healthcare’s carbon footprint. Reducing waste by avoiding single-use items (such as stockings) is a key component of sustainable healthcare [21].

We therefore propose that healthcare organizations seek to rationalize their use of AES via a strategic, systems-level approach. This includes engaging key stakeholders, updating clinical guidelines and patient information, and ensuring staff are equipped to change longstanding practice. As stocking use is deeply embedded in routine care, staff education is essential for clinician confidence in the evidence base and rationale for discontinuation. Postimplementation surveillance is suggested to monitor intermittent pneumatic compression use and hospital-associated thrombosis rates to ensure that the benefits of reducing AES use are not offset by unintended consequences such as underuse of anticoagulant prophylaxis or inappropriate substitution. A structured evaluation framework can help confirm that changes in practice maintain patient safety while improving value.

In conclusion, recent evidence challenges the routine use of AES in contemporary VTE prevention. Modern trials show minimal benefit, poor adherence, and meaningful harms, while operational and environmental costs further limit their value. As early mobilization and shorter length of stay are now standard, the clinical context in which stockings are effective has narrowed considerably. A planned, systems-level approach to deimplementation, supported by staff education and updated guidance, is warranted.
