DNA methylation driven genes shape prognosis and tumor immune landscape in rectal cancer
Authors: Weipeng Liu, Lugao Tian, Yongbin Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDNA methylation alterations play a crucial role in rectal cancer development. This study identifies and characterizes DNA methylation-driven genes in the TCGA-READ cohort, constructs a prognostic model, and validates its robustness and biological relevance in independent external cohorts.MethodsUsing TCGA-READ (n = 160) as the discovery cohort, we identified 490 methylation-driven genes via MethylMix analysis. A LASSO-derived prognostic model was constructed integrating expression of CCNI2, LINC00899, and ST6GALNAC1. Model performance was assessed by Kaplan–Meier analysis, time-dependent ROC, calibration, and decision curve analysis. Mechanistic insights were explored using differential expression, PPI network analysis, GSEA, and immune infiltration estimation. External validation of the prognostic model was performed in the independent dataset GSE103479 (n = 155). Introduction

Rectal cancer represents a major global health burden, accounting for approximately one-third of colorectal malignancies with heterogeneous clinical outcomes despite standardized treatment protocols [1]. While TNM staging provides foundational prognostic information, substantial variability exists within stage-matched patients, underscoring the urgent need for molecular biomarkers that capture tumor biology more comprehensively [2]. Recent studies have increasingly focused on the molecular mechanisms underlying rectal cancer development, with DNA methylation emerging as a pivotal factor [3]. DNA methylation, a key epigenetic modification involving the addition of methyl groups to the DNA molecule, plays an essential role in regulating gene expression [4]. Aberrant DNA methylation patterns have been implicated in various stages of cancer development, including initiation, progression, and metastasis [5]. Therefore, understanding the role of DNA methylation-driven genes in rectal cancer is critical for unraveling the disease’s molecular underpinnings and improving patient management.

Epigenetic alterations are widely recognized as hallmarks of cancer, and aberrant DNA methylation is frequently observed in rectal tumors [6, 7]. Methylation changes can silence tumor suppressor genes or deregulate oncogenic programs, thereby facilitating tumor growth and progression [8, 9]. Hypermethylation at promoter regions is a common mechanism of tumor suppressor inactivation [10]. In contrast, hypomethylation may activate oncogenes or promote genomic instability [11, 12]. However, methylation-driven genes that are specific to rectal cancer have not been comprehensively defined, and many candidates lack functional characterization. These limitations constrain translation of methylation markers into clinically useful tools.

The significance of DNA methylation patterns in cancer has been substantiated through various genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and high-throughput screening techniques, which have identified numerous methylation markers with potential diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic value [5, 13]. However, the integration of these markers into clinically applicable models has been slow, partly due to the complexity of the epigenetic landscape and the multifactorial nature of cancer development [14]. Moreover, the functional implications of many identified methylation-driven genes in rectal cancer remain poorly understood, highlighting the need for in-depth functional analysis and validation.

This study addresses these knowledge gaps through a multi-cohort integrative analysis. We first constructed a parsimonious prognostic model from TCGA-READ discovery data, then validated its clinical utility in an entirely independent rectal cancer cohort (GSE103479). Additionally, to overcome the limitations of single-dataset analysis, we investigated the expression patterns and biological functions of the model genes (CCNI2, LINC00899, and ST6GALNAC1) in two additional external datasets (GSE71187 and GSE87211). By combining methylation analysis, prognostic modeling, and multi-dataset functional validation, this study aims to provide a robust tool for patient stratification and new insights into the molecular mechanisms driving rectal cancer.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The Cancer Genome Atlas-Rectum Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-READ) dataset was obtained from the National Cancer Institute’s Genomic Data Commons (GDC). DNA methylation, gene expression, and clinical outcome data were downloaded for subsequent analysis. Raw data were preprocessed to remove batch effects and normalize the expression values. For methylation data, beta-values were calculated representing the ratio of methylated probe intensity to the overall intensity (sum of methylated and unmethylated probe intensities).

Identification of DNA methylation-driven genes

DNA methylation-driven genes were identified using the MethylMix R package [15], which employs a three-step algorithm designed to detect functionally relevant methylation events without relying on arbitrary fold-change thresholds. First, mixture modeling identifies genes with distinct methylation states in tumor versus normal samples. Second, differential methylation is tested using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Third, a transcriptional predictive effect is required, evidenced by a significant inverse correlation between methylation levels and gene expression.

Following MethylMix identification, methylation-driven genes were filtered using standard criteria: Wilcoxon p < 0.05, correlation coefficient < − 0.3, and |logFC| > 0. Genes were then classified as hypermethylated (increased methylation in tumor tissue; logFC > 0) or hypomethylated (decreased methylation in tumor tissue; logFC < 0) based on the direction of methylation change relative to matched normal tissue. This approach ensures the capture of all statistically significant, transcriptionally predictive methylation events consistent with established MethylMix applications.

Identification of prognostic methylation-driven genes

To identify methylation-driven genes with clinical relevance, we first intersected the identified methylation-driven genes with genes associated with patient prognosis. A two-stage selection strategy was employed to balance statistical stringency for visualization with a comprehensive approach for model development.

Stage 1: Discovery and Visualization. To isolate the most statistically robust signals for focused presentation, we initially identified a core set of genes by intersecting the methylation-driven genes with a set of prognostic genes at a stringent significance threshold of p < 0.01. This yielded 5 top-tier methylation-associated prognostic genes, which were used for key visualization analyses.

Stage 2: Comprehensive Model Building. To ensure an unbiased and comprehensive gene pool for the subsequent prognostic model, we created a broader candidate set by intersecting the methylation-driven genes with all genes associated with prognosis at the standard significance threshold of p < 0.05. This resulted in a pool of 30 genes, which served as the input for the variable selection procedure.

Prognostic model construction and internal evaluation (TCGA-READ)

A prognostic model was developed using the 30-gene candidate pool identified in Stage 2. A LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression with ten-fold cross-validation was employed for variable selection and regularization to prevent overfitting and identify the most predictive gene set for the final model.

The performance of the developed prognostic model was evaluated using several metrics. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to compare the overall survival between high- and low-risk patient groups, with differences assessed using the log-rank test. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the model’s predictive accuracy over time. Calibration curves were plotted to compare predicted survival probabilities with observed outcomes. Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the clinical utility of the prognostic model by quantifying the net benefits at different threshold probabilities.

Expression, correlation, and friend’s analysis

Expression and Spearman correlation analyses were performed to assess the expression levels and expression relationships among methylation-driven genes identified as prognostic. Furthermore, a friend’s analysis visualized the semantic similarity among intersecting genes, with each gene’s average similarity score calculated to highlight potential biological interconnections.

Methylation variation analysis

Methylation variation across tumor samples for the prognostic genes was visualized using distribution plots, facilitated by a mixture model approach. The inverse correlation between methylation variation and gene expression was explored to demonstrate the regulatory impact of methylation on gene function.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

Functional annotation of the DNA methylation-driven genes was conducted through GO enrichment analysis using the R package clusterProfiler. The enrichment within biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories was depicted by bubble plots and heatmaps. Network plots elucidated interrelationships among enriched terms. Adjusted p-values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR).

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was conducted to identify dysregulated genes in high risk score groups compared to low risk score groups, applying thresholds of absolute log fold change (|LogFC|) greater than 0.58 and adjusted p-value (p.adj) less than 0.05. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were constructed to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To elucidate the cancer-promoting mechanisms associated with the high-risk score group identified by our prognostic model, we performed GSEA using clusterProfiler package, with c5.go.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt [Gene ontology] (10561) from MSigDB database as the reference gene set. The terms with p.adj < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Tumor microenvironment analyses

Immune infiltration was estimated using MCPcounter, QuanTIseq, ESTIMATE, CIBERSORT, and ssGSEA, and immunotherapy response was approximated using Immunophenoscore (IPS).

External independent cohort validation (GSE103479)

To evaluate generalizability, the established 3-gene model was applied to an external cohort (GSE103479). Risk scores were calculated using the same model structure, and patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups. Predictive performance was assessed using time-dependent ROC curves at 1, 3, and 5 years, calibration analysis comparing predicted versus observed survival probabilities, and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses for overall survival and relapse-free survival.

Expression and functional validation of model genes

The expression levels of CCNI2, LINC00899, and ST6GALNAC1 were validated in two independent datasets: GSE71187 and GSE87211. To investigate the potential biological functions of these three genes, we performed GSEA using a correlation-based ranking method. First, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the target gene and all other genes in the external datasets. These coefficients were then averaged across datasets to improve result stability. Genes were ranked by their average correlation and compared against GO, KEGG, and Hallmark gene sets from MSigDB.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2), with packages specific to each analysis type. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless stated otherwise.

Results

Identification of DNA methylation-driven genes in rectal cancer

The study initiated with the identification of DNA methylation-driven genes within the TCGA-READ cohort. A significant discovery was made, pinpointing 490 genes influenced by DNA methylation patterns specific to rectal cancer. The analysis showcased the contrast in DNA methylation beta-values between tumor and normal tissues. This comparison not only highlighted the presence of both hypermethylation (logFC > 0) and hypomethylation (logFC < 0) in tumor tissues compared to their normal counterparts but also revealed their association with gene expression levels within the cancerous tissues (Fig. 1A).

Identification of DNA methylation-driven genes in the TCGA-READ cohort. A Bubble plot showing differential methylation between tumor and normal tissues and the correlation between methylation and gene expression. B Venn diagram of methylation-driven genes and prognosis-associated genes. C, D Expression of overlapping genes in unpaired (C) and paired (D) tumor versus normal tissues. E, I Pairwise Spearman correlations among the overlapping genes. J Functional similarity (“friends”) analysis of the five overlapping genes

Following the initial identification, the study delved into the prognostic relevance of these methylation-driven genes. Through a rigorous comparison, detailed in Fig. 1B, we overlapped the 490 methylation-driven genes with a larger set of 1315 genes identified as prognostic indicators for READ outcomes, under a stringent significance threshold (p < 0.01). This meticulous approach unveiled a critical intersection of five genes: CCNI2, C3orf20, LINC00899, POLR3H, and ST6GALNAC1, marking them as pivotal for further investigation due to their dual roles in DNA methylation-driven alterations and prognostic significance in rectal cancer (Fig. 1B).

The subsequent phase of the analysis focused on elucidating the expression levels and relationships of the intersecting genes within the TCGA-READ dataset. Higher expression levels of CCNI2 and POLR3H, and lower expression of ST6GALNAC1, were observed in both unpaired and paired tumor tissues (Fig. 1C, D). Spearman correlation analyses revealed a negative correlation between CCNI2 and C3orf20 expression, and positive correlations among several other gene pairs: C3orf20 and LINC00899; CCNI2 and POLR3H; CCNI2 and ST6GALNAC1; as well as POLR3H and LINC00899 (Fig. 1E–I). These findings suggest potential cooperative or antagonistic interactions between these genes in the context of rectal cancer. Additionally, the Friends analysis provided insights into the semantic similarity among the genes. POLR3H emerged as a gene of interest with the highest average semantic similarity score (0.2777), suggesting its potential as a central player in the mechanistic pathways underlying rectal cancer (Fig. 1J). This analysis not only underscored the interconnectedness of these genes but also hinted at POLR3H’s significant role in driving the biological processes associated with rectal cancer progression and prognosis.

Methylation variation and gene expression dynamics

The exploration of methylation variation and its relationship with gene expression in rectal cancer further extended to an in-depth analysis of the five intersecting genes: CCNI2, C3orf20, LINC00899, POLR3H, and ST6GALNAC1. A mixture model revealed multiple methylation states for these genes within the tumor samples, indicative of the complex methylation landscape (Fig. 2A, C, E, G, and I). Notably, a comparison with the methylation levels in normal tissues—represented by a horizontal line in each panel—underscored significant variations, with C3orf20 emerging as hypermethylated, while the remaining four genes showed patterns of hypomethylation in tumor samples.

Methylation states and methylation–expression relationships of five overlapping genes. A, C, E, G, I Mixture-model plots showing methylation distributions in tumors with normal-tissue reference levels. B, D, F, H, J Scatter plots showing the inverse association between methylation and gene expression for each gene

Moreover, the inverse correlation between methylation variation and gene expression for each of the five genes provided concrete evidence of the reciprocal relationship between methylation status and gene activity in tumor tissues (Fig. 2B, D, F, H, and J). For instance, increased methylation levels in C3orf20 were associated with decreased gene expression, aligning with the typical silencing effect of hypermethylation on gene activity. Conversely, the hypomethylated states of CCNI2, LINC00899, POLR3H, and ST6GALNAC1 corresponded with enhanced gene expression, suggesting a release from methylation-induced transcriptional repression.

Functional implications of DNA methylation-driven gene alterations in rectal cancer

The landscape of functional implications associated with 490 DNA methylation-driven genes was observed in terms such as “Wnt-activated receptor activity”, “Wnt-protein binding”, and “cell fate commitment involved in the formation of the primary germ layer” (Fig. 3A). The prominence of these terms underscores the integral role of Wnt signaling and cellular differentiation pathways in the etiology of rectal cancer, highlighting how alterations in DNA methylation might affect these critical developmental processes. Besides, the distribution of DNA methylation-driven genes across the enriched GO categories was depicted (Fig. 3B) and the complex interrelationships among the functional enrichments were identified (Fig. 3C). This network plot is particularly revealing, as it not only highlights the interconnectedness of different biological processes and molecular functions but also suggests a cohesive network of pathways potentially disrupted by DNA methylation alterations in rectal cancer.

Subtype identification based on DNA methylation profiles

To identify the READ subtypes, we utilized the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) plot (Figure S1A) and Delta Area Plot (Figure S1B) to determine the optimal number of clusters (k) for subtype division, based on the differential methylation values of DNA methylation-driven genes. These analyses identified k = 2 as the optimal number, signifying the existence of two distinct subtypes within the READ patient cohort (Figure S1C). Item-Consensus (Figure S2A) and Cluster-Consensus (Figure S2B) analyses further underscored the reliability of the clustering. Originally, we thought that this bifurcation underscores the heterogeneity in methylation patterns among patients and suggests the potential for differential disease progression and therapeutic response. However, survival probability analysis found no significant differences in overall survival (Figure S3A), disease-specific survival (Figure S3B), and progression-free interval (Figure S3C) between the two subtypes of READ patients.

Further, a heatmap provided a comprehensive visualization of the differential methylation values for 490 DNA methylation-driven genes stratified by the two clusters (Figure S4).

Development and validation of a prognostic model based on DNA methylation-driven genes in READ

In an effort to refine our understanding of rectal cancer prognosis, we intersected two pivotal gene sets: 490 DNA methylation-driven genes identified from our initial analysis, and a broader set of 4528 genes previously associated with overall survival in READ, each marked by statistical significance at p < 0.05. This intersection yielded a focused cohort of 30 genes that are both influenced by DNA methylation and have established prognostic value (Fig. 4A).

Construction of the prognostic model. A Intersection of methylation-driven genes and prognosis-associated genes. B, C LASSO regression with cross-validation for feature selection. D Risk score distribution, survival status, and expression heatmap of the model genes

To distill these 30 candidate genes into a practical prognostic tool, we employed LASSO regression coupled with ten-fold cross-validation, a technique well-suited for handling high-dimensional data while preventing overfitting. The optimal lambda was determined at the point of minimum likelihood deviation, leading to the selection of three pivotal genes: CCNI2, LINC00899, and ST6GALNAC1 (Fig. 4B). These genes emerged as the final variables for our prognostic model. The coefficients of the selected genes across various lambda values during the LASSO selection process provided an insightful perspective on the dynamic nature of each gene’s contribution to the prognostic model (Fig. 4C).

With the prognostic model established, we proceeded to evaluate its performance in stratifying patients into risk categories. Based on the expression levels of CCNI2, LINC00899, and ST6GALNAC1, individual patient risk score was calculated and READ patients were then divided into high-risk and low-risk groups using the median risk score. The clear demarcation between the survival outcomes of high-risk and low-risk groups validates the model’s efficacy (Fig. 4D).

Evaluation of the prognostic model in the TCGA-READ dataset

The Kaplan-Meier survival curves distinctly show that READ patients in the high-risk group have significantly lower overall survival probabilities than those in the low-risk group (Fig. 5A). The predictive accuracy of the model was quantified using Receiver Operating Characteristic-Area under the Curve (ROC-AUC) values at 1, 3, and 5 years. The AUC values at these intervals were 0.63559, 0.71439, and 0.89847, respectively (Fig. 5B). The increasing AUC values over time demonstrate that the model’s ability to accurately predict patient outcomes improves significantly for longer follow-up durations. The close alignment of calibration curves with the 45-degree line at 1, 3, and 5 years indicates that the model’s predictions are highly consistent with actual clinical outcomes (Fig. 5C). This strong calibration performance suggests that the model can be reliably used in clinical practice to predict patient survival with a high degree of accuracy. The clinical utility of the prognostic model was further examined through decision curve analysis (DCA) for 1, 3, and 5 years. As these time points progressed, the DCA based on the risk score increasingly diverged from the DCA curves based on single model variables, highlighting the superiority of using the risk score for clinical decision-making over reliance on single model variables (Fig. 5D–F).

Differential gene expression between high and low risk score groups

A total of 89 DEGs were identified as being significantly underexpressed in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group, indicating their possible role in the progression of the adverse phenotypes associated with the high-risk group (Fig. 6A). To understand the molecular interactions among these DEGs and potentially identify therapeutic targets, a PPI network was constructed using data from the STRING database. The PPI visualization revealed SULF1 as a hub gene with a degree of 17, suggesting it has significant interactions with several other proteins (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, the expression of 89 DEGs between high- and low-risk score groups was illustrated and organized based on Euclidean distance-based clustering, highlighting the underlying resemblance and structure in expression profiles among the DEGs (Fig. 7).

GSEA of high-risk score group

To elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the divergent clinical outcomes between risk groups, we performed GSEA on the TCGA-READ cohort. A total of 1,005 GO terms were significantly enriched (adjusted p < 0.05). Notably, the high-risk score group was characterized by a significant downregulation of gene sets involved in the negative regulation of several oncogenic pathways and cellular processes (Fig. 8A–G).

GSEA and schematic of pathways enriched in the high-risk group. A–G GSEA plots of significantly enriched GO terms in the TCGA-READ cohort. H Conceptual schematic illustrating the biological mechanism: the high-risk score reflects a loss of inhibitory control (“molecular brakes”), resulting in the hyper-activation of oncogenic signaling (Wnt, MAPK, and TGF-β pathways) and aggressive tumor progression, which contributes to poor clinical prognosis. NES Normalized enrichment score; P.adj Adjusted p-value; FDR False discovery rate

Specifically, we observed a suppression of the negative regulatory mechanisms for the canonical Wnt signaling pathway (NES = − 1.290, Fig. 8A), the MAPK cascade (NES = − 1.274, Fig. 8F), and the TGF-β receptor signaling pathway (NES = − 1.242, Fig. 8G). Additionally, negative regulators of the cell cycle, cell growth, and epithelial cell proliferation were significantly depleted in high-risk patients (Fig. 8B–E). To synthesize these findings, we constructed a conceptual schematic (Fig. 8H) illustrating the proposed “loss of molecular brakes” mechanism. In this model, the high-risk score corresponds to a deficiency in intrinsic inhibitory controls, leading to the hyper-activation of clinically actionable pathways (Wnt, MAPK, and TGF-β). This molecular environment facilitates unchecked cell cycle progression and aggressive tumor proliferation, ultimately driving the poor overall survival observed in the high-risk group.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration in tumor tissues and immunotherapy response characteristics

Utilizing various algorithms, our analysis illuminated significant variances in immune cell presence between high-risk and low-risk score groups. MCPcounter algorithm assessment underscored a diminished presence of critical immune cells, including B lineage cells, cytotoxic lymphocytes, endothelial cells, monocytic lineage cells, myeloid dendritic cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and T cells in the high-risk score group when juxtaposed with the low-risk cohort (Fig. 9A). QuanTIseq algorithm assessment revealed a notable decrease in the abundance of M2 macrophages, NK cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the high-risk score group compared to their counterparts in the low-risk score group (Fig. 9B). This decrease in diverse immune cell populations suggests a compromised immune surveillance and response system within the high-risk group’s tumor microenvironment. For another three immune infiltration algorithms—ESTIMATE, CIBERSORT, and Single-Sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA)—no differential immune component infiltration was observed (Figure S5A, S5B, and S5C).

Furthermore, Immunophenoscore (IPS) assessment indicated no difference in responsiveness to CTLA-4 and PD-1 antibodies between high- and low-risk READ patients. (Figure S5D).

External validation of the 3-gene prognostic model in GSE103479

To address model generalizability, we applied the established CCNI2/LINC00899/ST6GALNAC1 risk score to the independent GSE103479 cohort. Time-dependent ROC analysis showed AUCs of 0.638 (1-year), 0.579 (3-year), and 0.481 (5-year) (Fig. 10A), indicating modest short-term discrimination and reduced long-term discrimination in this dataset. Calibration analysis suggested reasonable agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities, particularly at earlier time horizons, while later time-point calibration appeared less stable (Fig. 10B), consistent with decreasing AUC at 5 years. Kaplan–Meier analysis for overall survival showed a trend toward poorer survival in the high-risk group but did not reach statistical significance (log-rank p = 0.2; Fig. 10C). Similarly, relapse-free survival displayed a separation trend with borderline significance (log-rank p = 0.062; Fig. 10D). Together, these data provide independent-cohort support for the model’s applicability, while also highlighting cohort-dependent performance variability that is expected given platform differences, endpoint definitions, and clinical heterogeneity.

External expression and pathway association analyses for model genes

We evaluated CCNI2 expression in two external datasets. In GSE71187, CCNI2 expression did not significantly differ between tumor and normal tissues (Wilcoxon p = 0.21; Fig. 11A, left), whereas in GSE87211, CCNI2 showed a marked differential pattern (Wilcoxon p < 2.2e − 16; Fig. 11A, right), supporting dataset-dependent but reproducible evidence of altered CCNI2 expression in rectal/colorectal tumor biology.

External expression validation and correlation-ranked GSEA for CCNI2. A CCNI2 expression in tumor versus normal tissues in GSE71187 and GSE87211. B–D Hallmark, GO, and KEGG enrichment results from correlation-ranked GSEA

Correlation-ranked, cross-dataset-averaged GSEA further linked CCNI2-associated transcriptional programs to hallmark proliferation and checkpoint regulation (e.g., E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, MYC targets) and inflammatory/immune signaling modules (e.g., TNFα signaling via NFκB, interferon responses), as well as EMT-related signaling (Fig. 11B). GO and KEGG enrichments highlighted DNA replication/replication initiation, chromosome segregation, cell cycle, antigen processing/presentation, leukocyte migration/transendothelial migration, and cytokine/chemokine signaling pathways (Fig. 11C, D). These findings align with the concept that CCNI2-associated expression networks may reflect proliferative capacity and tumor–immune interface alterations.

In external expression analyses, LINC00899 showed no significant tumor–normal difference in GSE71187 (Wilcoxon p = 0.66; Fig. 12A, left), while exhibiting a statistically significant difference in GSE87211 (Wilcoxon p = 0.0062; Fig. 12A, right). This supports the notion that LINC00899 dysregulation may be context-dependent but detectable across independent cohorts.

External expression validation and correlation-ranked GSEA for LINC00899. A LINC00899 expression in tumor versus normal tissues in GSE71187 and GSE87211. B–D Hallmark, GO, and KEGG enrichment results from correlation-ranked GSEA

Correlation-based GSEA indicated that LINC00899-associated programs involved MYC targets, mTORC1 signaling, metabolic pathways (glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, cholesterol homeostasis, reactive oxygen species pathway), hypoxia, cell-cycle-related modules (E2F targets, G2M checkpoint), and TGF-β signaling (Fig. 12B). GO and KEGG results emphasized translation/ribosome-related biology, oxidative phosphorylation/mitochondrial respiration, DNA repair processes, and metabolic pathways, with additional signals in leukocyte transendothelial migration (Fig. 12C, D). Collectively, these patterns suggest LINC00899 may be embedded in networks balancing metabolic remodeling and proliferative control—features commonly implicated in rectal cancer progression.

ST6GALNAC1 expression showed highly significant tumor–normal differences in both external datasets (GSE71187 and GSE87211; Wilcoxon p < 2.2e − 16 in each; Fig. 13A), providing the most consistent external expression evidence among the three model genes. Functionally, Hallmark enrichment associated ST6GALNAC1-related programs with EMT, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, angiogenesis, Wnt/β-catenin signaling, and mitotic spindle signatures (Fig. 13B). GO and KEGG enrichments similarly emphasized aerobic respiration/oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid catabolic processes, glycan biosynthesis (O-glycan), ECM receptor interaction, and cell-cycle-related pathways (Fig. 13C, D). Given ST6GALNAC1’s role in glycosylation biology, the enrichment of glycan and microenvironment-related pathways provides biologically coherent support for its inclusion in the prognostic model and potential involvement in tumor–stroma interactions.

Discussion

Our study underscores the pivotal role of DNA methylation-driven genes in shaping the clinical and molecular landscape of rectal cancer. By integrating discovery data from TCGA with multiple external validation datasets, we have developed a robust three-gene prognostic model consisting of CCNI2, LINC00899, and ST6GALNAC1.

The GO enrichment analysis of 490 DNA methylation-driven genes revealed critical biological processes, particularly highlighting the Wnt signaling pathway and cell fate commitment, as pivotal in rectal cancer development. These findings are in harmony with previous studies that have implicated the Wnt signaling pathway in colorectal cancer carcinogenesis, emphasizing its role in maintaining the balance between cell proliferation and apoptosis [16]. For instance, Zai-Qiu Wang et al. demonstrated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway’s critical role in rectal cancer’s progression [17]. Our study extends these findings by linking specific DNA methylation-driven alterations to the modulation of this pathway, suggesting that aberrant methylation patterns may serve as a mechanism for Wnt signaling dysregulation in rectal cancer.

The prognostic model developed from CCNI2, LINC00899, and ST6GALNAC1 through Lasso regression analysis and validated by ten-fold cross-validation, Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and other statistical methods, demonstrated high predictive accuracy for patient outcomes. This model enriches the repertoire of molecular markers for rectal cancer prognosis and aligns with studies like those by Yoshinaga Okugawa et al., which emphasized the predictive power of epigenetic biomarkers in cancer prognostication [18]. Particularly, the inclusion of LINC00899, a long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), underscores the emerging role of lncRNAs in cancer biology, acting through mechanisms such as transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation, further implicating the broad spectrum of epigenetic regulation in rectal cancer prognosis [19, 20].

Moreover, the identification of SULF1 as a hub gene within the 89 underexpressed genes in the high-risk score group opens new avenues for understanding the epigenetic regulation of cancer-promoting pathways. The role of SULF1, primarily known for its modulation of heparan sulfate proteoglycans and subsequent impact on growth factor signaling, highlights the intricate network of gene regulation contributing to tumorigenesis [21]. This finding dovetails with the work of Carolina M Vicente et al., who demonstrated the tumor-promoting role of SULF1 in colorectal cancer, suggesting that hypomethylation of SULF1 could be a critical step in rectal cancer progression [22].

The study further delved into the immune landscape alterations associated with the high-risk score group. Algorithms such as MCPcounter and QuanTIseq revealed decreased infiltration of several immune cell populations—including cytotoxic lymphocytes, NK cells, and myeloid dendritic cells—in high-risk patients, suggesting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that may contribute to poorer prognosis. This observation aligns with recent studies highlighting the role of the immune contexture in cancer progression and therapy response [23, 24]. The reduced presence of NK cells, in particular, which are critical for tumor surveillance, supports the notion that impaired cytotoxic immunity underlies adverse outcomes in high-risk patients [25]. However, three other algorithms—ESTIMATE, CIBERSORT, and ssGSEA—did not detect significant differences in immune cell infiltration between risk groups. Similarly, Immunophenoscore analysis showed no differences in predicted responsiveness to CTLA-4 or PD-1 blockade. These discordant findings likely reflect differences in algorithmic sensitivity and methodological assumptions; for example, deconvolution-based methods (e.g., CIBERSORT) may be less robust in datasets with limited sample heterogeneity or when cell-type signatures overlap. Nevertheless, the consistent signal of reduced cytotoxic immune components across multiple approaches reinforces the clinical relevance of an immunosuppressive phenotype in high-risk rectal cancer.

We conducted consensus clustering using the DM values of methylation-driven genes to categorize READ into two subtypes. Although no statistically significant prognostic differences were observed between the subtypes, it is important to note that this analysis was based on a limited number of samples with methylation data. Additionally, the MethylMix package excludes samples that do not correspond with the gene expression profiles when identifying methylation-driven genes. Therefore, the clinical relevance of this classification warrants further investigation with an increased sample size.

External validation is critical for assessing whether such a model is robust beyond the discovery cohort. In GSE103479, the model demonstrated modest discrimination at 1 year (AUC = 0.638) but weaker performance at later time points, and survival stratification trends that did not reach conventional significance for overall survival (p = 0.2) and were borderline for relapse-free survival (p = 0.062). Several factors can explain attenuation in external performance: differences in patient composition, treatment regimens, follow-up distributions, platform/normalization differences, and endpoint definitions. Nonetheless, the observed directional separation and measurable AUC at earlier time points provide evidence of portability and support the model’s broader applicability, while also emphasizing that multi-cohort calibration and potential recalibration may be necessary before clinical deployment.

The functional validation of the three model genes provides deeper mechanistic insights. ST6GALNAC1 showed highly consistent tumor–normal differential expression across both datasets, strengthening confidence that it is robustly dysregulated in external cohorts. CCNI2 and LINC00899 displayed dataset-dependent differential expression, which is not uncommon for genes influenced by tissue composition, technical platforms, and biological heterogeneity. To move beyond simple differential expression and improve stability of functional inference, we implemented a cross-dataset-averaged, correlation-ranked GSEA approach. This method reduces dependence on any single dataset’s correlation structure and generates consensus pathway associations. CCNI2 was identified as a pro-proliferative factor associated with the cell cycle and DNA repair. This aligns with its observed overexpression in tumor tissues. Conversely, ST6GALNAC1, which was significantly downregulated in tumors, appears to be involved in the maintenance of epithelial integrity and glycan biosynthesis. Its loss may facilitate EMT and metabolic reprogramming. LINC00899’s association with translation and oxidative phosphorylation suggests a complex role in regulating the energetic demands of the tumor. These findings are consistent with our GSEA of the high-risk group in TCGA, which suggested a “loss of molecular brakes” leading to hyper-activation of oncogenic pathways like Wnt and MAPK.

This study, while providing significant insights into the role of DNA methylation-driven genes in rectal cancer, has several limitations that warrant discussion. First, the identification of DNA methylation-driven genes and their prognostic value was performed using bioinformatics tools and statistical models that, despite rigorous validation, may not fully account for the complexity of gene regulation in vivo. Second, while GO and GSEA provide valuable insights into potential mechanistic pathways, these results are inherently correlative. Direct experimental validation is necessary to confirm the causal relationships and functional roles of these genes in rectal cancer development and progression. Third, our analysis of immune infiltration patterns offers a snapshot of the tumor microenvironment that may vary over time and with treatment, suggesting the need for longitudinal studies to better understand the dynamics of immune response in rectal cancer.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have identified and validated a DNA methylation-driven prognostic model for rectal cancer. Through external validation and comprehensive functional bioinformatic analysis, we have demonstrated that CCNI2, LINC00899, and ST6GALNAC1 are not only prognostic markers but also key players in the biological processes of proliferation, metabolism, and immune evasion. These genes represent promising targets for future epigenetic-based therapies and personalized management in rectal cancer.
