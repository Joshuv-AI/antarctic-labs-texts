Beyond the hype: A TAM-based analysis of blockchain adoption drivers in construction industry
Authors: Waeal J. Obidallah, Waleed Rashideh, Ahmed Mohammed Kamaruddeen, Taher Alzahrani, Yousef Alduraywish, Abdulaziz Alsahli, Nuha Alshuqayran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In complementing our existing knowledge of blockchain technology adoption in the construction industry, this research investigated how the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) applies to adopting blockchain technology among Saudi Arabian Construction companies. This study utilized cross-sectional data collection, causal research design, a quantitative research approach, and a simple random sampling technique to collect data from 248 Saudi Arabian construction companies. Partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) was utilized to analyze the data. The results showed that attitude toward adopting Blockchain (ATT) and perceived ease of use (PEU) are good predictors of behavioural intention to adopt Blockchain. The findings showed that when construction companies perceive the ease of use of Blockchain, they intend to adopt the technology. Attitude toward adopting Blockchain, directly and indirectly, influences behavioural intention to adopt the Technology. However, top management support only directly leads to intention once the companies perceive the usefulness of blockchain technology. This paper complements the existing literature on adopting Blockchain in the Saudi Arabian construction industry. The study provides insight into the influence of top management support, attitude toward adoption, and perceived ease of use in the blockchain adoption process.

Introduction

Saudi Arabia's strategic vision of transforming the kingdom into a developed nation by 2030 [1] has the potential to provide substantial business opportunities to the construction industry. However, increasing demand for quality, efficiency, and productivity pushes construction companies to adopt emerging technologies [2]. Hence, the survival of construction companies will largely depend on their ability to adopt new technologies, such as Blockchain, that enable them to improve their efficiency and provide high-quality services and products at a lower cost [3,4].

Several studies have examined the potential application and adoption of Blockchain technology in the global construction industry [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. However, few Studies focusing on Blockchain in the Saudi Arabian construction industry are available. Khan and Hakami [14] examined cryptocurrency from a usability perspective; Al-Rakhami and Al-Mashari [15] examined blockchain interoperability; and [16] examined the consumer perspective of Fintech. These studies did not focus on the Saudi Arabian construction industry.

Wang et al. [6] investigated the influence of technological, organizational, and environmental factors on Blockchain adoption in the construction industry. It is comprehensive to examine both the internal and external factors influencing the adoption of Blockchain in the construction industry. However, this paper argues that investigating the influence of internal factors alone while excluding any external factor on Blockchain in a study can provide a better understanding of internal predictors of blockchain adoption in the construction industry.

The global recognition of the importance of adopting digital technologies across sectors, including the construction industry, is undeniable. Blockchain technology offers significant benefits as one of the enablers of such digitalization [17,18]. However, its adoption is challenging. It requires people's support, skills, regulatory standards, and government support. Ebekozien et al. [19] have identified the barriers associated with Blockchain adoption, categorizing them as government (external) and employer and employee-related challenges (internal). Their findings underscore the influence of internal factors such as management commitment and support in adopting Blockchain.

Basiouni [20] assessed the customers' acceptance of Blockchain in Saudi Arabia through the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) without including any internal or external variable in the study. Sciarelli et al. [21] identified the determinants of users' behavioural adoption of Blockchain and included two internal organizational factors: reduced cause, efficiency, and security in the TAM model. TAM was developed to explain the theory of reasoning action (TRA) and the behavioural intention to adopt blockchain technology [[22], [23], [24]]. This research, therefore, excluded an external factor and examined the role of an organization's management support in the TAM model to explain the behavioural process of Blockchain adoption among construction companies.

This study complements our existing knowledge by investigating the direct effect of top management support and the extent to which TAM applies to construction companies in Saudi Arabian construction companies. Blockchain technology has gained considerable attention in recent years, increasing the demand for more research on its relevance beyond the financial sector [20]. This paper attempts to uncover the behavioural intention process for adopting blockchain technology in the Saudi Arabian Construction industry. It advances the existing literature on technology adoption, focusing on Blockchain. The paper has strengthened the field of technology adoption in the construction industry by adding top management support to the TAM model.

The paper seeks to shed light on the research question:

To what extent does TAM apply to the adoption of blockchain technology by construction companies in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia?

This study makes several significant contributions. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to shed light on the relevance of TAM on blockchain adoption process among the Saudi Arabian construction companies. Second, this study examines the influence of top management support in the blockchain adoption process among construction companies. Thirdly, this excludes the influence of external factors to enable a better understanding of how internal factors influence blockchain adoption.

The remaining parts of the paper are organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the relevant literature on Blockchain, theoretical models, and hypothesis development. Section 3 explains the methodology used in this paper. Section 4 presents the results, findings, and discussion. Section 5 concludes the paper and offers the implications of the research findings.

Literature review

Blockchain technology

Following Seebacher and Schuritz [25], this paper defines Blockchain as a distributed database shared among an agreed-upon peer-to-peer network. It consists of a linked sequence of blocks for storing timestamped transactions secured by cryptography (i.e., "hash") and verified by the network community. Blockchain differs from conventional technologies because it has a distributed peer-to-peer (P2P) feature.

Blockchain technology first conceptualized in the early 1990s [26] and popularized with cryptocurrencies [27], has emerged as a transformative force across industries. In essence, it's a distributed ledger technology that enables secure, transparent, and immutable record-keeping [28]. While blockchain comes in various forms, including public and private networks [29] its potential to revolutionize the construction industry is of particular interest to this study.

Blockchain is a technology that disrupts, revolutionizes, and drives innovation across all economic sectors, including the construction industry. The Technology is unique in eliminating intermediaries, transparency, consensus, and mutual trust among the blockchain network participants [9].

Blockchain can transform and digitalize the construction industry and solve its challenges. However, the industry's readiness to adopt the Technology further must be determined [5]. Despite its relevance and potential to enable solutions to challenges, the Technology has yet to be widely adopted in the construction industry [6].

Blockchain technology is applicable in managing the construction supply chain, managing payments, maintaining buildings, and managing construction. Kim et al. [7] have also ascertained the applications of Blockchain in the construction life cycle, project costing, managing change, tendering, and evaluating and managing procurement. Other applications of Blockchain technology include managing contracts, documents, funding, and real estate [5,9].

Smart contract

The development of a type of blockchain technology called Ethereum by Ref. [30] led to the emergence of smart contracts. These digital protocols allow two or more parties to automatically execute, verify, or enforce the contract between them once the contract conditions, specified in the form of a computer program, are met [31]. A smart contract has transformed traditional contracts and how business transactions are carried out. For example, the traditional payment procedure requires a supplier or contractor to send an invoice for goods or services and wait a few days before payment is received. Instead, software in the Blockchain can automatically trigger payments once the goods are received and acknowledged, thereby reducing the business transaction period [32]. The smart contract has also enhanced global business and trade, which involve cross-border payments. It handles cross-border transfers and eliminates the involvement of intermediary banks, reducing transaction charges and time [32].

Technology acceptance model

Previous research examining technology adoption often uses Davis et al.'s [22] technology acceptance model, while others use Parasuraman's [33] technology readiness model. Although TAM and Technology Readiness (TR) are interconnected [34], this paper argues that TR will be more relevant to technology readiness studies than technology behavioural adoption-related studies. For example, Sciarelli et al. [21] used TAM to identify users' behavioural adoption of Blockchain.

Okpala et al. [35] examined the technology acceptance model, the theory of planned behaviour and the unified theory of acceptance and found no statistically significant predictive power among them. The dimensions of Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), namely Performance Expectancy, Effort Expectancy, Social Influence, Facilitating Conditions, Hedonic Motivation, Price Value, and Habit, do not influence one another as in TAM. Following [6,20,21,36], this paper chooses TAM as the relevant model that explains all the stages of blockchain technology adoption among construction companies in Saudi Arabia.

TAM [22] has been widely used to explain users' behaviours before adopting a technology such as Blockchain. The model has helped predict a wide range of technologies. For example, the adoption of information systems [37], software [38], and e-commerce [39]. TAM theory suggests that the behavioural intention of a potential technology user or adopter is a strong indicator that the user will perform the behaviour [40]. The model also suggests that behavioural intention can be determined by two antecedent response behaviours toward the features of a technology, namely the affective and cognitive responses. The affective response describes the user's attitude (ATT) or individual feelings about a specific behaviour. The cognitive response describes the perceived ease of use (PEU) and perceived usefulness (PU) of a technology [41]. Hence, perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness are antecedents of attitude toward using a technology. However [40], noted that external factors might influence the affective and cognitive response when adopting the TAM to a specific context. Therefore, following [42,43], we introduced top management support to the TAM because scholars have tested the model in emerging-technology-related studies, such as determinants of blockchain adoption, by including external variables [44,45].

Methodology

Research method

Following [65], we used a three-phased research framework which consisted of conducting a structured literature review and survey design, validating the research instrument, collecting the data, and testing the hypotheses. The literature review enabled the identification of the TAM, which guided the development of this study's research instrument. In line with [44], the research instrument was reviewed by three experienced researchers and two professionals familiar with Blockchain in the construction industry.

Research design

This study used a quantitative research method and a causal research design to collect cross-sectional primary data from Saudi Arabian construction companies [66]. A simple random sampling technique was used to select the construction companies participating in the survey. The initial English-language version of the questionnaire was translated into Arabic and back into English [67].

Measurement

We used 21 items to measure the TAM constructs. Five items were for PEU, seven for PU, five for ATT, and four for BI, all adapted from Refs. [22,46,[68], [69], [70], [71], [72], [73]]. TMS was measured with four items and was adapted from Ref. [74]. A five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = “Not at all” to 5 = “completely true” was used for all items. Following [75], we stated the purpose of the study, confidentiality, and anonymity of the respondents on the questionnaire cover page.

Data collection

Following [76], the G-Power sample size determination tool was used to obtain 129 samples. However, to increase the generalizability of this study [35], we distributed 387 online questionnaires to construction companies operating within Saudi Arabia. We received 248 responses. Data was collected through a Google Form. The online questionnaire link was sent to the selected construction companies for them to fill out and submit online.

Data analysis and results

This study analyzed the primary data using the structural equation modelling technique (with the aid of the Smart PLS 3 software). Initially, SPSS software version 26 was used for data screening and descriptive and demographic statistics.

Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) is increasingly preferred in management research due to its robustness in handling complex models with multiple constructs and indicators. PLS-SEM is particularly advantageous for exploratory research where the primary goal is theory building rather than theory testing, making it suitable for emerging fields or new concepts [77]. Unlike covariance-based SEM, PLS-SEM can handle small sample sizes and non-normal data, offering flexibility in practical research settings [78]. Equally, the PLS-SEM approach supports reflective and formative measurement models, accommodating the diverse nature of data typically found in interdisciplinary studies, such as the application of Blockchain technology in the construction industry [79]. This approach is optimal for studies aiming to predict key target constructs or identify the driving constructs with the most substantial impact, as it focuses on maximizing the explained variance of dependent variables [80].

One of the paramount considerations was guaranteeing the respondents their anonymity during and after the survey exercise to prevent common method bias, in adherence with the recommendations of [81]. [82] also suggested that this method be used to observe common method bias for studies based on the PLS-SEM technique. Again, the survey instrument's scale items were also improved to reduce “method biases.” This was achieved by avoiding ambiguous statements in the questionnaire. According to Ref. [77], path coefficients should assess the statistical significance of the model with a minimum t-value of 1.65 and p-values of less than or equal to a 0.01 confidence interval. This study initiated the PLS bootstrapping with 5000 subsamples [94].

The same insignificant scenario about the hypothesized direct relationship between top management support and perceived usefulness was recorded due to the negative beta value (β = −0.357; t-value = 6.096; p ≤ 0.01). Similarly, when TMS was tested against BI, the result showed an insignificant relationship due to the negative beta value (β = −0.239; t-value = 4.315; p ≤ 0.01).

To evaluate indirect effects, the guidelines of [97] were adopted to examine the specific indirect effects. The initial significant relationship between TMS and BI improved with the introduction of PU, indicating a full mediation in that path. The mediation result involving PEU – ATT – BI, an initially significant path, was also significant when ATT was introduced. While the effect of PU on BI was insignificant (as reported in Fig. 3 and Table 6), the relationship remained diminished when ATT mediated the path, indicating a non-mediation scenario.

Discussion

This paper aims to contribute to the body of knowledge by investigating the behavioural factors that influence blockchain adoption in the Saudi Arabian construction industry. The study extended the TAM by introducing the construction company's top management support and observing the model's outcome. This study recorded exciting results, albeit contradicting those from some previous studies. The sampled construction companies' behavioural intention to adopt Blockchain was supported by the TAM predictors (ATT, PU, and PEU) with a slight exception of PU, which recorded a negative beta value—necessitating the rejection of the hypothesis. This aligns with previous findings such as [21,98]. Also, this study's significant relationship between PEU and ATT aligns with previous studies [88], implying that the ease with which Blockchain is adopted will improve the professionals' attitude toward it. Like [99], this study also found a positive relationship between TMS and ATT. This shows the importance of construction firms' senior management support in attitudinal change toward blockchain adoption. Our study also intends to enhance the blockchain body of knowledge by considering the influence of TMS on BI and PU. While our study complements the findings in previous studies [21], our study recorded a different outcome. We found that TMS does not directly influence PU and BI. However, our study has shown significant strength and effects of the critical predictors of BI within the context of the construction industry.

This study presented beneficial results for construction professionals and the industry. The insignificant relationships between PU on both ATT and BI, as well as TMS on BI, suggest a crucial insight for sampled firms: adopting Blockchain technology requires a paradigm shift away from traditional construction methods. The lack of significant direct effects indicates that these firms may not yet perceive the clear advantages of Blockchain, reflecting a potential resistance or lack of awareness, as indicated in previous studies [100]. TMS's insignificant influence on BI could also be explained by the management's irresponsive attitude toward supporting blockchain technology adoption. Studies have shown that construction firms' top managers are always slow to replace traditional techniques with new technology [101].

Furthermore, this study's findings show that ATT is the most significant predictor of BI; hence, practitioners should initiate rigorous awareness among the project team to leverage their attitude toward blockchain technology adoption. The result of the PEU and PU path was strongly significant, which implies that blockchain technology users perceive its adoption/usage to be without hassles. Expectedly, this ease of use will assist construction companies in garnering the utmost value from utilising blockchain technology in their construction project delivery.

Finally, PU indirectly influenced the TMS – BI relationship, implying a full mediation. This shows that top managers must continuously prompt their workers toward adopting blockchain technology to achieve the desired intention. The insignificant indirect effects of ATT on the PU – BI path are expected because the direct path was also not significant. This could be because the construction companies’ intention to adopt blockchain technology is explained by other variables that differ from PU. While this might slightly deviate from previous studies such as [86,87], it also indicates the uniqueness of our findings.

Practical and theoretical implications

Practical implications

The first contribution is that the study indicates the perceived ease of use (PEU), as well as the attitude towards adopting blockchain technology (ATT), are two of the most important factors in the behavioural intention (BI) of using blockchain technology. Companies in the construction industry should look for easy-to-use blockchain solutions and encourage a positive attitude toward these technologies among their employees. Education and training programs in the form of workshops that help employees understand and get familiar with Blockchain can go a long way. The study also shows that top management support (TMS) indirectly affects the intention to adopt Blockchain through perceived usefulness (PU). It is then that employees understand the added value of the blockchain technology that their company hires, allowing direct support from the management to be more beneficial. Hence, construction companies should concentrate on showcasing the tangible benefits of Blockchain that they can achieve regardless of the site or facility, i.e., efficiency, transparency, and security, to be supported by the entirety of their organizational fabric. Thirdly, it provides a pointer for the construction companies in Saudi Arabia towards the usage of blockchain technology. With the assistance of the TAM framework, the companies should be able to address those inhibitors in a structured manner to create interventions that encourage a favourable perception of blockchain technology.

Theoretical implications

Theoretically, this study identifies several contributions to the extant literature on technology adoption in the construction industry. The paper extends TAM to include TMS as an additional construct to explain the drivers of blockchain adoption more comprehensively. This extension helps to authenticate that the TAM is relevant in explaining the adoption behaviour of emerging technologies in a particular industrial setting. The study also contributes to the broader space of blockchain adoption studies about an industry and region over which very little has been discussed in the existing literature. These results reveal the significance of internal organizational factors (e.g., management support and perceived ease of use) in adopting. This contrasts with several other studies that focus on external mechanisms, revealing a more multifaceted understanding of the determinants of blockchain adoption. Secondly, the study deploys PLS in modelling the complex relationship between various constructs, thereby affirming the robustness and suitability of PLS-SEM for this type of complex analytical inquiry. This methodological insight may instruct future technology adoption studies, especially when data normality and sample size are part of the issue.

Conclusion

Using the Technology Acceptance Model, this paper investigated the behavioural antecedents of blockchain adoption among construction companies operating in Saudi Arabia. This also extends the TAM by examining the influence of top management support on blockchain technology adoption among construction companies operating in Saudi Arabia. This paper shows that perceptions of Blockchain are relevant in explaining the behaviour of the Saudi Arabian construction companies’ adoption of blockchain technology. More precisely, attitude toward adoption (ATT), perceived ease of use (PEU), and top management support are important indicators of blockchain technology adoption among construction companies. This research finding could provide insight into the relevance of the TAM and how the model applies to the Saudi Arabian construction industry scenario regarding blockchain adoption. Understanding the behavioural antecedents can assist the Saudi Arabian government and the construction industry regulatory agency develop policies promoting and encouraging blockchain adoption. A significant limitation of this paper is that it uses a cross-sectional technique to collect data once from construction companies. Longitudinal data collection might provide additional insight into blockchain technology adoption among construction companies. Future research could explore why perceived usefulness (PU) is not a good indicator of blockchain technology adoption among construction companies.

Future scope based on research gaps and limitations

This study used cross-sectional data collection and will not measure changes that would have happened over time. It therefore recommends a longitudinal study, where changes or effects that would have occurred over time in the construction industry's different aspects of blockchain technology are tracked. This research focused on the construction companies in Saudi Arabia. Further research in another region may give a clearer insight into the extent of blockchain use in the global construction industry. Comparative studies can illuminate peculiar challenges and enablers of Blockchain. The quantitative approach used in this study provided broad insights into the factors influencing blockchain adoption. However, qualitative research, such as case studies and interviews, could provide insights into the specific challenges and successes experienced by companies adopting blockchain technology. This would help in understanding the practical implementation nuances. Although this study excluded external factors and focused on internal organizational aspects, future research should explore the influence of external factors such as regulatory frameworks, market dynamics, and technological advancements. Understanding how these external elements interact with internal factors could provide a different view of blockchain adoption. Comparative studies could identify industry-specific barriers and facilitators. Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) was appropriate for this study. However, future research could benefit from using advanced analytical methods, such as machine learning and artificial intelligence, to predict blockchain adoption patterns and identify hidden trends in the data.
