Emergence of tet(X6) and blaIMP−45 in a multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas asiatica strain isolated from chicken
Authors: Xiaowei Lin, Renxi Zhou, Xinfeng Zhao, Zelin Yan, Rong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate a Pseudomonas asiatica (P. asiatica) strain J68 isolated from chicken in a poultry that produced tigecycline resistance gene tet(X6), carbapenem resistance gene blaIMP−45 and other multiple antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs).MethodsStrain J68 was identified using MALDI-TOF MS and average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis. Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined by broth microdilution method. PCR screening, conjugation assay and whole-genome sequencing were performed to characterize the strain J68.ResultsThe strain J68 was identified as P. asiatica, it exhibited resistance to multiple antibiotics, including tigecycline and carbapenem. Subsequent analysis revealed that 24 antimicrobial resistance genes, including the tigecycline resistance gene tet(X6) and the carbapenemase gene blaIMP−45 were identified in the genome of the strain J68. The isolate belonged to sequence type ST15.ConclusionFor the first time, we report the emergence of tet(X6)- and blaIMP−45-positive P. asiatica strain isolated from a poultry source. The strain J68 exhibited resistance to last-line antibiotics, including tigecycline and carbapenems. The emergence of tigecycline resistance gene tet(X6), along with carbapenem resistance gene blaIMP−45 in strain of chicken origin is noteworthy. Our findings highlight the need for continued monitoring of resistance genes in agricultural environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05367-z.

Introduction

Pseudomonas asiatica (P. asiatica) is a member of the Pseudomonas putida group and is phylogenetically closely related to Pseudomonas monteilii (P. monteilii) and Pseudomonas putida (P. putida). This bacterial species was initially isolated from clinical specimens. Mari Tohya et al. reported two strains isolated from stool samples of patients hospitalized with diarrhea in Japan as well as another strain isolated from a urine sample of a patient hospitalized with urinary tract infection in Myanmar. All three strains were identified as P. asiatica [1, 2]. Beyond its clinical relevance, P. asiatica is also relevant to animal health, Taiya Sultana et al. reported that P. asiatica was an emerging mastitis pathogen in dairy cows [3]. Additionally, P. asiatica has been recovered from environmental samples including soil and water [4]. Its isolation across diverse ecological niches highlights its remarkable adaptability and capacity for widespread dissemination.

The emergence and dissemination of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria has been a serious problem worldwide. Carbapenem resistance is often mediated by the production of carbapenemase, including metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), such as IMP-, NDM- and VIM-type enzymes [5]. Carbapenem resistant P. asiatica has been reported in Myanmar and Japan [1, 6], with strains producing NDM-1 or VIM-2, indicating that MBL-producing P. asiatica strains are spreading worldwide.

Tigecycline is one of the last-resort antimicrobial agents to treat infections caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria, especially carbapenem resistant Gram-negative pathogens [7]. However, the increasing use of tigecycline has been accompanied by the emergence of its resistance. The tet(X) gene was first identified on the chromosome of the Bacteroides fragilis [8, 9]. Subsequently, tet(X) and its variants (at least 22 variants) have been reported in various species from humans, animals and environment [10–12]. The tet(X6) gene was initially reported in Proteus spp [13]., which confers high-level tigecycline resistance. These findings highlight the potential for widespread dissemination of tigecycline resistance. However, the prevalence and genetic determinants of tigecycline resistance in P. asiatica remain largely unexplored.

The emergence of pathogens harboring multiple last-line antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) seriously compromises the effectiveness of clinical therapy. In this study, we report a multidrug-resistant P. asiatica strain J68 of chicken origin producing tet(X6), blaIMP−45 and other multiple ARGs. Continued surveillance and further epidemiological studies are needed to assess the prevalence of such strains.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolate and collection

Strain J68 was isolated from a chicken fecal sample in a poultry farm in Hangzhou, China in 2018. Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) was conducted to identify the bacterial species. The tigecycline resistance gene tet(X6) was identified using PCR and Sanger sequencing as described previously [14].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The antimicrobial susceptibility of 16 antibiotics (amikacin, gentamicin, meropenem, ampicillin, aztreonam, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefoperazone/sulbactam, ciprofloxacin, florfenicol, chloramphenicol, colistin, tigecycline, eravacycline, ceftazidime/avibactam and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole) for strain J68 were determined using the broth microdilution method following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [15]. The reagents were provided by Zhuhai DL Biotech (https://en.medicaldl.com) to perform antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). The results were interpreted according to CLSI and European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) standards (Table S2). Owing to the lack of official breakpoints for P. asiatica, CLSI breakpoints for Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) were used. Drugs without Pseudomonas-specific criteria were interpreted by CLSI Enterobacterales breakpoints. The breakpoint defined by EUCAST criteria (MIC ≥ 2 mg/L) for Gram-negative bacteria was used to classify tigecycline resistance [16]. Florfenicol was interpreted by veterinary susceptibility criteria. P. aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 was served as quality control.

Conjugation experiments

A conjugation assay was performed using filter-mating method [17], strain J68 was used as the donor, and sodium azide-resistant E. coli J53 was used as the recipient. Transconjugants were selected on Mueller Hinton (MH) agar plates containing 2 µg/mL tigecycline and 300 µg/mL sodium azide. MALDI-TOF MS and PCR were used to confirm the presumptive transconjugants.

Whole-genome sequencing and analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted using the HiPure Bacteria DNA Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted genome was sent to Novogene (China) for whole genome sequencing (WGS) on the Illumina NextSeq platform. Genome de novo assembly was performed using SPAdes version 3.15.1 (https://bioinf.spbau.ru/spades). The quality of the genome assembly was assessed using CheckM version 1.2.4, genome completeness and contamination were calculated accordingly. Further species identification was carried out by using average nucleotide identity (ANI) (https://jspecies.ribohost.com/jspeciesws/), the within-species ANI cut-off value was set at 95%. Digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) analysis was performed using Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC) version 4.0 (http://ggdc.dsmz.de), the within-species DDH cut-off values was set at 70%. Multilocus Sequence typing (MLST) was conducted by using the online MLST database (https://pubmlst.org/). ARGs were analyzed by using ResFinder version 4.7.2 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/ResFinder/) with default parameters (identity threshold ≥ 80% and coverage threshold ≥ 60%). Mobile genetic elements (MGEs) were analyzed by using MobileElementFinder vesion 1.0.3 and VRprofile2 (https://tool2-mml.sjtu.edu.cn/VRprofile/home.php) with default parameters. The contigs were annotated using Prokka version 1.2.0 with default parameters. The genetic contents of tet(X6) and blaIMP−45 were analyzed by comparing with similar items available in the NCBI database, Easyfig version 2.2.5 was used to visualize the genetic context comparisons. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the balanced minimum evolution (BME) method inplemented in the FastME version 2.1.6.1.

Results

Identification and antimicrobial susceptibility of strain J68

Strain J68 was tentatively identified as P. putida by MALDI-TOF MS. Bacterial identification analysis based on ANI revealed that the strain J68 belonged to P. asiatica. The best match type-strain for strain J68 is P. asiatica RYU5 (genome accession number GCA_009932335.1) with the ANI value of 99.89% (assembly coverage 80.34%). The dDDH value between J68 and RYU5 was 99.1%.

The AST revealed that this isolate was resistant to multiple antibiotics, including amikacin, gentamicin, meropenem, ampicillin, aztreonam, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefoperazone/sulbactam, ciprofloxacin, florfenicol, chloramphenicol, tigecycline, ceftazidime/avibactam and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole; however, it remained susceptible to colistin. The MIC of eravacycline is 2 mg/L, but the breakpoint for Pseudomonas against eravacycline has not been defined. The antimicrobial resistance profiles and mechanisms of resistance of strain J68 were shown in Table 1. The presence of tet(X6) gene was detected in further screening of tigecycline resistance gene using PCR and Sanger sequencing.

Conjugation

Our results showed no transconjugant was obtained, suggesting that strain J68 not transferable to E. coli J53 under the tested conditions.

Whole genome sequencing results

Whole genome sequencing of strain J68 revealed a full-length genome of 6897484 bp with a total G + C content of 61.55%. The genome comprised 6862 predicted open reading frames (ORFs) (Fig. 1; Table S3). Quality assessment using CheckM v1.2.4 revealed a completeness of 99.68% and contamination of 4.07%. MLST analysis suggested that strain J68 belonged to sequence type ST15. A total of 24 ARGs were identified in strain J68, conferring resistance to different classes of antimicrobials (Table S1), including aminoglycosides [aph(3’)-Ia, aph(3’)-VIa, aph(3’’)-Ib, aph(4)-Ia, aph(6)-Id, aac(3)-IV, aac(6’)-Ib3, aac(6’)-IIa, aadA1 and armA], β-lactams and carbapenems [blaCTX−M−3, blaTEM−1B, blaOXA−1 and blaIMP−45], fluoroquinolones [qnrVC1], florfenicol [floR], Chloramphenicol [catB3, catB8], tetracycline [tet(X6) and tet(G)], sulfonamides [sul1], macrolides [msr(E) and mph(E)] and rifampin [arr-3].

Discussion

The identification of strain J68 as ST15 is noteworthy in light of recent genomic surveillance studies. Urbanowicz et al. (2022) Reported that ST15 was identified as the major clone of P. asiatica in hospital (n = 9/11) [18]. Our strain J68, isolated from a poultry farm in China, shares the same ST15 genotype with these clinical isolates, suggesting that ST15 represents a potentially international lineage.

Tigecycline and carbapenems are crucial in the treatment of severe clinical infections. Although tigecycline is prohibited for use in the livestock industry, tetracycline antibiotics are still extensively applied in poultry farming [19]. This antimicrobial selective pressure has contributed to the accumulation of tetracycline resistant pathogens, part of which exhibit cross-resistance against tigecycline.

To our knowledge, we are the first to identify tet(X6) in a chicken-derived P. asiatica strain. The genetic context of tet(X6) in P. asiatica J68 (tet(X6)-abh-guaA-ISVsa3) is nearly identical to other reported studies [20, 21], indicating this genetic structure is maintained across different bacterial species. Furthermore, ISVsa3 has been confirmed to be related to the transfer of tet(X6) [22].

IMP-45 was first reported in 2014 in a P. aeruginosa strain. Subsequently, blaIMP−45 was identified in P. aeruginosa isolates from various regions in China [23–25], indicating that blaIMP−45 has disseminated in the country. Identification of blaIMP−45 in clinical P. putida has been reported [23], while our study reports a P. asiatica strain J68 of chicken origin that produced blaIMP−45 for the first time. In our study, blaIMP−45 was found within an integron structure, and the genetic context of aac(6’)-blaIMP−45-hp-blaOXA−1-catB3 was nearly identical to previous reports, suggesting that this genetic context is conserved across different Pseudomonas species.

The co-occurrence of tet(X6) and blaIMP−45 in a single P. asiatica isolate highlights the convergence of resistance mechanisms targeting last-line antibiotics. The detection of the same sequence type in both clinical and agricultural settings across different geographic regions raises concern about the potential international dissemination of P. asiatica ST15 and its capacity to serve as a vector for diverse resistance genes. Furthermore, the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria in food-producing animals is a growing concern within the One Health framework, largely due to the potential for foodborne transmission to humans.

Nevertheless, this study was based on a single P. asiatica isolate harboring multidrug-resistance genes. Accordingly, larger-scale surveillance is necessary to assess the true prevalence and geographic distribution of such strains. Due to the limitations of short-read sequencing, the exact structures of the plasmid and integron associated with tet(X6) and blaIMP−45 in strain J68 could not be fully resolved; therefore, further research is required.

Conclusion

We report the emergence of tet(X6) and blaIMP−45 in P. asiatica for the first time. The strain J68 exhibited resistance to multiple antibiotics, including last-line antibiotics. The rising threat of antibiotic resistance, driven by the spread of crucial resistance genes such as tet(X6) and blaIMP−45, has drawn increasing public health attention. These findings support the need for stricter regulation and more prudent use of antibiotic in veterinary medicine to prevent the further dissemination of such strains.
