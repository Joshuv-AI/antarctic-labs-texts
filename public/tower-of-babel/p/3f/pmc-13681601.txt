The 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine: The Treg, FoxP3, and STAT-regulated immune network
Authors: Xin-Yuan Fu
Published: 2025

The 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, awarded for groundbreaking research on regulatory T cells (Tregs) and peripheral immune regulation, marks a major milestone in our understanding of immune homeostasis. At the same time, this achievement highlights the broader framework of signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) protein–mediated regulation, which orchestrates the differentiation and balance of multiple T helper (Th) cell subsets. Each Th lineage is guided by distinct cytokines that activate specific STAT proteins, thereby shaping the overall equilibrium of the peripheral immune system [1], [2].

Tregs—specialized CD4+ T cells that maintain immune tolerance through the STAT5–CD25–FoxP3 axis—represent a vital component of this system. Beyond Tregs, a number of other Th lineages collectively form the STAT-regulated immune network.

SIGNIFICANCE OF THE 2025 NOBEL PRIZE

The Nobel recognition of Treg research justly emphasizes the discovery of the FoxP3–Treg pathway as a central mechanism for immune tolerance. Yet, it is important to recognize a much broader STAT-governed immune architecture that coordinates diverse Th cell lineages to maintain systemic immune balance. These STAT-defined programs collectively enable the immune system to respond precisely to varied pathogenic and inflammatory challenges. The Treg pathway, while crucial, thus represents one of several specialized outcomes within this unified regulatory framework.

OUTSTANDING QUESTIONS: DIVERGENT STAT5 ROLES IN TREG AND THGM LINEAGES

A central unresolved question concerns the divergent roles of STAT5 in Treg and ThGM differentiation. Although STAT5 signaling promotes both lineages, their immunological functions are often opposing. Tregs suppress immune activation and inflammation, whereas ThGM cells, as demonstrated by Sheng et al., enhance inflammatory responses [8].

Notably, T cell-specific STAT5 knockout in mice leads to profound immune suppression, underscoring the paradoxical dual functions of STAT5. Understanding how a single STAT factor directs such opposing outcomes remains an open and important challenge for future immunological research.

HISTORICAL FOUNDATIONS

Our current understanding of T-cell biology rests on key breakthroughs over the past 40 years. Jacques F. (Jack) Miller discovered the function of the thymus and identified T cells, establishing the fundamental distinction between T and B lymphocytes—pioneering work that laid the foundation for modern cellular immunology, long before the discovery of Tregs [9]. Tak Wah Mak and Mark M. Davis independently codiscovered the T-cell receptor (TCR) in the early 1980s, defining the molecular basis of antigen recognition by T cells [10,11]. Tim Mosmann and Robert L. Coffman elucidated the Th1/Th2 paradigm, revolutionizing our understanding of helper T-cell heterogeneity and cytokine-mediated immune regulation [12]. Together, these discoveries have had a transformative impact on immunology and therapeutic development—and arguably represent earlier and broader contributions than those recognized by the 2025 Nobel Prize for Treg research.
