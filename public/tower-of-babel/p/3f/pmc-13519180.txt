Association of semaglutide use with kidney outcomes in adults with ADPKD: A propensity score–matched real-world study
Authors: To-Pang Chen, Shang-Feng Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAutosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is a progressive hereditary kidney disorder with limited treatment options. Although semaglutide has demonstrated kidney-protective effects in other populations, its renal effects in ADPKD remain unknown.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective propensity score–matched cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network. Adults with ADPKD between 2000 and 2024 were identified. Patients receiving sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, tolvaptan, or other glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists were excluded. Semaglutide users were matched 1:1 with non-users. Outcomes included creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD), end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), body mass index (BMI), acute kidney injury (AKI), urinary tract infection (UTI), volume depletion, and all-cause mortality.ResultsAmong 45,613 adults with ADPKD, 323 semaglutide users and 31,300 non-users met the eligibility criteria; 320 matched pairs were analyzed. At follow-up, semaglutide users had a higher mean creatinine-based eGFR than non-users (57.52 ± 28.19 vs. 50.93 ± 33.80 mL/min/1.73 m2; p = 0.013) and a lower risk of progression to stage 5 CKD (hazard ratio, 0.447; 95% confidence interval, 0.230–0.872). No significant differences were observed in ESKD, AKI, or UTI. Semaglutide use was also associated with lower incidences of volume depletion and all-cause mortality.ConclusionsSemaglutide use was associated with higher follow-up eGFR of creatinine-based eGFR and a lower risk of stage 5 CKD in patients with ADPKD. Because semaglutide-induced weight loss and reductions in muscle mass may lower serum creatinine independently of true glomerular filtration, the observed preservation of creatinine-based eGFR should be interpreted cautiously. Given the observational design, limited follow-up, and potential residual confounding, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective studies.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is highly prevalent among individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM). 1 CKD is defined by persistent albuminuria and/or a sustained reduction in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) for at least three months. 2 When CKD develops in individuals with diabetes, the condition is broadly referred to as diabetic kidney disease (DKD). 1 Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i)3,4 are established treatments not only for DKD but also for several forms of non-diabetic CKD. However, major SGLT2i trials such as DAPA-CKD 3 and EMPA-Kidney 4 excluded patients with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD). Nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists have emerged as a key therapeutic option for DKD, 5 whereas their role in non-DKD etiologies, including ADPKD, is being evaluated in the ongoing FIND-CKD trial (NCT05047263). 6

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)–based therapies, including GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) and dual GLP-1/glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonists, exert glycemic benefits through multiple mechanisms such as augmentation of glucose-dependent insulin secretion, delayed gastric emptying, appetite suppression, and reduction of postprandial glucagon levels. Semaglutide, a long-acting GLP-1RA, recently demonstrated significant renal and cardiovascular benefits in the FLOW trial. 7 This trial enrolled 3533 participants with type 2 diabetes, a mean HbA1c of 7.8%, baseline eGFR between 25 and 75 mL/min/1.73 m2, and albuminuria—with approximately two-thirds having a urine albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥300 mg/g—despite maximally tolerated ACE inhibitor or ARB therapy. Semaglutide significantly reduced the risk of major kidney disease events in patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD. The primary outcome was a composite of kidney failure (dialysis, transplantation, or eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2), a sustained ≥50% decline in eGFR, or death from kidney-related or cardiovascular causes. In addition, semaglutide slowed eGFR decline and reduced the risks of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality. These findings have generated substantial interest in the potential renoprotective effects of semaglutide across different kidney disease populations. Notably, these renoprotective effects appear to be largely independent of glucose lowering and may be mediated by anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, or other pleiotropic mechanisms. However, no clinical evidence currently supports its efficacy in non-DM–related CKD.

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) affects approximately 1 in 1000 individuals and is a major genetic cause of CKD. 8 The majority of affected patients ultimately progress to ESKD requiring renal replacement therapy. Tolvaptan is currently the only approved disease-modifying therapy for ADPKD,9,10 while RAAS blockade provides standard supportive management for ADPKD-related CKD. Emerging preclinical data suggest that GLP-1RA may modulate cystogenesis, 11 and a recent review has also highlighted this potential through metabolic reprogramming mechanisms. 12 Nevertheless, safety concerns persist due to gastrointestinal adverse effects of GLP-1RA, which may compromise hydration status—a critical component of ADPKD management. 13 A phase 2 randomized controlled trial evaluating tirzepatide in ADPKD (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06582875) is ongoing but is not expected to be completed until 2029. To date, no clinical study has directly evaluated the impact of GLP-1RA on ADPKD outcomes.

Increasing evidence suggests that metabolic dysfunction may contribute to the progression of ADPKD.14,15 Obesity, 14 insulin resistance, 16 and other components of metabolic syndrome have been associated with larger total kidney volume, faster cyst growth, and more rapid decline in kidney function. In addition, obesity-related inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered energy metabolism may further promote cyst expansion and interstitial fibrosis. Clinical studies have similarly reported associations between higher body mass index and accelerated ADPKD progression, while dietary interventions 17 and caloric restriction have shown potential benefits in slowing kidney enlargement. 18 Collectively, these findings have generated increasing interest in metabolic-targeted therapies as potential disease-modifying approaches in ADPKD.

Given these knowledge gaps, we conducted a real-world study using a global collaborative network database and applied rigorous propensity score matching (PSM) to balance baseline characteristics. Our objective was to investigate the renal effects of semaglutide in patients with ADPKD.

Materials and methods

Database of TriNetx

We conducted this study using the TriNetX Research Network, a global federated real-world data platform that aggregates information from 163 healthcare organizations and includes records from more than 186 million individuals across 15 countries. TriNetX has supported over 2,300 peer-reviewed publications indexed in PubMed and provides a harmonized, de-identified dataset derived from electronic health records. Available data elements include demographics, diagnoses (ICD-10-CM), procedures (ICD-10-PCS and CPT), clinical terminologies (SNOMED CT), medications (Veterans Affairs National Formulary), laboratory measurements (LOINC), and healthcare utilization metrics. Contributing institutions span diverse care settings ranging from tertiary referral hospitals to primary care clinics and specialty centers, providing a robust and representative foundation for real-world analyses.

Ethics statement

All data accessed through the TriNetX Research Network are fully de-identified; therefore, individual informed consent was not required. The platform complies with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), ensuring stringent data privacy and security protections. The Western Institutional Review Board (WIRB) has reviewed TriNetX and granted a waiver of informed consent, as only aggregated results and anonymized statistical outputs are made available to researchers. The use of TriNetX for the present analysis was further approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (Approval No. CE24589B; approval date: November 25, 2024). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable data protection laws. The study used de-identified data, and all analyses were conducted in compliance with institutional and regulatory requirements. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975, as revised in 2024.

Study design

Patients were identified using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, and all eligible individuals meeting study criteria during the study period were included in the analysis. The patient selection process is summarized in Figure 1(a), and the overall study design for the semaglutide and non-semaglutide cohorts is illustrated in Figures 1(b) and (c) respectively. In the semaglutide cohort (Figure 1(b)), the index event was defined as the initiation of semaglutide therapy, with the index date corresponding to the first diagnosis of ADPKD. In the non-semaglutide cohort (Figure 1(c)), the index event was defined as an ambulatory visit, with the requirement that the initial diagnosis of ADPKD occurred prior to this index date. To mitigate immortal time bias, the two cohorts were propensity score matched (PSM) based on all baseline variables associated with renal outcomes. Patients were excluded from the control group if they had received semaglutide at any point during the study period. Furthermore, to isolate the specific effect of semaglutide, we excluded patients in both groups who had ever received SGLT2 inhibitors, tolvaptan, or other GLP-1 receptor agonists (including exenatide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, oral semaglutide, and tirzepatide).Figure 1.Flowchart of cohort construction and study designs for semaglutide and non-semaglutide groups. For both cohorts, baseline characteristics were evaluated using clinical data recorded from one year up to one day before the index date to ensure incorporation of the most current pre-exposure information. To reduce the risk of reverse causality, outcome evaluation commenced one month after the index date and continued through the end of follow-up. Only adults aged ≥18 years at the time of index were eligible for inclusion. The study period, encompassing both baseline assessment and outcome observation, extended from January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2024. The reporting of this retrospective cohort study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 19

Definition of population

The study population was identified using the ICD-10-CM code Q61.2, corresponding to ADPKD. Individuals younger than 18 years were excluded, in accordance with the ethical regulations of the Institutional Review Board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital. The study period, encompassing baseline assessment and outcome observation, extended from January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2024.

Within the TriNetX network, ADPKD cases were defined as patients with a documented diagnosis (ICD-10-CM Q61.2) occurring at least one day prior to any record of semaglutide exposure. Semaglutide use was identified using the RxNorm code 1991302, and patients who received oral semaglutide were excluded. Medication exposure was defined using the following coding systems: SGLT2 inhibitors (ATC code 10BK), tolvaptan (RxNorm 358257), exenatide (RxNorm 60548), dulaglutide (RxNorm 1551291), liraglutide (RxNorm 475968), and tirzepatide (RxNorm 2601723).

For subgroup analyses, patients were stratified by sex (male vs. female), age (18–65 years vs. >65 years), DM status, and treatment intensity, defined by cumulative semaglutide prescription counts (≥1, ≥3, ≥6, and ≥12 refills). In addition, we assessed the outcomes associated with semaglutide use within U.S.-based population subgroups.

Baseline data collection

Propensity score matching was performed using 12 prespecified baseline covariates selected a priori based on their known associations with kidney disease progression and treatment allocation. These covariates included age at index date, sex, race (White or Asian), systolic blood pressure (mmHg; TNX curated code 9085), body mass index (kg/m2; LOINC 39156-5), DM (ICD-10-CM E08–E13), ischemic heart disease (I20–I25), heart failure (I50), use of agents acting on the renin–angiotensin system (ATC C09), use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (ATC M01A), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), calculated using the CKD-EPI 2021 equation (LOINC 98979-8), and urinary protein–creatinine ratio (mg/g; LOINC LG34791-0).

All baseline variables were obtained from data recorded during the one-year period preceding the index date (from 365 days to 1 day prior) to ensure clinical relevance and minimize the risk of immortal time bias. These covariates were selected based on prior evidence demonstrating their association with renal function.

Definition of outcome

The renal outcomes included eGFR, calculated using the CKD-EPI equation, stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD; ICD-10-CM N18.5), end-stage kidney disease (ESKD; ICD-10-CM N18.6), and acute kidney injury (AKI; ICD-10-CM N17). Stage 5 CKD and ESKD were evaluated as separate renal outcomes based on distinct administrative diagnostic codes. Because these endpoints were identified through coding rather than longitudinal clinical staging, they were analyzed independently and were not assumed to represent a strictly hierarchical progression sequence.

Additional outcomes included BMI, urinary tract infection (N39.0), and all-cause mortality. To reduce the risk of reverse causality, outcome assessment began one month after the index date. To maintain a comparable follow-up duration between groups, the maximum follow-up period was limited to 2 years.

To evaluate potential safety concerns related to volume status, additional outcomes included volume depletion (ICD-10-CM E86), hypotension (ICD-10-CM I95), and AKI (ICD-10-CM N17). These outcomes were assessed because adequate hydration is a cornerstone of ADPKD management and gastrointestinal adverse effects associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists may potentially affect fluid balance and renal perfusion.

Statistical analyses

Propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted within the TriNetX platform to generate 1:1 matched cohorts with comparable baseline characteristics. Greedy nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations was used to minimize systematic differences between treatment groups. Matching was performed using complete and available covariates and was repeated independently for each subgroup analysis to ensure appropriate balance across stratified populations.

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values <0.1 indicating satisfactory balance.

Clinical outcomes were compared using hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Time-to-event analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier estimates, and the proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using scaled Schoenfeld residuals available within the TriNetX analytics environment. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient selection algorithms

The detailed patient selection algorithm is illustrated in Figure 1(a). Initially, data were extracted from 188,655,054 patients across 163 healthcare organizations. Among them, 45,613 patients had a diagnosis of ADPKD. We then restricted the cohort to adult patients and applied a time frame from January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2024. To minimize confounding, patients who had used medications potentially affecting the evaluation were excluded, including SGLT2 inhibitors (n = 1,311), tolvaptan (n = 1,946), exenatide (n = 40), dulaglutide (n = 292), liraglutide (n = 176), oral semaglutide (n = 92), and tirzepatide (n = 174). After these exclusions, 323 patients had a history of semaglutide use, while 31,300 had no prior semaglutide exposure. Using 1:1 propensity score matching based on 12 covariates, a final cohort of 640 patients (320 in each group) was selected for subsequent outcome analysis.

After 1:1 matching, the two groups achieved good covariate balance across all major demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables, with all SMDs reduced to <0.25, and most <0.10. Age (53.3 ± 13.2 vs. 55.2 ± 15.5 years; SMD = 0.098), sex distribution (34.7% vs. 36.6% male; SMD = 0.039), and racial composition became comparable. The mean BMI remained slightly higher in the semaglutide group (35.0 ± 6.5 vs. 33.4 ± 7.1 kg/m2), but overall BMI category distributions were substantially improved. Notably, both groups had a high baseline BMI, reflecting the substantial burden of obesity in this study population and supporting the clinical relevance of evaluating weight-related effects of semaglutide in ADPKD. Key comorbidities—including DM, ischemic heart disease, and heart failure—were also well balanced. Laboratory profiles, including eGFR, UPCR, and systolic blood pressure, showed no statistically significant differences post-matching. Medication use, including renin–angiotensin system agents and NSAIDs, was also comparable between groups. Overall, PSM effectively minimized baseline differences, enabling a more reliable comparison of clinical outcomes between semaglutide and non-semaglutide users (Supplementary Figure 1). The overall cohort consisted of 97% of patients from U.S. regions and 3% from regions outside the United States (Supplementary Figure 2).

Regarding renal outcomes, progression to stage 5 CKD occurred less frequently in the semaglutide group (11 vs. 30 events), corresponding to a significantly lower risk (HR, 0.447; 95% CI, 0.230–0.872; P = 0.014). No statistically significant difference was observed in the incidence of ESKD (12 vs. 23 events; HR, 0.675; 95% CI, 0.347–1.315; P = 0.243). Among safety outcomes, the incidence of UTI, hypotension, and AKI did not differ significantly between the two groups. However, volume depletion was less frequent in semaglutide-treated patients (19 vs. 34 events), with a significantly lower risk (HR, 0.559; 95% CI, 0.325–0.960; P = 0.032). All-cause mortality was also significantly lower in the semaglutide group (12 vs. 27 events; HR, 0.444; 95% CI, 0.229–0.862; P = 0.013). In contrast, the composite outcome of ESKD or all-cause mortality was comparable between the two groups (115 vs. 102 events; HR, 1.127; 95% CI, 0.908–1.401; P = 0.277).

Baseline characteristics before and after PSM are detailed in Supplementary Table 1 (male subgroup), Supplementary Table 2 (female subgroup), Supplementary Table 3 (younger cohort), Supplementary Table 4 (older cohort), Supplementary Table 5 (patients with ≥3 medication refills), Supplementary Table 6 (patients with ≥6 medication refills), Supplementary Table 7 (patients with ≥12 medication refills), Supplementary Table 8 (patients with DM), Supplementary Table 9 (patients without DM). Baseline characteristics before and after PSM across all predefined subgroups are presented in Supplementary Tables 1–9. Before matching, substantial imbalances were observed between semaglutide and non-semaglutide users in every subgroup, most notably in age distribution, BMI categories, and the prevalence of metabolic comorbidities such as DM. To explore the potential influence of treatment persistence, semaglutide users were further stratified according to the number of recorded prescription refill events (≥1, ≥3, ≥6, and ≥12 refills). Because detailed information on actual medication adherence, treatment duration, and cumulative dose exposure was unavailable in TriNetX, refill frequency was used as a surrogate measure of treatment persistence and cumulative exposure.

Across subgroup analyses, baseline characteristics were generally consistent with those observed in the overall matched cohort. Similar patterns were observed in male and female participants (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), younger and older age groups (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4), refill-frequency subgroups (Supplementary Tables 5–7), and the U.S.-only cohort (Supplementary Table 10). In these analyses, semaglutide users generally exhibited higher BMI and a greater cardiometabolic burden than non-users. After PSM, all measured demographic, clinical, laboratory, and medication variables achieved acceptable balance across the semaglutide and non-semaglutide cohorts in each subgroup, with standardized mean differences substantially reduced. Overall, PSM effectively harmonized baseline characteristics across all eight subgroup analyses, ensuring comparability for subsequent outcome evaluations.

Subgroup analyses after propensity score matching are summarized in Supplementary Table 11. Across gender subgroups, mean eGFR values were generally higher in semaglutide users than in non-users, although differences did not reach statistical significance in either the male or female cohorts. In age-based subgroups, semaglutide users aged 18–65 years exhibited a significantly higher mean eGFR compared with matched controls (61.3 ± 29.7 vs. 50.4 ± 35.4 mL/min/1.73 m2; p = 0.003), whereas the ≥65-year subgroup showed a numerically higher but non-significant eGFR difference. When stratified by medication refill frequency, mean eGFR remained consistently higher among semaglutide users across the ≥1, ≥3, ≥6, and ≥12 refill subgroups, though statistical significance was observed only in the overall cohort (≥1 refill group). BMI values were comparable across semaglutide and non-semaglutide users in all subgroups, with no clinically meaningful differences detected after matching. For adverse clinical outcomes, the incidence of urinary tract infection was similar between groups across all subgroups, with risk ratios ranging from 0.524 to 1.024 and none reaching statistical significance. Due to low event counts (n < 10), subgroup analyses for stage 5 CKD, ESKD, and all-cause mortality were not estimable in most strata. In the overall matched cohort, semaglutide use remained associated with lower mortality and numerically fewer renal events, but subgroup-level estimations were limited by small sample sizes. Overall, results across all eight subgroups demonstrated consistent patterns with the primary analysis, with semaglutide users showing higher eGFR and similar rates of adverse renal and infectious outcomes compared with matched non-users. Subgroup analyses according to diabetes status are shown in Supplementary Table 12. Owing to the limited number of outcome events, particularly in the non-diabetic subgroup, several renal and mortality outcomes could not be reported because they did not meet the minimum case threshold required by the TriNetX platform. Therefore, these analyses should be considered exploratory and were not sufficiently powered to assess potential differences in semaglutide-associated outcomes according to diabetes status.

Sensitivity analysis based on the U.S.-only population

In the U.S.-only population, baseline characteristics prior to PSM showed notable differences between semaglutide and non-semaglutide users, particularly in age distribution, BMI, and the prevalence of metabolic comorbidities (Supplementary Table 10). Semaglutide users generally exhibited higher BMI, a greater burden of diabetes, and more frequent use of renin–angiotensin system agents compared with non-users. Following PSM, these imbalances were substantially reduced, resulting in well-matched cohorts with comparable demographic, clinical, and laboratory features. Key variables— including age, sex, race distribution, BMI categories, comorbidities, baseline eGFR, urinary protein measures, blood pressure, and medication use—achieved adequate covariate balance, with standardized mean differences falling within acceptable ranges. Overall, PSM effectively harmonized baseline characteristics within the U.S.-only subgroup, ensuring comparability for subsequent outcome analyses.

In the U.S.-only sensitivity analysis, semaglutide users demonstrated a significantly higher mean eGFR compared with non-users (57.8 ± 29.3 vs. 51.3 ± 33.3 mL/min/1.73 m2; p = 0.023) (Supplementary Table 13). BMI was numerically lower in the semaglutide group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (33.0 ± 6.9 vs. 31.8 ± 7.2 kg/m2; p = 0.060). The risks of stage 5 chronic kidney disease and progression to end-stage kidney disease were similar between groups (RR = 0.693 [95% CI 0.339–1.414], p = 0.309; and RR = 0.615 [95% CI 0.309–1.224], p = 0.160, respectively). The incidence of urinary tract infection did not differ significantly (RR = 0.908 [95% CI 0.662–1.245]; p = 0.548). Notably, semaglutide use was associated with a significantly lower all-cause mortality rate (3.8% vs. 9.2%; RR = 0.391 [95% CI 0.196–0.780]; p = 0.006). Overall, the U.S.-only analysis yielded results consistent with the main findings, reinforcing the observed mortality benefit associated with semaglutide use.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is among the first real-world studies evaluating semaglutide use in patients with ADPKD. In this propensity score-matched cohort with relatively short-term follow-up, semaglutide use was associated with differences in BMI and creatinine-based eGFR compared with matched non-users. The semaglutide group maintained a more favorable mean eGFR throughout the 2-year follow-up period, with mean eGFR remaining relatively stable (55.4 to 57.5 mL/min/1.73 m2), whereas the non-semaglutide group experienced a decline (53.0 to 50.9 mL/min/1.73 m2). Overall, the mean change from baseline to the end of follow-up was +2.1 mL/min/1.73 m2 in the semaglutide group and −2.1 mL/min/1.73 m2 in the non-semaglutide group. However, because ADPKD typically progresses over decades, the relatively short follow-up period in the present study limits the ability to determine whether this observed association represents true slowing of disease progression. The higher creatinine-based eGFR observed during follow-up may instead reflect short-term hemodynamic or metabolic effects, including changes in creatinine generation associated with weight loss, rather than sustained preservation of glomerular filtration. Semaglutide is known to induce weight loss and may reduce skeletal muscle mass, thereby lowering serum creatinine production independently of actual changes in glomerular filtration. Consequently, the higher creatinine-based eGFR observed in semaglutide users may partially reflect reduced creatinine generation rather than true preservation of kidney function. This observed association between semaglutide use and higher creatinine-based eGFR was accompanied by a lower observed risk of progression to stage 5 CKD. In addition, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a disease-modifying effect because causality cannot be established in this retrospective observational study. Several factors may explain this apparent discrepancy. First, eGFR is a continuous measure of kidney function, whereas stage 5 CKD and ESKD were identified using administrative diagnostic codes, which may be less sensitive for detecting transitions in kidney disease severity. Second, competing risks may have influenced disease progression, as the higher mortality observed among non-users could have reduced the opportunity for progression to ESKD to be observed. Third, progression to ESKD generally requires prolonged observation. Given the relatively preserved kidney function at both baseline and follow-up in this cohort, the 2-year follow-up period may have been insufficient for a meaningful number of patients to reach ESKD. Therefore, the ESKD analysis should be considered exploratory, and the absence of a statistically significant association should not be interpreted as evidence that semaglutide lacks long-term renoprotective effects. Although eGFR remains the cornerstone for monitoring ADPKD progression, emerging biomarkers such as kidney injury molecule-1 KIM-1 and galectin-3 may provide complementary information regarding early tubular injury and disease progression before substantial declines in renal function become evident. 20

The first evidence supporting the renal benefits of semaglutide in ADPKD was reported in a preclinical study published in August 2025. 11 In this murine model, semaglutide delayed cyst expansion through a novel mechanism that may involve ketosis induction, reflected by increased serum β-hydroxybutyrate levels in ADPKD mice. Additionally, semaglutide has been shown to attenuate inflammation21,22 and TGF-β–mediated fibroblast activation in DKD. 23 Treatment with semaglutide decreased profibrotic markers—including fibronectin, α-SMA, and type I collagen—and reduced extracellular matrix deposition in Pkd1-mutant mice. 24 These findings suggest that the antifibrotic effects of semaglutide, via suppression of interstitial fibrosis, may represent one possible biological explanation for the observed association between semaglutide use and renal outcomes.

Another plausible mechanism in ADPKD relates to the metabolic effects of semaglutide, particularly weight loss and improved metabolic health. Obesity has been reported to be associated with increased kidney volume and accelerated renal function decline in ADPKD.14,25 Approximately two-thirds of adult patients with ADPKD are overweight or obese. 12 Caloric restriction in ADPKD has been reported to confer potential efficacy benefits primarily through weight loss. 26 In addition, another pilot study demonstrated that both changes in body weight (r = 0.67, P < 0.01) and changes in visceral adiposity (r = 0.54, P < 0.01) were positively correlated with changes in total kidney volume, supporting the link between body weight control and slower cystic kidney growth. 27 Our findings support this hypothesis, as patients receiving semaglutide demonstrated greater BMI reduction, which may contribute to slower disease progression. However, an alternative explanation should also be considered. Because semaglutide induces weight loss and may reduce lean body and skeletal muscle mass, lower serum creatinine concentrations may partly reflect reduced creatinine generation rather than true preservation of glomerular filtration. Therefore, the higher creatinine-based eGFR observed in semaglutide users should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a direct renoprotective effect or attenuation of cyst progression. Future studies incorporating cystatin C–based eGFR, directly measured GFR, body composition assessment, and imaging markers such as total kidney volume are needed to clarify this issue.

Blood pressure control is also a well-established determinant of outcomes in ADPKD. However, because baseline hypertension status was matched between the two groups in our study, the independent impact of blood pressure reduction could not be adequately evaluated in this analysis. Dysregulated glucose metabolism (eg. insulin resistance 16 ) has also been shown to play an important role in the development and progression of ADPKD in multiple murine models. 12 DM was balanced between the semaglutide and non-semaglutide groups after PSM; therefore, the independent contribution of improved glucose metabolism could not be directly evaluated. Nevertheless, the prevalence of diabetes remained high in both groups (46% and 50%, respectively), suggesting that metabolic effects may still have influenced the observed associations. Although semaglutide’s regulation of glucose metabolism could potentially contribute to the more favorable kidney function observed in this study, subgroup analyses stratified by diabetes status were limited by low event counts, particularly among non-diabetic patients, resulting in insufficient statistical power to formally assess treatment-effect heterogeneity. Consistent with a possible metabolic contribution, we observed a numerically lower incidence of UTI among semaglutide users. Although this was not a primary outcome and should be interpreted cautiously, improved glycemic control and reduced glucosuria may have contributed to this finding. However, because longitudinal measures of glycemic control, hemoglobin A1c, and urinary glucose excretion were unavailable, the extent to which diabetes-related mechanisms influenced the observed associations could not be determined. Because nearly half of the matched cohort had type 2 diabetes, it is also possible that the observed preservation of kidney function and lower mortality partly reflect the established renal and cardiovascular benefits of semaglutide in diabetic populations rather than a disease-specific effect on ADPKD. Although diabetes status was balanced after propensity score matching, residual differences in metabolic health cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that semaglutide directly modifies cyst progression but rather as an association requiring confirmation in prospective ADPKD-specific studies incorporating imaging and mechanistic endpoints. Accordingly, the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a disease-specific effect in ADPKD. Definitive conclusions will require prospective randomized studies. Notably, a phase 2 randomized controlled trial evaluating tirzepatide in ADPKD (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06582875) is currently ongoing and is expected to provide important mechanistic and clinical insights into the role of incretin-based therapies in this population.

Hydration status is another important consideration in the management of ADPKD, as increased water intake is commonly recommended to suppress vasopressin activity and potentially slow disease progression. Because gastrointestinal adverse effects associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists may theoretically increase the risk of dehydration, we additionally evaluated volume-related safety outcomes. Reassuringly, semaglutide use was not associated with increased risks of hypotension or acute kidney injury and was associated with a lower incidence of volume depletion. Although this finding appears counterintuitive given the gastrointestinal adverse effects commonly associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists, several explanations may be considered. Improved glycemic control may reduce osmotic diuresis, particularly among patients with diabetes, thereby partially offsetting fluid losses associated with nausea or vomiting. In addition, patients prescribed semaglutide may receive closer clinical follow-up and counseling regarding adequate hydration during treatment initiation, which could reduce dehydration-related events. Finally, residual confounding and healthy-user bias cannot be excluded in this observational study. Therefore, the observed association should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed in prospective studies specifically designed to evaluate hydration status and volume-related outcomes.

The observed reduction in all-cause mortality should also be interpreted with caution. Cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of death among patients with ADPKD, and the survival benefit observed in the semaglutide group may therefore reflect cardiovascular as well as renal effects. In addition, semaglutide has been shown in previous studies to reduce major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with obesity and type 2 diabetes, which may also have contributed to the observed mortality difference. However, given the observational nature of this study, causality cannot be established, and we were unable to determine the relative contributions of cardiovascular and renal mechanisms to the observed survival benefit. Further studies with longer follow-up are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying the reduced mortality observed among semaglutide users. Similarly, the composite endpoint of ESKD or all-cause mortality should be interpreted cautiously because its individual components may reflect different biological mechanisms. Accordingly, the separate renal and mortality outcomes are likely to provide more clinically meaningful information than the composite endpoint alone.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective observational design precludes causal inference despite the use of propensity score matching. Although the continuous BMI variable retained a modest residual imbalance after propensity score matching (SMD = 0.237), the distribution across predefined BMI categories was well balanced between groups after matching. Nevertheless, residual confounding related to body composition, visceral adiposity, or other obesity-related factors cannot be completely excluded because these variables were unavailable in the TriNetX database. Second, imaging-based measures of disease severity, including height-adjusted total kidney volume (htTKV) and Mayo Imaging Classification, were unavailable in the TriNetX database. Because these variables are among the most important predictors of disease progression in ADPKD and are routinely used to guide treatment decisions, we were unable to fully adjust for baseline structural disease severity. Consequently, residual confounding related to disease severity cannot be excluded despite propensity score matching. Third, patients receiving tolvaptan were excluded to minimize confounding from an established disease-modifying therapy. Because tolvaptan is generally prescribed to patients with rapidly progressive ADPKD, excluding these individuals may have preferentially selected patients with less advanced disease and thereby limited the generalizability of our findings. Consequently, the present results should not be extrapolated to patients eligible for or receiving tolvaptan therapy. Future studies should evaluate whether semaglutide provides additional benefit when used alongside or compared with tolvaptan in well-characterized ADPKD populations with imaging-based assessment of disease severity. Accordingly, the generalizability of these findings to patients with rapidly progressive ADPKD or those receiving tolvaptan remains uncertain and requires evaluation in future prospective studies. Fourth, detailed information regarding semaglutide dose, treatment duration, and medication adherence was unavailable. Consequently, prescription refill frequency was used only as a surrogate measure of treatment persistence and cumulative exposure. Because prescription refills do not necessarily reflect actual medication use or adherence, these analyses should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, kidney function was assessed using creatinine-based eGFR rather than directly measured GFR. Semaglutide-induced weight loss and changes in muscle mass may reduce serum creatinine concentrations and thereby influence creatinine-based eGFR estimates. In addition, changes in body size may affect body surface area-adjusted eGFR over time. Because cystatin C measurements, direct GFR assessments, body composition data, and serial kidney imaging were unavailable, we could not determine whether the observed preservation of eGFR reflected true improvements in glomerular filtration or changes in creatinine generation. Therefore, the observed preservation of creatinine-based eGFR may partially represent changes in creatinine generation rather than true preservation of glomerular filtration. This limitation has also been recognized in previous studies of GLP-1 receptor agonists, in which weight loss–related reductions in muscle mass may influence creatinine-based estimates of kidney function. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed by prospective studies incorporating muscle mass–independent kidney function markers. Finally, the subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution because the matched cohort was relatively small and several clinical outcomes occurred infrequently. Consequently, many subgroup analyses were underpowered, and several estimates could not be calculated because the number of outcome events did not meet the minimum reporting threshold of the TriNetX platform. Therefore, these subgroup findings should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory and should not be overinterpreted in the absence of statistical significance.

Conclusion

In this real-world propensity score–matched cohort of patients with ADPKD, semaglutide use was associated with higher follo-up creatinine-based eGFR and a lower risk of progression to stage 5 CKD over 2 years of follow-up. No significant differences were observed in the incidence of ESKD, and the observed reduction in all-cause mortality should be interpreted cautiously. Given the observational design, limited follow-up duration, residual baseline imbalance, and the potential influence of semaglutide-induced changes in creatinine generation, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Prospective studies incorporating imaging-based disease severity, direct measures of kidney function, and longer follow-up are warranted to determine whether semaglutide confers disease-specific renal benefits in patients with ADPKD.
