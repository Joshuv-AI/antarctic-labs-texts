Unveiling postpartum PTSD: predicting risk factors using decision trees and logistic regression in Chinese women
Authors: Xiao Fei Nie, Lan Lan Xu, Wen Ping Guo, Jin Hui Li, Li Cheng, Tao Tao Zhang, Jun-Yan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile traditional logistic regression emphasizes main effects with limited capacity for interaction detection, emerging decision trees excel in uncovering complex associations. However, no studies have yet integrated both approaches to investigate postpartum posttraumatic stress disorder (PP-PTSD). This study aims to explore the factors associated with postpartum posttraumatic stress disorder (PP-PTSD) in Chinese women using decision tree and logistic regression models, while also comparing the predictive performance of both approaches.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited postpartum women using convenience sampling between June 2021 and December 2022. PTSD was assessed using the City Birth Trauma Scale (City BiTS). The Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS), Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ), Pregnancy Stress Rating Scale (PSRS), and Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) were employed to evaluate perceived social support, psychological coping strategies, pregnancy stress and resilience, respectively. Decision tree and logistic regression models were applied to identify factors associated with PTSD.ResultsAmong 704 valid participants, 36 (5.11%) screened positive for PP-PTSD. Logistic regression identified postpartum duration, sleep quality, pregnancy stress, family support, and positive coping as significant predictors of PP-PTSD (p < 0.05). The decision tree model highlighted postpartum sleep quality as the primary determinant, followed by pregnancy stress and postpartum duration. While both models achieved perfect sensitivity (100%), logistic regression demonstrated superior overall performance, with a 2.28% higher classification accuracy (97.73% vs. 95.45%) and enhanced specificity (97.9% vs. 88.9%). The AUC values further validated this advantage (0.992 vs. 0.968).ConclusionsThis study utilized Logistic Regression and Decision Tree models to identify key factors influencing PP-PTSD, which include postpartum duration, sleep quality, pregnancy stress, family support, and positive coping. The identified modifiable factors enable targeted PP-PTSD prevention, with Logistic Regression providing high-accuracy screening tools and Decision Trees simplifying risk assessment in community settings.

Background

Postpartum posttraumatic stress disorder (PP-PTSD) has garnered significant global concern due to its widespread prevalence and profound impact, supported by data from various countries [1, 2]. A systematic review underscores the urgency of this issue, revealing an average incidence rate of 4.0% for PP-PTSD, with rates as high as 18.5% observed in high-risk pregnancy cohorts [3]. Additionally, reviews indicate incidence rates of 10.7% among women undergoing cesarean sections and 19.4% following traumatic births [4, 5]. In Mainland China, the pooled prevalence of PP-PTSD was found to be 11.2% [6]. Addressing the mental health needs of Chinese mothers postpartum thus remains a significant societal challenge.

The PP-PTSD refers to delayed onset posttraumatic stress disorder experienced by mothers following childbirth, characterized primarily by intrusive symptoms, avoidance behaviors, negative cognitive and emotional changes, as well as alterations in arousal and reactivity [7]. It significantly impacts mothers’ physical and mental health, contributing to issues such as sleep disturbances and depression [3, 8]. Socially, it disrupts mother-infant bonding, family relationships, and social functioning, potentially leading to long-term negative impacts on children’s behavior and emotional development [3, 9]. Understanding and predicting the occurrence of PP-PTSD, alongside its contributing factors, is critical for enhancing maternal health and improving overall quality of life.

Factors influencing PP-PTSD include individual physiological conditions, psychological characteristics, social support, and childbirth experiences. Physiological issues cover factors such as complications of pregnancy or birth and sleep disturbances. Additionally, factors like dissociation during birth, depression, and childhood sexual abuse contribute to the PP-PTSD occurrence. The cesarean section and birth trauma during delivery are closely associated with PP-PTSD. Furthermore, the risk of the PP-PTSD increases with low social support systems and experiences of intimate partner violence [5–8, 10]. In terms of disease etiology, influencing factors can be categorized as proximal factors directly contributing to the condition and distal factors exerting indirect effects. Consequently, many of these factors interact synergistically rather than acting independently [11]. However, there is limited research on the synergistic interactions among factors influencing the PP-PTSD.

Traditional logistic regression focuses on main effects but struggles with interaction effects and providing robust decision guidance [12, 13]. Recently, the medical field has seen a profound impact from artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML), among which the CHAID (Chi-squared Automatic Interaction Detection) decision tree model stands out prominently [14]. The decision tree models transcend traditional linear methods by addressing multicollinearity and employing logical decision pathways from root to leaf nodes, each path representing a specific rule. Despite its ability to rank indicator importance, the classification performance of decision tree model may vary with changes in node structure, potentially complicating main effects analysis and limiting result interpretability [12, 13]. Integrating CHAID decision tree model with logistic regression enhances analytical depth and capability in medical research and decision-making.

A combination of the decision tree model and logistic regression for analyzing factors related to PP-PTSD is currently lacking both domestically and internationally. This study focuses on a sample of postpartum women, proposing two models to analyze factors associated with the PP-PTSD. The aim is to deepen our understanding of the PP-PTSD and provide a reference for enhancing the health and quality of life of postpartum women.

Methods

This cross-sectional study used convenience sampling to select postpartum women from three hospitals in Shiyan City. Participants were drawn from obstetric wards, postpartum pelvic floor rehabilitation centers, and child preventive healthcare centers between June 2021 and December 2022.

Assessment tools

General Demographic Questionnaire: This tool was developed by the research team based on literature review and consultation with obstetric nursing experts. It includes 13 items covering age, educational level, marital status, cohabitation status with husband, monthly household income, only-child status, gravidity (number of pregnancies), history of miscarriage, gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, postpartum duration, level of knowledge about childbirth, and postpartum sleep quality.

City Birth Trauma Scale (City BiTS): This scale, designed to assess PTSD, was developed by Professor Ayers at the University of London in 2018 [7]. It is based on the latest PTSD diagnostic criteria from the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5). Nie Xiaofei and colleagues translated and adapted it into Chinese [15]. There are 27 items: [A] Stressor Criterion (Q1-Q2): 0 = No, 1 = Yes; [B] Re-experiencing symptoms (Q3-Q7), [C] Avoidance symptoms (Q8-Q9), [D] Negative cognitions and mood (Q10-Q16), [E] Hyperarousal (Q17-Q22): 0 = Not at all, 1 = Once, 2 = 2–4 times, 3 = 5 or more times; [F] Duration: Symptom Onset Timing (Q23) and Duration of Symptoms (Q24): 0 = less than 1 month, 1 = 1 ~ 3 months, 2 = 3 months or more; [G] Distress and impairment (Q25-Q26):0 = No, 1 = sometimes, 2 = Yes; [H] Exclusion criteria: Medication-Caused Symptoms (Q27): 0 = No, 1 = Sometimes, 2 = Yes. PP-PTSD was defined based on the following criteria: one stressor event, one re-experiencing symptom, one avoidance symptom, two negative cognition and mood symptoms, and two hyperarousal symptoms. Additionally, symptoms must persist for more than one month, cause distress and impairment, and meet exclusion criterion H (Q27). The original English version of the City BiTS has a Cronbach’s α of 0.925 [7]. Recent validations, including the latest Italian version (Cronbach’s α = 0.91), support its cross-cultural reliability [16]. The Chinese version has a Cronbach’s α of 0.889 and a three-week test-retest reliability of 0.860, indicating good reliability and validity [15].

Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS): The Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS) was originally developed by Zimet et al. [17] and later adapted into Chinese by Jiang Qianjin (2011) [18], measures the individual’s overall perceived social support. It uses a 7-point Likert scale and consists of 12 items across three dimensions: family, friends, and significant other, with four items each. The total score ranges from 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support. The original English version has a Cronbach’s α of 0.990, while the Chinese version has a Cronbach’s α of 0.840.

Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ): This questionnaire, developed by Xie Yaning in 1998, is used to assess postpartum psychological changes and coping strategies [19]. The questionnaire comprises 20 items and two dimensions: positive coping (items 1–12) and negative coping (items 13–20). The SCSQ is self-administered using a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (‘not used’) to 3 (‘frequently used’). Results are reported as positive (0–36 scores) and negative (0–24 scores) coping scores, with higher scores indicating a greater tendency to employ the corresponding coping strategies. The Cronbach’s α of the total scale, positive coping dimension and negative coping dimension were 0.900, 0.890 and 0.780, respectively.

Pregnancy Stress Rating Scale (PSRS): This scale, developed by Professor Chen.

Zhanghui from the Department of Nursing at Kaohsiung Medical University in Taiwan, assesses stress level associated with pregnancy [20]. It comprises 36 items on a 1–5 Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 36 to 180; higher scores indicate greater stress. Li Dan and colleagues validated it among pregnant women in mainland China, reporting a Cronbach’s α of 0.900, indicating good reliability and validity [21].

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC): The scale was developed by Connor et al. in 2003 to assess resilience in cancer patients [22]. Ye Zengjie translated it into Chinese, creating a 10-item version [23]. It uses a 1–5 Likert scale with total scores ranging from 10 to 50, with higher scores reflecting greater resilience. The Chinese version has a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.855, demonstrating good reliability and validity, and is widely used among pregnant and postpartum women in China.

Data collection preparation

Research assistants underwent standardized training to ensure uniform instructions during questionnaire collection. This study utilized the enterprise version of the Wenjuanxing platform (https://www.wjx.cn) to generate QR codes for data collection from eligible postpartum women. To prevent duplicates, each IP address was limited to one submission.

Data preprocessing

After data collection, research assistants reviewed and invalidated questionnaires with uniform responses or completion times under 10 min.The data analysis was divided into four steps, with the first three conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0.

Step 1: Descriptive statistics and univariable analysis

Continuous variables (e.g., age, postpartum duration, gestational age at delivery, resilience scores, and social support scores) were summarized utilizing means and standard deviations. Categorical variables (e.g., gravidity, history of miscarriage, and depression status) were described using frequencies and percentages. Subsequently, univariable analysis was performed to identify significant variables using t-tests, Pearson’s chi-square tests, and Fisher’s exact test. PP- PTSD was the dependent variable. Independent variables included 13 demographic factors (e.g., age, gravidity, history of miscarriage) and scores from scales measuring perceived social support (family, friend, significant other), psychological coping strategies (positive, negative), pregnancy stress, and resilience.

Step 2: Constructing the logistic regression model

Significant variables identified in Step 1 were incorporated into a multivariable Logistic regression analysis using the LR forward method. A two-stage approach (univariate screening followed by multivariate stepwise selection) was employed to balance statistical rigor and computational feasibility. Single-predictor screening reduces the dimensionality of candidate variables while retaining potential confounders, whereas subsequent LR forward selection refines the model by capturing synergistic effects among variables. Before fitting the model, we checked for multicollinearity by calculating the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for all variables. This analysis yielded the Logistic regression model, which generated the PP-PTSD prediction probability 1.

Step 3: Constructing a decision tree model

Similarly, the variables selected through univariable analysis were utilized to build this model using the CHAID algorithm. Initially, the optimal cutoff values for continuous variables were computed based on the Youden index and transformed into binary variables. Detailed binary coding rules for each variable are in the Appendix Table A. Subsequently, the decision tree model’s parameters included a significance level of 0.05 for node splitting and category merging, a maximum depth of 3 layers, and minimum sample sizes of 100 for parent nodes and 50 for child nodes. Subsequently, PP-PTSD prediction probability 2 was derived from the decision tree model.

Step 4: Comparing logistic regression and decision tree model

MedCalc version 19.1 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium) was used to plot the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves. The actual measurement of PP-PTSD (positive or negative) was used as the dependent variable, while the predicted probabilities of PTSD from the Logistic Regression model (Prediction 1) and the Decision Tree model (Prediction 2) were the independent variables.

Internal validation was conducted using bootstrapping (with 1,000 resamples) to assess the robustness and generalizability of both the logistic regression and decision tree models.

Subsequently, the area under the ROC curve (AUC), the difference in areas, standard error (SE), sensitivity, and specificity for both models were calculated. Finally, to assess the statistical significance of the differences between the two models, a Z-test was conducted on the AUCs, computing the Z-value and P-value. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Due to the limited number of positive cases, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) in the entire dataset to address class imbalance. SMOTE generates synthetic samples by interpolating existing minority class instances, thereby enriching the decision boundary without duplicating observations.

Results

General demographic data of participants

Of the 739 distributed questionnaires, 704 were valid, yielding a response rate of 95.26%. Participants’ ages ranged from 21 to 41 years, with a mean age of 30.19 ± 3.802 years. The average postpartum period was 4.32 ± 3.677 months. Among the participants, 36 (5.11%) were positive for PP-PTSD, while 668 were negative.

Results of univariable analysis for PP- PTSD

The univariable analysis indicated that age(p = 0.001), postpartum duration(p < 0.001), mode of delivery(p = 0,014), sleep quality(p < 0.001), pregnancy stress(p < 0.001), resilience(p < 0.001), perceived support (family, friend, significant other) (p < 0.001), and coping (positive, negative) (p < 0.001) were significantly associated with PP-PTSD. Compared to women who were dissatisfied with their sleep, those reporting average sleep quality (OR = 0.912, 95% CI: 0.877–0.94) or satisfaction with sleep (OR = 0.225, 95% CI: 0.064–0.732) had a significantly lower risk of PP-PTSD. Comparison of model prediction results

Both models identified postpartum duration, sleep quality, and pregnancy stress as common factors. However, family support and positive coping strategies were significant only in the logistic regression model.

In the PP-PTSD negative group (668 cases), the Logistic regression model predicted 8 PTSD cases, and the decision tree model predicted 16. In the positive group (36 cases), the models predicted 28 and 20 PTSD cases, respectively. Consequently, the classification accuracy of the Logistic regression model was 97.73% [(28 + 668–8)/(668 + 36)], and the decision tree model was 95.45% [(20 + 668 − 16)/(668 + 36)].

After 1,000 bootstrap iterations, the Logistic regression model achieved an AUC of 0.992 (0.982–0.997), with 100% sensitivity, 97.9% specificity, and a Youden index of 97.9%, PPV = 77.78%, NPV = 98.80%, F1 score = 0.78. The decision tree model had an AUC of 0.968 (0.952–0.980), also with 100% sensitivity, but 88.9% specificity, and a Youden index of 88.9%, PPV = 55.56%, NPV = 97.60%, F1 score = 0.56. The difference in AUC between the two models was statistically significant (Z = 4.047, P < 0.001) (see Fig. 3). Accuracy and sensitivity were stable across resamples, suggesting minimal overfitting.

Discussion

In this study, the incidence rate of PP-PTSD was determined to be 5.11%. This finding aligns with UK estimates, where 4–6% of individuals develop PTSD following childbirth [1]. However, it is lower than the 9% reported by Postpartum Support International (n.d.) and significantly below the Australian rate, where 1 in 16 meet al.l PTSD criteria [2]. The relatively low incidence of PP-PTSD observed in this study may be attributed to a combination of cultural, social, and healthcare-related factors. One crucial aspect is the influence of cultural traditions such as “zuo yuezi”—a traditional postpartum practice that emphasizes rest and confinement for new mothers. While this practice is intended to promote recovery and well-being, it may also shape attitudes toward mental health, fostering a preference for non-medical interventions and leading to reluctance in seeking professional support [24]. Additionally, conservative cultural attitudes towards mental disorders in China contribute to a tendency to conceal psychological discomfort, resulting in underreporting [25]. Such attitudes can be exacerbated by stigma and even discrimination within healthcare settings, where negative perceptions of mental illness may hinder effective intervention [26].

Healthcare accessibility also significantly impacts the reporting of PP-PTSD and other mental health issues. Limited mental health resources in rural areas, a shortage of professionals, and poor integration into primary care hinder timely diagnosis and support, leading to underreporting in China [27].

Furthermore, the incidence of PTSD identified in our study is lower than the 6.1% reported by Ying Liu, who utilized the Perinatal Post-traumatic Stress Questionnaire [8]. This discrepancy may result from differing measurement tools. A systematic review of nine studies indicates that PP-PTSD incidence is significantly influenced by the screening tools used [5]. Future research should utilize precise instruments, such as the City Birth Trauma Scale (City BiTS) based on DSM-5 criteria, which was employed in this study. Xiaofei Nie has validated its reliability and validity [15], recommending its use for screening PP-PTSD symptoms.

Postpartum duration and PP-PTSD

This study indicates that 3.41% of women display symptoms of PP-PTSD beyond six months postpartum, a prevalence rate that surpasses that observed within the initial six-month period. This aligns with Yildiz et al. (2017) [3], who reported PTSD prevalence rates of 5.8% at 4–6 weeks, 1.4% at 3 months, and 6.8% at 6 months postpartum across 28 international studies. These findings suggest that PTSD symptoms can persist and even emerge long after the traumatic event.

One plausible explanation is that factors encountered during the early postpartum period—including physical pain, intense childbirth experiences, disruptions to daily routines, and challenges associated with newborn care and feeding—negatively impact the physical and psychological well-being of mothers [28, 29]. Consequently, symptoms of PTSD may persist at elevated levels for a prolonged duration, potentially exceeding six months postpartum. Thus, postpartum psychological health intervention programs should prioritize extended care, lasting at least six months and potentially longer.

Postpartum sleep quality and PP-PTSD

Postpartum women reporting poor sleep quality exhibited a significantly elevated risk for PP-PTSD. This result is consistent with Deforges et al., who also found a strong correlation between PP-PTSD and prenatal and postnatal insomnia [30].

Postpartum sleep quality and PP-PTSD have a reciprocal relationship. A study highlighted two main findings: (1) reduced and lower-quality sleep forecasts more severe PTSD symptoms the following day, and (2) heightened PTSD symptoms lead to nightmares and worse sleep that night [31]. Hormonal changes, childbirth pain, infant care responsibilities and stress, and irregular newborn sleep disrupt postpartum sleep, leading to fragmented sleep architecture, increased light sleep stages, heightened arousal, and nightmares, which elevate PTSD risk [9].

Therefore, PP-PTSD and sleep quality mutually influence each other, creating a vicious cycle. Addressing this issue necessitates concentrated academic investigation to enhance postpartum sleep quality and mitigate the risk of developing PTSD.

Pregnancy stress and PP-PTSD

This study demonstrates a positive correlation between pregnancy stress and the incidence of PP-PTSD. Elevated stress levels during pregnancy significantly increase the risk of developing PP-PTSD. Research indicated that specific stressors and pregnancy stress contribute to PP-PTSD [32, 33]. Elevated levels of stress during pregnancy are associated with increased psychological distress. Concerns regarding fetal health, fear of childbirth, and anxieties about newborn care can intensify negative emotional states, potentially leading to the onset of PTSD. In certain cases, PTSD may manifest even during the prenatal period.

However, the assessment of pregnancy stress in this study relied on retrospective self-reports from postpartum mothers, which may introduce bias. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to prospectively assess pregnancy stress and psychological health from early pregnancy through 12 months postpartum. This approach will offer a more robust basis for developing targeted mental health interventions for perinatal women.

Family support and PP-PTSD

Among postpartum women, those who receive substantial familial support exhibit a decreased risk of developing PTSD. This finding is corroborated by additional research, which indicates that social support, especially from family members, during pregnancy and childbirth significantly mitigates the risk of PP-PTSD [34, 35]. Physiological studies have revealed that insufficient social support from family and friends during the perinatal period increases cortisol secretion, enhancing biological sensitivity to psychological stress and intensifying negative emotions [36]. Additionally, Ayers theorized that higher levels of family support enhance perceived control and reduce perceived trauma, thereby lowering the incidence of PP-PTSD [37]. Moreover, the influence of family dynamics and support systems is significant in the Chinese context. For instance, the involvement of the baby’s grandmother as the primary family caregiver has been associated with a reduction in maternal depression risk, highlighting the importance of extended family support in mitigating postpartum mental health issues [38].

Thus, positive family support significantly promotes the psychological health of postpartum women. It is essential to educate family members on providing effective support to perinatal women.

Positive coping and PP-PTSD

An interesting phenomenon was found in this study that positive coping strategies are protective factors against PP-PTSD. When mothers employ positive coping methods to handle childbirth trauma and stress, the likelihood of developing PP-PTSD is reduced. This result is similar to Cleveland et al.’s study, positive coping strategies alleviate PP-PTSD symptoms [33].

In contrast, this study found no effect of negative coping strategies on PP-PTSD. However, other research has shown that negative coping strategies, such as self-blame, rumination, and perceived resource loss, are closely linked to PP-PTSD. Positive coping strategies, such as resource reallocation, can prevent PP-PTSD and alleviate its symptoms [39]. These findings underscore the importance of promoting positive coping mechanisms among perinatal women to mitigate the risk of PP-PTSD.

Comparison of logistic regression model and decision tree model

In this study, both the Logistic Regression and Decision Tree models achieved classification accuracies above 70%. The Logistic Regression model showed superior classification accuracy and overall performance. Both models had a sensitivity of 100%, indicating high stability. However, the Logistic Regression model outperformed the Decision Tree model in specificity and Youden index, demonstrating better capability in identifying PTSD in postpartum women. Additionally, the AUC of the Logistic Regression model was significantly higher than that of the Decision Tree model (p < 0.05), indicating superior predictive performance. The Logistic Regression model’s inclusion of five variables, compared to the Decision Tree model’s three, further contributed to its greater stability.

Research suggested that both models have distinct advantages and should be combined for comprehensive variable interpretation [13, 40]. The Logistic Regression model captures the relationship between PP-PTSD and multiple variables (postpartum duration, sleep quality, pregnancy stress, family support, positive coping). However, it is less effective in reflecting variable interactions compared to the Decision Tree model.

Clinically, reliance on complete-case analysis may bias estimates in routine practice where mental health documentation is often incomplete.

The Decision Tree model, unaffected by multicollinearity, clearly illustrates variable interactions and the significance of each variable to PTSD in an intuitive probability tree format, facilitating clinical application. Postpartum sleep quality emerged as the primary determinant of PP-PTSD risk. If a woman reported being dissatisfied with sleep quality, she was directly classified as high risk for PP-PTSD, without the need to assess secondary variables. For women with moderate sleep quality, risk stratification depended on both pregnancy stress scores and postpartum duration. Specifically, when the pregnancy stress score exceeded 112.5 points: If the postpartum duration was ≤ 6 months, the individual was classified as medium risk. If the postpartum duration was > 6 months, the risk was classified as medium-low. In contrast, if a woman reported moderate or good sleep quality with a pregnancy stress score ≤ 112.5 points, she was consistently categorized as low risk, regardless of postpartum duration. These decision rules offer a straightforward and practical framework for identifying women at elevated risk of PP-PTSD, allowing for timely intervention and targeted support.

Nonetheless, the model’s reliance on specific cut-off values and categorical decisions should be interpreted cautiously, as they may not fully capture the complexity of individual patient presentations. For example, the decision tree model lies in the data-derived cut-off for pregnancy stress (112.5), which was generated using the Youden index in the absence of established clinical thresholds. While such thresholds enhance statistical discrimination within the decision tree model, their applicability in real-world clinical practice requires further validation. This highlights a broader limitation of decision tree approaches, where variable splits are data-dependent and may lack external clinical grounding. Healthcare systems prioritizing pharmacoeconomic analysis require OR metrics for cost-benefit modeling, which tree-based approaches cannot natively provide. Furthermore, its classification results vary with the number of leaf nodes, making it less stable than the Logistic Regression model.

Given the distinct characteristics of the two models, we suggest using the decision tree model for initial, frontline screening to quickly identify individuals at high risk. Its simplicity and interpretability make it suitable for use in community or primary care settings. Subsequently, logistic regression can be applied in more advanced clinical assessments, leveraging its stability and precision for detailed risk estimation. The decision tree model may also be useful during follow-up after treatment to efficiently monitor changes in risk status. This combined approach allows for efficient triage, reduces the risk of over-treatment, and helps streamline the diagnostic process.

Strength and limitations

This study’s scientific contribution is highlighted by its methodological innovation in predicting risk factors for PP-PTSD among Chinese women. Employing dual-model analysis (decision tree and logistic regression) harmonizing predictive accuracy with interpretability, the research establishes a robust framework for both empirical validation and clinical translation. Crucially, it addresses a critical research gap through culturally contextualized investigation of biological, psychological, and sociocultural determinants via multivariate analysis.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling restricts generalizability, as it may underrepresent vulnerable populations with limited healthcare access or elevated psychosocial risks, potentially underestimating the prevalence of PP-PTSD. Research indicates that socioeconomically disadvantaged women often face a higher prevalence of PTSD, exacerbated by their living conditions and limited access to mental health care. A study examining the frequency of PTSD among African-American women in a disadvantaged urban neighborhood found a high rate of PTSD diagnoses and subthreshold PTSD among participants with depressive symptoms [41]. This highlights the intersection of socio-economic disadvantage and mental health issues, suggesting that women in these circumstances may experience compounded stressors that increase their risk for PTSD.

Additionally, recruitment through clinical settings inherently excludes non-help-seeking individuals who may experience more severe symptoms. A study using community-based participatory research identified economic, family, cultural, and neighborhood issues as significant determinants of mental health among urban, ethnically diverse, impoverished women [42]. These factors not only contribute to the onset of mental health issues like PTSD but also impede access to treatment, as these women may prioritize immediate economic survival over seeking mental health care. As a result, the findings may not fully capture the experiences and risk factors present in these vulnerable groups. It underscores the need for inclusive research methodologies that consider the socio-economic and cultural contexts influencing mental health and help-seeking behaviors.

Second, self-reported data may be subject to recall bias, affecting the accuracy of exposure or outcome measurements. Temporal misclassification could attenuate observed associations, particularly for long-latency outcomes. Future prospective longitudinal studies incorporating objective indicators are recommended to improve accuracy and reliability.

Third, this study was conducted solely in Shiyan, a midsized inland city in central China, which may limit external validity and generalizability to coastal megacities or rural areas. Furthermore, although participants were recruited from three geographically close hospitals, the distribution of participants across these hospitals was not considered, which may have introduced context-specific biases.

Fourth, the study did not account for established risk factors for PP-PTSD, such as postpartum depression, anxiety, or a history of mental health disorders. Unmeasured sociocultural factors (e.g., cultural stigma, healthcare access disparities) may have also confounded the relationships between variables like pregnancy stress and sleep quality. Future studies should include these important factors to enhance model robustness.

Fifth, the limited number of positive PP-PTSD cases in the sample necessitates cautious interpretation, particularly when inferring the impact of specific risk factors. Future research should consider increasing the sample size and adopting a multicenter design to improve representativeness and accuracy.

Sixth, this study used the City Birth Trauma Scale (City BiTS) as a screening tool for probable PP-PTSD symptoms. However, without clinical diagnostic interviews, there is a risk of misclassification.

Finally, as this study focused on comparing logistic regression and decision tree models, only internal validation using bootstrapping was performed. The lack of external validation limits the generalizability of the findings, and future studies should validate these models in independent populations.

Conclusion

This study utilized Logistic Regression and Decision Tree models to identify key factors influencing PP-PTSD, which include postpartum duration, sleep quality, pregnancy stress, family support, and positive coping. The Logistic Regression model demonstrated higher predictive efficacy, while the Decision Tree model offered greater intuitiveness and ease of application. The decision tree model is well-suited for community-based screening and follow-up due to its simplicity and interpretability, while logistic regression is more appropriate for advanced clinical assessment requiring precise risk estimation. Combining these models allows healthcare professionals to assess PP-PTSD risk from both statistical and probabilistic perspectives, enhancing clinical decision-making.
