Effect of Edible Cricket Enriched Complementary Porridge and Nutrition Education on Linear Growth of Children 6–14 Months in Siaya County, Kenya: A Randomized 2 × 2 Factorial Trial
Authors: Nicky O Okeyo, Cecilie E Wille, Samuel M Kiiru, Asenath J Sigot, Jeremiah Ng'ang'a, John Kinyuru, Nanna Roos, Silvenus O Konyole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEdible crickets are commonly farmed insects containing high‐quality protein and micronutrients. They can potentially replace common animal source protein in complementary food (CF). We evaluated the effect of edible cricket‐enriched CF on linear growth in a 2 × 2 factorial randomized control trial based on Cricket treatment (+/−CR) and Nutrition Education treatment (+/−ED). We enrolled 284 eligible mother–infant dyads with infants from 6 months having a middle upper arm circumference (MUAC) > 11.5 cm at Rwambwa Sub‐County Hospital, Siaya County, Kenya. Participants were randomized to treatment arms with intervention foods issued monthly as a take‐home food ration adjusted for age, were offered +ED sessions monthly as personalized audio–visual sessions where education messages and reminders were sent to the mother's mobile phones. Monthly infant anthropometry, feeding practices and child health data were collected. A significant contrast (Z = −0.28 (95% CI: 0.03, 0.53) [p = 0.03]) in length‐for‐age Z‐score (LAZ) between −CR and +CR was observed. By MUAC, girls were significantly 1.98 (1.15, 3.41) (p = 0.01) times likely to be undernourished. Increased illness episodes significantly increased the child's risk to undernutrition, with those experiencing three episodes being 4.30 (95% CI: 1.21, 15.37) times likely to suffer undernutrition, while those with above five episodes were almost six times as likely to be malnourished. Consuming either of the intervention foods resulted in a similar growth rate irrespective of nutrition education treatment offered. Nutrition education improved weight‐for‐length, irrespective of CF consumed. The similarity in growth rate across study arms signifies the potential of edible cricket protein in growth of infants' comparative to the super cereal. Introduction

Malnutrition, often driven by multiple determinants including poor diet and inadequate feeding practices (Black et al. 2013), affects a child's growth, health and social interaction (Cooke et al. 2023; Grantham‐McGregor et al. 2007). About 41% of the global stunted children under 5 years (U5s) old live in Africa (UNICEF; WHO 2021). In spite of gradual decline in Kenyan stunting rate from 40% in 1993 to 18% in 2022, the rates are still unacceptably high (KNBS, ICF 2022). Maternal nutritional knowledge gaps such as knowledge on breastfeeding and safe complementary feeding practices, are recognized underlying causes of malnutrition (Bhutta et al. 2013). While early childhood growth faltering reflects past exposures, it is accelerated at the introduction of complementary feeding (Leroy and Frongillo 2019) which increases the risk of stunting (Dewey 2003; World Health Organization WHO 2023). In low‐income settings, complementary food with low diversity and nutrient density, along with inadequate feeding practices remains a challenge for optimal child growth and nutrition (Tomori et al. 2024). Combating child stunting therefore requires both nutrition‐specific and nutrition‐sensitive interventions (Hossain et al. 2017). However, a child's growth response to improved complementary food and feeding practices remains to be fully understood.

In the complementary diet, animal source foods (ASFs) are considered to provide high‐quality protein with the required essential amino acids and bioavailable micronutrients for optimal growth (Dewey 2003; Pokharel et al. 2023). With climate change, global warming and an increase in population, there is a need for high‐quality, socially equitable and fairly accessible ASFs having low environmental impacts (Willett et al. 2019). Globally, edible insects are considered possible alternative sources of dietary protein and micronutrients (Van Huis et al. 2021) that can potentially enhance infant complementary feeds (Siddiqui et al.2023) while protecting the environment.

Worldwide, more than 2000 insect species are recognized as edible. Overall, insects share the nutritional profiles of ASFs. However, the specific nutritional quality for each relevant species needs to be established. Studies of the nutritional and health impacts of proving insects in children's diet remain scattered (Boit et al. 2024). For example, in Kenya, locally formulated complementary foods, of which one composition included 10% dried powdered termites along with other local ingredients, did not impact growth or body composition of infants differently than a corn‐soy‐blend (CSB) while haemoglobin (Hb) level deteriorated (Konyole et al. 2019). In Democratic Republic of Congo, providing a daily supplement of 30 g caterpillar powder added to infant complementary diets did not impact on stunting while Hb level improved, compared to usual Congolese diet (Bauserman et al. 2015). In Cambodia, providing complementary local foods with edible cricket powder along with nutritional education was found to increase Hb with no significant improvement in linear growth, compared with nutritional education alone (Menasria et al. 2018).

Among the edible insect species, different cricket species, including Gryllus bimaculatus, are favoured for farming (Omuse et al. 2024). This is due to their efficient feed conversion ratio with minimal impact on the environment (Halloran et al. 2017) and good protein quality (Malla et al. 2022). Cricket has an indicatively positive impact on gut microbiota in adults, potentially promoting nutrient absorption (Stull et al. 2018). Cricket has been shown to be a good source of well‐absorbable zinc (Hilaj et al. 2025), while adding cricket powder to maize porridge lowered iron absorption (Mwangi et al. 2022).

This study was a sub‐study of the multi‐country HEALTHYNSECT research project that was aimed at generating evidence on the effectiveness of educational interventions in promoting insect farming for household consumption in Africa (Alemu et al. 2023). This study particularly assessed the efficacy of exposing infants to a daily serving of complementary porridge containing edible crickets, combined with or without maternal nutrition education, on infant growth over 8 months intervention period. A super cereal porridge product used in the management of malnutrition based on plant‐protein without edible cricket was included as a positive control. The primary outcome assessed was impacts on linear growth, and secondary outcomes included other anthropometric measures.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a blinded randomized control trial (RCT), testing the effect of enriching a complementary porridge with edible cricket (cricket treatment, CR) in combinations with or without nutrition education sessions to mothers (education treatment, ED). The 2 × 2 factorial study was designed with two main factors having two levels; cricket treatment provides porridge with or without edible cricket (+CR vs. −CR) and education treatment included provision or non‐provision of education intervention (+ED vs. −ED). The resulting four study arms were infants receiving cricket treatment with education treatment (+CR & +ED), cricket treatment with no education treatment (+CR & −ED), no cricket treatment with education treatment (−CR & +ED), and no cricket treatment with no education treatment (−CR & −ED). From the study we report the effect on the primary outcome on linear growth, effects on secondary anthropometric outcomes (weight, MUAC, skinfolds) and nutrition‐ associated factors.

Study Site and Participants

The study was conducted at Rwambwa Sub‐County Hospital in Alego Usonga Constituency, Siaya County, Kenya with mother–infant dyads as participants. Mothers of 6‐month‐old infants were identified and invited to the health facility for screening by a trained nutritionist. All the mothers with infants invited for screening were informed of the study. The infants were screened for eligibility by a trained nutritionist based on middle upper arm circumference (MUAC) > 11.5 cm, defining severe acute malnutrition (SAM) (WHO Guideline 2017), infant age of 6 months and seeking health post‐natal services at Rwambwa health facility. Any infant with a low Hb of ≤ 7 g/dL was excluded based on a finger‐prick haemoglobin sample (WHO WHO 2011). Similarly, those severely stunted (LAZ ≤ −3 Z‐scores) or underweight (WAZ ≤ −3 Z‐scores) by anthropometric measurement (WHO Guideline 2017), and judged to be unwell because they were on medication or seeking medical care (largely malaria and diarrhoeal cases) at the time of assessment were also excluded. The infants who were unwell were referred for medical care at the Rwambwa Health facility. Eligible mother–infant dyads were enrolled upon consenting. In the consent the mothers were informed of the right to withdraw at any time and the use of data collected for research purposes only. This information was provided orally and as a written detailed consent form. The mother was asked to consent for mother–child dyad participation through the signing of the written consent form. A total of 284 mother–infant dyads consented and were enrolled between February 2023 and April 2023.

Randomization and Blinding

Enrolled mother–infant dyads were randomized into four study arms in a ratio of 1:1:1:1. The randomization lists were generated by a third party, who was not part of the research team, using sealedenvelope.com. The lists were generated in blocks of 8, stratified by sex aiming at enrolling equal numbers of male and female infants. Randomization to treatment arm was done continuously after consenting. The treatments were identified by letters (A, B, C, D) with the treatment allocation code kept at the University of Copenhagen by a faculty staff not involved in the study. Researchers and data analyst were blinded from the treatment code until the end of the primary data analysis. At the implementation site, the code was assigned in trust to one study administrator solely responsible for field randomization and intervention food distribution. Partial blinding was achieved by distributing the intervention foods in similar plastic containers distinguished only by a food label code, with no disclosure as to who received which intervention food to the study team. Blinding on nutrition education for the study implementation team was not possible.

Intervention

Eligible dyads were enrolled, participated in the baseline survey, randomized to a treatment arm and were offered the month's food portion. The food experimental group received flour with protein from edible crickets (+CR), while the positive control received the standard Corn Soy Blend plus (super cereal) with no cricket powder (−CR) added. Nutrition education (+ED) was conducted using audio‐visual teaching methods from the second month after enrolment. At every visit, the child's health history was taken by asking the mother incidences of child illness in the last month. Details of the symptoms experienced in the 2 weeks were recorded. Some of the symptoms tracked over time included fever, diarrhoea, vomiting, cough and skin rash. Any number of incidences within the month constituted a single disease episode.

Experimental Food Production

The cereals used were locally sourced with finger millet (Eleusine coracana) from Juja cereal market and dehulled maize (Zea mays) from Nafaka maize millers located at Kasarani, Kenya. Edible crickets (Gryllus bimaculatus) were sourced as dried milled powder from Insectipro, Kenya. Vitamin/mineral premix (FBF‐V‐13) was sourced from Dutch State Mines, South Africa. Ingredients of +CR were blended using an automated mixer, preconditioned by mixing 2 L of water in 50 kg of raw materials and extruded in a locally fabricated twin‐screw extruder. The extrudates were milled and sieved through a 0.45‐mm sieve size and caramel essence flavour added at the rate of 50 g/100 kg as detailed in Kiiru et al. (2024). The positive control intervention food without edible crickets (−CR) was identical to the Corn‐Soy Blend plus product provided as a ‘Super cereal' in humanitarian programs. At production, all formulations were standardized to contain standard proportions of energy, protein and carbohydrates for the whole study. A similar mineral and vitamin premix used in the standard corn‐soy product was added to standardize the micronutrient content of the product with edible crickets in Kiiru et. al. (2024) (summarised in Supporting Information S1: 1). The intervention and positive control foods (+CR and −CR) were packed in similar airtight plastic containers, each weighing 500 g.

Nutrition Education Session

The education training materials were consolidated from key nutrition messages in a workshop with contributions from nutrition and education experts. Mothers in the education arm were trained on nutrition and feeding concepts, including the value of nutrition, food diversity, food quality and texture for infants, responsive feeding, infant feeding frequency, continued breastfeeding up to 2 years, how to promote and sustain breast milk production, feeding during illness and general hygiene. The training was done using audio‐visual techniques, including recorded video clips and wall chart hangings, in a face‐to‐face session in combination with phone‐based selected education short text messages (SMS) that were (see Supporting Information S1: 2 and 3) sent regularly. The education sessions were conducted monthly from the second to the seventh visit, making a total of six contact sessions. The no education (−ED) group were excluded from participating in targeted education training or receiving SMS with targeted education messaging.

Study Implementation

All participants were provided the assigned CF in monthly batches as a take‐home food ratio for a period of 8 months. This monthly ratio was estimated to meet the target infant's daily serving, covering 30 days, and was issued monthly with each child receiving the ration for a total of 8 months between February 2023 to January 2024. The food ratios were adjusted appropriately for age, as per WHO guidelines for complementary feeding of breastfed children (Dewey 2003). This adjustment resulted in two containers of intervention food (each 500 g) monthly for infants aged between 6 and 8 months, three containers between 9 and 11months and five containers beyond 11months. Mothers assigned to the nutrition treatment in addition to the intervention food received the six‐session training offered monthly, as described in the nutrition education section. Monthly return visits were scheduled with general health messages and appointment reminders sent to all mothers via SMS. At follow‐up visits, the infants' anthropometric measurements (length, weight, MUAC, skinfolds) were collected. Questionnaires assessing food diversity and household food security were administered at every visit by a trained nutritionist. The child's consumption within the last 7 days was documented at every visit by the nutritionist, through maternal dietary recall. Household food security assessment was done at the sixth visit. Other trained research assistants were responsible for guiding the mothers and conducting routine health surveys on a monthly basis. To retain participants in the study, mothers were given monthly appointment cards and sent SMS appointment reminders 2 days prior to their appointment time.

Data Collection

Anthropometric measurements of mothers and infants were collected at baseline with infants' anthropometric measurements being repeated at every monthly visit. Infant length and head circumference were measured using the Seca 210 infant length measure mat and a measuring tape calibrated to an accuracy of 5 and 1 mm, respectively. Both mother and infant's weight were measured using KERN & SOHN GmbH, Germany‐made Kern 572‐30 digital scale to an accuracy of 0.1 kg. Infants' weights were determined by the mother–baby weight difference method. Skinfolds were measured in millimetres using stainless steel Harpenden skinfold callipers. Mothers' heights were measured using adult ShorrBoard ICA Measuring Board manufactured by Weigh and Measure LLC in the USA, at an accuracy level of 0.1 cm. All questionnaires on household characteristics, family size, feeding practices and child health were built on KoboCollect App. Study data expert working on‐site downloaded and reviewed electronic study data weekly. Data cleaning was done using Stata study‐specific data management codes and validated with support of field data staff for clarity and obvious edits. Both data and Stata do‐files were stored in a password‐protected computer. Data were backed up weekly using an external hard drive, which was then securely stored in a lockable drawer.

Compliance

Compliance to study feeding procedures was monitored indirectly by routinely asking the mother when the child last consumed the study porridge. If study porridge consumption happened multiple days prior to a visit date, the reason why was documented and the mother encouraged to strictly feed the porridge to the index child, and no other family member. Additionally, in every subsequent visit, mothers brought back empty containers (see Supporting Information S1: 4) from previous supply. These empty containers were collected and assessed for evidence of repeated finger touch to establish repeated consumption (Teshome et al. 2018). Based on both compliance assessments, the mother was one more time encouraged to continue feeding the index child daily.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size was based on four groups: effect size = 0.2, significance level = 0.05, power of test = 80%, yielding 70 clients per study arm, and giving a total required sample size of 280 infants. The effect size of 0.2 standard deviations allowed us to detect a 0.58 cm child length difference between the groups after intervening for 8 months.

We used descriptive statistics to describe population distribution across the study arms at baseline. Absolute length gained per child from baseline to end‐line was a key effect estimate used to answer the question, “how does cricket enriched porridge and provision of nutrition education influence a child's growth?”. We also calculated length for age Z‐score (LAZ) for assessment of stunting status. Overall paired anthropometric differences between baseline and end‐line measurements were calculated and both intentions‐to‐treat (ITT) and per‐protocol (PP) analyses conducted based on mixed linear model. We modelled data as a two‐level repeated measure with Level 1 being the repeated visits and Level 2 representing the individual participating child. Linear mixed models were fitted with the baseline value of the outcome variable, included as part of the dependent variable. The analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model included three‐way interactions between time and the two treatments: Nutrition education and CF porridge type. The models were a priori adjusted for sex as fixed effects, and participant ID as random effects to account for differences between individual infants in the study while estimating the effect of the two intervention treatments. Secondary to investigating the impacts of the intervention, we conducted logistic regression analysis on selected household, maternal and child characteristics as predictors of child nutritional status and growth in the entire study population. The outcome for the two logistic models were defined by a MUAC cut‐off < 136 mm for those at risk of undernutrition (Dhungana 2014; Eaton‐Evans 2013), and a LAZ of cut‐off of (Z‐score < −2) used to classify stunted growth.

Ethics Statement

We obtained ethical approval from Institutional Scientific Ethics Review Committee of the university of East Africa Baraton (UEAB/ISERC/23/12/2022) and research permit from National Commission for Science, Technology and Innovation (NACOSTI) (Ref No. 475925). This trial is registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov (ID number: NCT06002620). We discussed the study protocol and procedures with the local health authority within Siaya County and got an implementation clearance. Each mother of the infant gave a written informed consent after either reading or being read to the consent in the language of their choice. Mothers took home a copy of a fully signed consent form. All data sets were stored in password‐protected computers with personal identifiers stripped off the analytical dataset. Study arms codes were keyed in as codes during the analysis phase and only broken at the end of the analysis.

Results

A total of 303 mothers were contacted and invited for screening. Of the 303 invited, 19 infants dropped off at screening; 3 of them, the parents declined participation, 8 did not meet the inclusion criteria while another 8 were not reachable on phone contact they provided. Besides being unreachable the eight never honoured the screening appointment given at during invitation. The primary caregivers of all 284 infants enrolled and randomized were the biological mothers to the infants. Among the enrolled infants were six pairs of twins. Each twin pair was randomized and assigned to the same treatment group. A total of 244 of the enrolled infants completed the end‐line survey giving a retention rate of 85.9%. Of the 1988 (284 × 7) potential monthly follow‐up visits, 1607 (80.8%) visits were honoured. During the study period, 40 participants were lost to follow‐up, 2 of whom died. We conducted verbal autopsies on death cause with results indicating no attributable link of the deaths to any study product or procedures (see Figure 1).

Intervention Impacts on Primary and Secondary Outcomes

Comparing primary and secondary measures at baseline to end‐line, using paired mean difference for participants completing both baseline and end‐line (n = 244) resulted in an average gain of 8.5 cm in length (translating to slightly above 1 cm growth rate per month), 1.9 kg body weight and 3.2 cm in head circumference. For the primary outcome, results of ITT analysis in Table 2 show no significant treatment interaction between ED and CR treatments. There was a significant effect on the cricket treatment for the primary outcome, linear growth measured by LAZ (with a contrast Z = −0.28 [95% CI: 0.03, 0.53] [p = 0.03]) and no significant effect (0.18 [95% CI: −0.07, 0.43] [p = 0.17]) of education or cricket treatment on primary outcomes of linear growth measured by total length gained. Generally, the linear growth trend was more or less similar across the four treatment arms as depicted by Figure 2. For the secondary outcomes, a significant difference was observed on WLZ (−0.45 [95% CI: −0.78, −0.13] [p = 0.01] in Z‐score) between infants whose mothers did not receive nutrition education and those who did (−ED vs. +ED), irrespective of the cricket treatment (−CR or +CR). A small significant difference of 0.95 mm with 95% CI of (−0.92, 0.55) [p = 0.01] in abdominal skinfold was observed between those who received −CR versus +CR. Comparing +ED against −ED, resulted in an increase of 0.02 (95% CI: −0.67, 0.72) mm in suprailiac skinfold, while comparing −CR versus +CR resulted in an increase of 0.08 (95% CI: −0.61, 0.78) mm in suprailiac skinfold.

Per protocol analysis in Table 3 shows results of linear mixed‐effect models of infants who completed the study, having both baseline and end‐line interview with complete anthropometric measurements documented. The findings showed no significant effects of either education or cricket treatment on the primary outcomes of linear growth as measured by length gained. The effect of cricket treatment on LAZ was significantly different by −0.28 (95% CI: 0.01, 0.54) [p = 0.04] Z‐scores indicates that the observed difference in LAZ scores is not likely to be due to random chance, suggesting an actual underlying difference in growth status between those on +CR compared to −CR. Similar to ITT, comparing +ED to −ED was associated with a significant difference in Weight‐for‐Length Z‐score −0.59 WLZ (95% CI: −0.93, −0.26) [p = 0.001] and significant difference in abdominal skinfold of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.12, 1.62) [p = 0.02] mm. Adjusting for bottle feeding in the per‐protocol analysis did not change the results (see Supporting Information S1: 5).

To explore underlying causes which may explain the impacts of the intervention on nutritional status, we performed a secondary analysis of risks of undernutrition, irrespective of the intervention allocation. For this analysis, we used MUAC cut‐off < 136 mm to categorize infants at risk of undernutrition (Dhungana 2014; Eaton‐Evans 2013) and LAZ cut‐off of −2 Z‐score to classify those stunted, we conducted logistic regression shown in Table 4. Girls were significantly (p = 0.01) 1.98 times at higher risk of malnutrition by MUAC compared to boys. Those who consumed ASFs more than twice in the week prior to end‐line survey were (p = 0.12, CI 0.26–1.16) less likely (OR = 0.55) to be stunted than those who consumed ASFs less than three times per week. The 66 infants reported to be bottle fed before 6 months of age were almost twice at risk (OR 1.95, p = 0.09, CI 0.89–4.27) of being stunted than those who were not. Cumulative number of disease episodes significantly increased the risk of undernutrition by MUAC. Compared to those with no disease episode over the study period, those having 1–2 episodes were significantly (p = 0.07) 3.29 times likely to be undernourished. Those with three or four episodes were significantly (p = 0.03) 4.30 times likely while those with 5+ episodes were significantly (p = 0.01) 5.85 times likely to suffer undernutrition as indicated by MUAC. Of the 44 infants who had either vomiting or diarrhoea symptom, 10 experienced vomiting, 14 suffered diarrhoea while 20 experienced both diarrhoea and vomiting within the study period. A vomiting infant was significantly 4.50 times likely to be stunted. Those with diarrhoea were 1.91 times likely stunted. Those who reported both diarrhoea and vomiting incidence had 0.89 times likelihood of being stunted. Additionally, those who reported both diarrhoea and vomiting incidence were 1.06 times likely to experience undernutrition (MUAC < 136 mm) than those with neither diarrhoea nor vomiting episode.

Discussion

Effect of the Nutrition and Education Intervention on Stunting

We observed a significant difference in LAZ for the intervention foods even though there was no significant difference in linear growth assessed by total length gained across the study arms in both ITT (all 284 infants) and per protocol analysis with 244 infants. The difference between absolute length gain and LAZ may also be attributed to smaller error margins in indices, hence smaller confidence intervals compared to absolute values.

Prevention or correction of stunting in infants is inherently complex. Low consumption of ASFs is associated with high prevalence of stunting, suggesting ASFs may play a greater and more nuanced role in reducing malnutrition in early life than previously thought (Headey et al. 2018). For instance, in a recent meta‐analysis of data from over 112,000 children aged 6–23 months from 46 countries, Headey et al. (2018). found that differences between children who did, and did not, consume ASFs became increasingly significant as children aged, with the largest differences around 15–18 months. Food‐based interventions targeting prevention of stunting using insect protein diet, however, have previously reported no significant reduction in stunting which is comparable to our finding (Konyole et al. 2019; Bauserman et al. 2015; Menasria et al. 2018). At the end of the trial period, across intervention arms, the infants in our study each grew by 8.5 cm on average, while the prevalence of stunting increased slightly from 12.4% to 16.0%.

A study in Cambodia by Menasria et al. (2018) observed an increased stunting prevalence from 20.7% to 42.3%. This cluster‐randomized controlled community trial daily provided three groups of children, aged 6–23 months, with 41 g edible cricket‐enriched powder with nutrition education, 16 g moringa powder with nutrition education and to the third group nutrition education alone. Unlike our study, this study had a wider enrolment age range (6–23 months) and a shorter follow‐up period of 6 months. In another individually randomized study in Cambodia, infants were enrolled at the age of 6 months. These children continued with growth faltering despite being put on daily supplement with different complementary intervention foods. One of the complementary food used was an unfortified product containing a small proportion of edible tarantula (Skau et al. 2019).

Another study in Democratic Republic of Congo provided caterpillar cereal supplemented complementary food to children aged 6–18 months and reported a dramatic increase in stunting prevalence from 35% to 69% at age 18 months (Bauserman et al. 2015). Unlike our study, this study used a local diet for control while we used super cereal. An individually randomized study by Konyole et al. (2019) observed an increase in stunting from 13% to 32%. This study enrolled infants at 6 months, randomized them to different daily complementary foods and followed the infants to 15 months within Kenya in a geographical region similar to our study. Stunting increased in all treatment groups, of which one group included a product with edible termites. The two individually randomized studies in Kenya and Cambodia both included intervention groups receiving standard fortified humanitarian corn‐soy blends (CSBs), including milk‐based CSB++, which also failed to prevent stunting. The increase in prevalence of stunting in our study was therefore considerably less than these previous edible insect studies.

A study in Uganda enrolling only already stunted children at a mean age of 30 months found supplementing with energy‐dense lipid‐based supplements reversed stunting and induced catch‐up growth, in contrast to an un‐supplemented control group (Mbabazi et al. 2023). The successful reversal of stunting in already stunted children in the Uganda study indicates that nutrition programs providing high‐quality food supplements are relevant in the treatment of stunting, while the prevention of stunting in the general population remains complex, and calls for multi‐sectorial approaches (Leroy and Frongillo 2019). These findings suggest that stunting is a manifestation of a complex biological response to compromised past and present living conditions. Such that, supporting the critical transition from exclusive breast milk with fortified nutritionally optimal supplements alone though beneficial (Taneja et al. 2022) is insufficient to prevent stunting.

Another possible explanation for the finding could lie in the duration and period when the study was implemented. This study was implemented within the first 14 months leaving the child with additional 10 months to complete the child's first 1000 days, which ends at the second birth date. Shi et al (2023) confirm that nutrition interventions with short intervention time may not show obvious effect. In their study, they referred to the period below 6 months as short, yet we find comparable results with 8 months follow‐up period. Similar argument on duration of intervention for nutrition studies was advanced by Elisaria et al. (2021) and validated by Palmer et al. who observed that high‐quality protein with or without presumptive treatment for enteric pathogens did not improve linear growth between 6 months and 1 year (Palmer et al. 2025). Our observation of a significant difference LAZ between −CR and +CR in spite of insignificant change in absolute length is similar to the observation of Lundeen et al. who observed LAZ score increased despite deficits in length growth among children in developing countries (Lundeen et al. 2014).

In addition, the comparable linear growth effect across the study arms in our study could also be attributed to other causes. First, though the supplement was provided in the early phase of introducing complementary foods, infants in the study were well breastfed at baseline and throughout the 8 months intervention. Continued breastfeeding was also promoted in the nutrition education sessions with 231 (95.1%) reported being breastfed at end‐line survey. A sub‐study of 58 mother–infant dyads in this study was assessed for breastmilk intake using the deuterium dose‐to‐mother method. This sub‐study established the mean breast milk intake of 783±211 g/day for infants with an average age of 6.9 ± 0.6 months (unpublished data). With breast milk being the best diet for a child, this overall high breastfeeding intensity could likely mask any potential effects of provided food supplements.

Second, in families with other young siblings, potential food sharing could not be fully controlled for. Our secondary analysis (Table 4) did not indicate family size as risk factor for malnutrition, but independent of the household size, food sharing could be an unaccounted factor within families. Third, the severe household food insecurity in half of the households could also potentially limit the efficacy of the intervention food. This food situation level is similar to the findings of Oloo et al. (2021), who observed that 36.6% of households reported just surviving and 37.7% were insecure in Ugunja, Siaya County. A situation affecting the household's ability to sufficiently access adequate and quality food for its members.

Effect of Nutrition Education on Weight

Those who received education had comparatively better WLZ‐score than those who did not. Specifically, ITT analysis indicates those on +ED were better than −ED, by 0.45 weight‐for‐length z‐score. This weight gain in the +ED group may be attributed to changes in child food and feeding practices following mothers' exposure to educational intervention and adequate breastfeeding practices, leading to higher energy availability for tissue deposition (Mennella et al. 2018; Dharod et al. 2023). Improved food diversity among children 6–23 months following nutrition education was also observed by Adugna et al. (2024) in a community‐based cluster randomized trial in Ethiopia. Similarly, weight gain following improved dietary behaviour has been observed in pregnant mothers, children or both in other studies (Beressa et al. 2024; Hadi et al. 2023; Jahan et al. 2014). This clearly demonstrates mothers' abilities to translate nutrition education content to practice leading to increased nutrient intake. The repeated consumption and positive practices support the greater positive protein dose response rate associated with animal source protein as advanced by Kittisakmontri et al. (2022).

Effect of Study Intervention on Skinfold

The body composition of infant changes progressively from birth throughout infancy, as they grow and mature. Suprailiac and abdominal skinfolds dropped within standard trends band of skinfolds for age in the PP analysis (WHO 2024). In ITT analysis, those who received nutrition education had a positive though not significant effect size of 0.07 mm on suprailiac compared to those without. The triceps had a contrast of between 0.24 in the education treatment to 0.002 mm in the cricket treatment (Table 2). This observed triceps contrasts mimic the trend observed by Reddy and Rao while studying growth patterns in India. They noted a consistent accumulation of muscle and fat in children aged between 6 and 14 months (Reddy and Papa Rao 2000). Additionally, the positive effect on abdominal fold observed in the ITT analysis for those receiving +CR against those on −CR could be attributed to a rapid increase in the diameter of fat cells, for those on +CR associated with the body's response to accommodate surplus energy (Boulton et al. 1978).

Effect of Illness on Child Nutrition

The growth and nutritional faltering observed during episodes of illness is similar to the observations of Bourdon et al. (2024), who following children aged 2–23 months for 180 days during recovery from acute illness in Africa and South Asia, found that poor growth and underweight persisted after acute illness. Disease episodes of whatever nature were detrimental, with increased episodes worsening the nutritional situation. Post‐primary education (Secondary+, OR = 0.81), male sex (Female, OR = 1.98) and cumulative food diversity (4+ Visits with 4+ Food Groups, OR = 1.15) were protective against malnutrition. Indicating that mothers with below secondary education, female infants and those consuming four food groups in less than four study visits, had a higher risk of being malnourished. A higher stunting likelihood was observed among those with below secondary education and females. Mkhize and Sibanda (2020) outline similar factors as sex, monotonous diet, nutritional knowledge and illiterate caregiver among others. This emphasises the need to include sex, maternal education and child's food diversity in public health child nutrition interventions as also recommended by Okutse and Athiany (2025). Cautiously the diarrhoea and vomiting episodes reported in this study may not meet the WHO definition (WHO 2025), as we queried symptoms following a reported incidence of illness. Hence, we interpret the diarrhoea and vomiting findings with caution due to the possible risk of overreporting diarrhoeal cases, and low number reporting vomiting too limits our conclusions.

Early Introduction of Bottle Feeding

Bottle feeding is routinely discouraged in Kenya based on challenges of sanitation and potential contamination when left exposed. However, some mothers continued bottle feeding in the early months of the child's life, as also observed by Terefe et al. (2024). A practice probably encouraged by suckling bottles mimicking breastfeeding in situations where work or other maternal engagements force mothers to separate from their children for a while. It is therefore critical to acknowledge that while breastfeeding is the global priority, bottle feeding is still a prevalent practice. Although our analysis reveals a non‐significant (at alpha 0.05) risk of bottle feeding to malnutrition (OR: 1.29, p = 0.41) and stunting (OR: 1.95, p = 0.09), the challenges of cleaning, handling and storing feeding bottles should be emphasised to reduce inappropriate bottle feeding practices (Kotowski et al. 2020).

Practical Significance

This intervention demonstrates how using cricket‐enriched porridge could expand the dietary diversity in complementary feeding addressing the protein and other micronutrients deficiencies associated with low ASF consumption. With the high acceptance rate of this study on porridge (Okeyo et al. 2025), global efforts to locally produce and increase adoption of insects as food open an avenue for entrepreneurial production of sustainable nutrient‐rich food. Locally produced cricket‐enriched porridge will potentially relieve the household budgets with lower environmental footprint (less water, land, and greenhouse gas emissions) compared to large animal production.

Limitation

The study design was limited by the lack of an un‐supplemented feeding group, which would have added to understanding the effect of providing a complementary food supplement, irrespective of the composition. Ethics of enrolling children without providing a supplement limited the study design in this respect. Second, adherence to consuming intervention food was based on mother‐reported responses, a method that can potentially increase reporting bias. To minimize this bias, we ensured mothers returned empty food packs during subsequent visits for verification. The study suffered some loss to follow‐up, which may introduce potential selection bias and limit the power of the study. Finally, bottle‐feeding was defined as at least an instance of suckling a bottle with teat without assessing the frequency per day, duration of bottle‐feeding or circumstances leading to bottle feeding.

Conclusion

In conclusion, childhood disease episodes of whatever nature is detrimental to the nutrition status of the child. Nutrition education can improve the WLZ. The significant positive change in LAZ for +CR against −CR is indicative of the contribution of including edible crickets in a fortified complementary porridge, compared to CSB+, a standard super cereal. Absolute length gain was similar in children consuming the two porridges irrespective of the education arm. This could therefore indicate the competitive nature of the edible crickets fortified complementary porridge in comparison to the super cereal used as a positive control in the study. In conclusion, the similarity in growth rate across study arms signifies the potential of including farmed edible cricket in the diversification of the child diet at home.
