The overweight and obesity landscape in italy: data from the Italy cohort of the multi-country IMPACT-O study
Authors: Dario Tuccinardi, Caterina Conte, Anastasia Lampropoulou, Marco Galasso, Laura Bertoccini, Mengyuan Shang, Esther Artime
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe IMPACT-O study was a multi-country, retrospective cohort study that utilized healthcare databases to describe the landscape/impact of obesity. Here, we describe the characteristics and management of adults with a first record of overweight/obesity or obesity in Italy between 2018 and 2022.MethodsThe IQVIA longitudinal patient database in Italy was used to identify individuals with a first record of overweight/obesity, as determined by diagnosis codes and/or body mass index (BMI) measurements (overweight/obesity cohort: BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2; obesity cohort: BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), in the years 2018–2022. Demographic parameters, obesity-related complications (ORCs), and lifestyle, pharmacological, and surgical interventions—as recorded by general practitioners contributing to the database—were described.ResultsThe overweight/obesity cohort included 38,144 individuals, and the obesity cohort 16,489 individuals. Most individuals with overweight/obesity (72.7%) and obesity (77.1%) presented with ≥ 1 ORCs. The most frequent ORCs among people with obesity were hypertension (48.0%), dyslipidemia (26.2%), osteoarthritis (23.9%), and type 2 diabetes (14.0%). The proportion of individuals receiving pharmacological therapies with an effect on weight was ≤ 1%; there were no records of lifestyle interventions or bariatric surgery.ConclusionsThis analysis suggests a high rate of ORCs in people with overweight or obesity at first available record, and limited use of pharmacological treatment with an effect on weight. This highlights the need for prompt recognition and formal diagnosis of overweight and obesity in healthcare settings, accompanied by early and intensive intervention. Patient management could be improved with a systematic and centralized recording of all interventions in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40618-025-02693-3.

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic relapsing disease affecting a large proportion of children, adolescents, and adults globally [1]. In Italy, the national survey conducted in 2020–2022 estimated rates of 32.6% self-reported overweight and 10.4% self-reported obesity among adults [2]. A clear geographical trend is observed, with southern regions of Italy presenting higher rates of obesity and an inverse correlation with the presence of obesity-focused facilities [3]. Also, a trend towards higher obesity rates is observed in people with a more disadvantaged socioeconomic status [4], a pattern also detected in other developed countries [5]. Further, although adult obesity rates are among the lowest in Europe, data from the Childhood Obesity Surveillance Initiative (2015–2017) of the World Health Organization indicate that 21% of Italian children live with overweight or obesity, one of the highest in Europe [6].

In Italy, obesity is associated with high direct and indirect costs, and could be considered a public health threat [7]. Healthcare costs increase with increasing body mass index (BMI) in all age categories, especially in individuals below 65 years of age [8]. As untreated obesity has a significant economic impact on the Italian healthcare system [9], obesity prevention programs are considered fundamental and urgent.

Obesity is associated with an increased risk of chronic diseases such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and certain cancer types [10–12]. These obesity-related complications (ORCs) are linked to reduced health-related quality of life (HRQoL), and self-esteem [13, 14]. In Italy, 3.6% of cancers in men and 4.0% in women were attributable to overweight and obesity in 2020 [11]. It is estimated that obesity is responsible for over 64,000 deaths (or 10% of all deaths) and over 571,000 person-years lived with disabilities [15]. People living with obesity are at higher cardiovascular risk, and the risk assessment presents notable challenges in these people [16]. According to the Italian Association of Hospital Cardiologists, quantifying cardiovascular risk is critical to understanding the health risk due to obesity and the clinical benefit derived from weight loss [12].

Therapies to treat obesity, including lifestyle interventions, medications, or surgery, have become increasingly available in recent years [17]. The number of bariatric surgery procedures have increased more than threefold in Italy in the past 10 years, and the types of procedures have significantly evolved [18].

Until recently, only three drugs were available in Italy for weight management: orlistat (a selective inhibitor of pancreatic lipases), naltrexone/bupropion (a combination of drugs that reduce appetite), and liraglutide (a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist [GLP-1 RA]). Semaglutide, a second-generation GLP-1 RA, has been approved by the European Medicines Agency for weight management since July 2021, while the dual GLP-1/glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide agonist tirzepatide was approved for weight management in November 2023. In addition to effective and sustainable weight loss, second-generation GLP-1 RA-based obesity-management medications (OMMs) have the potential to improve the treatment of ORCs, such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes (T2D), or liver disease [19]. However, a major barrier to optimal treatment is the lack of recognition of obesity as a disease. In Italy, there is a need to recognize obesity as a disease and to support proactive treatment among policymakers and the general population [6]. Additional evidence from real-world settings is needed to better understand physician awareness of obesity as a disease, the characteristics of people living with overweight/obesity, and how obesity is managed in Italy. Data routinely recorded in electronic medical records (EMR) could provide valuable insights on these aspects.

The epIdeMiology landscape and PAtient Care paThways of Obesity (IMPACT-O) study was a multi-national real-world study that used existing healthcare databases to describe the landscape and impact of overweight and obesity in seven European and Asia-Pacific countries: France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the United Kingdom, Australia and Japan [20]. The results of the first analysis of this multi-country study found that only 13.9% of active subjects (those with medical records in the previous 12 months) in the Italy Longitudinal Patient Database (LPD) were living with overweight/obesity based on BMI record and/or diagnosis codes [20]. Of these, only 23.5% had a formal diagnosis code present. Further, it found that in Italy > 80% of people with overweight or obesity have at least one ORC documented in their records.

Here, we present additional data derived from the IMPACT-O study to further characterize the sociodemographic, clinical, and treatment characteristics of adults in Italy with a first record of overweight/obesity or obesity in the timeframe 2018–2022.

Methods

Study design

The IMPACT-O study was a retrospective cohort study using EMR and claims databases standardized to the Observed Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM) [21]. Multi-country results have been described before [20]. All analyses performed in this study were conducted in accordance with Data Use Agreement terms as specified by the data owner (IQVIA). The present analysis reports country-level data for the Italy LPD extracted in April 2023 for the period 1 January 2018 to 30 September 2022. There was no Institutional Review Board applicable to the usage and dissemination of the result sets or required protocol registration with additional ethics oversight for Italy.

Database

The Italy LPD comprises anonymized patient records collected from software used by general practitioners (GPs) during an office visit to document patients’ clinical records. Data coverage includes approximately 2.4 million cumulative patients with at least one visit. It is estimated that the coverage over the total population of Italy is 4.0%. Approximately 1,500 GPs contribute to the database. Dates of service include from 2012 through to the present. Diagnoses were captured with the International Classification of Diseases 9th Revision Clinical Modification (ICD-9CM) codes, mapped to the Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine (SNOMED) diagnosis codes in the OMOP CDM.

Population

This analysis focused on two cohorts with a first record of overweight or obesity during 2018–2022. The study time periods and variables collected are shown in Fig. 1. The overweight/obesity and the obesity cohorts consisted of adults with at least one diagnostic code and/or BMI measurement above the threshold indicating the presence of overweight/obesity (≥ 25 kg/m2) or obesity (≥ 30 kg/m2), respectively. Therefore, the overweight/obesity cohort included the obesity cohort. BMI thresholds were defined according to the World Health Organization classification [22]. For both cohorts, the index date was defined as the patient’s first record, in the study period, of either a diagnostic code or BMI measurement indicating overweight/obesity or obesity. Individuals were included if ≥ 18 years of age at the index date, with ≥ 12 months of follow-up before and ≥ 12 months after the index date. Individuals were excluded if data on age or gender were missing, there was a BMI record that indicated non-overweight/obesity or obesity within ± 30 days from the index date (to avoid contradicting diagnostic code and BMI record), or if there was a BMI record that indicated overweight/obesity or obesity at any time before the index date.

Variables

Demographics (age and gender) were captured at the index date. In individuals identified by a diagnostic code for overweight/obesity, BMI values recorded within ± 60 days of the index date were considered. If multiple measurements were available, the one closest to the index date was used. BMI was utilized as recorded or as derived from weight and height measurements (BMI = [weight in kg]/[height in meters]2). Baseline cardiometabolic parameters (glycated hemoglobin, lipids, estimated glomerular filtration rate, blood pressure) were captured within ± 60 days of the index date and, if there were > 1 recorded measurement, the one on the date closest to the index date was used. ORCs were captured within 12 months before the index date, including the index date. ORCs of interest were pre-established in the study protocol. The selection of ORCs of interest was guided by previous literature, clinical guidance, and the feasibility of identifying records indicating the presence of an ORC in the study datasets. The presence of ORCs of interest were defined based on the availability in the database of relevant diagnosis codes and/or medication, where it was possible to assign medication to a specific disease (diseases identified by diagnosis and medication were hypertension, dyslipidemia, T2D, depression, and anxiety). Data on concomitant medications within the 12-month follow-up period were also collected. Interventions of interest captured within the 12-month follow-up period were lifestyle interventions, pharmacological treatment with an effect on weight (e.g. GLP-1 RAs and orlistat, as naltrexone/bupropion is only prescribed by specialist physicians in Italy), and bariatric surgery.

Statistical analyses

Given the descriptive nature of the study, the sample size was determined by the availability of data and the number of individuals retrieved from the database. Analyses were conducted using OMOP analytical tools in Structured Query Language (SQL) through snowflake and R (https://www.r-project.org) [21]. Descriptive statistics were reported using frequency and percentage distributions for categorical variables, including the percentage of missing data as applicable. Continuous and count variables were described using mean, median, and standard deviation (SD). Subgroup analyses were performed to compare the profile of adults aged < 65 versus ≥ 65 years and females versus males. Given the time period of data extraction, a sensitivity analysis was also performed to evaluate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, including only people with an index date from 2018 to 1 January 2020.

Descriptive results are provided separately for each cohort.

Results

Of the 1,791,140 active people in the Italy LPD from 2018 to 2022, 389,979 (21.8%) had a BMI measurement. Of the people with a first record of overweight or obesity in 2018–2022, 38,144 (2.1%) were included in the overweight/obesity cohort, and 16,489 (0.9%) were included in the obesity cohort (Fig. 2).

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

The demographic and clinical characteristics of both cohorts are shown in Table 1. In the overweight/obesity and obesity cohorts, 56.1% and 59.3% were female, respectively. Most (69.0%) individuals in the overweight/obesity cohort presented with a BMI of 25.0 to < 30.0 kg/m2; 31.0% had a BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2 (Fig. 3). In the obesity cohort, most (73.9%) had a BMI in the range 30.0 to < 35.0 kg/m2.

Obesity-related complications and interventions

A total of 72.7% of individuals with overweight/obesity and 77.1% of individuals with obesity presented with ≥ 1 ORCs in the 12-months before the index date (Fig. 4). The most frequent ORCs among individuals with overweight/obesity or obesity were cardiovascular, including hypertension (40.9% and 48.0%, respectively), and dyslipidemia (25.2% and 26.2%, respectively) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1); 21.4% and 23.9% of individuals with overweight/obesity or obesity had osteoarthritis, and 10.4% and 14.0% T2D, respectively. Also, 11.6% and 12.9% had depression, and 9.7% and 10.4% had anxiety, respectively. Approximately 50% of individuals with obesity were taking medication for the treatment of hypertension and cardiovascular disease (Table 1), and 16.9% were taking antidepressants and anxiolytics. There were no records of lifestyle interventions or bariatric surgery in the overweight/obesity or obesity cohorts. The proportions of patients receiving pharmacological therapies with an effect on weight was ≤ 1% (Table 1).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The subgroup analysis by age showed that in both the overweight/obesity and obesity groups, the rates of ORCs and concomitant medication use increased substantially in people aged ≥ 65 years compared with people aged < 65 years, particularly concerning the cardiovascular disease-related ORCs and medications; however, use of pharmacological therapies with an effect on weight was not different between age groups (Supplementary Table S2). When stratified by sex, males in the overweight/obesity and obesity groups had slightly higher rates of hypertension and dyslipidemia than females in the corresponding groups, but substantially lower rates of osteoarthritis, depression, and anxiety (Supplementary Table S3). The use of pharmacological therapies with an effect on weight was similar between sexes for both groups.

The sensitivity analysis compared the demographic and clinical characteristics of the whole cohort with those of people with an index date before 1 January 2020 and showed no differences between cohorts for the overweight/obesity and overweight groups (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

This study described the demographic and clinical characteristics of new cases of overweight/obesity or obesity in the Italy LPD in the period January 2018 to September 2022. The results show that the study population already presented with a high rate of ORCs at first overweight or obesity record available, mainly cardiovascular. The study also suggests a lack of formal recording of interventions outside of pharmacological treatment with an effect on weight (GLP-1 RAs and orlistat), with no cases of lifestyle interventions or bariatric surgery recorded. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses showed no impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the results, and an expected increase in ORCs and concomitant medication use with age.

In this study, the overweight/obesity and the obesity cohorts were described at the first record of overweight or obesity. This study showed small percentages of adults presenting with new cases of overweight/obesity (2.1%) or obesity (0.9%) in the Italy LPD. In the multi-country analysis of people with any record of overweight or obesity, only 13.9% of individuals in the Italy LPD were identified with overweight or obesity based on BMI and/or diagnosis codes, and of these, less than a quarter (23.5%) had a diagnosis code recorded [20]. As self-reported estimates of overweight and obesity in Italy show prevalences of > 30% for overweight and ~ 10% for obesity [2], the IMPACT-O results overall suggest a limited recognition and reporting of overweight or obesity by GPs in Italy. Also, the Italian data from the ACTION-IO study showed that only 34% of the participants with obesity reported having received a formal diagnosis of the disease [23].

Under-recording of overweight and obesity is likely to lead to an underestimation of the impact on healthcare settings and consequently suboptimal care for these individuals [24]. Documenting a diagnosis of obesity may be an important step towards appropriate weight management [25, 26]. It has been suggested that implementing a referral network of specialist services and multidisciplinary team centers for obesity management could help GPs in the management of people with obesity [27].

In the present study, hypertension and dyslipidemia were the most frequent ORCs, consistent with other studies [28, 29]. Hypertension was more commonly recorded in the obesity cohort than in the overweight/obesity cohort, as was T2D. Depression and anxiety were also frequent ORCs among individuals in these cohorts. Numerous studies indicate a strong association between mood disorders and obesity [30]. The relationship between obesity and poor mental health is bidirectional, with obesity causing anxiety, depression, stress, and low self-esteem, and the presence of mental health problems such as depression and anxiety leading to overeating [31]. It has been suggested that policies aimed at reducing obesity could also reduce depressive symptoms (i.e., sleep trouble and eating disturbances) in Italy [32]. The use of antidepressants or anxiolytics was also relatively common in the overweight/obesity (15.7%) and the obesity (16.9%) cohorts. Some of these medications are associated with changes in body weight, which can further complicate the management of obesity [33]. In the present study, a subgroup analysis by sex showed that men in the overweight/obesity and obesity groups had lower rates of depression and anxiety than women in the corresponding cohort, which aligns with reports in the literature that women with obesity may be at greater risk of mental health disorders than men with obesity [34].

It is noteworthy that the ORC burden among people with overweight or obesity was high at the first available record, with more than two-thirds presenting with at least one ORC. Our study captures the formally recognized diagnosis of overweight or obesity and BMI measurement over the threshold within healthcare settings as the first record. Additionally, a recent survey found that ORCs are often a trigger for initiation of weight management and treatment-related discussions [35]. Together with our results, this suggests that formal diagnosis of obesity may be delayed and only “recognized” or captured when other diseases are present. People with overweight or obesity without ORCs may, therefore, be underrepresented in this study and underrecognized by the healthcare system. Further research to better understand the temporal correlation of ORCs with the documentation, assessment, and management of obesity, as well as the impact on outcomes, is warranted.

It is increasingly recognized that BMI alone is not an indicator of health. Studies have shown that many ORCs, including hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, are associated with alternative markers of high adiposity, such as abdominal obesity and high waist-to-height ratio [36, 37]. In addition, people with a BMI in the normal range but with a high body fat percentage (so-called “normal-weight obesity”) have been shown to have increased levels of cardiometabolic risk factors [38–40]. As a result, recent guidelines for obesity are moving away from BMI as the sole way of diagnosing obesity, recommending instead BMI plus anthropometric measures (waist-to-height ratio, waist circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio) and/or direct body fat measurement, as well as evidence of reduced tissue/organ function, impairment of daily activities, or psychological impacts [41, 42].

Over most of the time period of data collection (2018–2022), only the GLP-1 RA liraglutide was available in Italy, with semaglutide available only from 2021. A very small proportion of adults received GLP-1 RAs (≤ 1%), and most likely these medications were prescribed for the treatment of T2D rather than for weight management or for both, although it is not possible to confirm this since recording of GLP-1 RA use in the Italy LPD did not include dose or indication. The Italy LPD comprises records by GPs, who frequently refer individuals with excess weight to obesity specialists for treatment [27]. However, despite the availability of codes for lifestyle or bariatric surgery interventions in this database, no records were found for these treatments. In contrast, a recent study found that 36.8% of people discharged from hospital in Italy who had a severe obesity diagnosis code had undergone at least one bariatric surgery procedure [15]. This study highlighted the significant and increasing burden of severe obesity on Italy’s healthcare system, and the growing utilization of bariatric surgery [15, 18]. The discrepancy highlights gaps in the recording of obesity interventions in healthcare databases.

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the data. As the Italy LPD contains anonymized patient data collected from EMR used by GPs, and EMR data are not originally designed for research purposes, it is possible that they are incomplete or contain errors. The database relied on convenience sampling from selected regional areas rather than nationally representative population-based cohorts. Therefore, the cohorts included here may not represent the Italian population as a whole. As noted above, reliance on BMI alone as a measure of obesity is flawed, however there was a lack of recording of waist and hip measurements, which prevented an analysis using anthropometric measures. The stratification of the cohorts into overlapping populations (overweight/obesity and obesity) is likely to have obscured some of the differences between people with overweight and people with obesity with respect to ORCs and interventions. Also, as mentioned before, some interventions could be misclassified or poorly recorded, such as lifestyle interventions or other pharmacological treatments. Data were derived from individuals who visited GPs, so under-recording of some interventions only prescribed by specialists is possible. Additionally, the database does not allow patient linkage between data sources, leading to the potential for duplicated records if people seek alternate opinions, which can complicate quantifying treatment patterns. However, the large sample sizes likely minimize major distortion.

The study used medications as a proxy, where possible, for the presence of some ORCs. This approach was not used consistently for all relevant ORCs as some do not have specific treatments, or were not specifically analyzed for the purpose of the present study. Therefore, data on the relative presence of ORCs need to be interpreted cautiously.

In conclusion, this study suggests that the recognition and recording of people with obesity in Italy could be suboptimal. Also, this study highlights the high number of cases of ORCs in people with a first record of overweight or obesity in the database in Italy. More specifically, our study confirms the burden of ORCs in adults with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) and highlights that ORCs may also occur frequently in adults with overweight (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), suggesting the need for prompt formal diagnosis before ORCs develop, and earlier and more intensive intervention for adults with overweight or obesity. Patient management could be improved with a systematic and centralized recording of all interventions in this population (including structured diet and exercise programs) and the sharing of patient data across all disciplines/specialties responsible for weight management. Some of these individuals could benefit from the new pharmacological treatments, which target both obesity and chronic conditions such as T2D or cardiovascular disease. The results of this study also highlight the need for further treatment data for obesity in Italy to inform future obesity management policies in order to improve the care of people with overweight and obesity.
