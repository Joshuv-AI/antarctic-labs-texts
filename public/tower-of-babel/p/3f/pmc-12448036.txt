Hidden misinformation in YouTube short videos on syphilis: A mixed-methods study
Authors: Emi Furukawa, Tsuyoshi Okuhara, Shinya Ito, Ritsuko Shirabe, Hiroko Okada, Takahiro Kiuchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveYouTube short videos constitute a key informational resource for individuals at high risk of sexually transmitted infections (STIs). We conducted a quality assessment of short videos about syphilis intended for the general public in Japan.MethodsIn October 2024, a comprehensive sample of YouTube short videos on syphilis was retrieved using keywords frequently used to search for information on the disease. mDISCERN criteria were employed to assess the reliability of the information in the 72 videos selected for analysis. Reflexive thematic analysis was used to qualitatively examine misinformation embedded within the videos.ResultsThe mean mDISCERN score of the videos was 2.0 (SD 0.9) and 80 % of the videos did not meet the reliability criteria. One in five videos potentially hindered audience engagement in health behaviors by inducing fear of the disease, reinforcing stigma and insensitivity toward high-risk individuals, and punitively portraying those infected. Some of these messages originated from healthcare professionals, indicating their potential role in reinforcing such biases.ConclusionYouTube short videos can support syphilis awareness and prevention, but difficulties with information reliability and lack of quality are common. Stigmatizing content may hinder health-seeking behaviors. Enhancing the quality and sensitivity of messages, particularly those from healthcare professionals, is essential to maximize their public health impact.InnovationThis study is among the first to analyze YouTube short videos about STIs, combining qualitative and quantitative methods to assess misinformation.

Background

The syphilis epidemic worldwide and in Japan

Globally, sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are widespread and negatively impact public health [1]. Syphilis is a common STI [2] that can cause severe systemic complications and poses serious risks to fetal health when contracted during pregnancy [3]. An estimated 8 million new syphilis cases were reported per year in the 15–49-year-old age group worldwide in 2022 [4]. Cases of syphilis have been increasing in Japan since 2013. More than 14,000 cases were documented in 2023, despite the decrease in in-person contact due to the COVID-19 pandemic [5,6].

Research on syphilis prevention through social media

Effective health communication for STI prevention involves a combination of media outreach, community-specific messaging, and digital platforms to reach diverse populations and promote preventive behaviors [7,8]. A systematic review by Friedman et al. [7] described social marketing and media campaigns about STIs, noting that several communication strategies, such as disseminating behavior-specific messages and using social media, are promising in encouraging preventive behaviors such as condom use and STI testing. Swanton et al.'s systematic review found online interventions to be effective, noting that social media and app-based education positively impact sexual health knowledge and screening behaviors [8]. Advances in health promotion using audiovisual materials may offer new opportunities to provide health interventions to young people, thereby reducing the risk of STIs. For example, online video platforms such as YouTube and TikTok are gaining a growing presence as health information sources. Increasingly, people are using their smartphones to select and consume information and entertainment for brief periods of time. Short videos meet their demand by providing quick and highly engaging content, and have potential application across a range of clinical areas [[9], [10], [11]].

Studies of misinformation risks in videos on STIs

Despite the potential educational value of videos on STIs, some videos contain erroneous or misleading information, referred to as misinformation [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Misinformation is defined as “information that is contrary to the epistemic consensus of the scientific community regarding a phenomenon” [16]. In the context of infectious diseases, myths and misinformation related to HIV/AIDS include misperceptions about medical facts as well as biases toward attributes of the infected population. For example, a 2012 survey of Americans on HIV/AIDS pointed out that one-third of the respondents harbored misperceptions about transmission, such as HIV is transmitted by sharing a drinking glass, contact with a toilet seat, or swimming in the same pool as an HIV-positive person [17]. The misperceptions still exist as of the 2020s, as Smallwood et al. reported that prejudices such as “half of Blacks are HIV positive“ and “HIV/AIDS is a gay men's disease” [18]. The spread of misinformation about HIV/AIDS [19], Ebola virus [20], H1N1 [21], and COVID-19 [22] has hampered efforts to eradicate or contain such diseases [23,24]. Misinformation also has a negative impact on infected individuals and individuals at high risk for STIs. Research has shown that women exposed to misinformation on sexual health internalize stigma and reduce their intention to seek help [25]. Moreover, people with HIV/AIDS have been reported to avoid having conversations about HIV/AIDS and to be reluctant to obtain further information about their health [26]. Thus, the spread of misinformation about syphilis is likely a major factor in the persisting syphilis epidemic [27]. Although online video platforms have provided many short videos on the topics of sexual health and STIs [28], studies evaluating information reliability or misinformation of online videos are scarce. Sahin et al. [29] quantitatively evaluated the quality of syphilis-related videos in terms of understandability and actionability. However, to date, no studies have assessed the accuracy and reliability of the information presented in videos about syphilis. Moreover, the factors that may undermine the reliability of such videos have not been thoroughly analyzed.

The objective of this study was to comprehensively evaluate the content and quality of short videos on syphilis aimed at the general public in Japan. To achieve this goal, the following research questions were asked:RQ1: How reliable is information presented in short videos about syphilis?RQ1–1: Which elements in reliability are most likely to be aware of by the information providers?RQ1–2: Do the attributes of the publishers or supervisors in videos influence the reliability of the information?RQ2: What misinformation is contained in those videos?

Methods

Study design

We adopted an explanatory sequential design. First, we quantitatively assessed the reliability of the information using established evidence-based indicators. To further explain the results of the quantitative data, we conducted a qualitative analysis using the reflexive thematic analysis. This study was exempt from review by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Tokyo Hospital because YouTube videos are publicly accessible and do not involve patients or their records.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study incorporated a comprehensive set of short videos explaining syphilis to the general public. YouTube is accessed by more than 2 billion users each month worldwide [13], and 95.2 % of users in Japan who watch free video streaming services reported using YouTube [30]. Short videos provided by YouTube are defined as vertical videos of 60 s or less as of October 1, 2024, and are known to appeal to a younger audience [31,32]. Given that Japanese syphilis cases are skewed toward the younger population, with women in their 20s and men in their 20s to 50s [[33], [34], [35]], it is assumed that high-risk and infected individuals with syphilis use short videos as their primary source of medical information. Therefore, we considered the need to evaluate short videos independently from regular YouTube videos without time limitations. The exclusion criteria were: 1) duplicates of videos already included in the study sample; 2) non-Japanese videos; 3) videos with irrelevant topics (e.g., topics that included STIs other than syphilis or when syphilis was featured in a historical program or drama), and 4) videos with a duration of more than 1 min.

Search strategy

The first author (EF) searched Google Trends on October 1, 2024, for the following most frequently searched related keywords within the last 3 months (July 1, 2024 – October 1, 2024): “syphilis,” “syphilis symptoms,” “syphilis infection,” “syphilis test,” “STD syphilis,” “syphilis early-stage,” “syphilis treatment,” “syphilis early symptoms,” “syphilis transmission,” “syphilis women,” “syphilis rash,” “syphilis images,” “syphilis men.” Google Trends data are measured in relative search volumes, with scores ranging from 0 to 100 and constituting a normalized proportion of the absolute search volume compared with the total search volume for the given topics, time periods, and locations specified. All the keywords were entered in Japanese. Search results were displayed in the default order of relevance, and no filtering criteria were established.

Using the YouTube API, the top 50 videos per keyword were extracted [36]. Research on internet search engines has reported that over 90 % of search engine users click on results only within the first three pages [37]. Similar to web pages, we inferred that users would be unlikely to view beyond the fiftieth video. Online searches were performed in incognito mode to minimize bias in the search results.

Evaluation methods

Quantitative evaluation of the reliability of information (mDISCERN)

mDISCERN is a simplified version of DISCERN developed by Charnock et al. [38], which is an indicator dedicated to information reliability. In past social media studies, this indicator has been frequently used for the purpose of detecting and analyzing misinformation; DISCERN focuses on clarity, source reliability, and bias, but since these factors are often inconsistent across social media platforms and are particularly suited for evaluation by DISCERN [39,40]. mDISCERN was chosen for this study for three reasons: (1) It is widely used as an indicator to assess the quality of healthcare information. (2) It is helpful in content analysis in the context of non-English-speaking countries [41], and (3) It is frequently used in studies targeting social media [42,43]. All items are binary variables (Yes: 1 point; No: 0 point), with a total score range of 0–5. Higher mDISCERN scores indicate higher-quality information provision (Table 2). This study considered videos with an mDISCERN score of 3 or more to have high information reliability [44,45].

One internist and one gynecologist (EF and RS, both board-certified and experienced in STIs) independently rated a quarter of the videos according to the mDISCERN coding manual developed for this study (Supplemental Table 1) [46].To evaluate the accuracy and bias of the information, the raters referred to domestic and international guidelines and information from professional organizations [[47], [48], [49], [50]]. The inter-rater reliability was analyzed using Gwet's AC1 for the rating tool on each video. Gwet's AC1 results are interpreted as excellent (1.00–0.75), good (0.74–0.60), fair (0.59–0.40), and poor agreement (<0.40). After calculating the inter-rater reliability, raters discussed the discrepancies and created a set of consensus scores for each video.

Qualitative evaluation of the content and tone of the information

We conducted a reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) of videos that did not meet the criteria for information reliability based on the mDISCERN tool [51]. RTA is a qualitative research process that positions the researcher's interpretive perspective as an integral component of the analytic process. This methodology allows the dataset to be organized minimally and described in rich and detail by identifying and analyzing patterns or themes in the data [52].

In the context of patient education, multimedia instruction involves using words and visual elements (e.g., images, diagrams, charts, and graphs) to help patients and the general public better understand health information. The multimedia principle suggests that people learn more effectively from a combination of words and images than from words alone [53,54]. Based on the video content analysis by Iye et al. [55], the following elements were the subject of the qualitative evaluation:•Non-verbal images: images without verbal information, including facial expressions, gestures, and background images.•Verbal images: verbal information conveyed through visuals, such as subtitles and captions.•Verbal audio: information spoken by the video cast or narration.•Non-verbal audio: background music, sound effects, and non-verbal sounds produced by the cast (e.g., applause, laughter, or gasps).

Characteristics of the videos

Metadata

Metadata extracted from the videos included video length, the number of views, likes, and comments based on information on the page containing the video.

Source

Based on previous studies [13,57,58], we categorized the sources into six categories: 1) medical institutions (university laboratories, hospitals, and clinics); 2) healthcare professionals (individuals who identified themselves as doctors, nurses, pharmacists, or other healthcare professionals); 3) government agencies (prefectures and municipalities); 4) media (newspapers, TV stations, or Internet media); 5) for-profit companies, and 6) non-professional individuals (influencers, or individuals whose profession or expertise were unclear).

Medical supervision

Videos published by medical institutions or healthcare professionals were coded as “with medical supervision.” Videos that stated “supervised by a medical professional” or included commentary by a medical professional were also coded as “with medical supervision,” even if the publisher was not a medical professional.

Topic

We assessed the video content using the six categories from Kong [13] and Goobie [27] as follows: 1) signs and symptoms, 2) transmission, 3) screening and testing, 4) treatment, and 5) outcome. For videos with multiple topics, all applicable topics were coded.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the duration, number of views, video characteristics, and distributions of the mDISCERN scores. Comparisons of mDISCERN scores between groups were made using Student's t-test for comparisons between videos with and without medical supervision, and videos published by medical and non-medical experts. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed for comparisons between sources (medical institutions, healthcare professionals, governmental organizations, media, for-profit companies, and individual non-healthcare professionals). Tukey's multiple comparison test was used for comparisons between each source.

P-values were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.0 (2024-04-24).

Misinformation in short videos

Misinformation was found in 14 (19.4 %) videos. Of these, one (1.3 %) was created by a medical institution, three (4.2 %) by healthcare professionals, and two (2.8 %) were from other sources but supervised by healthcare professionals. Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

This study employed an explanatory sequential design, in which the initial quantitative assessment of information reliability—based on established evidence-based indicators—was followed by qualitative analysis. The reflexive thematic analysis enabled a deeper interpretation of the quantitative findings, shedding light on the nature and implications of the identified misinformation. Moreover, the integration of the quantitative and qualitative results warns that the videos sent out by healthcare professionals are not as reliable as other videos and that some of these videos may in fact reinforce discrimination and prejudice.

Reliability of the videos

Fewer than 30 % of the evaluated short videos met the reliability criteria of the mDISCERN tool. A scoping review of 21 studies analyzing the quality of YouTube content related to women's health, including topics on sexual health, similarly reported that the overall quality of these videos was low, with poor ratings for usefulness, accuracy, and reliability [59]. Our study further demonstrated that the absence of source attribution and a lack of reference to uncertainty are factors that frequently undermine the credibility of videos. Given that short videos are limited to a runtime of 60 s, the extent of health information that can be conveyed through video alone is inherently limited. Therefore, content creators must provide supplementary information to prevent audience confusion and support appropriate health-related behaviors. It is recommended that additional sources be included within the video or its description, and that the creators explicitly acknowledge uncertainty while encouraging viewers to consult healthcare professionals.

A notable finding from this study is that even videos involving healthcare professionals often exhibited low reliability. In the field of sexual health, studies of YouTube videos related to topics such as hysteroscopy and cervical spondylosis reported that videos deemed reliable were predominantly those produced by physicians or professional organizations [60,61]. In other clinical areas, the quality of information disseminated by non-medical professionals tends to be low, but healthcare professionals are typically relied on to produce higher-quality videos [62,63]. Based on these findings, Wang et al. [61] suggested that encouraging academic societies and clinicians to create more educational content is a potential solution to improve the reliability and quality of YouTube videos. However, in the context of syphilis-related information dissemination in Japan, even healthcare professionals appear to lack sufficient awareness regarding the reliability of the information they provide. Our findings suggest the need to establish enforceable guidelines for Japanese healthcare professionals, promoting the inclusion of unbiased, evidence-based information, clear source attribution, and explicit acknowledgment of uncertainty in their video content.

Misinformation and bias within the short video messages

This study qualitatively analyzed the nature of misinformation that compromised the reliability of the videos and poses direct harm to viewers. Misinformation was found in nearly 20 % of the top-searched short videos on syphilis. Of these, approximately half used AI-generated, overly grotesque and exaggerated images to evoke fear of the disease. Fear arousal communication has traditionally been used to incite the general public to take appropriate health actions. However, the effectiveness of such approaches is controversial. Tannenbaum et al.'s meta-analysis of psychological studies found that fear appeals are effective in positively influencing attitudes, intentions, and behavior [64]. In contrast, studies during the COVID-19 or influenza pandemic indicated that fear arousal communication is ineffective in ensuring adherence to infection prevention behaviors [[65], [66], [67]]. Studies in the context of non-communicable diseases, such as cancer, have also shown that fear is associated with help-seeking avoidance or delays in even thinking about cancer, and may prolong the interval before help-seeking occurs [68]. In the present study, the visual images shared in some videos are a double-edged sword for viewers' health behavior: they draw the attention of those who are indifferent to the disease, but on the other hand, they may inhibit the help-seeking behavior of high-risk individuals.

Punitive expressions toward those infected or at high risk for syphilis were also prominent, alongside language that attributed infection to “irresponsible” sexual behavior. Females constitute one-third of patients affected by the syphilis epidemic in Japan, who are predominantly in their 20s [33,34]. Based on this epidemiological data, some videos attributed the syphilis epidemic to the increase in compensated dating or matching apps [69]. The actual cause of the syphilis epidemic is unknown, and a survey conducted by the Japanese Society for Sexually Transmitted Infections and Japan Broadcasting Corporation reported that 70 % of women infected with syphilis were infected by a specific partner [70]. Furthermore, a 2015 systematic review noted that syphilis is transmitted to heterosexual partners at a rate as high as 51 % to 64 % during a single sexual encounter [71]. However, none of the videos mentioned those facts, leading to concerns that the viewing public may have misperceptions about the transmission of the disease, placing infected individuals at risk of being socially disadvantaged and leading actual high-risk individuals to remain unaware of their infection.

Medical professionals were involved in disseminating misinformation and promoting prejudice and discrimination. In a video shared by a medical professional, misinformation was presented in stating that syphilis can be transmitted merely by “passing a glass around” or by kissing. Most syphilis transmission is through sexual activity involving mucous membrane contact with an infected individual. While syphilis transmission was reported when an individual's wounded hand came in contact with an object contaminated with large amounts of T. pallidum, such cases are extremely rare [72]. Concern arises that infected individuals or those at high risk who watch the videos may delay testing or treatment because of fear of discriminatory behavior from healthcare professionals. For instance, prior research has demonstrated that stigma regarding STIs serves as a major obstacle to screening behaviors among young people [73]. Healthcare professionals should always be aware that their patients might be exposed to bias and misinformation about STIs through online videos. They should provide evidence-based, unbiased information to their patients and create an environment where they feel comfortable and free from stigma.

The study has several limitations. First, we included only Japanese-language videos in our analysis, thereby limiting the study generalizability in the broader healthcare context. Second, we performed the search in order of relevance based on YouTube's default settings. Therefore, it is possible that the short videos viewed by users will not precisely match the videos we retrieved. For example, since YouTube prioritizes videos originating from reliable sources for medical information [74], it is possible that videos that presented misinformation about treatments, which could be potentially life-threatening to patients, were excluded from the screening process. Nevertheless, this study revealed that expressions that may foster misconceptions about the disease, or reinforce stigma and prejudice, are not subject to such filtering and remain publicly accessible. Third, this study focused on short videos of 60 s or less in length. The little variation in playback time and the focus on multiple topics in these videos made it difficult to conduct a comparative analysis on all the factors discussed in previous studies [[75], [76], [77]]. For this reason, we focused our comparative study on the sources and supervisors covered by the majority of prior studies. Finally, given that this study analyzed only the video content, audience perception of the videos remains unclear. In particular, message effectiveness was not measured in this study and is a subject for future research.

Innovation

This study, for the first time, comprehensively evaluated the reliability and misinformation of YouTube videos focusing on syphilis. Social media platforms, including Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, and YouTube, have been widely studied as health communication channels, primarily for non-communicable diseases [78]. For communicable diseases, video media such as YouTube have only been studied in recent years, particularly since the COVID-19 pandemic [79,80]. In contrast, social network-based videos on STIs have not yet been fully evaluated, with only one YouTube analysis by Sahin et al. [29] focusing on syphilis in English-speaking countries. Sahin et al. assessed videos for understandability and actionability, stating that many were easy to understand. In particular, they indicated that videos featuring narrators, live-action footage, and photographic illustrations were easier to understand than animations or simple explanations. However, to date, there has been no study that discusses the quality of the media presentation in terms of misinformation, with deep reflection. Our study builds upon and extends this work by applying validated quantitative content analysis methods in conjunction with reflexive thematic analysis to provide deeper insights into STI-related social media communication and misinformation. By integrating these complementary approaches, the study offers a richer, multidimensional understanding of how syphilis-related health information is presented and potentially interpreted.

Conclusion

The overall reliability of Japanese YouTube short videos about syphilis is poor, with limited reference to additional information and acknowledgment of uncertainty. Some videos contained messages that reinforced prejudice and discrimination against those infected with syphilis, and the evidence suggests that healthcare professionals can also contribute to this issue.
