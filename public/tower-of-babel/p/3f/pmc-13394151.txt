Oncogenic BRAF and KRAS Promote Global DNA Hypomethylation Through a Directed Pathway That Upregulates TET3
Authors: Ichiro Onoyama, W Rod Hardy, Shoichiro Takeishi, Lihua Julie Zhu, Yanglan Tan, Lloyd Hutchinson, Jianhong Ou, Leyuan Ma, Ching‐Man Virbasius, Masafumi Yasunaga, Keiichi I Nakayama, Scott A Shaffer, Michael R Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAberrant epigenetic modification is one of the characteristics of the cancer genome. DNA hypermethylation of cytosine‐phospho‐guanine (CpG) islands, a hallmark of cancer cells, is well‐studied and contributes to cancer development by silencing tumor suppressor genes. However, the mechanisms and biological significance of global DNA hypomethylation in cancer are still unclear. Here, using the v‐Raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B1 (BRAF) V600E knock‐in mouse models, we demonstrate that endogenous expression of oncogenic BRAFV600E in non‐transformed cells promotes global DNA hypomethylation by increasing the levels of ten‐eleven translocation 3 (TET3), which converts 5‐methylcytosine (5‐mC) into 5‐hydroxymethylcytosine (5‐hmC). Furthermore, TET3 is targeted for proteasomal degradation by F‐box and WD repeat domain containing 7 (FBXW7). BRAFV600E increases TET3 levels by inhibiting glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), which phosphorylates TET3 and leads to its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. We further found elevated levels of TET3 and 5‐hmC in BRAFV600E‐induced mouse lung tumors and show that TET3 enhances the ability of BRAFV600E to induce the formation of lung tumors. Notably, endogenous expression of oncogenic Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS) G12D also promotes global DNA hypomethylation and induces lung tumors through a similar TET3‐mediated mechanism. Our findings elucidate one of the unknown mechanisms of global DNA hypomethylation promoted by oncogenic BRAF and KRAS and establish a role for TET3 to promote transformation in cooperation with BRAF and KRAS at an early stage of tumorigenesis.Keywords: BRAF, carcinogenesis, DNA demethylation, epigenetics, FBXW7, global DNA hypomethylation, KRAS

Introduction

Epigenetic dysregulation of gene expression plays a major role in the initiation and progression of cancer (reviewed in [1, 2, 3]). The critical role of aberrant epigenetic regulation in cancer development is highlighted by the finding that genes encoding chromatin regulatory factors and histone proteins are recurrently mutated or overexpressed in specific solid tumors and hematological malignancies [4]. Among the various epigenetic alterations in cancer genomes, alterations in DNA methylation are the most extensively studied. DNA methylation typically occurs on cytosine residues in CpG dinucleotides and is mediated by three DNA methyltransferases: DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B [5]. DNMT3A and DNMT3B are responsible for de novo DNA methylation, while DNMT1 is solely responsible for the maintenance of DNA methylation and is unable to mediate de novo DNA methylation [6, 7].

The aberrant DNA methylation of cancer genomes comprises both focal DNA hypermethylation and global DNA hypomethylation (reviewed in [1, 2, 3, 8]). Site‐specific DNA hypermethylation of promoter‐associated CpG islands of tumor suppressor genes and DNA repair genes results in transcriptional silencing (commonly referred to as epigenetic silencing), thereby facilitating the initiation and progression of cancer [1, 2, 3, 8]. Global DNA hypomethylation occurs at various genomic sequences including repetitive elements, retrotransposons, CpG‐poor promoters, introns, and gene deserts. DNA hypomethylation at repeat sequences promotes chromosomal rearrangements, inducing genomic instability [9, 10]. The loss of methylation can also result in inappropriate activation of genes that drive cancer progression [11, 12].

There are two general mechanisms for DNA demethylation. First, decreased DNMT activity can lead to the progressive dilution of DNA methylation during successive rounds of DNA replication, a process called “passive demethylation.” Second, DNA demethylation can occur in a replication‐independent manner by TET proteins (TET1, TET2, or TET3), a process called “active demethylation.” TET proteins function as dioxygenases and convert 5‐mC into 5‐hmC. Further iterative oxidations catalyzed by TET result in 5‐formylcytosine and 5‐carboxylcytosine, which are removed by thymidine DNA glycosylase (reviewed in [13]). While the factors and pathways that mediate DNA hypermethylation in cancer cells have been described [14, 15, 16], the mechanisms of DNA hypomethylation largely remain to be determined.

While the post‐translational regulation of TET proteins has not yet been reported, many proteins are known to be degraded by the ubiquitin‐proteasome system. Fbxw7 is one of the F‐box proteins that constitute the SCF (Skp1‐Cllin‐F‐box protein) ubiquitin ligase complex. F‐box proteins recognize substrates and target them for ubiquitination and consequent degradation. SCFFbxw7 targets many oncoproteins such as cyclin E, c‐Myc, c‐Jun, and Notch. Fbxw7 functions as a tumor suppressor, and mutations in the Fbxw7 gene are detected in many human malignancies [17].

Here we show that endogenous expression of oncogenic BRAF (BRAFV600E) or KRAS (KRASG12D) in non‐transformed primary cultured cells is sufficient to induce a pattern of aberrant DNA methylation, global DNA hypomethylation, and DNA hypermethylation of CpG islands, resembling that found in patient‐derived tumors. Our data indicated that BRAFV600E and KRASG12D impair TET3 degradation by SCFFbxw7‐mediated ubiquitination, promoting genome‐wide DNA hypomethylation. We also showed the biological role of BRAFV600E‐ and KRASG12D‐mediated DNA hypomethylation in tumorigenesis using mouse models.

Materials and Methods

Generation of Mouse Embryonic Fibroblasts (MEFs) and Drug Treatment

To obtain Rosa26‐Cre‐ER T2 ; Braf+/LSL‐V600E MEFs and Rosa26‐Cre‐ER T2 ; Kras+/LSL‐G12D MEFs, Rosa26‐Cre‐ER T2 mice (#008463, Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were crossed with Braf+/LSL‐V600E mice (also called Braf(tm1Cpri) mice; kindly provided by Catrin Pritchard, University of Leicester, UK) or Kras+/LSL‐G12D mice (also called B6.129S4‐Kras(tm4Tyj)IJ; #008179, Jackson Laboratories). Primary MEFs were isolated from male and female E13.5 embryos and cultured in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS at 37°C and 5% CO2. Embryos were genotyped by PCR using the primers listed in Table S1. MEFs from Rosa26‐Cre‐ER T2 ; Braf+/+ or Rosa26‐Cre‐ER T2 ; Kras+/+ littermates of the same sex were used as wild‐type controls.

For conditional activation of BRAFV600E or KRASG12D, MEFs were cultured with 0.1 μM 4‐hydroxytamoxifen (4‐OHT, H6278, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cells were harvested 0, 7, 14, and 21 days later. Cre‐mediated recombination of Braf+/LSL‐V600E to Braf+/V600E and Kras+/LSL‐G12D to Kras+/G12D was detected by genomic PCR using the primers listed in Table S1.

In some experiments, MEFs were treated with 10 μM MG132 (133407‐82‐6, Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) for 8 h, 10 μM IM12 (1129669‐05‐1, EMD Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA) for 24 h, 20 μM BI‐2865 (E1474, Selleck Chemicals LLC, Houston, TX, USA) for 96 h, 20 μM PLX‐4720 (S1152, Selleck Chemicals LLC) for 96 h, or 40 μM U0126 (V1121, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) for 24 h.

Mice were housed and monitored in accordance with protocols approved by the Institution Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Massachusetts Chan Medical School (A‐2300) and Kyushu University, and excluded if they were sick. Because this was a pilot study, a formal power calculation was not required.

RNA Interference

Cells were seeded in a six‐well plate to 70% confluency and subsequently infected with 200 μL lentiviral particles expressing shRNAs (Table S2) in 1 mL of appropriate media supplemented with 10 μg/mL polybrene. After overnight incubation, the medium was replaced. Cells were selected using puromycin (5 μg/mL) for 2 days.

Reduced Representation Bisulfite Sequencing (RRBS)

RRBS libraries were prepared from BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs and KRASG12D‐expressing MEFs (female mice, 3 littermates of each genotype) at 0 and 21 days of 4‐OHT treatment as previously described [18]. BRAF‐wt and KRAS‐wt MEFs (female mice, 3 littermates of each genotype) served as controls. Samples (in triplicate) were run on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 and images were analyzed using OLB 1.9 and CASAVA_v1.8.2. Sequences were binned and trimmed using the barcodes with a perl script written in house. Binned sequences were mapped to the mouse mm10 genome using BSMAP v2.6, which was also used for extracting the methylation ratio of mapped cytosines. Mouse CpG island track from mm10/GRCm38 assembly was downloaded from the UCSC genome browser. Mapped cytosines were assigned to nearest genes using ChIPpeakAnno package [19] in R [20]. Arcsine‐transformed methylation ratios were compared for each 200 bp window using limma package (RRID: SCR_010943) [21] in R to derive “Braf.difference” and “Kras.difference” values (Data S2 and S3). A region was considered hypermethylated in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs if Braf.difference < 0 or hypomethylated if > 0. KRASG12D‐expressing MEFs were analyzed in the same manner. To adjust for multiple comparisons, p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg method [22].

Statistical Analysis

To achieve statistical significance, data were collected from experiments performed in at least technical triplicate; each experiment was repeated two or three times and statistically significant results were obtained in independent biological replicates. Differences between groups were assayed using a two‐tailed student t‐test using Microsoft Excel (RRID: SCR_016137). For IHC quantification, differences between groups were determined using a Chi‐squared test, unless the expected value was under 5, in which case a Fischer's exact test was used. p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

BRAFV600E Promotes Global DNA Hypomethylation by Upregulating TET3

To investigate aberrant DNA methylation occurred in non‐transformed primary cells, we used primary MEFs isolated from Rosa26‐Cre‐ER T2 ; Braf+/LSL‐V600E embryos as a model system [23]. The Braf+/LSL‐V600E transgenic mouse strain allows inducible expression of BRAFV600E from the endogenous Braf locus using LoxP‐stop‐LoxP (LSL)/Cre recombinase technology [23]. Rosa26‐Cre‐ER T2 mice express a 4‐hydroxytamoxifen (4‐OHT)‐activated version of Cre from the ubiquitously expressed Rosa26 locus [24]. Treatment of MEFs with 4‐OHT activates Cre recombinase, leading to the induction of expression of BRAFV600E from the endogenous Braf locus (Figure 1A). While ectopic over‐expression of BRAFV600E in primary cultured cells can induce senescence [25, 26], importantly, previous studies have shown that MEFs expressing BRAFV600E from the endogenous Braf locus do not undergo senescence [23]. We used MEFs from sex‐matched Rosa26‐Cre‐ER T2 ; Braf+/+ littermates (BRAF‐wt MEFs) as wild‐type controls (Figure 1A).

MEFs were treated with 4‐OHT for 7 days to induce the expression of BRAFV600E and analyzed at 0, 7, 14, or 21 days after the initiation of 4‐OHT treatment. We detected Lox‐BrafV600E allele 7 and 21 days after the initiation of 4‐OHT treatment from Rosa26‐Cre‐ERT2; Braf+/LSL‐V600E MEFs by genomic PCR (Figure 1A). To measure the global changes in 5‐mC levels promoted by BRAFV600E, we performed LC–MS analysis. BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs showed significantly lower 5‐mC levels compared with BRAF‐wt MEFs at 21 days after the initiation of 4‐OHT treatment (Figure 1B). This result was also confirmed by ELISA (Figure S1A). These data showed that expression of BRAFV600E promotes DNA hypomethylation.

The conversion of 5‐mC to its oxidation product 5‐hmC is considered the first step in the pathway leading to complete DNA demethylation [27]. To investigate whether BRAFV600E‐mediated global DNA hypomethylation occurs through this pathway, we analyzed 5‐hmC levels in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs using LC–MS and ELISA. At 14 days after the initiation of 4‐OHT treatment, BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs had significantly higher levels of 5‐hmC compared with BRAF‐wt MEFs (Figure 1C and Figure S1B). BRAF‐wt MEFs showed decreased 5‐hmC levels at 14 and 21 days, which was considered a physiological change. However, at 21 days, the 5‐hmC levels were similar between both groups, consistent with the idea that 5‐hmC is a transient intermediate product of DNA demethylation (see 4. Discussion). These results indicate that BRAFV600E‐mediated global DNA hypomethylation occurs through the canonical pathway involving the conversion of 5‐mC to 5‐hmC.

Global DNA hypomethylation results from decreased levels of DNMTs and/or increased levels of proteins that promote DNA demethylation. Immunoblot analysis showed that the expression of BRAFV600E led to increased levels of both DNMT3B (Day 7) and TET3 (Day 14), raising the possibility that TET3 promotes global loss of DNA methylation, whereas DNMT3B promotes site‐specific gain of DNA methylation (Figure 1D). To investigate the mechanism of DNA hypomethylation, we focused on TET3.

To determine whether TET3 promotes the loss of global DNA methylation, we first examined whether loss of TET3 function impacted global 5‐hmC levels. BRAFV600E‐expressing and BRAF‐wt MEFs were infected with lentivirus expressing Cre (LV‐Cre) or Cre plus a Tet3 shRNA (LV‐Cre/shTet3), and 5‐hmC levels were analyzed by ELISA. Tet3 knockdown (Figure S1C) resulted in decreased 5‐hmC levels in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs but not BRAF‐wt MEFs (Figure 1E). Similar results were obtained with a second, unrelated Tet3 shRNA (Figure S1D). Decreased 5‐mC levels in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs were also recovered by Tet3 knockdown (Figure S1E).

We also analyzed DNA methylation on a genome‐wide level using RRBS [28, 29]. While RRBS mainly targets 5‐hmC as well as 5‐mC in promoters and CpG islands, it is still useful to evaluate the genome‐wide methylation pattern. The RRBS analysis identified several thousand regions at which BRAFV600E promoted a significant gain or loss of DNA methylation compared with BRAF‐wt (Data S2). Notably, the number of hypermethylated regions was higher than that of hypomethylated regions within 500 bp from a transcriptional start site (TSS) in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs (Figure 1F) despite the genome‐wide hypomethylation (Figure 1B and Figure S1A). We thus performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments to evaluate the binding of TET3 and DNMT3B to representative hypomethylated and hypermethylated regions identified by RRBS (Data S4) in BRAFV600E‐expressing and BRAF‐wt MEFs. The results showed that TET3 and DNMT3B bound to representative hypomethylated and hypermethylated regions, respectively, in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs (Figure 1G).

Together, these data indicate that endogenous expression of oncogenic BRAFV600E promotes global DNA hypomethylation and hypermethylation near the TSSs in MEFs.

In Non‐Transformed Cells, TET3 Levels Are Maintained at a Low Level by SCFFBXW7 ‐Mediated Ubiquitination and Proteasomal Degradation

We next investigated the mechanism by which TET3 protein levels were increased in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs. qRT‐PCR analysis revealed that Tet3 mRNA levels were not increased 14 days after BRAFV600E activation (Figure 2A), a time at which TET3 protein levels were increased (Figure 1D). It is reported that TET3 promoter region is methylated by DNMT3b in human cells [30], however, the decrease of Tet3 mRNA levels was transient and RRBS data clarified there is no methylated region in upstream regions of TET3 gene in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs (Data S2). This pattern (increased protein level and decreased mRNA level) is often seen when proteasomal degradation is disturbed [31], which raised the possibility that TET3 protein may be degraded by the ubiquitin‐proteasomal system. Consistent with this idea, treatment of MEFs with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 resulted in accumulation of TET3 protein (Figure 2B). Furthermore, cycloheximide chase and immunoblot analysis revealed that TET3 protein was more stable in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs than in BRAF‐wt controls (Figure 2C).

We noticed that TET3 contains two potential cell division control protein 4 (CDC4) phosphodegron (CPD) motifs (Figure 2D), which are high‐affinity binding sites for the F‐box protein FBXW7 (also called FBW7) [32]. These two potential CPD motifs are conserved between human and mouse TET3 (Figure 2D). The CPD motif is not present in TET1 or TET2. FBXW7 is a component of the SCF ubiquitin ligase, and it binds to the phosphorylated CPD motif of target proteins, leading to proteasomal degradation. To determine whether TET3 is a substrate of SCFFBXW7, we investigated whether TET3 and FBXW7 interact. Of the three known isoforms of FBXW7 (FBXW7α, −β, and ‐γ), only FBXW7α localizes in the nucleus [33]. We therefore examined the interaction between TET3 and FBXW7α. Co‐immunoprecipitation experiments confirmed an interaction between ectopically expressed Flag‐FBXW7α and endogenous TET3 (Figure 2E) and ectopically expressed HA‐TET3 (Figure 2F and Figure S2A).

FBXW7 selectively recognizes the phosphorylated CPD motif of substrate proteins. To determine whether the potential CPD motifs of TET3 are important for the interaction with FBXW7α, we mutated the putative phosphorylation sites to alanine in the first CPD motif (T305A/T309A), the second CPD motif (T665A/S669A), and both motifs (T305A/T309A/T665A/S669A, or 4A). Co‐immunoprecipitation analysis revealed that the CPD mutants showed reduced or abolished interactions with FBXW7α (Figure 2F).

We next explored whether FBXW7α is involved in the ubiquitination of TET3. Wild‐type FBXW7α, but not an FBXW7α mutant lacking the F‐box domain (FBXW7αΔF) that does not form the SCF ubiquitin ligase complex, promoted TET3 ubiquitination in an in vivo ubiquitination assay (Figure 2G). Similarly, SCFFBXW7 ubiquitinated TET3 in vitro in the presence of known cofactors (E1, E2, ubiquitin, and ATP) (Figure S2B). Furthermore, ectopic overexpression of wild‐type FBXW7α but not FBXW7αΔF led to decreased endogenous TET3 levels in MEFs (Figure S2C). Conversely, shRNA‐mediated knockdown of Fbxw7 (Figure 2H and Figure S2D) resulted in increased TET3 protein levels (Figure 2H), although Tet3 mRNA levels were not increased, but rather decreased after Fbxw7 knockdown (Figure 2I). This discrepancy is consistent with the observations after BRAFV600E activation (Figures 1D and 2A), which indicated inhibition of proteasomal degradation of Tet3. Cycloheximide chase and immunoblot analysis further showed that the half‐life of TET3 was increased in Fbxw7 knockdown MEFs (Figure S2E). Moreover, the TET3 mutant harboring mutations in both CPD motifs [TET3(4A)], which does not interact with FBXW7α (Figure 2F), was more stable than wild‐type TET3 (Figure S2F). Collectively, these data indicate that SCFFBXW7 is responsible for TET3 ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. Consistent with a role for FBXW7α in modulating TET3 stability, shRNA‐mediated knockdown of Fbxw7 significantly increased 5‐hmC levels, which was mitigated by simultaneous knockdown of Tet3 (Figure 2J and Figure S2G). As expected, Fbxw7 knockdown decreased 5‐mC levels, which were restored by Tet3 knockdown (Figure 2K).

Thus, these results confirm that FBXW7α modulates the ubiquitination and stability of TET3, thereby regulating 5‐mC and 5‐hmC levels.

BRAFV600E‐Mediated Upregulation of TET3 Occurs Through the Inhibition of GSK3β

Previous studies have shown that the ubiquitination of FBXW7 substrates requires phosphorylation of the CPD motif in substrates by glycogen synthase kinase‐3β (GSK3β) [34]. We therefore investigated whether FBXW7α‐mediated ubiquitination of TET3 requires phosphorylation of TET3 by GSK3β. We confirmed that ectopically expressed HA‐TET3 co‐immunoprecipitated with GSK3β (Figure 3A). In vitro kinase assay showed that TET3 was phosphorylated by GSK3β in vitro, and phosphorylation was substantially reduced by mutation of the phosphorylation sites in the CPD motifs (Figure 3B). Moreover, TET3 accumulated in MEFs treated with the GSK3β inhibitor IM12 (Figure 3C). However, IM12 treatment did not affect Tet3 mRNA levels (Figure 3D). To further examine the effect of GSK3β inhibition, we performed co‐immunoprecipitation experiments of Flag‐FBXW7α and HA‐TET3 in 293 T cells treated with IM12. However, the interaction between Flag‐FBXW7α and HA‐TET3 was lost following GSK3β inhibition (Figure S2H). In addition, ELISA showed that 5‐mC levels were decreased after IM12 treatment in MEFs (Figure S2I). These data indicate that GSK3β phosphorylates the CPD motifs of TET3, and GSK3β inhibition leads to the accumulation of TET3 protein without transcriptional upregulation, resulting in reduced 5‐mC levels.

We next investigated why TET3 accumulates in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs. Neither Fbxw7 mRNA (Figure 3E) nor FBXW7α protein levels (Figure 3F) were affected by BRAFV600E expression. We therefore tested whether BRAFV600E affects GSK3β kinase activity. Previous studies showed that GSK3β kinase activity is inhibited by phosphorylation at Ser9 [35]. Immunoblot analysis using a phospho‐Ser9‐specific antibody indicated that expression of BRAFV600E resulted in increased phosphorylation of GSK3β at Ser9 (Figure 3G). In vitro kinase assay confirmed the decreased GSK3β activity in extracts prepared from BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs compared with BRAF‐wt MEFs (Figure 3H). Collectively, these results indicate that BRAFV600E expression results in Ser9 phosphorylation of GSK3β and suppression of its activity, and that reduced GSK3β activity leads to TET3 protein stabilization.

TET3 Promotes BRAFV600E‐Mediated Tumorigenesis at an Early Stage

Global DNA hypomethylation occurs in the early stages of tumorigenesis [36]. To investigate the possible role of TET3 at the early stage of BRAFV600E‐mediated tumorigenesis, we used Braf+/LSL‐V600E mice, which develop benign lung adenomas upon induction of BRAFV600E [23]. We administered LV‐Cre to Braf+/LSL‐V600E mice by nasal injection and analyzed the resulting lung tumors for 5‐hmC and TET3 using immunohistochemistry. Braf+/+ littermates were used as wild‐type controls. As shown in Figure 4A, 5‐hmC and TET3 were virtually undetectable in normal lungs from Braf+/+ mice (see also Figure S3A). By contrast, high levels of 5‐hmC and TET3 were observed in lung tumors 16 weeks after induction of BRAFV600E, and the levels substantially declined after 24 weeks (Figure 4A and Figure S3A). Likewise, while the levels of phosphorylated GSK3β were low in normal lungs from Braf+/+ mice, phosphorylated GSK3β increased in lung tumors 16 weeks following BRAFV600E induction and declined in lung tumors after 24 weeks (Figure 4B and Figure S3A). 5‐mC levels were low in these lung tumor cells (Figure S3B,C).

We next examined whether the ability of BRAFV600E to induce tumors requires TET3. We nasally injected LV‐Cre or LV‐Cre/shTet3 into Braf+/LSL‐V600E mice and analyzed the resulting lung tumors after 16 weeks. Notably, the tumor burden of Braf+/LSL‐V600E mice injected with LV‐Cre/shTet3 was significantly lower than that of Braf+/LSL‐V600E mice injected with control LV‐Cre (Figure 4C and Figure S3D). TET3 levels in lung tumors from Braf+/LSL‐V600E mice 16 weeks following injection with LV‐Cre/shTet3 were lower compared with those in tumors from Braf+/LSL‐V600E mice with LV‐Cre injection, but TET3 expression was still detectable in many cells (Figure S3E). Thus, these tumors may have arisen because they “escaped” Tet3 knockdown. Notably, we found that knockdown of Tet3 induced apoptosis in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs but not control BRAF‐wt MEFs, as evidenced by the increased levels of cleaved caspase 3 (Figure 4D) and Annexin V staining (Figure 4E). Likewise, lungs of Braf+/LSL‐V600E mice injected with LV‐Cre/shTet3 had significantly higher levels of cleaved caspase 3 than those injected with LV‐Cre (Figure 4F). These data indicated that Tet3 expression may prevent apoptosis in BRAF‐activated cells.

KRASG12D Promotes Global DNA Hypomethylation by Upregulating TET3

The BRAFV600E and KRASG12D mouse models of lung tumorigenesis show some similarities. Activation of these oncoproteins leads to continuous activation of MAPK, and Kras+/LSL‐G12D mice also develop lung tumors after Kras activation [37]. To test the generality of our findings, we thus repeated several key experiments described above using KRASG12D MEFs (Figure 5A). Similar to the results with BRAFV600E, KRASG12D‐expressing MEFs showed significantly lower 5‐mC levels compared with their KRAS‐wt counterparts 21 days after the start of 4‐OHT treatment (Figure 5B and Figure S4A) and a transient increase in 5‐hmC levels at 14 days (Figure 5C and Figure S4B). Endogenous expression of KRASG12D led to increased levels of DNMT3B and TET3 (Figure 5D), and Tet3 knockdown resulted in decreased 5‐hmC levels in KRASG12D‐expressing MEFs but not their KRAS‐wt counterparts (Figure 5E and Figure S4C). As shown in Figure 5F, expression of KRASG12D resulted in increased phosphorylation of GSK3β at Ser9. These results indicate that, like BRAFV600E, KRASG12D induces global DNA hypomethylation by upregulating TET3 through the inhibition of GSK3β activity. While KRAS12D was found to induce DNA hypomethylation, RRBS analysis showed that the number of hypermethylated regions was more than that of hypomethylated regions close to TSSs in KRASG12D‐expressing MEFs, similar to observations in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs (Figure 5G and S3).

Thus, BRAFV600E‐expressing and KRASG12D‐expressing MEFs showed the same results regarding DNA hypomethylation. As BRAF and KRAS signaling is well known to work under the same pathway, we next treated BRAFV600E‐expressing and KRASG12D‐expressing MEFs with the KRAS inhibitor, BI‐2865, or the BRAF inhibitor, PLX‐4720 to investigate whether the hypomethylation induced by BRAF or KRAS activation is regulated by the same pathway (Figure S4D). DNA methylation level was increased after PLX‐4720 and BI‐2865 treatment in KRASG12D MEFs. On the other hand, the methylation level of BRAFV600E MEFs was increased after PLX‐4720, but after BI‐2865 treatment. Moreover, the MEK inhibitor, U0126, suppressed the transient increase of 5hmC level in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs (Figure S4E). These results indicate that the KRAS‐BRAF‐MEK pathway is involved in hypomethylation after KRAS or BRAF activation. Notably, WT and BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs showed decreased methylation levels after BI‐2865 treatment (Figure S4D), suggesting that there should be another pathway to regulate DNA methylation levels under non‐mutated KRAS conditions.

KRASG12D Promotes Tumorigenesis in Mice by Upregulating TET3

We next investigated whether TET plays a role early during KRASG12D‐mediated tumorigenesis, as observed with BRAFV600E. High levels of TET3 and 5‐hmC were observed in mouse lung tumors 16 weeks after induction of KRASG12D and subsequently declined (Figure 6A and Figure S5A). Consistent with these results, while the levels of phosphorylated GSK3β were low in normal lungs from Kras+/+ mice, phosphorylated GSK3β increased in lung tumors 16 weeks following KRASG12D induction and subsequently declined (Figure 6B and Figure S5A). 5‐mC levels were low in these lung tumor cells (Figure S5B,C). Moreover, knockdown of Tet3 decreased the ability of KRASG12D to promote the formation of lung tumors in mice (Figure 6C and Figure S5D). Thus, like BRAFV600E, TET3 enhances the ability of KRASG12D to promote the formation of lung tumors.

Tet3 Is Elevated in Human BRAFV600E‐Positive Colorectal Adenomas

To investigate the physiological relevance of our findings, we also analyzed 5‐hmC and TET3 levels in human samples. Given that BRAFV600E‐positive colorectal adenomas are known to be precursors of colorectal adenocarcinomas [38], we examined colorectal adenomas and colorectal carcinomas with BRAFV600E mutation (Table S3). In normal colonic epithelium and colorectal adenomas containing BRAF‐wt, only weak 5‐hmC and TET3 signals could be detected (Figure 7A and Figure S6). By contrast, in BRAFV600E‐positive colorectal adenomas, both 5‐hmC and TET3 signals were much stronger. In BRAFV600E‐positive colorectal adenocarcinomas, 5‐hmC levels were very low, consistent with previous reports [39, 40, 41, 42], and TET3 levels were also very low (Figure 7A and Figure S6). Similarly, the levels of phosphorylated GSK3β were low in human BRAF‐wt colorectal adenoma, high in human BRAFV600E‐positive colorectal adenoma, and low in human BRAFV600E‐positive colorectal adenocarcinoma (Figure 7B and Figure S6). Collectively, these results indicate that 5‐hmC and TET3 levels are elevated in the early stages of tumorigenesis and subsequently decline.

Discussion

TET family proteins are generally considered as tumor suppressors [42]. However, in this study, we showed that TET3 helps to establish genome‐wide DNA hypomethylation that promotes carcinogenesis. We demonstrated that the expression of oncogenic BRAFV600E or KRASG12D is sufficient to promote focal DNA hypermethylation and global DNA hypomethylation. Focal DNA hypermethylation, as evidenced by an increase in methylation in regions close to the TSS, is likely mediated by DNMT3B [7], whereas global DNA hypomethylation is mediated by TET3. Higher levels of 5hmC after BRAF or KRAS activation were transiently observed in both MEFs and lung tumor models. Given that 5hmC production was mediated by TET3 that escaped from ubiquitin‐proteasomal degradation just after BRAF or KRAS activation, this DNA demethylation mechanism is considered to be fundamental to the genome‐wide hypomethylation in cancer cells.

From these results, we propose a model by which oncogenic BRAF or KRAS promotes global DNA hypomethylation (Figure 8). In normal cells, TET3 is phosphorylated by GSK3β and targeted for ubiquitination by SCFFBXW7, resulting in TET3 proteasomal degradation. Previous studies have reported that oncogenic BRAF/MEK/ERK signaling increases Ser9 phosphorylation on GSK3β [43] and oncogenic BRAF leads to phosphorylation of GSK3β on Ser9 in a MEK‐dependent, AKT‐independent manner [44]. GSK3β is phosphorylated on Ser9 by a number of protein kinases [45], including p90‐RSK [46], which itself is phosphorylated and activated by ERK1/2 [47]. Our study indicates that expression of oncogenic BRAF or KRAS results in reduced GSK3β activity, which blocks SCFFBXW7‐mediated TET3 ubiquitination and degradation, leading to elevated TET3 levels. The resulting oncogenic BRAF/KRAS‐mediated increase in TET3 levels is one mechanism that promotes the conversion of 5‐mC to 5‐hmC, followed by demethylation to cytosine. Our results indicate that DNA hypomethylation does not occur in random manner that is subsequently selected for during cancer development but rather is established by a defined pathway initiated by an oncoprotein.

Model of global DNA hypomethylation promoted by oncogenic BRAF or KRAS. In normal cells, TET3 is phosphorylated by GSK3β and targeted for ubiquitination by SCFFBXW7, resulting in its proteasome‐mediated degradation. Expression of oncogenic BRAF or KRAS results in reduced GSK3β activity, which blocks SCFFBXW7‐mediated TET3 ubiquitination and degradation, leading to elevated TET3 levels and increased conversion of 5‐mC to 5‐hmC followed by complete demethylation to cytosine.

Gain of DNA methylation near the TSS after BRAF or KRAS activation may contribute to tumorigenesis by silencing tumor suppressor genes and DNA repair genes. Additionally, several lines of evidence suggest that TET3 functions as an oncoprotein at an early stage of tumorigenesis in conjunction with oncogenic BRAF or KRAS. First, we found that TET3 enhances the ability of BRAFV600E or KRASG12D to promote the development of experimentally induced mouse lung tumors. Our finding that Tet3 knockdown decreases tumor formation is in line with previous results showing that conditional knockout of Dnmt1 increases adenoma formation in mice [48]. Second, we found that, at an early stage of mouse lung tumor formation, TET3 and 5‐hmC levels were increased and subsequently declined; a similar pattern was also observed in human BRAFV600E‐positive colorectal adenomas and colorectal carcinomas. Although we have not investigated KRAS mutations or other organs in human clinical samples, the transient increase in 5‐hmC levels during tumorigenesis is consistent with the notion that global DNA hypomethylation occurs early at a pre‐neoplastic step [36]. Collectively, these findings indicate, unexpectedly, that TET3 functions by a “hit‐and‐run” mechanism and is required for tumor initiation but not maintenance.

We also found that knockdown of Tet3 induces apoptosis in BRAFV600E‐expressing MEFs and mouse lung tumors that express BRAFV600E. Thus, the mechanism by which TET3 cooperates with BRAFV600E and KRASG12D may be by preventing apoptosis. Finally, in support of our conclusion that TET3 functions as an oncoprotein, previous studies reported that FBXW7, which we showed targets TET3 for ubiquitin‐mediated proteasomal degradation, has tumor suppressor activity (reviewed in [49]).

We recognize that the role of TET3 as an oncoprotein contrasts with the general view that TET family proteins are tumor suppressors [42]. However, while there is compelling evidence that TET2 is a tumor suppressor in hematological disorders [50], the roles for TET1 and TET3 are less clear. For example, whereas mutations and deletions in TET2 are relatively common in myeloid malignancies, comparable alterations in TET1 and TET3 are extremely rare [51]. Moreover, TET1 has been found to help promote MLL‐rearranged leukemia [52], and TET1 and TET3 have been reported to have an important role in the formation of breast tumor‐initiating cells [53]. Thus, the role of TET proteins in promoting or preventing tumorigenesis appears to be dependent on the specific TET protein as well as the cancer type.

Overall, our study identified a new mechanism and a biological role of DNA demethylation induced by the BRAF/KRAS‐TET3 axis. BRAF or KRAS signaling activates many signal transduction pathways involved in epigenetic changes, and our findings clarified one of the mechanisms that leads to DNA demethylation just after BRAF/KRAS activation. Given that there are many driver mutations other than oncogenic BRAF/KRAS activation, it is possible that there may be many other mechanisms to establish DNA hypomethylation in cancer cells. Nevertheless, our results could point to a fundamental DNA demethylation mechanism because BRAF/KRAS mutations have been identified as driver mutations in many human cancers. Our findings suggest that upregulation of TET3 and 5hmC in the context of BRAF/KRAS mutations may serve as a biomarker to monitor early stages of carcinogenesis. This work helps to clarify DNA methylation changes in carcinogenesis and may aid in the development of new strategies to prevent cancers.
