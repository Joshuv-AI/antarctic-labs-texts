History of Presentation

A 28-year-old gravida 2 para 1 (G2P1001) woman presented at 30 weeks' gestation with a 4-day history of progressively worsening palpitations, exertional dyspnea, lightheadedness, and fatigue. She reported significant limitations in daily activities, including difficulty climbing stairs. She denied chest pain, syncope, presyncope, fevers, or obstetric complications. Fetal movements were normal. Vital signs at a routine prenatal visit demonstrated persistent tachycardia to 128 beats/min with otherwise stable parameters, prompting referral to the emergency department for further evaluation.Take-Home Messages•Fascicular ventricular tachycardia in pregnancy requires recognition of characteristic electrocardiogram findings—particularly right bundle branch block morphology with left-axis deviation—to ensure accurate diagnosis.•Management typically involves pregnancy-safe beta-blockade (with cautious use of verapamil), deferral of catheter ablation until postpartum when feasible, and coordinated multidisciplinary care to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of anxiety and depression, well controlled with counseling. She had no prior cardiovascular disease, arrhythmias, or history of syncope. Family history was notable for a maternal grandmother with supraventricular tachycardia treated with catheter ablation. Her first pregnancy was uncomplicated.

Investigations

On arrival, the patient was tachycardic to 155 beats/min, and blood pressure was 135/83. Physical examination revealed clear lung fields, normal heart sounds without murmurs, and a soft, nontender gravid abdomen. Peripheral pulses were symmetric. Bedside ultrasonography demonstrated a collapsible inferior vena cava, suggestive of relative intravascular volume depletion.

A follow-up 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated a wide-complex tachycardia with right bundle branch block morphology and left-axis deviation, consistent with posterior fascicular ventricular tachycardia (VT) as seen in Figure 1. This electrocardiogram demonstrates a classic morphology for posterior fascicular VT, although the presence of apparent 1:1 ventriculoatrial conduction—common in slower VT—may complicate differentiation from supraventricular tachycardia and reduce the need for adenosine. In addition, the QRS duration is narrower (∼120 ms) than typically observed in VT associated with structural heart disease. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular systolic and diastolic function with no structural abnormalities. Laboratory studies, including high-sensitivity troponin, thyroid function testing, and D-dimer, were within normal limits. Management (Medical/Interventions)

Initial management included intravenous fluid administration, with subsequent spontaneous conversion to sinus rhythm. Low-dose metoprolol tartrate (12.5 mg twice daily) was initiated for symptomatic control. Due to ongoing symptomatic episodes, the dose was escalated during hospitalization to 25 mg every 4 hours and later transitioned to 50 mg twice daily, resulting in sustained symptom relief.

Verapamil, the first-line therapy for fascicular VT in nonpregnant patients, was avoided because of concerns regarding maternal hypotension and fetal safety. Catheter ablation was deferred until the postpartum period because of fetal radiation exposure risks. Beyond fetal radiation exposure—which can now be minimized or eliminated using electroanatomic mapping or zero-fluoroscopy techniques—other risks are also reduced. These include limiting the use of medications such as sedatives that may affect the fetus and preventing complications that could cause maternal hypotension and necessitate further interventions. Care was coordinated through a multidisciplinary team including cardiology, electrophysiology, and maternal-fetal medicine to optimize maternal and fetal safety.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient, who had been asymptomatic prior to the current pregnancy, experienced complete resolution of palpitations, dyspnea, and exertional fatigue. She tolerated daily activity and ambulation with only minimal, transient increases in heart rate. She was discharged home in stable condition with close outpatient follow-up arranged with cardiology and electrophysiology. No maternal or fetal complications occurred during the remainder of the pregnancy. Postpartum reassessment was planned, with consideration of catheter ablation should symptomatic VT recur.

Discussion

This case reinforces the clinical relevance of fascicular VT as a rare but important consideration in pregnant patients presenting with persistent tachycardia (Figure 2). Fascicular VT is an idiopathic, calcium-dependent ventricular arrhythmia that typically affects young individuals without structural heart disease and is generally well tolerated.1,2 Among idiopathic VTs, outflow tract VT is far more common, accounting for approximately 90% of cases and has been well described during pregnancy, whereas fascicular VT is considerably less frequent and overall shows a male predominance. However, the physiological adaptations of pregnancy—particularly in the third trimester—can significantly alter arrhythmia burden and symptom severity. Fascicular Ventricular Tachycardia in Pregnancy

A 28-year-old woman at 30 weeks' gestation presented with persistent wide-complex tachycardia (right bundle branch block morphology with left-axis deviation) consistent with posterior fascicular ventricular tachycardia, with normal cardiac structure and biomarkers. She converted with intravenous fluids and was managed with metoprolol, with ablation deferred postpartum, highlighting the importance of early electrocardiogram recognition and pregnancy-adapted, multidisciplinary care.

The patient's presenting symptoms of dyspnea, palpitations, and fatigue initially overlapped with common physiologic complaints of pregnancy, underscoring the diagnostic challenge frequently encountered in this population.4 Nevertheless, the persistence and progression of symptoms, coupled with sustained resting tachycardia, appropriately prompted further evaluation. The electrocardiographic finding of wide-complex tachycardia with left axis deviation was characteristic of fascicular VT, which classically manifests with a right bundle branch block morphology and superior axis due to its origin from the left posterior fascicle.2 Prompt recognition of this electrocardiogram pattern was central to establishing the diagnosis while avoiding unnecessary invasive testing.

Consistent with the typical phenotype of fascicular VT, structural heart disease was excluded by transthoracic echocardiography, and cardiac biomarkers remained normal. Although the arrhythmia was initially hemodynamically tolerated—aligning with existing literature describing fascicular VT as relatively stable1—the frequency and duration of episodes raised concern for cumulative hemodynamic stress. Prior reports have demonstrated that sustained or recurrent fascicular VT can lead to tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy if inadequately controlled, reinforcing the need for timely intervention even in otherwise stable patients.5

Therapeutic decision-making in this case closely mirrored pregnancy-adapted guideline recommendations. While verapamil is considered the treatment of choice for fascicular VT in nonpregnant patients because of its targeted effect on Purkinje-mediated reentrant circuits,2 beta-blockers are more commonly favored during pregnancy due to their more established maternal and fetal safety profiles.6 In this patient, metoprolol provided partial symptom relief but required careful dose escalation to achieve durable rhythm control, illustrating the need for individualized titration and close monitoring.

Although catheter ablation is regarded as definitive therapy for fascicular VT and is associated with high success rates outside of pregnancy, its use during gestation is limited by concerns regarding fetal radiation exposure.7 In pregnant patients with recurrent symptomatic or hemodynamically unstable VT who do not respond to or cannot receive medical therapy, catheter ablation is a reasonable option when performed by experienced operators using strategies to minimize or eliminate radiation exposure to the lowest reasonably achievable levels. In this case, successful medical management and sustained hemodynamic stability supported a conservative approach, allowing deferral of invasive therapy until the postpartum period.

Finally, this case highlights the critical role of multidisciplinary care in managing rare arrhythmias during pregnancy. Close collaboration among obstetrics, cardiology, and electrophysiology facilitated balanced decision-making that prioritized both maternal cardiac stability and fetal safety—an approach emphasized in current position statements and reflected in the favorable clinical outcome observed here.6,7

Conclusions

Fascicular VT during pregnancy, though rare, should be considered in pregnant patients presenting with persistent or symptomatic wide-complex tachycardia with a right bundle morphology. Early recognition, careful electrocardiographic interpretation, and pregnancy-adapted management strategies are essential to prevent complications such as tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy and to support favorable maternal and fetal outcomes.
