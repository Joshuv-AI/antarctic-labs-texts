Thrombospondin-1 proteomimetic polymers exhibit anti-angiogenic activity in a neovascular age-related macular degeneration mouse model
Authors: Wonmin Choi, Ashley K. Nensel, Steven Droho, Mara A. Fattah, Soumitra Mokashi-Punekar, David I. Swygart, Spencer T. Burton, Greg W. Schwartz, Jeremy A. Lavine, Nathan C. Gianneschi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) is the leading cause of blindness in the developed world. Current therapy includes monthly intraocular injections of anti-VEGF antibodies, which are ineffective in up to one third of patients. Thrombospondin-1 (TSP1) inhibits angiogenesis via CD36 binding, and its down-regulated expression is negatively associated with the onset of nAMD. Here, we describe TSP1 mimetic protein-like polymers (TSP1 PLPs). TSP1 PLPs bind CD36 with high affinity, resist degradation, show prolonged intraocular half-lives (13.1 hours), have no toxicity at relevant concentrations in vivo (40 μM), and are more efficacious in ex vivo choroidal sprouting assays compared to the peptide sequence and Eylea (aflibercept), the current standard of care anti-VEGF treatment. Furthermore, PLPs exhibit superior in vivo efficacy in a mouse model for nAMD compared to control PLPs consisting of scrambled peptide sequences, using fluorescein angiography and immunofluorescence. Since TSP-1 inhibits angiogenesis by VEGF-dependent and independent mechanisms, TSP1 PLPs are a potential therapeutic for patients with anti-VEGF treatment–resistant nAMD.

INTRODUCTION

Neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) affects more than 200 million people worldwide and is the leading cause of irreversible vision loss in older populations (1, 2). Approximately 90% of vision loss is attributed to choroidal neovascularization (CNV), the key pathology of nAMD (3). CNV is characterized by the abnormal growth of blood vessels either underneath the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) or in the subretinal space on top of the RPE (4). Current therapies, such as Eylea (aflibercept), are intraocular antivascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) injections, which improve vision by an average of five letters (5). However, up to one-third of patients do not improve or lose vision despite monthly anti-VEGF injections (6). With the global prevalence of nAMD projected to double in the next decade, there is an unmet clinical need for novel treatment targets that are effective in patients with anti-VEGF treatment–resistant nAMD (7, 8) and new drug delivery methods to ease treatment burden, including refillable implants, topical delivery methods, and the use of gene therapy for drug delivery (9).

Thrombospondin-1 (Thbs1 or TSP1) is an extracellular matrix protein that interacts with cell surface receptors, like CD36, and modulates several in vivo processes, including angiogenesis (10). In immunohistochemical studies of postmortem mouse eyes, TSP1 has been shown to be expressed in the RPE, Bruch’s membrane, and choriocapillaris. TSP1 levels are reduced with age and further decrease in late stages of AMD, suggesting that TSP1 is protective in nAMD pathogenesis (10–13). In agreement, Thbs1−/− mice demonstrate an eightfold increase compared to the wild-type mouse in the laser-induced CNV mouse model of nAMD (11). Furthermore, ABT898, a short, eight–amino acid synthetic TSP1 peptide mimic that binds the CD36 receptor, reverses the effects of Thbs1 deficiency and inhibits CNV by 50% in wild-type mice (11, 12). These data suggest that CD36 agonism with ABT898 is a potential alternative nAMD therapy. However, as we confirm here, the key challenges that have so far prevented the clinical use of therapeutic peptides like ABT898 in the eye are their fast proteolysis under physiologically relevant conditions, poor binding affinities, and rapid ocular clearance (14). We seek to address these critical deficiencies by introducing a distinct paradigm enabling the use of peptides as a therapeutic modality by using a polymer-based approach.

As opposed to the traditional peptidomimetic routes for modifying peptides (e.g., cyclization, stapling, and the use of nonnatural amino acids) (15–19), we propose a synthetic polymer–based proteomimetic strategy. This approach packages peptides together as high-density brush polymers termed protein-like polymers (PLPs) (20–25). PLPs are generated by graft-through ring-opening metathesis polymerization (ROMP) of peptide-modified monomers yielding polymers with tuned molecular mass and low dispersities (21, 23). In general, PLPs, by virtue of their globular structures, are resistant to proteolysis yet maintain their bioactivity (21, 23). This provides stability in the biological milieu and resistance to direct treatment with proteases (21, 23). The inherent features of proteolytic stability and multivalency combine to make PLPs suitable for the design of materials capable of engaging extracellular target proteins like CD36. Here, we sought to design and test ABT898-based TSP1 mimetic PLPs (Fig. 1) as anti-angiogenic therapeutics for nAMD in ex vivo and in vivo mouse models of the disease.

RESULTS

TSP1 PLPs and control PLPs are synthesized and characterized

Peptides were synthesized via solid-phase peptide synthesis (26, 27). To generate polymerizable peptides, a norbornene unit was added as a nonnatural amino acid at the N termini (Fig. 1, A to C, and table S1). Specifically, the ABT898 sequence [GVi(allo)SQIRP, fig. S1] was conjugated to either N-(hexanoic acid)-exo-5-norbornene-2,3-dicarboximide to yield amide-linked peptide monomers (M1, fig. S2), or N-(1-pentanol)-exo-5-norbornene-2,3-dicarboximide at the succinylated N terminus of peptide sequences to give an ester-linked peptide monomer (M3) (Fig. 1D and fig. S4). In addition, we synthesized peptide monomers with two additional arginine residues to increase aqueous solubility to achieve required in vivo concentrations (M2 and M4, 80 μM) and a separate peptide monomer consisting of a nonfunctional, aqueous soluble peptide (GSGSGS) (M5) for the formation of water-soluble block copolymers (Fig. 1D and figs. S3, S5, and S6). In addition, control peptide monomers were synthesized with scrambled sequences of ABT898 peptide analogous to M1 [M1scr = Pi(allo)QRGSVI] and a scrambled sequence analogous to M2 [M2scr = Pi(allo)QRGSVIRR] (figs. S7 and S8). Peptide monomers were subjected to through ROMP using the Grubbs’ initiator (IMesH2)(C5H5N)2(Cl)2Ru = CHPh (28, 29) to produce the respective TSP1-mimetic PLPs (P1, P2, P3, P4, and Co-P1) and control PLPs (P2scr, P5, and Co-P1scr) (Table 1). Co-P1 was designed to include amide-linked M1 for optimal stability and M5 to increase solubility without the potential cytotoxic effects of positively charged arginine residues (Table 1) (30, 31). The conversion of monomers was tracked via nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR) in the formation of homopolymers (P1, P2, P3, P4, and P5, fig. S9). Following the complete conversion of the monomer, polymerization reactions were terminated with ethyl vinyl ether (EVE). These polymerizations resulted in materials of tunable molecular mass with narrow dispersities (Table 1, tables S2 and S3, and fig. S10). The molecular mass (Mn), degree of polymerization (DP), and dispersity (Ð) of each polymer was determined by either organic or aqueous phase size exclusion chromatography with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS) (Fig. 1B, Table 1, and figs. S10 to S12). Molecular mass could further be confirmed by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) (Fig. 1C). In addition, a set of rhodamine-labeled polymers were synthesized via the introduction of rhodamine-labeled norbornene monomers to monitor serum stability (Nor-Rho, fig. S13) (32, 33) and biotin-terminated polymers for a study quantifying peptide concentration in vitreous fluid over time (fig. S14) (34).

TSP1 PLPs inhibit choroidal sprouting ex vivo

We first performed an ex vivo choroidal sprouting assay to assess the anti-angiogenic properties of TSP1 PLPs (Fig. 2). Eyes from healthy mice were enucleated and dissected to remove the cornea, iris, lens, retina, and vitreous. The peripheral RPE-choroid-sclera complex was dissected into 1- to 2-mm pieces and plated in Matrigel. On day 2 following plating, TSP1 homopolymers were added (P1-P4, 115 μM with respect to peptide). Choroidal sprouting developed spontaneously and was quantitated by reverse-phase brightfield microscopy over day 4 to day 7 (Fig. 2A). The amide-linked polymers (P1: 81% on day 7, P < 0.0001 vs control; P2: 91% on day 7, P < 0.0001 versus control) were highly effective in reducing choroidal sprouting, while the ester-linked polymers (P3: 24% on day 7, P < 0.0001 versus control; P4: 23% on day 7, P < 0.0001 versus control) inhibited choroidal sprouting with smaller effect sizes. These results indicated that the noncleavable, amide-linked PLPs (P1 and P2) had enhanced bioactivity compared to the hydrolysable, ester-linked PLPs (P3 and P4). We hypothesize this is due to instability in the Matrigel milieu. Further, ABT898 peptide was not effective in this assay, likely due to rapid degradation.

In addition, we assessed the anti-angiogenic activity of the block copolymer at two different ratios of the solubilizing sequence (M5) to the ABT898 monomers (M1) (Fig. 2B). Both polymers had a theoretical Mn of 30 kDa (fig. S11). One block copolymer (Co-P12:1) had a ratio of 2:1 of the solubilizing block M5 to therapeutic block M1, while the other (Co-P11:1) had a ratio of 1:1 (table S2). At 5 and 10 μM, Co-P11:1 inhibited choroidal sprouting by 57% [P < 0.01 versus phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) control] and 86% (P < 0.0001 versus PBS) respectively, while Co-P12:1 was less effective at 18% (P = ns versus PBS) and 73% (P < 0.0001 versus PBS) on day 4 (Fig. 2B). We attribute this enhanced inhibition to the higher ratio of the therapeutic peptide in Co-P11:1. On the basis of the increased solubility and enhanced performance of Co-P11:1, we established that a theoretical ratio of 1:1 of M5 to M1 was the lead anti-angiogenic block copolymer (Co-P1, Table 1).

Additional experiments were carried out with the block copolymers (Co-P1 and Co-P1scr) to acquire a dose-response curve on day 4 (Fig. 2C). We found that Co-P1 decreased choroidal sprouting by 18% at 5 μM (P = ns), 60% at 10 μM (P < 0.001), and 90% at 20 μM (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2C), while Co-P1scr at 20 μM was ineffective (Fig. 2, C and E). In addition, we compared 20 μM Co-P1 to 115 μM ABT898 peptide and 4.35 μM Eylea (0.5 mg/ml, its clinical dose). We found that ABT898 peptide had no effect while Eylea only moderately reduced choroidal sprouting by 35% (P < 0.05, Fig. 2, D and E).

TSP1 PLPs are nontoxic in ARPE-19 cells in culture

The cytotoxicity of TSP1 PLPs was assessed in vitro in adult retinal pigmented epithelial cells (ARPE-19) using the LIVE/DEAD cell imaging kit (488/570) and a CellTiter Blue (CTB) assay. Homopolymers (P1 and P2), block copolymer (Co-P1), and ABT898 peptide were incubated with the human RPE cell line ARPE-19 at 10 μM for 48 hours followed by addition of LIVE/DEAD cell imaging agent (fig. S16A). Fluorescence was imaged by confocal laser scanning microscopy. We observed green fluorescence from cells treated with ABT898 peptide, P1, and Co-P1, indicating the cells had intact, healthy cellular membranes. However, cells treated with P2 showed red fluorescence, indicating compromised cellular membranes. These results suggest that P2 is cytotoxic at 10 μM, while P1-treated and Co-P1–treated cells are viable at doses of 10 μM.

The cytotoxicity of TSP1 PLPs was also analyzed via a fluorometric viability assay using CTB, which estimates the number of living cells by measuring their metabolic activity through the reduction of resazurin to resorufin. Fluorescence was tracked hourly over 4 hours. Similar to the LIVE/DEAD cell imaging, P2 and P2scr exhibited cellular toxicity at concentrations greater than 3 μM (fig. S16B), while P1 and ABT898 peptide did not show cellular toxicity up to 10 μM (fig. S16B). Next, we tested the toxicity of Co-P1 at high concentrations. Co-P1 showed cellular toxicity at 50 μM levels (fig. S16C). Since P1 exhibited limiting aqueous solubility above 40 μM, Co-P1 was chosen to move forward to in vivo analyses because of its efficacy in the choroidal sprouting assay (Fig. 2, B to D), high aqueous solubility, and limited cytotoxicity.

TSP1 PLPs are nontoxic to the retina in vivo and RGC function ex vivo

We next tested the toxicity of TSP1 PLP in vivo in healthy mice (Fig. 3). We injected 80 μM Co-P1, Co-P1scr, or PBS control into the mouse vitreous cavity (final concentration 40 μM after dilution in the vitreous). Eyes were harvested 7 days later for toxicity assessment by hematoxylin and eosin staining. We found that retinal morphology was intact (Fig. 3, A to C) with no change in the thickness of the outer nuclear layer (ONL) where the photoreceptor nuclei reside (Fig. 3D) nor in the thickness of the total retina (Fig. 3E).

In vivo cytotoxicity assessment of Co-P1 and Co-P1scr in the retina.

Mouse eyes were injected with either 1 μl of (A) PBS, (B) 80 μM Co-P1scr, or (C) 80 μM Co-P1. Thickness was measured equidistant from the optic nerve on sections cut through the nerve and pupil. No change in outer nuclear layer (ONL; D) or total retinal (E) thickness was detected (n = 7 to 10 eyes per group). Mean and SEM for each treatment are shown in black.

As the output of the retina, light responses of ganglion cells (RGCs) are the physiologically relevant measure of the function of complete retinal circuits (35–38). To determine whether the presence of Co-P1 alters RGC function, we compared light responses in ex vivo cell-attached retinal recording in eyes treated with the TSP1 PLP compared to eyes from the same animals injected with a saline negative control (Fig. 4). Co-P1 (80 μM, final concentration 40 μM after dilution in the vitreous) showed no clear effect on either the light responses or morphology of ON alpha RGCs compared to the untreated control (Fig. 4, A to D), which are one of the two most light-sensitive RGC types (39, 40). These results suggest that TSP1 PLP Co-P1 does not lead to loss of the highly sensitive photoreceptors or downstream components of the retinal circuit.

TSP1 PLPs resist proteolytic degradation

We performed an enzyme resistance assay to test TSP1 PLPs for protease resistance compared to the peptide alone. These studies were conducted such that peptide and polymers were examined at the same concentrations with respect to the peptide sequence itself. ABT898 peptide (200 μM) was treated with thermolysin enzyme (0.1 μM), and the degradation profile was monitored using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Thermolysin was chosen as a representative enzyme as it cleaves the ABT898 peptide selectively at the glutamine residue, allowing for analysis of enzymatic activity by HPLC. We observed rapid degradation of the peptide, yielding defined fragment peaks over time (fig. S17B).

We then assessed the proteolytic stability of Co-P1 using SDS-PAGE. To visualize the stability of Co-P1 using SDS-PAGE, we synthesized the rhodamine-labeled block copolymer (RhoCo-P1) by incorporation of Nor-Rho during polymerization (fig. S13). Thermolysin (0.1 μM) was added to a solution of RhoCo-P1 (200 μM with respect to peptide) and incubated for 24 hours. Controls wells were loaded with heated and unheated RhoCo-P1. The band of RhoCo-P1 treated with thermolysin remained unchanged, confirming that TSP1 PLPs were resistant to proteolysis (fig. S18). In addition, TSP1 PLPs were stable in serum for extended time periods. RhoCo-P1 (65 μM) was incubated in 25% mouse serum at 37°C for up to 1 day showing no change in molecular mass by SDS-PAGE (fig. S19).

TSP1 PLP binding to CD36 is multivalent and superior to ABT898 peptide

TSP1 PLP binding was compared to ABT898 peptide via biolayer interferometry (BLI). First, a sensor labeled with an anti-histidine tag was equilibrated with solvent before incubation with the histidine tag–labeled CD36 receptor. TSP PLPs were then added to the solution cell at various concentrations. The kon rates of the TSP1 PLPs were approximately twice that of the ABT898 peptide. We attribute this slight increase in on rate to increased steric hindrance of the PLP species. However, Co-P1 and P1 dissociation constants were recorded in the low nanomolar range at 1.2 × 10−8 M−1, an order of magnitude lower than the ABT898 peptide at 2.4 × 10−7 M−1, driven in large part by the notably decreased off-rate kinetics (Table 2 and fig. S20). Together, these data indicate that TSP1 PLP has a strong affinity, driven by slower off rates compared to the peptide alone, which is attributed to multivalent statistical rebinding to the CD36 receptor.

TSP1 PLP selectively inhibits CD36 function in cell culture

Fatty acid uptake plays an essential role in metabolic regulation and is facilitated in large part by the transmembrane protein, CD36. A role of the TSP1 protein is the prevention of nitric oxide signaling through the inhibition of myristic acid uptake by CD36 (41). To confirm binding of CD36 by Co-P1, a cell-based in vitro fatty acid inhibition assay was performed. We used a fatty acid–labeled dye, 4,4-difluoro-5,7-dimethyl-4-bora-3a,4a-diaza-sindacene-3-dodecanoic acid (BODIPY FL-C12), to monitor cellular uptake via flow cytometry. MCF-7 and HT1080 cells were used due to their high expression of CD36 as previously reported (42) and confirmed via flow cytometry. Co-P1, Co-P1scr, P5, or ABT898 peptide were co-incubated with 1 μM BODIPY FL-C12 and the mean fluorescence was measured (figs. S21 and S22). A low intracellular fluorescence signal indicated inhibition of fatty acid uptake. Co-P1 showed substantial inhibition of fatty acid uptake in both MCF-7 cells [half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) = 870.5 nM] and HT1080 (IC50 = 506 nM), while the scrambled control (Co-P1scr), nonfunctional homopolymer (P5), and peptide alone showed minimal to no inhibition (figs. S21 and S22). These results confirm cellular CD36 engagement by Co-P1.

TSP1 PLP demonstrates an extended half-life after intravitreal injection

We injected a biotin-tagged version of both ABT898 peptide and biotin-tagged PLP (Biotin-P2, figs. S14 and S15) intravitreally into the eyes of mice in vivo and then measured concentrations following vitreous extraction at various time points. We note that Co-P1 could not be tested as a biotin-labeled version of the polymer was insoluble in buffered solutions at the high concentrations needed to detect biotin (400 μM). While ABT898 peptide was removed rapidly (half-life = 0.77 hours, fig. S23A), Biotin-P2 was retained 17 times as long (half-life = 13.1 hours, fig. S23, B and C). The 13.1-hour half-life of the TSP1 PLP in the mouse eye translates to an expected 1.6 to 5.5 days in rabbit eyes, which is comparable to all three U.S. Food and Drug Administration–approved anti-VEGF agents (43).

TSP1 PLP suppresses laser-induced CNV in vivo

We next tested the ability of Co-P1 to inhibit the laser-induced CNV model in vivo, which is the current gold standard animal model for nAMD (Fig. 5) (44). In this model, a laser is applied to rupture Bruch’s membrane. Through this rupture, angiogenesis develops from the choroid into the subretinal space. This was the original model used to identify anti-VEGF as a target for nAMD and was the principal preclinical model used for the development of current anti-VEGF therapeutics (44).

Intravitreal injection of 80 μM Co-P1 reduced laser induced CNV.

(A to C) Co-P1 reduced fluorescein leakage area by 2.0-fold (n = 21 eyes, 78 lesions for Co-P1scr and n = 19 eyes, 68 lesions for Co-P1). (D and E) A representative immunofluorescence image of a single CNV lesion is shown for each group. (F) CNV area decreased by 2.8-fold on immunofluorescence staining of choroidal wholemounts (n = 20 eyes, 74 lesions for Co-P1scr and n = 18 eyes, 64 lesions for Co-P1, P < 0.05, Welch’s t test).

Since 40 μM is nontoxic in vivo (Fig. 3C) and Co-P1 is highly effective in the inhibition of choroidal sprouting at 20 μM ex vivo (Fig. 2D), we injected 80 μM Co-P1 or Co-P1scr into the mouse vitreous (diluted 1:1 for final concentration of 40 μM in the eye). Intravitreal injections were performed on day 2 after laser injury to treat laser-induced CNV. We then performed both fluorescein angiography (Fig. 5, A to C) and immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5, D to F) of choroidal wholemounts to measure CNV leakage on day 7. There were no differences in the number of CNV lesions per group. We found that Co-P1 reduced fluorescein leakage by twofold (P < 0.05) and decreased laser-induced CNV area by 2.8-fold (P < 0.05) on immunofluorescence imaging (Fig. 5, C and F). These data demonstrate that Co-P1 PLP effectively inhibits laser-induced CNV in vivo.

DISCUSSION

Current treatments for nAMD require monthly intraocular injections of anti-VEGF antibodies, which are both expensive and show limited improvement in visual activity for one-third of patients (6). While peptides are proposed as promising alternative therapeutics due to their binding specificity and biological delivery, they suffer from severely limited half-lives, low in vivo activity due to degradation, and low binding affinities (45, 46). Few peptidomimetic drugs have been investigated to target ocular diseases, like nAMD (47). A small cyclic retro-inverted VEGF-targeting peptide, Vasotide, was administered via eyedrops and showed substantial reduction in angiogenesis (19). However, these studies lacked pharmacokinetic profiles (19, 47). As opposed to traditional peptidomimetic strategies such as cyclization, we address these efficacy and stability challenges by using an anti-angiogenic peptide-polymer conjugate with notably enhanced affinity for its receptor and vastly improved efficacy in models of nAMD.

In this work, we have detailed several chemical compositions of TSP1 PLPs to identify and optimize design criteria imbuing the materials with aqueous solubility and efficacy while limiting cytotoxicity (Fig. 1 and Table 1). Initially, an amide-linked ABT898 homopolymer (P1) was tested and found to be effective at inhibiting choroidal sprouting ex vivo (Fig. 2A). However, P1 exhibited low aqueous solubility, limiting the accessible concentration range for in vivo studies. To enhance aqueous solubility, P2 was designed with two additional arginine residues at the C terminus of the peptide (Fig. 1 and Table 1). While the initial ex vivo experiments showed promising inhibition of choroidal sprouting (Fig. 2A), the dose-limiting cytotoxicity of P2 (fig. S16), likely due to overall cationic charge of the polymer, motivated us to pursue alternative polymer architectures. In parallel, we investigated modifying the linkage between peptide and norbornene backbone through substitution of the amide-linked peptide with an ester bond (P3 and P4, Fig. 1 and Table 1). We found that these ester-linked TSP1 PLPs were less effective at inhibiting sprouting, an outcome we attribute to the limited stability of the ester linkage in the Matrigel milieu used in the choroidal sprouting assay (Fig. 2A). This implies that the intact PLP is required for enhanced activity, consistent with enhanced overall stability and higher affinity for CD36 compared to the eight–amino acid ABT898 peptide sequence alone as measured by BLI (vide infra, Table 2).

To enhance solubility in aqueous conditions and to avoid the use of arginine or other modifications to the base ABT898 sequence, the active monomer (M1) was copolymerized at an optimal ratio of 1:1 with a nonfunctional solubilizing peptide monomer (M5) to form a block copolymer (Co-P1, Fig. 1 and Table 1). This PLP design was well tolerated in vitro in cell culture, as analyzed in viability assays against retinal cells (fig. S16C) and exhibited no retinal toxicity in vivo or changes in RCGs ex vivo (Figs. 3 and 4). Moreover, Co-P1 was superior in its anti-angiogenic properties in ex vivo choroidal sprouting assays compared to a control and scrambled sequence version of Co-P1 (Co-P1scr), to the ABT898 peptide, and Eylea, the current standard of care anti-VEGF treatment (aflibercept, Fig. 2D). In addition, as with homopolymers of ABT898, the Co-P1 PLP showed exceptional resistance to proteolytic degradation. In these analyses, Co-P1 was treated with either thermolysin- or protease-rich serum. Both sets of data indicate that Co-P1 resists degradation and is stable for up to 1 day in enzyme-rich environments versus minutes in the case of ordinary, linear peptides (fig. S19) (44).

Previously, the pharmacokinetics of all three clinically available anti-VEGF treatments have been measured in both rabbits and humans, establishing a clear baseline for the expected half-life of an effective treatment: ranibizumab, 2.8 days in rabbit, 5.8 to 9 days in human; bevacizumab, 4 to 6 days in rabbit, 6 to 8 days in human; and Eylea, 2 to 2.5 days in rabbit, 7 to 11 days in human (48). Half-lives of compounds with a wide range of molecular mass were shown to be retained 3 to 10 times longer in rabbit eyes than in mouse eyes (43). Because of the limited solubility of Co-P1 as mentioned above, the Biotin-P2 TSP1 PLP was used to measure retention. The half-life of the TSP1 PLP in the mouse eye of 13.1 hours corresponds to an expected 1.6 to 5.5 days in rabbit eyes. When one adjusts for known species differences in clearance from the eye, these results are comparable to those obtained with the standard-of-care anti-VEGF drugs. Further studies will assess where these noncleavable PLPs would accumulate in the body and cause possible side effects following intravitreal injection.

To assess engagement of Co-P1 to the natural receptor for TSP1, CD36, we used BLI to determine binding kinetics and investigated CD36 engagement at cell surfaces in cell culture. These studies showed that Co-P1 exhibited a strong binding affinity to CD36 with slow off-rates to which we implicate the entropic effect of having a high density of peptide sidechains arrayed on the polymer backbone. In addition, the homopolymer (P1) and the block copolymer (Co-P1) show similar binding kinetics to CD36, each demonstrating cooperative binding on the order of 20 times that of the ABT898 peptide alone (Table 2 and fig. S20). In vitro cellular experiments also confirmed that Co-P1 engaged CD36 in 2D culture by inhibiting fatty acid uptake to a greater degree compared to ABT898 peptide and scrambled PLP controls (figs. S21 and S22).

Success in converging on TSP1 PLP designs that demonstrate limited cytotoxicity in vivo at relevant concentrations, enhanced anti-angiogenic activity ex vivo, enhanced proteolytic stability and CD36 engagement led to an examination of activity in an in vivo nAMD model. Here, mice were subjected to laser-induced CNV followed by treatment with Co-P1 or Co-P1scr. Co-P1 at 40 mM robustly inhibited the laser-induced CNV model by 2.8-fold (64%) via immunofluorescence imaging (Fig. 5). Encouragingly, as reported in the literature, aflibercept at 10 μg (87 μM) decreased laser-induced CNV by 32%. These data confirm that Co-P1 is at least comparable to aflibercept in terms of efficacy for CNV inhibition in the established laser-induced CNV model (49, 50).

TSP1 inhibits angiogenesis through CD36 in a variety of different VEGF-dependent and VEGF-independent pathways. CD36 agonism inhibits the phosphorylation of VEGF receptor 2 (VEGFR2), the key pro-angiogenic VEGF receptor in endothelial cells (51). In addition, CD36 agonisms interfere with VEGF-dependent nitric oxide–driven angiogenesis (41). Independent of VEGF, CD36 agonism leads to endothelial cell apoptosis through several mechanisms. First, CD36 agonism releases cytochrome C, causing activation of caspase 9 and caspase 3, leading to apoptosis (52). Furthermore, CD36 activates Jun amino-terminal kinase (JNK), leading to increased expression of death receptors 4 and 5, making endothelial cells susceptible to tumor necrosis factor–related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL)–mediated apoptosis (52). Last, CD36 agonism directly inhibits endothelial cell migration independent of VEGF (53). Thus, CD36 agonism via ABT898 has the potential to inhibit angiogenesis via multiple mechanisms and has the potential to be efficacious in patients with anti-VEGF treatment–resistant nAMD. Additional studies will be performed to determine whether TSP1 PLP at low doses will have additive and/or synergistic affects when combined with anti-VEGF agents.

In summary, we have established PLPs as effective in engaging an extracellular target in the eye of live mice in a model for age-related macular degeneration. On the basis of these encouraging results and ongoing optimization, the PLP platform technology could potentially provide an alternative for the millions of patients who are resistant to current anti-VEGF treatments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

Preparation of peptide monomers via solid-phase peptide synthesis

All peptide monomers were synthesized on Rink resin using standard Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis procedures on an AAPPTec Focus XC automated synthesizer or Liberty Blue Automated Microwave Synthesizer. Peptide monomers connected via amide bond (M1, M2, and M5) were prepared via amide coupling to N-(hexanoic acid)-cis-5-norbornene-exo-dicarboximide (3.0 equiv) in the presence of N,N,N′,N′-Tetramethyl-O-(1H-benzotriazol-1-yl)uronium hexafluorophosphate [(HBTU) 2.9 equiv] and N, N-Diisopropylethylamine [(DIPEA) 6.0 equiv]. The peptide monomers with ester bond (M3 and M4) were prepared by adding succinic anhydride (1.5 equiv) and triethylamine (1.1 equiv) at the N terminus of peptide sequences on the resin followed by ester coupling to N-(1-pentanol)-exo-5-norbornene-2,3-dicarboximide (3.0 equiv) at the succinylated N terminus of each peptide sequence in the presence of HBTU (2.9 equiv) and DIPEA (6.0 equiv). The peptide monomers were cleaved from resin by treatment with TFA/H2O/TIPS (95:2.5:2.5, v/v) for 2 hours. The crude products were filtered to remove the resin and precipitated in cold diethyl ether. The pellet was dried under reduced pressure and purified via preparative HPLC.

General procedure for NMR-scale polymerization reaction kinetics

Polymerizations were performed under dry dinitrogen atmosphere. Norbornene-conjugated peptide monomers (25 mg/ml, 15.0 equiv) were dissolved in DMF-d7 with 1 M LiCl and an initial NMR experiment was conducted. Grubbs’ catalyst (IMesH2)(C5H5N)2(Cl)2 Ru = CHPh (1.0 equiv, 20 mg/ml in DMF) was added and the reaction was tracked via NMR until full conversion of the monomer. The reaction was quenched with EVE (excess) and stirred for 30 min. The polymer solution was precipitated into diethyl ether and centrifuged. The pellet was dried under reduced pressure and dialyzed in MilliQ water. The product was obtained by lyophilization as a white solid.

General procedure for ROMP of homopolymer PLPs

Polymerizations were performed under dry dinitrogen atmosphere. Norbornene-conjugated peptide monomers (25 mg/ml, 15.0 equiv) were dissolved in degassed DMF with 1 M LiCl. Grubbs’ catalyst (IMesH2)(C5H5N)2(Cl)2 Ru = CHPh (1.0 equiv, 20 mg/ml in DMF) was added and the reaction was stirred for 2 to 7 hours. After full conversion of the monomer, the reaction was quenched with EVE (excess) and stirred for 30 min. The polymer solution was precipitated into diethyl ether and centrifuged. The pellet was dried under reduced pressure and dialyzed in MilliQ water. The product was obtained by lyophilization as a white solid.

General procedure for ROMP of block copolymer PLPs

Polymerizations were performed under dry dinitrogen atmosphere. Norbornene-conjugated peptide monomers (25 mg/ml, 15.0 equiv) were dissolved in degassed DMF with 1 M LiCl. Grubbs’ catalyst (IMesH2)(C5H5N)2(Cl)2Ru = CHPh (1.0 equiv, 20 mg/ml in DMF) was added and the reaction was stirred for 2 to 7 hours. An aliquot of the reaction was taken for SEC-MALS analysis. The second monomer (25 mg/ml, 15.0 equiv) was added, and the reaction stirred for 2 to 7 hours. After full conversion of both monomers, the reaction was quenched with EVE (excess) and stirred for 30 min. The polymer solution was precipitated into diethyl ether and centrifuged. The pellet was dried under reduced pressure and dialyzed in MilliQ water. The product was obtained by lyophilization as a white solid.

Ex vivo choroidal sprouting assay

The choroidal sprouting assay was performed as previously described (54). In brief, C57BL/6J 10- to 12-week-old male mice were euthanized, eyes were enucleated, and dissected into 1- to 2-mm peripheral RPE-choroid-scleral complex pieces. Choroidal tissue was plated into 48-well plates with 50 μl of growth factor reduced Matrigel on ice. Matrigel was solidified by incubating at 37°C without medium for 10 min. EGM2-MV medium was added to each well and incubated (37°C with 5% CO2). Medium (and any treatment) was changed every 48 hours. Wells were treated with ABT898 peptide, TSP1 PLP, or aflibercept/Eylea on day 2. Phase-contrast photos of individual explants were taken on days 4 to 7. Images were analyzed as previously described (54).

LIVE/DEAD cell imaging

Cells (ARPE-19, density of 30,000 cell per well) were plated in four-well plates and incubated (37°C with 5% CO2) for 24 hours. Cells were treated with either ABT898 peptide or TSP1 PLP (10 μM) and incubated for 48 hours. Cells were washed three times with PBS buffer then treated with media (250 μl) and a mixture of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and Texas Red dye (250 μl) from the LIVE/DEAD cell imaging kit. Cells were incubated for 15 min in darkness at room temperature and subsequently imaged with confocal laser scanning microscopy.

CTB cell viability assay

Cells (ARPE-19, density of 5000 cell per well) were plated in 96-well plates and incubated (37°C with 5% CO2) for 24 hours. Cells were treated with ABT898 peptide (1 to 150 μM) or PLP (0.1 to 50 μM) in water. After 48 hours, CTB (20 μL) was added. Cells were incubated for 2 to 4 hours at 37°C. Fluorescence was measured at 590 nm with excitation at 560 nm using a Perkin Elmer EnSpire plate reader. The average background fluorescence of CTB in media without cells was subtracted from the average fluorescence readings of the experimental wells. Viability was calculated as the average background-subtracted signal in a well compared to that of a negative control well (treatment with vehicle). Three replicates were performed for each independent sample. A 10% dimethyl sulfoxide was used as a positive control and untreated cells in complete medium as a negative control. Viability is reported as the percent of cells exhibiting metabolic activity.

In vivo cytotoxicity assessment

Eyes underwent intravitreal injection as previously described (55). In brief, mice were anesthetized, and eyes were dilated. Under a dissecting microscope, a 30-gauge needle was used to create an incision posterior to the limbus. One microliter of PBS, Co-P1, or Co-P1scr was injected intravitreally using a Hamilton Syringe (7648-01, Hamilton, Reno, Nevada) with a 32-gauge needle (0.4 in. PT3, Hamilton). Eyes were covered in antibiotic ointment and the mice were allowed to recover from anesthesia on a heating pad. Mice were euthanized 14 days later. Eyes were fixed in modified Davidson’s buffer [11% (v/v) Glacial acetic acid, 2.2% (v/v) neutral buffered formalin, 32% (v/v) ethanol (all Millipore-Sigma, St. Louis, MO) at least 1 ml per eye] at room temperature overnight. Samples were moved to 10% neutral-buffered formalin at room temperature for 2 hours, then stored in 70% ethanol until embedding in paraffin. Tissue blocks were cut at 4 μm thickness and stained with hematoxylin and eosin.

Assessment of retinal ganglion cell function

Retinal ganglion cells were recorded and filled with dye for imaging in an ex vivo preparation of the mouse retina as described in (38).

Enzyme degradation assay via analytical HPLC

Thermolysin (5 μl, 10 μM) was added to TSP1 PLP or peptide in Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS; 500 μl, 200 μM with respect to peptide). Solutions were stirred at 37°C and aliquots were taken at predetermined time points. The degradation of the PLP or peptide was tracked via analytical HPLC. Degradation experiments were performed in triplicate.

Enzyme degradation assay via SDS-PAGE

Thermolysin (5 μl, 10 μM) was added to RhoCo-P1 in DPBS (500 μl, 200 μM with respect to peptide). Solutions were stirred at 37°C for 14 hours. Laemli running buffer was added to each time point and heated for 5 min at 95°C. The solution was loaded into the polyacrylamide gel and run with two nontreated controls (heated and unheated PLP).

Serum stability assay

Serum solution (25% mouse serum in water, 7.5 μl) was added to RhoCo-P1 (0.14 μM) in MilliQ water (22.5 μl) at designated time points over 1 day (24, 8, 4, 2, 1 hour, and 10 min) at 37°C. As a control, serum-free, heated PLP was also prepared. Laemli running buffer was added to each time point and heated for 5 min at 95°C. Samples for each time point were loaded into the polyacrylamide gel and imaged.

Binding assessment via BLI

The amine reactive sensor (ARG2) was first hydrated and installed onto the BLI instrument. The sensors were treated with fresh activation solution 40 mM of 1-1-ethyl-3-[3-dimethylaminopropyl] carbodiimide hydrochloride and 20 mM of N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide solution. CD36 proteins (50 μg/ml in sodium acetate buffer at pH 6.5) were immobilized on the sensor and passivated with 1 M ethanolamine. ABT898 peptide or the TSP1 PLP (5 μM, 1 μM, 700 nM, 500 nM, 250 nM, 100 nM, 75 nM, 50 nM, or 25 nM) was dissolved in the tris buffer (pH 8.4). The dissociation constant (KD), on-rate (kon), and off-rate (koff) of each analyte were then measured.

Fatty acid inhibition assay

Expression of CD36 in MCF-7 and HT-1080 cells was confirmed via flow cytometry. Cells were seeded at 105 cell per well in a 24-well plate and incubated for 24 hours. Plating media was removed and cells were treated with TSP1 PLP or peptide at the respective concentrations in reduced serum media (500 μl, Gibco Opti-MEM, catalog no. 31985070) and left to sit over 24 hours to allow for basal conditions. Cells were then treated with BODIPY FL-C12 (500 μl, final concentration 1 μM) in 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (w/v) in DPBS. Cells were then incubated for 5 min at 37°C. Wells were aspirated and the cells were washed 3× with ice cold 1% BSA (w/v) DPBS (−) solution to stop uptake and scavenge residual fatty acid dye. Cells were treated with 0.25% trypsin solution (200 μl) for 5 min. DMEM, as a stopping buffer (500 μl), was added to each well, and the contents were transferred to Eppendorf tubes. The tubes were centrifuged at 0.2 × 1000 gravity for 5 min, and the media and trypsin were aspirated off. The cell pellet was resuspended in ice-cold 10% fetal bovine serum (v/v) DPBS (−) (200 μl) and transferred to a 96-well plate for fluorescence-activated cell sorting. For analysis, fluorescence in the FITC channel was normalized using this equation: [fluorescence (observed)]/[fluorescence (untreated)]. Where fluorescence (untreated) is the fluorescent intensity of cells incubated with BODIPY fatty acid at a given concentration, without PLP or peptide. Comparisons were made between cells of the same well plate, to avoid day-to-day and batch-to-batch variation in the assay.

Retention in vitreous fluid quantification

Mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane and then directly held in place with a sterotax (<2 to 5 min, Kopf, model 1900). One microliter of compound (peptide or Biotin-P2 PLP) was injected into the vitreous cavity with a 33-gauge needle. At the indicated time points, mice were again anaesthetized with isoflurane before being euthanized by cervical dislocation. Eyes of euthanized mice were immediately punctured with a 33-gauge needle for vitreous extraction. Extracted volumes ranged from 1.0 to 2.8 μl. Biotin was quantified with the QuantTag Kit based on manufacturer protocol (Vector Labs, BDK-2000).

In vivo laser-induced CNV

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The laser-induced CNV model was performed as previously described (54). In brief, male 10- to 12-week-old C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized, whiskers were trimmed, eyes were dilated, and pain prophylaxis provided. Mice were placed on a slit lamp stage, a cover slip was coupled to the cornea, and eyes were treated with four laser burns (75 μm, 110 mW, 100 ms) to each eye using an IRIDEX laser. Intravitreal injections were performed on day 2 as described above. On day 7, fluorescein angiography followed by immunofluorescence imaging of choroidal flatmounts was performed as previously described (54) using ICAM-2 to visualize CNV vessels. Images were masked by SD and CNV area was measured by JAL in ImageJ. Since intravitreal injections are variable, CNV lesions in each eye were averaged and each eye was treated as an experimental unit.
