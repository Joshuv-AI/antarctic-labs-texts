Pre-treatment with royal jelly, environmental enrichment, and their combination role in stress induced neurobehavioral deficits in male rats
Authors: Hakimeh Saadati, Payman Raise-Abdullahi, Alireza Moradi Khankandi, Reza Panahizadeh, Meysam Saboor, Samin Nahavandi, Masoomeh Dadkhah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStress plays a pivotal role in anxiety-like disorders and cognitive decline. The present study investigated the potential effects of prior royal jelly supplementation and environmental enrichment against stress-induced anxiety-like behaviors, serum corticosterone, hippocampal brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels, and cognitive performance deficits in stressed rats. Male Wistar rats were randomly devised into 8 experimental groups. Rats were subjected to royal jelly (200 mg/kg) via oral gavage, standard environmental enrichment, or combination all for 14 days and control rats received saline in the same period of time. Stress induction was done on the 7th day of treatments by exposure to the restrainer under 10°C. Then open field, elevated plus maze, and inhibitory passive avoidance memory tests were used to explore emotional-cognitive behaviour. Also, corticosterone levels, and hippocampal BDNF expression were measured. Stress resulted in an increase in the serum corticosterone levels, anxiety-like behaviors, and decreased hippocampal BDNF expression which reversed by environmental enrichment and royal jelly treatments. Remarkably, the combined treatment exerts a more pronounced effect on the aforementioned outcomes. Our study strongly proposes a novel emerging therapeutic approach through nutritional interventions, emphasizing the potential of these treatments to mitigate stress-induced anxiety and memory impairments prior to stress exposure.Keywords: Pre-treatment, Stress, Environmental Enrichment, Royal Jelly, Hippocampus, BDNF

Introduction

Stress-related disorders are among the most prevalent neuropsychiatric conditions, affecting millions of individuals and public health worldwide (Bermudo-Soriano et al. 2012). Stress is a natural response to preserve physiological and psychological hemostasis in the face of external threats (Gold and Chrousos, 2002). While this response is essential for maintaining bodily balance, it is closely linked to several mood disorders in the brain, including depression, anxiety, and trauma (Ruiz et al., 2007, Heim et al., 2008, Saveanu and Nemeroff, 2012). Research on animals has demonstrated a significant correlation between restraint conditions, cold stress, and anxiety-like behaviors, highlighting the pivotal role of stressful events in developing anxiety-related disorders (Guedri et al. 2017). Additionally, various paradigms involving restraint and cold stress have been found to induce anxiety-like symptoms in animals repeatedly exposed to physical stressors (Yoon et al. 2014). Now the question arises whether prior protevtive treatments can decrease the risk of cognitive deficits and anxiety like symptoms after experience of stressed conditions.

In recent years, additional stress mechanisms that elicit anxiety-like behaviors have emerged, particularly involving the role of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a crucial factor in cognitive functions (Zhang et al. 2020). Consequently, chronic stress has been associated with reduced BDNF levels (Liu et al. 2010), leading to anxiety and cognitive impairments (Moradi-Kor et al., 2020a, Moradi-Kor et al., 2020b). BDNF plays a significant role in neuronal plasticity, neuroprotection, and stress response, with prominent expression in brain regions like hippocampal formation (Shafia et al., 2017, Sperry et al., 2017, Zhang et al., 2020). Abnormal BDNF levels have been observed in both human and experimental animal models with anxiety disorders, including those induced by chronic stress procedures. These anxiety disorders are often accompanied by changes in hippocampal function attributed to decreased BDNF levels (Ghalandari-Shamami et al. 2019). Moreover, rats with reduced BDNF expression have displayed depressive-like behaviors, potentially stemming from compromised neurogenesis in the hippocampus (Taliaz et al. 2010).

Royal jelly (RJ), derived from young worker bees (Apis mellifera), exhibits neuroprotective properties (Khodaverdiloo et al. 2021) along with antioxidant (Nagai and Inoue, 2004), hypocholesterolaemic (Kamakura et al. 2006), and anxiolytic effects (Ghorbanpour et al. 2021). RJ, as a natural product, contains proteins, sugars, vitamins, free amino acids, and a unique component called 10-hydroxy-2-decanoic acid (HDA-10), which has been found to protect against anxiety symptoms, suggesting its anxiolytic properties (Sefirin et al. 2019). Given the detrimental impact of prolonged stress on brain function, several natural products have been explored to ameliorate stress-induced cognitive impairments. In the stress-inducible depression-like mouse model, intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of RJ demonstrated anxiolytic effects (Ito et al. 2012).

In addition to RJ, environmental enrichment (EE) has long been recognized for its anxiolytic effects, highlighting its probable beneficial impacts on anxiety disorders (Zanca et al. 2015). EE offers various avenues for physical activity, social engagement, and cognitive stimulation, all of which contribute to alleviating anxiety symptoms. By fostering neuroplasticity, the brain's capacity to adapt and restructure, environmental enrichment facilitates enhanced emotional regulation and resilience, ultimately reducing anxiety symptoms (Dandi et al. 2018). Furthermore, EE plays a role in mitigating stress levels, a common catalyst for anxiety disorders (Dandi et al. 2018). In captive animals, EE, such as larger cages, sensory stimulating objects, and opportunities for social interaction and physical exercise, has decreased emotional reactivity and improved aberrant behaviors (Ball et al. 2019). Stress disrupts hippocampal glucocortocid receptor (GR) expression and EE reverses stress induced alternations in GR expression (Orock et al. 2024). EE has been linked to a broad range of physiological, affective, and cognitive advantages in rodents, encompassing reduced anxiety and mitigated stress responses. This indicates that engaging and stimulating environments can potentially alleviate stress levels, often acting as triggers for anxiety disorders (Dandi et al. 2018).

Due to the involvement of chronic stress in progression of several neurodegenerative disorders, long lasting exposure with stress increased risk factor of such disorders resulting in cognitive disturbance. Drawing from prior research highlighting the therapeutic advantages of RJ and EE in stress-induced anxiety-like behaviors, the aims of this study were: first to investigated the stress induced higher corticosterone levels; second to assesse possible potential of RJ supplementation, EE intervention, and their combination prior to stress on hippocampal BDNF levels and cognitive impairments induced by stress.

Material and methods

Animals

56 adult male Wistar rats weighing 200–220 g were purchased from the Research Center of Physiology, Urmia University of Medical Sciences, SUMS, Urmia, Iran. Animals were housed at a relatively standard temperature of 23 ± 1ºC under a 12-hour light-dark cycle and allowed free access to food and water except during the brief test periods. All experiments were completed in agreement with the ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethical Review Board of Ardabil University of Medical Sciences, Ardabil, Iran, NO: IR.ARUMS.REC.1399.247.

Experimental groups and design

On day 1, the animals were randomly assigned into two main groups: non-stress (not exposed to any stress) and stress groups (subjected to daily stress sessions). Then, each main group was divided into four subgroups (n = 7 per group): control group,kept undisturbed in their home cage under standard housing conditions (polypropylene cages,) for 14 days and administered with saline), EE group (housed undisturbed in an EE condition for 14 consecutive days and received saline daily), RJ group (kept undisturbed in their home cage for 14 days and received daily RJ), and EE+RJ group (housed undisturbed in an EE condition for 14 consecutive days and received daily RJ). Saline or RJ supplemented rats were housed in large cages (50 ×26 ×25 cm height) 5 animals in each cage. The housing condition for EE groups were difrferent in which transparent polycarbonate cages that were larger than the standard ones (100 cm ×100 cm ×10 cm) with several stimuli such as pipes, ramps, ladders, steel chain, and running wheels were used (Moradi-Kor et al., 2020a, Moradi-Kor et al., 2020b). Rats in stress groups exposed to stress after seven days of daily treatment, from day 8 to day 14. All rats had the same bedding conditions with free access to food and water exept stress exposure period. On day 15, animals were returned to their home cages and behavioral tests were conducted on days 15–18. On day 19, the animals were decapitated to assess serum corticosterone and hippocampal BDNF levels (Fig. 1).

Experimental Timeline: Rats were treated with EE, RJ, and EE + RJ for 14 consecutive days. Stress was induced on days 8–14. Behavioral tests were conducted on days 15–18. On day 19, the animals were decapitated to assess serum corticosterone and hippocampal BDNF levels.

Stress induction

Each rat was subjected to a 120-minute restraint in the morning (10:00–12:00 a.m.) in a Plexiglas restrainer measuring 21 cm × 6 cm. During this period, the rats experienced a tight position and had limited movement, being able to partially move forward and back but not turn their heads. Then animals kept in cold chamber for 2 h in late afternoon (4:00–6:00 p.m.). The rats were exposed to stress in the morning between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM, following which they were transferred to individually cleaned cages in a cold chamber set at 10°C for 120 min daily for seven days (Teixeira et al. 2017). The animals subjected to stress were administered either saline or RJ supplementation 1 h before the stress induction.

Royal jelly

Fresh RJ was purchased from Poonik Daru Company, Iran. This sample was stored at −4˚C until use. Fresh RJ samples (contains 10-HDA and proteins concentrations were approximate>10% and < 5% respectively) (25) were diluted with distilled water at 200 mg/kg concentration, chosen according to the dose administrated in our previous study (Ghorbanpour et al. 2021). Animals received RJ with an oral gavage tube at a volume of 0.5 ml daily for 14 days (10:00–11:00 AM). The control groups were given orally distilled water alone.

Environmental enrichment

The animals were exposed to a large cage (100 cm×100 cm×40 cm) enriched with the methal running wheel, an elevated platform equipped with a complex of plastic tunnels, steel chains, and round plastic shapes in different sizes, replaced every 5 days. Alternation in the location of objects was made daily to produce novelty and stimulate the rats' exploring behaviors (Sadegzadeh, Sakhaie et al. 2020).

Behavioral tests

Open field test (OFT)

The behavioral test was conducted immediately after the final oral gavage was administered to all groups. The open field apparatus utilized in the experiment consisted of a dark wooden area measuring 60 × 60 × 50 cm, divided into 25 equal squares by 1 cm lines (10 ×10 cm each). Each rat was placed in the central zone, and after a 30-second adaptation phase, the animal's activity was observed and recorded for 5 min. A camera positioned 2 m above the testing apparatus captured the behavior analysis through video recording. Various parameters were measured, including the time spent in the central zone, the number of line crossings between the two arenas (number of entries into inner zone), rearing (where the rat stood on its hind limbs in both the center and margin zones), grooming behavior, and fecal output (Sakhaie, Sadegzadeh et al. 2020).

Elevated plus-maze (EPM)

The EPM apparatus consisted of two wooden open arms measuring 50 cm× 10 cm and two closed arms measuring 50 cm× 10 cm× 40 cm, all surrounded by walls that were 50 × 7 cm high. The entire maze was elevated 50 cm above the floor. Each animal was gently placed in the center of the maze, facing a choice between open and closed arms. The arms were connected, allowing the rats to freely explore the maze for 5 min. An infrared camera was used to monitor the rats' performance continuously. To assess anxiety-like behaviors, time spent in open and close arms, also number of entries into open and close arms were accessed. The total number of arms entries was defined as an index of general motor activity.

Passive avoidance task

This instrument consisted of a box (20 × 20 × 30 cm) divided by a guillotine door into two light and a dark compartment. The floor of both compartments was composed of stainless-steel rods (3 mm diameter and separated by 1 cm apart). An isolated stimulator was designed on the floor to deliver electrical footshocks in the dark chamber. At first, two habituation trials were given to all experimental groups. These habituation trials were conducted in a noise-free room, and each rat was located in the illuminated compartment of the device. The rat was facing away from the door of the light compartment, and after 10 s, the dividing guillotine door was raised. The animal naturally prefers the dark compartment. The door was closed once the animals were inside the dark compartment. Entrance latency into the dark compartment (step-through latency, STL) was obtained when the animal had placed all four paws into the dark compartment. 20 s later, the animal was removed and returned to the homecage from the dark compartment. 30 min later, the same interval by the primary acquisition trial was followed. In the training session, the guillotine door was raised down, and an electrical footshock (50-Hz square wave, 1 mA for 3 s) was delivered following the spontaneous pass of the rat in the dark compartment. The rat was returned to its homecage after 20 s. The second trial was conducted after 2 min. A footshock was delivered in each reentrance of the rat to the dark compartment. At the trial's end, each rat stayed in the light compartment for 2 extra minutes. Similarly, the retention test was conducted 24 h after the acquisition session to determine long-term memory. Meanwhile, each rat was placed in the illuminated compartment, and after 9 s, the guillotine door was elevated. The latency of entrance to the dark section or step-through latency (STL), the total time spent in the dark compartment, and the number of entries to the light and dark parts were recorded as indices of memory (Ghorbanpour et al. 2021). No footshock was delivered during the retention test.

Serum corticosterone and hippocampal BDNF measurement

On day 19, following the completion of the behavioral tests, the rats were deeply anesthetized using CO2 and subsequently decapitated. Trunk blood samples were promptly collected between 10 AM and 1 PM. These blood samples were then subjected to centrifugation at 4000 rpm for a duration of 10 min. After centrifugation, the serum samples were carefully extracted and stored at −20ºC until they were ready to be used for corticosterone measurement. The evaluation of serum corticosterone levels and hippocampal BDNF levels was conducted using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with a commercially available ELISA kit (E20160505043, Hangzhou Eastbiopharm Co. Ltd., Zhejiang Province, China) and rat BDNF ELISA kits from Boster Biological Technology Co. with a sensitivity limit of 4 pg/ml following the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

All measurements were performed at room temperature based on Biolegend instructions using microplate reader with 450 nm filter. The concentration of corticosterone and BDNF were extrapolated from the standard curves of corticosterone and BDNF standards included in the assay kits and expressed as pg /ml.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8 software. The data were presented as mean ± SEM for each experimental test. Statistical significance was determined using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's test for comparisons between groups. p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The key findings of the present study are outlined as follows: 1. Stress exposure leads to elevated corticosterone levels, which are mitigated by both EE and RJ treatments; 2. Stress-induced anxiety-like behaviors are alleviated by administering EE and RJ; 3. Stress-induced impairment of PA memory is ameliorated by EE and RJ interventions; 4. Stress-induced reduction in hippocampal BDNF levels is enhanced by applying EE and RJ; 5. Notably, the combined EE + RJ treatment exerts a more pronounced effect on the aforementioned outcomes.

Although corticosterone facilitates the acquisition and consolidation of memory (Meamar et al. 2023), we found that the higher levels of corticosterone induced by chronic stress play a role in memory impairement. The higher corticosterone levels found in two type of stress experienced rats, can be interpreted as consequences of response to a stressful condition. This finding suggesting the HPA axis activation, called as extremely sensitivity of physiological systems to stressful conditions (Kenjale et al. 2007), which causes the secretion of adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH) and corticosterone from the adrenal glands to help the body cope with stress (Zafir and Banu, 2009). One possible mechanism is the cold stress role in triggering a reduction in the serotonin levels in brain regions (Hata et al. 1991) and the involvement of serotonergic system in the modulation of anxiety (Millan, 2003). In this regard, decreased hippocampal 5-HT release is thought to be related to stress exposure and such rats that showed high levels of anxiety (Keck et al. 2005).

Our findings on stress induced anxiety-like behaviors are in line with the well-established link between stress and behavioral changes, encompassing cognitive disturbances and anxiety-like behaviors, involves the pivotal role of HPA axis dysfunction in developing stress-related psychiatric disorders (De Bellis and Zisk, 2014).

Vafaei et al. (Vafaei et al. 2023) emphesises on comprehending the neurobiology of stress and its effect on memory that may result in finding new therapeutic options for stress-induced disorders. By subjecting restraint and cold stressed rats to both the EE intervention and RJ treatment, this study aimed to investigate whether the protective effects of EE and RJ could mitigate stress-induced anxiety-like behaviors. Our study revealed that animals subjected to restraint and cold stress exhibited elevated corticosterone levels, indicating HPA axis dysregulation. Conversely, as we reported in the current study, the EE conditions suppressed HPA-enhanced negative feedback, reducing corticosterone levels (Fan et al. 2021). Notably, employing the pre-treatment with RJ approach, we observed that RJ significantly lowered plasma corticosterone levels in stressed rats, reflecting an augmented inhibition of HPA negative feedback. This observation aligns with our earlier findings involving RJ treatment after stress induction in rats (Ghorbanpour et al. 2021).

The profound influence of environmental factors on brain development and anxiety-like behavior is widely acknowledged (Lopes et al. 2017). A growing body of evidence supports the notion that EE can safeguard against the detrimental impacts of stress on brain function, operating at behavioral, morphological, and molecular levels, ultimately leading to the amelioration of anxiety-like behaviors and cognitive deficits (Moradi-Kor et al., 2019, Smail et al., 2020). Nonetheless, the underlying mechanisms responsible for these effects remain incompletely understood.

Furthermore, considering the limited efficacy of pharmacotherapy in addressing psychiatric disorders and recognizing the significant role of natural products in the pathophysiology of such conditions, recent studies have turned their attention toward non-pharmacological alternatives. RJ is among these alternatives and has garnered interest (Sefirin et al. 2019). Rats exposed to restraint and cold stress demonstrated diminished time spent in the open arms during the EPM test, accompanied by an increased preference for closed arms. Moreover, stressed rats exhibited a noteworthy reduction in center time in the OFT, indicative of anxiety-like behaviors.

In the present study, stressed rats exhibited reduced locomotor activity and heightened anxiety-like behaviors compared to their non-stressed counterparts. This was evident through decreased crossings in the OFT, consistent with prior research utilizing the same immobilization stress paradigm (Gomaa et al., 2017, Ghorbanpour et al., 2022). As anticipated, EE significantly elevated the locomotor activity of stressed rats and mitigated their anxiety levels (Fan et al. 2021). This observed reduction in anxiety-like behaviors aligns with findings from other studies (Shafia et al. 2017, Sadegzadeh, Sakhaie et al. 2020, Sakhaie, Sadegzadeh et al., 2020a, Sadegzadeh et al., 2020b) that have reported anxiogenic behaviors induced by single prolonged stress.

RJ is renowned for its high caloric content, which could elucidate the elevated locomotor activity observed in stressed rats receiving RJ and housed in enriched conditions. Also, RJ serves as a potent mediator, inhibiting the biosynthesis of corticosterone (an adrenal gland-produced stress-related hormone) which may reduce depression-like behavior (Iegaki et al. 2020). We noted a discernible enhancement in PA memory across all experimental groups, particularly notable through the combined treatment of EE + RJ. Enriched living conditions have previously been linked to improved memory performance in PA tasks (Sadegzadeh, Sakhaie et al. 2020).

EE tends to have positive results in the animal models for cognitive function, anxiety, and behavior, and sometimes in human studies also. However, reports of neutral or even negative effects are also present. Similarly, RJ was shown to have neuroprotective and cognitive benefits; however, inconsistent results have been documented in the literature depending on models and protocols. Rarely, studies observe that highly

EE's anxiolytic and memory-enhancing attributes are believed to stem from its association with BDNF expression in the brain. Moreover, we observed that stress reduced BDNF protein levels within the rat hippocampus; however, the capacity of RJ to reverse these changes became evident when administered before stress induction.

Furthermore, beyond EE's role as a non-invasive intervention for alleviating restraint and cold stress-induced anxiety symptoms, BDNF-related alterations represent another plausible mechanism substantiated by previous studies (Novkovic et al. 2015, Sadegzadeh, Sakhaie et al. 2020). Similarly, in this study, RJ treatment restored stress-induced hippocampal BDNF levels, signifying its beneficial effects on BDNF-associated memory performance.

Several previous research have indicated the correlations between engagement of the stress response system for a long time with abnormalities in cognitive functions (Danet et al., 2010, Safarzadeh et al., 2024). This relationship is critical for both pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments against cognitive decline. Prevention of stress-induced learning and memory deficits can through prior treatment strategies that can exert sufficient efficacy through molecular mechanisms underlying the higher production of BDNF (Shafia et al., 2017, Shafia et al., 2023).

Enhancing cognitive related outcomes may arise from molecular mechanisms shared by RJ and EE, such as increased BDNF signaling. BDNF signaling is one of the mechanisms leading to these impairments especially in relevant learning and memory circuitry (e.g., hippocampus-medial prefrontal cortex).

The combined effects of RJ and EE may, therefore, be synergistic on cognition and behavior, as shown in models where both interventions were used to prevent deficits caused by stress. However, several studies have identified individual variability, genetic factors, and environmental context as factors influencing the effectiveness of both interventions, leading to disparate findings across studies (Sampedro-Piquero and Begega, 2017, Bachetti et al., 2024).

Since exposure to daily stress can exacerbate the risk factor of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzhimer disease (Safarzadeh et al. 2024), non-pharmacological therapies such as RJ administration and enriched condition are vital for preventing the cognitive impairement induced by stress. Prevention of chronic stress induced cognitive deficit before stress exposure via enriched condition or RJ supplementation seems to be promising treatment to slow down the progression of dementia induced by stress. Regarding the effect of RJ, EE, and combination on cognitive behaviors, we found that simultaneous stress exposure and mentioned treatments restored the stress response system and brain functions, corroborating the elucidation of the neuroprotective effects of RJ and EE in further stress situation. Our work introduces an innovative perspective on nutritional interventions by highlighting the remarkable efficacy of pre-treatment with these interventions in mitigating stress-induced modifications in brain. This perspective holds promise as an alternative therapeutic avenue, providing a fresh dimension to address stress-related concerns.

Conclusions

In conclusion, restraint and cold stress exposure leads to elevated serum corticosterone levels, increased anxiety-like behaviors with no effect on motor activity, impaired PA memory, and reduced hippocampal BDNF levels. EE and RJ treatments reduced corticosterone serum levels, decreased anxiety-like behaviors, ameliorated PA memory, and enhanced hippocampal BDNF levels. A combined treatment with EE and RJ exerts a more pronounced effect on the aforementioned outcomes.

Our research pioneers novel nutritional avenues by underscoring the remarkable pre-stress efficacy of these interventions in mitigating stress-induced alterations and could potentially be suggested as a therapeutic approach to prevent stress-related disorders later.

Although this study provides relevant evidence on how two stressors affects the physiology of the organism, future studies recommended the further evaluating the influence of biochemical mechanisms and different pathways such as serotonergic system alternations following exposure to restratint and cold stress.

Study limitations

In the present research, we did not determine the other biochemical factors. A broader neurobiological markers (e.g., oxidative stress, neuroinflammatory cytokines) or other molecular pathways beyond BDNF shoud be explored. Also, since sex hormones can alter behavioral and physiological events, any changes in the menstrual cycle can influence the results. Therefore it would have been better to design thease experiments in femal rats too. The hippocampus is not the only brain region in stress responces and for more convincing results analyzing the BDNF and other biochemical factors should be considered in future studies.
