Experimental study and geochemical modeling of the effect of asphaltene during smart water flooding in carbonate reservoirs
Authors: Maryam Ghorbani-Bavariani, Arezoo Rezaei, Mohammad Ahmadi, Hamid Vatanparast, Abdolhossein Hemmati-Sarapardeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the role of crude oil properties, especially the asphaltene content of crude oil is crucial for enhancing the efficiency of smart water flooding, as it significantly impacts wettability and recovery factors. This study evaluates the predictive capabilities of a developed geochemical model for predicting wettability alteration during smart water flooding in carbonate reservoirs. Three types of oil samples with varying asphaltene content were employed: crude oil (8.5% asphaltene), deasphalted oil (1.8% asphaltene), and asphaltene-enriched toluene (98% asphaltene). Core flooding experiments were conducted using five types of brines: seawater, twice the sulfate concentration (2SO₄), four times the sulfate concentration (4SO₄), four times the calcium concentration (4Ca), and four times the magnesium concentration (4Mg). The model integrates DLVO (Derjaguin, Landau, Verwey, and Overbeek) theory and calculates zeta potential and disjoining pressure based on the crude oil/brine/rock (COBR) system properties such as ionic composition and oil properties. Recovery factor measurements and contact angle analyses confirmed the model’s accuracy, with seawater injection in asphaltene-rich oil resulting in the most negative electrical double layer (EDL) pressure (− 350 MPa) and a recovery factor of 10%. Conversely, 4Mg brine demonstrated superior performance in promoting water-wet conditions for both crude oil (21.34% recovery factor) and deasphalted oil (18.41% recovery factor). This study underscores the critical role of brine composition and crude oil properties in optimizing smart water flooding strategies and validates the geochemical model as a robust tool for tailoring brine compositions to enhance oil recovery in carbonate reservoirs.Keywords: Zeta potential, Smart water injection, Geochemical model, Asphaltene content, Brine compositionSubject terms: Crude oil, Petrology, Chemical engineering

Introduction

The primary challenge in extracting oil from carbonate reservoirs is that a significant amount of oil remains trapped inside the rock pore spaces even after conventional water flooding. This issue arises because carbonate rocks typically have oil-wet properties, which means that ordinary water flooding is ineffective in changing the rock surface’s wettability to release substantial quantities of oil. As a result, most of the oil is trapped within the pores and throats of the carbonate rock1. Enhanced oil recovery (EOR) techniques are crucial for maximizing hydrocarbon production from mature reservoirs. Therefore, any EOR method that can modify the initial wettability from oil-wet to water-wet conditions will be effective in increasing the oil recovery factor. Among these, smart water flooding (SWF) has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional water flooding, offering the potential for improved oil recovery in carbonate reservoirs. SWF involves injecting specifically compositional tuned brines, often called "smart water," with optimized ionic compositions and salinities to enhance oil displacement and minimize formation damage2. Carbonate reservoirs exhibit unique characteristics, including complex pore structures, heterogeneities, and reactive minerals3. These factors significantly influence fluid flow patterns, wettability, and the effectiveness of EOR techniques. Wettability is a crucial factor that influences both the rate of oil recovery and the residual oil saturation, which are key targets in enhanced oil recovery technology. It determines how quickly and effectively water can spontaneously imbibe into the oil, as well as the efficiency of oil displacement when water is injected, and whether or not additives are used4. Asphaltenes, structurally and chemically the most complex compound in crude oil, highly affect wettability.

Laboratory experiments play a vital role in evaluating the effectiveness of SWF and determining the optimal brine compositions for specific reservoir conditions. Key experimental techniques include contact angle, interfacial tension5, and zeta potential measurements6,7. Core flooding experiments allow researchers to simulate the injection of different brines into reservoir core samples, measuring oil recovery and breakthrough time under controlled conditions, Contact angle measurements quantify the wettability of the reservoir rock, providing insights into the oil–water interactions and the potential for improved oil displacement through SWF, Interfacial tension measurements determine the interfacial tension between oil and brine helps assess the potential for reducing interfacial forces and enhancing oil mobility, and zeta potential measurements evaluate the surface charge of the rock and oil droplets helps understand the interaction between fluids and the reservoir surface, providing insights into wettability alteration8.

Numerous studies have highlighted the critical role of brine composition in influencing oil recovery in carbonate reservoirs. Factors such as salinity, ionic composition, and pH significantly affect the wettability of the reservoir, the interfacial tension between oil and water, and the stability of oil–water emulsions9. For example, divalent cations like Ca2+ and Mg2+ can alter rock surface properties, influencing wettability and oil displacement efficiency10. Additionally, injecting brines with specific ionic compositions can reduce interfacial tension, enhancing oil mobility and improving recovery11,12.

Asphaltenes play a significant role in influencing the properties of crude oil and reservoir behavior. Their presence can alter wettability, affect fluid flow properties, and contribute to the formation of emulsions13. Additionally, asphaltenes can precipitate under specific conditions, which results in formation damage and permeability reduction14. Understanding the influence of asphaltenes on reservoir performance is crucial for optimizing oil recovery strategies. A key reason for the limited use of smart waterflooding in carbonates is that the involved physicochemical mechanisms are not yet fully understood15. Understanding the mineral composition of rocks is crucial for wettability in reservoirs. Identifying minerals of rock and their interactions with the crude oil/brine/rock (COBR) system is essential during enhanced oil recovery (EOR) processes16. Therefore, it is vital to use a model that reflects the interaction between the rock system and reservoir fluids.

Geochemical modeling offers a powerful tool for simulating SWF performance and optimizing brine compositions. These models take into account chemical reactions between crude oil, brine, and reservoir rock. Geochemical modeling has become a crucial tool for simulating and predicting reservoir behavior. Models like PHREEQC17, TOUGHREACT18, and Geochemist’s Workbench19 incorporate complex thermodynamic and kinetic parameters to simulate chemical reactions occurring in the reservoir, including mineral dissolution/precipitation, fluid flow, and rock-fluid interaction. These models integrate chemical reactions and phase behavior of oil and brine mixtures in contact with reservoir rock. However, the accuracy of these models depends on the quality of input data and the comprehensive understanding of specific reservoir conditions.

In this study, the relationship between the asphaltene content in oil samples and the efficiency of oil recovery under various low-salinity water injection scenarios is explored. Findings from previous laboratory experiments20 that specifically investigate how different asphaltene contents of oil affect oil recovery from carbonates for different LSWF scenarios are analyzed. A comprehensive assessment of a geochemical model that has been established and rigorously validated against data found in existing literature21 is involved in our approach. By integrating the experimental results with this geochemical model, its predictive ability is enhanced, and deeper insights into the mechanisms involved in oil recovery during low-salinity water flooding are provided.

Experimental study

The carbonate core samples saturated with 3 types of oil, including asphaltene-rich toluene, deasphalted oil, and crude oil, were flooded with five types of brines, which included seawater (SW), SW with four times the Magnesium ion concentration, SW with four times the Calcium ion concentration, SW with two times the Sulfate ion concentration, and SW with four times the sulfate concentration.

Materials

The study used three oil samples to investigate the impact of asphaltene content on smart water injection performance. The asphaltene-rich toluene was prepared by extracting asphaltene from crude oil using a standard test. The deasphalted oil was obtained by precipitating asphaltenes from crude oil. Both synthetic oil samples were used to study the effect of asphaltene on ion-tuned water performance, although they do not represent typical reservoir oil.

To study the composition of brine, Seawater, and modified seawaters were tested, including seawater synthesized to match the Persian Gulf Sea’s composition and formation water replicating an oil field’s ion composition. The seawater was spiked with magnesium, calcium, and sulfate ions. Deionized water and various salts were used to prepare the brine waters. Adjustment of ion concentrations was necessary to achieve a consistent ionic strength for assessing brine water performance.

The core plugs were obtained from the same oil field as the crude oil sample and formation water data. These core plugs were utilized to create rock sections for wettability measurements. The X-ray crystallography (XRD) and physical properties of the rock plugs are presented in Fig. 1 and Table 4, respectively, showing that the plugs and sections are primarily composed of calcium carbonate.

Oil sample preparation

To prepare the asphaltene-rich toluene, it was required to extract the asphaltene from the crude oil. In this regard, the asphaltene of the crude oil was extracted by using a standard test of the IP-14322. To do so, the normal heptane (Merck, Germany) was mixed with the crude oil with a mixing ratio of 1 gr: 30 ml. The mixture of heptane-crude oil was kept in a dark place for 24 h. After being centrifuged at the speed of 1000 rpm, the mixture was filtered from a Whatman paper (No.42). The filtered precipitant was not pure asphaltene, and it might bear long chain wax and paraffin. To purify the precipitant, the Whatman paper was washed with the normal heptane in a soxhlet setup. Then, the Whatman paper was washed with toluene solution using the soxhlet set up to recover the asphaltenes. Finally, the pure asphaltene powders were obtained by evaporating the toluene. The asphaltene-rich toluene, as one of the synthesized oil samples, was prepared by dissolving 3 gr of the extracted asphaltene in 100 ml of toluene. To prepare the deasphalted oil, the crude oil was mixed with the normal heptane with a mixing ratio of 1:30. After stirring the mixture on a magnetic stirrer (IKA, Germany), it was kept for 24 h, giving time for the entire precipitation of the asphaltenes. By heating the heptane-bearing mixture, the normal heptane was evaporated, and the remained oil was considered as a deasphalted oil sample.

Methods

FTIR spectroscopy and elemental analysis were used to characterize the oil samples. The samples were prepared and analyzed using an FTIR refractometer and numerical data was obtained using Origin software. Additionally, the elemental content of the samples was determined using an elemental analyzer and SLFA60 to contribute to the interpretation of the FTIR spectra.

To investigate the dynamic interfacial tension (IFT) between the oil samples and the synthesized waters, we used the pendant drop technique and Vinci IFT measurement device at 28 °C for 2400 s to ensure stability.

The contact angle measurement was conducted to study the wettability change of oil-wet rock sections after treatment. The rock sections were washed, dried, saturated with brine and crude oil, aged, and then imbibed with various brine solutions. The contact angle of the treated sections was periodically evaluated until it reached a stable value using the sessile drop technique.

The plug saturation process involved washing with toluene and methanol, injection with formation water brine followed by crude oil, and aging in crude oil. Imbibition tests were conducted at 90 °C and 300 bar, using a standard Amott cell device. Brine water was injected at a flow rate of 0.1cc/min and replaced 5 times without moving the plug.

Contact angle measurements

Contact angle measurements were carried out to assess changes in the wettability of oil-wet rock samples after various treatments. The rock samples, obtained from an oil field in southern Iran, underwent a cleaning process in a Soxhlet apparatus using toluene and methanol for 14 days and 2 days, respectively, to remove organic and inorganic contaminants. The cleaned samples were then dried in an oven at 90 °C for two days. Before the aging process, the samples were saturated with formation brine and crude oil. This was done by first saturating the samples with formation brine using a vacuum pump at 300 psi, followed by saturating them with crude oil under the same conditions. The samples were then aged at 90 °C for 45 days to simulate reservoir wettability conditions. After aging, the oil-wet rock samples were exposed to various brine solutions, including formation brine, seawater, and ion-modified seawaters, at 90 °C. The contact angle of the treated samples was then measured to evaluate the wettability changes.

Description of the model

The computational model used in this study was thoroughly validated in our previous work through systematic comparisons with established datasets and standard modeling tools21,23. The validation process involved testing the model against published experimental results from peer-reviewed studies, which demonstrated a strong agreement in predicting these results. We also conducted detailed comparisons with PHREEQC, a widely recognized open-source geochemical modeling software, confirming that our model’s outputs align well with experimental findings. These validation efforts focused on fundamental aspects such as reaction kinetics, mineral saturation states, and aqueous speciation. This ensured the model’s reliability in simulating relevant geochemical processes. In the current study, we further evaluate the model’s performance using new experimental data obtained under controlled laboratory conditions. This step enhances the validation framework by examining the model’s predictive accuracy in more complex and realistic scenarios.

The developed geochemical model encompasses five main reaction groups21: phase equilibrium reactions, aqueous species reactions, mineral species reactions, oil surface complexation reactions, and rock surface complexation reactions. This model estimates the zeta potential of the rock by calculating its surface potential. DLVO (Derjaguin, Landau, Verwey, and Overbeek) theory is employed to elucidate the interactions between particles and surfaces in a liquid containing charged entities. These interactions can be repulsive (pushing particles apart) or attractive (pulling them together), ultimately determining the system’s stability24. The theory describes two layers of charged particles surrounding a central particle, detailing how these layers interact to generate an electric potential. As the distance from the central particle increases, the electric potential diminishes. DLVO theory also examines how varying concentrations of charged particles in the liquid influence these forces25–27. This theory is crucial in calculating the pressure that maintains the stability of the water film between oil and rock surfaces, thereby affecting the system’s wettability. The double layer around a charged particle consists of two parallel layers of ions. The first layer, adhered to the particle surface, is immobile, while the second layer, from the electrolyte medium, is loosely attached28. These layers are separated by the Stern layer and are bounded by the slipping plane, beyond which ions can move freely in the electrolyte. The ionic cloud progresses towards electroneutrality as the distance from the particle increases, and the electrical potential is proportional to the ionic density, peaking at the particle surface. The potential profile, according to the Gouy–Chapman theory, exhibits an exponential decay with increased distance beyond the slipping plane29.

With higher ionic concentrations, the contribution of double-layer forces to the disjoining pressure and contact angle diminishes, causing surface potentials to approach zero. The contributions of structural and London van der Waals forces to surface potentials remain constant, irrespective of salinity and pH30. At elevated brine salinities, short-range forces dominate the energetic balance, though classical DLVO theory cannot adequately describe this balance in certain scenarios without dilution31.

Calculation of disjoining pressure

The DLVO (Derjaguin, Landau, Verwey, and Overbeek) theory is applied to analyze the oil/brine/rock system, focusing on the interactions among these components. In this system, the rock surface is separated from the oil by a water film of finite thickness, which is significantly smaller than the size of the pores that contain the fluids within the porous medium. The brine serves as the electrolyte medium, and its stability is crucial in determining the wettability of the system. A stable water film indicates a positive total disjoining pressure, while instability arises with a negative disjoining pressure. Notably, a shift towards water-wet conditions is consistently associated with an increase in the total disjoining pressure.

The primary DLVO forces acting on charged surfaces include London–van der Waals (VdW) forces, structural forces, and double-layer (EDL) forces, all of which contribute to the total disjoining pressure. The distance (denoted as "h") between the rock and oil surfaces is a critical parameter, influencing the total disjoining pressure (∏t), which comprises double-layer forces (∏EDL), London-van der Waals forces, VdW, (∏VDW), and structural forces (∏S) play key roles. The nature of London-van der Waals forces between dissimilar bodies, separated by a third material, can be either attractive or repulsive. Structural forces, on the other hand, are characterized by short-range repulsion. When double-layer forces are absent, structural forces dominate near the rock surface when water films are thin, whereas London-van der Waals forces dominate when water films are thicker, increasing the distance from the rock surface. Modeling results

Prediction of wettability alteration

In this study, we aim to investigate the predictive capability of the geochemical model concerning various crude oil compositions, specifically considering the asphaltene content of the oil. The effectiveness of the developed geochemical model is evaluated by incorporating experimental data and using it to calculate the zeta potential. The model’s zeta potential predictions have been previously validated by comparing them with experimental data obtained from the literature concerning various compositions of injected brine31.

The inputs for the model include COBR system properties such as ionic composition and concentration of injected brines, the LLNL and PHREEQC thermodynamic databases for reaction equilibrium constants, the mineral composition of the studied core, the physical properties and composition of oil samples, and the pressure and temperature of the experiments. These inputs are shown in Table 2, and Table 5.

The model’s selected outputs are the equilibrium concentration of ions and the surface and zeta potential of oil-brine and rock-brine systems. The calculated zeta potential is then used to estimate the disjoining pressure based on DLVO (Derjaguin, Landau, Verwey, and Overbeek) theory.

The estimated disjoining pressure for each crude oil-brine-rock system will then be used to assess the predictive capability of our developed geochemical system.

Results and discussion

As illustrated in the previous section, for all three types of oil, 4Mg brine shows a greater affinity than 4Ca brine for adhering to the carbonate rock surface. This occurs because calcium reacts vigorously with water to form calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂). In the case of 4Mg brine, the positive charge density is higher than that of 4Ca brine, causing it to adhere firmly to the carbonate rock and promote water-wet conditions. This alignment between experimental results and modeling predictions further validates our findings.

For crude oil, 4Ca brine performs better than 4SO₄ brine, as shown in Fig. 3. This is due to crude oil containing significantly more aromatic hydrocarbons compared to asphaltene-rich oil (approximately 50 times more). Aromatic hydrocarbons, with their large hydrocarbon component, hinder hydrogen bonding, reducing their affinity for interacting with brines. Additionally, 4SO₄ brine has a higher anion content, leading to attractive forces between the positive side of the rock surface and sulfate anions. Conversely, crude oil, with its higher acid number, has more positive sites on its surface, making it less water-wet compared to 4Ca brine. The reactive nature of 4Ca brine with water molecules also has a repelling effect on the positive oil surface sites. These explanations are confirmed by both experimental and modeling results.

In the case of asphaltene-rich oil, the lower percentage of aromatic compounds increases the interaction of oil molecules with brine ions, leading to oil-wet conditions on the rock surface. This finding is confirmed by both experimental and modeling results, as shown in Fig. 2.

For the deasphalted oil sample, experimental results align with the modeling predictions. It was observed that 4SO₄ brine performs better than 4Ca brine, as shown in Fig. 4.

Overall, the research findings indicate that the zeta potential of the studied COBR systems can be accurately estimated using the geochemical model. This accuracy enables proper modeling of disjoining pressure trends using DLVO theory, which aligns well with experimental observations. The validated geochemical model, which considers oil properties, can be effectively utilized to optimize smart water flooding strategies and predict the performance of specific COBR systems, ultimately enhancing oil recovery in carbonate reservoirs.

This study also acknowledges the role of rock-water interaction mechanisms, including mineral dissolution, and surface complexation in wettability alteration. Recent studies have highlighted the geochemical complexity33,34, geochemical aspect and wettability alteration35,36, Thermal disturbance and reservoir compaction due to production caused by fluid injection37–39, and formation damage phenomena36,40 during smart water flooding.

Conclusion

This study has effectively validated a geochemical model for predicting wettability alteration during smart water flooding in carbonate reservoirs, utilizing extensive experimental data. The model integrates DLVO theory and accounts for COBR system properties, such as the ionic composition and concentration of injected brines, alongside oil properties, to calculate zeta potential and disjoining pressure. The model’s predictions align closely with our experimental results, underscoring its reliability and accuracy.

The results indicate that the composition of the injected brine significantly influences wettability. For asphaltene-rich oil, seawater injection results in the most negative EDL pressure, indicating a strong oil-wet condition. Conversely, 4 Mg brine exhibits the best performance in promoting water-wet conditions for both crude and deasphalted oils, as shown by contact angle measurements and recovery factors from our experiments.

Additionally, the study highlights the impact of crude oil composition on wettability. Reducing asphaltene content leads to less negative EDL and disjoining pressures, suggesting that asphaltene is a crucial factor in oil wetness. For deasphalted oil, 4SO₄ brine outperforms 4Ca brine, consistent with our experimental findings.

Overall, this research emphasizes the importance of understanding the interplay between brine composition and crude oil properties in optimizing smart water flooding strategies. The validated geochemical model serves as a robust tool for predicting wettability alteration and tailoring brine compositions to enhance oil recovery in carbonate reservoirs. Future work can build on these findings to further refine smart water flooding techniques and improve oil recovery efficiency.
