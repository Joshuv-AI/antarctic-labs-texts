A Monte Carlo study of proton LET calculations: Comparison between the codes PENHAN, FLUKA, and TOPAS
Authors: D Puerta, M Anguiano, W González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLinear energy transfer (LET) is frequently used to characterize radiation quality in proton therapy and is an important quantity for radiobiological modeling. However, LET calculations can, in principle, vary between Monte Carlo (MC) codes, affecting consistency in treatment planning and relative biological effectiveness (RBE) estimation.PurposeThis study aims to investigate the variability of LET results when using different MC codes.MethodsAn intercomparison of LET results was performed using penhan, topas MC, and CERN fluka, together with recently published experimental data. Track‐averaged (L¯t) and dose‐averaged (L¯d) LET were evaluated under different irradiation configurations, including pencil and broad beams, with and without nuclear reactions.ResultsThe results obtained indicate that discrepancies in LET calculations are primarily linked to the choice of stopping powers and the handling of nuclear interactions. Calculations considering only primary protons exhibited great agreement with recent experimental LET measurements, validating our simulations.Conclusions L¯t showed higher robustness and consistency across codes, suggesting it as potentially a more reliable metric for LET‐based treatment planning and RBE modeling. Our findings emphasize the importance of consistent LET calculations, result reporting, and code benchmarking in proton therapy calculations.Keywords: LET, Monte Carlo simulation, proton therapy

INTRODUCTION

Proton therapy has established itself as a solid alternative to conventional photon and electron radiotherapies for certain patient profiles and tumor types. Its main advantage lies in its precision when depositing dose, which allows greater preservation of healthy tissues, 1 , 2 resulting in an almost complete elimination of the exit dose and a clear reduction of the proximal one. For this reason, proton therapy is indicated for some specific tumor types, such as head and neck or pediatric cancers. 1

However, absorbed dose is not the only factor that determines the damage that proton beams will produce in tissue. For the same absorbed dose, protons are generally more effective than photons. The difference in effectiveness is usually measured by the relative biological effectiveness, or RBE, 3 defined as the ratio of absorbed doses of a reference radiation, usually , and the radiation of interest, to produce the same biological response (or endpoint). Today, most proton therapy treatments use the recommended RBE value of 1.1. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 This is being reevaluated due to the observation of unexpected side effects occurring at the distal edge of the beams. 9 Characterizing RBE is not easy since it depends, among other factors, on the kind of tissue, its oxygenation level, the endpoint considered and the quality of the radiation, that is, its type and energy.

In this context, radiation quality is usually assessed with the concept of linear energy transfer (LET) for the case of charged particles. This practice is also increasingly questioned due to evidence showing that LET may not reliably predict RBE in complex mixed fields, particularly near the distal edge of proton beams. 10 , 11 Yet, it remains the preferred clinical metric for characterizing RBE due to its prevalence and lack of a consensus alternative. 12

One of the problems of modeling RBE in a more realistic way is, not only the obvious difficulty of obtaining biological data for all relevant tissues, but also the variability introduced when such data are derived from different research groups or methodologies. 13 Then, to improve treatment plan optimization without requiring explicit biological input, a possible approach is to optimize the LET distribution, considering that RBE tends to increase with LET in clinical proton beams. 14 , 15 Consequently, accurate LET information becomes essential. 16

Monte Carlo (MC) simulations are widely recognized as the gold standard for particle transport and interactions in matter due to their high physical accuracy. Despite their widespread use in proton LET calculations 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 comparisons between different MC codes have been largely neglected. The aim of this work is to analyze the potential differences that may exist between various MC codes in the determination of this quantity, with the objective of identifying the possible uncertainties due to the physics and proton electronic stopping powers included in each code, as well as to aspects related to the tracking algorithms and the contribution of primary and secondary protons. We also evaluate our calculations with the recent experimental results of Muñoz et al. 20

MATERIAL AND METHODS

LET averages: Definitions

For a charged particle of a given type and energy traversing a material, LET (or restricted linear electronic stopping power, LΔ) is defined as the quotient between the mean energy lost due to electronic interactions while traveling a distance, restricted to energy transfers less than Δ, and the distance traversed by the particle. Consequently, unrestricted LET (L∞ or simply L) is the corresponding electronic stopping power. 21 , 22

A radiotherapy beam passing through a volume is composed of several particles, of different types and energies. In order to characterize it with a single parameter we need an average value of LET. The two most commonly used averages are the track‐averaged LET, L¯t, and the dose‐averaged LET, L¯d. 21 , 23

Monte Carlo codes

PENHAN

One of the MC codes used in our study of LET is penhan (version 2024), 28 , 29 an extension of the electron, positron, and photon transport code penelope, 24 which also simulates the transport of protons, alphas, and neutrons. 30

It is a class II simulation code, meaning that it simulates hard interactions in a detailed way, from the corresponding differential cross sections, while the combined effect of soft interactions is simulated by using approximate multiple scattering theory. Hard (soft) interactions refer to events in which the energy loss and/or the angular deflection of the transported particle are above (below) certain cutoff values.

Energy cutoffs (Wcc, Wcr) and absorption energies (Eabs) are user‐defined, as are the tracking parameters C1 and C2 (ranging from 0 to 0.2), with C1=C2=0 giving a fully detailed simulation. The values of the simulation parameters adopted in the present penhan simulations are listed in Table 1. Finally, in order to obtain the spectra, we used the impact detector IMPDET in 1 μm thick surfaces centered in the corresponding value of z0. IMPDET is a command that tallies the energy spectrum entering a volume.

Regarding the proton stopping powers S(E), penhan computes them from the differential cross section between projectile and the atoms of the material using the plane‐wave Born approximation (PWBA) with the Sternheimer–Liljequist generalized oscillator strength model. 30 The Rao–Sahib and Wittry extrapolation 31 is used at low energies (in water, from approximately 0.27 MeV). S(E) values calculated in this way are included in the penhan material files.

In contrast, sbethe 33 is a Fortran program that provides hadronic S(E) values directly by means of a modified Bethe formula with the shell, Lindlhard–Sørensen and Barkas corrections. At low E, two approaches are adopted. If the material is included in the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) experimental database, sbethe uses the empirical formula in the ICRU Report 32 fitted to the IAEA experimental database for E<0.75 MeV. If there is no experimental data available, sbethe uses an analytical formula. The penhan material files can be modified to use the S(E) from the code sbethe.

We studied how these two different sets of S(E) values affect the results obtained with penhan. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first time that penhan (or its earlier versions) are used for LET calculations.

FLUKA

The CERN code fluka (version 4.3) 34 , 35 was also used in our simulations. It is based on a class I tracking algorithm for charged particles. To prepare the fluka input files, the FLAIR Graphical User Interface (version 3.1‐15.1) 39 was used. We used the DEFAULTS card to activate the PRECISION mode. An energy transport cutoff of 25 keV was set for protons. In order to score the spectra, the USRYIELD card was activated at the specific value of z0. We used the S(E) calculated using the function “GETLET”.

TOPAS

We also performed simulations with topas MC version 3.9, a wrapper of the Geant4.10.07.p03 simulation toolkit. 40 , 41 The topas default physics modules (G4EmStandardPhysics_opt4, g4h‐elastic_HP, g4decay, g4stopping, g4ion‐binarycascade, and g4h‐phy_QGSP_BIC_HP) were used, 42 modifying only the proton production cut, which was set to 6.413·10−5 cm, in order to have a similar value to the absorption energy used in penhan, which is 25 keV (see Table 1). Spectra were obtained using the scorer SurfaceTrackCount in 1 μm thick surfaces centered in the corresponding value of z0. We used the S(E) calculated using the Geant4 method “ComputeElectronicDEDX”.

Intercomparison between codes

This section outlines the approach adopted to intercompare L¯t and L¯d calculations among the different MC codes. The simulation configurations and settings and the statistical methods used to quantify discrepancies are described below.

Configurations considered

In order to compare the results of L¯t and L¯d between the different codes, we considered a monoenergetic proton beam of 160 MeV, in the middle of the proton therapy range, impinging on a water phantom of dimensions 50×50×30.1cm3, with two different geometries:

(a)Pencil beam (PB), using scoring voxels of 50×50×0.2cm3, eliminating in that way any possible difference due to how the codes treat proton scattering.

Pencil beam (PB), using scoring voxels of 50×50×0.2cm3, eliminating in that way any possible difference due to how the codes treat proton scattering.

(b)Broad beam (BB) of 5×5cm2 with scoring voxels of 4×4×0.2cm3, reflecting a more realistic therapy setting.

Broad beam (BB) of 5×5cm2 with scoring voxels of 4×4×0.2cm3, reflecting a more realistic therapy setting.

We also considered simulations activating (On) and deactivating (Off) nuclear reactions.

Φ(E,z0) were calculated considering N=640, E1=12.5 keV, and EN=160.125 MeV. A bin width of ΔE≈0.25 MeV was chosen following Wilkens and Oelfke. 17 However only bins with Ei>0.3 MeV were included in the calculation of Equation (8) since that is the minimum energy for which there are experimental data on S(E) for liquid water (see the IAEA online experimental). 44 Data below that minimum can only be interpreted as semi‐quantitative. 24 Hence, in practice, the sums start at i=3 in Equation (8).

The comparison between codes was done as follows. First, we considered the configuration 160 PB Off and analyzed the influence of changing proton S(E) in the case of the penhan code, as we explained in section 2.3.1. Once this was done, we compared the results of the three codes for the same configuration. After that, we activated nuclear reactions (configuration 160 PB On) to investigate how their implementation affect the LET values. Finally, we considered the scenario most relevant to the clinic practice in which a broad beam was used (160 BB On). It is important to note that electrons were not tracked since only protons were scored at the spectra and at these energies electrons cannot produce them in water. Only points z in the depth distributions with more than 0.1% of the maximum dose were included. 18 The number of histories was 107 in all simulations.

All uncertainties in this section are given with a coverage factor k=3 (or a confidence interval of 99.7%) unless otherwise stated. The term compatible is used to describe two results (either individual data points or complete curves) whose uncertainties overlap.

Statistical methods

The gamma evaluation method or γ‐test 45 gives a measure of the agreement between two datasets g1(r1) and g2(r2), defined at the points r1 and r2. We will define fγ(Δr,Δg) as the percentage of points r1 that has not passed the γ‐test for given spatial, Δr, and function, Δg, thresholds. The bigger fγ(Δr,Δg), the worse the agreement. We are going to consider the local version of the method, in which Δg is expressed as a percentage of the reference value in the specific point, that is, Δg∝g1(r1).

The standard γ‐test does not explicitly incorporate the uncertainties of the functions being compared, it assumes deterministic input values for g1(r1) and g2(r2). However, since we aim to ensure that observed discrepancies between simulation codes are statistically meaningful and not simply due to uncertainty, we propose a slight relaxation of the gamma criterion. Specifically, if the evaluation is performed at the same point (r1=r2), and the corresponding values g1(r1) and g2(r2) are compatible within their uncertainties (i.e., their uncertainty intervals overlap), we will assign γ(r1)=0, indicating agreement at that point.

We will present our results for fγ(1,3) using Δz=1 mm and ΔL=3%, where ΔL denotes both ΔL¯t and ΔL¯d. It is important to note that the choice of Δz=1 mm enforces a point‐by‐point comparison for the γ‐test. On the other hand, a ΔL=3% value was selected, following the criterion for absorbed dose in photon 47 and proton 25 therapy.

Finally, we remark that the γ‐test is typically applied to absorbed dose comparisons. To our knowledge, this is the first application of the method to compare L¯t and L¯d distributions.

Comparison with experiment

Recently, Muñoz et al. 20 have developed an experimental study in which LET measurements for protons and ions have been carried out using fluorescent nuclear track detectors. Our goal in this work was to compare MC simulations done with penhan with these experimental data. According to the reference paper, 20 we implemented a geometry that consists of a nozzle and a 30×30×20cm3 PTW RW3 solid water box with the entrance surface placed at 1.0 m from the nozzle exit. The space between nozzle exits and phantom is filled with air. The nozzle is composed of a 2 mm water slab representing the beam application and monitoring system and a 1.43 mm PMMA slab accounting for the ripple filter.

Similarly to the original article, our source was modeled as a square field of 9×9cm2 impinging perpendicularly to the geometry and made of multiple sources with energies and fluence weights determined to have a 5 cm SOBP (spread‐out Bragg peak), at a depth from 10 to 15 cm in water. 46 In total, 17 beams were considered, with energies from 117.656 to 148.234 MeV. DISCUSSION

First, a comment about our methodology is due. In order to obtain the spectra, we selected an energy bin equal to 0.25 MeV, following a seminal work on the determination of L¯t and L¯d, 17 and serving as a well‐established reference. But, one drawback of our method is the uncertainty that the chosen energy bin introduces in the determination of the spectra. This uncertainty could be important for protons below 1 MeV, where LET varies significantly, potentially affecting the determination of LET averages around the Bragg peak, especially for L¯d. Given this, it is useful to consider how changes in the bin width could influence the spectra. To evaluate this impact, the bin width must be increased or decreased. A larger bin width may be required when the resulting spectra appear noisy or not smooth enough; however, this behavior is not observed in our results (see Figures 7, 8, 9). Reducing the bin width would produce a more detailed spectrum, which is important at the low proton energies where S(E) grows rapidly. In our case, only bins with energy greater than 0.3 MeV were included in our calculations (as explained in Section 2.4), and the steepest increase in S(E) for protons in water occurs below this threshold. Consequently, using a smaller bin width would not affect our results.

Thus, when computing L¯t without nuclear reactions, variations due to physics models and tracking algorithms are negligible, whereas large discrepancies in S(E) at low energies can induce relatively significant differences. In any case, these differences do not impact fγ(1,3) notably.

We now discuss the differences in L¯t between codes when introducing nuclear reactions. In Table 3, we see that fγ(1,3) for 160 PB On between penhan and the other two codes increases to approximately 1%. This corresponds to only one point exceeding differences greater than 3%. As seen in Figure 3, this discrepancy occurs at the very last point of the L¯t distribution.

To analyze this case, we plot the proton spectra obtained with penhan, topas, and fluka at that point in Figure 7. We see that the energies for which the spectrum reaches its maximum values are below 10 MeV and in this region penhan presents the highest values, whereas topas and fluka are lower and closely aligned. This discrepancy arises from the different implementations of neutron transport in these codes. After secondary protons, neutrons are the most generated particle by a 160 MeV proton beam in water (see Paganetti et al. 3 and references therein). Secondary protons beyond the Bragg peak are produced through (n,p)‐reactions, which are implemented in topas and fluka but not in penhan, where the process is considered as purely absorptive. The inclusion of these high‐energy, low‐LET secondary protons lowers the resulting L¯t.

In Table 3, we observe that for the 160 BB On configuration, fγ(1,3) decreases to zero for all combinations. The corresponding graph, Figure 4, shows that the previously incompatible point in the 160 PB On configuration is now compatible. We note that the L¯t values from both fluka and topas have increased. This is due to a reduction in the high‐energy proton spectrum seen earlier. As shown in Figure 8, fluka and topas no longer show the high‐energy proton tail because of the change in beam‐voxel configuration. In this geometry, the high‐energy tail is suppressed because many of these protons, or the neutrons generating them, likely escape the narrower scoring region compared to the PB configuration. Overall, including nuclear reactions does not significantly impact fγ(1,3) for L¯t.

In summary, we conclude that L¯t is robust against changes in both the S(E) and MC code even when nuclear reactions are included, as fγ(1,3) is lower than 7% in all the cases.

Analysis of L¯d

Focusing on the fγ(1,3) values for L¯d in Table 3 for the 160 PB Off configuration, we observe a similar behavior to L¯t. The fγ(1,3) values for penhan–sbethe are higher than those for penhan–topas and penhan–fluka. The conclusion remains the same: when nuclear reactions are neglected, the choice of S(E) is more decisive than the choice of the MC code. We must highlight that the fγ(1,3) values are higher for L¯d than for L¯t because the first gives more importance to larger values of S(E). In Figure 5, we plot the values of L¯d and Eα,βL¯d, for this configuration. It is clear that differences between codes are more important for L¯d, and higher between penhan and any of the other codes.

Once nuclear reactions are introduced, fγ(1,3) increases significantly for all cases. Figure 6 shows L¯d and Eα,βL¯d, indicating substantial data incompatibility, especially at the entrance of the phantom, where nuclear reactions are more significant. Furthermore, we see that fluka systematically produces lower results for L¯d compared to penhan and topas in this region. Figure 9 illustrates the proton spectrum at z=1.99 cm, a point where the differences are notable. It shows that fluka provides a lower contribution of low‐energy proton, which is crucial for L¯d as it corresponds to high values of S(E). A possible reason could be that proton‐induced nuclear reactions on (the most abundant isotope in water for which non‐elastic nuclear reactions can take place) are producing more secondary protons in penhan and topas than in fluka. penhan uses the experimental database from ICRU Report 63, while topas and fluka implement two different theoretical approaches, both based on the intranuclear cascade framework. One explanation may be that fluka models give a lower production of low‐energy protons in nuclear reactions.

When considering a broad beam, fγ(1,3) increases in all cases except for the topas–fluka combination. The behavior of L¯d is similar to that shown in Figure 6, but the differences are more pronounced, as the fγ(1,3) values indicate.

In summary, we conclude that when calculating L¯d, S(E) plays a more decisive role than the choice of the MC code in the absence of nuclear reactions, as in the L¯t case. The inclusion of nuclear reactions introduces significant differences between codes, reaching fγ(1,3) of up to 55.91%. These differences are primarily due to the small‐energy proton spectrum, which plays a key role in L¯d calculations.

Differences in L¯t and L¯d behavior

In Table 3, we see that, when nuclear reactions are introduced, an increase in fγ(1,3) is observed in both L¯t and L¯d (albeit with the comments seen above). For L¯t, this increase is due to the region beyond the Bragg peak (see Figure 3), where high‐energy protons are produced in all codes except penhan. In contrast, for L¯d, discrepancies also arise at the entrance of the phantom, due to the presence of low‐energy protons, which correspond to high LET values. We attribute these differences to the variations in the implementation of nuclear reactions among codes.

The stability and robustness of L¯t are remarkable, with fγ(1,3) values always remaining below 7%. This permits us to conclude that L¯t is inherently less sensitive to variations than L¯d as their definition suggest, serving as a more reliable indicator of radiation quality. If proton RBE models are to be developed, their physical foundation must be as sound as possible to ensure that L¯t calculations based on different methodologies lead to consistent or comparable RBE values.

Concerning L¯d, we observe fγ(1,3) of more than 40% in some case and differences of up to ≈15% between the codes (see Figure 6) when nuclear reactions are activated. In the region where the differences between codes are higher, L¯d values range between 0.5 and 1 MeV/mm, significantly lower than ≈15 MeV/mm observed near the Bragg peak. Non‐constant RBE models were initially proposed to account for the enhanced biological damage beyond the Bragg peak. 48 Since the L¯d differences occur at depths before the Bragg peak, they should not pose an important issue when modeling RBE in the distal region. Furthermore, most widespread proton RBE models produce maximum RBE values (defined when the absorbed dose in a single fraction tends to zero, as occurs beyond the Bragg peak) 49 that are linear in L¯d. 50 , 51 Hence variations of the extent seen in Figure 6 in the Bragg peak region (around 3%) will propagate to RBE and may need to be considered along with other sources of uncertainty in RBE modeling.

Comparison with experiment

In Figure 10, we showed that the penhan result with only primary protons produced the lowest relative error with respect to the experiment for L¯t and L¯d of all simulations (at most data points). The better agreement of this simulation compared to the experiment could be due to limitations in the experimental setup. As discussed in the original work, 20 protons produced through nuclear reactions or hard elastic scattering may not be adequately detected due to their minor contribution to the proton spectrum.

Even if future experiments were able to measure this region, discrepancies between the simulations including all particles and the experimental data (particularly in L¯d) are expected due to the sensitivity of this low‐energy proton component.

CONCLUSIONS

In this work, we have studied the two most commonly used LET averages, and L¯d, using the MC codes penhan, topas MC, and CERN fluka. We have observed a better agreement between the results of L¯t for the different configurations considered, which supports its use as an indicator of radiation quality. Furthermore, it has been found that, even if L¯d presents a higher degree of disagreement between codes, as the fγ(1,3) values indicate, it should not be an issue when modeling RBE, since most of the discrepancies are minor and occur at the beginning of the phantom, where RBE is generally lower.

Moreover, when only primary protons are included in the spectra, the experimental data on L¯t and L¯d in Muñoz et al. 20 are more accurately reproduced, highlighting the robustness and reliability of the simulations developed. Further improvements are needed in the experimental detection of low‐energy protons in order to validate the simulations considering the full proton spectra.
