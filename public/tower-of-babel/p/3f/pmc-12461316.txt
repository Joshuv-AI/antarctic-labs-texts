Solvothermal Synthesis of Functionalized UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs for Selective Removal of Oxytetracycline
Authors: Vu Nhat Uyen Pham, Dat Van Doan, Thuoc Hoang Ho, Van Cuong Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study reports the design and solvothermal synthesis of a novel ternary nanocomposite named UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs. The material was developed for the selective adsorption and magnetic recovery of oxytetracycline (OTC) from water. The composite combines three functional components. CNCs derived from Neptunia oleracea serve as a fibrous scaffold. Fe3O4 nanoparticles provide magnetic separation, while UiO-66 contributes high surface area and active binding sites as a porous MOF. The Fe3O4@CNCs hybrid was prefunctionalized with mercaptoacetic acid to enhance interfacial interactions and promote uniform UiO-66 crystallization, yielding a well-defined core–shell porous architecture. The resulting UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs composite exhibited a high specific surface area (360.68 ± 1.82 m2/g), mesoporous structure (2–14 nm), and excellent magnetic responsiveness. Under optimized conditions (pH 6, 303 K), the composite demonstrated a maximum OTC adsorption capacity of 48.55 mg/g. Kinetic analysis followed a pseudo-second-order model, confirming chemisorption. Langmuir isotherm fitting (R 2 = 0.9932) indicated a monolayer capacity of 110.13 mg/g, with uniform and high-affinity sites. Thermodynamic parameters (ΔG < 0, ΔH = −62.32 kJ/mol, ΔS = −168.06 J/mol·K) confirmed the spontaneous and exothermic nature of the process. The composite also showed high selectivity for OTC compared to other antibiotics. It retained good adsorption performance after several reuse cycles. These findings highlight the potential of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs as a sustainable, efficient, and recyclable adsorbent platform for emerging contaminant removal in wastewater treatment.

Introduction

Antibiotics are widely used in modern animal farming, especially in poultry, livestock, and aquaculture. This practice has raised serious concerns about environmental pollution and the spread of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). − These antibiotics are commonly administered not only for disease prevention and treatment but also as growth promoters, especially in large-scale and high-density farming operations. , However, a substantial portion of these drugs is not fully metabolized and is excreted into the environment in active or partially active forms through animal feces and urine. , If not properly managed, this waste releases antibiotics and ARGs into soil and water. Common pathways include agricultural runoff, manure application, and untreated wastewater discharge. , These residues exert selective pressure on environmental microorganisms, promoting the development and dissemination of ARGs via horizontal and vertical gene transfer. Poultry farms pose higher risks due to crowded housing, poor manure treatment, and high stocking densities. Similar concerns apply to aquaculture, where residues build up in sediments and water. Antibiotic-contaminated wastewater is now a key source of ARGs. It threatens ecosystems, food safety, and human health by reducing antibiotic treatment effectiveness.

Oxytetracycline (OTC), a tetracycline antibiotic, is widely used in animal farming, especially in poultry and aquaculture. OTC is commonly used due to its broad antibacterial spectrum and low cost. However, it is highly persistent in water because of its high solubility, chemical stability, and poor biodegradability.

Traditional treatmentslike chemical oxidation, biodegradation, and filtrationoften fail to remove OTC effectively. This is due to its complex structure, high stability in water, and strong metal-binding ability. , These conventional approaches often suffer from incomplete degradation, low selectivity, secondary pollution, or high energy requirements. In contrast, advanced adsorption technologies have attracted considerable attention owing to their operational simplicity, high efficiency, and potential for regeneration. Functionalized nanocomposites are especially attractive. They offer a large surface area, adjustable surface chemistry, and strong binding to contaminants. − Hybrid materials that combine MOFs, magnetic nanoparticles, and biobased supports have shown excellent potential. These systems selectively and efficiently remove antibiotics from water. − ,,

Cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) are an emerging biobased material for nanocomposite adsorbents. They offer a renewable and versatile support for functional materials. CNCs are highly crystalline, rod-like nanomaterials derived from natural cellulose, featuring superior mechanical properties and a high surface-to-volume ratio. Their dimensions typically range from 5 to 20 nm in width and 100–600 nm in length, depending on the plant source and processing method. CNCs are commonly made by acid hydrolysis, which removes amorphous regions and leaves behind stable nanocrystals in water. Sulfuric acid hydrolysis raises environmental concerns. As a result, greener methodslike enzymatic hydrolysis, oxidation, and ionic liquid-assisted techniqueshave been studied to make CNC production more sustainable. −

Finding renewable, low-lignin plant sources is key to lowering the environmental impact and cost of CNC production. A promising new approach involves utilizing the airspace tissue of Neptunia oleracea, commonly known as water mimosa. This aquatic plant grows in low-oxygen conditions and forms porous, cellulose-rich tissues with much less lignin than wood. Such properties make it particularly suitable for CNCs extraction, offering advantages in terms of processing efficiency and material quality. Water mimosa tissue is often discarded as agricultural waste. Using it adds value and supports circular economy goals. CNCs from water mimosa offer a green, low-cost alternative to traditional sources. They show promise for use in composite materials, water treatment, and antimicrobial systems.

Incorporating inorganic nanomaterials into CNCs is a promising way to improve their adsorption and selectivity. UiO-66 is a zirconium-based MOF made from Zr4+ ions and benzene-1,4-dicarboxylate linkers. It forms a strong and highly crystalline structure. The structure features face-centered cubic geometry with secondary building units formed by Zr6O8 clusters, contributing to its exceptional stability. UiO-66 has a high surface area (1000–1600 m2/g) and large pore volume (∼0.77 cm3/g), which makes it a strong adsorbent for many pollutants. , It is also thermally stable up to 673 K and chemically robust. The pores can be tuned through ligand modification, making it useful in many environmental applications. ,

Thanks to these properties, researchers are developing UiO-66 hybrids. These often include magnetic nanoparticles and biobased supports like CNCs. , Combining UiO-66 with Fe3O4 and CNCs creates a multifunctional system. It improves adsorption, allows magnetic separation, and adds structural stability through CNCs. This integration aligns with current efforts to design sustainable and high-performance nanocomposite adsorbents for the selective removal of emerging contaminants, including antibiotic residues like oxytetracycline, from aqueous environments. ,

Adding magnetic nanoparticles like magnetite (Fe3O4) to UiO-66/CNC composites improves their strength, magnetic recovery, and adsorption efficiency. Magnetite (Fe3O4) is a mixed-valence iron oxide with a 1:2 ratio of Fe2+ to Fe3+. It adopts an inverse spinel structure. In this configuration, Fe3+ ions occupy both octahedral and tetrahedral sites, while Fe2+ ions are localized in octahedral positions within a face-centered cubic oxygen lattice. The uncompensated electron spins of Fe2+ and Fe3+ generate distinct magnetic sublattices, giving rise to the intrinsic ferrimagnetic properties of magnetite.

One of the most promising aspects of Fe3O4 nanoparticles is their ability to be easily manipulated and separated using external magnetic fields. This magnetic property makes Fe3O4 ideal for wastewater treatment composites. It allows fast recovery and repeated use. Furthermore, the nanoscale size and high surface area of Fe3O4 contribute to improved interfacial interactions, which enhance adsorption performance and potentially catalytic activity. In combination with UiO-66 and CNCs, Fe3O4 serves both structural and functional roles. It helps form a magnetic, high-surface-area nanocomposite for efficient and selective OTC removal from water. ,

Magnetic metal–organic frameworks (M-MOFs) have attracted increasing attention over the past decade. This trend builds on the integration of magnetic nanoparticles and MOFs. M-MOFs are valued for their multifunctionality and ease of operation. Key studies by Ke et al. (2012) and Ricco et al. (2013) introduced Fe3O4@MOF core–shell architectures. These structures laid the foundation for further development in catalysis, adsorption, antibacterial applications, energy storage, and environmental cleanup. In the domain of adsorption, incorporating Fe3O4 into MOF matrices has been shown to significantly improve material separation and recyclability without compromising adsorption capacity. M-MOFs have proven highly efficient in removing doxycycline, tetracycline, , and bisphenol A. , This success is due to their magnetic responsiveness, large surface area, and adjustable pore structure. Many of these materials exhibit sustained performance over multiple adsorption–desorption cycles, underscoring their practical viability.

In this study, we designed a novel nanocompositeUiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCsusing solvothermal synthesis. The material was developed to selectively adsorb oxytetracycline (OTC) and allow magnetic recovery from water. Each component was chosen to contribute unique functionality. CNCs act as a biobased scaffold, improving mechanical strength and dispersion. Fe3O4 offers magnetic responsiveness, enabling easy separation and reuse. UiO-66 provides a large surface area, tunable porosity, and active sites for binding OTC molecules. The Fe3O4@CNCs surface was prefunctionalized with mercaptoacetic acid to improve interfacial bonding and promote UiO-66 nucleation, yielding a well-defined porous architecture.

Materials and Methods

Materials

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Zirconium oxychloride octahydrate (ZrOCl2·8H2O, ≥99%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Iron­(II) sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O, ≥99%) and iron­(III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, ≥97%) were also obtained from the same supplier. Terephthalic acid (H2BDC, ≥98%) was purchased from Himedia (India). Mercaptoacetic acid (MAA, 99%) was obtained from Shanghai Zhanyun Chemical (China). N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%) was obtained from VN-CHEMSOL (Vietnam). Oxytetracycline (C22H24N2O9, ≥98%), used as the target antibiotic, was sourced from MedChemExpress (USA). All experiments used double-distilled water with conductivity ≤0.05 μS/cm. The water was prepared in-house using a Milli-Q purification system.

Synthesis Procedures

Synthesis of Cellulose Nanocrystals

CNCs were synthesized from the white aerated tissue of water mimosa following the method reported by PK et al. (2023). Adjustments were made to suit the specific characteristics of this biomass source.

The collected plant tissues were thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove surface impurities and then dried at 333 K until constant weight. The dried plant tissues were ground into a fine powder. Approximately 15 g of the powder was treated with 250 mL of 2% NaOH solution at 353 K for 1 h under continuous stirring (350 rpm). After filtration, the solid residue was rinsed with deionized water until the pH reached neutrality.

Delignification was performed using a mixture of 0.07 M NaOH and 5% H2O2 (v/v = 1:1, total volume 300 mL) at 343 K for 40 min. This process was repeated twice. The sample was subsequently treated with 0.25 M NaOH at 343 K for 1 h, followed by rinsing with deionized water to achieve neutral pH. The resulting purified cellulose was dried at 323 K and stored for further use.

For CNCs preparation, 4 g of the purified cellulose was hydrolyzed in 100 mL of 64% (w/w) sulfuric acid (solid-to-liquid ratio 1:25) at 318 K for 90 min under continuous stirring. The hydrolysis reaction was quenched in an ice bath, followed by repeated centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min to remove excess acid. The obtained suspension was washed several times with deionized water until the pH reached neutrality and then adjusted with 0.5 N NaOH. The neutralized suspension was then subjected to ultrasonication using an ultrasonic bath (Elma Schmidbauer GmbH S80 H Elmasonic, Germany) at 40 kHz for 1 h. Afterward, it was freeze-dried at −303 K for 24 h to obtain CNCs.

Synthesis of Fe3O4@CNCs

Fe3O4@CNCs were synthesized via a coprecipitation method following the procedure reported by Janićijević et al. (2021), with minor modifications.

Briefly, 0.3514 g of ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O, 1.264 mmol) and 0.6831 g of ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, 2.528 mmol) were each dissolved in 25 mL of deionized water. The two solutions were then combined and heated to 333 K under continuous magnetic stirring at 350 rpm. Subsequently, 1.4635 g of CNCs was gradually introduced into the iron salt mixture. The suspension was stirred at 333 K for 1 h to promote homogeneous dispersion and initial interaction between CNCs and iron ions. It was then allowed to rest for another hour to promote adsorption equilibrium.

Afterward, a 25% (v/v) ammonium hydroxide solution was added dropwise under constant stirring until the pH reached approximately 9. This induced the in situ formation of Fe3O4 nanoparticles. The reaction was maintained at 333 K for another 1 h to complete the coprecipitation, followed by a 2 h static aging process to ensure crystallinity and strong anchoring of Fe3O4 onto CNCs.

The resulting Fe3O4@CNCs nanocomposite was collected by magnetic decantation and centrifugation. It was thoroughly washed with deionized water until reaching neutral pH and dried at 323 K for 12 h.

Synthesis of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs

UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs nanocomposite was synthesized via a solvothermal approach, with prior surface functionalization to improve interfacial compatibility between the metal–organic framework and magnetic support.

Initially, 0.5 g of Fe3O4@CNCs was dispersed in 80 mL of 0.58 mM MAA solution. The mixture was shaken at 250 rpm for 24 h to introduce thiol groups that facilitate coordination with Zr-based nodes. The MAA-functionalized material was then washed thoroughly with ethanol to remove excess MAA and dried at 343 K for 12 h.

To prepare solution A, 0.4832 g of zirconium oxychloride octahydrate, 0.2492 g of terephthalic acid, and 1.5 g of benzoic acid were dissolved in 100 mL of DMF. The solution was sonicated for 15 min to ensure complete dissolution. Simultaneously, 0.2416 g of the MAA-functionalized Fe3O4@CNCs was ultrasonically dispersed in 20 mL of DMF for 1 h to obtain solution B. Solution B was then added to solution A and stirred until a uniform dispersion was achieved.

The mixture was transferred into a 150 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 393 K for 24 h. After natural cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was separated by centrifugation. It was washed twice with DMF and three times with ethanol to remove residual precursors and unreacted ligands. The final product was dried at 343 K for 8 h, yielding UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs as a dark brown powder.

Characterization

Multiple analytical techniques were employed to comprehensively characterize the synthesized materials. These analyses focused on their structural, morphological, compositional, and physicochemical properties. Surface morphology was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S-4800, Hitachi, Japan). Internal structure was observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100, JEOL, Japan). Elemental mapping and qualitative analysis were performed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) integrated with SEM. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Tensor 27, Bruker, Germany) was employed to identify surface functional groups and chemical bonding. Crystalline structures were investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD, LabX XRD-6100, Shimadzu, Japan). Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were used to determine the specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was applied using a Gemini VII 5.02 analyzer (Micromeritics, USA). Magnetic properties of the samples were evaluated using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM, EZ9, Microsense, USA).

Adsorption Experiments

The adsorption performance of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs toward OTC was systematically investigated under varying experimental parameters, including adsorbent dosage, initial OTC concentration, solution pH, contact time, and temperature. A stock solution of OTC was prepared at a concentration of 1000 mg/L by dissolving the appropriate amount of OTC in deionized water, and working solutions were obtained by serial dilution.

To evaluate the effects of dosage and initial concentration, a series of batch adsorption experiments were conducted by adding 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mg of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs into 50 mL of OTC solutions with initial concentrations of 10, 20, 40, 60, and 80 mg/L. The pH of the solutions was adjusted from 2 to 7 using 0.1 mol/L HNO3 or 0.1 mol/L NaOH, depending on the experimental design. The suspensions were placed in 100 mL conical flasks and agitated at 250 rpm in a thermostatic shaker at various temperatures (303, 313, 323, and 333 K) for different contact times ranging from 5 to 120 min (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min).

At each selected time point, a strong neodymium magnet was used to separate the magnetic adsorbent from the solution. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 2 min to ensure complete removal of residual particulates. The residual OTC concentration was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 355 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Cary 3500, Agilent Technologies, USA). After each liquid sample was withdrawn at the designated time points, an equal volume of the supernatant obtained after centrifugation was returned to the original reaction solution. This step ensured that the volume of the OTC solution remained constant at 50 mL throughout the entire experiment.

The adsorption capacity at equilibrium (q e, mg/g) was calculated according to eq 1 qe=(C0−Ce)·Vm where C 0 and C e (mg/L) are the initial and equilibrium concentrations of OTC, respectively; V (L) is the volume of the solution, and m (g) is the mass of the adsorbent.

The adsorption capacity at a specific time t (q t , mg/g) was calculated using eq 2 qt=(C0−Ct)·Vm where C t (mg/L) is the concentration of OTC at time t.

The removal efficiency (R, %) was determined by eq 3 removal=R=C0−CtC0·100%

These experiments were conducted in triplicate, and average values were used for analysis to ensure reproducibility and accuracy.

Regeneration and Reusability Test

The reusability of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs was assessed through successive adsorption–desorption cycles under the same experimental conditions as the initial adsorption tests. After reaching equilibrium, the spent adsorbent was magnetically separated. It was then immersed in 50 mL of a desorption solution comprising ethanol and 0.1 M HCl in a volume ratio of 8:2. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 h to facilitate the release of adsorbed oxytetracycline.

Following desorption, the material was recovered using magnetic separation. It was thoroughly washed several times with ethanol to remove residual desorbates, and dried at 313 K for 8 h. The regenerated UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs was then reused in subsequent adsorption experiments under identical conditions.

This adsorption–desorption process was repeated for four cycles. After each cycle, adsorption capacity was recorded to evaluate regeneration efficiency and structural stability.

Evaluation of Adsorption Selectivity

The adsorption selectivity of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs was evaluated using five commonly detected antibiotics: oxytetracycline, ciprofloxacin, enrofloxacin, ofloxacin, and amoxicillin. For each test, 30 mg of the composite was added to a 100 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of a 20 mg/L antibiotic solution, with the pH adjusted to 6. The flasks were agitated at 250 rpm using an orbital shaker.

At 30 min intervals, 5 mL aliquots were withdrawn and transferred into centrifuge tubes. These samples were then centrifuged at 4000 rpm to remove any suspended particles. The absorbance of the supernatant was then measured to determine the residual antibiotic concentration.

Oxytetracycline (OTC) was selected for further adsorption studies due to several key factors. First, preliminary screening experiments indicated that OTC exhibited the highest initial removal efficiency among the five tested antibiotics, suggesting strong affinity with the UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs adsorbent. Second, OTC is a representative member of the tetracycline antibiotic class, which is widely used in both veterinary and human medicine, and is frequently detected in aquatic environments, including wastewater and surface waterhighlighting its environmental relevance. In addition, OTC has favorable analytical properties, particularly a strong and stable UV absorbance peak at 355 nm. This enables accurate and reproducible quantification using UV–Vis spectrophotometry, making it a practical choice for systematic adsorption studies.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs

Optimization of Fe3O4@CNCs and UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs Composition

To evaluate how CNCs content affects the formation and adsorption performance of Fe3O4@CNCs, different masses of CNCs (0.59–1.76 g) were used. Initially, CNCs were presoaked in the iron salt solution for 1 h to facilitate uniform adsorption of Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions onto their surfaces. After coprecipitation, the mixture was left undisturbed for 2 h. This resting period promoted stable Fe3O4 formation and improved their anchoring onto CNCs.

Subsequently, different mass ratios of Fe3O4@CNCs to UiO-66 were investigated to optimize the composite structure and adsorption efficiency. Adsorption experiments were carried out using 20 mg of each composite in 50 mL of OTC solution (20 mg/L, pH 6), with a contact time of 120 min. The results, summarized in Table S1, indicated that the optimal Fe3O4/CNCs mass ratio was 1:5, while the most effective UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs ratio was 12:5.

The selected Fe3O4/CNCs ratio (1:5) is consistent with previous reports for similar nanocomposites. This ratio offers a balance: Fe3O4 ensures magnetic recovery and surface reactivity, while CNCs promote dispersion and prevent aggregation. Excessive CNCs beyond this ratio were found to shield Fe3O4 active sites, reduce the available surface area, and weaken the magnetic response.

For the UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs composite, a UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs mass ratio of 12:5 resulted in uniform MOF coating, structural integrity, and maximum exposure of UiO-66’s porous frameworks for OTC adsorption. Furthermore, the retained Fe3O4 content ensured the composite’s magnetic separability, enabling efficient recovery after adsorption.

Characterization

The crystallinity of CNCs was assessed under two hydrolysis durations. CNCs­(H2SO4_64_90) were prepared by hydrolysis with 64% H2SO4 at 318 K for 90 min. The XRD pattern showed a distinct peak at 2θ ≈ 22°, corresponding to the (200) plane of cellulose I. This indicates a well-defined nanocrystalline structure (Figure S1a). In contrast, CNCs­(H2SO4_64_120), prepared using a longer hydrolysis time (120 min), exhibited lower peak intensity and broader diffraction patterns. These changes suggest partial loss of crystallinity and increased aggregation due to overhydrolysis.

XRD patterns of Fe3O4@CNCs synthesized with different Fe3O4/CNCs ratios (1:2 to 1:6) confirmed the presence of the cubic spinel structure of Fe3O4 (JCPDS No. 19-0629). This is evidenced by the diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 30.2°, 35.5°, 43.1°, 53.4°, 57.0°, and 62.8° (Figure S1b). Among the tested ratios, 1:5 showed the best crystallinity and dispersion. The XRD peaks were sharper and well-defined, with minimal aggregation. This indicates that CNCs effectively stabilized Fe3O4 nanoparticles.

For UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs, XRD analysis of composites with varying UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs mass ratios (Figure S1c) revealed that the 12:5 composition best preserved the characteristic diffraction peaks of both UiO-66 (notably at 7.3°, 8.4°, and 25.6°) and Fe3O4 (30.2°, 35.5°, 62.8°). A lower UiO-66 content led to incomplete MOF coating. In contrast, excessive UiO-66 reduced Fe3O4 peak intensity, which may compromise magnetic separation. Therefore, the 12:5 ratio offered an optimal balance between crystallinity, structural integrity, adsorption efficiency, and magnetic responsiveness.

The XRD pattern of the final composite synthesized at this optimal ratio is shown in Figure a. All characteristic peaks of CNCs, Fe3O4, and UiO-66 were clearly observed. No extra peaks appeared, confirming the successful integration of components. This also indicates the absence of impurities or unwanted crystalline byproducts. These findings validate the structural integrity and compositional compatibility of the UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs nanocomposite.

XRD patterns (a) and FTIR spectra (b) of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs composite and its components.

The XRD results provide detailed information about the nanoparticle size, calculated using the Scherrer eq (eq )­ 4 D=K·λβ·cos⁡θ where D (nm) is the crystallite size, K is the shape factor (commonly taken as 0.9), λ is the X-ray wavelength (0.15406 nm for Cu Kα), β (radians) is the full width at half-maximum (fwhm) of the diffraction peak, and θ (degrees) is the diffraction angle.

For the Fe3O4 phase, diffraction peaks at 30.2°, 35.5°, and 62.8°, corresponding to the (220), (311), and (440) planes, were selected. The average crystallite size was calculated to be approximately 130 nm with a standard deviation of ±1.05 nm. Similarly, peaks at 7.3°, 8.4°, and 25.6°, corresponding to the (111), (002), and (022) planes of the UiO-66 phase, yielded an average crystallite size of 63 nm with a standard deviation of ±1.57 nm. Although characteristic peaks of CNCs were also identified, they appeared broad and of low intensity, which is consistent with the semicrystalline nature of cellulose. Therefore, the Scherrer equation was not applied to CNCs, as such peak profiles are unsuitable for accurate crystallite size estimation.

FTIR analysis of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs synthesized at the optimal ratios (Figure b) reveals characteristic functional groups, confirming the successful integration of UiO-66, Fe3O4, and CNCs. A broad absorption band between 3000 and 3750 cm–1 corresponds to –OH stretching vibrations. This band indicates hydroxyl groups from CNCs and possible residual moisture. Peaks at 2800–3000 cm–1 are due to C–H stretching in sp3 carbons. These peaks reflect the aliphatic chains of the cellulose structure. A strong absorption band at 1700–1750 cm–1 corresponds to CO stretching vibrations. This confirms the presence of carbonyl groups from the terephthalic acid linker of UiO-66. Peaks at 1400–1600 cm–1 correspond to CC stretching in aromatic rings. This supports the aromatic structure of the UiO-66 framework. The region at 1000–1100 cm–1 corresponds to C–O stretching vibrations mainly from cellulose. This highlights the cellulose-based nature of the composite matrix. Absorption bands at 550–580 cm–1 correspond to Fe–O stretching in Fe3O4. Bands at 750–780 cm–1 correspond to Zr–O stretching in UiO-66. These confirm the successful incorporation of Fe3O4 nanoparticles and UiO-66 onto the CNCs scaffold. The presence of well-preserved functional groups from all components indicates effective synthesis and strong interfacial interactions. Together with the XRD results, the FTIR data confirm the structural integrity and compatibility of the three-component UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs composite.

SEM, and TEM Analysis

The surface morphologies of the individual components and their composites were characterized by scanning electron microscopy and transmission electron microscopy, as shown in Figure . The SEM image of UiO-66 (Figure a) displays well-defined octahedral crystals, consistent with the typical morphology of this zirconium-based MOF. Fe3O4 nanoparticles (Figure b) exhibit uniform spherical shapes with smooth surfaces. In contrast, CNCs (Figure c) appear as fibrous, web-like structures, reflecting their high aspect ratio and network-forming capability.

SEM images of (a) UiO-66, (b) Fe3O4, (c) CNCs (H2SO4_64_90), (d) Fe3O4@CNCs, (e) UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs; (f) TEM image of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs.

Upon forming the Fe3O4@CNCs hybrid (1:5 weight ratio), the SEM image (Figure d) reveals a roughened surface, indicating the successful deposition of Fe3O4 nanoparticles onto the CNCs scaffold. The integration appears homogeneous, suggesting strong interfacial interaction between the magnetic nanoparticles and the CNCs.

The ternary composite UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs (12:5) shows a continuous and relatively uniform coating of UiO-66 over the Fe3O4@CNCs framework (Figure e). This observation confirms the efficient assembly of UiO-66 onto the Fe3O4@CNCs surface, resulting in a well-defined core–shell-like architecture.

Figure f shows the TEM image of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs, offering further microstructural insight. CNCs are visible as nanoscale fibers forming a porous, entangled network. Dark-contrast particleseither spherical or cubicare Fe3O4 nanoparticles, well-dispersed across the CNC matrix. Lighter-contrast regions surrounding these particles correspond to UiO-66 crystals, confirming their successful deposition. These results collectively confirm the formation of a hierarchical ternary composite with well-integrated UiO-66, Fe3O4, and CNCs components.

Quantitative analysis of particle dimensions was conducted using ImageJ software based on SEM and TEM images. In the TEM micrographs, Fe3O4 nanoparticles appeared as high-contrast spherical or cubic shapes. They exhibited an average diameter of 325 nm, with a standard deviation of ±1.74 nm. CNCs were observed as elongated, fibrous structures. They formed a highly porous and interconnected network, with an average length of 14,580 nm (14.58 μm) and a standard deviation of ±1.37 nm. UiO-66 nanocrystals were identified by their characteristic octahedral morphology. They formed a relatively uniform coating over the Fe3O4@CNCs hybrid. The average particle size was 85 nm, with a standard deviation of ±1.28 nm.

Each component within the composite plays a complementary and essential role in constructing the multifunctional architecture. CNCs serve as a flexible and biocompatible support framework. They offer a high surface area and abundant hydroxyl groups, which facilitate the immobilization of Fe3O4 nanoparticles. Their fibrous and interconnected structure helps prevent particle aggregation, ensuring uniform dispersion throughout the matrix. Fe3O4 contributes magnetic functionality, enabling facile separation and potential magnetic targeting. Its intimate contact with CNCs promotes stability and distribution across the scaffold. UiO-66, as the outermost layer, provides high porosity and a large surface area. This enhances the composite’s adsorption capacity and may support catalytic activity. Its uniform coating enhances the surface functionality and stability of the hybrid system. Altogether, the synergy among CNCs, Fe3O4, and UiO-66 enables the formation of a robust and multifunctional material suitable for applications such as environmental remediation, catalysis, or targeted delivery.

BET and VSM Analysis

The BET analysis of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs reveals important textural characteristics of the composite material. The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm (Figure a) exhibits a type IV profile. It includes an H2 hysteresis loop, characteristic of mesoporous structures with ink-bottle-shaped pores. This porous architecture is advantageous for adsorptive applications, as it facilitates molecular diffusion while providing structural integrity. The BET surface area is determined to be 360.68 ± 1.82 m2/g (Figure b), indicating a highly developed surface. The pore size distribution curve (Figure c) shows the presence of mainly mesopores concentrated in the range of 2–14 nm, with a predominant distribution between 2 and 4 nm. While this time frame is acceptable for batch experiments, it may present a limitation for large-scale fixed-bed applications that demand faster kinetics. Nevertheless, the high adsorption capacity and selectivity compensate for the slower kinetics, and future studies may focus on optimizing the adsorbent’s structure or integrating flow-through systems to reduce contact time while preserving performance.

Effect of pH

Figure S2c shows that q t increases rapidly within the first 30 min. It reaches equilibrium between 60 and 120 min across the pH range of 2–7. The highest q e (48.55 mg/g) and R (96.06%) are observed at pH 6 (Figure c), which is close to the material’s point of zero charge (pHpzc = 5.65; Figure a). At this pH, the surface is nearly neutral or slightly negatively charged. Meanwhile, OTC primarily exists in its neutral form (H2OTC), which favors nonelectrostatic adsorption.

At lower pH values (2–4), both q e and R decline sharply due to electrostatic repulsion between positively charged surfaces and protonated OTC species (H3OTC+). At pH 7, adsorption remains high (q e = 48.32 mg/g; R = 95.57%) but decreases slightly. This reduction is attributed to increased surface negativity, which weakens interactions with anionic OTC forms (HOTC– and OTC2–).

Effect of Adsorbent Dosage

The dosage of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs was increased from 10 to 50 mg, corresponding to 0.2–1.0 mg/mL in 50 mL of OTC solution. Figure S2d shows that q t increased rapidly during the first 30 min across all dosages. It reached equilibrium between 60 and 120 min. At the lowest dosage (0.2 mg/mL), q t reached the highest value of 87.44 mg/g at 120 min, owing to the availability of numerous adsorption sites relative to the OTC concentration in the solution. As the dosage increased to 0.4 mg/mL, q t decreased to 48.55 mg/g. At even higher dosages (0.6–1.0 mg/mL), q t further decreased to 32.39 mg/g, 24.56 mg/g, and 19.88 mg/g, respectively. This decreasing trend is due to adsorption site saturation. At higher dosages, the amount of OTC in the solution is insufficient to occupy all available surface sites. Figure d illustrates the inverse correlation between q e and adsorbent dosage, while R increased linearly with dosage. R achieved its highest value of 98.52% at a dosage of 1.0 mg/mL, due to the increased total number of adsorption sites, resulting in nearly complete removal of OTC. However, q e reached its lowest value at higher dosages. This is because the number of adsorption sites exceeded the available OTC molecules in solution. When the adsorbent dosage exceeded 20 mg, the removal efficiency for OTC showed no significant improvement (96.06–98.52%). Therefore, 20 mg of material is the optimal dosage for adsorbing OTC at a concentration of 20 mg/L, ensuring both high efficiency and material economy.

At higher OTC concentrations (40, 60, and 80 mg/L), q e increases significantly: 82.47 mg/g, 95.20 mg/g, and 109.19 mg/g, respectively. In contrast, R tends to decrease, achieving only 81.29%, 62.22%, and 47.17% at these concentrations. This is due to insufficient adsorption sites relative to the OTC concentration. Figure e illustrates the inverse relationship between q e and R at higher OTC concentrations: q e increases with OTC availability, but R decreases due to site saturation. These results suggest that UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs performs optimally at low to moderate OTC concentrations. A dosage of 20 mg with 20 mg/L OTC achieves both high q e and R.

Effect of Temperature

The adsorption behavior of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs at different temperatures (303–333 K) is presented in Figures S2f and f. Figure S2f shows that q t increases rapidly during the first 30 min at all temperatures, then reaches equilibrium between 60 and 120 min. At higher temperatures, such as 323 and 333 K, q e dropped significantly to 44.52 mg/g and 39.35 mg/g, while R decreased to 87.71% and 76.91%, respectively. Figure f highlights the inverse relationship between temperature and adsorption efficiency, with both q e and R declining as temperature rises, emphasizing the exothermic nature of the adsorption process. These findings confirm the best performance at 303 K, where both adsorption capacity and removal efficiency are highest.

Regeneration Analysis

Figure c shows that the adsorption capacity of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs for OTC gradually decreases with each regeneration cycle. In the first cycle, q e reached 45.51 mg/g, with a R of 89.66%, showing only a slight reduction compared to the initial use (q e of 48.55 mg/g and R of 96.06%). This shows that the material remains highly effective after the first regeneration. However, in the second cycle, q e decreased to 41.43 mg/g, and R dropped to 80.73%, marking a noticeable decline in performance. In the third cycle, q e and R further decreased to 38.83 mg/g and 75.46%, respectively. By the fourth cycle, q e dropped to 27.58 mg/g, and R declined to 51.15%, reflecting a significant loss in adsorption efficiency. Several factors contribute to this decline. These include irreversible binding of OTC to the surface, reduced site activity due to incomplete regeneration, and gradual structural degradation from repeated use. Nevertheless, the UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs material maintains substantial adsorption efficiency during the first two cycles, indicating good potential for applications in wastewater treatment containing OTC.

XRD results (Figure d) show that the characteristic peaks of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs remain after four reuse cycles. However, their intensity decreases significantly. The retention of these peaks confirms that the crystalline structure of the material is largely preserved, demonstrating structural stability throughout the adsorption and regeneration processes. However, the reduction in peak intensity suggests partial degradation of the material or a loss of crystallinity over repeated cycles of use. This degradation may stem from multiple sources. These include chemical stress during desorption, mechanical disruption from handling, or residue accumulation that interferes with XRD signals. Despite the reduced intensity, the persistence of identifiable XRD peaks indicates that the framework of UiO-66 and its integration with Fe3O4 and CNCs remain functional. This partial stability implies that the material can still maintain a certain level of adsorption performance, albeit diminished, as evidenced by the decline in q e and removal efficiency over successive cycles.

To improve regeneration, the desorption process could be optimized by adjusting conditions or adding auxiliary agents. These results underscore that despite performance decline, UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs remains a viable option for short-term reuse applications in environmental remediation.

Adsorption Isotherms

To investigate and analyze the adsorption process of OTC on UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs, the Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models were applied. The Langmuir model describes adsorption occurring on homogeneous sites with the formation of a monolayer, represented by the linear eq (eq )­ 8 Ceqe=1qmax·KL+Ceqmax

In contrast, the Freundlich model describes adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces with the formation of multilayer adsorption, represented by the eq (eq )­ 9 log⁡qe=log⁡KF+1nlog⁡Ce where q max (mg/g) is the maximum adsorption capacity, K L (L/mg) is the Langmuir adsorption constant, K F (mg/g (L/mg)1/n ) is the Freundlich constant related to adsorption capacity, and n (dimensionless) is the Freundlich exponent indicating the favorability of the adsorption process. The parameters from both models were calculated based on the linear equations to analyze the adsorption capacity and mechanism of the material.

The analysis of Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms for the adsorption of OTC onto UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs provides valuable insights into the adsorption mechanism while comparing the suitability of the two models. The Langmuir model, with a high determination coefficient R 2 of 0.9932, indicates an excellent fit to the experimental data. The calculated parameters include a maximum adsorption capacity q max of 110.13 mg/g, demonstrating the material’s superior adsorption capability for OTC, and a Langmuir constant K L of 1.5584 L/mg, reflecting a strong adsorption affinity. Additionally, the residual sum of squares of 0.0006 confirms the model’s high accuracy in describing the adsorption behavior. These findings indicate that OTC adsorption primarily follows a monolayer mechanism on uniform sites, especially suitable for high-concentration conditions.

In contrast, the Freundlich model has a lower determination coefficient R 2 of 0.9083 but still reasonably describes the adsorption process on heterogeneous surfaces. The Freundlich constant K F, which is 43.7633 (mg/g (L/mg)1/n ), indicates strong adsorption at low concentrations, while the Freundlich exponent n, at 3.6982, confirms the favorability of the adsorption process. However, the higher RSS value of 0.0263 suggests that the model fits less well compared to Langmuir. The Freundlich model highlights the heterogeneous nature of the adsorption surface, reflecting the uneven distribution of energy across adsorption sites.

Langmuir exhibits better fit (higher R 2, lower RSS), supporting a monolayer adsorption mechanism on homogeneous sites. Meanwhile, the Freundlich model emphasizes the role of surface heterogeneity and better describes adsorption at lower concentrations. In conclusion, UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs exhibits exceptional adsorption efficiency for OTC, with high adsorption capacity and strong adsorption affinity. Thus, Langmuir is more applicable at high OTC levels, whereas Freundlich captures surface heterogeneity at low concentrations.

To further validate the model applicability, a comparative analysis was performed between the experimentally observed adsorption capacities (q e) and those predicted by both Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms (Figure d). At low equilibrium concentrations (e.g., C e = 10 mg/L), the Freundlich model provided a closer estimation (30.98 mg/g) to the experimental value (24.30 mg/g) than Langmuir (33.34 mg/g), although both slightly overpredicted the adsorption. However, at higher concentrations (C e = 40–80 mg/L), the Langmuir model consistently yielded predictions that were much closer to experimental values, with absolute errors ranging from 0.97 to 9.52 mg/g and relative errors below 10%. In contrast, the Freundlich model exhibited increasing deviations, particularly at C e = 80 mg/L, where it overestimated q e by more than 6%. These results further support that Langmuir is more suitable for describing the adsorption behavior of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs at elevated OTC concentrations, while Freundlich remains useful for capturing adsorption trends under dilute conditions.

Adsorption Selectivity

The adsorption selectivity results of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs (Figure ) for five different antibiotics reveal significant differences in R at 150 min. Overall, these results confirm UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs’ high selectivity for OTC, driven by optimal molecular compatibility in structure, size, and chemistry. This highlights the potential application of the material in wastewater treatment containing antibiotics, particularly oxytetracycline.

Figure illustrates that OTC adsorption on UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs is driven by surface chemistry, OTC ionization states, and molecular interactions. pH effects and the material’s pHpzc offer key mechanistic insights. OTC, with pK a values of 3.57, 7.49, and 9.88, exists in various protonation states (H3OTC+, H2OTC, HOTC–, and OTC2–) depending on the pH of the solution, significantly affecting its interaction with the adsorbent.

At pH 6 (near pHpzc), the adsorbent surface is neutral or slightly negative, and OTC exists mainly as neutral H2OTC. Kinetic and thermodynamic data suggest chemisorption-driven adsorption: pseudo-second-order kinetics, negative ΔG (spontaneous), and negative ΔH (exothermic) support strong chemical interactions.

In summary, OTC adsorption onto UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs involves pore diffusion, chemical interactions (hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking), and electrostatic effects. Near pHpzc, these mechanisms balance effectively, making the material a potent adsorbent for OTC and related contaminants.

Conclusion

In this study, a multifunctional composite adsorbent, UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs, was successfully synthesized via a solvothermal method by integrating a metal–organic framework (UiO-66), magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles, and bioderived CNCs extracted from water mimosa. The resulting hybrid material exhibited a high specific surface area (360.68 ± 1.82 m2/g), mesoporous architecture, and excellent magnetic recoverability. Under optimal conditions (pH 6, 303 K), the maximum adsorption capacity for OTC reached 48.55 mg/g.

Kinetic modeling revealed that the adsorption process followed a pseudo-second-order mechanism, indicating that chemisorption was the rate-determining step. Equilibrium data fitted well to the Langmuir isotherm (R 2 = 0.9932), with a maximum monolayer adsorption capacity (q max) of 110.13 mg/g, suggesting homogeneous adsorption sites and strong molecular affinity. Thermodynamic analysis confirmed the spontaneity (ΔG < 0) and exothermic nature (ΔH = −62.32 kJ/mol) of the process, along with decreased randomness at the solid–liquid interface (ΔS = −168.06 J/mol·K), reflecting the formation of stable adsorbate–adsorbent interactions.

The composite also exhibited remarkable selectivity toward OTC over other antibiotics, attributed to molecular size compatibility and functional group interactions. These findings underscore the potential of UiO-66/Fe3O4@CNCs as a sustainable and efficient adsorbent for antibiotic removal in wastewater treatment applications.
