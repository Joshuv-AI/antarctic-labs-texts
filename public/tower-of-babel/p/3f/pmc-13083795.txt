A humanized engineered heart tissue platform for cardiotoxicity assessment
Authors: Yao-Hui Sun, Daphne A Diloretto, Hillary KJ Kao, Padmini Sirish, Christophe Morisseau, Rasheed Sule, Ming-Fo Hsu, Regan L Smithers, James Overton, Valeriy Timofeyev, Alexander Merleev, Gabriela Grigorean, Brett S Phinney, Emanual M Maverakis, Aldrin V Gomes, Fawaz G Haj, Jan A Nolta, James W Chan, Bruce D Hammock, Deborah K Lieu, Nipavan Chiamvimonvat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCardiovascular diseases (CVDs), many of which are influenced by exposure to environmental xenobiotics, lack physiologically relevant in vitro models for cardiotoxicity assessment. Although some pollutants have established associations with CVD, the effects of a wide range of potential toxicants remains unknown. Here, we developed a three-dimensional recellularized humanized engineered heart tissue (rHHT) platform by integrating decellularized human left ventricular extracellular matrix with human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs), yielding spontaneously contracting tissues that recapitulate key features of native ventricular myocardium. We also generated a hiPSC line stably expressing the calcium indicator GCaMP6f, enabling real-time and longitudinal monitoring of calcium transients. Using ethanol and rotenone as examples, we demonstrate that the rHHT platform provides a sensitive system for evaluating cardiotoxicity and is more stringent than conventional monolayer approaches. This study presents a scalable platform for xenobiotic cardiotoxicity assessment, with potential applicability to high-throughput screening, mechanistic studies, and future personalized medicine applications.Keywords: calcium transient, cardiac tissue engineering, cardiotoxicity, extracellular matrix, human induced pluripotent stem cells, human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes, drug screening, xenobiotics, ethanol, rotenone.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major global health problem and the leading cause of death worldwide. The World Health Organization estimates that CVD causes ∼17.9 million deaths each year, or roughly 31% of all deaths worldwide (Mozaffarian et al., 2016). Environmental exposures are estimated to contribute to ∼23% of CVD cases (Tzoulaki et al., 2016). For example, ethanol, the type of alcohol in alcoholic beverages, is linked to both acute and chronic cardiac problems, while exposure to rotenone, a broad-spectrum insecticide, can cause cardiovascular toxicity and, in severe cases, heart failure (Wood et al., 2005).

The conventional methods of cardiotoxicity evaluation have predominantly involved the use of large animal models. While these models have provided invaluable insights, they are mired by ethical concerns, high costs, and complex logistics (Sirenko et al., 2017). Moreover, interspecies variation often casts doubts on the direct translatability of such findings to human health. Thus, there exists an imperative to seek alternatives that can reliably mimic human cardiac physiology while being ethical and cost-effective.

In this context, human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) have marked a transformative era in cardiotoxicity testing (Takeda et al., 2018). These cells offer a more physiologically relevant and highly reproducible model compared to their animal counterparts. However, most current methods engage these cells in a two-dimensional setting, which does not fully replicate the intricate three-dimensional architecture and dynamics of native cardiac tissue. This limitation underscores the necessity of a more integrated approach that considers not just the cellular components but also their extracellular milieu.

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is pivotal in mediating vital cellular functions, dictating cell fate through a complex interplay of biochemical and mechanical signals (Porzionato et al., 2018). Recognizing the crucial role of ECM, particularly in the cardiac sphere, there is a growing consensus that tissue-specific ECMs can serve as the optimal scaffolds for tissue engineering. Such ECMs not only present the native microenvironment to the resident cells but also potentiate the engineered tissue’s functional attributes.

Building upon these foundations, our research aims to overcome current limitations by developing a humanized engineered heart tissue (EHT) platform. The platform integrates genetically engineered hiPSC-CMs, which express a single copy of the calcium indicator GCaMP6f from the AAVS1 “safe harbor” site, for real-time, longitudinal monitoring of calcium transients within a scaffold derived from human heart ECM. This approach bridges the gap between traditional in vitro studies and human physiology, offering a robust model to assess the cardiotoxic potential of environmental chemicals and pharmaceutical compounds. By enhancing the precision of cardiotoxicity assessments, we take a step toward the rational design of safer therapeutic agents and deepen our understanding of environmental CVDs.

Results

Characterization of decellularized human left ventricular tissues

The study was performed in accordance to the University of California, Davis IRB-approved protocol and the Stem Cell Research Oversight Committee (SCRO)-approved protocol. We harvested left ventricular (LV) tissues from human donor hearts. Tissues were flash frozen. Biopsy punches (10 mm in diameter) were done on frozen samples and then cryo-sectioned into 300 μm slices. A sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-based treatment followed by DNase incubation was employed to achieve complete decellularization, as evidenced by the resulting transparent ECMs, contrasting the native whitish appearance (Figure 1A).

The ultrastructure of the decellularized ECM was assessed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The SEM images revealed a mesh-like structure (Figure S1) with scattered pores of various sizes and shapes, corresponding to spaces previously occupied by cardiomyocytes (CMs) (Figure 1B). Image analysis of the ECM highlighted the distribution of pore size, roundness, and fractional area, showing that the pores occupied 25%–45% of the ECM area, consistent with a substantial myocardial composition in the LV (Figure 1C).

To investigate the biochemical composition of the matrix proteins, we performed mass spectrometry. The analysis showed that the ECMs primarily consisted of fibrous collagens, including laminin (Figure 1D). Subtype analysis highlighted abundant collagen subtypes in LV ECM (Figure 1D). Notably, elastin content was not assessed due to extraction limitations of our sample processing protocol, as further discussed in the “methods” section.

These comprehensive characterizations confirm the successful decellularization of human LV tissues and the preservation of essential ECM components, providing a suitable scaffold for further tissue engineering applications.

Development of a hiPSC line stably expressing an ultrasensitive calcium indicator GCaMP6f

To advance our toolkit for studying CM dynamics, we engineered a hiPSC line that stably expresses GCaMP6f, an ultrasensitive calcium indicator, consisting of a green fluorescent protein, the calcium-binding protein calmodulin (CaM) and CaM-interacting M13 peptide (Broussard et al., 2018). This line was created through the precise integration of GCaMP6f into the AAVS1 safe harbor site in the PPP1R12C gene on chromosome 19, employing the CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing system (Ben Jehuda et al., 2018). The successful insertion was guided by a sgRNA sequence targeting the AAVS1 locus, and the specificity was confirmed by the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence (Figure 2A).

Following electroporation and selection with puromycin, we obtained colonies that uniformly expressed the GCaMP6f reporter driven by the CAG promoter (Figure 2B) (Kotin et al., 1992). This resulted in consistently distributed cytosolic fluorescence, which was crucial for the future monitoring of calcium transients across the hiPSC population (Figure 2C). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed high expression levels of GCaMP6f, demonstrating its utility for real-time, non-invasive monitoring of CM calcium handling (Figure 2D). Sanger sequencing further verified the integration of a single copy of GCaMP6f by comparing the wild-type AAVS1 sequence with the edited allele, showing precise insertion of the GCaMP6f construct (Figure 2E).

These results confirm the successful development of a hiPSC line stably expressing GCaMP6f, providing a robust tool for detailed and dynamic studies of calcium signaling in the GCaMP6f-hiPSC differentiated CMs.

Engineering humanized heart tissues that structurally resemble native ventricular tissues in vitro

The ventricular phenotype in hiPSC-derived CMs was established through a modified differentiation protocol that precisely manipulates Wnt signaling via the small molecule inhibitor IWR1 (Figure 3A), as described by Lian et al., 2013. Cardiac differentiation was quantitatively assessed using flow cytometry to detect cardiac troponin T (cTnT) expression. Our differentiation strategy resulted in a high CM yield, with ∼89.9% of cells expressing cTnT (Figure 3B), which was further confirmed by immunostaining for cardiac transcription factor, Nkx2.5, and cTnT (Figure 3C). The electrophysiological properties of the ventricular-like CMs were further characterized by optical mapping (Figure 3D), where recording of action potentials with the voltage-sensitive dye FluoVolt (Yechikov et al., 2016) confirmed that 56% of the differentiated CMs exhibited ventricular-like action potentials (Figure 3E).

For the construction of recellularized humanized engineered heart tissues (rHHTs), decellularized ECMs were prepared on 24-well plates or glass-bottom dishes and recellularized with the differentiated CMs (Figure 4A). Within a few days of in vitro culture, the CMs, together with the decellularized ECM scaffolds, formed self-organized heart tissue-like structures and underwent robust spontaneous contractions. Immunostaining for cTnT and nuclei with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) illustrated that hiPSC-CMs assembled into bundles, aligning with the matrix scaffolds, recapitulating the structural organization of native cardiac tissue (Figure 4B).

Functional assessment of the rHHTs

These engineered tissues demonstrated sustained functionality, with spontaneous contractions lasted for at least 180 days. The contractions of the CMs in the EHTs can be quantified using MUSCLEMOTION software as described by Christine Mummery’s group (Sala et al., 2018). The rHHTs underwent robust contractions with variable amplitude and kinetics (Figure 4C; both in arbitrary units). These contractions were then quantified by using particle image velocimetry (PIV) (Thielicke and Sonntag, 2021), demonstrating rHHT contractility and mechanical function (Figure 4D; Video S3).

Displacement magnitude ranges from 0 to 7.5 μm. Time stamps in milliseconds. Scale bars, 100 μm. Slow motion.

Calcium handling is a hallmark of CMs and is critical for functional assessment and cellular phenotyping of hiPSC-CMs and heart tissues (Itzhaki et al., 2011; Shinnawi et al., 2015). The ultra-sensitive GCaMP6f expression was retained after cardiac differentiation using Wnt inhibitor, which enabled faithful optical recording of calcium transients (CaTs) of the hiPSC-CMs in both single-cell level (Figure 3F; Video S1) and monolayers (Figure 3G; Video S2), allowing direct and repeated measurements of intracellular calcium dynamics, thus avoiding possible issues associated with uneven fluorescent dye loading in thick heart tissues (Broyles et al., 2018). We performed optical recording of CaTs of the CMs that were differentiated from GCaMP6f hiPSCs and repopulated on decellularized heart ECM. Figure 4E shows typical CaT traces of the spontaneously contracting rHHTs with the amplitude of the CaT defined by the relative fluorescence intensity change to baseline (ΔF/F0). The peak-to-peak interval was used to determine the beating frequency.

We previously developed R-based software (Sun et al., 2019) for the analysis of action potentials of hiPSC-CMs expressing ArcLight (Jin et al., 2012)—a genetically engineered voltage indicator (Kaestner et al., 2015). We modified the program to profile and analyze the CaTs optically recorded from GCaMP6f hiPSC-CMs or rHHTs. CaT duration CaTD50 and CaTD90 were measured as the time interval between 50% maximal upstroke amplitude and 50% or 90% of decay, respectively (Figure 4E). The analysis pipeline is publicly available (see “data and code availability”).

Electrical stimulation and drug testing of rHHTs

After generating and functionally validating the rHHTs and developing software for the analysis, we tested the responses to various pharmacological agents and electrical stimulation in vitro. The rHHTs could be paced using electrical stimuli, showing synchronized responses with expected changes in CaT profiles (Figure 5A). Quantification of CaTs of spontaneously contracting rHHTs that were paced at 2 Hz demonstrated a significant increase of contraction frequency but not the CaT amplitude (Figure 5B).

We then confirmed the dependency of rHHT functionality on extracellular calcium. Experiments with calcium chelation demonstrated that the rHHTs’ functionality is highly dependent on extracellular calcium (Figure S2). The reduction of calcium transients upon adding a calcium chelator and their partial recovery after washout confirms the expected role of extracellular calcium in maintaining rHHT contractility.

We further tested the effect of isoproterenol, a beta-adrenergic agonist, on the rHHTs. Treatment with 1 μM isoproterenol resulted in a significant increase in CaT amplitude and frequency with little changes in the duration, suggesting increased calcium handling activity (Figure 5C). This response is consistent with positive chronotropic and inotropic effects of beta-adrenergic stimulation (Ronaldson-Bouchard et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2015).

Since functionality of the rHHTs is extracellular calcium-dependent, we tested the response to nifedipine, an L-type calcium channel blocker. The addition of 200 nM nifedipine resulted in the abolition of CaTs (Figure S3; Video S4), confirming the essential role of calcium influx through L-type calcium channels.

Video S4. Intracellular Ca²⁺ dynamics in an rHHT before and after treatment with 200 nM nifedipine, showing progressive reduction in calcium transient amplitude and frequency leading to contraction arrest, related to Figure S3

Time stamps in seconds. Scale bars, 200 μm. Real time.

We next examined the effect of E-4031, a specific blocker of the human Ether-à-go-go-Related Gene (hERG) potassium channel. Consistent with other reports, application of E-4031 to the rHHTs significantly delayed CaT duration (Figure 5E) as indicated by prolonged CaTD50 and CaTD90 (Figure 5F) (Spencer et al., 2014). These effects align with the known pharmacological actions of E-4031, which blocks hERG potassium channels and delays action potential repolarization. No early after-depolarization has been detected in these rHHTs (da Rocha et al., 2017). Collectively, these data validate the functional responsiveness of the rHHT platform to pharmacological agents and electrical stimulation. The rHHT platform successfully mimics native cardiac tissue responses, underscoring its potential for preclinical drug safety testing and cardiovascular research.

rHHTs are more stringent than hiPSC-CMs alone in assessing alcohol cardiotoxicity

A primary goal of this study was to establish a humanized in vitro platform for assessing cardiotoxic effects of environmental substances (xenobiotics). Ethanol, a commonly used organic solvent and environmental xenobiotic, as well as the principal psychoactive component of alcoholic beverages, is well documented to impair cardiac function. Previous studies have reported dose-dependent cellular dysfunction and reduced viability in hiPSC-derived CMs following ethanol exposure (Rampoldi et al., 2019). To evaluate ethanol-induced cardiotoxicity in the rHHT platform, we exposed rHHTs to 100 mM ethanol (Figure 6A) and monitored calcium transient parameters over 5 days (Figure 6B), using a dose previously shown to disrupt calcium signaling and contractile function in hiPSC-derived CMs (Liu et al., 2021). Acute ethanol exposure produced a transient increase in beating frequency (Figure 6C); however, rHHTs otherwise maintained stable functional parameters, with no significant changes in CaT amplitude (ΔF/F0), CaTD50, or CaTD90 across all time points examined (Figure 6D; Videos S5, S6, S7, and S8). These results demonstrate preserved functional integrity of rHHTs under ethanol challenge.

In contrast, hiPSC-CM monolayers lacking ECM scaffolds exhibited markedly greater sensitivity to ethanol exposure. Monolayers treated with increasing ethanol concentrations (0.5%–6%) showed a clear dose-dependent deterioration in rhythmic activity over 5 days (Figure 6E; Table S1). While 0.5% ethanol had minimal effects (Video S9), concentrations ≥1% induced abnormal calcium transients (Video S10), with higher doses resulting in loss of coordinated beating and extensive cell death (Videos S11 and S12). Partial recovery following ethanol removal was observed at intermediate concentrations (2%–4%; Video S13), indicating limited reversibility.

Together, these findings show that rHHTs impose a more stringent and physiologically relevant context for ethanol cardiotoxicity assessment than hiPSC-CM monolayers. The three-dimensional, ECM-supported architecture of rHHTs confers functional resilience to metabolic stress, underscoring the importance of tissue-level organization for predictive cardiotoxicity testing.

rHHT platform reveals reversible and dose-dependent cardiotoxicity of rotenone

Rotenone, an extensively utilized insecticide, is known to elevate reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in cardiac cells (Li et al., 2003), prompting an investigation into its potential cardiotoxic effects using our rHHT platform. In the control condition with 0.1% DMSO, rHHTs presented strong, synchronized contractions and CaTs (Figure 7A; Video S14). Upon the introduction of 1 μM rotenone, irregular or fast CaTs were observed at 4 h post-treatment. More pronounced disruptions, including the emergence of multiple CaTs and spatially desynchronized contractions, were detected within just 1 h of 10 μM rotenone treatment (Video S15). This disruption led to some regions ceasing to contract entirely (Figure 7A). Continued exposure to rotenone exacerbated these effects. At 4 h post-treatment with 10 μM rotenone, most rHHT tissues had stopped contracting, with only isolated areas showing rapid yet small CaTs (Video S16). After 24 h, contractions were barely detectable across all samples, including those treated with 1 μM rotenone (Video S17), indicating a profound loss of functional activity despite the preserved structural appearance of the rHHTs. Quantitative analyses of the CaT parameters—including amplitude, CaTD50, CaTD90, frequency, and SNR (signal to noise ratio)—revealed significant deviations not only between the rotenone- and DMSO-treated rHHTs but also across different time points following rotenone application (Figure 7B). These data elucidate a clear, dose- and time-dependent cardiotoxic response to rotenone within the rHHT platform. Notably, removing rotenone at 24 h after 1 μM treatment resulted in partial recovery of CaTs and contractions, with complete recovery observed 48 h after drug removal, lasting for at least 2 weeks. However, cardiotoxicity induced by 10 μM rotenone proved irreversible. Despite extensive washing and medium changes 24 h after 10 μM rotenone treatment, no recovery of spontaneous contractions or CaTs was noted after a 7-day period (Figure 7A).

In summary, these findings illustrate the rHHT platform’s capability to discern both low-dose reversible and high-dose irreversible cardiotoxicity effects of rotenone, establishing its utility in the evaluation of cardiotoxic agents. The data, supported by detailed optical recordings, comprehensive CaT parameter quantifications, and rigorous statistical analyses, affirm the rHHT’s stringency and physiological relevance for cardiotoxicity testing.

Discussion

In the current study, we developed an engineered humanized heart tissue platform to evaluate cardiotoxicity of environmental substances and pharmacological compounds. By integrating hiPSC-derived CMs with decellularized human LV ECM and utilizing the ultrasensitive genetically encoded calcium indicator GCaMP6f, we created a physiologically relevant in vitro system. Our findings demonstrated the platform’s ability to detect dose-dependent and time-dependent cardiotoxic effects of ethanol and rotenone, showcasing its potential as a robust tool for cardiac safety assessment and drug screening.

A physiologically more relevant in vitro system for cardiotoxicity assessment

Our study highlights the potential of an engineered humanized heart tissue platform for evaluating the cardiotoxicity of environmental substances. The platform was designed to closely resemble the native human heart in vitro by using tissue engineering components. Specifically, hiPSC-derived CMs were integrated into decellularized human LV ECM. The human ventricular myocardium matrix preserved unique biochemical components such as collagens I and III and glycosaminoglycans while also possessing mechanical properties conducive to the function of contractile CMs (Fomovsky et al., 2010; Gluck et al., 2017), providing an optimal architectural scaffold and environment (Ronaldson-Bouchard et al., 2018; Taylor et al., 2017).

In this study, we focused on calcium handling associated with cardiotoxicants. We integrated this engineered humanized heart tissue with an ultrasensitive genetically encoded calcium indicator, GCaMP6f (Broussard et al., 2018). GCaMPs consist of the calcium-binding protein CaM and the CaM-interacting M13 peptide (Crivici and Ikura, 1995), fused with a circularly permuted green fluorescent protein (cpGFP) (Baird et al., 1999). Changes in intracellular Ca2+ levels lead to fast and dynamic changes in emission fluorescence intensity. GCaMP6f offers advantages over calcium-sensitive dyes like Fluo-4 (Gee et al., 2000), including its noninvasive nature, negligible cell toxicity, and high signal-to-noise ratio without the need for loading, washing, or medium change.

Unlike methods involving lentiviral transduction (Saleem et al., 2020) that could result in random and multiple integrations, potentially disrupting gene expression as well as losing the reporter element during and after differentiation, our hiPSC-CMs contain a single copy of GCaMP6f targeted to a safe harbor site in the genome. This results in stable and uniform expression in differentiated CMs, making it ideal for high-throughput drug screening or continuous and repeated long-term calcium transient monitoring.

Our approach advocates for the adoption of rHHT platforms embedded with genetically encoded calcium indicators (GECIs) for more insightful cardiotoxicity assessments. Such platforms bridge the gap between traditional in vitro assays and the complex, multifaceted nature of cardiac responses in vivo, offering a more predictive model for understanding the cardiac safety profile of various compounds (Ronaldson-Bouchard et al., 2018; Shinnawi et al., 2015).

Differential response of rHHT and hiPSC-CM monolayers to ethanol

Alcohol, as one of the most historically prevalent psychoactive substances, poses significant risks of addiction and contributes substantially to global morbidity and mortality. In our investigation, the acute cardiotoxic effects of ethanol were evaluated using both hiPSC-CM monolayers and the rHHT platform. The hiPSC-CM monolayers exhibited a dose-dependent increase in cardiotoxicity, marked by notable alterations in CaT amplitudes and beating rates. In contrast, the rHHT platform demonstrated remarkable robustness under similar conditions. Despite a temporal increase in the beating rate after ethanol exposure (Figure 6D), the rHHTs maintained stable functional parameters. This stability suggests that the rHHT platform may more accurately mimic the heart’s in vivo response due to its three-dimensional tissue architecture, which includes multicellular interactions and ECM components. The temporal dynamics of ethanol’s cardiotoxic effects in the hiPSC-CM monolayers revealed heightened vulnerability to higher ethanol concentrations. In these monolayers, ethanol exposure led to significant disruptions in calcium signaling and contractility, indicating a greater sensitivity compared to the rHHTs.

Our findings underscore the rHHT platform’s potential as a more stringent and physiologically relevant model for cardiotoxicity assessment. The platform’s ability to withstand acute ethanol-induced cardiotoxicity, in contrast to the hiPSC-CM monolayers, positions it as a crucial tool for accurate risk evaluation of substances like alcohol, which is widely consumed and has complex multi-systemic effects. This differential response highlights the importance of using advanced tissue-engineered models for more reliable and predictive cardiotoxicity testing.

Low-dose reversible and high-dose permanent cardiotoxicity of rotenone

Unlike ethanol, which primarily acts as a metabolic stressor, rotenone inhibits mitochondrial respiratory complex I in cardiac cells. Our proof-of-concept experiments using the rHHT model provided valuable insights into the time-dependent cardiotoxic effects of rotenone. Early effects were detected within 1 h of drug addition, manifesting as desynchronized CaTs characterized by irregular and arrhythmogenic activities. With prolonged exposure, cardiac contractions progressively deteriorated and ultimately ceased within 24 h following treatment with 10 μM rotenone. This progression—from reversible functional disturbances at low doses to permanent dysfunction at higher doses—underscores the utility of the engineered humanized heart tissue platform for cardiotoxicity assessment. Together, these findings highlight how compound-specific mechanisms interact with three-dimensional tissue architecture to shape functional resilience or vulnerability.

Limitations of the study and future application potentials

One limitation of the study is the exclusive focus on hiPSC-CMs within the rHHT platform, which simplifies the tissue’s cellular complexity since CM differentiation rarely yields a purely mature population. As noted, hiPSC-CMs typically exhibit a partially immature phenotype, and this inherent immaturity is an important consideration when interpreting assay performance. Future investigations should explore incorporating additional cell types to better mimic the native cardiac environment. Specifically, cardiac fibrosis has been shown to be one of the prominent effects of multiple xenobiotics; therefore, proportionally including hiPSC-derived cardiac fibroblasts in the rHHT platform would be informative. The study also focused on specific concentration ranges for ethanol and rotenone, warranting further studies across a broader dose-dependent response. In the case of ethanol, the 100 mM concentration used is within the upper physiological range observed in individuals with acute intoxication; clinically, such levels primarily produce neurological impairment with relatively modest acute cardiac effects—often presenting as sinus tachycardia with no direct or immediate impairment of myocardial structure or functionalities. These clinical features align with our observations in rHHTs and further suggest that the rHHT platform, which responds in a more physiologically relevant and stringent manner than monolayer cultures, is better suited for modeling high-range exposures and their nuanced cardiac effects. Additionally, while the study provided valuable insights into short-term exposure effects, examining long-term or chronic substance exposure in rHHT constructs could offer a more complete understanding of cardiotoxicity (Liu et al., 2021). Considering additional environmental factors such as shear stress and electrical stimulation in future experiments may further enhance the platform’s in vivo relevance.

Another limitation is the availability of human heart tissues for the ECM. Obtaining human heart tissues is challenging due to limited donor availability and ethical constraints. To address this limitation, we have explored the use of porcine heart tissues as an alternative source for the ECM. The porcine ECM shares similar architecture (Figure S4) as the human heart ECM and preserves essential components such as collagens I and III, glycosaminoglycans, while exhibiting mechanical properties conducive to the contractile function of hiPSC-CMs (Gluck et al., 2017). Similar to rHHTs generated using human ECM, hiPSC-CMs recellularized on porcine LV ECM formed three-dimensional EHTs with improved structural organization compared to the two-dimensional monolayer cultures, as evidenced by enhanced cTnT-positive fiber alignment (Figures S5A and S5B). Importantly, tissues engineered with porcine LV ECM also showed significantly increased expression of the ventricular gap junction protein connexin 43 compared with non-ventricular control ECM (Figures S5C and S5D). These observations support the use of porcine LV ECM as a structural surrogate for ventricular myocardium and are consistent with our previous work demonstrating the feasibility of porcine ECM for cardiac tissue engineering (Sun et al., 2023). Together, incorporation of porcine-derived ECM provides a practical and scalable approach that preserves key architectural features relevant to tissue function, while acknowledging species-specific differences and mitigating limitations associated with human tissue availability.

The rHHT platform holds great promise as a high-throughput screening tool for pharmaceutical compound evaluation. Moreover, its ability to generate patient-specific cardiac tissues offers opportunities for personalized medicine, enabling individualized drug testing and treatment optimization. The platform’s sensitivity to environmental substances also positions it as a valuable system for assessing cardiotoxicity of environmental compounds. Exploring mechanistic aspects of cardiotoxicity, and integrating the rHHT platform with advanced technologies such as CRISPR gene editing or organ-on-a-chip approaches, may further enhance its utility in cardiovascular research.

Methods

Human and porcine LV ECM preparation

Use of human tissues was approved by the UC Davis IRB (protocol #285560). Human and porcine LV tissues were dissected, cryosectioned, and decellularized using established protocols (Becker et al., 2017; Gluck et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2019). Briefly, LV tissue slices were subjected to detergent-based decellularization followed by enzymatic removal of residual nucleic acids. Decellularized matrices were stored in antibiotic-containing PBS and verified for effective decellularization by absence of nuclear staining.

Scanning electron microscopy

Ultrastructural features of decellularized LV ECM were examined by SEM using standard fixation and sputter-coating procedures, as described previously (Gluck et al., 2017). Images were analyzed to assess ECM fiber architecture and pore size.

Proteomic analysis of human LV ECM

Proteomic profiling of pooled human LV ECM was performed following detergent-based protein extraction and enzymatic digestion. Peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using an Orbitrap mass spectrometer. Protein identification and quantification were performed using established label-free workflows, with relative ECM protein abundance reported as a percentage of total identified ECM content (Gluck et al., 2017).

hiPSC culture and cardiac differentiation

A hiPSC line, iPS-DF6-9-9T.B, was purchased from WiCell (WiCell, Madison, MI) under a material transfer agreement. Cells were maintained under feeder-free conditions. A stable GCaMP6f-expressing hiPSC line was generated via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated targeting of the AAVS1 locus, as previously described (Sun et al., 2019). Correctly targeted clones were confirmed by PCR and sequencing. All primers and plasmids used in this study are listed in Table S2. hiPSCs were differentiated into CMs using a stage-specific Wnt/β-catenin modulation protocol (Lian et al., 2013). CMs were harvested between days 7 and 20 post-differentiation and recellularized onto decellularized LV ECM to generate humanized EHTs. Detailed flow cytometry, CM subtype classification, and immunostaining procedures (antibodies and fluorescent dyes listed in Table S3) are provided in the supplemental information.

Optical recordings and calcium transient analysis

Calcium transients were recorded from rHHTs and CM monolayers using GCaMP6f fluorescence imaging under physiological conditions. Pharmacological agents, including isoproterenol, nifedipine, ethanol, and rotenone, were applied at indicated concentrations. Calcium transient amplitude (ΔF/F0), duration (CaTD50, CaTD90), and frequency were quantified using automated image analysis pipelines. Detailed imaging parameters and contractility measurements are provided in the supplemental information.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. Statistical comparisons were performed using two-tailed Student’s t tests or one-way ANOVA with appropriate post hoc testing, as indicated in figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
