Effects on LuxR‐Regulated Bioluminescence of Cyclodextrin‐Acyl‐L‐Homoserine Lactone Hybrids
Authors: Nicolas Tinet, David Lesur, Yves Queneau, Laurent Soulère, Florence Djedaini‐Pilard, Véronique Bonnet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this work, acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs) are grafted, the most studied signaling molecules in many Gram‐negative bacteria, onto cyclodextrins (CDs) by copper‐catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition “click” coupling between alkynyl‐AHLs and 6‐azido‐CDs derived from α‐ and β‐CD, native or methylated. Attaching biomolecules onto a CD scaffold is a known strategy to enhance their properties, but designing CD‐AHL conjugates has never been reported. These molecules were fully characterized by NMR and high‐resolution mass spectrometry, and the study of their solubility and conformation reveals significant conformational changes due to the presence of the AHL appendage on the CD structure. One of the hybrids (per‐AHL‐β‐CD, 9 B ) exhibits higher solubility in water than AHL and β‐CD alone. The new CD‐AHLs conjugates are found to modulate bioluminescence in a quorum Sensing LuxR‐regulated light‐producing bacterial model, with significant variations depending on the structure. The per‐AHL‐β‐CD, 9 B , is found to be the most active compound in the series.

Introduction

Cyclodextrins (CDs), well‐known cyclic oligosaccharide, can be interesting architectures acting either by the formation of inclusion complexes based on their supramolecular properties, or as scaffolds for designing conjugates with other molecules. In the field of quorum sensing (QS) modulation, only the first aspect has already been studied. QS is a mechanism of cell–cell communication allowing bacteria to synchronize their behavior by modulating the expression of some genes in relation to cell population density, based on small molecules called autoinducers.[ 1 ] External interference with QS is a way to modulate bacterial behaviors, with various applications in biotechnology and medicine.[ 2 ] Among autoinducers, N‐acyl‐L‐homoserine lactones (referred to as AHLs or HSLs) serve as signaling molecules in many Gram‐negative bacteria.[ 3 ] Regarding the effect of CDs on QS‐related phenomena, Kato et al. have proposed that the diminution of prodigiosin production in Serratia marcescens in the presence of 10 mM hydroxypropyl‐β‐CD (HPBCD) is ascribed to the formation of inclusion complexes with C6‐, C7‐, C8‐HSL, or 3‐oxo‐C6‐HSL.[ 4 ] Similar complexes led to the inhibition of violacein production in Chromobacterium violaceum CV026,[ 5 ] while the use of β‐CD immobilized on microspheres led to 30% inhibition of prodigiosin production in Serratia marcescens. [ 6 ] More recently, Molnàr et al. conducted a study on the impact of native CDs on quorum quenching in Aliivibrio fischeri, a bioluminescence model system, revealing significant differences in the inhibition of bioluminescence depending on the CD structure. For example, 10 mM of α‐CD resulted in 64% inhibition, while β‐CD and HPBCD were less efficient. A 1:1 stoichiometry interaction between the aliphatic side chain of 3‐oxo‐C6‐HSL and the host cavity was observed, though with modest association constant values. The cavity of γ‐CD appeared to be too large to form any complex.[ 7 ] The same group recently reported the inhibition of the production of the pyoverdine pigment in Pseudomonas aeruginosa in the presence of 10 mM of α‐CD and β‐CD.[ 8 ] Some modified β‐CDs (HPBCD, randomly methylated β‐CD, RAMEB, and sulfobutylether‐β‐CD, SBEBCD) were found to be less active than native CDs. The same group also studied the impact of various CDs on the growth, viability, and formation of biofilms in Candida biodinii, with the best results observed for native α‐CD and β‐CD.[ 9 ]

Palmer and coworkers investigated inclusion complexes between β‐CD and acyl‐functionalized AHL containing highly hydrophobic aromatic groups.[ 10 ] The obtained 1:1 complexes, which exhibited enhanced stability in phosphate buffer, were evaluated with respect to their ability to prevent the virulence of wild‐type Pseudomonas aeruginosa in a Caenorhabditis elegans host infection study. A few other studies have focused on modified CDs and their effect on QS. Wu and coworkers recently reported biofilm carriers with the property of increasing C6‐HSL production. They demonstrated that the presence of modified β‐CD grafted onto a polymeric support enhanced the efficiency of the system.[ 11 ] Kato et al. investigated the effect of mono‐6‐alkylacylamino‐β‐CD derivatives with chains ranging from C3 to C18 on the inhibition of prodigiosin production in Serratia marcescens. [ 12 ] They observed that compounds with a chain between C7 and C12 exhibited superior effects in comparison to the native β‐CD, suggesting a relationship between aqueous solubility, self‐inclusion of the alkyl chain, inclusion properties of AHLs, and inhibitory activities. Molnar recently reported water‐soluble CD derivatives with enhanced binding capabilities, such as monosubstituted α‐ and β‐CDs possessing alkylthio moieties and derivatized with 2–4 quaternary ammonium groups, which led to luminescence decrease in Aliivibrio fischeri, with mono 6S‐C12‐α‐CD and 6S‐C12‐β‐CD being the most.[ 13 ]

The design of CD conjugates by grafting molecules of interest (carbohydrates, peptides, nucleosides, phospholipids, etc.) onto a CD scaffold to enhance their properties is another interesting viewpoint for involving CDs.[ 14 , 15 ] To the best of our knowledge, the covalent coupling of AHLs and CDs has never been described. This study hypothesized that CDs‐AHL hybrids might exhibit activity on LuxR‐regulated bioluminescence and that this activity might be modulated as a function of the CD structure. This is the purpose of the present study, which reports the synthesis of the first CDs‐AHL conjugates varying the type (α or β) and hydrophilicity (native or methylated), their structural characterization, their solubility, and self‐organization properties, and their effect on the bioluminescence in a light‐producing bacterium model.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis

The synthesis of the new per‐AHL‐CDs was based on a well‐controlled click‐chemistry route from per‐azido‐CDs and propargyl ligands.[ 16 , 17 ] This strategy has proven to be highly effective; however, AHLs being inherently fragile compounds, it is imperative to ensure the integrity of the AHL moiety, avoiding the opening of the lactone and any racemization. Furthermore, the total substitution of all 6‐positions of CD moieties in good yield and with high purity of the final products remains a challenge in the field of CD chemistry. The effect of multivalence can be studied by comparison with the products obtained from the monoazido‐CDs, which are also accessible.[ 18 ] Among the three commercially available CDs, the α‐ and β‐CDs are the most rigid ones, generally leading to the best scaffolds for multivalence effects. Moreover, they are the CDs previously used in QS‐related studies.

First, the per‐6‐azido‐6‐deoxy α‐ 2 A and β‐CD 2 B were synthesized from α‐ and β‐CD, respectively, in two steps via iodination (74–86% yield) and substitution of the iodo group by the azide with 94% yield (Scheme 1a). As the methylation of CDs can considerably change the behavior of the CD in water,[ 19 ] the methylation of 2 A and 2 B was carried out in classical basic conditions with iodomethane in organic medium, leading to per‐6‐azido‐6‐deoxy‐2,3‐di‐O‐methyl α‐ 3 A and β‐CD 3 B in 80–83% yield.

Synthesis of a) per‐6‐azido‐6‐deoxy α‐ and β‐CD 2 A,B and their methylated derivatives 3 A, B and b) mono‐6‐azido‐6‐deoxy β‐CD 5.

The mono‐6‐azido‐6‐deoxy β‐CD 5 was prepared by monotosylation followed by the substitution of the tosyl group by an azido group in 21% overall yield (Scheme 1b). The monotosyl β‐CD 4 was obtained using 1‐(p‐toluenesulfonyl)imidazole in basic aqueous conditions, as described in the.[ 20 ] This method was chosen for its fast reaction processing. The compounds were fully characterized and the data comply with the literature.[ 16 , 17 ]

Among the numerous structures of AHL described in the literature,[ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 – 25 ] the C6‐HSL is analogous the autoinducer 3‐oxo‐hexanoyl homoserine lactone (OHHL). Furthermore, its analog having a terminal alkynyl group was reported and can easily be clicked by copper‐catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC).[ 26 ] Compound 7 was synthesized in two steps with 66% overall yield by amidation of l‐homoserine lactone hydrobromide obtained from l‐methionine following literature protocols (Scheme 2 ).[ 24 ] As a pure L‐amino acid was used as starting material, special attention was paid to the enantiomeric purity of the product, avoiding racemization during the peptidic coupling by using HOBt instead of PyBOP.[ 26 ] The enantiomeric purity of 7 was confirmed by polarimetry.

Synthesis of a)5‐hexynoyl‐l‐homoserine lactone 7.

The coupling between the alkynyl AHL 7 and the per‐6‐azido‐6‐deoxy α‐ 2 A and β‐CD 2 B and their methylated derivatives 3 A and 3 B , respectively, was performed using CuAAC click reaction under microwave activation (Scheme 3a). We have already demonstrated the efficiency of such conditions to per modify α‐ and β‐CD in only 1 h.[ 16 , 17 ] The solvent of the reaction is DMF, which is appropriate for both hydroxylated and methylated CDs, allowing an identical procedure for the four CD derivatives. We did not observe any opening of the lactone ring of homoserine lactone derivatives in the reaction conditions, proved by the absence of signals corresponding to the open forms in the mass spectra (Figure 1 and 2 ). The solvent was evaporated and the methanol was used to resolubilize the crude product.

The high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) analyses indicated the presence of remaining copper (I) salts in the mixture even after a few washings (Figure 1 bottom, S71, Supporting Information).

All these compounds exhibit a high affinity for copper, which must be removed prior to the biological evaluation. It is indeed well‐established that copper is toxic to cells and bacteria when present in minimum inhibitory concentrations exceeding 140 µM.[ 27 ] A resin SiliaMetS Imidazole (IMI) was thus used to remove the copper by adsorption, as described by Paolino et al.[ 28 ] After this treatment, the mass spectra indicate that the compounds do not exhibit any copper adducts, as illustrated on Figure 1 top, confirming the efficiency of this purification method. To further assay in more depth the presence or absence of copper, a Cu+ detection study was conducted by HRMS, in which a 2 µM solution of CuI (corresponding to the maximum solubility in water) was added to a 29 µM solution of pure 9 A . In such conditions, the presence of multiple adducts between 9 A and Cu+ is once again observed (see Figure S75, Supporting Information). Consequently, it can be deduced that after purification on resins, 9 A contains less than 0.07 eq of Cu+, which is well below its minimum inhibitory concentration.[ 27 ]

The four per‐AHL‐CDs 9 A , 9 B (free hydroxyl groups) and 10 A , 10 B (O‐methyl groups) were purified by preparative high performance liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (HPLC‐MS) using a C4 reverse‐phase column eluted with a water/methanol gradient. A C18 column was first used, but resulted in too long retention times and improper separation of the products. The C4 column with less grafted surface area, thus lower affinity of the compounds and shorter retention time, allowed more efficient elution and separation. The four new compounds were fully characterized by 1H, 13C, correlated spectrocopy, heteronuclear single‐quantum coherence (HSQC) NMR, and high‐resolution mass spectrometry experiments (Figure S43–S62, Supporting Information).

HPLC and NMR analysis demonstrated that the four compounds are pure, and exist each as a unique diastereoisomer (Figure 2). NMR data were characteristic of the 1,2,3‐triazole formation, with the disappearance of the alkyne signals and appearance of the new CH signals of the triazole group (for example, for 9 B 1H 7.7 ppm; 13C 123 ppm) and the quaternary carbon (146 ppm). These patterns indicate a C4 substitution of the triazole ring.[ 29 ] In addition, the presence of a unique set of signals, such as those at 47.8 ppm assigned to C7’ of 9 B , indicates the presence of a single diastereoisomer.

Two other compounds were synthesized to study the structure/property relationships. The mono‐AHL‐β‐CD 11 was targeted to evaluate a multivalence effect using 9 B . It was prepared by CuAAC coupling of the mono‐6‐azido‐6‐deoxy‐β‐CD 5 and AHL derivative 7 in the same reaction conditions (Scheme 3b). Compound 11, being less soluble in organic solvents, it was recovered by precipitation in acetonitrile, filtered, and washed with this solvent to lead to 80% yield. No HPLC purification was necessary. The structure of this new compound was determined by NMR and mass spectrometry (Figure S63–S66, Supporting Information).

Finally, the last new hybrid prepared in this series was the per‐[6‐deoxy‐6‐(N‐ethyl‐5‐butynamide)triazol‐1‐yl]‐β‐CD 12, keeping the triazolyl chain but missing the lactone moiety, designed to evaluate the importance of the AHL in all other compounds. The N‐ethyl‐5‐hexynamide 8 was first synthesized (Scheme 3c) from ethylamine and hexynoic acid with EDC as a coupling agent with 80% yield. The CuACC click chemistry reaction was then carried out between alkyne 8 and the per‐6‐azido‐6‐deoxy‐β‐CD 2 B in the same conditions as for previous click reactions. Compound 12 was then isolated by precipitation in ethyl acetate, followed by filtration and washing and obtained in 75% yield.

Solubility Studies

The solubility of compounds 7 and 9– 12 in pure water at 20 °C was assessed. This assessment entailed the integration of dedicated signals from the 1H NMR spectra, with β‐CD serving as the reference sample.

The measured solubilities are reported in Table 1 . The compound 7, which is characterized by a global hydrophobicity, exhibits poor solubility in water, as expected. The solubility of the modified derivative 9 A (per‐AHL‐α‐CD) was measured as 2.6 mM, thus ≈50 times less soluble than the native α‐CD (150 mM) under the same conditions. This is in agreement with the grafting of six hydrophobic moieties in the primary position. Surprisingly, the persubstituted β‐CD analogs 9 B and 12 showed a solubility in the range of 31 mM, i.e., twice as much as the value for native β‐CD (16 mM). It should also be noted that hybrid 9 B exhibits higher aqueous solubility than AHL 7 (1.1 mM) or β‐CD alone. In contrast, the monosubstituted analog 11 is only slightly soluble in water (5.2 mM). The increase in solubility of 9 B and 12 could be explained by a modification of the inter‐residue hydrogen bonding network of OH2 and OH3 secondary hydroxyls. As anticipated, per‐methylation of the free hydroxyl groups (compounds 10 A and 10 B ) led to a decrease in solubility in water (8.7 mM).

Conformational Studies by NMR

The 1H NMR spectra of compounds 9 A,B, and 10 A,B were obtained in D2O and DMSO‐d6 under identical experimental conditions. A comparison of the spectra obtained for 9 A and 9 B (Figure S72–S73, Supporting Information) shows well‐resolved spectra which are very similar in terms of chemical shift values and resolution. Only the CD signals appear slightly broadened in water and in DMSO‐d6. The same observations were made with derivatives 10 A,B . This demonstrates the absence of aggregation and self‐inclusion for these compounds in water and DMSO, the latter being known to be unfavorable to such interactions. The next steps of the NMR study were carried out in DMSO‐d6 because all hydroxyl signals could be differentiated and all compounds were fully soluble. To better understand the effect of persubstitution on CD, the 1H NMR spectra of per‐azido β‐CD 2 B , per‐AHL‐β‐CD 9 B , and per‐ethylamide‐β‐CD 12 were compared (Figure 3 ). The grafting of the hydrophobic moiety to compound 2 B by "click chemistry" resulted in the expected strong downfield shift of the signals of the H6 and H5 protons (+0.6 ppm), but also, to a lesser extent, of the H1, H3, and H4 protons. Only the H2 proton signal showed an upfield shift (−0.12 ppm).

Partial 1H NMR spectra of 2 B (top), 9 B (middle), and 12 (bottom) (600 MHz, 298 K, DMSO‐d6).

This would be consistent with a change in the hydrogen bonding network at the OH2 position for compounds 9 B and 12.[ 30 ]

In order to assess the impact of coupling AHL appendages on the CD on the self‐organization properties of the conjugates, 2D diffusion ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) and Rotating frame Overhauser Effect Spectroscopy (ROESY) NMR experiments were carried out under identical experimental conditions on each individual component, i.e., AHL 7 and the native β‐CD, as well as on the corresponding hybrid 9 B . The results of the DOSY experiments are illustrated in Figure 4 . The D value of 7 is approximately twice as much as the value for β‐CD, which is itself twice the D value of 9 B . These observations are consistent with the relative size (or molar mass) of each compound, eliminating the hypothesis of the self‐organization of 9 B in solution.

Overlay of DOSY experiments of 9 B (top), β‐CD (middle), and 7 (bottom) (600 MHz, 298 K, DMSO‐d6).

In the ROESY experiment, it is possible to distinguish between Rotating Overhauser Effect (ROE), which indicates spatial proximity between two protons, and chemical exchange, which indicates hydrogen bonding or conformational transitions, by the correlation's phase (or sign). Cross peaks due to ROE are of opposite phase to the diagonal for all molecules since those due to chemical exchange are of the same phase.[ 31 ] Figure 5 (top side figure) displays the ROESY experiment performed on compound 7: only correlations (in red) of the opposite sign to the diagonal (in blue) are observed, corresponding to ROE correlations.

Partial ROESY experiments of 7 (top), 9 B (bottom) (600 MHz, 298 K, DMSO‐d6).

Additionally, in Figure 5 (bottom side figure), the ROESY experiment performed on per‐AHL‐β‐CD 9 B shows the same correlations but also correlations with the same sign as the diagonal, corresponding to chemical exchanges. One part is attributed to interactions between H2O and the OH2 and OH3 hydroxyls of the CD and the other part to a conformational exchange between the H8 and H9 protons of the lactone. A further comparison of the ROESY experiments of 9 B and native β‐CD (Figure 5 and S74, Supporting Information), carried out under identical conditions, reveals notable differences. (Figure S74, Supporting Information) exclusively displays strong ROE correlation peaks characteristic of native β‐CD: in contrast to compound 9 B , no exchange cross‐peaks are observed between the OH2 and OH3 hydroxyls and the water signal. In addition, strong cross‐peaks are observed between the H1 proton and the H2 proton of the same glucose unit and the H4 proton of the following unit, which is not observed for compound 9 B . All these observations are consistent with a conformational change in the CD moiety after grafting the AHLs.

Modulation of LuxR‐Regulated Bioluminescence

The study of modulation of LuxR‐regulated bioluminescence production was achieved using a reported strain E. coli DH5α equipped with a pSB401 plasmid responding to the A. fischeri natural ligand OHHL and analogs. The sensitivity of the AHL receptors in A. fischeri for its cognate autoinducer OHHL has been determined previously (Table 2 ).[ 32 ]

First, the ability of the modified CDs to induce the LuxR‐regulated bioluminescence was assessed at concentrations ranging from 1 to 200 µM (Figure 6 ). The per‐AHL‐β‐CD 9 B was found to induce bioluminescence with an EC50 value of 64 µM. The per‐AHL‐α‐CD 9 A was 8 times less efficient, while the methylated analogs 10 A and 10 B exhibited approximately four‐ and twofold decreased activity, respectively, compared to 9 B (Table 2). The per‐ethylamide modified β‐CD 12 and the mono‐AHL‐β‐CD 11 used as control were found to be deprived of any activity (Figure 6). It should be noted that native α‐CD and β‐CD, along with permethylated analogs per‐Me‐αCD and per‐Me‐βCD did not exhibit any activity either (Table 2). The per‐AHL‐β‐CD 9 B appears to be more active than the α‐CD counterpart. Moreover, the methylated analog of 9 B appears less active, even if 10 B exhibits better activity than the α‐CD counterparts 9 A or 10 A . The presence of AHL moieties was found to be crucial in this context, as evidenced by the absence of activity of the per‐ethylamide‐β‐CD 12. Finally, the mono‐AHL β‐CD 11 was found 12 times less efficient than per‐AHL‐β‐CD 9 B , bearing seven AHL moieties.

Bioluminescence induction of modified CDs, 9A (), 10A (), 9B (), 10B (), 7 (), 11 (), and12 (). Error bars correspond to standard deviation from triplicate.

Then, the ability of the modified CDs, and of compound 7 for comparison, to inhibit the LuxR‐regulated bioluminescence induced by OHHL (200 nM) was examined (Figure 7 ). The modified β‐CD 9 B reduced bioluminescence with an IC50 value of 10–20 µM while compound 7 exhibited an IC50 value of 1–4 µM. Thus, the persubstituted β‐CD 9 B can reduce bioluminescence, in contrast to other modified CDs used in this study which were found to be significantly less active (Table 2).

The dual ability to induce and inhibit bioluminescence depending on the concentration has already been reported for other AHL‐analogs.[ 33 ] None of the other conjugates, either the α‐CD derivative 9 A , the per‐ethylamide‐β‐CD 12, the mono‐AHL derivative 11, or the methylated CD conjugates 10 A and 10 B induced any inhibition.

Among the CD‐AHL hybrid molecules, per‐AHL‐β‐CD (9 B ) exhibited a particularly strong effect on regulating the QS. At this stage, several structural factors appear to be crucial for this activity: the presence of the AHL moiety and its multivalency, the nature of the CD, and the presence of free secondary hydroxyl groups. It was observed that 9 B demonstrated the highest solubility in pure water, while 12 did not show any activity in biological media, thereby preventing a direct correlation between solubility and activity.

The ability of CD hybrids to modulate bioluminescence production was clearly demonstrated by either inhibiting and/or inducing bioluminescence, depending on their structure. This phenomenon prompts the question of the underlying mechanism, which remains to be elucidated. One hypothesis suggests that CD hybrids interact with bacterial membrane, as previously mentioned in the literature. For instance, γ‐CDs bearing eight alkylamino groups at the primary position, through the implementation of a click reaction, have been shown to possess antibacterial activity against both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria.[ 34 , 35 ] In Gram‐negative bacteria, the membrane‐permeabilizing action of these derivatives permitted the entry of cotreated traditional antibiotics, which subsequently became active against these bacteria. Furthermore, other investigations focused on the use of "Trojan Horse" nanocarriers for antibiotics to enhance the activity of antibiotics against susceptible and resistant bacteria.[ 36 ] Antibiotic carriers are prepared from β‐CD grafted with sugar molecules (d‐mannose and d‐glucose, respectively) via azide–alkyne click reaction. The sugar molecules function as a chemoattractant, drawing in higher amounts of the antibiotic and leading to the rapid killing of the bacteria.

Conclusion

Several CD‐AHLs conjugates were successfully synthesized and purified. The study of modulation of LuxR‐regulated bioluminescence production showed that per‐AHL‐β‐CD 9B was not only able to induce bioluminescence with an EC50 value of 64 µM but also to reduce bioluminescence with an IC50 value of 10–20 µM. This initial investigation suggests that AHL‐β‐CD conjugates have improved efficacy compared to α‐CD analogs. The presence of free secondary hydroxyl groups and the persubstitution of CD by the AHL moieties appear to be crucial. In order to better understand the phenomena and the mechanism involved, the synthesis of new derivatives is underway to assess the relevance of linker length and triazole function in modulating LuxR‐regulated bioluminescence production. In future studies, a potential Trojan horse strategy based on the inclusion properties of these CD hybrids, such as 9B, by formation of inclusion complexes with dedicated guests, will also be explored to give insight into the mechanism of action of these compounds against Gram‐negative bacteria.

Experimental Section

Materials

Sigma–Aldrich, Fluka, Acros, Silicycle, and TCI supplied reagents, and solvents, respectively. Cyclohexakis‐(1 → 4)‐α‐D‐glucopyranosyl (α‐CD, MW = 972.85 g mol−1, Cavamax W6 Pharma, purity ≥ 98.0%) and Cycloheptakis‐(1 → 4)‐β‐D‐glucopyranosyl (β‐CD, MW = 1135.0 g mol−1, Cavamax W7 Pharma, purity ≥ 98.0%) were supplied by Wacker Chemie AG (Munich, Germany). Cyclohexakis‐(2,3,6‐tri‐O‐methyl)‐(1 → 4)‐α‐D‐glucopyranosyl (per‐Me‐α‐CD, MW = 1225.32 g mol−1, purity ≥ 98.0%) was supplied by Glentham Life Sciences (UK), Cycloheptakis‐(2,3,6‐tri‐O‐methyl)‐(1 → 4)‐β‐D‐glucopyranosyl (per‐Me‐β‐CD, MW = 1429.55 g mol−1, purity ≥ 98.0%) was supplied by Sigma–Aldrich AG (Germany). Purchased chemicals were used without further purification. Reaction solvents were dried and deoxygenated with an INERT PureSolv MD7.

The microwave (MW) reactions (CEM Discover, France) were carried out using a glassware configuration for atmospheric pressure reactions and a 10 mL Teflon reactor (measurements of the internal reaction temperature with an optical fiber sensor) and in dynamic mode with a preagitation of 2 min. The pressure was checked at 250 psi with a power of 100 W, and a temperature of 110 °C for 1 h. The reactions were monitored using thin layer analytical chromatography (TLC) with Silica gel 60 F254 plates, and detection was carried out using UV light at 254 nm or charring. This involved adding the sample to an ethanol solution containing sulfuric vanillin, followed by heating.

1H NMR (400 and 600 MHz) and 13C NMR (101 and 151 MHz) spectra were recorded at 298 K using AVANCE III DRX400 (Brüker) or AVANCE III DRX600 (Brüker) spectrometers, respectively. Chemical shifts (δ) are expressed in ppm. Data acquisition was performed with TopSpin and processing with MestReNova softwares. Samples were dissolved in deuterated solvent (CD3OD, CDCl3, DMSO‐d 6 , and D2O) purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. The proton and carbon signal assignments were determined from decoupling experiments, as well as Cozy, HSQC, and HMBC spectra.

High‐resolution electrospray mass spectrometry (ESI‐HRMS) was operated on a Synapt G2‐Si Q‐TOF high‐resolution hybrid Mass Spectrometer provided with an ESI ionization source (Waters, Manchester, UK). Data acquisition and processing were performed with MassLynx software (V4.1, Waters).

To evaluate the Cu adduct formation by HRMS, a saturated CuI solution in H2O was prepared (0.00042 mg mL−1; 2.20 µM). Compound 9 A (1 mg) was dissolved in 1.5 mL of a 1:1 H2O/MeOH mixture (290 µM). The samples that were analyzed—with and without Cu+—were prepared by first diluting the 9 A stock solution 10‐fold. This was done using either H2O or the saturated CuI solution (100 into 900 µL).

Optical rotations were measured by an Anton Paar MCP 100 polarimeter at 20 or 25 °C in a 10 cm cell with chloroform as solvent. [α]D values are given in deg mL dm−1 g−1 (concentration c given as g dL−1). Melting points were determined using a Stuart SMP10 apparatus.

The purification of compounds containing copper after the click reaction was carried out with Silicycle (IMI (R79230B)) resin. Also, the compounds were purified by chromatography on silica gel 60 (230 mesh) or by the auto‐purification system with a 1525 binary pump coupled with a single quadrupole mass spectrometer SQD2 (Waters‐Micromass, Manchester, UK) equipped with an electrospray ion source (ESI‐MS). The separation was performed at room temperature on a Fuji SPS‐300‐5‐C4T (250, 21 mm, 5 μm) column using a sample injection volume of 600 μL (solution of crude mixture at 80 g L−1 in DMSO). The following mobile phase gradient at a flow rate of 23 mL min−1 was used : A) H2O + 0.1% AF (formic acid), B) MeOH + 0.1% AF; (A:B): 95:5 (t = 0 min), 95:5 (t = 5 min), 0:100 (t = 40 min), 0:100 (t = 40.1 min), 0:100 (t = 50 min), 95:5 (t = 50.1 min), and 95:5 (t = 60 min). An in‐line splitter was set at 1/1000 to reduce the flow toward the ESI‐MS source. The ESI source was operated in the positive ionization mode using a capillary voltage of 2.53 kV and the following conditions: cone voltage, 60 V; source temperature, 150 °C; desolvation gas temperature, 350 °C; desolvation gas flow, 500 L h−1; and cone gas flow, 50 L h−1. Nitrogen (>99.5%) was employed as the desolvation gas. Both [M + 2H]2+ and [M + 3H]3+ were used to detect and auto‐collect the desired compounds. Data acquisition and processing were performed with the MassLynx V4.1 software.

Preliminary solubility tests were performed as follows: small amounts of the desired product (≈5 mg) were placed in a vial, and MilliQ‐water was added in volumes of 20 μL until complete solubilization of the product was observed with the naked eye without stirring. Solubility tests were carried out according to the following protocol. Concentrated solutions (2 mM of 7, 10 mM of 9 A , 10 A,B, and 11, 40 mM of 9 B and 12) of the previously lyophilized products are prepared in D2O and left to stir overnight. The product is vortexed and then centrifuged. The supernatant is collected and analyzed by NMR (main experimental parameters: 600 MHz, TD 128 k, NS 128, RG 18, SW 20.0 ppm, AQ 5.45 s,and D1 5 s). A 5 mM native β‐CD solution is used as a reference. The absolute values of the integration of the peaks of the reference β‐CD are then compared with the peaks of the analyzed products (H1’, H1) for the hybrids and (H8’A, H9’A) for alkynyl‐AHLs to determine their concentration in D2O. The solubility values of commercial CDs (α‐CD, β‐CD, per‐Me‐α‐CD, and per‐Me‐β‐CD) in water, at 20 °C, have been extracted from the specifications provided by Wacker Chemie AG.

Biological Evaluation: Modulation of LuxR‐Regulated Bioluminescence

Bioluminescence induction and inhibition assays were achieved using the sensor strain Escherichia coli. E. coli DH5α equipped with the plasmid pSB401 for bioluminescence production by responding to exogenous OHHL, MW = 241.28 g mol−1, purity ≥ 97% was supplied by Sigma Aldrich AG (Germany). Assays were performed in 96 well plates in triplicate in the absence (control) or the presence of various concentrations of the tested compound with a maximum of 2% DMSO (v/v). Tested compounds’ samples were prepared in DMSO solutions.

Biological Evaluation: Induction of Bioluminescence

The sensor strain pSB401 was inoculated in 5 mL of fresh Luria–Bertani broth (LB) supplemented with 10 μg mL− 1 tetracycline and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. The strain was then diluted 1:10 in LB and 0.1 mL was added to 0.1 mL of fresh LB without or with the tested compounds at concentrations ranging from 1 to 200 µM with a maximum of 2% DMSO (v/v, final volume 0.2 mL). The plates were placed at 30 °C for 5 h and bioluminescence and optical density OD600 were recorded using a Tecan Spark microplate reader.

Biological Evaluation: Inhibition of Bioluminescence

The sensor strain pSB401 was inoculated in 5 mL of fresh LB supplemented with 10 μg mL− 1 tetracycline and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. The strain was then diluted 1:10 in LB and OHHL was added to obtain a final concentration of 200 nM in the final volume of 0.2 mL. This solution (0.1 mL) was added to 0.1 mL of fresh LB without or with the tested compounds at concentrations ranging from 1 to 200 µM with a maximum of 2% DMSO (v/v, final volume 0.2 mL). The plates were placed at 30 °C for 5 h and bioluminescence and optical density OD600 were recorded with a Tecan Spark microplate reader.
