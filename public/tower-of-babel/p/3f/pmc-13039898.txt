Aescin and rutin mitigate hyperthyroidism-induced multisystem dysfunction via antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms in a rat model
Authors: Minnatullah A. Makadi, Ibrahim A. Ibrahim, Hoda I. Bahr, Amr Elkelish, Zongo Oumarou, Hassan Rudayni, Amal A. M. Ahmed, Yasmina K. Mahmoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperthyroidism triggers systemic complications, including oxidative stress, dyslipidemia, hepatic injury, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular dysfunction. Conventional treatments such as Propylthiouracil (PTU) often exhibit adverse effects, highlighting the need for safer alternatives. We evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of the natural bioactives aescin and rutin, alone and in combination with PTU, in L-thyroxine–induced hyperthyroid rats. Comprehensive analyses included thyroid hormone levels, metabolic and hepatic biomarkers, oxidative stress indices, and histopathological/immunohistochemical evaluations of liver, heart, and pancreas. Both compounds significantly restored thyroid function by reducing FT3 and FT4 and normalizing TSH levels. Treatments improved lipid and glucose profiles, attenuated hepatic enzyme leakage, reduced proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α), and enhanced antioxidant defense (↑TAC, ↓MDA). Histopathology and immunohistochemistry indicated structural recovery and reduced apoptosis/oxidative stress. Aescin, particularly in combination with PTU, demonstrated superior efficacy compared to rutin or PTU monotherapy. Aescin and rutin exhibit strong antithyroid, antioxidant, hepatoprotective, cardioprotective, and neuroprotective effects. These phytocompounds may serve as potential adjuncts or alternatives to conventional antithyroid drugs, with potential translational implications for hyperthyroidism management.

Introduction

The thyroid gland, resembling a butterfly, is located on the trachea at the anterior aspect of the neck1. It holds significant importance as a vital endocrine organ that synthesizes and secretes thyroid hormones2 and possesses a crucial role in regulating energy and metabolism3. Moreover, they interact with specific receptors present in various tissues throughout the body4,5.

Since the thyroid axis regulates cellular development and metabolism in virtually all nucleated cells, disorders of thyroid function can lead to extensive systemic consequences.6,7. Globally, thyroid disease is the second most common metabolic condition, after diabetes8,9. Hyperthyroidism, characterized by excessive circulating thyroid hormones, affects 0.1% to 2.9% of the worldwide population, as reported by Abood and Alhussaynei10 and Wiersinga et al.11.

In addition to hormonal excess, hyperthyroidism is increasingly acknowledged as a multisystem condition associated with cardiovascular complications12, hepatic dysfunction13, insulin resistance and diabetes14, hyperlipidemia15, and neurological disorders6. Substantial evidence indicates that oxidative stress and inflammatory processes are intertwined in the pathophysiology of hyperthyroidism16. Oxidative stress can harm cellular structures, proteins, lipids, and deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), which, in turn, has been attributed to the initiation and progression of various diseases in hyperthyroid states17,18.

There are three efficacious therapy choices for hyperthyroidism, notably antithyroid medications (ATD), radioactive iodine (RAI), or surgery19. Propylthiouracil (PTU) and methimazole (MMI) are the two often recommended pharmacological agents employed in the treatment of hyperthyroidism20. Although effective in suppressing thyroid hormone synthesis, these treatments may be associated with adverse effects and may not effectively mitigate oxidative or inflammatory complications. This limitation has prompted growing interest in adjunctive strategies aiming at alleviating systemic adverse effects related to hyperthyroidism21,22.

The immediate correlation between oxidative damage and disease progression underscores the importance of exploring antioxidant approaches in the management of hyperthyroidism23,24. There are many naturally occurring antioxidants, each with its own unique composition, physical and chemical characteristics, mechanisms, and action sites25. Among these, flavonoids and saponins are recognized for their notable antioxidant and cytoprotective properties. Rutin, an edible flavonoid, possesses various medicinal effects, most of which are attributed to its potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, cardioprotective, vasodilatory, immunomodulatory, and hepatoprotective properties26–28. Regarding thyroid gland disorders, some recent studies have indicated that the flavonoid rutin may impair thyroid function29,30.

Aescin is the primary bioactive molecule in horse chestnut seeds, with a chemical structure closely related to triterpene glycosides31. Aescin exhibits a range of therapeutic effects, including antioxidant, antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, vasoprotective, antibacterial, antipyretic, analgesic, and antiaging properties32,33.

Aescin has demonstrated evidence of its clinically noteworthy efficacy in the therapy of thyroid cancer34, liver dysfunction35, hyperlipidemia36, diabetes37, cardiac vein insufficiency, post-operative edema38, cardiac damage39, brain trauma40, and neurobehavioral disorders41.

Despite evidence supporting the antioxidant and partial antithyroid effects of rutin, its role in mitigating systemic complications of hyperthyroidism remains unclear. Moreover, aescin has been scarcely investigated in this context. Therefore, this study aimed to comparatively evaluate the therapeutic potential of rutin and aescin, alone and in combination with PTU, in attenuating multisystem dysfunction associated with experimental hyperthyroidism.

Material and methods

Drugs and chemicals

Tablets of Euthyrox (levothyroxine sodium) were purchased from Merck KGaA Co., Germany. Thiouracil (Propylthiouracil) was obtained from Amoun Pharmaceutical Co., Egypt. Reparil tablets (Aescin) were purchased from Madaus GmbH, Germany. Rutin was ordered from NOW Foods Co., USA.

Animals

56 non-medicated, clinically healthy adult male Swiss albino rats (150 to 200 g) were gathered from the animal house of the Medical Research Center, Faculty of Medicine, Ain Shams University. The rats were housed in groups in conventional cages with a routine light: dark (14 h:10 h) schedule and a monitored room temperature (27 ± 2°C), with unlimited access to food and water. Animals were maintained for two weeks for acclimation before the beginning of the study at the Animal House of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Suez Canal University. All the methods were conformed to the guidelines released by the National Research Council42. They were also officially approved by the research ethics committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Suez Canal University, Egypt (Approval number: 2022040). This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines for reporting animal research.

Experimental design

The experimental timeline consisted of a 20-day hyperthyroidism induction phase followed by a 30-day treatment phase. After 2 weeks of acclimatization, rats were allocated to the negative control and hyperthyroid-induced groups. Hyperthyroidism was induced by oral administration of euthyrox (levothyroxine sodium tablets, 240 μg/kg BW, dissolved in distilled water) daily for 20 days, as described by Chen et al.43. After 20 days, blood samples were collected and analyzed for free thyroxine (FT4), free triiodothyronine (FT3), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). Only animals that met the hyperthyroid criteria were included and subsequently randomized into the six treatment groups, with each group consisting of eight rats. Each group was housed in its cage and assigned according to the following categories, as shown in Table 1. All treatments were administered at the same time each day to minimize circadian variability. Experimental design.

Collection of blood and tissue samples

At the end of the experimental period and following overnight fasting, rats were anesthetized using an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg body weight) and xylazine (10 mg/kg body weight). The absence of pedal and corneal reflexes confirmed adequate depth of anesthesia. Blood samples were collected from the medial canthus using micro-hematocrit tubes.

Animals were then humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. Throughout the experimental period, animals were monitored daily for signs of distress, including severe lethargy, impaired mobility, abnormal posture, or inability to access food and water. Humane endpoints were predefined, and any animal exhibiting severe distress would have been immediately euthanized to minimize suffering.

Following euthanasia, liver, heart, and pancreas tissues were rapidly excised and fixed in buffered formalin-saline for subsequent histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses.

Thyroid hormonal profile

Thyroid serum hormonal profile (free T3, free T4, and TSH levels) was measured using rats’ reagent enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Catalog No. CSB-E05079r, CSB-E05076r, CSB-E05115r, respectively) obtained from Cusabio Biotech Co., China.

Biochemical analyses

Serum alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), creatine kinase (CK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activities, along with albumin (Alb), total proteins (TP), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), HDL-cholesterol (HDL-C), LDL-cholesterol (LDL-C) and fasting blood glucose (FBG) were evaluated spectrophotometrically using reagent kits (Catalog No. 46361, 46263, 46962, 47562, 41253, 41953, 47063, 47163, 41411, 41411S, 41411, 41411S and GL1320, correspondingly) purchased from clinichem Co., Hungary.

Immunoassay of insulin, inflammatory, and brain markers

Serum level of insulin was measured using rats’ reagent (ELISA) kits (Catalog No. BQ 130D), purchased from BQ KITS Co., USA. Serum levels of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin 6 (IL-6), dopamine (DA), and activity of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) were measured using rats’ reagent (ELISA) kits (CSB-E11987r, CSB-E04640r, CSB-E08660r, KT-5344, respectively), obtained from Cusabio Biotech Co., China, and Kamiya Biomedical Co., USA.

Insulin resistance index

Homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated using fasting insulin and blood glucose levels using the formula provided by Matthews et al.47.

HOMA-IR = fasting serum insulin in (μU/mL) x fasting blood glucose in (mg/dL)/405.

Oxidative stress markers

Tissue levels of total antioxidant capacity (TAC) and malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured by colorimetric/fluorometric assay kit (K274-100, K739-100, respectively) purchased from BioVision Co., USA.

Histopathology

According to the guidelines of Carson et al.48, the histological technique entails the dissection of liver, heart, and pancreas tissue specimens, which are washed with saline, immersed in a fixative, and subsequently processed for histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses. The fixative used was 4% paraformaldehyde, and the specimens were dehydrated, cleaned, and fixed in paraffin wax before sectioning at 5–7 μm thickness and subsequent mounting.

H&E histological sections were scored according to49 (Ming-wei Liu previously, 2018): pancreas was scored for edema, hemorrhage, inflammation, and necrosis, with a score of 0–4. A blinded investigator examined sections without knowledge of other data on the experimental groups.

Liver H&E sections were assessed for the detection of the number of lipid droplets. Ten sections from each group were analyzed using ImageJ, a software package developed by the National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, Maryland, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

The fixed tissues were processed using routine histological procedures and mounted on positively charged slides. Then, slides were subjected to immunostaining including deparaffinization, rehydration with a descending series of ethanol, antigen retrieval; incubation in citrate buffer, quenching of peroxidase activity, incubation with primary antibodies for B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) (Rabbit pAb antibody, Cat # A16776, Abclonal, Cummings Park, Woburn, USA) at dilution of 1:100 at 4 °C for about 24 h. was done, followed by rinsing in PBS. Also, slides were treated with primary antibodies for heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1). The Catalog number of the primary antibody for heme oxygenase-1 was Cat # A19062 (Abclonal, Cummings Park, Woburn, USA). Incubation with the peroxidase-labeled secondary antibodies, counterstaining with Harris hematoxylin, dehydration in alcohol, and mounting in DPX. The immunohistochemical examination was performed using a light microscope; images were captured with the attached digital camera (Olympus Dp25, Japan).

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 22.0 for Windows (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). The sample size (n = 8 per group) was selected based on commonly reported group sizes in similar experimental hyperthyroidism studies and to ensure sufficient power for detecting biologically relevant differences in biochemical and histopathological parameters. A one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple-range test was used for biochemical parameters. For histological and immunohistochemical quantifications, ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests was used to control for type I error in multiple comparisons. Data were assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance before ANOVA. Results are expressed as mean ± SE. The level of significance at which the null hypothesis was rejected was P ≤ 0.05. Figures were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Evaluation of serum thyroid profile

The present study demonstrated that the thyroid profile was significantly altered following the administration of euthyrox to healthy animals, resulting in the manifestation of a hyperthyroid state. It is noteworthy that PTU, rutin, and aescin therapy in hyperthyroidism-induced rats led to significant normalization of thyroid hormone levels, reflected by reductions in FT3 and FT4 and an increase in TSH levels. This effect was evidenced by significant downregulation of free T4 and free T3 levels, alongside upregulation of TSH toward values comparable to the control group. Measurement of serum inflammatory, cardiac, and brain markers

The level of proinflammatory markers (IL-6 and TNF-α) in HT rats were significantly elevated compared to that of control animals. Therapy with PTU, rutin, and aescin effectively lowered TNF-α and IL-6 levels in rats to levels comparable to those of healthy controls (Fig. 3). Cardiac and neurochemical biomarkers were also significantly altered in hyperthyroid-induced rats compared to the controls. Hyperthyroidism induced a substantial increase in serum CK and LDH activities compared to the control values. Concurrently, a marked reduction in AChE activity and a significant elevation in DA concentration were observed compared with the control group. Treatment with PTU alone partially ameliorated these alterations, as reflected by reductions in CK and LDH activities, along with modest improvements in AChE activity and DA levels (Table 4). Monotherapy with rutin or aescin resulted in further improvement, with the HT + Aescin group as evidenced by reduced CK and LDH activities, along with increased AChE activity and decreased DA compared with the hyperthyroid group. Combined treatments produced the most pronounced protective effects. Discussion

Hyperthyroidism is associated with various severe complications and subsequent diseases, including liver dysfunction13, dyslipidemia49, diabetes50, insulin resistance18, cardiovascular disorders51, neurological complications, and cognitive impairment52,53. While several natural antioxidants have been reported to mitigate thyroid dysfunction, rutin and aescin remain insufficiently investigated in hyperthyroid conditions54. Therefore, the present study provides several novel contributions by evaluating the possible antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiapoptotic, hepatoprotective, cardioprotective, and neuroprotective effects of supplementation with rutin and aescin, alone and in combination with the commercial drug propylthiouracil, in hyperthyroidism-induced rats.

To our knowledge, this is among the first trials to comprehensively assess aescin in this context and to compare its efficacy directly with rutin while investigating adjunctive interaction with standard antithyroid medication. The current work additionally adopts a multisystem perspective by simultaneously evaluating hepatic, cardiac, metabolic, inflammatory, and neurochemical alterations in experimental hyperthyroidism.

L-thyroxine administration successfully induced a hyperthyroid state, as evidenced by marked reduction in TSH and elevations in FT3 and FT4 levels, in agreement with previous studies by Samy et al.55, Aksu et al.56, and Arkalı et al.57. On the contrary, supplementation with rutin and aescin has remarkably reduced FT3 and FT4 levels and concurrently elevated TSH levels, resembling the effects observed in rats treated with PTU. This impact can be attributed to the redox-buffering and antioxidant activities of rutin and aescin58,59. It further suggests that rutin and aescin mimic the effects of the standard antithyroid medication PTU, potentially via mechanisms documented in prior studies, including modulation of 5′-deiodinase activity60,61.

Monotherapy with aescin and its combination with PTU have shown significant efficacy in reducing hyperthyroidism, surpassing rutin alone or in combination with PTU. While little information is available regarding the role of aescin in regulating hyperthyroidism, few researchers, such as Panda and Kar70, have suggested that the saponin component may inhibit thyroid hormone production and release at both glandular and peripheral levels. Interestingly, Mei et al.62 conducted a study to investigate the efficacy of aescin in the treatment of thyroid cancer, suggest substantial antitumor activity across several cancer cell lines.

Given that hyperthyroidism is known to exacerbate oxidative stress significantly. The liver is the most susceptible organ to pre-oxidative damage due to its high concentration of oxidizable substances; therefore, greater liver damage results in elevated secretion of liver enzymes63. These findings suggest a statistically significant elevation in serum AST and ALT activities, and a statistically significant reduction in serum TP and Alb levels in the hyperthyroid group. These results align with previous studies by Luo et al.73 and Zhao et al.74, indicating that hyperthyroid state and liver damage have been verified in hyperthyroidism-induced rats.

Multiple direct and indirect pathways have been suggested as the fundamental cause of liver dysfunction in hyperthyroidism. In brief, these factors encompass direct toxicity from prolonged thyroid hormones excess, autoimmune-mediated injury, heart failure–related hepatic necrosis, increased metabolic rate and oxidative stress, and potential hepatotoxicity from antithyroid medications64–67. The above factors contribute to increased leakage of liver enzymes and to decreased albumin and total protein levels. Decreased hepatic TAC, increased hepatic MDA, and histological alterations in hepatic tissue further supported these findings in the current study. The hepatic architecture of the hyperthyroid group displayed abundant macrovesicular lipid droplets and large intracytoplasmic lipid vacuoles, displacing the nuclei to the periphery. Moreover, inflammatory cells increased in the periportal area with lymphocytic infiltration. The increased Bcl-2 expression observed in the hyperthyroid group may represent a compensatory cellular survival response to oxidative and metabolic stress. Our findings coincide with the results of previous studies by Luo et al.73 and Zhao et al.68.

On the other hand, supplementation with PTU, rutin, and aescin significantly reduced serum ALT and AST activities and elevated serum Alb and TP levels compared with the hyperthyroid group. Indeed, PTU ameliorated liver damage to a lesser extent than rutin and aescin, consistent with the recent study by Zhao et al.68. These findings suggest that aescin outperforms rutin in improving liver function test results. These ameliorative properties of aescin against liver damage have been stated in various literature58,59. Moreover, the treated groups exhibited significant improvement in antioxidant status as evidenced by increased hepatic TAC and decreased hepatic MDA, thus supporting biochemical recovery. Hence, these findings suggest enhancement of liver functions with variable amounts in the treated groups could be attributed to improvement of animal’s antioxidant status. Although enzymatic antioxidant parameters including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) were not evaluated in the present study, the current results displayed promising antioxidant potentials of the current treatments. Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) evaluates the cumulative action of all antioxidants present in the biological system and correlates positively with overall antioxidant status69. Moreover, MDA, is an important marker of lipid peroxidation which is formed when ROS attack polyunsaturated fatty acids content of cell membranes70. Therefore, TAC together with MDA serves as a reliable indicator of the organism’s ability to counteract oxidative stress. Histopathological examination revealed restoration of hepatic architecture, with nearly regular cords and sinusoids and a marked decline in lipid droplets and lymphocytic infiltration. The normalization of Bcl-2 expression may indicate reduced cellular stress and improved tissue homeostasis; however, further validation using apoptosis-specific markers is required to draw definitive conclusions.

Dyslipidemia is a frequently occurring metabolic disorder in hyperthyroidism cases. It is the outcome of thyroid hormone’s impact on all aspects of lipid metabolism, resulting in quantitative and/or qualitative alterations in triglycerides, cholesterol, and other lipoproteins71. The estimated serum TC, TG, and LDL-C lipid markers were elevated in the hyperthyroid group. Still, HDL-C was considerably reduced. These results are consistent with those of various recent studies by Ashry et al.72, Ibrahim et al.73, and Yoon et al.74.

On the other hand, PTU has a minor impact on alleviating the hyperlipidemic state75. Conversely, rutin significantly reversed the atherosclerotic predisposing abnormalities associated with hyperthyroidism. It is supported by multiple studies by Chen et al.87, Livingston Raja et al.88, and Patil et al.89, and is credited with the swift elimination of chylomicron remnants from the bloodstream76. Aescin also showed a notable hypolipidemic effect, consistent with prior literature36,59,77. Moreover, our results revealed that aescin’s hypolipidemic effect surpasses that of rutin; it was even more effective when combined with PTU than rutin alone.

Thyroid hormones have long been known to have significant impacts on glucose homeostasis. There is a clear association between hyperthyroidism and the development of glucose intolerance and insulin resistance78. In the present trial, hyperthyroidism induced hyperglycemia and insulin resistance, as evidenced by significant increases in fasting blood glucose, insulin, and HOMA-IR in the hyperthyroid group compared to controls. This picture aligns with the prior findings of Ashry et al.81 and Karbalaei et al.79. Also, pancreatic tissue from hyperthyroid rats showed large, variably sized vacuoles with edema.

In the current study, rutin and aescin effectively normalized elevated blood glucose levels in hyperthyroid rats, bringing them to levels comparable to those of control animals. These results are consistent with those of Hsu et al.80, Hunyadi et al.81, and Jadhav and Puchchakayala82. However, PTU exhibited a minor effect on resolving the hyperglycemic state83. They were further indicated by the improvement of pathological changes in the treated groups. Furthermore, the combination of PTU and aescin suggest a synergistic effects, resulting in enhanced outcomes.

Thyroid dysfunction has been linked to inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor alpha and interleukin 684. It has been suggested that hyperthyroidism acts as a hormonal trigger for the activation of nuclear factor kappa, transcription factor involved in the regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-685,86. Studies conducted by Ajayi et al.87, Alnahdi et al.88, and Mannaa et al.89 reported elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-6 in rats hyperthyroid rats, consistent with the present findings showing significant increases in TNF-α and IL-6 in the hyperthyroid group.

Even though treatment with rutin and aescin led to a substantial decline in TNF-α and IL-6 concentrations, PTU therapy still slightly alleviated the inflammatory response, as reported by Akalin et al.90. A decrease in TNF-α and IL-6 concentrations owing to rutin has been reported previously in various cells and tissues91. These results are consistent with previous studies showing that TNF-α and IL-6 levels decreased following rutin treatment92–94. Despite the mechanism underlying the anti-inflammatory potential of rutin remaining unknown, it may be attributed to its capacity to suppress NF-κB signaling and improve cellular redox homeostasis94.

The anti-inflammatory properties of aescin have been indicated in many inflammatory diseases95–97. Its triterpene constituent imparts structural similarities to cortisol; this illustrates its glucocorticoid-like and anti-inflammatory actions98. Furthermore, previous studies suggest that aescin may modulate NF-κB signaling, thereby contributing to its anti-inflammatory effects127. Our findings align with prior research, indicating that aescin therapy significantly reduced TNF-α and IL-6 levels32,99,100.

Since the heart is an organ sensitive to thyroid hormones, even slight alterations in thyroid hormone levels can cause detectable changes in cardiac performance12. To evaluate the impact of hyperthyroidism on cardiac function, we examined the activity of circulating CK and LDH, which serve as indicators of cardiac function. Enzymatic activities increased significantly in the hyperthyroid group. These results are interpreted as a strong indication of injury or damage to heart tissue in the hyperthyroid state, consistent with prior investigations by Sridharan and Shyamaladevi101, Iqbal et al.102, and Revnic et al.103.

Our findings were indicated by decreased cardiac TAC and increased MDA in the present study, consistent with previous literature104,105. Sultana et al.17 also reported a notable decrease in total antioxidant capacity and a marked increase in malondialdehyde levels in hyperthyroid patients. Moreover, histopathological investigations in the hyperthyroid group suggested a extravasation of blood among cardiac myocytes, with wide spaces between cardiac fibers.

Given that rutin is notably marked by its therapeutically essential antioxidant polyphenols106,107. This resulted in restoration of the activities of the cardiac enzymes CK and LDH to nearly normal levels, and this comes along with the findings of previous literature accomplished by Topal et al.108, Xianchu et al.107, and Sengul et al.109. Nevertheless, rats treated with propylthiouracil had marginally altered levels of cardiac enzymes, highlighting the negligible efficacy of PTU in mitigating hyperthyroidism-induced cardiac complications110.

Furthermore, aescin substantially reduced cardiac enzymes to levels close to normal, indicating its potential to prevent membrane breakdown and subsequent enzyme leakage111,112. The results are in harmony with the findings of Panda et al.45 and Kaliyamoorthi et al.39. Our results were supported by increased cardiac TAC, decreased MDA, and the apparent amelioration of cardiac tissue in all treated groups. Aescin also suggested its effectiveness as a cardioprotective drug to a greater extent than rutin, especially in its combination with PTU.

Heme oxygenase-1 plays a vital cytoprotective role through its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiapoptotic properties. It is one of the key defense mechanisms activated in response to various cellular stress conditions, including ischemic stress113. Under oxidative stress, HO-1 expression is upregulated, enhancing cellular resilience through its protective functions114. Multiple studies have shown that thyroid hormones can increase ROS and induce heat shock proteins (Hsp70 and Hsp27) in unstressed rat hearts115,116. Therefore, it is plausible that thyroid hormones can upregulate HO-1 expression via ROS signaling. Our findings revealed that the hearts of the hyperthyroid group showed a substantial elevation of heme oxygenase-1 expression. However, all treated groups displayed a significant decrease in HO-1 expression. Still, aescin outperforms PTU and rutin in ameliorating HO-1 expression elevation and even surpasses rutin when combined with PTU.

Proper thyroid metabolism plays a critical role in regulating energy metabolism. It is also involved in central nervous system (CNS) activities, including the maintenance and development of neurons117,118. Thyroid hormones play a pivotal function in adult mammalian brain neurotransmission, as suggested by several studies119–121. Specifically, there is a strong correlation between the thyroid hormones, cholinergic, and dopaminergic functions in the brain122. Although thyroid disorders have been shown to affect AChE and DA activities in both developing and adult rats123,124, there is insufficient and inconsistent information on the effects of thyroid hormones on these enzymes’ activities.

In this study, we observed a decline in AChE activity. Carageorgiou et al.125 also noted a reduction in acetylcholinesterase activity throughout the entire brain during hyperthyroidism produced by T4. Overall, Phillips et al.126 found that a decrease in AChE activity was slightly associated with higher FT4 levels, but not with TSH levels. Thyroid hormones are known to influence dopaminergic neurons, with triiodothyronine and thyroxine acting as stimulatory factors127. Accordingly, hyperthyroidism significantly alters dopamine levels in several brain regions118. The findings from our experiment suggested that hyperthyroidism led to substantial increases in blood dopamine levels. In agreement with previous studies128–130, our findings suggested a marked increase in blood dopamine levels in hyperthyroid rats.

Propylthiouracil-treated rats have shown slightly altered levels of AChE and DA. We assumed that alleviation of hyperthyroidism by PTU administration would affect AChE and DA levels. Conversely, treatment with rutin and aescin has significantly elevated AChE activity compared with the hyperthyroid group. These effects may be ascribed to their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective capabilities as evidenced by various studies131,132.

Various studies have established a connection between the neuroprotective effects of phenolic compounds and their antioxidative133 and antiapoptotic properties. Flavonoids, such as rutin, can effectively manage neurodegenerative disorders by counteracting oxidative damage, a key factor in their development134–136. Aescin also has intriguing neuroprotective capabilities through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions137. It helps minimize oxidative stress and neuronal damage by suppressing proinflammatory cytokines and stabilizing cell membranes138,139. These mechanisms suggest that aescin may reduce neurodegenerative processes and maintain brain function. Notably, the augmented biochemical and histopathological profiles in the propylthiouracil-, rutin-, and aescin-treated groups (data not shown) were reflected in improved cognitive function in the open field and novel object tests compared with the hyperthyroid group140. Additionally, the absence of molecular pathway analysis limits mechanistic interpretation of the observed protective effects. The observed effects are likely multifactorial, involving redox modulation, anti-inflammatory signaling, and potential endocrine interactions.

Conclusions

N conclusion, rutin and aescin attenuated multiple pathological alterations associated with hyperthyroidism in an experimental model, primarily through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Aescin demonstrated comparatively greater efficacy, particularly when combined with PTU. These findings support the potential of these compounds as adjunctive therapeutic strategies. However, further studies are required to elucidate underlying mechanisms and confirm clinical applicability.
