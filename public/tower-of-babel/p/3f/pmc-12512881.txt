Accumulation patterns of sedentary and breaks and adiposity risk in Chinese children and adolescents: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Ting Li, Li‑Juan Wang, Ai-jun Cui, Yuwei Liu, Nan Zheng, Huan Chen, Chang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to identify and characterize the sedentary behavior (SED) and breaks accumulation patterns of children and adolescents and investigate the associations of these derived patterns with adiposity indicators.MethodsA total of 348 children and 562 adolescents from China participated in this study. Accelerometers were used to measure the bouts of SED and breaks. Adiposity indicators included body mass index (BMI) z-score, fat mass percentage (FM%), and fat mass index (FMI). Latent profile analysis was used to identify the SED and breaks accumulation patterns on the basis of 11 compositions of SED bouts and breaks. Mixed-effects multivariable linear regression models were used to analyze the associations of accumulation patterns with adiposity indicators.ResultsFour accumulation patterns were identified in children: “prolonged sitters” (N = 77, 22.1%), “shortened sitters” (N = 90, 25.9%), “LPA breakers” (N = 69, 19.8%), and “MVPA breakers”(N = 112, 32.2%). “MVPA breakers” had significantly lower BMI z-score, FM%, and FMI than “prolonged sitters.” No significant differences in adiposity indicators were observed among the other three patterns. In adolescents, “prolonged sitters” (N = 250, 44.5%), “moderate sitters” (N = 211, 37.5%), and “breakers” (N = 101, 18.0%) were identified. “Breakers” had the lowest BMI z-score, FM%, and FMI among the three groups, followed by “moderate sitters” and “prolonged sitters.”ConclusionDifferent accumulation patterns of SED and breaks were identified for children and adolescents in China. Among them, “MVPA breakers” and “Breakers” are most beneficial to maintain a normal weight status. Health promotion efforts could consider increasing MVPA and decreasing SED time for children and restricting SED to at least 30 min for adolescents to improve their adiposity indicators.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24540-z.Keywords: Sedentary behavior, Accumulation pattern, Adiposity, Latent profile analysis

Background

Overweight and obesity among children and adolescents have become a major public health issue in China over the past three decades [1]. An excessive amount of time spent in sedentary behavior (SED) is associated with increased adiposity in children and adolescents [2, 3]. However, recent experimental evidence showed that breaking up SED with short and sustained moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity (MVPA) and light intensity physical activity (LPA) bouts could modify the detrimental effects on adiposity indicators caused by SED in children and adolescents [4, 5]. A group of studies examined how the accumulation of SED and breaks, including SED bouts (i.e., a continuous period of SED time) and breaks bouts (i.e., a continuous period of MVPA or LPA time), is associated with adiposity of children and adolescents in isolation [5–7] to learn when SED time should be interrupted and the length and intensity of interruptions. Canabrava et al. [8] systematically reviewed 36 studies on the independent relationship between accumulation of SED and breaks and adiposity of children and adolescents. They found that short SED bouts and increased number of breaks were related to reduced BMI and waist circumference.

The above research focused on the independent relationship between SED bouts and breaks and health outcomes. However, these studies fail to consider the codependence between SED bouts and breaks. SED bouts and breaks intermittently occur within a day to form distinct accumulation patterns, and they are differently associated with health [9, 10]. For example, individuals who engage in prolonged sitting may have a sustained or short MVPA or LPA to break sitting and thus have distinct accumulation patterns, which may have specific associations with health outcomes. Therefore, some researchers recommended further research to identify patterns of SED bouts and breaks and explore how these accumulation patterns relate to health [9, 10].

One way to accomplish this goal is to use data-driven, person-centered statistical approaches, such as latent profile analysis [11]. Compared with commonly used variable-centered statistical models (e.g., regressions and ANOVAs), a notable advantage of the latent profile analysis is that different subgroups can be identified in a population that varies on the constituent components, thereby enabling a deeper understanding of the combined pattern of sedentary bouts and breaks in a population and designing tailored interventions according to specific subgroups’ behavior [12].

To the authors’ knowledge, only two studies have identified groups who share similar accumulation patterns of SED and breaks and examined associations between these patterns and health outcomes in children and adolescents by using latent profile analysis [9, 10]. Mitchell and Steele [10] examined the relationship between SED accumulation patterns and health-related quality of life of children aged 8–11 years in the U.S. Three profiles, including “Active” (highest time in ≥ 5 min MVPA bouts), “Inactive” (highest levels of ≥ 10 min SED bouts), and “Moderate” (moderate time in ≥ 5 min MVPA bouts and ≥ 10 min SED bouts) were identified. The majority of children (72.5%) fell into the “Moderate” profile, and the “Active” profile had significantly higher psychosocial health-related quality of life than the “Moderate” and “Inactive” profiles. Verswijveren et al. [9] focused on the association between accumulation patterns of SED and breaks and the adiposity of children aged 7–13 years in Australia. Three patterns emerged: “prolonged sitters,” characterized by the most time in sustained SED bouts and the least time in vigorous physical activity (PA) bouts; “breakers,” characterized by the highest frequency of SED breaks and lowest PA bouts; and “prolonged movers,” characterized by the least time in SED bouts and the most in PA bouts. Most children belonged to “breakers,” and the group with this pattern had lower adiposity risk than the other two groups.

Based on the limited evidence about the association between such accumulation patterns of SED and breaks and adiposity indicators of children and adolescents [9, 10], more studies are needed to understand it from other countries (e.g., China) or under other cultures. Much research evidence showed that rapid economic development and urbanisation have contributed to the increased prevalence of obesity among Chinese children and adolescents [13]. High academic demands often result in children spending a long time sitting down [14]. Moreover, with the increase in age or grade, the accumulation pattern of SED and breaks varies. Previous studies have reported that adolescents have more prolonged SED bouts and fewer breaks than children, because of higher academic pressure [15, 16]. Therefore, the association between SED and breaks accumulation and adiposity may differ between these age groups. Recognizing these potential differences can assist in devising targeted and effective interventions. This study aimed to (1) identify and characterize the accumulation patterns of SED and breaks in Chinese children and adolescents using latent profile analysis; and (2) investigate the associations between these derived patterns and adiposity indicators.

Method

Participants

The participants were recruited from two elementary (i.e., grades 1–6 with participants aged 6–13 years) and three secondary (i.e., grades 7–8 and 10–11 with participants aged 14–18 years) schools in Changsha and Wuhan, two cities located in the central part of China. Grade 9 and 12 students declined to participate in our study because they will attend the entrance examination of high schools or universities soon and have a tight academic learning schedule. One or two classes were randomly selected from each grade in each school. The sample size was calculated using Kendall’s sample size estimation method, ensuring it would be 5–10 times that of the number of variables [17]; the number of variables in this study was 17, and so the sample size was estimated to be 85–170. Considering the possibility of invalid questionnaires, the sample size was expanded by 20%, for a final sample size of 107–212. Moreover, to ensure reliable and precise subgroup results in LPA, a minimum of 300 participants is necessary [18]. All 1479 participants (436 children and 1043 adolescents) from 35 classes were invited to join the study, and a consent letter was sent to their parents. A total of 1394 participants agreed to participate. Out of them, 484 participants were excluded on the basis of the following exclusion criteria: (1) severe chronic diseases and physical disability (three participants); (2) accelerometer malfunction or loss (21 participants); (3) valid accelerometer data not covering at least two valid weekdays and one valid weekend day (73.1% efficiency, 375 participants); and (4) lack of adiposity indicators and confounder information (85 participants). In total, 910 children and adolescents participated in the study. No significant differences were found in the general characteristics between the excluded (n = 484) and included participants (n = 910). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (102772023RT090).

Measurements

SED and physical activity

Data were collected by the first author and two research assistants in June 2023. Actigraph wGT3X-BT accelerometers (Pensacola, FL, USA) were used to measure the SED and PA of the participants, which have been validated [19] and widely applied to measure PA of children and adolescent populations in China and other countries [20–22]. The participants were instructed to wear the accelerometer on the right hip during waking hours for 7 consecutive days, except for water-based activities and sleeping time. The sampling interval (epoch) was set at 60 s. While shorter epochs (e.g., 5–15 s) are more sensitive to short bursts of MVPA, they tend to overestimate sedentary breaks and misclassify subtle, non-purposeful movements, such as fidgeting while sitting [23, 24]. In contrast, the 60-second epoch offers more stable and conservative estimates of SED and has been recommended when the primary aim is to classify SED patterns [25]. Raw accelerometer data were downloaded and analyzed using Actilife software (version 6.13.3). Non-wear time was defined as ≥ 20 consecutive minutes of zero-intensity counts and subtracted from each day to determine wear time [26]. Participants with ≥ 3 valid days were included in further analysis, with each valid day defined as ≥ 10 h of wear time [27]. The cutoffs by Evenson et al. [28] were adopted to determine the different intensity levels, including SED ≤ 100 counts/min (CPM), LPA (101–2295 CPM), and MVPA (≥ 2296 CPM), which was a valid and reliable measure and applied in a wider age range (5–18 years) [29, 30]. The total time spent in each of these intensities was averaged over all valid days.

SED bouts and breaks

SED bouts and breaks were measured and calculated. A SED bout is defined as a continuous period of SED time lasting for ≥ 1 min, with no tolerance in the current study. No tolerance was allowed in the SED bouts because interruptions can attenuate the inverse health effects of prolonged SED in children and adolescents [4, 5]. Altenburg and colleagues [31] compared the relationship between SED bouts and health indicators when allowing 0, 30, or 60 s ≥ 100 CPM within bouts and concluded that no tolerance should be allowed within SED bouts. A consensus definition of SED bout was reached by the Sedentary Behavior Research Network as “a period of uninterrupted sedentary time” [32]. SED breaks were defined as the whole period between two consecutive SED bouts, and could comprise one or more bouts of PA of any intensity and duration [33]. Each intensity of break bout was then categorized as LPA and MVPA bouts based on the cutoffs of Evenson et al. [28].

Based on existing literature [34–36] and the preliminary exploration of participants’ SED bouts and breaks in the current study, the following categories were identified: five single SED bout duration (1–4, 5–9, 10–14, 15–29, and ≥ 30 min); six SED breaks bouts duration, such as 1–9, 10–19, and ≥ 20 min LPA bouts and 1–4, 5–9, and ≥ 10 min MVPA bouts; and the frequency of SED breaks (Table S1). The bout of ≥ 10 min MVPA was excluded because of the low proportion of the participants engaged in this bout (i.e., 10.3% of the samples). Therefore, 5–9 min and ≥ 10 min MVPA bouts were combined into a ≥ 5 min MVPA bout and included in subsequent analyses. Finally, a total of 11 variables were included to determine the accumulation patterns of SED and breaks. The total time (in minutes per day) spent in bouts of each intensity and frequency of breaks in SED per day were averaged across all valid days.

Adiposity indicators

Body mass index (BMI) z-score, fat mass percentage (FM%), and fat mass index (FMI) were included as adiposity indicators. Height was measured using a portable instrument (GMCS-IV; Jianmin, Beijing, China), and weight, fat mass, and FM% were measured by a multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer (Inbody 770; Inbody Co., Seoul, Korea). Age- and sex-specific BMI z-scores were computed using the World Health Organization’s growth reference [37]. FMI was calculated by dividing the amount of fat mass (kg) by body height squared (m2).

Confounders

The demographic information of the participants, unhealthy diet, and accelerometer wear time were included as confounders because they were reported to influence SED accumulation and adiposity of children and adolescents [38, 39]. Demographic information includes participants’ sex, age, educational level of parents, and annual household income. An unhealthy diet was assessed using the following question: “How many times a week do you usually eat or drink fries, sweets, and sodas?” with responses ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (more than once a day) [40]. All this information were obtained from the self-reports of participants over 10 years and the parental reports of participants below 10 years. The daily volumes of SED and PA were not included as covariates in the regression analysis because they are highly correlated with the SED bouts and breaks, which were used to create distinct groups.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Mplus software (version 8.3) and Stata software (version 18.0, StataCorp, Texas, USA). Latent profile analysis was used to identify the distinct profiles of children and adolescents who shared similar accumulation patterns on the basis of 11 compositions of bouts of SED, MVPA, and LPA (i.e., 1–4, 5–9, 10–14, 15–29, and ≥ 30 min SED; 1–9, 10–19, and ≥ 20 min LPA; 1–4, and ≥ 5 min MVPA; and SED break frequency). The model fit was judged on the basis of the following three indices [41, 42]: (1) Model fit based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which indicates the goodness of fit and simplicity (lower BIC values indicate better fit); (2) Lo–Mendell–Rubin Adjusted (LMRA) likelihood ratio test and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), which assess the fitting difference between models with different numbers of classes (P < 0.05 indicates the k–1 class is better than the k class model); and (3) Entropy, which evaluates the classification precision of the model (higher values indicate greater precision of model fit). Ultimately, the sample sizes (≥ 10% of the total sample size) and the interpretation of the profiles were considered in the selection of the best classification model [43, 44].

The chi-square test for categorical variables and the one-way ANOVA for continuous variables were used to assess the profile differences. Mixed-effects multivariable linear regression models were used to examine the associations between groups (i.e., profiles) and adiposity indicators (BMI z-score, FM%, and FMI). Confounders were included in the models as fixed effects, and grade and class were added as random effects to account for the grouping effect of class, nested in grade. Post-hoc Bonferroni tests were used to identify where specific differences occurred between the groups. Significance was set at P < 0.05. All data analyses were stratified in accordance with children and adolescents.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of participants

A total of 348 (38.2%) children and 562 (61.8%) adolescents participated in the study. The mean age was 12.8 years (SD = 3.0). The breakdown by gender was 441 (48.5%) males and 469 (51.5%) females. The mean BMI z-score, FM%, and FMI of the participants were 0.2, 23.6%, and 5.1 kg/m², respectively. The participants spent an average of 37.8, 252.2, and 514 min on MVPA, LPA, and SED each day, respectively. Moreover, 6.4% of the participants reported an unhealthy diet. In adolescents, the results showed that AIC, BIC, aBIC, BLRT statistics, and the overall entropy statistics all favored the five profiles, and LMRA favored the four-profile model. However, the four- and five-profile models resulted in one profile that included two (0.3%) participants only. In one- to three-profile models, AIC, BIC, aBIC, BLRT statistics, and the overall entropy statistics favored the three profiles, except LMRA. Therefore, considering statistical fit, classification accuracy, and class size balance, the three-profile model best fits the data.

Groups of participants with similar SED and breaks accumulation characteristics were identified by their distinguished features (i.e., the highest or lowest values relative to other patterns). Figures 1 and 2 show the z-scores for 11 variables of SED bouts and breaks to provide visual comparisons between profiles [45]. Four accumulation patterns, such as “prolonged sitters,” “shortened sitters,” “LPA breakers,” and “MVPA breakers”, emerged among children (Fig. 1). “Prolonged sitters” (n = 77, 22.1%) were characterized by the most time in longer SED bouts (≥ 15 min) and the least time in 1–4 min MVPA and all LPA bouts. “Shortened sitters” (n = 90, 25.9%) were engaged the most in shorter SED bouts (5–9 and 10–14 min bouts). “LPA breakers” (n = 69, 19.8%) had the highest frequency of SED breaks and the most time in < 20 min LPA bouts. “MVPA breakers” (n = 112, 32.2%) were characterized by the most time in all MVPA bouts and the least time in shorter SED bouts (5–9 and 10–14 min bouts).

Only three accumulation patterns were observed: “prolonged sitters (n = 250, 44.5%),” “moderate sitters (n = 211, 37.5%),” and “breakers (n = 101, 18.0%)” among adolescents (Fig. 2). “Prolonged sitters” were characterized by the most time in ≥ 30 min SED bouts and the least time in all LPA and MVPA bouts. “Moderate sitters” were engaged in most moderate SED bouts (10–14 and 15–29 min bouts). “Breakers” had the highest frequency of SED breaks and the most time in 1–9 min SED bouts and all LPA and MVPA bouts. Given that “prolonged sitters” spent the most time in longer SED bouts and had the least frequency of SED breaks, this group was considered the unhealthiest and selected as a reference for comparisons in children and adolescents.

Differences between groups

The distribution of the demographical information, volume of SED, MVPA, and LPA, bouts of SED, MVPA, and LPA, and diet across the distinct patterns of children and adolescents are shown in Table 3. Among children, “prolonged sitters” and “shortened sitters” were significantly older than “LPA breakers” and “MVPA breakers” (10.5 ± 1.8 and 10.1 ± 1.5 > 9.2 ± 1.7 and 8.9 ± 2.0; P < 0.05). The proportion of parents (47.8%) not completing high school was significantly higher in “LPA breaker” than in the other three groups (47.8% >32.2%, 29.9%, and 16.1%; P < 0.05). Among adolescents, “prolonged sitters” and “moderate sitters” were significantly older than “breakers” (14.9 ± 1.3 and 14.8 ± 1.4 > 14.3 ± 1.3; P < 0.05). The proportion of females was significantly higher in “prolonged sitters” than in the other two groups (62.0% >51.7% and 41.6%; P < 0.05). “Prolonged sitters” were predominantly from high-income families, with a significantly higher proportion reporting an annual household income of over 100,000 RMB than “moderate sitters” and “breakers” (11.6% >8.5% and 5.9%; P < 0.05).

Discussion

SED bouts and breaks intermittently occur within a day to form distinct accumulation patterns, and they are differently associated with obesity. Understanding these patterns allows for more precise intervention strategies. In this study, we identified unique accumulation patterns of SED and breaks among children and adolescents, revealing important differences in activity patterns across these groups.

Accumulation pattern characteristics of SED and breaks in children and adolescents

Four unique accumulation profiles were identified among children: “prolonged sitters,” “shortened sitters,” “MVPA breakers,” and “LPA breakers.” Three distinct patterns were observed for adolescents: “prolonged sitters,” “moderate sitters,” and “breakers.” The results showed that most children (33.2%) in this study fell into the “MVPA breakers” profile, whereas most adolescents (44.5%) belonged to the “prolonged sitters” profile. This finding indicates that children in China tend to be more physically active, whereas adolescents are more prone to prolonged sitting. It is consistent with the study by Verswijveren et al. [9] and Mitchell and Steele [10], who found that most children are “breakers” or “moderately active.” Verswijveren, Lamb [9] found that children tend to engage in more frequent breaks from SED, suggesting that active play or movement during the day is prevalent. Similarly, Mitchell and Steele [10] highlighted that older youth were significantly more likely to be in the Inactive profile. Much research evidence supports that children are naturally active and become more physically inactive, especially girls, during puberty [46–48]. Children in primary schools in China have less academic burden than adolescents, and they have more time and opportunities to engage in PA. Adolescents who are secondary or high school students face more academic challenges, including high school and university entrance examinations in China. They must study harder to obtain academic achievements, leading to long and continuous SED time in learning [22]. Therefore, the SED pattern of children being more active than adolescents in China is understandable. However, a notable detail that 66.8% of children fell into inactive accumulation patterns such as “prolonged sitters,” “shortened sitters,” and “LPA breakers.” Interventions to adjust the accumulation pattern of SED and breaks of children and adolescents, especially adolescents in China, are necessary to improve their weight status.

Association between accumulation patterns of SED and breaks and adiposity in children

Among children, “MVPA breakers” had the healthiest adiposity indicators. This result aligns with expectations, given that “MVPA breakers” are characterized by the highest engagement in all MVPA bouts and have the most total MVPA time and the least total time in SED among the four groups. This finding is also consistent with that of Verswijveren et al. [9], who found that “breakers” had the lower adiposity risks. A large amount of evidence has shown that more MVPA and less SED are associated with low adiposity indicators in children [7, 34, 40]. Consequently, the more beneficial outcomes in “MVPA breakers” versus the other three groups may be explained by their most MVPA and least SED daily volumes in this group. Moreover, “MVPA breakers” had the most time for all MVPA bouts, and they had no preference for a certain MVPA bout. The total MVPA daily volumes may be beneficial for lowering adiposity levels in children, either accumulated sporadically or in sustained bouts. Several variable-centered studies also found that MVPA volume, rather than bout duration, is inversely associated with adiposity [49, 50]. These findings supported the current guidelines of the World Health Organization [51] and Composing and Editorial Board of Physical Activity Guidelines for Chinese [52], which recommend amassing a daily total of 60 min MVPA but do not address how to accumulate MVPA time.

No significant difference was found in all adiposity indicators between “prolonged sitters” and “LPA breakers” despite “LPA breakers” spending more time on LPA bouts to break continuous SED than “prolonged sitters.” This finding may reveal that LPA breaks do not strongly contribute to adiposity of children. Some experimental studies showed that avoiding prolonged sedentary bouts with light-intensity activities (e.g., walking) could be beneficial for cardiometabolic health in adults [53–55], whereas others found that LPA interruption did not improve metabolic health [56–58]. Although the present study supported that the intensity of LPA break is not sufficient to lower the adiposity indicators of Chinese children, this result should be interpreted with caution. The cross-sectional nature of the study limits causal inference; further longitudinal or interventional studies are warranted to clarify these relationships.

No significant differences in adiposity indicators were observed either between “prolonged sitters” and “shortened sitters.” “Shortened sitters” are characterized by the most time on short SED bouts, and “prolonged sitters” spend the most time on long SED bouts. It may indicate that the length of SED bouts cannot make the adiposity of children different. This finding is different from those of existing studies, which found that sporadic or prolonged SED bouts are significantly related to adiposity in children in addition to the total volume of SED [34, 59–62]. Children are generally more physically active, and their SED is accumulated in shorter bouts. The descriptive statistics of the current study showed that the SED time of children was significantly shorter than that of adolescents (396.7 min vs. 586.6 min), and this finding may lead to the insignificant influence of SED bouts on adiposity. In addition, a small sample size (N = 90) may weaken the relationship between “shortened sitters” (compared with “prolonged sitters”) and adiposity indicators in the present study [63, 64].

In summary, children may have lower adiposity levels through the total daily MVPA and SED volumes beyond such accumulation profiles. Health promotion efforts should consider increasing the daily MVPA time and decreasing the SED time of children. Meanwhile, children are encouraged to break up their SED with MVPA of any duration. Moreover, “prolonged sitters” and “shortened sitters” were older, and thus, more intention may be focused on older children.

Association between accumulation patterns of SED and breaks and adiposity in adolescents

Three profiles, including “prolonged sitters,” “moderate sitters,” and “breakers,” were identified for adolescents. “Breakers,” which were characterized by the highest amount of time in short SED bouts with the most interruption, had the lowest level of adiposity indicators, followed by “moderate sitters,” which were characterized by the most time in moderate SED bouts, and “prolonged sitters,” which were characterized by the highest amount of time in long SED bouts with the least interruption. Moreover, “breakers” had the most MVPA and LPA volume and the least SED time. On the contrary, “prolonged sitters” had the least MVPA and LPA volume and the most SED time. The distinguished differences in SED, MVPA, LPA bouts, and volume among these three patterns indicated that the SED accumulation pattern may be important for the adiposity of adolescents in addition to the daily volume of MVPA, LPA, and SED time. This finding is different from that in children, which showed that the length of SED bouts and breaks was not related to the adiposity of children. The possible reason behind this difference is that children had a more active SED accumulation pattern in this study, in which the negative influence of the prolonged sitting of children may be counteracted by MVPA breaks [31, 65, 66].

“Prolonged sitters,” “moderate sitters,” and “breakers,” with the most time in long (≥ 30 min), moderate (10–14 and 15–29 min), and short (1–9 min) SED bouts, respectively, had the least, moderate, and the most benefit to the weight status of adolescents, respectively. This finding indicated that longer SED bouts are related to higher adiposity levels. Moreover, SED bouts should be restricted to at least 30 min in duration to improve weight status. It is in accordance with the findings of existing studies, which reported that SED time in uninterrupted bouts is detrimentally associated with adiposity in adolescents [6, 61, 67]. However, the SED bout duration beneficial for adiposity is inconsistent across existing studies [61, 67, 68]. Colley et al. [68] indicated that prolonged bouts of SED lasting at least 80 min were associated with BMI and waist circumference in Canadian boys. Júdice et al. [67] found that non-prolonged sedentary bouts (< 30 min) were inversely associated with BMI in Portuguese girls. Werneck et al. [61] reported that the 1–4 min SED bout was associated with lower adiposity in Brazilian adolescents. The reason for the inconsistency among studies may be attributed to the disparities among adolescents’ daily routins and activities across a range of cultural and educational contexts. Furthermore, these variations may be influenced by the different categorization of single SED bouts and the operational definitions employed (e.g., varying tolerance values) [31].

“Breakers” had the highest amount of time in all MVPA and LPA bouts, whereas “prolonged sitters” had the lowest amount of time in all MVPA and LPA bouts, with no preference for the duration of MVPA and LPA bouts. These results suggested that accumulating more MVPA and LPA time with any duration in between SED bouts may confer favorable differences in adiposity indicators. In agreement with the results of the present study, several studies consistently reported that more active behaviors (MVPA and/or LPA) could benefit adiposity in adolescents, either accumulated sporadically or in sustained bouts [69, 70].

These results collectively implied that to achieve enhanced adiposity level, health promotion efforts could consider encouraging adolescents to use any active behavior of any duration to restrict SED time to at least 30 min. “Prolonged sitters” were older, females, and from high-income families, and thus, more intention may be focused on these groups of adolescents. It is also worth noting that individuals classified as “prolonged sitters” might derive greater marginal benefits from increasing PA or reducing SED. This could be particularly meaningful for populations that find it difficult to transition from “prolonged sitters” to “breakers,” suggesting that even if changing the behavior patterns of “prolonged sitters” is challenging, increasing any PA could help prevent obesity.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to employ a data-driven and person-centered method to derive patterns of accumulation of SED and breaks and examine their associations with adiposity indicators in children and adolescents in China. Additionally, SED bouts and breaks were measured using objective methods. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causality between these patterns and adiposity. Future longitudinal studies are necessary to verify the relationship among these variables. Second, the participants were from five schools and two cities in China, and the absence of students in grades 9 and 12 thus restricted the generalizability of the findings to all secondary school students. Lastly, some factors, such as (e.g., total dietary intake, anxiety and depression, and sleep habits) may affect the estimates of the association between these accumulation patterns and adiposity, and they do not account for in this study.

Conclusion

This study identified distinct accumulation patterns of SED and breaks in children (“prolonged sitters,” “shortened sitters,” “LPA breakers,” and “MVPA breakers”) and adolescents (“prolonged sitters,” “moderate sitters,” and “breakers”). In children, “MVPA breakers” had significantly lower levels of adiposity indicators than “prolonged sitters,” and no significant differences in adiposity indicators were found among “prolonged sitters,” “shortened sitters,” and “LPA breakers.” In adolescents, “breakers” had the lowest BMI z-score, FM%, and FMI among the three groups, followed by “moderate sitters” and “prolonged sitters.” Interventions to increase the total daily MVPA and decrease SED volumes may be provided to children, and interventions to restrict SED time to at least 30 min by using any active behavior of any duration may be offered to adolescents to improve their weight status. Older children, older and female adolescents, and adolescents from high-income families should receive more attention in future interventions. These insights emphasize the need for public health policies and school-based programs that not only promote PA but also optimize the pattern of SED and breaks throughout the day. Future efforts should focus on strengthening school-based supervision and enhancing parental support to promote healthier accumulation patterns of SED and breaks among students.
