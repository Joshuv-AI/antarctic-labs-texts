Functional Ca2+-permeable AMPA receptors in hippocampal astrocytes mediate calcium signaling in vitro
Authors: Javier Picañol, Aida Castellanos, Esther Gratacòs-Batlle, Roberto García, Silvia Ginés, Xavier Gasull, Lydia Jiménez-Díaz, Juan D. Navarro-López, David Soto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glutamate is the main excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain, acting through ionotropic receptors such as AMPA receptors (AMPARs). While their neuronal role is well established, AMPAR function in astrocytes remains poorly understood, especially in the hippocampus. In this study, we investigated the expression and functional properties of astrocytic AMPARs, focusing on their capacity to mediate calcium signaling and inter-cellular communication. Using astrocyte primary cultures, we confirmed the expression of GluA1 and GluA2 subunits, along with detectable levels of GluA4. Calcium imaging revealed that pharmacological AMPAR stimulation elicited intracellular Ca²⁺ signals in a subset of astrocytes which were markedly potentiated when desensitization was impaired. Notably, some of these responses persisted without extracellular Na⁺, compatible with the presence of functional calcium-permeable AMPARs (CP-AMPARs) in hippocampal astrocytes. However, sodium was required for full amplification of the Ca²⁺ signals, suggesting a synergistic role of Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ influx. Pharmacological inhibition of GluA2-lacking AMPARs reduced the responses, supporting the coexistence of GluA2-lacking and GluA2-containing subtypes. Sniffer-calcium assays suggested that strong AMPAR stimulation can engage ATP-dependent signaling, facilitating calcium wave propagation. Altogether, our results reveal functional and heterogeneous AMPAR populations in hippocampal astrocytes. Further investigations will elucidate their physiological roles, localization, and contribution to neuroglial interactions under physiological and pathological conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41403-8.

Introduction

As the principal excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain, glutamate mediates signaling between neurons through the activation of ionotropic glutamate receptors1,2. Among these, α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors (AMPARs) are particularly notable, as they are essential for rapid synaptic transmission and neuroplasticity1. The dynamic regulation of AMPAR presence and function is key to modulating synaptic efficacy, as seen in processes like long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD), mechanisms underlying learning and memory3,4.

AMPAR function is governed by multiple factors, including the specific assembly of pore-forming GluA subunits and the interaction with auxiliary proteins1,5. The core of AMPARs is composed of homo- or heterotetrameric assemblies of GluA subunits (GluA1-4), whose region-specific expression patterns contribute to the molecular and functional diversity of AMPARs6. The vast majority of AMPARs in the central nervous system are assembled as heteromeric complexes that include the GluA2 subunit. For example, GluA1/A2 and GluA2/A3 heteromers are the predominant receptors in the hippocampus, while GluA2/A4 combinations predominate in the cerebellum – being these AMPAR subtypes primarily permeable to Na⁺6–8. A key determinant of AMPAR ionic permeability is the presence or absence of the GluA2 subunit, which critically modulates Ca2+ permeability. Depending on its incorporation in the core receptor, AMPARs can be classified as either calcium-impermeable (CI-AMPARs) or calcium-permeable (CP-AMPARs) receptors9. This functional difference is due to a posttranscriptional RNA editing at the so-called Q/R site, located at position 607 within the pore-forming loop of the M2 segment. Here, a genetically encoded glutamine (Q) is converted to arginine (R), thereby reducing Ca2+ permeability10. This highly efficient editing process is crucial for survival and influences multiple aspects of AMPAR biology, including channel conductance, intracellular polyamine block, ER exit, and receptor tetramerization11–15. Although CP-AMPARs are generally expressed at low levels, their dynamic regulation is crucial in synaptic remodeling, playing significant roles in both physiological and pathological contexts9,16.

To date, most efforts have focused on neuronal AMPARs, while their role in glial cells remains comparatively understudied. AMPARs are expressed in astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), and microglia, with accumulating transcriptomic and electrophysiological evidence supporting their presence across non-neuronal cell types17–22. In astrocytes, qPCR has revealed the expression of GRIA and CACNG genes encoding GluA subunits and their main auxiliary subunits TARPs (Transmembrane AMPAR Regulatory Proteins), respectively.20 Consistent with astrocytic heterogeneity, GRIA transcript levels vary across brain regions, with GRIA2 being notably enriched in the hippocampus. Within the hippocampus, intra-regional variability exists among astrocytic subtypes with differential GRIA1 expression observed particularly in the subgranular layer of the dentate gyrus20,21. Among available functional reports, two distinct types of currents – rapid ionic currents mediated by ionotropic glutamate receptors, and slower electrogenic currents associated with glutamate uptake transporters – have been characterized in hippocampal astrocytes using patch-clamp techniques. However, early studies raised questions regarding the cellular identity of these recorded cells, with some suggesting contributions from NG2 glia23,24. In cortical astrocytes, glutamatergic receptors have been implicated in mediating intracellular Ca2+ signaling, with miniature events attributed to spontaneous AMPA and NMDA receptor activity25. Similarly, in the olfactory bulb, CP-AMPARs have been shown to modulate calcium signaling26,27. In Bergmann glia, the expression of AMPARs is well established by electrophysiological and molecular evidence17,28–32. Building on this foundation, conditional deletion of GluA1 and GluA4 subunits in these cells further demonstrates a functional role for astrocytic AMPARs in vivo, raising the question of whether similar mechanisms operate in hippocampal astrocytes33.

Together, this body of evidence supports the view that astrocytic AMPARs constitute an additional pathway through which astrocytes sense activity and generate Ca²⁺ signals, paralleling mechanisms described for NMDA receptors34. Astrocytic Ca²⁺ provides a framework for understanding how astrocytes respond to and integrate activity35–37. This signaling is involved in synaptic modulation, the release of signaling molecules, and neurovascular coupling, among other functions, reinforcing the importance of the tripartite synapse model35,38,39. Calcium increases have been linked to the propagation of intracellular calcium waves in response to different stimuli, capable of triggering intracellular Ca2+ release from cellular stores37. This signaling is often amplified by the activation of inositol-1,4,5-triphosphate receptors (IP3Rs). In glutamate-induced calcium waves, metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluRs) coupled to Gq proteins, particularly mGluR5 and mGluR1, have been implicated in intracellular calcium signaling40,41. However, this model has been increasingly questioned, as mGluRs appear less relevant in mature astrocytes, which lack substantial intracellular Ca2+ stores. Notably, Ca2+ signaling persists in astrocytic processes even following IP3R deletion, suggesting alternative mechanisms of Ca2+ signaling42–44.

In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to rapid astrocyte–neuron interactions mediated by Ca²⁺-permeable ion channels. In this context, TRPA1, P2X receptors, and NMDA receptors have emerged as contributors to astrocytic signaling37. The present study addresses whether AMPARs in hippocampal astrocytes are functionally active and capable of mediating Ca²⁺ signaling.

Results

Characterization of hippocampal astrocytes in vitro

To test our hypothesis, we first established primary astrocyte cultures and confirmed their purity through immunohistochemistry for subsequent analyses. Cultures were prepared following previously published protocols45. Astrocytes were initially identified using glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a standard astrocytic marker. However, GFAP labeling did not stain the entire astrocyte population, as various cells may express GFAP mRNA without translation46. Notably, GFAP-negative astrocytes can be identified by the expression of specific glutamate transporters47. Therefore, we combined GFAP labeling with Glutamate Aspartate Transporter 1 (GLAST-1) to enable a more comprehensive characterization of the hippocampal astrocytes under study (Fig. 1). Astrocyte cultures were fixed at 14 days in vitro (DIV14) and showed a robust expression of both GFAP and GLAST (Fig. 1A). Special attention was given to the cells with nuclei negative for both markers (Fig. 1B), and the number of positive cells for each condition was quantified. We observed that most cultured hippocampal astrocytes were double positive for GFAP and GLAST (GFAP+/GLAST+: 87.44%, SEM ± 2.46, 95% CI, 79.59–95.29, n = 562 cells from n = 4 cultures). Smaller subsets included GFAP−/GLAST+ (7.13%, SEM ± 2.07, 95% CI, 0.51–13.75, n = 36 cells from 4 cultures) or GFAP+/GLAST− (2.22%, SEM ± 1.41, 95% CI; -2.27–6.71, n = 17 cells from 4 cultures) and a minor population negative for both markers (GFAP−/GLAST; 3,2%, SEM ± 0.74, 95% CI, 0.8–5.59, n = 19 cells from 4 cultures) (Fig. 1C). Overall, 96.8% (SEM ± 0.75, 95% CI, 94.39–99.21, n = 615 cells from 4 cultures) of cells were positive for at least one of these astrocytic markers or both (Fig. 1D), confirming the purity of our culture.

GluA subunits are present in hippocampal astrocytes

Although previous studies have reported GRIA gene expression at the mRNA level, evidence for protein expression of GluA subunits in hippocampal astrocytes remains limited20. Thus, we next assessed the protein levels of GluA subunits that comprise AMPA receptors in our established cultures. Protein extracts from isolated astrocyte cultures were obtained at DIV14, under conditions consistent with those used across experiments. Immunoblotting confirmed the presence of GluA1, GluA2, and GluA4 at the expected molecular weight (~ 100 kDa) (Fig. 2). Tissue extracts from the hippocampus, cerebellum, and cortex were included as positive controls for validation. Uncropped blots corresponding to these experiments are provided in Supplementary Figs. 1 (astrocyte samples) and 2 (brain tissue controls). Interestingly, GluA3 was not detected under our experimental conditions.

Astrocytic AMPARs are functional and mediate calcium signaling

Despite confirmation of protein expression, the functionality of hippocampal astrocytic AMPARs remains a subject of debate22. To address this issue, we performed calcium imaging experiments, pre-incubating astrocytes with the Fura-2 calcium indicator and exposing the cells to various pharmacological stimuli.

We first stimulated the astrocytes with the specific agonist AMPA (20µM)1. Under these experimental conditions, a subset of astrocytes elicited intracellular Ca2+ responses (Fig. 3A). However, AMPAR-mediated signaling in astrocytes may be transient, spatially restricted, or subthreshold for ratiometric imaging under our conditions; therefore, functional AMPARs may be present but remain undetected in a fraction of cells. To enhance detectability, we applied cyclothiazide (CTZ), a positive allosteric modulator (PAM) that reduces AMPAR desensitization, in combination with AMPA48,49. From an initial percentage of 12.94% after AMPA stimulation alone (SEM ± 1.86, 95% CI, 7.74 to 18.13, n = 73 cells from 5 coverslips), we observed an increase to 82.83% when 10µM of CTZ was co-applicated together with the agonist (SEM ± 6.54, 95% CI, 64.66 to 101, n = 474 cells from 5 coverslips; Fig. 3D). Statistical analysis confirmed that this increase was significant (Two-tailed paired t-test, p = 0.0007), supporting that CTZ increases detectability of AMPAR-dependent Ca²⁺ responses under our imaging conditions. In addition to increasing response incidence, the effect of the CTZ was also altering the calcium response profile, leading to more sustained responses in the presence of the PAM (121.6 ± 3.06a.u. for AMPA, n = 73 vs. 136.8 ± 2.06a.u. for AMPA and CTZ, n = 474; two-tailed Wilcoxon matched pairs signed rank test; p = < 0.0001; Fig. 3C).

Consequently, subsequent calcium experiments were conducted in the presence of CTZ as a methodological strategy to facilitate the comparison of AMPAR-mediated responses across experimental conditions. The observed responses were confirmed to be AMPAR-specific by using the competitive antagonist NBQX50. Astrocytes were first stimulated with AMPA and CTZ, allowed to return to baseline, and then re-stimulated following pre-application of 50µM NBQX (Fig. 3B). The reverse application order yielded identical results. In all cases, NBQX abolished the increase in intracellular calcium elicited by AMPA, indicating a direct AMPAR dependency (Fig. 3E). Quantification of the Ca2+ fold change in the same cells showed a significant reduction in NBQX presence with responses remaining close to basal levels (1.611 ± 0.004 for AMPA, n = 226 vs. 1.011 ± 0.0005 for AMPA and NBQX, n = 268; two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test; p = < 0.0001).

Finally, we characterized the diversity of response profiles and observed significant heterogeneity in the responses, with some exhibiting rapid and acute Ca2+ transients, while others displayed slower dynamics characterized by a markedly variable time-to-peak (Fig. 3F). This heterogeneity could reflect the intrinsic differences among distinct astrocytic subtypes previously observed in our cultures.

Ca²⁺ and Na⁺-dependent mechanisms shape astrocytic AMPAR-mediated responses

In neurons, GluA2 expression normally leads to GluA2-containing AMPARs14,15, which typically are CI-AMPARs51 but see52, and constitute the predominant AMPAR subtype in neurons7. In our astrocyte’s cultures we found a high expression of this subunit, likely promoting the assembly of heteromeric CI-AMPARs. However, we also observed widespread Ca²⁺ responses upon astrocytic AMPAR stimulation. Hence, to further study this apparent discrepancy and clarify the source of Ca2+ signals, we decided to examine (1) whether Ca2+ signaling requires the presence of extracellular Ca²⁺ or, alternatively, it arises from intracellular stores37, and (2) whether indirect mechanisms associated with Na⁺ entry contribute to the observed Ca2+ elevations53.

To address the first point, astrocytic AMPARs were stimulated in a Ca2+-free solution, followed by re-stimulation in a Ca²⁺-containing solution (Fig. 4A). In these experiments, we identified two astrocyte populations: responders (44%) and non-responders (56%). Notably, responders only elicited a Ca2+ signal in the presence of extracellular Ca²⁺, with significant differences in fold-change observed upon solution replacement (0.996 ± 0.002 Ca2+i fold change for Ca+ 2-free solution vs. 1.155 ± 0.008 Ca2+i fold change for Ca+ 2-containing solution, n = 77; Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test; p = < 0.0001; Fig. 4B).

Due to the evident requirement for extracellular calcium in astrocytic Ca2+ responses, we assessed whether CP-AMPARs contributed to the signaling. To test this, we replaced extracellular Na+ with N-methyl-D-glucamine to generate a Na+-free solution (Fig. 4C). We observed that despite the Na⁺ removal, a significant percentage of astrocytes still responded to AMPAR stimulation (21,45%, SEM ± 4.92, n = 183/850 cells). Upon Na+ reintroduction, the proportion of responding astrocytes increased significantly to 87,94%, SEM ± 5.79, n = 748/850 cells (Two-tailed Wilcoxon matched pairs signed rank test; p = 0.0039; from n = 10 coverslips; Fig. 4D). When we analyzed the responses exclusively from these responder astrocytes in both types of solutions Ca2+ elevations were significantly higher in the presence of Na⁺ compared to Na⁺-free conditions (1.173, SEM ± 0.007 Ca2+i fold change for Na+-free solution, n = 183 vs. 1.262, SEM ± 0.009 Ca2+i fold change for Na+-containing, n = 183; Two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test; p = < 0.0001; Fig. 4E). Additionally, the response kinetics differed in terms of time-to-peak, with faster responses observed under sodium-free conditions (72.35, SEM ± 1.65 for Na+-containing solution vs. 116.2, SEM ± 2.81 for Na+-free solution; Two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test; p = < 0.0001; Fig. 4F). These results are compatible with the possible coexistence and functional interplay between CP- and CI-AMPARs, with Ca2+ signaling being modulated both thought Ca2+ entry and by Na+ dependent mechanisms.

To further support this, we conducted experiments where we applied to the extracellular media the blocker 1-naphthyl acetyl spermine (NASPM; 50µM), commonly used to probe GluA2-lacking AMPAR activity (Fig. 5A)54. Upon these conditions of GluA2-lacking AMPARs blockage, there were still a significant population of responder astrocytes to AMPA stimulation 21,54% (SEM ± 9.09, CI95% 0.04 to 43.04, 179/1122 cells, n = 8 coverslips). These data indicate that a substantial fraction of astrocytes remains responsive to AMPAR stimulation under NASPM treatment, consistent with signaling mechanisms not fully sensitive to NASPM. In contrast, in the absence of NASPM, the percentage of responders increased substantially to 65,12% (SEM ± 11,57, CI95% 37.75 to 92.48, 728/1122 cells, n = 8 coverslips) (Two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test; p = 0.0078; Fig. 5B). Additionally, the total calcium signal was significantly greater in the absence of NASPM, consistent with a contribution of NASPM-sensitive receptor populations to the overall Ca²⁺ response (1.388 ± 0.011 Ca2+i fold change for AMPA and CTZ, n = 179 vs. 1.154 ± 0.003 Ca2+i fold change with NASPM, n = 179; Two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test; p = < 0.0001; Fig. 5C). In line with the Na⁺-manipulation experiments, responses obtained in the presence of NASPM displayed slower kinetics, as reflected by an increased time-to-peak (144, SEM ± 3.67 s for NASPM presence vs. 92.95, SEM ± 2.69 for NASPM absence; n = 178; Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test; **** p < 0,0001; Fig. 5D).

Sustained astrocytic AMPAR activation is associated with ATP-dependent signaling

The percentage of astrocytes responding to AMPA in the presence CTZ was notably high and significant across various experimental conditions. Furthermore, considering the heterogeneity in calcium response kinetics – ranging from rapid to slow dynamics – (Fig. 3F), we hypothesized that a part of this calcium signaling could be indirectly mediated through astrocyte-to-astrocyte communication, potentially amplifying the response. To investigate whether AMPAR activity could engage ATP-dependent signaling, we implemented “sniffer-calcium” experiments, adapting the concept of “sniffer-patch” approach previously employed in patch-clamp studies55.

To investigate this, we co-cultured astrocytes with HEK-293T cells transfected to function as biosensors (Fig. 6A). To ensure specificity, we chose the purinergic ionotropic P2X7 receptor since (1) is endogenously expressed in astrocytes and (2) responds to ATP, a purinergic signaling molecule released by astrocytes, and permeates Ca²⁺ upon activation56–58. We first confirmed the expression and activation of the purinergic receptor P2X7, known to be involved in astrocytic calcium signaling and purinergic intercellular communication (Fig. 6B and C). We then established co-cultures with HEK-293T cells transfected with P2X7 and GFP for identification (Fig. 6A). In this setup, we monitored intracellular calcium dynamics in both astrocytes and HEK-293T sensor cells, focusing on the responses after AMPAR stimulation. We first examined the responses of P2X7-containing HEK-293T cells in the absence of astrocytes. As expected, cells display no responses upon stimulation with AMPA and CTZ. However, transfected cells exhibited an increase in intracellular calcium when stimulated with ATP at 10µM (Supp. Figure 3).

After this initial validation, we proceeded to stimulate the co-cultures, first with AMPA and CTZ, followed by ATP application. As observed in previous experiments, a subset of astrocytes exhibited intracellular calcium responses to both stimuli (Fig. 6D). However, the proportion of the P2X7-expressing HEK-293T cells, which theoretically should not elicit any response, also display calcium transients upon AMPA and CTZ stimulation only when co-cultured with astrocytes, consistent with an indirect astrocyte-mediated mechanism (Fig. 6F). Not all transfected cells responded to AMPA, with approximately one-quarter of the cells (22,68%, SEM ± 6.89, CI95% -7.005 to 52.36, 11/55 cells, n = 3) showing a response. However, as a positive control, 89.45% (SEM ± 1.25, CI95% 84.06 to 94.83, 49/55 cells, n = 3) responded to ATP (Fig. 6F).

Given that the response of transfected HEK-293T cells to AMPA appeared to be consistent with astrocyte-dependent paracrine signaling from nearby activated astrocytes, we sought to corroborate this by analyzing the distance between responder and non-responder cells relative to the nearest active astrocyte. Our results showed that P2X7-containing HEK-293T cells exhibited calcium signals in response to AMPA when they were located closer to an activated astrocyte compared with non-responders (63.36 ± 9,821 μm for responders, n = 11 vs. 118,6 ± 10,97 μm for non-responders, n = 45; Two-tailed Mann Whitney test; p = 0.0076; Fig. 6G). We also found a modest inverse correlation between Ca²⁺ fold change and distance to the nearest activated astrocyte, with stronger calcium signals observed at shorter distances (r=-0.32; 95%CI -0.77 to 0.34; Fig. 6I).

Since HEK-293T was an indirect response with astrocytes acting as intermediaries, we hypothesized that the response profile to AMPA should differ between both cell types. Since astrocytes are activated via AMPARs, the effect of CTZ reducing desensitization is expected to produce a more sustained response compared to a more rapid response of P2X7 activation in HEK-293T cells. To assess this, we quantified the area under the curve (AUC) of the calcium responses in astrocytes compared to those responses in transfected HEK-293T cells. Consistent with the underlying mechanisms of intracellular calcium increments, we found that astrocytic responses generated a calcium signal with a significantly larger area under the curve (106,2 ± 3,333a.u. for P2X7-containing HEK-293T, n = 11 vs. 115,4 ± 1,778a.u. for astrocytes, n = 42; Two-tailed Mann Whitney test; p = 0.0121; Fig. 6H).

Finally, we corroborated two additional points: (1) whether these responses could also be elicited in the absence of CTZ, and (2) whether this response was induced in the presence of a specific P2X7 antagonist. First, we observed that the response in P2X7-containing HEK-293T cells requires the addition of CTZ (Supp. Figure 4A). Accordingly, the studied cells exhibited an increase in intracellular calcium only in the presence of the drug (1,017 ± 0,013 Ca2+i fold change for only AMPA vs. 1,201 ± 0,111 Ca2+i fold change for AMPA and CTZ, n = 49; Two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test; p = < 0,0001; Supp. Figure 4B). Second, under conditions including CTZ, this response was shown to be mediated by P2X7, as the presence of A-438079, a competitive P2X7 competitive antagonist, prevented the corresponding response (Supp. Figure 4C). Notably, the same cells responded upon re-stimulation following washout of the antagonist (1,039 ± 0,041 Ca2+i fold change in presence of A43 vs. 1,185 ± 0,098 in its absence; n = 107; Two-tailed Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test; p = < 0,0001; Fig. 4D).

Taking together, these experiments indicate that sustained pharmacological AMPAR activation can engage ATP-dependent signaling in neighboring cells through astrocyte-dependent mechanisms, without implying that this represents a direct or physiological ATP-mediated release pathway.

Discussion

Astrocytic Ca²⁺ transients are a widely used readout of astrocyte activation and have been linked to multiple forms of neuroglial signaling, including local microdomain activity and intercellular propagation37,59. However, the upstream mechanisms that initiate these signals and the pathways that shape their spatiotemporal profiles remain incompletely defined. Substantial evidence now highlights the role of ionotropic channels in mediating Ca²⁺ influx into astrocytes. In this line, iGluRs, such as NMDARs, have been studied in the astrocytic context25,34,60,61. These receptors can generate calcium influx, depolarize the membrane, and contribute to microdomain activity, thereby influencing neuronal synchronization and ATP release60,61. However, despite the extensive characterization of other receptor types, functional evidence regarding AMPARs remains underexplored. Although most AMPARs exhibit low calcium permeability due to GluA2 incorporation, we hypothesized that these receptors may play an important astrocytic role sensing extracellular glutamate increments. Previous research has shown that astrocytic AMPARs are functional in most brain regions, displaying heterogeneity based on their subunit composition25,26,28,62. For example, Bergmann glia, show a high prevalence of CP-AMPARs due to the dominant expression of GluA1 and GluA4, which leads to the formation of GluA2-lacking AMPA receptors17,31,33. Similarly, in the olfactory bulb (OB), CP-AMPARs are prominently expressed26,27. However, patch-clamp experiments in the OB region continue to detect functional currents in the presence of NASPM, suggesting the co-existence of both CP- and CI-AMPARs26. Beyond these regions, astrocytic AMPARs in the hippocampus have a predominantly presence of GRIA2, which can contribute to the assumption of a limited role for AMPARs in calcium signaling20.

Here, we demonstrate that hippocampal astrocytic AMPARs are functionally present and that their pharmacological activation can initiate intracellular Ca²⁺ elevations in vitro. Given the mismatch between rapid AMPAR gating and the slower timescale of Ca²⁺ imaging, such activation is unlikely to reflect a direct one-to-one coupling, but rather an initiating step that recruits downstream amplification mechanisms. This finding diverges from previous studies in cortical astrocytes, where GluA2-containing AMPARs predominate, and their stimulation has been shown to be insufficient to generate astrocytic Ca²⁺ influx, except when AMPAR desensitization is blocked63. This was not the scenario found in our experiments, as we observed that a subset of astrocytes could generate calcium signaling even in the absence of CTZ. Nonetheless, when receptor desensitization was impaired with CTZ, a more robust and generalized response was observed (Fig. 3A).

Sodium ions are essential for neuronal excitability, as they generate inward currents that underlie action potentials. However, their role has long been underestimated in the astroglia context. Astrocyte excitability is a complex process that should not be limited solely to calcium signaling, as other ions, including Na+ also play a crucial role in this dynamic process64. In this regard, astrocytes exhibit sodium transients, and it is an additional perspective to conceptualize their activity. In response to neurotransmitters such as glutamate, significant increases in intracellular Na+ have been observed linking neuronal activity to rapid local astrocytic responses65. Furthermore, elevations in Na+ can drive calcium influx through Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchangers (NCX) further linking sodium dynamics to calcium signaling in astrocytes66. In the hippocampus, GluA2-containing AMPARs are predominant and mainly permeate sodium. This led us to hypothesize that part of the observed calcium signaling might arise indirectly from intracellular Na+ increases rather than directly from CP-AMPAR-mediated calcium influx. To test this possibility, we reasoned that AMPARs should not elicit any calcium signaling in the absence of sodium. However, under these conditions, we observed that a subset of astrocytes still exhibited calcium transients, a response that was consistent and specific. These findings indicate that a specific subpopulation of receptors may allow direct calcium influx through AMPARs, supporting the existence of functional CP-AMPARs in hippocampal astrocytes. When sodium was reintroduced, the calcium response became more widespread and robust, consistent with the possible coexistence of CI- and CP-AMPARs, as has been reported in other regions26. Our findings align with two observations: (i) the pronounced expression of GRIA1 in the dentate gyrus at the RNA level21, and (ii) the potential formation of GluA1 homomers and/or GluA1/A4 heteromers, consistent with the presence of these subunits in western blot experiments (Fig. 2). Interestingly AMPARs composed by GluA1 and/or GluA4 subunits are present in Bergmann Glia from cerebellum where they are essential for proper fine motor coordination33. Given the substantial increase in calcium response in the presence of sodium, it is plausible that Na+ influx might also influence intracellular calcium release mechanisms67. A localized increase in sodium could create a positive feedback loop where the initial Na⁺ influx and resulting depolarization lead to further calcium elevation through voltage-gated calcium channels. Supporting this hypothesis, our experiments in Ca2+-free solution have demonstrated that Na+ alone is insufficient to elicit a response, and calcium signaling via AMPARs activation requires the presence of extracellular calcium (Fig. 4).

Application of NASPM, which preferentially blocks GluA2-lacking AMPARs, reduced both the proportion of responding astrocytes and the amplitude of Ca²⁺ signals (Fig. 5). A substantial fraction of cells remained responsive, consistent with GluA2-containing AMPAR signaling. Previous patch-clamp recordings in astrocytes have shown that the effects of NASPM on kainate-induced inward currents are variable, with most cells displaying a partial NASPM-sensitive component. NASPM reduced kainate-evoked currents to ~ 37.4% of control in NASPM-sensitive cells, while GYKI produced a more uniform and pronounced block26. Under our Ca²⁺ imaging conditions, NASPM sensitivity cannot be interpreted quantitatively in terms of AMPAR subunit composition, as NASPM is voltage-dependent and membrane potential was not monitored54. In addition, NASPM has been reported to be non-specific affecting NMDA receptor function68, and therefore a contribution of NMDAR-dependent Ca²⁺ influx or amplification cannot be excluded. Taken together, our NASPM sensitivity observed during the experiments should be interpreted only as directionally informative and supportive of GluA2-lacking AMPAR involvement.

Astrocytes can release a variety of signaling molecules through both calcium-dependent and calcium-independent mechanisms. Among these, ATP is particularly relevant in modulating the propagation of astrocytic calcium waves56. The calcium-dependent release of these molecules involves mechanisms such as SNARE proteins, lysosomal exocytosis, gap junctions like connexin 43, anion channels, or even through P2X7 receptors56,69–72. This supports the idea that the proportion of AMPA-responsive astrocytes observed in our study might be partly attributed to a smaller subset of astrocytes amplifying the response through the release of ATP. Our observations made using calcium sniffer assays are consistent with this hypothesis (Fig. 6). Thus, the percentage of responding astrocytes does not directly reflect astrocytes with functional AMPARs. Furthermore, this raises the idea of a potential physiological role for AMPARs, where their permeability to Ca2+ or Na+ (or both) might contribute to Ca²⁺-dependent intercellular signaling processes in response to glutamate release from neurons and/or other cell types. However, AMPA stimulation alone did not evoke consistent sniffer responses (Supp. Figure 4), indicating that ATP-dependent signaling was reliably detected only in the presence of CTZ. Accordingly, our sniffer-based purinergic readout should be interpreted as a pharmacologically unmasked mechanism, rather than evidence for a physiological pathway, suggesting that part of the observed Ca²⁺ responses may reflect secondary astrocyte–astrocyte communication rather than direct AMPAR activation in every responding cell.

Nevertheless, astrocytes are well positioned to detect extracellular glutamate released during synaptic activity and thus have the potential to modulate neurotransmission73. Our data support that astrocytic AMPAR activation participate in Ca²⁺ elevations and, when receptor desensitization is experimentally inhibited, can engage ATP-dependent signaling that propagate to neighboring cells. In this context, purinergic receptors are broadly expressed throughout the CNS and contribute to co-transmission, neuromodulation, and trophic signaling across brain regions74. These findings are compatible with a model in which strong or sustained AMPAR activation could recruit purinergic amplification loops, although whether this occurs under physiological or pathophysiological patterns of glutamate release remains to be established. In this regard, accumulating evidence indicates that the dynamics of AMPAR gating are not strictly unidirectional. Certain auxiliary subunits, particularly TARPs such as γ-4, γ-7 and γ-8, can confer to AMPAR complexes a pronounced steady-state activity or partial resensitization during sustained glutamate exposure75–77. These effects are characterized by a resurgence or maintenance of current after the initial desensitization phase under continuous agonist application, suggesting that AMPARs can maintain or recover functionality in specific molecular contexts. Notably, CACNG4 transcript levels are relatively superior to other TARPs, and γ-4 expression has been previously reported in glia20,78. Such mechanisms might become particularly relevant under conditions in which glutamate clearance is compromised and extracellular glutamate remains elevated for prolonged periods. In these scenarios, astrocytic AMPARs, could be more robustly engaged, providing a potential route for sustained receptor activation and contribution to signaling dynamics. It remains true that CTZ artificially amplifies steady-state currents by preventing desensitization, resulting in responses that do not directly reflect physiological conditions. Whether similar effects occur under normal or pathophysiological patterns of glutamate release remains to be established.

Thus, overall, our findings demonstrate that (1) hippocampal astrocytes express functional AMPARs in vitro, (2) these AMPARs are capable of generating calcium signaling, (3) a subset of these AMPARs are calcium-permeable despite the abundant expression of GluA2 subunit in the astrocytic cultures, and (4) calcium signaling driven by AMPAR activity can engage ATP-mediated intercellular signaling only in the presence of CTZ. All observations were obtained in dissociated primary astrocyte cultures and rely on pharmacological stimulation, both of which impose an artificial context. Therefore, extrapolation to intact tissue should be made with caution.

Methods

Animals and housing

All animal procedures are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Wild-type C57BL/6J mice were housed in standard cages with ad libitum access to food and water, under controlled light–dark cycles. The animals were handled in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the European Directive 2010/63/EU, and Spanish and Catalan regulations (Decret 214/97; Generalitat de Catalunya) concerning the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. All procedures were approved by the Ethical Committee for Animal Research (CEEA-UB) at the University of Barcelona, under license number OB117/16, with DS as the principal investigator.

Astrocytic primary cultures from Hippocampus

Astrocytes were obtained from postnatal P0–P2 C57BL/6J mice. Neonatal mice were euthanized by rapid decapitation under ice anesthesia, in accordance with institutional and European ethical guidelines. The absence of reflexes was confirmed prior to decapitation, and all procedures were performed by trained personnel. Hippocampi were then dissected and preserved in pre-cooled Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, Gibco), supplemented with 0.45% glucose to maintain tissue viability. The hippocampal tissue was finely chopped and subjected to a 20 min-Trypsin digestion (Type XI; Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After enzymatic treatment, the tissue was washed twice with pre-warmed HBSS to eliminate residual trypsin activity. Mechanical dissociation was then performed in plating medium by gently passing the hippocampal tissue through a small-diameter pipette. The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 1,000×g for 5 min and the pelleted cells were resuspended and plated into poly-D-lysine-coated T25 flasks. The cultures were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 2% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) until they reached confluence. To remove oligodendrocytes and neurons, the flasks were subjected to 2 h of shaking. After discarding the media and to separate astrocytes from microglial cells, the cultures were trypsinized and reseeded onto successive culture dishes or plates. Microglial cells adhered tightly to the flask surfaces, while astrocytes remained in suspension and were collected from the supernatant. The isolated primary astrocytes were maintained for an additional 7 days in complete growth medium before further experimentation.

Immunofluorescence

The immunofluorescence was performed in astrocytic cultures on coverslips fixed at 14DIV. Astrocytes were washed and fixed for 10 min in fixation solution (4% PFA in PBS) at room temperature (RT). After three washes, cells were permeabilized (0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS-CM) for 5–10 min and blocked (10% NGS, 2% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 30 min at RT. Cells were first incubated with anti-GFAP conjugated with 488 (Invitrogen, No. 53-9892-82, 1:250) in antibody incubation solution (4% normal goat serum and 0.1% BSA in PBS + 0.1% Triton X-100) during 2 h at RT. After six washes astrocytes were incubated in anti-GLAST-1 conjugated with phycoerythrin (Miltenyi Biotec, No. 130118344, 1:50) in antibody incubation solution, overnight at 4 °C. After 6 washes coverslips were mounted in ProLongTM Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen, No. P36971). Incubations with antibodies were performed in a humid chamber. Washes were performed by immersion of the coverslips in PBS with Calcium and Magnesium (PBS-CM).

Confocal Imaging

Images were collected using a Leica AF6000 motorized microscopy system (Leica Microsystems, Manheim, Germany) equipped with a DMI6000 microscope, a Leica PL Fluotar 40x Numerical aperture 0.6 dry, a high-resolution monochroma Hamamatsu Orca ER C4742-80 Digital Camera and a mercury metal halide bulb Leica EL6000 as light source. Image acquisition program was LASX Navigator. DAPI was acquired with a band pass excitation filter 340–380 nm, dichromatic mirror (400 nm) and a long pass emission filter (425 nm). Alexa Fluor 488 was acquired with a band pass excitation filter 480/40nm, dichromatic mirror 505 nm and a band pass emission filter (527/30 nm). Phycoerythrin was acquired with a band pass excitation filter 531/40nm, dichromatic mirror band99-555 and transmission 659–730 nm and a band pass emission filter (593/40 nm). Images were acquired in a 1344 × 1024 pixels format, zoom at 1 and pixel size of 294.7 × 294.7 nm.

Protein extraction and wester-blotting

Protein extraction process from hippocampal astrocyte culture began with a gentle PBS wash at room temperature. Cultured cells were then scraped into ice-cold lysis buffer (1% IGEPAL, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10% Glycerol, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche) and PMSF). After a 10-minute incubation on ice and a brief 2” sonication, the insoluble material was pelleted by 4º centrifugation at 20,000×g for 15–20 min and supernatants were quantified. Hippocampi, cerebellums and frontal cortices were obtained from C57BL/6J mice of 1 or 2 months of age. Mice were euthanized by rapid decapitation under isoflurane anesthesia (3% for induction), in accordance with institutional and European ethical guidelines. The absence of reflexes was confirmed prior to decapitation, and all procedures were performed by trained personnel. After dissection, they were sonicated in cold Tris-HCl pH 7.4 (with Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche) and PMSF) with a Broanson Sonifier 150 (5 watts). The homogenate was centrifuged at 16,000×g for 30 min at 4 Cº. The pellet was resuspended by pipetting up and down in ice-cold lysis buffer and solubilized during 30 min in an orbital agitator. Insoluble material was pelleted at 16,000×g for 30 min.

Protein concentration in both supernatants was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) and stored at -80 °C. Samples were prepared for protein separation using protein loading buffer x2 supplemented with 10 mM DTT and heated at 80 °C for 10 min ensuring proper denaturation. 40 µg of hippocampal astrocyte cultures homogenates and 10 µg of brain extracts were then resolved on 8% mini protean SDS/PAGE gels using transfer buffer (BioRad). The transfer was performed using the Blot Turbo Transfer System onto nitrocellulose membranes, which were subsequently marked for identification. Upon completion, the membranes were thoroughly immersed and blocked utilizing TBS-T (20 mM Tris, 137 mM NaCl, 3.9 mM HCl, 0.1% Tween 20) and 3% (wt/vol) BSA, for 1 h. The gel employed in this process was stained with the esteemed Bio Safe Coomassie, enabling an assessment of the protein transfer efficacy. Once blocked, a series of five thorough washes, each lasting 5 min, were performed in TBS-T with a subsequent overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C. Membranes were subsequently washed and incubated in peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories), diluted in a blocking solution, for 2 h at RT. Finally, five washes were performed with TBS-T and detection was performed utilizing WesternBright ECL reagent (Advansta), with the signal detected in the Chemidoc system (BioRad). Quantification of bands was performed with ImageLab software (BioRad). Antibodies and concentrations used are as follows: Anti-GluA1 (N-terminus) MAB2263 Merck Millipore (1:1000) - Anti-Mouse (1:5000); Anti-GluA2 MAB397 Merck Millipore (1:1000) – Anti-Mouse (1:2000); Anti-GluA3 MAB5416 Merck Millipore (1:400)- Anti-Mouse (1:5000); Anti-GluA4 AGC019 Alomone labs (1:200) - Anti-Rabbit (1:1000); Anti-Actin A5316 Sigma (1:5000)-AntiMouse (1:20000); Anti-P2 × 7 (1:200) Alomone labs - Anti Rabbit (1:2000).

Calcium-imaging

Primary astrocyte cultures from wild-type mice were incubated with 5 µM fura-2/AM (Invitrogen, Nº F1221) for 20–30 min at 37 °C in their culture medium. Following incubation, the fura-2-loaded cells on coverslips were transferred to an open flow chamber (0.5 ml) mounted on the stage of an inverted Olympus IX70 microscope. The system was equipped with a LED illumination system Lambda 721 (Sutter Instrument, Novato, Ca) and a cooled CCD camera (Orca Fusion, Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan) for image acquisition. Captured images were saved and analyzed using HCImage Software (Hamamatsu Corporation, Bridgewater, NJ, USA). Following a stabilization period, image pairs were collected every 4 s to monitor calcium dynamics. Excitation wavelengths of 340 nm (λ1) and 385 nm (λ2) with 10-nm bandwidth filters were used to selectively excite the Ca²⁺-bound and Ca²⁺-free forms of fura-2, respectively, while emissions were detected at 510 nm using a 12-nm bandwidth filter. Intracellular calcium concentrations ([Ca²⁺]i) were calculated for individual cells from the ratio of fluorescence intensities at 340 nm to 385 nm over time. For visualization and quantification, calcium signals were expressed as fold change relative to baseline, with baseline values normalized to 1; vertical scale bars in trace panels reflect this fold-change scaling. An increase of 8% of the baseline signal was considered a positive response. Only astrocytes that exhibited a calcium response to ionomycin depolarization (3 µM) were included in the analysis. To ensure generalizability, experiments were performed with cells from multiple primary cultures and animals across all experimental groups. The standard extracellular (bath) solution contained 140 mM NaCl, 4 mM KCl, 1.3 mM CaCl₂, 1 mM MgCl₂, 5 mM glucose, and 10 mM HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH. A Na⁺-free solution (composition: 140 mM N-methyl-D-glucamine, 4 mM KCl, 1.3 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 5 mM glucose, 10 mM HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4 with HCl) and a Ca²⁺-free solution (composition: 145 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 10 mM glucose, 1 mM EGTA, and 20 mM HEPES, at pH 7.4 with NaOH; nominally Ca2+ free, estimated < 10 nM) were also employed where indicated. All experiments were conducted at room temperature to minimize variability in calcium signaling dynamics. The drugs applied during the different recordings included alpha-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) at 20 µM (Alomone, Cat No. A-267), 2,3-dihydroxy-6-nitro-7-sulfamoyl-benzo[f]quinoxaline (NBQX) at 50 µM (Tocris, Cat. No. 1044), cyclothiazide (CTZ) at 10 µM (Tocris, Cat. No. 0713), ionomycin at 3 µM (Sigma-Aldrich Merck, Cat. No. I0634), N-acetylspermine (NASPM) at 50 µM (Tocris, Cat. No. 2766), adenosine triphosphate (ATP) at 10 µM (Sigma-Aldrich Merck, Cat. No. A1852-1VL), and A-438079 hydrochloride hydrate at 10 µM (Sigma-Aldrich Merck, Cat. No. A9736).

Data analysis

Calcium imaging recordings were initially analyzed in HCImage Software (Hamamatsu Corporation, Bridgewater, NJ, USA). Subsequently, raw data were extracted and exported to Excel and further processed and analyzed using MATLAB vR2023b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Western blotting data were analyzed using Bio-Rad ImageLab (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), while immunofluorescence images were processed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed to identify the appropriate statistical assumptions for inferential analysis. For normality, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used in conjunction with Q-Q plots. Regarding variance, Levene’s and Bartlett’s tests were applied to determine whether the group variances were sufficiently similar. Subsequently, for paired data meeting the normality assumption, parametric tests (paired t test) were performed, while for paired data not following a normal distribution, non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon matched-pairs) were applied. In cases where the data were unpaired, group comparisons were conducted using unpaired t test (normal distribution) and Mann-Whitney test (non-normal distribution). In all cases, a 95% confidence level with a 5% alpha error (α) was assumed. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism v5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and figure design was completed with Adobe Illustrator 25.2.3 2021 (Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA, USA). Data is presented as bar graphs showing group means, with error bars representing the standard error of the mean (SEM). In the text, p-values, confidence intervals, standard deviations, effect sizes (Cohen’s d), and/or rank-biserial correlations are reported as appropriate.
