Intergenerational initiatives to support health and well-being of people aging with traumatic brain injury: an exploratory qualitative research protocol
Authors: Megan Veilleux, Pascale Simard, Mia Lapointe, Ronen Ore, Ann-Frédérique Giguère, Angéline Labbé, Marie-Christine Ouellet, Krista L. Best, Samuel Turcotte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increase in traumatic brain injury (TBI) among older adults in the context of an aging population poses significant challenges, including limited opportunities for social participation and decreased overall well-being. The double stigmatization due to ableism and ageism may further worsen these challenges. To date, interventions designed to support social participation of this population remain scarce and under documented, particularly within community-based settings. Intergenerational initiatives have shown positive impacts on health and social participation. Yet they remain largely unexplored for people aging with TBI.ObjectiveThis project aims to (1) explore the potential outcomes of implementing intergenerational initiatives for people aging with TBI in community settings; (2) identify facilitators and barriers to the planning, implementation, and evaluation of such initiatives; and (3) determine appropriate evaluation strategies for these initiatives for people aging with TBI.MethodsThis descriptive qualitative project is part of a broader participatory action research project in which an intergenerational initiative was co-designed by and for people living with TBI. Semi-structured interviews will be conducted with people aging with TBI (n = 4), intergenerational program experts (n = 4), community workers (n = 4), and managers from community-based organizations (n = 4). The interview guide was co-developed by the lead student researcher (M.V.) and the lead researcher (S.T.) through an iterative process, in collaboration with members of the research team (M.L., P.S., A.L.). Data will be analyzed using an inductive approach structured by the Framework Method.ConclusionThis project will generate essential knowledge to support the development of intergenerational initiatives tailored to the needs and realities of people aging with TBI, an area that remains largely unaddressed. This project will offer insights into the potential of these initiatives to enhance social participation, reduce isolation, and foster inclusive community environments. In addition, the findings will highlight the main conditions and obstacles influencing the implementation process, providing actionable recommendations for practitioners and organizations. Finally, it will inform best practices for evaluating the outcomes and impacts of intergenerational initiatives, considering participant profiles and contextual factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07318-9.

Main text

Background

By 2031, traumatic brain injury (TBI) is expected to become one of the most prevalent neurological conditions in Canada, alongside Alzheimer’s disease and epilepsy [1]. TBI is a brain injury resulting from an external force that alters normal brain function or causes detectable brain pathology [2]. The most common causes of TBI are falls, sports and recreational activities, and transportation accidents, with falls being the leading cause among older adults [3, 4]. Globally, TBI incidence is expected to rise, particularly in high-income countries, due to an increasing number of falls in older populations [5]. Currently, 2% of the Canadian population lives with chronic consequences of TBI, and each year, approximately 18,000 hospitalizations are related to this condition [6]. Indirect costs associated with TBI, including disability and productivity loss, are projected to reach $8.2 billion annually by 2031 for TBIs requiring hospitalization [1]. Advances in medical care, social services, and rehabilitation have increased life expectancy for people living with TBI, leading to a growing number of people aging with TBI [7–9]. TBIs can result in multiple long-term consequences, including cognitive, behavioral, emotional, and physical impairments [6, 10]. These consequences vary depending on the brain regions affected and may significantly impact daily life, reducing quality of life and increasing social isolation [6, 10]. Aging people with TBI also face a heightened risk of poor functional outcomes and long-term care admissions [11]. Furthermore, they may experience intersectional stigma related to both age and disability, which can further limit their social participation and impact their overall health [12–17].

Social participation in later life is defined as « a person involvement in initiatives providing interactions with others in community life and important shared spaces, evolving according to available time and resources, and based on the societal context and what people want and is meaningful to them» [18]. It is a key determinant of physical, cognitive, and psychological health and an essential factor in healthy aging [19, 20].

Among promising approaches to support social participation, intergenerational initiatives are gaining interest. These initiatives intentionally bring together younger and older people through varied activities (e.g., artistic, physical, culinary). Studies have shown that such initiatives improve older adults’ health and social participation [21–23] while reducing age-related stereotypes by encouraging experience-sharing and meaningful connections [21, 22, 24, 25]. Specifically, they enhance life satisfaction, cognitive abilities, and both mental and physical health among older adults [22]. In addition to strong empirical support, intergenerational initiatives are endorsed by policy frameworks [15, 26]. As an example, the World Health Organization promotes those initiatives as a strategy to combat ageism [15], and Quebec’s provincial 2024–2029 plan, The Pride of Aging, identifies those initiatives as a preferred action to address ageism [26]. Despite their documented benefits for the general population, these initiatives remain largely underutilized for people aging with TBI. Considering the unique consequences associated with TBI, planning, implementing, and evaluating these initiatives will likely involve additional considerations. Given their potential, it is crucial to adapt intergenerational initiatives to meet the specific needs and preferences of people aging with TBI.

In Canada, community-based organizations play a crucial role in supporting social participation for people with moderate to severe TBI, by providing interaction spaces that foster peer support and social identity reconstruction. These organizations help address isolation and promote inclusion [27]. However, few interventions have been specifically developed or evaluated for older adults with TBI [20, 28].

Objectives

The research question guiding this project is as follows: can intergenerational initiatives involving people aging with TBI be offered in community settings?

To address this question, three objectives have been identified:(1) Explore the potential outcomes of implementing intergenerational initiatives for people aging with TBI in community settings;(2) Examine the facilitators and barriers to the planning, implementation, and evaluation of intergenerational initiatives for this population in community contexts; and.(3) Explore strategies for evaluating intergenerational initiatives for people aging with TBI.

The findings of this project will help clarify not only the potential relevance and anticipated impacts of intergenerational initiatives involving people aging with TBI in community settings, but also the practical conditions under which such initiatives can be planned, supported, and evaluated. Beyond documenting perceived needs, expectations, and interests, the study will identify concrete methodological avenues for assessing intergenerational programming in this context. Collectively, these insights will provide foundational guidance for organizations seeking to design or adapt intergenerational activities that meaningfully promote social participation and quality of life among people aging with TBI.

Research team

This study is grounded in a socio-constructivist epistemological paradigm, which assumes that knowledge is co-constructed through social interaction and shaped by contextual, experiential, and relational processes [29]. From this perspective, realities are understood as multiple and situated, and meaning emerges through dialogue between researchers, community partners, and participants. This stance aligns with the exploratory and community-based nature of the project [29]. The research team is interdisciplinary and brings together complementary expertise in participatory research and co-development (K.B.), TBI (M-C.O.), intergenerational initiatives, and social inclusion (S.T.). Team members also have experience in knowledge mobilization and community-engaged research, supporting the translation of findings into practice. Graduate students (M.V., P.S., M.L., R.O., A-F. G.) and a research professional (A.L.) from diverse disciplinary backgrounds (e.g. psychology, criminology) will contribute to data collection and analysis, further enriching reflexive dialogue within the team.

The team adopts a “with and for” approach to research, emphasizing collaboration with community partners and valuing experiential, professional, and scientific knowledge equally [30]. This orientation is consistent with evidence showing that co-designed and partnership-based interventions are more likely to be relevant, feasible, and successfully implemented in real-world settings [31, 32].

Reflexivity will be supported through interdisciplinary collaboration, iterative team discussions, and ongoing engagement with the partner organization to ensure that interpretations remain grounded in community realities.

Method

Context

Several key concepts will guide this project. First, intergenerational initiatives are understood as initiatives that intentionally bring together younger and older people through shared actions, taking various forms and spanning different sectors [33, 34]. The concept of potential outcomes refers to any anticipated positive or negative effects generated by the implementation of intergenerational initiatives for people aging with TBI. Facilitators are understood as elements that support the planning, implementation, or evaluation of intergenerational initiatives, particularly by helping to overcome one or more barriers. Conversely, barriers refer to factors that may hinder or prevent these same processes. Lastly, evaluation strategies are defined as the systematic methods and approaches used to assess the implementation, outcomes, and impacts of intergenerational initiatives, with the aim of informing practice and supporting decision-making [35].

Study design

For this study, a descriptive interpretive qualitative design will be used, also referred to as an exploratory study, as it facilitates an in-depth understanding of a selected phenomenon [36]. This design emphasizes a descriptive, low-inference analysis, which enables the research team to accurately capture the realities, perceptions, and experiences of participants in community settings [36]. It is particularly suited to small-scale, exploratory studies, such as the present project, addressing a specific and understudied topic [37]. Descriptive interpretive designs are flexible and pragmatic, allowing the collection and analysis of rich data without imposing strict theoretical constraints, while maintaining rigor through systematic coding and iterative team review [37]. This qualitative study is nested within a broader participatory action research project (PAR). While the larger project aims to co-develop an intergenerational program designed by and for people aging with TBI, the present study does not constitute a co-development phase. Rather, this qualitative study represents the foundational exploratory stage of the innovation process, generating preliminary insights to inform key phases of the intergenerational program development (Ideate, Prototype, and Test) [38], support the scaling of this innovation, and guide the co-development of other community-based intergenerational initiatives involving people aging with TBI.

Study setting

This study and the PAR are conducted in partnership with a community-based organization in Quebec, Canada, that offers individualized support and group-based activities for people living with TBI, with a focus on social participation and community inclusion. The organization provides a range of creative, recreational, and social initiatives aimed at fostering meaningful engagement among its members.

Over time, the organization has observed changes in its membership profile, including an increasing number of people aging with TBI alongside younger members experiencing difficulties connecting across age groups. These evolving dynamics highlighted emerging challenges related to social participation and peer relationships within the community setting. In response, the organization initiated discussions with the research team to explore intergenerational approaches as a potential avenue to better address these changing needs. This collaboration laid the foundation for the broader PAR, within which the present exploratory study is embedded.

Ethical approvals

Ethical approval for the broader PAR was obtained from the Research Ethics Board of the Integrated University Health and Social Services Center of the National Capital (Project 2024–3050). An additional amendment will be submitted to authorize the interviews conducted as part of the present study. All participants will provide written informed consent prior to participation.

Participants and sampling

A purposive non-probabilistic sampling strategy will be used for this project to foster diversity in the characteristics of recruited participants (i.e., sex, gender, diagnosis, time since diagnosis, etc.) [39]. This approach is particularly appropriate in the exploratory phase of qualitative research, such as the present project, where empirical data on the topic remain limited [39]. In the context of this project, purposive sampling will allow for the collection of in-depth, context-specific knowledge from people with relevant expertise in TBI and intergenerational initiatives. In this project, knowledge is not limited to academic expertise but also includes experiential knowledge, in alignment with the principles of PAR, which values the complementarity and diversity of knowledge forms [40].

Four participant groups will be included: people living with TBI, intergenerational program experts, community workers and managers of community-based organizations providing services to people living with TBI. Recruitment

The recruitment of community workers, managers and people living with TBI will be carried out in partnership with the partner organization among people who have completed the prior training. The intergenerational program experts will be identified and recruited based on their established professional and academic networks, leveraging the research team’s prior collaborations and knowledge of recognized experts in the field. All participants will be contacted either by email or in person for those who have previously provided consent in a prior study. Interested individuals will be invited to respond directly to either the lead student researcher (M.V.) or the lead investigator (S.T.) to indicate their willingness to participate. They will then receive a detailed information sheet and provide written informed consent before any study activities begin. Participants will also be able to contact M.V. or S.T. at any time with questions or to withdraw from the study. An estimated total of 16 interviews (4 participants/per group) is planned, although this number may be adjusted based on the achievement of data saturation, defined in an inductive analysis as the point at which no new codes or themes are identified [41]. This estimate is informed by sample size thresholds typically reported in the literature for similar qualitative designs [42] and by the projected availability of eligible participants. However, the heterogeneity of participant groups and the limited number of eligible people may limit the feasibility of achieving complete data saturation [43].

Data collection

Individual semi-structured interviews will be used with each participant. The semi-structured interview guide (see Table 2 in the Appendix) was co-developed by the lead student researcher (M.V.) and the lead researcher (S.T.) through an iterative process, in collaboration with members of the research team (M.L., P.S., A.L.). A toolkit designed for practice settings to support the development, implementation, and evaluation of intergenerational initiatives [44, 45] was used as a conceptual framework to inform key content areas. Based on this framework, researchers collaboratively developed and refined the interview questions to ensure alignment with the study objectives and relevance for the target population. The tool was subsequently pre-tested with the partner organization’s knowledge broker via a pilot administration, followed by a structured debriefing to document comprehension issues, feasibility, and contextual relevance. As a liaison between the community and research sectors, she offers in-depth insight into the project context, realities associated with TBI, and scientific requirements.

Interviews will be scheduled at times and locations convenient for participants: at home, at the partner organization’s facilities, or remotely via videoconference, to minimize logistical barriers such as transportation. The interview guide (see Table 2 in the Appendix) and a sociodemographic questionnaire will be provided to participants prior to the interviews. Written informed consent will be obtained from each participant prior to the start of each interview. Given the potential effects of fatigue, participants will be offered breaks or the option to complete interviews over multiple sessions if needed. They will be conducted individually, lasting approximately 60 min, by a graduate student holding a bachelor’s degree in psychology (M.V). Each interview will be audio-recorded and fully transcribed verbatim by the lead student researcher (M.V.), with participants’ consent. Transcriptions will be completed by the lead student researcher (M.V.) to promote immersion in the data and subsequently. The findings will be reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) (see Additional file 1), a 32-item checklist designed to ensure the comprehensive reporting of qualitative research components [46]. The sociodemographic questionnaire will gather information such as age, sex, gender, occupation, and, where applicable, TBI-related characteristics.

Data analysis

Sociodemographic data will be analyzed using basic descriptive statistics (means, ranges, frequencies) and summarized narratively to develop a general participant profile. The study will focus on gathering participants’ perceptions, insights, and reflections regarding intergenerational initiatives, based on their exposure to the concept through the preliminary training on intergenerational initiatives. Ultimately, the aim is to integrate evidence-based knowledge, experiential insights, and field perspectives to provide a comprehensive understanding.

Data analysis will follow an inductive approach structured by the Framework Method [47] and will be conducted by the research team using NVivo 15 software to assist with data management and coding [48]. This method is particularly suitable for applied health research and PAR involving multidisciplinary teams and mixed expertise. Knowledge mobilization plan

A knowledge mobilization plan will be co-developed to ensure the integration and dissemination of knowledge generated through the project. The nature of the knowledge mobilization initiatives, as well as their format, will be determined in collaboration with the steering committee members. Initiatives could include the development of infographics or informational pamphlets for people living with TBI and various stakeholders within the community sector. Accessible articles intended for the various community organizations of Connexion TCC (the provincial network of TBI associations in Quebec, Canada) may also be produced. The choice of initiatives will be made in consultation with stakeholders to ensure alignment with their needs and interests concerning knowledge on intergenerational initiatives.

A peer-reviewed scientific article will result from this project and be submitted for publication (e.g., in Brain Injury or The Gerontologist). The project’s findings will also be presented at both local and international conferences (e.g., Aging and Social Change, 2025; 15th World Congress on Brain Injury, 2025; Faculty of Medicine and Affiliated Research Centers Research Day, 2025).

Discussion

This project is expected to contribute to a deeper understanding of the potential outcomes, facilitators, barriers and evaluation strategies associated with implementing intergenerational initiatives for people aging with TBI in community settings. Although intergenerational initiatives have shown positive effects on health and social participation in older adults [21, 22], very few studies have explored their application in populations living with neurological conditions, and even fewer in people aging with TBI [49]. The complexity of TBI-related consequences combined with intersectional stigma presents unique challenges that have rarely been addressed in existing intergenerational initiatives. This project will generate new knowledge by documenting the relevance and feasibility of such initiatives for this population and by identifying ways to tailor them to their specific needs and preferences.

Moreover, the project will contribute to refining methodological approaches for evaluating participatory community-based initiatives with populations presenting cognitive and functional limitations. The insights gained from this project could inform future rehabilitation practices and the design of social inclusion initiatives targeting people aging with acquired disabilities.

Strengths and methodological rigor

Several measures will be implemented to ensure the rigor and credibility of this exploratory qualitative project. Interviews will be transcribed verbatim by the lead student researcher (M.V.). To ensure transcription accuracy and consistency, three transcripts will be reviewed by a second team member (M.H.N.). The Framework Method [47] will provide a structured, transparent, and systematic analytical approach, allowing for comparisons both within and between participant groups. Open coding will be conducted independently by the graduate student and a qualitative research expert on the first three transcripts to enhance inter-coder reliability. Regular consensus meetings will be held to develop, refine, and stabilize the coding framework before its application to the entire dataset. The use of a framework matrix will further strengthen analytical consistency and facilitate an in-depth examination of similarities and differences across perspectives. Preliminary interpretations will be discussed with the partner organization to validate their relevance and ensure that they accurately reflect participants’ thoughts.

The diversity of participant profiles will allow for triangulation of viewpoints. The integration of equity, diversity, and inclusion (EDI) principles at all stages of recruitment, data collection and dissemination represent another important strength, ensuring that the realities of people aging with TBI are ethically and meaningfully considered. Finally, the methodological rigor of the Framework Method, supported by a team of experienced qualitative researchers and community partners, will contribute to the overall credibility, transparency, and trustworthiness of the results.

Limitations

Some potential limitations should be acknowledged. First, the small sample size, although consistent with qualitative research standards and justified by data saturation principles, may limit the generalizability of findings beyond the specific context of Quebec’s community-based organizations. Family members and caregivers will not be included in this exploratory phase, which may limit the range of perspectives captured. Although their contributions will be essential in subsequent stages of co-development, implementation, and evaluation, the present study prioritizes participants who would potentially be directly involved in the intergenerational initiatives and who had prior training on the concept to generate preliminary, experience-informed guidance. The dual role of the lead student researcher as both coordinator of the research action project and interviewer in this project may introduce interpretive bias, despite planned strategies such as independent coding, validation by a qualitative expert, and collaborative interpretation with the steering committee. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of participant profiles might complicate the interpretation of differences between subgroups. The structured matrix approach of the Framework Method and planned consensus meetings are designed to mitigate this risk. Moreover, the ultimate goal remains to integrate the findings from each group. Finally, although intergenerational initiatives have demonstrated benefits in the general aging population [22–24], the absence of comparable studies in people with TBI may limit direct comparison of possible outcomes [49].

Conclusion

This exploratory study aims to generate new knowledge on the potential outcomes, facilitators, barriers, and evaluation strategies of intergenerational initiatives for people aging with traumatic brain injury (TBI) in community settings. By documenting the relevance, and ways to tailor these initiatives to the specific needs and preferences of this population, the project will fill a critical gap in the literature. The findings are expected to inform the design of future community-based intergenerational programs and support the development of inclusive strategies that enhance social participation and well-being among people aging with TBI.
