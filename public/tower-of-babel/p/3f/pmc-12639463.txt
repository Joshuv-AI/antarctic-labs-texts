Quantification of eight clinically relevant serum adrenal steroids in infants by UHPLC-APCI-MS/MS
Authors: Manuela R Martinefski, Verónica Ambao, María Gabriela Ballerini, María Eugenia Rodriguez, Rodolfo A Rey, María Eugenia Monge, María Gabriela Ropelato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A new UHPLC-APCI-MS/MS-based method was validated to determine eight serum steroids.•It requires 4-fold less sample volume than traditional immunoassays.•It was successfully applied to infants under three months of age.•It has large impact on the assessment of paediatric adrenal disorders in Argentina.Keywords: Adrenal disorders, Paediatric samples, Steroid profile, UHPLC-APCI-MS/MS

Introduction

Accurate and timely diagnosis of adrenal disease is challenging in paediatrics. It requires highly sensitive and specific analytical methods, the ability to assess a panel of steroids in a small biological sample volume, and the availability of clinically relevant reference data [1]. Most clinical laboratories worldwide working on the diagnosis and monitoring of adrenal and gonadal disorders determine steroid hormone levels using immunoassays. This is mainly due to their technical ease, cost, and the availability of commercial reagents [2,3]. However, these analytical methods suffer from limited specificity due to cross-reactivity, leading to many false-positive results and poor predictive values [4,5]. There is growing interest in the endocrine community in replacing individual steroid immunoassays with ultra-high performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS)-based methods [1,5], which offer higher selectivity, specificity, and sensitivity, as well as the capability to resolve steroid panels with a single sample [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]].

The aim of this study was to develop and validate a UHPLC-MS/MS-based method using atmospheric pressure chemical ionisation (APCI) for the simultaneous analysis of cortisol, androstenedione, testosterone, pregnenolone, progesterone, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, 17-hydroxypregnenolone, and dehydroepiandrosterone. The clinical performance of the method was assessed in 61 infants under three months of age, including healthy infants born at term or prematurely, and patients with confirmed adrenal disorders.

Material and methods

Materials

Standards of cortisol, androstenedione, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, progesterone, 17-hydroxypregnenolone, pregnenolone, testosterone, dehydroepiandrosterone, and pregnenolone-13C2-d2 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The Internal Standard Mix MassChrom® Steroids in serum/plasma (IS Mix) was supplied by Chromsystems (Gräfelfing, Germany). LC-MS grade solvents and zinc sulphate were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Steroid-free serum (SFS) was prepared from a pool of surplus serum samples from prepubertal children by treatment with activated charcoal using a method adapted from Peitzsch et al. [12]. The external quality control (QC) materials Lyphochek® Immunoassay Plus (Bio-Rad; Hercules, CA, USA) and RIQAS (Randox Laboratories Ltd.; Crumlin, ANT, UK) were used. The trueness of the method was evaluated for cortisol, testosterone, and progesterone using the NIST® SRM® 1950 Metabolites in Frozen Plasma (National Institute of Standards and Technology; Gaithersburg, MD, USA).

Preparation of chemical standard solutions, quality control and study samples

Stock solutions of each steroid standard were prepared in methanol at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and stored at –20 °C. They were used to prepare working standard mixtures by diluting with methanol. All calibrators and internal QC samples were freshly prepared in SFS and processed in the same manner as study samples.

Zinc sulphate in methanol was used for protein precipitation [13,14]. Briefly, to obtain each calibrator, 100 µL of SFS was spiked with 10 µL of the appropriate working standard mix. Clinical samples and QC samples were supplemented with 10 μL of methanol to unify the conditions between samples and calibrators. All samples were treated with 90 μL of protein-precipitating solution [0.55 M ZnSO4 aqueous stock solution diluted with methanol (1/10 v/v), including internal standards (31 nmol/L pregnenolone-13C2-d2 and the IS Mix solution 1/10 v/v in methanol)]. Subsequently, all samples were vortex-mixed for 30 s, incubated at −20 °C for 15 min, and centrifuged at 14,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. Afterwards, 150 µL of supernatant was transferred into LC vials for UHPLC-MS/MS analysis.

UHPLC-MS/MS method

UHPLC-MS/MS analyses were performed in a Shimadzu Nexera X2 UHPLC system (Shimadzu Corporation; Kioto, Japan) coupled to a QTRAP®6500 + mass spectrometer (Sciex; Framingham, MA, USA) with an APCI source fitted with a Kinetex®C18 column (100 × 3 mm, 2.6 µm) and a SecurityGuard® guard-column (3 × 2 mm; Phenomenex; Torrance, CA, USA).

The mobile phases were ultrapure water (A) and methanol (B). The gradient for (B) was set as follows: 0–0.05 min 10 %, 0.05–0.55 min 10–55 %, 0.55–2.25 min 55–60 %, 2.25–4.5 min 60–80 %, 4.5–5.0 min 80–95 %, 5.0–7.5 min 95–95 %, 7.5–8.0 min 10 % and 8.0–10 min 10 %. The flow rate was set at 1 mL/min, and the column temperature at 45 °C. The injection volume was 50 μL; the autosampler tray was kept at 15 °C. The mass spectrometer was operated in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) and positive-ion mode. Two mass transitions (quantifier and qualifier) for each analyte were selected by direct infusion of pure standard solutions. The MS parameters were optimised by automated flow injection analysis to achieve maximum sensitivity. The source temperature, nebulizer current and curtain gas were also optimized, collision gas was set at medium, and gas 1 (ion source gas) was set at 40 psi. Unit resolution was used for the first and third quadrupoles. Data were acquired with Analyst 1.6.2 software and analysed using MultiQuant software (Sciex; Framingham, MA, USA).

Validation

Linearity, limit of detection (LOD), lower limit of quantification (LLOQ), precision, accuracy, matrix effects, recovery, selectivity, carry-over, dilution integrity, and stability were determined according to the acceptance criteria of both FDA [15] and EMA guidelines [16]. Briefly, linearity was evaluated using a seven-point calibration curve. The LOD and LLOQ were calculated as the concentrations given by signals equal to 3.3 sy / slope and 10 sy / slope, respectively. Precision and accuracy (evaluated through recovery assays) were assessed by spiking SFS at four levels (LLOQ, low, medium, and high QC). For within-run precision and accuracy, each sample was analysed five times. For between-run precision and accuracy, samples were analysed five times on three different days. Precision and accuracy results were expressed as coefficients of variation (CVs) and as percent of the nominal value, respectively. Recovery, matrix effects, and overall process efficiency were assessed by the method proposed by Matuszewski et al. [17]. The selectivity of the analytical method was evaluated by spiking different serum samples with potential interferences, such as haemolysis and icterus, at two concentration levels of target steroids. Carryover was investigated by analysing solvent blanks after the highest calibrator level using different washing solutions. Dilution integrity was evaluated by spiking SFS with concentrations above the upper LOQ (ULOQ) and subsequently diluting 2-fold with SFS (n = 5). Stability of steroids in the extracted samples was monitored by reanalyzing SFS spiked at four levels (LLOQ, low, medium, and high QC; three replicates at each level) after storage in the autosampler at 15 °C for 24 h.

Clinical applications

Clinical performance of the UHPLC-APCI-MS/MS-based method was evaluated by measuring the eight-steroid panel in surplus serum samples from 61 children under three months of age, who were undergoing thyroid profile investigation and were found to be clinically and biochemically healthy after assessment. Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee approval was obtained from Hospital de Niños Dr. Ricardo Gutiérrez. Serum from 50 babies born at term [gestational age (GA) ≥ 37 weeks, 25 girls and 25 boys, chronological age (CA) range: 3–90 days-old] and eight babies born preterm (four girls and four boys; GA: 24–36 weeks, CA: 26–58 days) were assayed. Additionally, surplus serum samples of three infants with confirmed adrenal disorders were evaluated: one boy with CYP21 deficiency at diagnosis and while under 10 months of hydrocortisone treatment; one boy with ambiguous genitalia, P450 oxidoreductase deficiency (PORD); and one girl with an adrenal tumour at diagnosis. Immunoassays

Serum levels of cortisol, testosterone, and progesterone were measured by electrochemiluminescent immunoassay (ECLIA) on a Cobas e411 autoanalyzer (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Serum androstenedione and 17-hydroxyprogesterone concentrations were measured by commercial RIAs (Active®-Androstenedione RIA and RIA 17-hydroxyprogesterone, respectively; Beckman Coulter-Immunotech, Prague, Czech Republic). All assays were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Mann-Whitney U test or Wilcoxon signed rank test, and Spearman or Pearson correlations were used as appropriate, using GraphPad Prism version 8.00 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Coefficient of variation

No interference was observed in hemolyzed samples; conversely, icterus affected the measurement of low levels of dehydroepiandrosterone, pregnenolone, and 17-hydroxypregnenolone (Table S2). Carryover < 0.01 % was obtained using acetonitrile:isopropanol:0.1 % (v/v) formic acid in water, 1:1:1 (v/v). Dilution integrity was also evaluated, showing that dilution did not affect accuracy or precision (Table S3). Stability of Rt values, peak shapes, and repeatability of peak-area values within each analytical batch were demonstrated using an IQC sample. The calibration curves were stable for at least 24 h.

The concentration levels for 17-hydroxyprogesterone, testosterone and progesterone obtained by UHPLC-MS/MS were lower than those reported for each commercial QC sample (RIQAS and Biorad) using immunoassays (Table S4). For cortisol, lower values were observed using the ECLIA method in Bio-Rad 1 and 3.

The applicability of the developed method was evaluated on samples from healthy infants below three months of age (Fig. 1). Of the eight steroids, seven showed no significant differences in their levels when comparing males with females in 50 healthy children. Sexual dimorphism was observed only for testosterone concentration, which was significantly lower in female infants than in males (p < 0.0001). Serum cortisol levels varied widely, from non-detectable values up to 305.6 nmol/L. Serum 17-hydroxyprogesterone and androstenedione levels were significantly higher in infants born preterm compared with infants born at term (p < 0.01), and a trend towards higher pregnenolone and 17-hydroxypregnenolone levels was observed. The applicability of the method was also tested in samples from 3 children with confirmed adrenal disorders. A male patient with disorders of sexual development and P450 oxidoreductase deficiency exhibited elevated levels of pregnenolone, progesterone, and 17-hydroxyprogesterone; low levels of testosterone; and non-detectable levels of dehydroepiandrosterone and androstenedione. A male patient with congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) showed elevated levels of 17-hydroxyprogesterone, androstenedione, progesterone, and dehydroepiandrosterone at diagnosis, which decreased after treatment. The female patient with the androgen-producing tumour exhibited an elevated androgen pattern in serum.

Discussion

An UHPLC-MS/MS-based method for the simultaneous analysis of eight steroids of clinical relevance in children with adrenal disorders was developed and analytically validated. One of the challenges of steroid analysis in paediatrics involves the use of a minimal sample volume due to the known difficulties in blood extraction, particularly in neonates. The main advantage of the herein developed UHPLC-MS/MS-based method compared to immunoassays, which are commonly used in Argentina, is the simultaneous quantification of eight steroids with a 4-fold sample volume reduction (100 µL for UHPLC-MS/MS analysis vs. 400 µL for immunoassays). Another strength of the method is the simple sample preparation, based on a single protein precipitation step, with no requirement for derivatization or solvent evaporation, and avoiding the use of solid-phase extraction and liquid–liquid extraction methods that are time-consuming. The validated method also shows similar or even lower LLOQ values than other LC-MS/MS-based published works, making it suitable for paediatric sample analysis, including newborns [13,[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [13], [26], [27], [28], [29]].

Results from commercial QC samples showed an overestimation of 17-hydroxyprogesterone, testosterone and progesterone levels by immunological methods due to cross-reactivity, which is consistent with previous findings [3,10].

A limitation of the method is associated with the chemical interference observed for dehydroepiandrosterone, pregnenolone and 17-hydroxypregnenolone in icteric samples. However, it should be noted that it only affected lower analyte levels, and these steroids exhibit elevated levels in infants with adrenal disorders. In addition, only 24 h autosampler stability was evaluated.

Although UHPLC-MS/MS-based data on serum steroid levels in children under three months of age are scarce, our results showed no sex differences except for testosterone, which was significantly higher in males, as expected. This finding is in agreement with previous reports [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. In addition, serum cortisol concentrations varied widely within the cohort, supporting previous data from our group and others using immunoassays [32,33]. This variability arises from differences in the age at appearance of the circadian rhythm, which characterizes normal development in healthy infants.

The finding that infants born preterm exhibited higher levels of steroid precursors of cortisol synthesis than infants born at term agrees with results described by Fiet et al. [25], probably due to persistence of the adrenal inner foetal zone after birth [34,35].

To further test the performance of the developed UHPC-MS/MS-based method in pathological conditions, we studied three patients with confirmed adrenal pathologies. For a boy with CAH due to CYP21 deficiency, the method detected high levels of 17-hydroxyprogesterone at diagnosis, which decreased after 10 months of treatment. The simultaneous measurement of eight steroids in the same analytical assay showed that other steroids, such as progesterone, androstenedione, and dehydroepiandrosterone, also exhibited higher levels than controls, in agreement with the steroid profile observed in this pathology. The inclusion of steroids from the Δ4 and Δ5 pathways constitutes a differential diagnostic tool for other adrenal enzyme deficiencies, such as3ß-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase. The second case was a 46,XY boy with DSD due to PORD, who exhibited a typical steroid profile of this pathology [36], with accumulation of pregnenolone, progesterone, and 17-hydroxyprogesterone; low testosterone levels; and undetectable levels of androstenedione and dehydroepiandrosterone. Low androgen concentrations were in agreement with his genital hypovirilization. Since CYP17A1, CYP21A2, and CYP19A1 enzymes require the activity of POR, clinical and biochemical phenotypes can be found in these patients across a wide spectrum, depending on the mutation [37]. Therefore, an expanded steroid panel obtained by tandem MS-based methods is useful to biochemically characterize these cases. Finally, for the female patient with an adrenal androgen-producing tumour, the developed UHPLC-MS/MS-based method quantified extremely high levels of testosterone, androstenedione, and dehydroepiandrosterone compared with reported normal values [20,28,39]. Since our analytical method was developed for the analysis of less polar steroids, it was not possible to detect dehydroepiandrosterone-sulphate, which is a marker for androgen-secreting adrenal tumours [38]. However, dehydroepiandrosterone-sulphate levels determined by immunoassay in our laboratory were more than 10-fold higher than reference values (Table 1). In addition, high levels of androgens determined by UHPLC-MS/MS in this sample were comparable with those obtained by immunoassays, showing the suitability of the developed method for hyperandrogenic conditions.

In conclusion, the UHPLC-MS/MS method developed and validated for quantifying an eight-steroid panel in serum was successfully applied to interrogate samples from subjects under three months of age. This is the first MS-based steroid analysis method for infants and paediatric patients in Argentina that is already implemented in clinics.
