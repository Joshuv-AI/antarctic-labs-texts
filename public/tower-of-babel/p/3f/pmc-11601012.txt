Families' perceptions of consumer‐grade, inexpensive oxygen saturation monitors
Authors: Danielle Charland, Thomas Kovesi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe evaluated what proportion of families have a consumer‐grade pulse oximeter, why they bought one, and how they choose to use it.Working HypothesisWe hypothesized that children followed in cardiorespiratory clinics would be more likely to have an oximeter than children attending a more general clinic.Study Design and Subject SelectionWe carried out a cross‐sectional study using a convenience sample of children attending a respirology, cardiology, or gastroenterology clinic at a children's hospital. Consenting guardians completed a survey.ResultsTwo‐hundred families completed the survey. Fifty‐three (26.5%; 53/200) had an oximeter at home. The proportion of children attending a cardiorespiratory clinic who had an oximeter was higher than another clinic (p = 0.08), but 15.5% of children attending the latter also had access to one. Of devices not funded by government insurance, over 80% of devices were “fingertip” clamp‐style oximeters, and 50% were purchased online. Most devices were used only when the child was ill (83.7%; 36/43). Only about 1/3 of families had received education about using an oximeter, and a similar proportion had compared their oximeter to a medical‐grade device. Only 2.4% (1/42) respondents did not feel that their device was “somewhat” or “very” accurate. The oxygen saturation that would prompt seeking emergency care was similar to most pediatric acute care guidelines.ConclusionsMany children, particularly those with cardiorespiratory conditions, have access to consumer‐grade pulse oximeters. Asking about the presence of an oximeter should be part of the pediatric history, and families responding affirmatively should be offered education.

INTRODUCTION

Measurement of pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2) by pulse oximetry is common in acute care settings, and is part of the evaluation algorithm of many medical conditions. 1 , 2 , 3 Parents are therefore familiar with pulse oximeters, and, understandably, may wish to have this device available for home use.

Inexpensive, consumer‐grade, and generally non‐Food and Drug Administration approved oximeters have become readily available, particularly online and in pharmacies. Most are “fingertip” (clamp‐style) oximeters, although oximeters may also be integrated into smartphones and smart watches. Several studies have evaluated their accuracy, but these examined only a tiny fraction of devices, and few studies have examined their accuracy in children. 4 , 5 , 6 We recently completed a study of fingertip oximeters marketed for adult or pediatric use, and an oximeter built into an Android smartphone. We found the adult fingertip and smartphone oximeters performed fairly well in normoxemic older children and youth. The “pediatric” fingertip oximeter performed moderately well in toddlers and school‐aged children, but poorly in infants. 7

To our knowledge, no studies have measured what proportion of families of children with lung or heart disease have a consumer‐grade oximeters and, for those who have one, what motivated them to purchase it. It is also unknown how well they trust the displayed values, as well as how they interpret the results and how they use the findings to assess their children's health. Our study was intended to answer these questions amongst children and youth attending specialized clinics in a children's hospital, using a survey administered in‐person. We hypothesized that the families of children and youth with cardiorespiratory disease would be more likely to own an oximeter than families attending another clinic.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross‐sectional observational study evaluated a convenience sample of 200 participants. Included subjects attended the respirology (general respirology, cystic fibrosis [CF], or sleep clinics), or cardiology clinics at the Children's Hospital of Eastern Ontario (CHEO), Ottawa, Canada. We also recruited subjects from the gastroenterology (GI) clinic, as an additional convenience sample of children who we assumed had no obvious reason for having an oximeter and would be more representative of the general pediatric population. Surveys were carried out between June 1 and July 31, 2023. Patients and families were interviewed if patients were less than 18, a guardian was available, and a family member could speak English, French, or had translator assistance. A guardian was required as we assumed that the decision to purchase an oximeter was made by a guardian rather than by a child or youth. There were no other exclusion criteria.

Data was collected via a structured survey in‐person. Participants were approached in their clinics and completed the survey with a researcher. The survey consisted of 20 closed and open‐ended questions. If participants did not have an oximeter at home, only six questions were asked.

The following information was identified for all participants: clinic attended, oximeter ownership, age, reported sex at birth, self‐reported race, and medical conditions. Self‐reported race was included based on widely publicized information during the COVID‐19 pandemic that oximetry was more likely to falsely identify Black Americans with COVID as normoxemic, than White Americans. 8 , 9

Additional data obtained for participants with oximeters at home included oximeter type, motivation to acquire the device, purchase location, usage, interpretation of SpO2 values as normal, usual for their child, worrisome and urgent, whether they had received education about the device by a healthcare professional (HCP), the parents' beliefs about their device's accuracy, and whether they had compared the readout on their device to a medical‐grade oximeter.

The survey was not formally tested. After completing the questionnaire, participants who reported owning an oximeter were given a brief, two‐page info‐sheet with general advice on how to use their device, understand its limitations, and interpret the display (Supporting Information: File 2 [English] and 3 [French]).

Subject data is presented using descriptive statistics. In the province of Ontario, medical‐grade pulse oximeters can be provided by the provincial medical insurance plan with a physician's prescription. They are typically prescribed for patients on positive airway pressure support and/or home oxygen therapy. This policy differs from typical practice in the United States, where oximeters are funded by Medicaid/Medicare only for patients on home oxygen, positive airway pressure support or home ventilation, or who have a tracheostomy. 10 , 11 Oximeters provided by the provincial government are all medical grade, and since 2010 have been manufactured by Masimo (Masimo Corp.; currently Masimo Rad‐G). 12 The signal averaging time is normally kept at the default 8 s. The package includes four self‐adhesive type (“Band‐Aid” type) probes and a reusable Y‐type flexible probe with foam bands for fixation. Dispensing is done by a respiratory therapist. As patients with these monitors would be expected to have different patterns of utilization and education, they were described and analyzed separately. Subjects who reported borrowing an oximeter from a family member or friend were coded as “owning” an oximeter. Two patients who had both fingertip and government‐provided oximeters were recorded as having both, but analyzed as having purchased an oximeter, as they had made the decision to get an oximeter on their own. Where patients reported that an SpO2 was below or above a given number, the next integer was used (e.g., the parent felt “an SpO2 > 98%” was normal, we recorded the value as “99%”). When parents reported a range of saturations, the average was used for analysis. Race was dichotomized as “White” (including White, East Asian, South Asian, Middle Eastern and Indigenous individuals) or “Black” based on the above considerations. 9

Survey responses were anonymized for analysis. Closed‐ended questions were summarized by frequency distributions. Meanwhile, open‐ended questions were recorded as verbatim and analyzed to extract and synthesize common themes.

Descriptive statistics such as means ± standard deviations and counts (percentages) were used. Differences in proportions were presented graphically and analyzed using a Chi Square or a Fisher's Exact test. Guardian‐reported SpO2 values did not follow normal distributions, and differences between groups for continuous variables were evaluated with the Mann–Whitney U test. Proportions exclude missing data. Potential predictors of oximeter ownership among families who purchased their own device were evaluated with logistic regression. The presence of a linear relationship between each explanatory variable and the logit of the response variable was tested with the Box‐Tidwell test. Sensitivity analyses were not performed. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 29, IBM). A p‐value < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

This study was reviewed for ethical compliance by the CHEO Research Ethics Board (Protocol No: 23/37X). As we did not record patient identifying information or validate guardian report with the health record, the requirement for signed consent was waived; an information sheet for implied consent was given to participants.

RESULTS

We approached 203 subjects. Three refused to participate. Fifty‐three participants (26.5%; 53/200) had oximeters at home. Of note, there were three groups of siblings included in this study, and for each, only one child was included per family. In addition, there were two families who each owned two oximeters (both had a government and a consumer‐grade device). Subjects had a mean age of 7.8 years (range <1–17 years). Twelve children whose families had purchased an oximeter (22.6%; 12/53) were 1 year old or younger. Males comprised 57.8% (115/199) of the study population. Eleven (20.8%) families had purchased the oximeter for another family member and used it on (or had it available for) their child. Eleven patients (5.5%) were Black. Most patients were recruited from the general respirology and the cardiology clinic (Table 1; reported diagnoses are provided in Supporting Information: Table 1). Families attending the general respirology, CF or cardiology clinics were more likely to have an oximeter than individuals in the GI clinic, although the difference was not significant (p = 0.08). Of interest, 15.5% of patients in the gastroenterology clinic “control group” had access to a home pulse oximeter. Thirteen (32.5%; 13/40) children with asthma had an oximeter. In a logistic regression, neither age, race, or attending a gastroenterology versus another clinic was significantly predictive of oximeter ownership; this was true whether sleep clinic patients were included or not (data available on request). Ten subjects (18.9%; 10/53) had an oximeter provided by the government. Of the remainder, half purchased their device online, and 30% at a pharmacy (Table 1).

Families with consumer‐grade oximeters

Over 80% of consumer‐grade oximeters were fingertip devices (Table 1). Two infants had an infant “sock” oximeter (Owlet®; Owlet, Inc.). Six families purchased their device because of concerns regarding COVID‐19. One parent who worked in healthcare would borrow a device when the parent was worried.

Most families used their oximeter only when they were acutely worried about their child (83.7%; 36/43). Five (11.6%; 5/43) used it every night or day, either at present or when the child was younger. One family who used it regularly reported their child had chronic aspiration, and used the oximeter “to monitor for aspiration.” One reported using the device “out of curiosity.” One family purchased their machine to use as an apnea monitor. Three families (5.6%; 3/54) had a monitor but never used it.

Among families who had an oximeter, the mean SpO2 that families considered to be “normal” was 97.4% (range 93%–100%) (Figure 1 and Supporting Information: Figure 1). One cardiology clinic patient was an outlier, whose family considered their child's usual SpO2 to be under 80%. Six guardians did not know what a normal oxygen saturation value was. The reported mean “usual SpO2” for their child was 96.7% (range 90%–100%). The average SpO2 that would concern families was 90.8% (range 77.5%–96.5%). Seven families (17.9%; 7/39) indicated they would be worried about their child's health when the SpO2 was 95% or higher. Eight families reported a range of usual values for their child and 10 described a range where they would start getting concerned. One guardian indicated that they would consult their device's instruction manual to decide when to be concerned, two said they would check the internet, and one said they would base their concern on their child's symptoms, rather than SpO2. The average SpO2 that would prompt a family to seek emergency care was 88.1% (range 65%–95%). The SpO2 prompting emergency care was significantly lower in cardiology patients than respiratory patients (85.7% vs. 89.3%, p < 0.001). Of families who had a consumer‐grade, inexpensive oximeter, 37.2% (16/43) reported going to the ED at least once because they were concerned about their child's SpO2.

SpO2 values considered “normal,” “usual for their child,” “worrisome for their child,” and prompting transport to the emergency department for nongovernment issued device users.

Only one guardian (2.4%; 1/42) believed their device was “not accurate.” Half (21/42) felt their oximeter was “somewhat accurate,” and nearly half (47.6%; 20/42) considered it to be “very accurate.” About 1/3 (34.9%; 15/43) reported that they had been taught how to use their oximeter by an HCP. A similar number (34.9%; 15/43) reported having compared their oximeter to a medical‐grade device. Reported values for normal SpO2, concerning SpO2, or SpO2 that would prompt proceeding to the ED did not vary significantly by prior education by an HCP (data not presented).

Families with government‐provided oximeters

Ten patients had an oximeter funded by the government (an additional patient reported they had an oximeter built into their continuous positive airway pressure machine). Four of the 10 patients reported they had been prescribed an oximeter because of home oxygen therapy (2) or because the patient was on positive pressure support (1) or had a tracheostomy (1). Four (40%) used their oximeter every night, three (30%) every day, and three (30%) only when they were worried about the child. The mean SpO2 families considered normal, usual for their child, concerning, and that would prompt families to proceed to the ED were 96.0 (range 93.0–100.0), 94.4 (range 81.5–98), 88.1 (range 74.0–94.0), and 84.6 (70.0–94.0), respectively. The values for SpO2 that would prompt an ED visit were significantly lower for families with medical‐grade oximeters than consumer‐grade oximeters (p < 0.037). Six families (60%; 6/10) had visited the ED because of a low SpO2 value. Seventy percent of families felt their device was “very accurate,” and 30% felt it was “somewhat accurate.” The proportion of families visiting the ED because of the SpO2 and perceived accuracy of their oximeter was not significantly different between families with medical or consumer‐grade oximeters. All the families with government‐provided oximeters indicated they had been taught by an HCP how to use their oximeter and interpret the results.

DISCUSSION

We observed that nearly 1/3 of children attending a respirology clinic, and 1/5 of subjects attending a cardiology clinic had access to a consumer‐grade pulse oximeter. Unexpectedly, approximately 16% of participants attending a “control” clinic also had access to an oximeter. Race did not appear to influence the decision to have an oximeter, although there were few Black patients in our study.

There appears to be a large opportunity for education, particularly for children with cardiopulmonary conditions. Nearly 18% of families expressed concern over a SpO2 of 95% or greater, and six families didn't know what a normal SpO2 was. Admittedly, HCP's may also vary in their perceived “normal range” for SpO2, and SpO2 also varies with altitude (Ottawa's elevation is 70 m, or 230 feet above sea level). 13 As only one of the seven families worried about a normal SpO2 had a child followed in the cardiology clinic, it is likely that most or all of these families were unaware of what a normal SpO2 actually is, rather than being concerned about the risk of pulmonary edema in a patient with an intracardiac shunt. The modal value for an SpO2 value leading families to seek emergency care was 90%, which is consistent with most pediatric guidelines indicating the presence of severe lung disease. 1 , 2 One could certainly argue that care should be obtained when the SpO2 is higher than this, though making this judgement with a fairly inaccurate device would be challenging. Several families provided a range of SpO2 values that would prompt concern, which is probably more realistic, given the limited accuracy of these devices and variability of any biological measurement. Families that worried only when the SpO2 was very low (Figure 1) tended to be the parents of cardiology patients, although the proportion was not significantly different (data not presented).

A related issue is whether families use a threshold SpO2 value to become concerned, or a change in the child's usual SpO2. Given the limitations of consumer‐grade oximeters, either approach has limitations. In our previous study, biases for inexpensive oximeters ranged from −0.7% to −4.5%, although it was not unusual to observe an error of 10%–17%. 7 There are therefore obvious concerns for a parent deciding not to proceed to the ED because their oximeter displays an SpO2 of 92% when the actual reading could be 88%, and might be 82%. Similarly, using trends in the child's SpO2 is also problematic as differences between two values could be much larger or smaller than they appear, and this effect could be exaggerated in circumstances where any oximeter performs more poorly, such as poor perfusion states. 14 It is probably reasonable to assume that if the SpO2 is very high it is likely normal, and if it is very low it is probably bad, but values between these extremes should be interpreted with caution, especially if families plan to base their decision on a threshold value. We found that only about 1/3 of families with an inexpensive oximeter had its use reviewed by an HCP, and the same proportion had compared their device to a medical‐grade monitor.

Trust in these machines seemed inappropriately high, with nearly half of respondents believing their machine was “very accurate.” Moreover, nearly one‐quarter of the families with an inexpensive oximeter monitored a child 1 year of age or younger. While two of these were Owlet® sock monitors, which were designed for use in infants, use of the other consumer‐grade oximeters is concerning, as we previously observed these devices lacked acceptable reliability in this age group, when compared to oximeters that use a self‐adhesive probe and flexible cable connecting to the oximeter. 7 , 15

To our knowledge, only one analogous study has been performed, but it evaluated seniors with chronic lung disease, many of whom were on home oxygen therapy. 16 They found that most users decided on their own to buy an oximeter, and all devices were bought online or at a local pharmacy. Participants reported that having an oximeter increased confidence and reduced anxiety; these monitors were mainly used to titrate home oxygen therapy. 16 These findings clearly have limited applicability in a pediatric population, relatively few of whom were on respiratory supports.

Our study had a number of limitations. As comparable data on the prevalence of oximeters in the pediatric population was unavailable, we were unable to perform sample size calculations. A larger sample size would have likely allowed us to observe significant differences between populations. Our questionnaire was not validated, and we did not formally assess whether guardians used a threshold value or a change from their child's usual range for health assessments. We did not assess whether families were aware of the importance of correlating SpO2 with heart rate. As our study was designed to be conducted very briefly near clinic waiting rooms, with no provision for consent to access the medical record, we did not have accurate information on subject diagnoses or what the healthcare team considered a normal SpO2 for a given patient, and therefore could not correlate family perceptions with the latter. In hindsight, we should have inquired what families considered the normal range of SpO2 to be: when guardians reported a SpO2 of 99% or 100% as “normal,” it is not clear whether they simply regarded this value as normal (which it is), or whether they believed any value less than this was abnormal. Using the gastroenterology clinic as the “control” population is likely not representative of the general pediatric population that does not have cardiorespiratory disease, as these patients may have had complex medical conditions requiring subspecialty care, and family anxiety may have been greater than in the general population. Race was self‐reported and relatively few patients were Black, limiting our ability to assess the effect of race on the decision to purchase a device. We did not document household income or education as a potential driver of the decision to purchase an oximeter. We did not inquire whether families had asked their treatment physicians if they should purchase an oximeter, and, if so, whether their doctors had recommended in favor, or against, purchase. We likely underestimated the proportion of subjects with government‐supplied monitors where the device had been prescribed because of home oxygen therapy, as we did not specifically ask about therapies. However, indications for oximeter prescriptions were probably broader than would be the case for American Medicare or Medicaid patients.

To summarize, up to a third of children with chronic medical conditions, particularly chronic lung or cardiac disease, may have a consumer‐grade oximeter. Most are fingertip devices, and most are purchased online. Families appeared to have a realistic recall of what their child's usual SpO2 range is, and some, but not all, reported an action value for SpO2 that should prompt seeking emergency care in accordance with various guidelines. Based on our findings, asking whether families have an oximeter should become of routine history‐taking in respirology and cardiology clinics, and in general pediatric care. When families report having one, education is essential and should include using the most appropriate sensor for the child's size and appropriate placement of the sensor, interpretation of the display, understanding the limitations of this technology, and, crucially, the need to consider the readout in the context of the guardians' overall impression of their child's health. Guardians should be encouraged to bring their device to routine appointments and to the ED and check their device against medical‐grade oximeters. This will provide some sense of the accuracy of their device in health and in illness. The importance of education is underscored by the resource implications of community use of oximeters: 37% of families with consumer‐grade oximeters, and 60% of those with medical‐grade devices, reported proceeding to the ED because of concern over their child's SpO2. Future research will be required to extend our findings in other locations with a different range of socioeconomic circumstances, and to evaluate the trust in, and use of, self‐reported home SpO2 values by healthcare providers, both in the clinic and in acute care settings.
