Acute corneal edema and residual subepithelial haze after bilateral selective laser trabeculoplasty: A case series
Authors: Jeffrey Nijs, Evelien Vandewalle, Ingeborg Stalmans, Sophie Lemmens
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The primary objective of this case series is to report three cases of acute corneal edema and subepithelial haze occurring shortly after bilateral selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT). SLT was performed to treat open-angle glaucoma in three female, middle-aged patients who subsequently manifested stromal haze within 24–48 h following the procedure. The patients were, therefore, treated with topical steroids for several weeks. Due to uncontrolled intraocular pressure, one patient underwent a trabeculectomy. The corneal edema resolved in all three patients. However, they exhibited a hyperopic shift, along with alterations in corneal astigmatism. The incidence of corneal edema and subepithelial haze following SLT is an exceedingly rare phenomenon. While certain factors are hypothesized to contribute to this complication, the predisposing factors leading to corneal changes following this laser procedure remain unclear.

INTRODUCTION

Glaucoma is known as the second-most common cause of blindness worldwide. So far, the only way to control disease progression is by reducing the intraocular pressure (IOP).[1] Historically, this has been achieved either medically, surgically, or by laser trabeculoplasty. Selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT), first described in 1995, has become a significant therapeutic option.[2] Its mechanism involves targeting pigmented trabecular meshwork (TM) endothelial cells using a frequency-doubled (532 nm) Q-switched neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG) laser, with a spot size of 400 µm and energy delivery initiated at 0.8 mJ per pulse and titrated by 0.1 mJ per pulse below the threshold where champagne-sized bubbles appear.[3] This process induces cytokine release, stimulating macrophage recruitment and extracellular matrix remodeling, thereby enhancing aqueous outflow.[4] Substantial evidence supports the effectiveness of SLT in controlling IOP, comparable to topical medications.[5]

Only eight cases have been reported so far. Nevertheless, this series reports three cases of subepithelial corneal haze in highly myopic female patients after bilateral SLT.

CASE REPORTS

Case 1

In addition to her ongoing topical treatment regimen consisting of latanoprost and brinzolamide-timolol, a woman in her sixties diagnosed with mild primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) was treated by SLT for improved IOP control. This patient had previously undergone phacoemulsification with bilateral intraocular lens (IOL) implantation 13 years before the SLT procedure and a vitrectomy for a macular hole in the left eye 5 years before SLT.

Preceding the SLT intervention, central corneal thickness (CCT) measured 619 µ and 592 µ for the right and left eyes, respectively. Visual field assessments indicated mean deviations (MD) of − 2.9 dB in the right eye and − 4.5 dB in the left eye. Gonioscopy revealed an open angle with normal pigmentation of the TM. IOP was measured at 15 mmHg in the right eye and 18 mmHg in the left eye, with a target pressure set at 16 mmHg for both eyes.

During the SLT procedure, 115 spots with energy levels ranging from 1.0 to 1.5 mJ per shot were applied to the pigmented TM over 360° in the right eye, and a similar approach was followed with 112 spots in the left eye according to the standardized protocol mentioned above. The VOLK® SLT lens was employed for the procedure, and before treatment, pilocarpine, apraclonidine, and tetracaine drops were administered. Postoperatively, the patient’s glaucoma drops were continued, and diclofenac 1 mg/ml 4 times daily was prescribed for 5 days.

The day after the procedure, the patient reported a rapid deterioration in vision in the left eye and increasing pain. Notably, she had experienced no pain immediately after the procedure, and vision was normal. Upon presentation at our department the following day, a bilateral central subepithelial corneal haze was diagnosed without anterior chamber inflammation.

Case 2

A female of similar age to the first patient, but with a more advanced POAG, underwent bilateral SLT at our facility while concurrently receiving triple topical therapy with latanoprost, brinzolamide, and timolol. Her medical history included amblyopia in the right eye with a myopia of − 20 diopters (D) and − 6D in the left eye. Before the laser treatment, IOP was measured at 18 mmHg in the right eye and 26 mmHg in the left. CCT measured 575 µ in the right and 550 µ in the left eye. Visual field changes indicated MD of − 10.6 dB for the right and − 14.8 dB for the left eye. Gonioscopy revealed a bilateral open angle with normal pigmentation of the TM.

During the SLT procedure, a total of 122 spots with energy intensities ranging from 0.8–1.5 mJ were applied to the pigmented TM over 360° in the right eye. In the left eye, 121 spots with energy levels between 1.0 mJ and 2.0 mJ were similarly applied. The standard protocol was again adhered to. Two days postprocedure, the patient presented with fulminant corneal edema in the left eye and a substantial loss of vision. Her IOP had elevated to 40 mmHg. Three weeks after the initial presentation with fulminant corneal edema, central corneal subepithelial haze was observed [Figure 1].

Case 3

Another female in her sixties, diagnosed with POAG, underwent bilateral SLT in another hospital and presented the day after the treatment due to blurred vision. She had a history of high myopia (−9D) and had undergone external repair for a retinal detachment in the right eye 22 years before the SLT. In addition, cataract surgery with IOL implantation was performed 14 years before SLT. In the left eye, preventive eyeball cerclage and IOL implantation were undertaken 5 years before the SLT procedure. The CCT before SLT was not documented. Gonioscopy revealed an open angle with normal pigmentation of the TM. During the SLT procedure, energy levels ranging between 0.7 and 0.9 mJ were applied to the pigmented TM with 80 spots in the right eye and 88 spots in the left. When she presented for the first time at our department, 3 weeks after the SLT procedure, slit-lamp examination revealed a central subepithelial corneal haze in both eyes without anterior chamber inflammation [Figures 2 and 3]. The patient’s IOP measured 18 mmHg in the right eye and 20 mmHg in the left eye.

Treatment and follow-up

In the first patient, topical diclofenac was substituted with dexamethasone 1 mg/ml, administered six times daily, 1-day postprocedure. The first clinical assessment occurred 1 week following the treatment, revealing a best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 20/29, IOP measuring 20 mmHg, and the presence of central subepithelial haze. Over a 4-week period, a gradual tapering of dexamethasone ensued until complete resolution of the subepithelial corneal haze was achieved after 2 months. Topical IOP-lowering drops were continued. Nevertheless, IOP persisted above the target level in the left eye, for which a trabeculectomy was recommended but not be performed due to loss to follow-up.

The approach taken for the second patient differed slightly due to a presentation with elevated IOP at 40 mmHg and a more severe corneal edema. Oral acetazolamide, hourly administration of dexamethasone 1 mg/ml, as well as the use of latanoprost and dorzolamide-timolol was advised. Dexamethasone was tapered for 1 month, resulting in subepithelial corneal haze resolution. Acetazolamide was gradually reduced until reaching an acceptable IOP level. Unfortunately, upon discontinuation of acetazolamide, a notable increase in IOP to 25 mmHg was observed, prompting a subsequent trabeculectomy 1 month after SLT with adequate IOP reduction 6 weeks after surgery, to this day 12 months after trabeculectomy.

The third patient had already received treatment with topical IOP-lowering drops and hydrocortisone 3.35 mg/ml drops post-SLT from the referring ophthalmologist. In conjunction, systemic acetazolamide at a dosage of 125 mg twice daily was added. However, no improvement in subepithelial corneal haze was observed with hydrocortisone 3.35 mg/ml and therefore switched to dexamethasone 1 mg/ml six times daily. Four months postinitiation of dexamethasone, the patient is still treated with dexamethasone four times daily, gradually tapered by one drop per week, resulting in a nearly complete resolution of corneal haze. In addition, dorzolamide-timolol and latanoprost were initiated in both eyes. Eight months post-SLT, topical use of dexamethasone was discontinued, whereas dorzolamide-timolol and latanoprost were continued.

All three patients initially experienced a decline in BCVA 1 week after SLT, with gradual recovery during follow-up. However, the second patient developed a cataract during follow-up, unrelated to the SLT procedure, necessitating future surgery [Table 5].

DISCUSSION

SLT stands as a widely employed, generally safe, and effective method for reducing IOP in POAG. Common complications such as anterior chamber reaction, IOP spikes, or ocular irritation are readily manageable, whereas severe adverse events are infrequent. In 2002, corneal edema post-SLT was rarely seen, with an incidence of 0.8%.[7] More recently, a larger population-based study found 36 cases of corneal edema after 86,634 SLT procedures, reporting a much lower incidence compared to the previous study in 2002.[8] Despite being a rare complication, corneal edema post-SLT is still challenging to deal with. Most cases resolve after several weeks or months of treatment, yet some patients are not responding to adequate therapy.

In 2009, Moubayed et al. reported two cases of corneal edema in females occurring 1 week after SLT, with resolution of residual subepithelial haze, respectively, 2 and 4 months after treatment.[9] Regina et al. similarly documented two cases of central corneal edema, one of which involved a highly myopic patient. The edema resolved after several weeks of topical steroid treatment, respectively, 3 weeks and 3 months after initial presentation.[10] Knickelbein et al. presented four cases of acute corneal edema characterized by hyperopic shift and stromal thinning following routine SLT.[11] Furthermore, Ozkok et al. reported 1 case of corneal edema post-SLT, in which there was no resolution by 17 months.[12] Holz and Pirouzian even reported diffuse lamellar keratitis following SLT in a laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) patient. LASIK, however, is no contraindication to perform SLT nowadays.[13]

Our report introduces three additional cases of acute corneal edema and residual subepithelial haze following routine SLT treatment, specifically in women who underwent the procedure simultaneously in both eyes.

Several cases have been described reporting corneal changes post-SLT. To date, there is no definitive consensus regarding the etiology of subepithelial haze, hypermetropic shift, and an increase in astigmatism post-SLT. We propose the following hypotheses as potential explanations for the etiology of these post-SLT phenomena.

Reactivation of a latent ocular herpes simplex virus infection

In 2017, Liu et al. reported three cases of keratitis after SLT, suggesting a potential association with herpes simplex virus (HSV)-endotheliitis.[14] Our first patient tested positive for serum HSV-IgG and negative for HSV-IgM, indicating the theoretical possibility of reactivation from latent HSV. However, it is pertinent to note that HSV type 1 exhibits a seroprevalence of 67% in the Belgian population, with type 2 HSV at 11%.[15] In addition, the first patients lacked any medical history of ocular HSV infection or cold sores. Given these considerations, the corneal edema, IOP elevation, and haze in this case are deemed unlikely to be attributed to a reactivation of latent HSV infection.

In the cases of the second and third patients, HSV serology was not conducted due to both the high seroprevalence and negative medical histories. Furthermore, the distribution pattern of corneal edema did not align with the typical presentation of herpetic stromal keratitis or endotheliitis. Notably, the edema resolved solely with the use of topical steroid drops, without the inclusion of antiviral medications. Moreover, given the low incidence of corneal edema following SLT and the globally high prevalence of HSV seropositivity, the likelihood of HSV reactivation causing these corneal changes is considered rather improbable.

Toxic reaction to hydroxypropyl methylcellulose or disinfectant of the contact lens used during the treatment

In accordance with our established protocol, the contact lens undergoes disinfection using 70% ethanol. In addition, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (Methocel®) is applied to facilitate the accurate placement of the lens. A potential elucidation for observed corneal changes involves epitheliopathy and epithelial toxicity, leading to subsequent corneal edema, possibly induced by one of these substances.[16] In the case of the first patient, no signs of epitheliopathy were identified before, during, or after treatment. In the second patient, an epithelial bulla was noted 2 days postprocedure, suggesting a plausible association with the corneal edema. As the third patient did not undergo SLT within our department, any assumptions regarding the utilization of hydroxypropyl methylcellulose cannot be formulated.

Defocus of the yttrium aluminum garnet-laser beam

SLT procedures utilize a frequency-doubled (532 nm) Q-switched Nd:YAG laser, with preset laser settings except for power adjustment. The laser energy delivered to the TM is converted into thermal energy inducing contraction of the TM. This process upregulates various pro-inflammatory factors such as interleukin 8, 1-alpha, 1-beta, and tumor necrosis factor, resulting in an augmented fluid permeability of Schlemm’s canal, causing an aqueous outflow increase causing a decrease in IOP.[17] A potential explanation for the pathogenesis of corneal edema following SLT posits that defocus of the laser beam during the procedure may have caused the laser energy to be directed onto the corneal endothelium instead of the TM. Therefore, thermal exposure of the endothelial cells and subsequent cell loss could contribute to postoperative corneal edema. Yet, it is important to note that, in our cases, no laser beam defocus was observed.

Corneal endothelial abnormalities after selective laser trabeculoplasty

Ong et al. have postulated a potential link between SLT and a reduction in the transparency of the corneal endothelium, coupled with transient endothelial abnormalities such as dysfunction and a decrease in the quantity of endothelial cells. This sequence of abnormalities could, in turn, be associated with the occurrence of corneal edema following repeated SLT.[18] However, none of these three patients had not undergone any form of laser trabeculoplasty before. They also suggest that these corneal abnormalities might be avoided by reducing the amount of energy and amount of laser shots, particularly in patients with decreased corneal endothelial transparency pre-SLT.[18] White et al. illustrated corneal endothelial changes 1–2 h post-SLT, for which the mechanism of action is not yet fully understood.[19] In all three of our patients, no decrease in corneal endothelial transparency was observed before treatment. Furthermore, it is important to highlight that every patient was treated by a different physician. The first 2 patients received a higher amount of energy and shots, whereas the third patient received a lower amount of energy and laser shots as mentioned before. Despite these variations, all patients developed similar corneal changes with central corneal thinning, subepithelial corneal haze, hyperopic shift, and a change in corneal astigmatism in two of them.

This comparison suggests that the energy and laser shot count during SLT may not be the primary cause of corneal complications. Nevertheless, a more extensive study should be conducted to further investigate these observations.

In conclusion, corneal edema, subsequent thinning, and hyperopic shift are rarely seen in highly myopic female, middle-aged patients after SLT. At this moment, many hypotheses are proposed, but the mechanism of action remains unclear and further research is necessary to better understand this complication. One must be precautious when planning SLT treatment in patients with these characteristics or a reduced transparency of the corneal endothelium. When properly treated with a high dosage of corticosteroids, the subepithelial corneal haze and secondary visual impairment is a challenging complication but not permanent.
