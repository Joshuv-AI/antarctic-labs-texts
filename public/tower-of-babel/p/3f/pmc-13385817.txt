Evolution of intragranular pores in potassium niobate ceramics during sintering
Authors: Xian-Xian Cai, Zhaonian Zhou, Yeming Huang, Ziqing Zhong, Yuqi Jiang, Weiwei Gao, Yi-Xuan Liu, Hao-Cheng Thong, Zhengqian Fu, Ze Xu, Ke Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potassium niobate ceramics are ideal models for studying perovskite niobates but suffer from poor densification and chemical stability. Here, pure potassium niobate ceramics with a high relative density (99%) are fabricated via hot-pressing, achieving piezoelectric performance comparable to that of pure (K,Na)NbO3 ceramics. Detailed microstructural analysis identifies a high concentration of nanoscale cuboidal intragranular pores. This study clarifies a pore-evolution mechanism: K+-driven lattice diffusion triggers crack formation in the Nb2O5 matrix during calcination. These initial cracks sequentially transform from disordered oblong and oriented elongated shapes into thermodynamically stable cuboidal configurations during further processing. These intragranular pores are the key factor leading to the poor chemical stability of potassium niobate ceramics. This work clarifies the grain growth and pore-evolution mechanisms in potassium niobate ceramics, providing critical insights for enhancing the stability and sintering quality of niobate-based materials.

Introduction

Growing environmental concerns and legislative restrictions on hazardous substances have driven intensive research into lead-free piezoelectric materials as alternatives to lead zirconate titanate (Pb(Zr,Ti)O3, PZT)-based ceramics1,2. Among various lead-free candidates, potassium sodium niobate ((K,Na)NbO3, KNN)-based systems have emerged as the most promising materials3. Texture engineering4,5, dopant design6 and advanced sintering strategies7,8 have driven the piezoelectric coefficient to continuously higher levels9, even exceeding 800 pC·N−1, enabling widespread application in sensors, actuators and energy harvesting devices3,10. As incremental performance gains approach a ceiling, attention is shifting toward fundamental mechanisms such as polymorphic phase boundary construction, domain engineering and soft/hard doping effects2. However, the coexistence of K and Na on the A-site introduces intrinsic variability into mechanistic analyses, particularly in density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Although the cations are expected to occupy the A-site randomly, the limited size of practical supercells precludes a faithful representation of this disorder. Studies, therefore, resort to simplified schemes such as the rock salt model11–13 and quasirandom structure14. However, the rock salt model risks neglecting key short-ranged interactions, potentially introducing quantitative errors, while more realistic quasirandom structures necessitate substantially larger unit cells and far greater computational resources14. Potassium niobate (KNbO3, KN), a component of KNN, possesses a single A-site cation, retains ferroelectricity, and mirrors the phase transition sequence and electrical response of KNN2,15,16. In contrast, sodium niobate, the other component, is antiferroelectric. Consequently, the KN system serves as an ideal model platform for probing intrinsic piezoelectric mechanisms, enabling straightforward DFT investigations of defects, dopants and phase transitions17. Should dense, chemically stable KN ceramics be realized, they would serve as a robust model system for elucidating mechanisms by bridging experiments with DFT calculations.

However, research on KN ceramics faces significant challenges in processing and stability, primarily due to the hygroscopic nature of potassium-containing compounds18 and the narrow temperature window for phase-pure synthesis19. Conventionally solid-state synthesized KN powders readily absorb moisture, leading to deliquescence and spontaneous cracking18,20. Therefore, strict control of calcination and sintering parameters or strategic doping is required to obtain dense, phase-pure ceramics with optimized properties. For example, water-proof KN ceramics with approximately 97% theoretical density have been obtained by adding a small amount of LaFeO3 together with carefully tailored processing to suppress potassium loss18,21. Furthermore, ZnO was used as an additive to enhance moisture stability, the resultant KNbO3−0.4ZnO composite ceramic exhibits a d33 of 120 pC·N−1, alongside excellent water resistance and aging-free characteristics22. Additional sintering aids such as CuO23,24, K4CuNb8O2325, BiFeO326, and MnO215, have been demonstrated to systematically enhance the sinterability of KN ceramics. However, these approaches introduce multiple foreign cations that generate complex secondary phases and composition-dependent properties, thereby obscuring the intrinsic piezoelectric enhancement mechanism and complicating supercell construction for subsequent DFT studies. Beyond compositional tuning, alternative synthesis routes have been explored to increase density and electrical performance. Methods such as hydrothermal27,28, sol-gel29, and molten-salt methods20,30,31 are widely used to prepare reactive KN powders. Simultaneously, advanced sintering methods, including flash sintering and two-stage calcination, effectively suppress K2O volatilization and yield dense, fine-grained ceramics with preserved stoichiometry32,33. However, most of these reports primarily concentrate on processing details, paying limited attention to the resulting piezo and ferroelectric properties, and leaving the microstructural evolution during sintering almost entirely unaddressed. Notably, hot-pressing, as an advanced sintering method, offers distinct advantages, utilizing external pressure to lower the sintering temperature, inhibit alkali volatilization, refine grain size, and promote densification35–37. Since 1967, when hot-pressing first produced KNN ceramics with 99% relative density34, the technique has generated KNN materials with excellent properties and has clarified the key mechanisms8,35–37.

In the present study, we applied hot-pressing to the pure KN system and obtained dense, relatively stable ceramics whose piezoelectric response rivals that of undoped KNN. Intriguingly, nanoscale rectangular pores embedded within the grains were observed. These pores appeared to be linked to the moisture sensitivity of KN. To uncover their origin, characterizations were performed on the Nb2O5-K2CO3 precursor mixture, the intermediate powders that form during solid-state reaction, the calcined KN powder, and the hot-pressed ceramic. The results reveal that K+ diffusion into the Nb2O5 lattice initiates cracks that sequentially evolve into disordered oblong pores and oriented elongated pores, which finally restructure into thermodynamically stable cuboidal configurations during further thermal processing. Moreover, water-immersion tests reveal that the intragranular pores act as internal stress concentrators, thereby driving grain cracking and leading to the poor chemical stability of KN ceramics.

Results

Electrical and microstructural characteristics

To evaluate the performance of hot-pressed KN ceramics (HP-KN), we compared their density, piezoelectric response, and dielectric behavior with those of hot-pressed KNN ceramics (HP-KNN) and conventionally sintered KN ceramics (CS-KN)38, as illustrated in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table S1. The HP-KN exhibited superior properties, achieving a relative density of 99.0% (4.57 g·cm−3) and a high d33 of 124 pC⋅N−1. This performance stands in stark contrast to that of CS-KN (relative density: 92.0%; d33: 90 pC⋅N−1) and is comparable to HP-KNN (relative density: 98.9%; d33: 132 pC⋅N−1), representing a significant enhancement attributed to meticulous powder processing and optimized hot-pressing conditions18,38,39. The temperature-dependent dielectric constant of HP-KN and HP-KNN (Fig. 1b) displayed analogous trends, suggesting their shared phase transition characteristics. The ferroelectric properties (Fig. 1c, d, and S1) of HP-KN under electric fields ranging from 0.5 kV·mm−1 to 5 kV·mm−1, demonstrated excellent high-voltage resistance. The HP-KN achieves a saturation polarization of 26 μC·cm−2 at 5 kV·mm−1, generates a bipolar strain exceeding 0.1% as well as a unipolar strain of 0.09% at the same field. Fig. 1e, f demonstrates the temperature stability of HP-KN. Below 150 °C, both its saturation polarization and maximum strain exhibit robust thermal stability. Microscopic morphology characterization was performed on HP-KNN and HP-KN. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of HP-KN exhibits density comparable to HP-KNN (Fig. 2a, b), which is consistent with its relative density of up to 99%. The polished surface of KN is uniformly dense across large areas, as documented in the low-magnification mapping (Supplementary Fig. S2). Notably, Fig. 2b reveals distinctive core-shell grain structures containing dark particulates. These particulates predominantly occupy the grain cores while the shell regions remain structurally uniform. Phase purity is confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Supplementary Fig. S3). This observation, combined with the simple elemental composition, effectively rules out the possibility that these particulates are secondary phases. Fracture surface SEM analysis (Fig. 2c) reveals the presence of nanovoids within the grains, suggesting the potential for intragranular pores. To further confirm this speculation, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was performed (Fig. 2d), revealing a representative cross-section of the HP-KN. The TEM images revealed arrays of rectangular, electron-transparent nanostructures (10-50 nm) with crystallographic alignment along specific directions. An analysis of the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern in Supplementary Fig. S4 revealed that both the grain boundaries and the electron-transparent nanostructures in HP-KN are aligned along the {100} family of planes of the pseudo-cubic perovskite cell, giving rise to their approximately rectangular morphology. The energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping in Fig. 2e revealed uniformly depressed signals for all elements within these nanostructures, suggesting a thickness reduction, definitively confirming their void nature. The high density of intragranular pores in HP-KN is hypothesized to contribute to its poor moisture resistance. Therefore, we conducted mechanistic studies on pore evolution during KN ceramic processing. The formation of intragranular pores in HP-KN could occur at multiple processing stages: primary calcination, secondary calcination, hot-pressing densification, or already present in the Nb2O5 and K2CO3 precursors. The initial focus was placed on examining whether the intragranular pores originated from the raw materials. As the diffusion coefficient of potassium is much higher than that of niobium, potassium predominantly diffuses into the Nb2O5 lattice during calcination40. Consequently, the K2CO3 grain structure has minimal influence on KN grains, so attention can be restricted to the Nb2O5 precursor. Comprehensive characterization (XRD/SEM/TEM in Supplementary Figs. S5 and S6) revealed that the Nb2O5 precursor exists as orthorhombic-phase particles (~ 300 nm in size, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S7a), and lacks discernible pores at the same TEM resolution employed for the ceramic analysis in Fig. 2d, e. These findings eliminate raw material pores as the source of intragranular pores in HP-KN.

Origin of intragranular pores

To identify the specific processing stage responsible for intragranular pore formation in HP-KN, the phase evolution dynamics during thermal processing were systematically investigated. Prior studies have established that perovskite phase synthesis occurs primarily during the primary calcination stage, with secondary calcination and hot-pressing procedures maintaining phase stability18,19. Experimental validation was conducted using homogenized KN precursor powder divided into four batches: three subjected to isothermal calcination at 400 °C, 500 °C, and 600 °C, respectively, and one retained as an unprocessed control. XRD analysis of these samples (Supplementary Fig. S8a) revealed two critical observations. First, the powder calcined at 400 °C exhibited diffraction patterns identical to the unprocessed mixture, confirming the absence of solid-state reactions below this temperature threshold. Second, complete perovskite crystallization was achieved at 500 °C, thereby defining a narrow 100 °C window (400–500 °C) as the primary phase formation regime. Further time-dependent XRD analysis at 500 °C (Supplementary Fig. S8b, c) demonstrated progressive Nb2O5 precursor consumption alongside the emergence of transient intermediate phases, including K4Nb6O17 and K6Nb10.88O30, during extended calcination periods40. These results conclusively identify 500 °C as the critical temperature for perovskite phase formation, thereby directing subsequent microstructural investigations to this temperature window. Based on these findings, four powder batches were selected for detailed microstructural characterization to elucidate the pore formation mechanisms: 400 °C-4 h (representing the unreacted state), 500 °C-0.5 h and 500 °C-2 h (representing intermediate reaction stages), and 500 °C-4 h (representing the fully synthesized perovskite state). Given that the 500 °C-0.5 h and 2 h samples captured intermediate states, which are inherently more sensitive to thermal processing variations, it was imperative to ensure their microstructures were not artifacts of the cooling process. To this end, the effects of cooling rates were evaluated by comparing furnace-cooled and air-quenched powders. XRD analysis (Supplementary Fig. S9) showed only minor differences in peak positions and intensities between the two cooling conditions. Furthermore, STEM characterization of the 500 °C-0.5 h samples (Supplementary Fig. S10) revealed nearly identical morphological features regardless of the cooling method. This indicates that the impact of the cooling mode on the powder microstructure can be essentially ruled out.

Analysis of the STEM image of the powder calcined at 400 °C for 4 h (Fig. 3a) reveals Nb2O5 grains measuring several hundred nanometers in size, with potassium exhibiting a dispersed spatial distribution. Elemental mapping shows no spatial correlation between K and Nb domains, indicating that no appreciable chemical reaction has occurred, in agreement with XRD. Notably, the uniform contrast in STEM imaging (compared to Fig. 3b, d, and f) further excludes raw material pores as the origin of intragranular pores. Following this progression, Fig. 3b presents a representative Nb2O5 grain from powder calcined at 500 °C for 0.5 h. Elemental mapping demonstrates potassium diffusion predominantly in peripheral regions, while the grain core maintains the pristine Nb2O5 composition. Crucially, this potassium-enriched periphery exhibits extensive microcracking not observed in the raw powder. SAED analysis (Fig. 3c) of the potassium-diffused particle reveals a hybrid pattern: a set of split diffraction spots together with an amorphous halo, indicating a transitional state of structural evolution. Supplementary Fig. S11b, c present the SAED patterns of the powder core and shell, respectively. The amorphous halo originates predominantly from the potassium-diffused shell, whereas the diffraction spots arise from the potassium-undiffused core. The amorphous halo indicates that the potassium-diffused shell is short-range ordered but long-range disordered. In Fig. 3c, the two perpendicular sets of diffraction spots exhibit reciprocal-vector lengths of approximately 3.22 nm−1 and 2.51 nm−1, matching the (200) and (001) spacings of orthorhombic Nb2O5 (Supplementary Fig. S12)41. This match confirms that the diffraction spots arise from the potassium-undiffused core of the Nb2O5 particle, while the spot splitting signals that the particle is undergoing a heterogeneous solid-state reaction. The coexistence of an amorphous halo and split diffraction spots evidences a drastic structural change in the powder at this stage, a conclusion further supported by the multiple intermediate phases detected in the preceding XRD data. The uniform spatial distribution of potassium, niobium, and oxygen in the powder calcined at 500 °C for 2 h confirms that potassium has fully diffused throughout the Nb2O5 grains (Fig. 3d). However, XRD analysis (Supplementary Fig. S8b) reveals that the powder has not yet fully formed the perovskite structure, existing instead in a transitional state where the perovskite phase coexists with various intermediate phases. Within these grains, both DF-STEM (Fig. 3d) and TEM (Fig. 3e) imaging reveal the presence of disordered, non-oriented oblong features. These features manifest as dark contrast in DF-STEM and light contrast in TEM, thereby confirming their nature as internal pores. These pores are distributed throughout the grain volume, typically measuring several nanometers in width and tens of nanometers in length. XRD analysis (Supplementary Fig. S8b) indicates that the 500 °C-4 h calcined powder consists predominantly of the perovskite phase, indicating a near-complete solid-state reaction. DF-STEM characterization (Fig. 3f) reveals nanoscale dot-shaped features and linear striations within the grains. Further TEM analysis (Fig. 3g) resolves these microstructural signatures into two distinct morphologies: blank dots and elongated light-contrast regions. To elucidate the nature of these features, STEM was employed (Supplementary Fig. S13). At higher magnification, STEM analysis identifies these regions as dark squares and gray rectangles in the DF image, with a corresponding contrast inversion observed in the BF image. Given that the 500 °C-4 h sample consists of a single KNbO3 phase, the STEM contrast is primarily governed by mass-thickness variations within the grain sample. Specifically, the significantly darker appearance of the square-shaped regions in the DF image indicates a substantially greater material deficiency along the electron beam direction compared to the rectangular regions. These observations confirm an orientation-dependent projection effect: the blank dots represent the cross-sections of elongated pores oriented perpendicular to the observation plane, whereas the elongated low-contrast regions correspond to pores oriented parallel to the plane.

In summary, a comparative analysis of the microstructural features across different calcination durations reveals a clear evolutionary trajectory of material deficiency, progressing from the initial stress-relief cracks at 0.5 h to the disordered oblong pores at 2 h and finally to the elongated pores at 4 h. It can be inferred that the initial diffusion of potassium into the Nb2O5 lattice induces extensive cracking driven by severe structural mismatch and coherency stresses. As the KNbO3 phase begins to form, these cracks transform into disordered, non-oriented oblong pores. With prolonged thermal treatment at 500 °C, the gradual maturation and stabilization of the perovskite KN crystals drive these pores to reorganize and eventually evolve into elongated pores with distinct crystallographic orientations.

Evolution of intragranular pores

To elucidate the transformation mechanism of pores from elongated to cuboidal geometries, systematic investigations of the calcination and sintering processes were conducted. Previous XRD results (Supplementary Fig. S8a) confirm that the powder calcined at 600 °C for 4 h has fully formed the perovskite phase, indicating that the primary-calcined powder (730 °C for 4 h) is also pure KN. The primary-calcined powder (Supplementary Fig. S7c) maintains a grain size consistent with the Nb2O5 precursor (Supplementary Fig. S7a) and the 500 °C-4 h calcined powder (Supplementary Fig. S7b). The average grain size for all three samples remains at approximately 300 nm, suggesting that no significant grain growth occurred even at 730 °C. Furthermore, the pores in Fig. 4b exhibit morphological characteristics similar to those in Fig. 3g, indicating that the evolution of pore morphology remains limited during the transition from 500 °C to 730 °C calcination. High-magnification TEM imaging (Fig. 4b) reveals a progressive reduction in the aspect ratio of elongated pores during primary calcination, with partial evolution into cuboidal geometries. For a fixed pore volume, the transformation from elongated to cuboidal shapes reduces surface area and lowers surface energy. This morphological evolution is consistent with surface energy minimization during prolonged thermal treatment. As illustrated in Fig. 4c, d, the pore surface morphology corresponds to the (100) crystallographic plane. First-principles calculations reveal that the reconstructed {100} surface of KN exhibits low surface energy42, which thermodynamically accounts for the preferential orientation of intracrystalline pores along the {100} direction in KN crystals. The internal pores in BaTiO3 and KNN also exhibit a similar cuboidal shape43,44. Extended heat treatment durations and elevated temperatures are anticipated to further drive energy minimization, ultimately stabilizing pores into quasi-cubic configurations. Notably, Supplementary Fig. S14 reveals the presence of surface-localized pores on selected grains (highlighted by red circles), with Fig. 4a magnifying the cuboidal pore morphology on a representative grain surface. The scarcity of observable pores on other grains likely stems from the sub-10 nm width of elongated pores, which falls below the resolution threshold of conventional SEM imaging at this magnification. This observation reveals that the cuboidal pores in the primary-calcined powder are distributed throughout the entire grain volume, encompassing both the interior and the surface regions. Figure 4e, S15 (SEM), and 4 f (TEM) provide a combined view of the microstructural evolution of secondary-calcined powder. SEM analysis reveals the disappearance of the cuboidal surface pores previously observed in the primary-calcined counterpart, a finding corroborated by TEM showing pore-free grain peripheries. This suggests that diffusional mass transport occurred during the secondary calcination, leading to the elimination of surface pores via curvature-driven boundary migration, which is the primary reason for the core-shell structure of the pore distribution. Notably, the average grain size only increased from ~ 350 nm (Supplementary Fig. S7c) to ~ 550 nm (Supplementary Fig. S7d). This relatively modest growth indicates that diffusional processes remained limited even at a calcination temperature of 930 °C, preventing significant coarsening of the secondary-calcined particles. Concurrently, the enlarged volume of individual intragranular pores compared to those in the primary-calcined powder indicates progressive pore maturation. The aspect ratio of the pores has continued to decrease, approaching a quasi-cubic morphology and becoming comparable to that observed in hot-pressed ceramics. These observations collectively demonstrate that both grains and intragranular pores undergo Ostwald ripening during secondary calcination. While hot-pressing further facilitates this process, the lower sintering temperature (920 °C) employed during hot-pressing, relative to that of secondary calcination (930 °C), retards the kinetics of grain growth and pore coarsening. Consequently, the morphological changes in grains and intragranular pores remain less pronounced than those in secondary-calcined powders, with the grain size in the hot-pressed ceramic only growing to approximately 700 nm (Supplementary Fig. S7e). This is further supported by the conventional sintering results at 1020 °C for 5 h; as shown in the fracture surface SEM (Supplementary Fig. S16), the grain size reaches tens of micrometers, with intragranular pores growing to hundreds of nanometers, both of which are significantly larger than those in the hot-pressed ceramics. Compared to conventional sintering, the hot-pressing process, utilizing a lower sintering temperature and shorter holding time, promotes the formation of ceramics with both fine-grain structure and high density. A comparative analysis of pore surfaces in the primary-calcined powder (Fig. 4c, d) and hot-pressed ceramic (Fig. 4g, h) reveals their orientation evolution: while both systems exhibit predominant {100} orientation, the pores in the ceramic display sharper crystallographic alignment, compared to the arc-shaped surface structure of the pores in the powder, indicating further energy minimization.

To clarify the relationship between the dense intragranular pores in KN ceramics and their poor moisture resistance, we polished the hot-pressed KN ceramics annealed in air at 880 °C for 24 h using water (Supplementary Fig. S17) and anhydrous ethanol (Supplementary Fig. S18), respectively. Extensive grain spalling and microcracking were observed in the water-polished samples, whereas no such degradation occurred in the samples polished with anhydrous ethanol. This observation indicates that the annealing process itself does not induce cracking. Rather, the cracking stems from the reaction between the annealed KN ceramic surface and water during the polishing process. To further verify this, we performed an additional annealing experiment on the hot-pressed ceramics under an Ar atmosphere, which allows for the relaxation of internal stresses without oxygen re-incorporation. SEM characterization of the Ar-annealed samples after water polishing (Supplementary Fig. S19) revealed no signs of deliquescence or microcracking. Consequently, we attribute the cracking to the reaction between water and the moisture-sensitive by-products formed during air-annealing. These by-products, such as K4Nb6O17, are prone to deliquescence and subsequent dissolution upon contact with water18. We suggest that the pre-existing intragranular pores may further facilitate this degradation process by acting as stress concentrators. According to stress concentration theory, the minute pores with high curvature can induce localized stress, potentially reducing the effective fracture toughness and promoting crack propagation once the chemical reaction initiates at the grain surfaces, as observed in Supplementary Fig. S17. The subsequent chemical reaction at these sites further drives crack propagation, eventually leading to grain spalling and the macroscopic disintegration of the material.

Through systematic analysis of pore evolution, this study proposes a multi-stage diffusion-reconstruction model (Fig. 5) elucidating the formation and transformation mechanisms of intragranular pores in KN ceramics. The model reveals three critical phases: (1) Crack Initiation: After ball-milling (Stage a), K2CO3 and Nb2O5 particles are homogeneously mixed. During the initial stage of primary calcination (Stage b), K⁺ diffusion into Nb2O5 drives the formation of KNbO3 via a solid-state reaction, accompanied by lattice mismatch-induced stresses that lead to crack propagation. (2) Pore Formation: During the later stage of primary calcination (Stages c and d), these cracks evolve into disordered oblong pores, which further develop into elongated pores through perovskite phase development, with subsequent aspect-ratio reduction driven by surface energy minimization, ultimately forming cuboidal pores. (3) Pore Stabilization and Coarsening: During secondary calcination and final hot-pressing (Stages e and f), further aspect-ratio reduction transforms the pores into quasi-cubic configurations. a Ball-milled precursor: Cyan regions represent Nb2O5 particles surrounded by potassium-rich particles (orange). b Intermediate powder (calcined at 500 °C for 0.5 h): A core-shell structure with potassium-diffused shell (green) and pristine Nb2O5 core (cyan), alongside orange particles representing incompletely consumed K2CO3 attached to the grain. Discussion

High relative density pure KN ceramics were fabricated by hot-pressing, delivering piezoelectric properties comparable to hot-pressed pure KNN. Using intragranular pores as microstructural markers, we followed calcination and sintering at different stages and propose a diffusion-reconstruction model: The K+ diffusion-controlled solid-state reaction initially induces microcracks via lattice-mismatch-induced stresses. These cracks subsequently evolve into disordered oblong and oriented elongated pores, before finally restructuring into thermodynamically stable cuboidal pores driven by surface-energy minimization. This work clarified the grain growth mechanism of KN-based ceramics during sintering, and further links intragranular pores to the poor moisture resistance, providing critical insights to enhance chemical stability and accelerate the practical application of KN-based materials.

Methods

Powder and ceramic preparation

Pure KNbO3, (K0.5Na0.5)NbO3 and KNbO3 intermediate powders were synthesized via solid-state reaction. High-purity precursors K2CO3 (99.0%, Sinopharm, China), Na2CO3 (99.8%, Sinopharm, China) and Nb2O5 (99.99%, Sinopharm, China) were dried at 120 °C for 4 h before being stoichiometrically weighed for pure KN and KNN compositions. The powder mixtures were subjected to planetary ball milling with yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) grinding media at 300 rpm for 24 h, using ethanol as a dispersant. After drying at 100 °C for 12 h in an oven, homogeneous powder mixtures of KN and KNN initial compositions were obtained. Portions of both the raw Nb2O5 powder and ball-milled KN initial mixture were separately retained as reference materials. To prepare KN intermediate powders (for mechanistic studies), the initial KN mixture was calcined in a furnace using a constant heating rate of 5 °C/min. Two independent parameter sets were employed: (1) varying temperatures of 400 °C, 500 °C, and 600 °C for 4 h each; and (2) varying durations of 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 h at a fixed temperature of 500 °C. For the samples presented in Fig. 3b, d, and e (calcined at 500 °C for 0.5 h and 2 h), air quenching was performed immediately after the dwell time; all other specimens were furnace-cooled to room temperature.

For KN and KNN ceramics fabrication, the ball-milled KN and KNN initial mixtures were sequentially calcined twice (730 °C and 930 °C for 4 h each) with intermediate ball milling (24 h per cycle). Reference samples of primary-calcined and secondary-calcined powders of KN were collected prior to each milling step. Hot-press sintering of KN and KNN ceramics was achieved at 920 °C for 2 h under 30 MPa uniaxial pressure in argon atmosphere, using a commercial furnace (HP P 12.5/4 LA, FCT Systeme GmbH, Germany). For the conventional sintering of KN ceramics, green bodies were fabricated using twice-calcined KN powder, followed by consolidation via cold isostatic pressing at 300 MPa. These green bodies were subsequently sintered under two distinct conditions: 920 °C for 2 h and 1020 °C for 5 h.

Characterization of powders and ceramics

The hot-pressed KN and KNN ceramics were sectioned into 5 × 5 × 1 mm3 specimens and annealed at 880 °C for 6 h to eliminate oxygen vacancies, thereby facilitating macroscopic electrical property characterizations. Notably, the ceramics used for the water-immersion experiments were subjected to one of two annealing treatments: prolonged air annealing at 880 °C for 24 h, or annealing in an Ar atmosphere at 920 °C for 2 h. Prior to electrical measurements, silver electrodes were formed by coating the samples with silver paste followed by annealing at 600 °C for 0.5 h. Temperature-dependent dielectric properties were characterized using an impedance analyzer (TH2827, Changzhou Tonghui Electronic Co., China). The polarization-electric field (P-E) and strain-electric field (S-E) curves were measured by a ferroelectric tester (TF-2000, aixACCT Systems GmbH). The quasi-static piezoelectric coefficient d33 was measured using a Berlincourt d33 meter (ZJ−3A; Institute of Acoustics, Chinese Academy of Science, Beijing, China). The densities of KN and KNN ceramics were determined by the Archimedes method.

Phase analysis of powders and ceramics was performed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, D/max-2500/PC, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation. Microstructural characterization was conducted using scanning electron microscopes (SEM, Merlin, Zeiss, Germany; Verios G4, Thermo Scientific, USA) to examine the Nb2O5 precursor, calcined powders, and hot-pressed ceramics. Microstructural details were further analyzed using transmission electron microscopes (TEM, JEM-F200 and JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan) equipped with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) capabilities. The TEM specimens were prepared using two distinct approaches depending on the sample form. For the bulk KN ceramics, samples were first mechanically polished to a thickness of approximately 20 μm. For the powder samples, the powders were dispersed in AB glue, deposited onto copper grids, and cured to allow for cross-sectional observation. Subsequently, both types of specimens were subjected to argon-ion milling (PIPS II 695, Gatan, USA) until electron transparency (30–50 nm) was achieved, to ensure high-quality imaging and analysis.
