Treatment of Refractory Lymphocele by Lymphangiography in Kidney Transplant
Authors: Soo Jin Lee, Jihyun Yeom, Hong Pil Hwang, Hee Chul Yu, Kun Yung Kim, Sik Lee
Published: 2025

Lymphocele is a lymphatic fluid collection resulting from disruption of lymphatic channels after kidney transplantation (KT), occurring in 0.6%–51% of recipients, but symptomatic in about 5%.1,2 It usually develops within 2 weeks to 6 months after KT and may cause abdominal discomfort, lower limb edema, graft dysfunction, or infection.1 Management options include percutaneous drainage, sclerotherapy, and laparoscopic marsupialization.1,2

A 66-year-old male patient with end-stage renal disease due to autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease underwent deceased donor KT in September 2022. He had a medical history of hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and gout. His immunosuppressive regimen consisted of tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisolone. On postoperative day (POD) 25, he presented with fever, dysuria, and left lower quadrant abdominal pain. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 7.1×103/μL and stable graft function (serum creatinine 1.66 mg/dL). His tacrolimus level was 5.6 ng/mL Abdominopelvic computed tomography (APCT) revealed a large fluid collection around the transplanted kidney without hydronephrosis (Fig. 1). Percutaneous catheter drainage (PCD) was performed and urinoma was excluded by fluid analysis. The drainage volume was maintained at 60–100 mL/day for 13 days. Since the drainage output remained steady without any significant decrease over time, we considered it refractory.2 On POD 40, ultrasound-guided intranodal lymphangiography via the left inguinal lymph node was performed using a 26-gauge spinal needle. Lipiodol® (6 to 7 mL) was slowly injected under fluoroscopy until a focal lymphatic leak was visualized (Fig. 2). Embolization was achieved by opacification of the leaking branch and absence of further extravasation. No adjunctive embolic agents such as N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA) were used. Drainage output decreased rapidly after the procedure and ceased within 10 days, at which point the catheter was removed following confirmation of resolution on follow-up CT (Fig. 3). He remains asymptomatic without recurrence at 31 months.

APCT 8 days after post-embolization. The previously observed leak is no longer visible, and Lipiodol® is visualized along the lymphatic channels, effective embolization and sealing of the leak.

Sclerotherapy, while effective in many cases, may be less suitable for high-output active leaks because sclerosants can fail to reach the leakage site.3 It also carries a recurrence rate of up to 20% and potential complications such as inflammation, fibrosis, or catheter-induced infection.2 Surgical marsupialization provides definitive drainage but is invasive and risks ureteral or vascular injury, limiting its use in fragile patients.1 Lymphangiography with Lipiodol® offers both diagnostic and therapeutic benefits by enabling direct visualization and embolization of leaking lymphatic channels in a single, minimally invasive procedure.3,4 Lipiodol® alone can seal leaks via its viscosity and inflammatory effects.3,5 Lipiodol® monotherapy may be effective in selected cases of lymphatic leakage.5 Compared with adhesive agents such as NBCA, it is less destructive to lymphatic channels, allowing the possibility of future re-intervention. Although no complications occurred, rare adverse events such as fat embolism and hypersensitivity have been reported.5

In conclusion, lymphangiography with Lipiodol® can provide durable resolution of refractory lymphocele after KT, and may be considered in selected patients in whom conventional treatments are unsuitable.
