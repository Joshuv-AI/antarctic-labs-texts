It's a game changer in terms of diabetic management’: A qualitative study of adolescents' and parents' experiences of a 6‐month trial of continuous glucose monitoring in type 2 diabetes
Authors: Maheesha Fernando, Alexia S Peña, Ashlee King, Melissa Oxlad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo qualitatively explore adolescents with type 2 diabetes (T2D) using any treatment regimen and parents of such adolescents' experiences of a 6‐month trial of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and its perceived barriers and facilitators.MethodsAdolescents provided with CGM sensors (Freestyle Libre 2) free of charge for 6 months and parents recruited from the outpatient paediatric diabetes clinic at the Women's and Children's Hospital, South Australia participated. Four adolescents and six parents participated in a separate focus group or a semi‐structured interview within 1 month (baseline) of CGM use; three adolescents and four parents participated in a focus group after 6 months of CGM use. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsWe generated four themes: (1) A Convenient and Practical Tool for Adolescent Monitoring, Self‐Management, Education, and Behaviour Change; (2) Enhanced Parental Diabetes Oversight and Reduced Family Emotional Burden; (3) Psychosocial, Practical, and Affordability Barriers to Adolescent CGM Use; (4) Education, Improved Adhesion, and Supportive School Environments as Facilitators of Adolescent CGM Use.ConclusionsAdolescents with T2D and parents perceived CGM as a more acceptable and convenient tool than finger prick blood glucose monitoring. CGM benefits included facilitating education, improving self‐management skills, and promoting behavioural modification. CGM was also perceived to reduce families' emotional burden associated with diabetes management. Device limitations, including technology accuracy, alarms, and adhesion issues, posed challenges for families. Equitable access and funding models were the main barriers to the wider adoption of CGM in this population within Australia.Keywords: adolescents, continuous glucose monitoring, sensor qualitative, teenagers, type 2 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of childhood onset type 2 diabetes (T2D) has been steadily increasing in Australia and globally. 1 , 2 T2D in adolescents is linked to a rapid accumulation of comorbidities, as well as micro and macro complications. 3 , 4 , 5 The observational follow‐up of the Treatment Options for Type 2 Diabetes in Adolescents and Youth study (TODAY) found that by a mean age of 26.4 years, 60.1% of participants had at least one microvascular complication, and 28.4% had two or more. 4 The alarming statistics highlight the need to develop strategies to effectively engage and treat children and adolescents with T2D early in their disease.

A key part of management for adolescents with T2D is promoting positive lifestyle changes, but promoting sustainable change is often challenging. In 2024, a parliamentary inquiry into Diabetes Mellitus in Australia recommended expanding subsidised access for CGM to those with T2D requiring regular insulin. 6 However, there is growing evidence that CGM offers benefits to adults with T2D, extending beyond those on insulin therapy. 7 In adults with T2D, CGM use facilitates improved glycaemic and metabolic outcomes 8 and may serve as a biofeedback tool to modify behaviour and improve self‐management independent of medication use. 9 , 10 , 11 CGM use allows for more efficient and timely escalation of therapies for early hyperglycaemia. 7 Currently, the Australian government provides subsidised CGM products through the National Diabetes Service Scheme for individuals with Type 1 diabetes (T1D). 12

Although adolescent and parent perspectives regarding CGM use in T1D have been well evaluated, 13 , 14 there are limited studies evaluating CGM use in adolescents with T2D. Where researchers have examined CGM use in adolescents with T2D, most studies to date have been quantitative, with few qualitative studies and, importantly, none conducted in Australia. Additionally, most studies among adolescents with T2D have included adolescents on insulin therapy. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 One recent randomised controlled trial explored the feasibility and acceptability of CGM among adolescents with T2D with varying treatment regimens (insulin and non‐insulin). 20 A critical aspect of promoting widespread CGM adoption is understanding users' experiences, enabling diabetes teams (e.g., multidisciplinary teams comprising medical practitioners, diabetes educators, dietitians, psychologists, social workers etc) and policymakers to better support implementation. As this question has not been examined in Australia, we aimed to qualitatively explore adolescents with T2D using any treatment regimen and parents of such adolescents' experiences of a 6‐month trial of CGM and its perceived barriers and facilitators.

METHODS

Study design

This study is part of a broader mixed‐methods project longitudinally examining CGM use among adolescents with T2D participating in a 6‐month trial. We employed a qualitative descriptive design using focus groups or semi‐structured interviews to explore the experiences of a 6‐month trial of CGM in adolescents with T2D and their parents, guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ; see Table S1). 21 We deemed that a qualitative descriptive design was appropriate to enable us to explore an area with limited research to date, and to stay close to participants' descriptions, using these, rather than our interpretations, to inform healthcare professionals, policymakers, and researchers about adolescents' and parents' experiences and better support CGM implementation. The Women's and Children's Hospital Human Research and Ethics Committee approved this study (HREC 2021/HRE00376). All participants over 16 years of age provided written consent. For participants under 16, written assent was obtained from the participants and written consent from their parents.

Participants and recruitment

Adolescents aged 10–19 years with T2D, regardless of their treatment regimen or previous exposure to CGM, who had been diagnosed with T2D for at least 1 month and were willing to wear regularly 14‐day freestyle Libre2 CGM sensors (Abbott Inc) for 6 months that were provided free of charge and parents of adolescents who met inclusion criteria were eligible to participate. Prior CGM exposure may have occurred and was allowed as our centre offers CGM to all adolescents admitted to the hospital with a diagnosis of diabetes while awaiting confirmation of the type of diabetes. Adolescents with T1D or other forms of diabetes (including monogenic) and people (adolescents and parents) with intellectual disability or inability to speak English were ineligible to participate.

We recruited eligible participants using convenience sampling from the outpatient paediatric diabetes clinic at the Women's and Children's Hospital, South Australia, a tertiary paediatric hospital (between April and November 2024). A diabetes educator (AK) reviewed participants at baseline for education/insertion of CGM and 2 months after they started on CGM to review CGM use and provide additional CGM support. Participants also attended their regular 3‐month review with their paediatric endocrinologist.

Data collection

Three authors, MO, AP, and MF developed the topic guides after reading relevant literature on the topic 15 , 17 and based on their clinical experience. Topics included experiences of living with T2D, knowledge and attitudes about CGM, including benefits and barriers of use, and the impacts of CGM on health behaviours.

We collected data via semi‐structured interview or separate focus groups with adolescents diagnosed with T2D and their parents guided by similar but tailored topic guides (see Table S2). Data collection entailed one individual semi‐structured interview and five separate focus groups (one adolescent and two parent baseline focus groups in April and July, and one adolescent and one parent focus group in November 2024). We interviewed adolescents and parents separately to minimise power imbalances, social desirability responding, and influence over each other's responses, enabling each group to speak more openly and freely and to provide more independent perspectives. After obtaining consent, two authors, MF and MO, facilitated the interview and focus groups using video conferencing software (Zoom) at baseline within 1 month of CGM use and after 6 months of CGM use. At baseline, the adolescent interview was approximately 17 min, and the focus group was 45 min, while the parent focus groups lasted approximately 58 and 66 min. The focus groups after 6 months of CGM use lasted approximately 41 min for adolescents and 60 min for parents. The interview and focus groups were audiorecorded and transcribed verbatim; all transcripts were deidentified. One facilitator (MF) made field notes after data collection. Participants received a separate $30AUD gift voucher for the interview or each focus group they participated in.

We reviewed adolescents' medical records within a month of study commencement to obtain clinical data including age, sex, HbA1c, comorbidities and complications of T2D, weight, height, and body mass index (BMI). We obtained demographic information including ethnicity, main language spoken at home, parental education status, and household structure by asking directly and reviewing medical records as part of our wider research project.

Data analysis

We analysed the data using inductive reflexive thematic analysis. 22 MO guided and supervised MF, enabling them to lead the analysis. MF and MO familiarised themselves with the transcripts through repeated reading. Reviewing transcripts line by line, MF manually generated initial codes by noting recurrent, interesting, or novel words, phrases, or ideas related to the research question. MO reviewed the codes and assisted MF in refining them by combining codes which contained the same information or were repetitive, clarifying some codes through further discussion, and grouping related codes to develop initial themes. All authors reviewed, refined, and named the themes. Relevant quotations were selected to illustrate each theme.

Reflexivity

The research team comprised four female health professionals from three different professions. Two female researchers, MF, a paediatric endocrine fellow, and MO, a health and clinical psychologist and qualitative researcher, conducted the interview and focus groups. AP, a female paediatric endocrinologist, AK, a female diabetes educator, MF, and MO have all practised as healthcare professionals. AK and AP may have been known to participants but were not involved in the interview or focus groups. All researchers have experience supporting people with diabetes, but none have personal experience of adolescent T2D. Neither of the interview or focus group facilitators were treating participants; one was possibly known to participants as part of a multidisciplinary team and the other was unknown. This combination of facilitators helped to provide an element of familiarity, especially for adolescents, while also providing the presence of a researcher who was an ‘outsider’ to the treatment setting. The team debriefed after each interview or focus group and met regularly to discuss data analysis and interpretation, and aid reflexivity.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

In total, six adolescents and nine parents participated. At baseline, four adolescents and six parents participated in a baseline focus group, while one adolescent participated in a semi‐structured interview. The single interview occurred because other potential focus group participants were unable to attend on the day, and the adolescent still wished to share their experiences. Three adolescents and four parents participated in a focus group after 6 months of CGM use. Two adolescents and one parent participated in both relevant focus groups. Six adolescents (100%) and eight parents (89%) had previous experience with CGM prior to the study, as our centre offers CGM to all adolescents admitted to the hospital with a diagnosis of diabetes while awaiting confirmation of the type of diabetes.

Adolescents were five females and one male, with a median age 14.4 (range 10–18) years, median BMI of 37 [range 29.2–47.6] kg/m2, median HbA1c of 8.7 [range 5.5–10.6] % 71.5 [36.6–92.8] mmol/mol and median diabetes duration of 12 [range 3–23] months. Adolescents' diabetes management included metformin only (n = 1), metformin plus sodium glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor (n = 2), metformin plus insulin (n = 1), metformin plus SGLT2 inhibitor plus glucagon like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP1RA) (n = 1), and metformin plus GLP1RA (n = 1). Adolescents identified as Australian (n = 3) and culturally and linguistically diverse (n = 3). Parents identified as Australian (n = 3), culturally and linguistically diverse (n = 4), and Aboriginal (n = 2).

Parent participants (n = 9) included eight mothers and one father with varying levels of education (n = 2 completed high school only and n = 7 completed further study diploma, bachelor or graduate degree). T2D was common among parents and self‐reported in eight parent participants. Two parents had children with a learning or intellectual disability and autism.

Theme 1: A convenient and practical tool for adolescent monitoring, self‐management, education, and behaviour change

Adolescents and parents described CGM as a practical, convenient, and easy to use tool for monitoring, self‐management, and education. Adolescents spoke about how integrating CGM into their school and daily life made it easier for them to monitor their blood glucose levels without the need to go to their lockers, the school office, or a dedicated room. They noted that monitoring with CGM was much easier than finger pricking as it invoked less fear and pain, was more portable, monitoring could occur for longer, and they did not need to dispose of sharps (Quotes 1 and 2).

Parents also reported that CGM was more acceptable to adolescents for monitoring than finger prick testing. CGM was reported as less invasive, contributing to increased willingness to monitor and more frequent monitoring than finger pricking, with less finger pricking contributing to less resistance to take insulin, particularly when adolescents had learning disabilities or sensory issues (Quote 3).

Enhanced monitoring through regular checking with CGM was also reinforced by adolescents (Quote 4).

Participants also described CGM as enhancing adolescents' self‐management, education, and independence. They indicated that adolescents used CGM as a self‐education tool (although the tool was also helpful for parents). Parents reported that adolescents used CGM to enhance dietary awareness, checking their levels before and after eating, and when eating different foods, which helped them learn how different foods and beverages affected their bodies and even to educate others (Quote 5).

Parents also shared that CGM empowered their children, giving them more control over their health, encouraging them to be more independent and involved in their own care, and giving them enhanced freedom (Quote 6).

CGM also assisted adolescents to modify their health behaviours, including physical activity and diet (foods to avoid and portion sizes). Adolescents explained how CGM increased their awareness of the impact of diet on their blood glucose levels and led them to modify their behaviour (Quote 7).

The visualisation of CGM data also fostered adolescents' motivation to change their dietary behaviour (Quote 8).

Parents also noticed that the independence and self‐sufficiency that came with CGM contributed to increased socialisation and willingness to attend social functions with family and friends and to manage diabetes without parental input (Quote 9).

Theme 2: Enhanced parental diabetes oversight and reduced family emotional burden

CGM also contributed to enhancing parental diabetes oversight and reducing families' emotional burden. Parents reported that prior to using CGM, they were working without necessary information (Quote 10), with adolescents' diabetes management largely unknown to them (Quote 11), especially when their children resisted or refused finger prick monitoring. They viewed CGM as making diabetes management easier for the whole family (Quote 12). CGM increased parents' awareness of their child's glycaemic management and provided them with timely information outside of medical appointments, particularly in the context of adolescents' potential low compliance with finger pricking (Quote 13).

Additionally, real‐time monitoring as well as the ability to detect impending low blood sugar levels early provided reassurance for parents (Quote 14).

Remote monitoring of CGM data when children are at school or away from home for other reasons, in particular, reduces anxiety, giving parents peace of mind and empowering them to better educate and support their children (Quote 15).

Parents reported that they could use CGM data to understand which foods are impacting their children, respond to glucose fluctuations, assist their child to make therapy adjustments, and protect them from hypoglycaemia (low blood glucose) and hyperglycaemia (high blood glucose). CGM was seen as invaluable in making appropriate adjustments, with parents explaining difficulties in managing diabetes and adjusting medication without CGM data (Quote 16).

Adolescents also reported parental oversight of CGM data to be beneficial, especially if they experienced hypoglycaemia while sleeping. Having access to CGM data enabled their parents to know what was happening to provide guidance, support, and encouragement to take any necessary medical actions and avoid confusion (Quote 17).

However, the benefits of parental real‐time monitoring must be balanced with respecting adolescents' privacy and autonomy. At times, adolescents did not appreciate parental involvement in monitoring. One parent spoke about their child's negative reactions when they monitored and asked about their levels (Quote 18).

Another parent also described how maintaining consistent long‐term use of CGM may be challenging, with their child becoming reluctant and resistant to replacing sensors (Quote 19).

Although negative reactions to parental monitoring and resistance to sensor replacements occurred at times, parents described that CGM generally reduced diabetes‐related stress and family conflict. CGM provided a sense of calm by enabling monitoring with fewer questions, challenging conversations and arguments, and without struggling to enforce finger pricks. It resulted in noticeable positive differences in family interactions. Parents explained that CGM resulted in less conflict (Quote 20) about diabetes management and their child's health (Quote 21).

Theme 3: Psychosocial, practical, and affordability barriers to adolescent CGM use

All participants described benefits of CGM; however, they also communicated several psychosocial and practical barriers to its use. A common barrier raised by most participants was sensor adhesion, with adolescents and parents describing sensors that would not stick or came off easily, including due to insufficient skin preparation, catching on clothing, bumping it, or contact with water. They reported this occurred even when using stickers, CGM tapes, strapping, and sporting tapes (Quote 22).

Aside from attachment challenges, parents also commented on the potential for adverse social consequences for adolescents. They explained that visibility of the device can result in questions or unwanted attention and judgement from peers (Quote 23).

In South Australia, student use of mobile phones during school hours is banned. While exceptions can be made on a case‐by‐case basis, especially for medical reasons, adolescents shared concerns that using their phone to check their levels may result in people asking questions or them getting into trouble with teachers (Quote 24).

Participants also shared technical issues they have experienced with CGM, including suboptimal accuracy, bluetooth connection failures, and issues with downloading apps onto compatible phones. Parents described concerns about device accuracy (Quote 25).

One adolescent also raised concerns about inaccurate CGM readings leading to confusion (Quote 26).

While they spoke of general concerns about accuracy, participants also reported difficulties with device alarms at times (out of range or false alarms), including during the night. Adolescents also reported that alarms generated negative reactions in the school environment (Quote 27).

Additionally, participants described a range of accessibility and affordability barriers to CGM use. In particular, device cost was the main barrier to ongoing CGM use. Parents stated that the device was expensive and a real burden that they could not afford (Quote 28).

They described distress at being unable to afford a device they perceived to be very important and beneficial for their child and were willing to use if accessible (Quote 29).

At present, in Australia, CGM is not subsidised for people with T2D. Parents suggested that CGM should be nationally funded to improve access and reduce the likelihood of possible diabetes complications and, in turn, the need for people to go on the National Disability Insurance Scheme (an Australian Federal government funding scheme for people with disability). Participants stated that subsidised or free access to CGM was very important, regardless of diabetes type, particularly for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples who tend to have higher rates of diabetes (Quote 30).

Theme 4: Education, improved adhesion, and supportive school environments as facilitators of adolescent CGM use

While participants described several barriers to CGM use, most parents and adolescents expressed a desire to continue using CGM and stated that they would recommend CGM to others. They also highlighted several things, in addition to affordability, that would facilitate CGM use. CGM education delivered using brochures, books, videos, and face‐to‐face discussions was stated to be important. Adolescents and parents suggested that face‐to‐face delivery fostered greater understanding, allowed for clarification questions, and provided opportunities for experiential learning (Quote 31).

In addition to general education, participants viewed technology troubleshooting support, particularly about bluetooth and Wi‐Fi connection, as important facilitators to CGM use. Having the choice of a hand‐held scanner rather than relying on a Wi‐Fi connection was helpful, as was educating adolescents about how best to use the technology (Quote 32).

As well as education, participants discussed practical features of CGM that would facilitate use. In particular, they suggested that efforts need to be made to improve and address sensor adhesion (Quote 33).

Participants also highlighted that schools could facilitate CGM use by providing a supportive environment, especially in the context of a no phone policy. They spoke about the importance of exemptions from the no phone policy, and teachers being aware of the need for and allowing the use of phones for monitoring without too many questions (Quote 34).

DISCUSSION

We aimed to qualitatively explore adolescents with T2D using any treatment regimen and parents of such adolescents' experiences of a 6‐month trial of CGM and its perceived barriers and facilitators. Our findings suggest that adolescents and parents find CGM to be an acceptable tool with several benefits, including the facilitation of increased self‐management and engagement with diabetes‐related care and reduced family‐related distress and emotional burden around blood glucose monitoring. Barriers to CGM adoption included device limitations (i.e., adhesion, accuracy, alarms) and concerns around affordability and accessibility. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first Australian study to explore experiences and acceptability of CGM over time for adolescents with T2D using any diabetes treatment regimen.

Previous studies to date examining CGM use in adolescents with T2D have predominantly included those being treated with insulin. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 In a randomised controlled trial of youth who were on insulin (not intensive insulin therapy) or non‐insulin diabetes medications, Patel et al. 20 reported that CGM was feasible and acceptable for youth with T2D, and while HbA1c did not change significantly from baseline to 3 months, it tended to reduce among those in the CGM group. Similarly, Manfredo et al. 16 found that a 10‐day trial of CGM in adolescents with T2D on insulin therapy was associated with behavioural modifications, where adolescents checked blood glucose and gave insulin more frequently and most participants wanted to continue using CGM. Adding to knowledge about CGM use in adolescents with T2D, our study demonstrated that families perceive CGM to be beneficial for adolescents with T2D not using insulin (5 out of 6 adolescents were not using insulin). While further research is needed to establish the effectiveness of CGM for adolescents with T2D, our findings suggest that CGM may be a convenient and practical tool that can assist adolescents with monitoring, self‐management, education, and behaviour change.

Our study revealed that adolescents and parents perceived improvement in adolescents' self‐management (i.e., diet and exercise) and engagement with diabetes care based on CGM feedback. These findings are comparable to a small focus group study with nine participants which supported CGM having a beneficial effect on quality of life and behavioural modification and participants expressing a desire to change their eating and exercise patterns based on visual glucose patterns. 17 Additionally, our study highlighted the benefits of CGM in increasing health literacy for adolescents and parents, where there is often an intergenerational diabetes burden.

We found that families attributed reduced conflict and improved family dynamics to CGM use and that most adolescents described parental oversight as beneficial. Our findings are in contrast to a qualitative study utilising semi‐structured interviews of 20 adolescents with T2D and 10 parents examining baseline knowledge and attitudes about CGM which found that although CGM was regarded as convenient over standard blood glucose monitoring, participants were concerned about worsening diabetes stigma, privacy, loss of autonomy, and conflict with parents. 15 Differences in perspectives may be due to Peyyety et al.'s study 15 being conducted at a single time point with varying levels of CGM use and prior experience, while our study assessed the experiences of CGM use at baseline and after 6 months of use.

Our findings suggest that access to technology like CGM reduces the management and emotional burden and assists families living with T2D who are often navigating multiple socioeconomic challenges and from disproportionately marginalised groups. 23 , 24 For example, an Australian study utilising semi‐structured interviews with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander young people with T2D, while not specifically examining CGM, identified multiple barriers to overall T2D management, particularly the challenges of complex lives and comorbidities. 25 Recognising the underlying social determinants of health and the interaction between multiple health comorbidities, socioeconomic disadvantage, and geographic and cultural factors that reinforce and perpetuate suboptimal T2D management is important to consider in diabetes technology uptake.

Our study highlighted that despite perceived benefits there are practical barriers to CGM use, including device limitations, skin reactions/adhesion, and cost. This finding is consistent with a recent study that reported unwillingness to wear the device, diabetes‐related stigma, adhesion issues, accidental removal, and lost devices to be barriers to CGM use among adolescents with T2D. 20 Similar barriers to CGM use have also been reported in adults with T2D using insulin. 26 However, our participants reported financial constraints to be one of the main barriers to CGM use. Financial accessibility must be prioritised by revising funding models to reduce inequities, particularly for adolescents, which will facilitate widespread CGM adoption. Adopting self‐management approaches supported by CGM devices requires a shift in education and service design. A critical step towards this is understanding adolescents' and parents' experiences of technology and the development of individual and/or family‐centred approaches. Positive relationships between adolescents and healthcare professionals, especially diabetes educators, are crucial for reinforcing self‐management skills with CGM and addressing non‐financial barriers to CGM (device limitations, skin reactions/adhesions) as well as the needs of adolescents with T2D. Our findings demonstrate that targeted education and support for parents are essential to address non‐financial barriers and will be particularly important in the context of intergenerational burden of diabetes and suboptimal emotional well‐being that are important to T2D. 27 , 28 Models of care using CGM in adolescents with T2D should be co‐designed with adolescents' and families' experiences in mind. Future research should explore the role of CGM biofeedback in fostering constructive self‐management behaviours and evaluate the cost–benefit of CGM use.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study include the qualitative study design, which enabled a deeper exploration of participants' perspectives. We recognised the importance of giving adolescents a platform to express their views on T2D management that directly impacts them. The 6‐month CGM trial provided insight into lived experiences of CGM use over time. However, only two adolescents and one parent chose to participate in both the baseline and 6‐month focus groups, limiting our ability to fully understand individual views about experiences of CGM use as they may vary with time. While the sample size was relatively small, our sample was diverse and representative of the real‐world clinic population, including participants using different treatment regimens. Additionally, we agreed that we could generate sufficient meaning from the data obtained. However, sampling bias may have occurred, with people who are more engaged in diabetes care and with higher health literacy perhaps being more likely to participate. Also, while our findings offer valuable insights into adolescents' and parents' experiences of CGM use in T2D, our institutional practice of CGM use in all new diabetes diagnoses means that our findings may not represent the experiences of those without prior exposure to CGM. Further research with CGM naïve participants would be highly beneficial.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study highlights the perceived benefits of CGM for adolescents with T2D using any treatment regimen as a potential early intervention tool that improves health literacy and self‐management skills. Using CGM also promotes behaviour modification and reduces families' emotional burden. Despite the benefits, equitable access and funding models remain key barriers to the wider adoption of CGM for T2D within Australia. Funding bodies should recognise the important benefits of CGM in this highly vulnerable population of adolescents with T2D at risk of early diabetes complications.
