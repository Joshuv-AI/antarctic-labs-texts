Development of Legal and Practical Framework for Naturally Occurring Radioactive Materials (NORM) Management
Authors: Boguslaw Michalik, Zbigniew Bzowski, Hubert Makuła, Kateřina Navrátilová Rovenská, Ivana Ženatá, Gareth Law
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article examines the regulatory frameworks governing the management of Naturally Occurring Radioactive Material (NORM) and highlights the challenges industries encounter in achieving compliance with these regulations. It outlines the evolution of NORM management within the radiation protection system and addresses difficulties related to categorizing NORM as radioactive waste, which can impede recovery and disposal efforts. Through detailed analysis, it identifies fundamental principles of conventional industrial waste management and emphasizes the potential utility of existing European Union directives—such as those on general waste and waste from extractive industries—in managing NORM. The discussion provides both a rationale and a legal context, noting that what is now regarded as NORM waste was previously included in broader industrial waste streams before radioactivity concerns were raised. The article also points out inconsistencies and ambiguities in the application of these directives to NORM waste, particularly concerning waste where the exemption level is exceeded. It recommends establishing a specific waste group that encompasses all waste produced by non-nuclear industries with potentially significant concentrations of naturally occurring radionuclides. This pragmatic proposal would help distinguish waste that should not be classified as radioactive, while ensuring that, when managed under the relevant directives, additional NORM-specific restrictions are considered. The article also briefly discusses key examples to illustrate considerations unique to radioactive materials when applying treatment and disposal methods developed for conventional waste. Ultimately, it underscores the importance of integrating NORM management into the broader waste management framework to ensure consistency with environmental protection, occupational health and safety regulations, and the management of hazardous substances, thereby fostering a holistic approach that accounts for all waste properties, including radioactivity and other pollutants.Keywords: NORM, waste, disposal, radiation protection, integration

Introduction

Contrary to widespread belief, the presence of radioactive materials is not limited only to nuclear power plants, high-tech chemical laboratories, or the military; rather, radioactivity is a part of the environment and has existed since the creation of the Universe. Radioactivity is found naturally in the rocks constituting the Earth’s crust, soil, water, and even outdoor air. Consequently, it is present in the human living environment, in buildings as well as in biota, including food and human bodies; as such, one cannot ever be in a radioactivity-free environment. Naturally Occurring Radionuclides (NOR) include primordial radionuclides and their decay products, as well as cosmogenic radionuclides. Primordial radionuclides have half-lives comparable to the age of the Earth. Cosmogenic radionuclides are continuously formed in the atmosphere through nuclear reactions induced by cosmic radiation on stable elements. Naturally occurring radionuclides are found in bedrock, soil, building materials, water, air, and the human body. The most abundantly present NOR in the environment are isotopes of different elements from the uranium (U-238 and U-235) and thorium (Th-232) decay series and potassium (K-40), which exhibit a broad range of half-life and activity concentration levels (UNSCEAR 2010).

The activity concentration of NOR in mineral-based materials varies due to the uneven distribution of these elements in the Earth’s crust, resulting from geological processes. NOR can contribute to exposure of humans and other biota to ionising radiation, which, under normal circumstances, is considered the natural background and typically falls outside the scope of radiation protection concerns. Nevertheless, NOR may be present in raw materials such as ores, fossil fuels, associated rocks, or formation water. Furthermore, industrial activities including mining, processing of raw materials, extractive metallurgy, ore beneficiation, smelting, chemical and physical separation, water and liquid effluent treatment, and flue gas cleaning have the potential to concentrate NOR, even when initially present at low levels. Consequently, residues, waste, by-products, and even final products with enhanced NOR activity concentrations may occur across various industrial sectors not directly connected to the nuclear industry or deliberate radiation use. In certain instances, resulting exposures of humans and the environment to radiation should not be disregarded from the perspective of radiation protection (Michalik et al., 2023).

The term NORM (Naturally Occurring Radioactive Material) is defined by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) as “Radioactive material containing no significant amounts of radionuclides other than naturally occurring radionuclides,” where the exact definition of ‘significant amounts’ is a regulatory decision. Additional explanation is provided that this term also covers “material in which the activity concentrations of the naturally occurring radionuclides have been changed by a process” (IAEA 2022). The cases when NOR fall into radiation protection concern, i.e., become NORM, as considered in this article, include situations when materials containing naturally occurring radionuclides are:

used in a technological process,

used in a technological process,

exploited/mined,

exploited/mined,

introduced to the human living environment, such as, e.g., building materials,

disposed of into the environment, e.g., waste.

When NORM is introduced into an industrial process, its distribution and final activity concentration are influenced by the composition of the NOR in the raw material, as well as the chemical and physical processes employed in the specific production technology. Several publications provide an overview of industrial sectors with the potential to produce NORM (UNSCEAR 1988, European Commission 1999a, UNSCEAR 2010, Council Directive 2013, ICRP 2019). Detailed information on some of the industrial sectors is provided by the IAEA (IAEA 2003, IAEA 2007, IAEA 2011, IAEA 2012, IAEA 2013). NORM can cause exposure of workers and members of the public exceeding the level of natural background, and sometimes even cause environmental pollution. Any of these situations is of interest to the radiation protection community from both a scientific and, recently, a regulatory perspective.

Regulation of exposure of workers from natural sources of ionizing radiation was already included in the previous version of the International Basic Safety Standards published in 1996 (IAEA 1996). Human activities such as “underground mining of coal and of phosphate rocks and other minerals” were considered as practices1 (IAEA 1996). At that time, the available quantitative recommendations of the International Committee on Radiological Protection (ICRP) were confined to radon, the General Obligations for practices concerning the natural sources of radiation were limited to exposure to radon. It was expected that other natural sources of radiation would be dealt with via the concept of exclusion or exemption of the source or dealt with by the Regulatory Authority (IAEA 1996). Issued in the same year, the Council Directive of the European Union 96/29/EURATOM laying down basic safety standards for the protection of workers and the general public against ionizing radiation (Council Directive 1996) pays attention to exposure from sources of natural radiation as well. According to this directive, Article 40(2), Member States of the European Union were obliged to identify situations that may lead to a significant increase in exposure, both to workers and to members of the public, due to the presence of natural radiation sources. To differentiate these situations from those described in this directive, which involve a risk from ionizing radiation originating from artificial sources or from natural sources where natural radionuclides have been processed for their radioactive, fissile, or fertile properties (Art. 2(1)), the specific term “work activities” has been established (Art. 40(2a, b, c, and d)).

Precise regulations for NORM-involving industries were established in 2014. The co-sponsored edition of the International Basic Safety Standards (IAEA 2014) was published by the IAEA, hereinafter referred to as IAEA BSS. It regards industries processing NORM a priori as existing exposure situations; however, if a certain level (1 Bq/g for U-238 series and Th-232 series and 10 Bq/g for K-40) is exceeded, then the requirements for planned exposure situations should apply.

More strict and systematic requirements were imposed on the EU Member States and industry operators by the Council Directive 2013/59/EURATOM laying down basic safety standards for protection against the dangers arising from exposure to ionising radiation (Council Directive 2013), hereinafter referred to as EU BSS. The EU BSS mandates that industries processing materials containing naturally occurring radionuclides should be managed within the same regulatory framework as planned exposure situations (Recital 16). It allows for the exemption of a given practice from regulatory control based on the same criteria as the IAEA BSS, expressed in terms of activity concentration. Additionally, a general exemption criterion is defined in terms of effective dose. For practices involving artificial radiation sources, this criterion is set at 10 μSv per year. In contrast, for practices involving materials containing NOR that are not undertaken for their radioactive, fissile, or fertile properties, the allowable dose increment, taking into account the prevailing natural background radiation, is of the order of 1 mSv per year or less for any individual (Annex VII).

The assessment of doses to the public must consider not only the disposal or recycling of solid residues but also pathways resulting from airborne or liquid effluent. Unlike solids, there are no exemption criteria expressed in terms of activity concentration for such effluents. Qualitative requirements are set, especially considering the possibility of drinking water contamination. The target qualitative criteria for drinking water are specified in Council Directive 2013/51/Euratom (Council Directive 2013a).

Although exposure to radon is generally treated as an existing exposure situation, both the EU Basic Safety Standards and the IAEA BSS require the application of planned exposure requirements in specific circumstances. These apply when radon isotopes and their progeny are present in workplaces where occupational exposure to other radionuclides in the U-238 or Th-232 decay series is already regulated as a planned exposure, or when radon activity concentrations persistently exceed the applicable reference level (EU BSS Recital 25, Art. 9, Art. 54, IAEA BSS requirements 5.27, 5.28 and 5.29).

Building materials, defined by the EU BSS as construction products used for permanent incorporation in residential and regularly used buildings, are regulated by both the EU BSS (Art. 1(d), Art. 75, Annex VIII) and IAEA SAFETY REPORTS SERIES No. 117 Regulatory Control of Exposure Due to Radionuclides in Building Materials and Construction Materials (IAEA 2023), as well as by the Construction Products Regulation Laying down harmonised rules for the marketing of construction products (Regulation (EU) 2024/3110). The reference level for national regulations is based on an annual effective dose value of 1 mSv (further explanation on the selection of such a value is available, e.g., in ICRP 82 (ICRP 2000). The screening tool, based on measured activity concentrations of K-40, Ra-226, and Th-232, is recommended to facilitate routine monitoring (EU BSS Annex VIII, IAEA 2023 art. 3.3.3). Radon exhalation from building materials is not covered by the current version of the EU BSS. However, there is a long-term strategy to implement regulations for radon exhalation from building materials (Ljubljana 2024).

Problem formulation and scope

Non-radioactive contaminants occurring in waste have been managed within a well-organized system developed under relevant EU directives and applied consistently across all EU Member States. While radiation protection is also governed by a robust and well-established regulatory framework, the interface between radiation protection legislation and EU waste legislation lacks a clear and operational linkage, particularly in cases involving waste streams with combined hazardous and radioactive properties. Accordingly, the initial idea is to integrate the property of radioactivity into the existing waste management system.

Building on this overview below, section 3 of this article focuses on the discussion of viable solutions for NORM waste management based on existing systems of common (ordinary, conventional) waste management. Throughout this article, the term “NORM waste” will be used to identify residues managed or to be managed under the EU Directive on waste, encompassing additional properties related to NOR content, while the definition of waste in the EU Waste Directive is largely consistent with that used in the IAEA Safety Glossary, the Directive goes further by explicitly promoting waste recovery and reuse in line with the waste hierarchy (Publications Office of the European Union, 2025), which is not addressed in the IAEA definition. However, this does not influence the sense of further discussion.

Key considerations

Although Article 23 of the EU BSS requires Member States to identify classes or types of practices involving NORM, the RadoNorm project e-survey across European countries (Mrdakovic Popic et al. 2023) showed that at least one third of responding countries (corresponding to 16 European Members States) did not carry out the NORM industry inventory. The e-survey indicates that in the EU, the main NORM-related industries are cement production, oil and gas production, coal-fired power plants, followed by phosphate fertiliser production and groundwater filtration. The data provided indicates that inventories, if any, are prepared on a case-by-case basis, utilizing indicative lists of potential NORM-involving industries as outlined in both EU and IAEA BSS guidelines. NORM-related issues were identified through research focused on detecting the presence of radionuclides, not for the presence of other harmful substances. Therefore, when identified, radioactivity was typically considered the primary (the only) stressor and perceived as a major issue requiring attention through radiation protection measures. Consequently, NORM problems are often addressed in a one-sided manner, with current approaches to overall NORM management being determined by the principles of radiation protection. All criteria, limits, or reference levels are expressed in quantities related to radiation, such as radionuclide activity concentration or effective dose. The publication associated with this survey (Mrdakovic Popic et al. 2023), which provides an up-to-date overview of the field of NORM-involving practices in Europe, is recommended to the reader. However, activities involving NORM span a broad range of industrial sectors with diverse characteristics, including various physical and chemical processes.

The use of NORM as feedstock, or in general, as raw materials, is often the only or one of very limited options for getting to valuable element(s) or mineral(s), which is why the practice is justified in terms of radiation protection with no interlocking dependencies with other regulations. In rare cases, the same situation may occur for some final products where radioactivity is inextricably linked with other expected product features, such as TIG welding rods or simple, glazed floor tile surfaces. Conversely, in the case of waste or residues resulting from technological processes, the issue of radioactivity becomes more complex. The wide range of chemical compositions of such residues, including the presence of numerous toxic (non-radioactive) compounds, raises questions about which contamination is most critical and whether it is possible to manage multiple stressors within a single framework.

In reality, NORM is utilized as a raw material in various industrial processes where its application is justified and typically regulated in terms of radiation protection as a planned exposure situation. Additionally, NORM may arise from the processing of raw materials not initially classified as NORM. For example, coal, which generally exhibits low activity concentrations of NOR, can result in significant accumulation of NOR in coal combustion products (CCP), warranting its classification as NORM. Respondents to the RadoNorm project e-survey reported NOR activity concentration data for a range of materials, including raw materials, products, by-products, residues, and waste, with values spanning from fractions of Bq/g to several thousand Bq/g. While comparable approaches are utilized for the management and treatment of NORM waste when NOR activity concentrations remain below reference thresholds, significant challenges arise when these concentrations exceed established limits.

In general, the processing of any mineral-origin raw material is expected to yield several types of residues. Considering the potential NOR content, the term “NORM residue” as defined by the IAEA Glossary (IAEA 2022), “material that remains from a process and comprises or is contaminated by NORM” is applicable to these residues. Many of these residues possess useful properties and can be further utilized, as emphasized by the IAEA document “Policies and Strategies for Management of NORM Residues and Waste” (IAEA 2024), which expands the NORM residue definition to “a material generated in a production which can serve a beneficial use and that use is authorized by the relevant regulatory authorities”. The IAEA definitions address the issue of natural radioactivity, which is relatively new to many industries currently accounted as involving NORM. Given that NORM-involving industries in many cases have existed for decades and materials, residues, or waste currently referred to as NORM did not appear suddenly and in the overwhelming majority have existed since a relevant industry was set into operation, the problem of radioactivity of NORM should be rather integrated into existing practices than considered separately. All waste has been managed in a well-defined manner, controlled according to the requirements set by EU regulations concerning waste or, in specific cases, mining waste for decades. The content of radionuclides is one of the properties of waste recently recognised for which specific criteria for evaluation are set within the framework of the radiation protection and often does not represent the main stressor.

Moreover, it should be highlighted that NORM waste differs from radioactive waste in the vast majority of cases, primarily in terms of their volume (medium to large), the predominant presence of naturally occurring radionuclides, and, in most cases, their low or moderate radionuclide activity concentration, whilst the half-life of some of the constituent radionuclides is very long. Long half-life’s create difficulties in extension of existing rules for radioactive waste management as described by EC Recommendation on a classification system for solid radioactive waste (European Commission 1999b), the EURATOM directive establishing a Community framework for the responsible and safe management of spent fuel and radioactive waste (Council Directive 2011) and the Euratom Treaty (Council of the EU 2016) into NORM waste at the level of legislation, as well as in the context of technical solutions, availability of relevant repositories, and economy.

According to existing rules, if useless residue emerges in the process, the industry operator must categorize it as waste. Waste, for this purpose, is defined as any substance or object that the holder discards, intends to discard, or is required to discard unless it can be repurposed. Currently, the management of (not only) industrial waste is governed by the Directive 2008/98/EC on waste and repealing certain Directives, hereinafter referred to as the EU Directive on waste (Directive 2008), and by the Directive 2006/21/EC on the management of waste from extractive industries and amending Directive 2004/35/EC, hereinafter referred to as the Directive on waste from the extractive industry (Directive 2006). These directives define all options for waste treatment, including recovery and disposal (for the full list, please see supplementary materials Annex 2). However, these directives do not address radioactivity as a property of waste, leading to ambiguities and challenges in practical implementation, particularly in cases where the waste contains more than negligible amounts of natural radionuclides. Although the RadoNorm e-survey showed that national legislative and regulatory frameworks for the regulation of NORM are developed in accordance with the EU BSS, the authors of the survey argue that the survey responses indicated the interest and “the need of development of improved and more consistent operational and management procedures for overall NORM” (Mrdakovic Popic et al. 2023). A variety of approaches to the management of NORM in European countries, with a focus on NORM waste disposal, was also provided by the recent study done by Gellerman et al. (Gellerman R et al. 2023).

Fundamentals of radiation-protection-based NORM waste management

From the perspective of radiation protection and the EU BSS, identified NORM involving industries are considered as practices (see Chapter II, Article 4, Definition 65 of the EU BSS) and are therefore subject to all relevant Articles and Annexes of the EU BSS, just like any workplace involving artificial radionuclides. Regulatory control should be commensurate with the magnitude and likelihood of exposures resulting from practices (Council Directive 2013, IAEA 2014). Notification, authorisation (i.e. registration and licensing), as well as inspection and the possible exemption from notification or authorization serve to this purpose. While exemption is a priori act, the government or the regulatory body shall determine which justified practices or sources within justified practices are to be exempted from some or all of the regulatory requirements, clearance is the process of release from regulatory control of material coming from notified or authorised practices (see Chapter II, Article 4, Definition 11 - clearance level of the EU BSS).

Here, it is worthwhile mentioning the concept of exclusion. Some of the types of human exposure to radiation, e.g., K-40 in the human body or cosmic radiation at the surface of the Earth, is not feasible to control. This type of exposure is excluded from the scope of regulation, therefore also from the scope of IAEA BSS and EU BSS. More information about the concept of exclusion, exemption, and clearance can be found in the IAEA General Safety Guides on application of the concepts of clearance and exemption (IAEA 2023a, IAEA 2023b).

Both exemption and clearance rely on general criteria, which are as follows: (i) the radiological risks to individuals caused by the practice are sufficiently low, as to be of no regulatory concern; (ii) the type of practice has been determined to be justified; and (iii) the practice is inherently safe (Council Directive 2013, Annex VII). For practical use, both exemption and clearance levels have been created in terms of radionuclide activity concentrations. A different approach for the development of radionuclide activity concentration levels was chosen for artificial and for natural radionuclides. While the former have been established through comprehensive dose-assessment models, employing conservative assumptions and radionuclide-specific properties to ensure that potential exposures remain minimal (below 10 µSv/year) the latter was subject to subsequent development. Early clearance as well as exemption levels for natural radionuclides not used for their radioactive, fissile, or fertile properties had been derived using models and calculations considering limited exposure scenarios, relied on conservative assumptions, ensuring doses remained well below safety thresholds in similar manner as for artificial radionuclides. However, in addition, the special attention was devoted to long lived radionuclides subject successive decay, and the contribution of decay product was included in calculations do not underestimate the exposure. This approach was outlined in Radiation Protection No. 65: Principles and methods for establishing concentrations and quantities (exemption values) below which reporting is not required in the European directive (European Commission, 1993), and, after some approximations, incorporated into the 1996 IAEA Basic Safety Standards (IAEA 1996) and EU Directive 96/29/Euratom (Council Directive 1996). The IAEA Safety Guide RS-G-1.7 (IAEA, 2004), later replaced by General Safety Guide GSG-18 (IAEA, 2023a) and General Safety Guide GSG-17 (IAEA, 2023b), shifted focus towards setting simple, globally applicable criteria based on typical environmental concentrations of naturally occurring radionuclides, balancing regulatory practicality and radiological protection. This means, that if Ra-226 was used as a radioactive source in medicine (for example), a different clearance level applies compared to Ra-226 in contaminated scales derived from the NORM-involving industry.

Exemption and clearance levels for natural radionuclides from the U-238 and Th-232 decay series apply to the decay chain, with the parent radionuclides in secular equilibrium with their progeny (Council Directive 2013, Annex VII). The IAEA is more specific saying that “the values are applicable to all radionuclides of natural origin in the U-238 decay chain and the Th-232 decay chain and for K-40. The same pragmatic approach should be used to determine the activity concentration clearance levels for other primordial radionuclides (e.g. Rb-87, La-138, Sm-147, Lu-176) in situations where these radionuclides are of interest for clearance” (IAEA 2023b). However, the open question remains, if in case of a non-exempted practice, NORM waste can be classified as radioactive waste or not?

From the perspective of the practical implementation of the concept of exemption and clearance, it is worth noting that there is often no secular equilibrium between the decay products present in NORM and NORM waste due to environmental or industrial processes (UNSCEAR 1988, Michalik et al. 2013, Michalik 2017, Michalik et al. 2018, García-Talavera et al. 2021). This may lead to significant overestimation or underestimation of the effective dose if it were calculated based solely on the concentration of parent radionuclides. This may affect NORM classification and the whole management process significantly; however, this problem exceeds the scope of this article and is not discussed further. It is however, worth mentioning that specific recommendations have been developed based on the concepts of exemption and clearance, taking into account specific exposure scenarios in RP 122 part II Application of the Concepts of Exemption and Clearance to Natural Radiation Sources (European Commission 2002). However, they are not included in the EU BSS, where exemption and clearance values are arbitrarily established and articulated in terms of the activity concentration of parent radionuclides across the entire decay series. The justification provided is based on a spectrum of NOR activity concentrations observed in the natural environment, with values selected from the upper end of the global distribution in soil as first reported in IAEA BSS from 1996 (IAEA 1996).

Despite the issue regarding secular equilibrium, the concepts of exemption and clearance for NORM waste management remain effective, interpretable, and applicable when NOR activity concentrations in waste are below established exemption or clearance levels. In cases where these values are exceeded, there is currently no NOR-specific guidance provided by either IAEA recommendations or the EU BSS. Under such circumstances, and in accordance with regulatory control provisions (see Fig. 1) and EU BSS Recital 16 “Protection against natural radiation sources, rather than being addressed separately in a specific title, should be fully integrated within the overall requirements. In particular, industries processing materials containing naturally occurring radionuclides should be managed within the same regulatory framework as other practices. It follows that NORM waste must be regarded as radioactive waste and subject to all relevant restrictions.

Regardless of the methodology employed, waste management systems and related treatment options based on exemption and clearance concepts are focused exclusively on radiation protection principles, without consideration of other material characteristics or its formal classification. Nonetheless, both the EU BSS and the EU Directive on waste operate concurrently, requiring that the obligations of each regulation be met simultaneously.

Practical implementation of radiation-protection-based NORM waste management

The collection of up-to-date practical experience with management of NORM residues and waste was the subject matter of two recent papers (Mrdakovic Popic et al. 2023, Gellerman R et al. 2023).

Gellerman points to the general problem of (any) presence of natural radionuclides in waste, summarizing the experience of a number of colleagues across Europe, and says that there is a “common misunderstanding that all radioactive material, regardless of activity concentration, is ‘dangerous’ (i.e. must be considered as radioactive waste)”(Gellerman R et al. 2023). Gellerman also highlights the diverse approach to NORM waste regulation across EU members states, despite the existence of Directives.

Results of the RadoNorm e-survey (Mrdakovic Popic et al. 2023) showed that “among respondents in about 37% of countries, NORM is never classified as radioactive waste, slightly above 12% declare the use of same limits for NORM as for naturally occurring radionuclides used as radioactive sources (e.g. radium), whereas above these limits, waste is considered as radioactive waste regardless of the purpose of use and physical and chemical form. However, in 50% of the responses, a special limit, expressed as a radionuclide activity concentration, has been established above which NORM residues are classified as radioactive waste. Sometimes, this activity concentration limit is so high (e.g. 1000 Bq/g for radium) that the likelihood of it being exceeded in NORM is extremely low. Moreover, a specific approach was noticed where NORM formally is not classified as radioactive waste, however, must be disposed of in the radioactive waste repository when dose evaluation indicates an excess of dose limits set either to workers or members of the public.”

Apart from the significant diversity in the regulatory approaches to NORM waste management among EU Member States, a major concern raised is that NORM involving industries will eventually need to determine whether their waste should be classified as radioactive waste. Categorizing NORM waste as radioactive can result in significant technical, organizational, and economic challenges for disposal, and it also prevents the recovery or reuse of NORM residues.

It should be emphasised that although all NORM waste contains radionuclides and is therefore physically considered radioactive, legislative definitions of radioactive waste include further criteria set out by relevant regulations. In this context, “radioactive waste” as defined in directives specifically refers to certain types of waste governed by radiation protection laws. Consequently, determining the binding definition of radioactive waste and evaluating its relevance to NORM waste is essential. Given the typical characteristics of NORM and the fact that radioactivity may not be the primary hazard present, acute effects on the public or workers due to NOR exposure are generally not a significant concern. Accordingly, applying all restrictions reserved for radioactive waste may not always be necessary. The primary issue pertains to long-term or chronic exposure, especially when the radiological properties of these materials are neglected over extended periods.

Current status and regulatory gaps

NORM as industrial waste

As mentioned above, waste is defined by the EU Directive on waste as any substance or object which the holder discards or intends or is required to discard (Directive 2008). This directive obliges Member States to adopt into their national laws the mandatory requirement to comply with the waste hierarchy as described in Article 4 of the EU Directive on waste. In waste treatment, priority is given to waste prevention, followed by waste recovery as preparation for re-use (i.e. products or parts of products that that have become waste are prepared so that they can be reused without any other preprocessing), recycling (waste is reprocessed into products, materials or substances for the original or other purposes, not including energy recovery and reprocessing into materials to be used as fuel or backfill material), other recovery (waste serves a useful purpose by replacing materials used for a specific purpose incl. including energy recovery (use similar to fuel for electricity generation)) and, if neither is possible, disposal (Publication Office of the EU 2025, European Commission 2025). This applies to all waste not excluded from the scope of the Directive.

The waste producer is obliged to classify the waste according to the European Waste Catalogue (Commission Decision of 3 May 2000 (2000/532/EC) establishing a List of Waste (Commission Decision 2000) to the chapter, type and category indicated, and to manage the waste according to its actual characteristics. The Catalogue is structured according to a system based on the sector from which the waste originates (i.e., origin, e.g., oil processing waste) and the category of the waste (e.g., mineral oil). A “hazardous waste” is understood as a waste that displays one or more of the hazardous properties listed in Annex III of the EU Directive.

Hazardous properties of waste, as defined by the EU Waste Directive, are demonstrated through testing, specifically by comparing the concentrations of substances in the waste or the results of tests with the criteria and threshold values established for these properties. It is also worth mentioning that the effects that may appear as a consequence of exposure to ionizing radiation are covered by the hazardous properties of waste, namely HP7 (Carcinogenic), HP10 (Toxic for reproduction), and HP11 (Mutagenic), which are already considered, however, based on the classification of substances according to their chemical properties and their known biological effects. These classifications are aligned with the criteria set out in the CLP (classification, labelling and packaging of substances and mixtures) regulation (Directive 2008/1272), where radioactivity itself is not included (Art. 1(2a). Thus, while the presence of NOR in waste may not be directly addressed within existing waste management frameworks, it is indirectly considered due to similar effects that may occur. Nevertheless, risks from external irradiation remain important due to the physical properties of radionuclides, separate from their chemical characteristics.

NORM as radioactive waste

Any NORM produced during technological processes in the EU, if it cannot be reused, is considered waste and must be managed according to national regulations based on EU directives. The EU Directive on waste excludes radioactivity from its scope at all (Article 2(1 d). The EU Directive on waste from extractive industries is slightly more flexible after all because, according to Recital 10, it covers the management of waste from the extractive industries which may be radioactive, unless aspects specific to radioactivity are a matter dealt with under the Treaty establishing the European Atomic Energy Community (Euratom) (Directive 2006). However, this condition needs special consideration as below:

The Euratom treaty (Council of the UE 2016) deals with activities within the fields listed in Annex I to this Treaty, which covers sourcing and processing radioactive materials for their radioactive, fissile, or fertile properties, including concentration and storage of radioactive waste, physics and physical chemistry applied to reactors, equipment, application of radionuclides, economic aspects of energy production, and study of harmful effects of ionizing radiation. However, the treaty does not provide any definition of radioactive waste. It is implicitly assumed that waste resulting from all listed activities is considered radioactive waste. From this perspective, it is crucial to recognize that NOR appearing in processes not focused on the radioactive, fissile, or fertile properties of processed materials may be considered outside the scope of the treaty. This justifies the conclusion that the entire problem related to NORM in extractive industries, classified as waste, may be managed based on requirements set by the directive on waste from extractive industries.

The EU Directive on RAW does not apply to authorised releases into the environment. The typical NORM waste, however, is either released through authorized releases or is mining waste.

Article 4 of the EU Directive on RAW, as well as the definition of RAW given in this directive, refers to the existence (establishment) of a legal and regulatory framework in the Member State, which, taking into account the situation in the country, decides which waste becomes RAW and which can be released, as well as how RAW will be handled or disposed of. There is no uniform system of classification and management of RAW at the European level.

If the Member State chooses not to exclude NORM waste from the national RAW classification system, that NORM waste should be disposed of as RAW, including the requirements for permanent containment, i.e., must be disposed of in a RAW repository. Otherwise, from the analysis of existing regulations, it could be concluded that both the EU Directive on waste (Directive 2008/98/EC) and the EU Directive on waste from the extractive industry (Directive 2006/21/EC) can be applied towards NORM, unless it is classified as radioactive waste according to other regulations. However, neither the EU Directive on waste nor the EU Directive on waste from the extractive industries set any specific requirements concerning NOR activity concentration applied as criteria in the above discussed process of waste classification.

The subsequent fate of NORM classified as waste critically depends on deciding whether it is designated as radioactive waste (RAW). Such a decision has many consequences: legal and technical. An essential characteristic for RAW, as specified in the EU Directive on RAW (see Recital 21 of the preamble), is the fact that, due to its nature (radioactivity, presence of ionizing radiation), RAW requires containment and isolation from humans and the living environment in the long term to protect human health and the environment against dangers arising from ionising radiation, including disposal in appropriate facilities as a RAW repository. This requirement applies to all waste that has been declared RAW in accordance with the national/domestic RAW classification system.

Based on the above, it is evident that there is a functional system in place for the management of waste and waste from the extractive industry, alongside a parallel system governing radiation protection through the principles of exemption and clearance applied to practices and sources within those practices. In order to align these systems in cases involving NORM waste where the activity concentration of NORM exceeds exemption levels, existing waste legislation should be supplemented by additional restrictions arising from the presence of radioactivity. To do so, one must underline that the already mentioned existing system of waste management is capable of dealing with hazards related to carcinogenic, mutagenic, and human reproduction toxicity properties of waste. This means that already defined options for waste disposal and recovery (see Appendix 2), e.g., landfill - built, operated, closed - including post-closure monitoring in compliance with the Council Directive on the landfill of waste (Council Directive 1999), must provide all necessary measures protecting humans and the environment against the hazard related to these agents. What makes NORM waste different from regular waste, and why it requires extra controls, is its ability to cause harm from a distance through external radiation, mainly gamma, and in some cases, beta. To adapt an existing (hazardous waste) landfill for radioactive waste, it should be sufficient at most to add measures that address the risk of external radiation exposure.

Criteria for evaluation of protective measures planned to be implemented in the radiation protection field are expressed in the form of the effective dose and absorbed dose/dose rate for humans and for biota, respectively (ICRP 2007). Typical situations describing the handling and management of NORM waste are presented in the form of exposure scenarios aimed at both workers and the public. The output of these scenarios can be, for example, derived criteria, which, if met, imply that the monitored quantity, e.g., the effective dose of the worker, does not exceed a specified value. A classic representative of a summary publication is RP 122 Part II (European Commission 2002). Recently, two papers have been published that already work with the new constraint coefficients published by the ICRP; the first is focused on the use of sludge as fertilizer (Venoso et al. 2024a), the second is focused on NORM waste on conventional landfill (Venoso et al. 2024b). In both cases, options for recovery or disposal included in the waste directive are discussed in terms of the radioactivity either to prove or provide a necessary recommendation to fulfil radiation protection requirements.

A reasonable question is whether the system is complete or whether there are any other requirements that should be introduced into the waste management system to ensure that the protection of human health and the environment is sufficient, even when dealing with NORM waste that has been released from regulatory control in accordance with established clearance procedures under the EU BSS. The following topics were identified during the work of the RadoNorm project work package 5.5 as requiring further considerations and elaboration, in the case where the methods proposed in this article solution would be planned to be put into reality.

Hazardous properties of waste and chemical toxicity of natural radionuclides

The hazardous properties of waste are demonstrated through testing, i.e. by comparing the concentrations of substances in the waste or the results of waste tests against the criteria and limit values defined for those properties. In some cases, leaching tests are used, and waste is considered hazardous if it meets the criteria for a given class based on those results. For specific hazardous properties and substances, these tests follow prescribed procedures (e.g., for heavy metals); however, they do not include the determination of natural radionuclides.

Some natural radionuclides are chemically toxic, and waste containing them may exhibit hazardous properties such as HP 5 – Specific Target Organ Toxicity, HP 6 – Acute Toxicity, or HP 10 – Toxic for Reproduction. An example of a chemically toxic natural radionuclide is uranium, which may be present in high concentrations in some waste materials (e.g., filter media used for the removal of uranium from drinking water). Although the standard mandatory tests mentioned above do not include the determination of uranium, its concentration should be assessed in waste where elevated uranium levels can be expected. At the same time, it should be noted that, for example, none of the water treatment waste that could reasonably be expected to contain elevated uranium concentrations is classified as hazardous in the EU Waste Catalogue. A radionuclide feature indicating the need to take into account chemical toxicity is extremely low specific activity (or exceptionally long half-life). Therefore, besides uranium, among radionuclides constituting decay series, thorium isotopes Th-232 and Th-230 should be considered in terms of chemical toxicity. However, considering that thorium mobility in the environment is much less than uranium, the problem of its chemical toxicity is often neglected.

Inertness of waste and leachability

NORM waste is often of mineral origin. As such, it frequently has physical and mechanical properties that allow for its use as construction products, landfill engineering materials, or backfilling material. Without addressing the legal framework under which such uses occur, it is important to note that there is a requirement for the inertness of NORM waste used in this way. Before NORM waste can be recovered via these routes, its inertness must be verified. This is typically done through leaching tests, which—similarly to hazardous waste testing—do not assess the leachability of NOR. Leachability testing of NORM in construction materials is addressed in detail in the publication Naturally Occurring Radioactive Materials in Construction (Schroeyers 2017). It must also be stressed that for the recovery in construction products, especially when the intended use is for residential properties (i.e., building materials according to the EU BSS), more restricted requirements than exemption level concerning NOR activity concertation are required by the EU BSS.

Waste incineration

A sizeable portion of NORM waste consists of combustible materials. Given the current emphasis on increasing the energy recovery from waste, it is expected that there will be efforts to utilize combustible NORM waste with sufficient calorific value for energy generation, rather than disposing of it by landfilling, as is often the case today. Annex VII of Directive 2013/59/Euratom states that general clearance criteria may also be applied to incineration. Therefore, taking into account significant mass reduction after combustion, there will be a need to develop specific clearance levels for NORM waste subject to incineration, along with defined conditions under which such clearance levels can be applied. Waste ash produced by incineration is considered hazardous waste (regardless of the content of NOR). Therefore, such waste should be disposed of in a hazardous waste landfill.

Long-term landfilling of NORM waste

The disposal of NORM waste in appropriately classified landfills, based on the properties of the waste, is currently the most common and accessible method of NORM waste disposal. However, it is necessary to consider the potential risks associated with landfilling of waste containing natural radionuclides with long half-lives (e.g., U-238, Ra-226). Because the radioactive decay of such radionuclides is minimal over the typical landfill lifetime (on the order of 100 years), the associated radiological risks may persist even after the landfill has been closed and rehabilitated, considering current requirements. However, considering that the chemical and hazardous properties of waste also (usually) do not change over time, this problem is of a generic nature. The question then arises: What is the basis for current assumptions about the necessary lifetime of a disposal site?

Metal waste contaminated with scale containing natural radionuclides

Metal-bearing wastes may arise particularly from mining activities, water treatment, spa facilities, and during the chemical processing of mineral raw materials. It often involves pipes and dismantled parts of industrial equipment. The removal of radioactive scales from metal surfaces is technically challenging and costly. Metal recyclers have strict requirements regarding the radioactivity levels of secondary raw materials. As a result, contaminated metal scrap may not be accepted for processing, even though there is a general objective to maximize the reuse of metal waste.

Administrative requirements for the management of NORM waste (record keeping)

The EU Directive on waste includes a requirement to ensure the traceability of hazardous waste from its production to its final destination. A similar requirement may also be applied by Member States to non-hazardous waste. However, since radioactivity is not a property monitored under this directive, there is currently no guarantee that the information accompanying a waste stream will include data indicating that it is NORM waste, its activity levels, or the conditions under which it can be treated or disposed of, and in which facilities. The obligation to document such information and to retain relevant records should be established in legislation.

Summary and open questions

Extraction and further industrial processing of natural materials can lead to the concentration of natural radionuclides. If there is no further use for NORM, it becomes waste. Waste can have different properties at the same time, not only elevated concentrations of natural radionuclides, but also diverse types of hazardous properties, and all must be considered when deciding on its recovery or disposal. Waste must be considered holistically and the solution chosen for management or reuse must be appropriate for all its properties. For example, in the case of uranium, radioactivity may not be the most important consideration; instead, its chemical toxicity can be.

The results of surveys conducted in Poland, the Czech Republic, Finland, and France on known waste from NORM involving industries and facilities, as well as findings from a questionnaire distributed within the RadoNorm project, have shown that the management of such waste under the current European legislation is far from consistent across EU Member States. Significant differences, ambiguities, and weaknesses have been identified. It is therefore evident that the management of waste from NORM involving industries and facilities containing natural radionuclides (NORM waste) requires a more detailed analysis followed by the design of a phased and optimised system.

Parallel regulatory frameworks do exist: one addressing non-radiological hazards (e.g., the EU Waste Framework Directive, mining-specific directives, and health and safety at work regulations), the other dealing with radiological hazards (e.g., the EU Basic Safety Standards Directive, Directive on Radioactive Waste). Existing systems for ordinary waste management in the EU allow for the management of all types of useless NORM, regardless of radionuclide activity concentrations. From the point of view of radiation protection, the possibility to release waste from the site is determined using clearance levels of 1 Bq/g for natural radionuclides from U-238 and Th-232 series and 10 Bq/g for K-40. If the clearance levels are exceeded, the possibility of authorised release complying with the general clearance criteria of the order of 1 mSv/year should be assessed. The last option is that such waste may become radioactive waste. The EU Directive on radioactive waste deals with safe management of radioactive waste and one of its requirements is that radioactive waste must be disposed of for the protection of human health and the protection of the environment. The Directive also does not apply to waste from the extractive industry, even if it is radioactive. Such types of waste fall under the scope of the EU Directive on waste from the extractive industry. The EU Directive on waste explicitly excludes radioactive waste from its scope.

To avoid the problem of ambiguous interpretation of existing EU directives (directives on waste, on radioactive waste, and on waste from extractive industry), the introduction of a specific waste group that would include all waste generated by non-nuclear industries that may contain a non-negligible concentration of naturally occurring radionuclides – NORM waste - is recommended. This pragmatic approach lets one point out waste that first should not be classified as radioactive waste, and second, while treated according to the directive on waste or on waste from extractive industries, additional restrictions caused by NOR present must be considered.

Using an already well-established waste management system - built on clearly defined terms and processes for industrial waste- automatically ensures alignment with a wide range of other regulations, such as those related to environmental protection, occupational health and safety (OHS), hazardous substances, and labour law. Activities related to radioactivity can be integrated into existing systems for monitoring, reporting, OHS, prevention, mitigation, and communication, often benefiting from the synergies between these areas. Finally, incorporating NORM into the existing waste management framework also helps to address potential interactions with other pollutants or stressors.

In contrast, the absence of, or insufficient or ambiguous, legal regulations worsens the situation and increases the risk of unauthorised handling of NORM waste. As an example, the radiological risks associated with the controlled reuse of NORM waste in the production of building materials are generally low and manageable, whereas the psychological and societal impact of labelling buildings as containing contaminated materials may be significant. Experience has shown that greater challenges arise in situations where such materials enter the market without information on radionuclide content, and this fact is discovered many years later and often, only incidentally.

A fundamental challenge is how to effectively combine these parallel systems, one addressing non-radiological hazards and the other dealing with radiological hazards, so that both can be jointly and consistently applied in practice. The issue of natural radioactivity is relatively new to many industries currently accounted as involving NORM. Given that:

• NORM-involving industries, in many cases, have existed for decades and materials, residues, or waste currently referred to as NORM did not appear suddenly and in the overwhelming majority of cases, have existed since a relevant industry started

• NORM waste differs from radioactive waste in the vast majority of cases, primarily in terms of their volume (medium to large), the predominant presence of naturally occurring radionuclides, and, in most cases, their low or moderate radionuclide activity concentrations

• All waste has been managed in a well-defined manner, controlled according to the requirements set by EU regulations concerning waste or, in specific cases, mining waste for decades.

• The presence of radionuclides is one of the waste properties that has only recently been explicitly recognised within the radiation protection framework, for which specific evaluation criteria have been established, and it often does not constitute the dominant stressor. The problem of radioactivity of NORM should rather be included in existing practices than considered separately.
