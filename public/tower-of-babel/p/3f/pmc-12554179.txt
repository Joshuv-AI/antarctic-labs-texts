PEA15 promotes osteosarcoma progression and cisplatin resistance by modulating autophagy through the FABP3-TNF signaling axis
Authors: Yuanxiang Peng, Feng Cai, Lang Liu, Chong Guo, Guohua Zhang, Feng Shen, Xiaofeng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryOsteosarcoma (OS), a primary malignant bone tumor, is characterized by resistance to chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin (DDP), posing a major obstacle to effective treatment. Tumor cells often exploit autophagy to survive chemotherapeutic stress, which contributes to this resistance. Using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), this study screened for autophagy-related genes associated with OS prognosis and identified PEA15 as a key indicator of poor outcomes. Through gene knockdown and overexpression experiments in OS cell lines and xenograft models, we found that PEA15 promotes tumor progression. Mechanistically, RNA sequencing revealed that PEA15 inhibits autophagy and apoptosis by modulating the downstream target FABP3 and the associated TNF signaling pathway. Notably, silencing PEA15 in resistant OS cells enhanced their sensitivity to cisplatin by activating autophagy. These findings identify the PEA15-FABP3-TNF signaling axis as a key pathway regulating chemoresistance in OS, suggesting that targeting PEA15 could be a promising therapeutic strategy to improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the commonest primary malignant bone tumor, predominantly affecting adolescents and young adults, with a characteristic bimodal age distribution pattern.1,2 It accounts for about 20% of all primary bone tumors, with an incidence rate of 3–5 cases per million people annually.3 OS typically arises in the metaphyseal regions of long bones, such as the distal femur, proximal tibia, and proximal humerus.4 Despite advances in treatment that have improved the survival rate to 60–70% for patients with non-metastatic OS through surgical resection combined with multi-drug chemotherapy, the 5-year survival rate for those with recurrent or metastatic OS remains less than 20%.5 Chemoresistance continues to be a significant obstacle to effective treatment, with cisplatin (DDP) resistance being particularly prevalent, often leading to treatment failure. As chemoresistance severely limits the efficacy of current therapies, identifying novel therapeutic targets is essential for developing more effective strategies to overcome resistance and improve clinical outcomes in patients with OS.6,7

Autophagy, a cellular degradation and recycling process, plays a pivotal but dual role in cancer.8 In osteosarcoma, tumor cells often exploit autophagy as a pro-survival mechanism to withstand chemotherapy-induced stress, leading to enhanced resistance against cornerstone drugs such as cisplatin.9,10,11 Although this phenomenon is well-documented, the specific molecular regulators that orchestrate this protective autophagy in OS remain largely unknown, representing a critical barrier to overcoming chemoresistance.12

Herein, we conducted weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to identify autophagy-related genes associated with OS prognosis. This unbiased screening highlighted PEA15 as a gene critically linked with poor patient prognosis. PEA15 (phosphoprotein enriched in astrocytes 15 kDa) is an influential death-effector domain (DED)-containing phosphoprotein. It functions as a crucial signaling scaffold, integrating multiple pathways that govern cell fate.13 Its anti-apoptotic action is well-established; for example, phosphorylation by Akt stabilizes PEA15, enhancing its protective effects, while its engagement with the ERK/MAPK pathway shields cells from stress-induced apoptosis.14,15 In oncology, PEA15 overexpression is frequently associated with tumor progression and chemoresistance in various cancers, including colorectal and hepatocellular carcinoma.16,17 More directly relevant to our focus, recent cutting-edge research has revealed that PEA15 is also a key modulator of autophagy. In glioma cells, PEA15 can activate autophagy through the JNK pathway, thereby promoting resistance to cellular stressors.18 This established nexus between PEA15, cell survival pathways, and autophagy regulation in other cancers provided a strong rationale for its investigation in OS. However, to date, no studies have reported on the function and regulatory mechanisms of PEA15 in OS.

This study aims to address this gap by systematically exploring the role and regulatory mechanism of PEA15 in OS via a combination of in vitro and in vivo experiments. We utilize various experimental approaches, including gene silencing and overexpression, immunofluorescence (IF), western blotting, and RNA sequencing, to comprehensively investigate the functions and regulatory mechanisms of PEA15 in OS. Our research aims to uncover potential biomarkers for OS prognosis and propose new therapeutic strategies to overcome DDP resistance, ultimately improving treatment outcomes and prognosis for patients with OS.

Results

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis clinical significance modules and autophagy-related prognostic model

We performed WGCNA on 88 OS samples and 396 adjacent cancer tissue samples. Using a soft threshold power of 3, we successfully constructed a co-expression network (Figures 1A and 1B). Hierarchical clustering analysis revealed several co-expression modules, with the clustering results displayed in Figure 1C. Further analysis identified two modules, the blue module (correlation coefficient = 0.53) and the turquoise module (correlation coefficient = 0.36), that were linked to tumorigenesis (p < 0.05) (Figure 1D). To identify autophagy-related genes in OS, we first generated a heatmap of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The genes in the blue and turquoise modules were then intersected with the DEGs to obtain a merged gene set. These genes were subsequently compared to an autophagy-related gene database, resulting in the selection of six genes significantly correlated with autophagy (Figures 1E–1H). Using LASSO regression analysis, we screened out three key genes with the smallest lambda values (Figures 1I and 1J). A risk prediction model was created based on the following formula: 0.575PEA15 + 0.209SAR1A - 0.313MYC. Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups in light of the median risk score. Elevated PEA15 expression correlates with poor prognosis and is an independent prognostic indicator

To assess the individual contribution of the three model genes, we evaluated their impact on patient survival. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that high PEA15 expression was significantly associated with poorer patient survival (p < 0.05), whereas the other two genes showed weaker correlations (Figure 2A; Figures S1A and S2B). Consistent with this, patients in the high-risk group, as defined by the model, exhibited significantly shorter survival times (Figure 2B). Both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that the risk score was an independent prognostic factor for patients with OS (Figures 2C and 2D). To validate the role of PEA15 in OS cells, we first confirmed its elevated expression in multiple OS cell lines compared to the normal osteoblast cell line hFOB1.19. Subsequent knockdown and overexpression experiments, validated at both the mRNA (Figures 2E–2G) and protein levels (Figures 2N and 2O), demonstrated that PEA15 significantly promoted tumor cell proliferation (Figures 2H and 2I). PEA15 facilitates tumor cell proliferation and metastasis

We next investigated the role of PEA15 in OS cell migration and invasion. Wound healing assays revealed that PEA15 knockdown significantly inhibited cell migration, while its overexpression accelerated it, with the most pronounced effects observed at 48 h (Figures 3A and 3B). Similarly, colony formation assays confirmed that PEA15 knockdown suppressed, while its overexpression enhanced, the clonogenic ability of OS cells, reinforcing its role in proliferation (Figures 3C and 3D). Transwell assays further demonstrated that PEA15 knockdown suppressed both migration and invasion, whereas its overexpression promoted these processes (Figures 3E and 3H). These functional changes were statistically significant (Figures 3F and 3I). PEA15 modulates autophagy and apoptosis in tumor cells

To dissect the underlying mechanism of PEA15-driven tumorigenesis, we examined its impact on autophagy and apoptosis. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that PEA15 knockdown significantly increased the formation of autophagic vesicles, whereas its overexpression had the opposite effect (Figures 4A and 4B). This was corroborated by Western blot analysis, which showed that PEA15 silencing upregulated the autophagy markers LC3B-II and ATG5, while PEA15 overexpression downregulated them (Figures 4E and 4F). These results indicate that PEA15 acts as a negative regulator of autophagy in OS cells. In parallel, flow cytometry analysis showed that PEA15 knockdown promoted apoptosis, while its overexpression inhibited it (Figures 4C and 4D). This was confirmed at the molecular level, where PEA15 knockdown increased the levels of pro-apoptotic proteins Cleaved Caspase-3 and Cleaved-PARP, and overexpression decreased them (Figures 4G and 4H). PEA15 mediates autophagy regulation in osteosarcoma progression through FABP3

To identify the downstream effectors through which PEA15 regulates OS progression, we performed RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) on OS cells following PEA15 knockdown. FABP3 was identified as one of the most significantly downregulated genes (Figure 5A; Figure S1C) and was selected for further investigation. Knockdown of FABP3 in MG63 cells (Figure S1D) phenocopied the effects of PEA15 overexpression, leading to reduced cell proliferation (Figure 5B), colony formation (Figure 5C), migration, and invasion (Figure 5D). This was accompanied by corresponding changes in the expression of key metastasis-related proteins (Figure 5E). Mechanistically, FABP3 knockdown promoted autophagy, as indicated by increased LC3B-II and ATG5 levels (Figures 5F and 5G), and induced apoptosis (Figures 5K–5L). Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis of the RNA-seq data implicated the TNF signaling pathway in PEA15-mediated effects (Figure 5M). Taken together, these results suggest that PEA15 promotes OS progression by downregulating FABP3, which in turn modulates autophagy, apoptosis, and the TNF signaling pathway. To elucidate the regulatory mechanism of PEA15 on FABP3, co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed no detectable binding between PEA15 and FABP3-specific antibody-conjugated agarose beads (Figure S1F), indicating the absence of direct interaction. Subsequent rescue experiments demonstrated that FABP3 overexpression in PEA15-knockdown cells reversed the upregulation of ATG5 and LC3B proteins induced by PEA15 depletion. Knockdown of PEA15 enhances sensitivity to DDP resistance in osteosarcoma

Cisplatin (DDP) is a first-line chemotherapeutic agent for OS, but acquired resistance limits its clinical efficacy. We hypothesized that targeting PEA15 could sensitize DDP-resistant OS cells. To test this, we used RNA interference to knock down PEA15 in a DDP-resistant U2OS cell line (U2OS/DDP-R). Colony formation assays demonstrated that combining DDP treatment with PEA15 knockdown (DDP/si-PEA15) synergistically inhibited the proliferation of resistant cells compared to either treatment alone (Figure 6A; Figure S1E). Similarly, the combined treatment group showed significantly reduced migration and invasion capabilities (Figure 6B), which was associated with the altered expression of metastasis-related proteins (Figure 6D). Mechanistically, immunofluorescence assays revealed a significant increase in autophagosome formation in the DDP/si-PEA15 group (Figure 6C). This was confirmed by Western blotting, which showed upregulated LC3B-II and ATG5 levels (Figure 6E), suggesting that PEA15 knockdown enhances DDP’s cytotoxic effects by inducing autophagy. Consistent with our earlier pathway analysis, we observed a significant increase in TNF-α expression in the combination treatment group (Figure 6F). The pharmacological inhibition of TNF-α with the antagonist R-7050 downregulated ATG5 and LC3B protein expression and suppressed autophagy, demonstrating that TNF signaling activation plays a pivotal role in inducing autophagic activity (Figure 6G). PEA15 knockout enhances sensitivity to chemotherapy in resistant osteosarcoma in vivo

To confirm these findings in vivo, we established a xenograft mouse model using U2OS/DDP-R cells stably expressing a short hairpin RNA targeting PEA15 (sh-PEA15). Mice treated with both DDP and PEA15 knockout (DDP/sh-PEA15) exhibited significantly reduced tumor volumes and weights compared to all other groups, demonstrating that PEA15 depletion enhances DDP’s anti-tumor activity in vivo (Figures 7B and 7C). Pathological analysis via H&E staining revealed that tumors from the DDP/sh-PEA15 group had a more scattered cellular arrangement and increased nuclear fragmentation, indicative of enhanced apoptosis (Figure 7D). Immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections supported these findings, showing a marked decrease in the proliferation marker Ki67 and a significant increase in the autophagy marker LC3B in the combination treatment group (Figures 7E and 7F). Collectively, our in vivo experiments confirm that PEA15 knockout sensitizes resistant OS to DDP by inhibiting proliferation and inducing autophagy. Discussion

OS is the most common primary malignant bone tumor, predominantly affecting adolescents and young adults. It is characterized by its high malignancy, rapid progression, and poor prognosis.19 Despite significant advances in multimodal therapies, including chemotherapy and surgery, the long-term survival rate for patients with OS remains suboptimal due to high recurrence rates and resistance to DDP. In this study, we developed an autophagy-related prognostic model through WGCNA and the identification of autophagy-related genes. We further explored the role and underlying molecular mechanisms of PEA15 in OS. Our findings unveil that PEA15 is crucial in promoting OS progression and DDP resistance by modulating autophagy and apoptosis. These results unfold the potential of PEA15 as a therapeutic target for improving OS treatment outcomes and overcoming chemoresistance.

Building on this model, this study highlights the critical role of PEA15 in OS progression. Through WGCNA, we identified blue and turquoise co-expression modules that were significantly associated with tumors. By combining these findings with LASSO regression analysis, we constructed an autophagy-related prognostic model based on three key genes: PEA15, SAR1A, and MYC. In this model, PEA15 was strongly correlated with poor patient prognosis and was selected as the core gene for further functional validation. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the high expression of PEA15 in OS cells distinctly facilitated cell proliferative, migrative, and invasive potentials. Conversely, silencing PEA15 inhibited these biological processes, suggesting that PEA15 may function as an oncogene in tumor cells. In an in vivo xenograft model, knockout of PEA15 significantly repressed tumor growth, further supporting its oncogenic role in OS progression. To elucidate the mechanisms behind these observations, our mechanistic studies revealed that PEA15 modulated tumor cell survival and metastasis through the TNF pathway, further emphasizing its crucial role in tumor progression. While our study focuses on the PEA15-autophagy axis, it is important to acknowledge that chemoresistance in OS is a multifaceted problem. The role of autophagy itself remains controversial, with evidence supporting both pro-survival and pro-death functions depending on the context.12,20 Beyond autophagy, other complex molecular mechanisms are critical drivers of OS progression and drug resistance. For instance, recent studies have increasingly highlighted the role of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs); for example, the m6A-related lncRNA LINC00941 has been shown to drive chemoresistance by regulating the METTL3/AXL/NF-κB axis,21 while circCHST15 has been found to promote OS progression through the metabolic reprogramming of glycolysis.22 These findings underscore the complexity of OS biology and situate our discovery of the PEA15/FABP3/TNF pathway as an important piece of this intricate regulatory network.

Notably, while PEA15 is an autophagy-related gene known to be critical in diverse cancers, its function in OS reveals a unique mechanism. For example, Bartholomeusz et al. identified PEA15 as a significant prognostic factor in ovarian cancer, where it promotes autophagy via the ERK1/2 pathway, contributing to poor patient prognosis.23 Additionally, PEA15 is a potential prognostic marker in colon and gastric cancers.24,25 A key finding of our study, however, is the identification of a PEA15 regulatory axis in OS that is distinct from the JNK and ERK pathways reported in other cancers. Our study provides evidence that PEA15 drives OS progression and chemoresistance by activating the TNF signaling pathway via FABP3, a downstream mediator not previously linked to PEA15’s function. The identification of this PEA15-FABP3-TNF axis clarifies a specific molecular mechanism in osteosarcoma and presents a potential therapeutic target. These findings support the prognostic value of PEA15 in multiple cancers, yet its specific role in osteosarcoma chemoresistance remained under-explored. It is well-established that autophagy is one of the key cellular mechanisms contributing to chemotherapy resistance in osteosarcoma, yet studies specifically examining the role of PEA15 in this process are limited. This study addresses this gap by uncovering the critical role of PEA15 in autophagy regulation in OS cells. Our results indicate that PEA15 negatively modulates autophagy in OS. IF and Western blot analyses demonstrated that silencing PEA15 elevated LC3B and ATG5 expression, suggesting that PEA15 may promote OS cell survival and proliferation by repressing autophagy. To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which PEA15 modulates autophagy and metastasis in OS cells, we identified FABP3 as a downstream target through RNA sequencing analysis.

FABP3, a key modulator of lipid metabolism and cell signaling, has recently attracted attention for its role in various cancers.26,27,28 In our study, we observed that silencing PEA15 significantly diminished FABP3 expression, and high levels of FABP3 in OS were closely linked to tumor proliferation and metastasis. Functional assays showed that silencing FABP3 markedly inhibited OS cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, accompanied by the significant upregulation of autophagy markers LC3B and ATG5. Additionally, silencing FABP3 also significantly promoted cell apoptosis, suggesting its critical role in modulating PEA15-mediated autophagy and apoptosis. This finding provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the role of PEA15 in OS progression. The involvement of specific signaling pathways is often crucial in tumorigenesis, and among these, the TNF pathway has garnered considerable interest for its role in a range of physiological and pathological processes. Several studies have highlighted the significance of the TNF pathway in OS progression.29,30 For instance, Liu et al. demonstrated that Ampelopsin inhibited the migration and invasion of U2OS OS cells induced by TNF-α.31 Consistent with this existing literature, our KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the TNF pathway might be an important mediator of PEA15’s function. This finding not only supports prior research but also suggests a potential direction for further investigation into the mechanisms of action of PEA15.

From a clinical standpoint, the most critical finding of this study is the identification of PEA15’s role in driving cisplatin (DDP) resistance in osteosarcoma. We demonstrated that silencing PEA15 robustly enhanced the sensitivity of resistant OS cells both in vitro and in our in vivo xenograft model, offering compelling evidence for new therapeutic strategies. Translating this finding is challenging, as PEA15 is not easily “druggable” with small molecules due to its scaffold protein nature. This positions RNA-based gene silencing as a more promising approach. The primary barrier to this strategy is effective, targeted delivery.32 While nanoparticles are a viable option, engineered exosomes represent a more sophisticated strategy. By leveraging the natural role of exosomes in intercellular communication—a process that can also transfer drug resistance7,33,34 we can engineer these vesicles to deliver therapeutic siRNAs specifically to OS cells.35 Such a system promises to maximize efficacy while mitigating off-target effects. Furthermore, our study unveiled the potential significance of the TNF pathway in the PEA15-mediated resistance mechanism. In the PEA15 silencing and DDP combination treatment group, TNF-α expression was evidently upregulated, suggesting the significance of the TNF pathway in enhancing treatment efficacy by targeting PEA15. This discovery not only deepens our understanding of the DDP resistance mechanism but also offers a potential molecular target for developing effective therapeutic strategies. Despite these promising findings, this study has some limitations that should be taken into consideration and addressed in future research. First, although we identified the PEA15-FABP3-TNF signaling axis as an important regulator in OS progression, the upstream regulatory mechanisms of this axis and its interactions with other signaling pathways require further investigation. Second, due to the lack of specific inhibitors targeting PEA15, our study primarily relied on gene knockdown and knockout strategies, which have limitations for clinical translation. Therefore, the development of small molecule inhibitors targeting PEA15 will be an important direction for future studies. Finally, while we validated the effects of PEA15-targeted therapy in a xenograft model, further validation in more clinically relevant models, such as orthotopic implantation or transgenic mouse models, is essential.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of the critical role of PEA15 in OS progression, uncovering its molecular mechanisms in modulating autophagy and apoptosis through the FABP3 and TNF pathways. Furthermore, it highlights the potential of PEA15 as a therapeutic target to overcome DDP resistance. These findings not only advance our understanding of the pathogenesis of OS but also establish a robust theoretical and experimental basis for the development of innovative treatment strategies. Future research should concentrate on further elucidating the specific molecular mechanisms of PEA15, designing targeted therapies, and validating their therapeutic effectiveness in more clinically relevant models to facilitate the translation of these findings into clinical applications.

Limitations of the study

Despite these promising findings, this study has some limitations that should be taken into consideration and addressed in future research. First, although we identified the PEA15-FABP3-TNF signaling axis as an important regulator in OS progression, the upstream regulatory mechanisms of this axis and its interactions with other signaling pathways require further investigation. Second, due to the lack of specific inhibitors targeting PEA15, our study primarily relied on gene knockdown and knockout strategies, which have limitations for clinical translation. Therefore, the development of small molecule inhibitors targeting PEA15 will be an important direction for future studies. Finally, while we validated the effects of PEA15-targeted therapy in a xenograft model, further validation in more clinically relevant models, such as orthotopic implantation or transgenic mouse models, is essential.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

The human osteoblastic cell line hFOB1.19 and human osteosarcoma (OS) cell lines (U2OS, MG63, SAOS2, 143B) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The 293T cell line was used for lentiviral packaging. hFOB1.19 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/F-12 (DMEM/F12) (1:1) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 2 mM L-glutamine. OS cell lines were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. All cell lines were maintained in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2; OS cells were kept at 37°C, while hFOB1.19 cells were kept at 34°C. All lines were passaged every 2–3 days using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA.

Ethical approval

All animal care and experiments conducted in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanchang University (NCULAE-20241029001) and were performed in accordance with all relevant regulatory standards. The study was reported according to the ARRIVE guidelines. The transcriptome data used for bioinformatic analysis were obtained from publicly accessible databases (TARGET, GTEx, and GEO), for which no supplementary ethical clearance was required.

Animal subjects

in vivo xenograft tumor model was established using female BALB/c nude mice, aged 4–6 weeks at the start of the experiment. As only female mice were used, sex was not a variable in the study, and its influence on the results was not assessed.

Method details

Sample data acquisition

RNA sequencing data from OS samples (n = 88) and adjacent non-cancerous tissues (n = 396) were retrieved from the TCGA and GTEx databases. The datasets were carefully integrated and underwent logarithmic transformation using log2(x + 1) to stabilize variance and normalize the distribution of the data.

Construction of WGCNA

A weighted gene co-expression network was constructed by virtue of the R package WGCNA (v1.70-3). The analysis began with sample clustering to detect and eliminate outliers. Pearson correlation coefficients between genes were computed, and the optimal soft threshold (β = 3) was determined by virtue of the pickSoftThreshold function to ensure network stability. An adjacency matrix was constructed, and the topological overlap matrix (TOM) was computed. Modules were identified by virtue of the dynamic tree cutting algorithm (dynamicTreeCut), with a minimum module size of 30.36,37

Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment

Autophagy-related genes are sourced from the GeneCards website (https://www.genecards.org/). Differential gene expression analysis was implemented utilizing the limma-voom pipeline, and adjusted p-values were computed with the empirical Bayes method. A significance threshold of |log2FC| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 was applied. DEGs were subjected to hierarchical clustering and visualized using the pheatmap package. DEGs intersected with key module genes from the WGCNA was determined. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (including biological processes [BP], molecular functions [MF], and cellular components [CC]) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed employing the clusterProfiler (v4.0.5) package.

Prognostic model construction and validation

LASSO regression analysis was made employing the “glmnet” package. The optimal λ value (λ.min) was selected, and genes with non-zero coefficients were identified as prognostic features. A risk score model was then constructed in the light of the expression levels of these selected genes.Riskscore=∑i=1nCoefi×xi

Patients were categorized into the high-risk and low-risk groups in the light of the median risk score. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was made employing the “survival” package. ROC curves were created utilizing the “timeROC” package to study the predictive performance of the model. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were implemented to further validate the independent prognostic value of the model.

Gene knockdown and overexpression

Three pairs of siRNA sequences targeting PEA15 and FABP3 were prepared and synthesized by GenePharma (Table S1). Prior to transfection, cells were seeded in 6-well plates (3×105 cells per well) and transfected by virtue of RNAiMAX transfection reagent. The final siRNA concentration was 50 nM, and the medium was replaced with fresh complete culture medium 12 h after transfection. To construct the PEA15 overexpression model, the CDS sequence of PEA15 was cloned into the multiple cloning site (MCS) of the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro vector. After sequencing verification, the plasmid was co-transfected with the packaging plasmid into 293T cells. The viral supernatant was harvested 48 h post-transfection. Polybrene (8 μg/mL) was supplemented to enhance cell infection, and stable cell lines were selected by virtue of puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 48 h. For HA-FABP3 overexpression, the CDS of human FABP3 with an N-terminal HA-tag was cloned into an expression plasmid. Cells were transfected with this plasmid using a suitable transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were harvested for subsequent experiments 24–48 h post-transfection.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA extraction was done employing TRIzol reagent, and RNA concentration was measured by virtue of a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. One microgram of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix. Real-time quantitative PCR was made on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system by virtue of a 20 μL reaction mixture consisting of 10 μL SYBR Premix Ex Taq II, 0.4 μL of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), and 2 μL cDNA. The PCR amplification conditions were as follows: 95°C for 30 s; 95°C for 5 s, followed by 60°C for 30 s, repeated for 40 cycles. Each sample was measured in triplicate, with GAPDH confirming as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression was calculated by virtue of the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Cells were washed twice with cold PBS and lysed on ice for 30 min in RIPA buffer enriched with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined employing the BCA assay. After adding 5× loading buffer, the samples were denatured by heating at 95°C for 10 min and separated by virtue of SDS-PAGE (10–12% polyacrylamide gel). Proteins were then transferred to a PVDF membrane at 300 mA. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, followed by incubation overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting PEA15 (CST, #13091,1:1000), LC3B (CST, #3868,1:1000), ATG5 (CST,#9980,1:1000),Cleaved-Caspase3 (CST,#9664,1:1000),Cleaved-PARP (CST, #9541,1:1000), FABP3 (CST,#14780,1:1000), TNF-α (CST,#3707,1:1000), and GAPDH (CST, #5174,1:1000). After washing with TBST, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST, #7074, 1:5000) were applied at room temperature for 1 h. Protein signals were detected utilizing enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), and densitometry analysis was implemented with the help of ImageJ software.

Cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis analysis

For cell proliferation assays, 2 × 103 U2OS/DDP-R cells were seeded into 96-well plates containing 200 μL complete medium. CCK-8 reagent (10 μL) was added at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, and absorbance at 450 nm was documented to assess cell proliferation. For colony formation assays, 500 cells were placed in 6-well plates and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with crystal violet for 30 min, and counted under a microscope. Migration assays were conducted using scratch assays; cells were grown to 80–85% confluence, scratched with a 200 μL pipette tip, and photographed at 0 and 48 h by virtue of an inverted microscope. Invasion assays were performed in Transwell chambers, followed by fixation with methanol for 20 min and staining with crystal violet for 30 min. The number of cells that invaded through the membrane was quantified under a microscope. Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining kit was adopted for apoptosis assessment, and apoptosis rates were analyzed by virtue of flow cytometry employing FlowJo software.

If analysis

For IF, 2 × 104 U2OS/DDP-R cells were seeded onto laser confocal dishes and cultured for 24 h. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and then permeabilized employing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting LC3B (CST, #3168, 1:200) and Ki67 (CST, #9449, 1:100). Following incubation, cells were stained with Alexa Fluor 488- or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. The slides were mounted with Prolong Gold anti-fade reagent. Images were acquired by virtue of a Zeiss laser confocal microscope, and the number of autophagosomes was quantified using ZEN software. Ten random fields were selected per group for quantitative analysis.

Establishment of drug-resistant cell lines

A drug-resistant U2OS/DDP-R cell line was established using a stepwise drug selection method. DDP was initially applied at 1/10th of its IC50 concentration, and the concentration was gradually increased by a factor of 1.2–1.5 every two weeks until the cells could stably grow in 5 μg/mL DDP. The IC50 value of the resistant cells was determined using the CCK-8 assay, and the resistance index (RI) was calculated: RI = IC50 of resistant cells/IC50 of parental cells. Cells were maintained in 2 μg/mL DDP, and drug resistance was reassessed every 3 months.

Histopathology and molecular analysis

At the study endpoint (4 weeks of treatment), mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were excised, weighed, and collected. A portion of the tumor tissues was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was implemented to study histopathological changes. For IF staining, antigen retrieval was conducted, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies against Ki67 (CST, #9449, 1:200) and LC3B (CST, #3868, 1:100). Secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature, followed by nuclear staining with DAPI. Apoptotic cells were detected using the TUNEL assay, with DAB staining for color development and counterstaining with hematoxylin. Quantification was performed by randomly selecting five high-power fields (400× magnification) per slide. The remaining tumor tissue was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent protein and RNA extraction.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For Co-IP assays, cells were harvested and lysed on ice for 30 min in cold IP lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40) supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The cell lysates were then centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected. A small fraction of the supernatant was reserved as the “input” control.

The remaining lysates were pre-cleared with Protein A/G magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0202) for 1 h. Subsequently, the pre-cleared lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against PEA15 (Abcam, ab277626), FABP3 (CST, # 13368S), or with a control Normal Rabbit IgG (CST, #2729). The following day, fresh Protein A/G magnetic beads were added and incubated for 4 h at 4°C to capture the immunocomplexes. The beads were then washed five times with cold IP lysis buffer. Finally, the bound proteins were eluted from the beads by boiling in 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer for 10 min. The eluted samples and input lysates were then analyzed by Western blotting as described above.

Xenograft tumor model construction

Female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were purchased and acclimated for 1 week before being randomized into four groups (n = 6): control, DDP, sh-PEA15, and combined treatment. U2OS/DDP-R cells (1×107 in 100 μL PBS) were subcutaneously injected into the right axilla of the mice. Treatment began when the tumor volume reached 50 mm3. The DDP group received intraperitoneal DDP (5 mg/kg, twice weekly), the sh-PEA15 group was treated with intravenous lentivirus (1×108 TU/mouse, once weekly), and the combined treatment group received both therapies. Tumor volume was measured every 7 days by virtue of the formula V = (length × width2)/2. Body weight and survival status of the mice were monitored throughout the study.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Unless otherwise stated, all in vitro experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t test. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log rank test. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. In figures, significance levels are denoted as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001. ‘n.s.’ indicates a non-significant result.
