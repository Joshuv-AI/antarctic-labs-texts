Are we reporting well enough? A systematic survey of missing data in patient-reported outcomes from non-small cell lung cancer randomized trials
Authors: Jinyi Ma, Wenbo He, Fangyuan Jing, Meixuan Li, Zhengchi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMissing patient-reported outcome (PRO) data represent a frequent and significant challenge in clinical trials. Inadequate handling or reporting of such data may introduce bias, undermine the validity of findings, and limit the interpretability of treatment effects, particularly in oncology research where patient experience is a key outcome.PurposeThis systematic survey evaluated how missing PRO data were handled in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov for NSCLC RCTs reporting PROs (up to June 6, 2024). Two independent reviewers assessed study eligibility and extracted predefined data on the extent of missing PROs and the statistical methods used to address them. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses were performed.ResultsOf 13,019 records screened, 252 RCTs met the inclusion criteria. Most trials (70.2%) reported missing PRO data; however, only 45.2% specified statistical handling methods, most commonly complete case analysis, mixed-effects models, or mixed-effects model for repeated measures. Among 52 trials with PROs as primary outcomes, only 12 reported sensitivity analyses. Trials with larger sample sizes or those with PROs as primary endpoints were significantly more likely to report missing data. Clinical events (e.g., death or disease progression) were the predominant reasons for missingness, while procedural or methodological causes were less frequently reported.ConclusionThe reporting and statistical management of missing PRO data in NSCLC RCTs remain suboptimal. Most trials relied on conventional methods with limited use of advanced techniques or sensitivity analyses, raising concerns about bias and interpretability. Future trials should prioritize prespecified strategies, robust statistical modeling, and transparent reporting to enhance the validity and clinical relevance of PRO evidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02503-1.

Background

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs) are defined as information directly provided by patients regarding their health status, symptom burden, functional capacity, and quality of life, without interpretation by healthcare professionals [1]. Over the past decade, PROs have gained growing recognition from regulatory agencies, clinicians, and patients as an essential tool for capturing patient-centered data [2–4]. Evidence shows that PROs not only enhance patient satisfaction and improve symptom identification but also reduce healthcare utilization and may even improve survival [5–7]. By revealing patients’ subjective experiences and unmet needs, PROs further promote health equity and provide a unique lens through which to assess treatment tolerability and long-term effects [2, 8, 9].

In oncology, the value of PROs is particularly pronounced. Cancer patients frequently undergo complex treatment regimens and experience substantial symptom burdens, making systematic assessment of patient experiences and quality of life essential for both research and clinical practice. Lung cancer, one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality [10]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases [11]. Its management involves a wide range of interventions—from screening and minimally invasive procedures to radiotherapy (including stereotactic ablative radiotherapy), targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [12]. These modalities can lead to marked fluctuations in symptoms and quality of life throughout the disease trajectory [13]. Therefore, integrating PROs into randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of NSCLC is increasingly regarded as a key strategy to enhance clinical relevance. Reflecting this trend, a growing number of NSCLC RCTs have reported PRO data in recent years [14, 15].

Despite progress in oncology trials, substantial challenges remain in the analysis and reporting of PRO data, particularly regarding missing data [8, 16–19]. Standardized methodological guidance is limited, and transparency about the extent, mechanisms, and handling of missing PRO data is often insufficient. Missing data are typically classified as missing completely at random (MCAR), missing at random (MAR), or missing not at random (MNAR) [20], with MNAR being especially prevalent in NSCLC trials, where dropout or clinical deterioration is often related to unobserved PRO values. A subset of MNAR, known as informative missingness or non-random information loss (I-MNAR), arises when missingness has logical, non-random causes [21, 22] and is common in NSCLC studies. Our prior retrospective review confirmed that essential information on missing PRO data is frequently omitted [23]. Inadequate handling of missing PRO data appropriately can introduce statistical bias, distort treatment effect estimates, and ultimately compromise the credibility and generalizability of trial findings [24, 25].

In this context, the present study conducted a cross-sectional methodological review of RCTs involving patients with NSCLC to systematically evaluate PRO reporting practices, with a specific emphasis on missing data handling and transparency. By identifying existing gaps and methodological shortcomings, this study aims to provide evidence-based recommendations to improve the collection, analysis, and reporting of PROs in future clinical trials—ultimately strengthening transparency, methodological rigor, and the clinical applicability of PRO evidence.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

This study was conducted as part of a larger research program examining statistical methods for analyzing PRO data from RCTs in NSCLC. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria have been reported previously [23]. In brief, we included RCTs that incorporated PROs as one of the outcome measures (regardless of whether they were designated as primary, secondary, or exploratory) in the abstract, methods, or results. We excluded studies that: (1) focused solely on psychosocial interventions or complementary and alternative therapies; (2) relied on proxy-reported outcomes; (3) involved heterogeneous cancer populations (i.e., studies with substantial variation in cancer types or sources); (4) were published in non-English languages; or (5) were review study.

Literature search

We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov. using the terms “randomized controlled trial,” “patient self-reported outcome,” “quality of life,” “non-small cell lung cancer,” and other relevant search terms to identify potentially eligible studies published from the time each database was constructed until June 6, 2024. A search strategy was developed by an experienced researcher with reference to similar studies (Supplementary File 1).

Study process

Two experienced researchers, trained in clinical epidemiology or medical statistics, independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of studies using predefined eligibility criteria. A pilot data extraction was conducted on 10 eligible studies, after which both researchers independently extracted data from all included trials using a structured table with detailed written instructions. When available, supplementary materials and published trial protocols were also reviewed for data extraction. Discrepancies during screening or data extraction were resolved through discussion and, if necessary, adjudicated by a third investigator.

Data extraction

We designed a structured questionnaire to collect information on the reporting and handling of missing PROs data in eligible RCTs. The questionnaire was developed based on the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement and its extensions, as well as a published methodological study [23, 26]. To enhance content validity, four experts in evidence-based medicine and clinical epidemiology were invited to brainstorm additional items related to missing data reporting in RCTs and to evaluate the relevance and appropriateness of each item. The questionnaire was finalized through pilot data extraction, which confirmed its completeness, appropriateness, and applicability.

General characteristics

From each eligible study, we extracted the following information: year of publication, journal, Journal Citation Reports (JCR) quartile, five-year impact factor, whether the trial was international, primary country of conduct, participant characteristics, mean age, sample size, disease stage, experimental and control groups, main clinical outcomes, and whether the study was industry-supported.

Characteristics of the PROs

For each PRO outcome, we extracted: (1) the measurement instrument used; (2) the method of data collection; and (3) whether PROs were reported at follow-up assessments.

Reporting of information regarding missing data

We extracted information on missing data reporting, including: (1) the section(s) of the article where missing data were reported; (2) whether a flow diagram illustrated missing data at the participant level; (3) whether reasons for missing data were provided; (4) whether baseline characteristics were compared between participants with and without missing data; (5) whether the assumed missing data mechanism was explicitly stated; (6) whether statistical methods for handling missing data were described; (7) whether the potential impact of missing data on study outcomes was discussed; (8) whether measures to minimize missingness (e.g., patient reminders) were reported; and (9) whether PRO-specific reporting guidelines (e.g., CONSORT-PRO) were cited.

Statistical analyses for handling missing data

Among studies that reported missing data, we extracted information on how missing data were handled for each PRO, including: (1) the assumed missing data mechanism used in the analysis; (2) whether justification was provided for the chosen method in the primary analysis; (3) the type of primary analysis (e.g., complete case analysis (CCA), all available data analysis, last observation carried forward (LOCF), single imputation (SI), multiple imputation (MI), mixed-effects model for repeated measures (MMRM), generalized estimating equations (GEE), other, or unclear); and (4) whether sensitivity analyses were performed for the primary outcome, the types of sensitivity analyses conducted, and whether these analyses altered the statistical significance of the results.

Analysis

For continuous variables, data were presented as mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed variables and as median (interquartile range) if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test in combination with visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Logistic regression was used to examine associations between study characteristics and whether missing data were reported at both the participant and outcome levels. The outcome variable was binary, and observations were assumed to be independent. Goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values < 5 considered acceptable [27].

Predictor selection was primarily theory-driven. Candidate variables were pre-specified based on expert consensus and prior methodological studies [24, 28], Seven study characteristics were included in the regression analysis (year of publication, multinational study, corporate/Industry supported, sample sizes, PRO endpoint, reported PROs in a subsequent publication, and PRO collection methods). Given the methodological nature of the study, a formal a priori power calculation was not performed. However, the adequacy of the sample size for multivariable logistic regression was evaluated using the events-per-variable (EPV) rule [29]. The number of outcome events exceeded the recommended minimum of 10 events per predictor, supporting the stability of the model estimates. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed significance level of 0.05 was used for all statistical tests, and P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0.

Results

Characteristics of the included RCTs

A total of 13,015 citations were identified through database searches, and four relevant full-text articles were retrieved from other sources, yielding 13,019 citations for screening. After title and abstract screening, 988 full-text studies were accessed for eligibility. Of these, 252 RCTs that reported PROs as primary, secondary, or exploratory outcomes were included in the final analysis. A PRISMA flowchart summarizing the study selection process is shown in Fig. 1, The list of included references is provided in Supplementary File 2.

Regarding PROs-related characteristics, the most frequently used instruments were the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) questionnaires (52.4%), followed by the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy (FACT) instruments (21.4%) and the EQ-5D (11.9%). For data collection methods, only 15 trials (6.0%) used electronic data capture, while 47 trials (18.7%) relied on paper-based collection; nearly three-quarters (190 trials, 75.4%) did not specify the mode of data collection. Notably, only eight trials (3.2%) published subsequent reports presenting PRO results.

Characteristics of missing PRO data in the included trials

Among the 252 included studies, 177 (70.2%) explicitly reported the occurrence of missing PRO data. Of these, 125 studies (70.6%) used PROs as secondary or exploratory outcomes, whereas 52 studies (29.4%) used PROs as primary outcomes. Regarding the reporting level, only one study (0.6%) reported missing data exclusively at the participant level, 40 studies (22.6%) reported missing data at both the participant and outcome levels, and 136 studies (76.8%) reported missing data solely at the outcome level (Fig. 2).

With respect to statistical handling, primary-outcome trials adhered more closely to methodological standards: 44.2% specified the assumed missing data mechanism, and 69.2% reported the statistical methods used to address missingness, compared with 17.6% and 35.2%, respectively, among secondary-outcome trials. In terms of study quality and risk-of-bias management, primary-outcome trials again performed better. Specifically, 38.5% discussed the potential impact of missing data on study results, and 19.2% described measures to minimize missingness, compared with 16.0% and 4.8% among secondary-outcome trials.

Logistic regression analysis indicated that RCTs with a sample size exceeding 200 participants were more likely to report missing PRO data compared with smaller trials (81.1% vs. 55.9%; P = 0.011). Trials using PROs as primary outcomes were also significantly more likely to report missing PRO data than those using PROs as secondary or exploratory outcomes (88.1% vs. 64.7%; P = 0.001). Other factors, including publication year, multinational status, industry support, subsequent PRO reporting, and data collection methods, were not significantly associated with the reporting of missing data (P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Information on the reasons for missing PRO data

As shown in Fig. 3, the most frequently reported reasons for missing PRO data were patient death, disease progression or treatment-related adverse events, and poor physical condition. Additional causes included institutional or procedural errors, such as administrative lapses or missed follow-up assessments, as well as voluntary withdrawal by patients.

Overall, clinical deterioration—rather than methodological or procedural factors—accounted for the majority of missing PRO data. This suggests that the occurrence of missingness was largely non-random and driven by underlying clinical outcomes, emphasizing the need for analytical approaches capable of addressing outcome-dependent attrition.

Reporting on missing data handling methods

As summarized in Table 4, among the 52 studies that designated PROs as primary outcomes, approximately 52.6% conducted baseline characteristic balance testing, most commonly for categorical variables. The Fisher’s exact test was the most frequently applied method (11 studies, 19.3%), reflecting attention to small-sample distributions and 2 × 2 contingency structures. For continuous variables, both parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA) and nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney/Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis) were employed.

Regarding assumptions about the missing data mechanism, 11 studies explicitly assumed data were MAR, while only 2 assumed data were MCAR. However, over half of the trials failed to report the assumed mechanism, limiting interpretability and reproducibility.

In terms of statistical handling, 23 studies justified their primary analytical methods for missing data, yet only 12 conducted sensitivity analyses. The most common sensitivity approaches were Pattern Mixture Models (PMM), Joint Models, and LOCF. Notably, few studies (23.1%) reported whether sensitivity analyses altered the statistical significance of PRO outcomes, highlighting insufficient transparency in robustness assessments.

As illustrated in Fig. 4, the CCA method was most frequently employed, followed by mixed-effects models and mixed-effects regression approaches. The predominance of CCA underscores the continued reliance on traditional yet potentially biased methods that assume data are MCAR.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional review of NSCLC RCTs reporting PROs, we identified substantial deficiencies in the reporting and management of missing data. Although approximately 70% of trials acknowledged missing PRO data, most provided only outcome-level summaries without participant-level detail. Such superficial reporting may obscure potential heterogeneity among patients, particularly those with severe symptom burden or psychological distress, whose incomplete responses may disproportionately bias estimates of quality of life and emotional well-being [16]. Therefore, improving transparency in PRO reporting requires not only acknowledging the existence of missing data but also clarifying the mechanisms underlying missingness and the analytic approaches applied to address it.

Our findings further revealed that descriptions of missing-data mechanisms and the statistical methods used to handle missingness were frequently insufficient, limiting readers’ ability to assess the direction and magnitude of potential bias. Even when PROs were designated as primary endpoints and reporting was comparatively more comprehensive by including reasons for missingness, analytic strategies, and related assumptions, the overall quality still fell below the standards recommended by the CONSORT-PRO extension. The lack of systematic explanation of missing-data mechanisms and insufficient transparency in statistical approaches remain notable shortcomings. Collectively, these results indicate that current oncology trial practices do not yet fully comply with the methodological rigor advocated by CONSORT-PRO extension and highlight the urgent need to establish standardized frameworks for PRO data collection, management, and reporting.

Regression analysis identified several factors associated with higher reporting quality. Studies with larger sample sizes were significantly more likely to report missing PRO data, which likely reflecting stronger editorial standards, more robust data management infrastructure, and enhanced oversight [24]. Trials that used PROs as primary endpoints demonstrated more complete reporting than those with secondary or exploratory endpoints, underscoring the influence of study design in prioritizing patient-centered data. Interestingly, publication year was not significantly associated with reporting quality, suggesting heterogeneity adoption of reporting guidelines and a lag in integrating methodological standards into practice.

Consistent with previous oncology research [30–32], we found that missing PRO data in NSCLC RCTs were predominantly driven by clinical factors such as disease progression, death, and physical deterioration. According to recommendations from the SISAQOL Consortium and ICH E9 (R1), death is typically not considered traditional missing data but is classified as an intercurrent event, occurring post-randomization and potentially precluding outcome observation or altering interpretability [33–35]. ICH E9 (R1) emphasizes that trial protocols should pre-specify how intercurrent events are addressed within the estimand framework, including treatment policy, composite, hypothetical, and while-on-treatment (“while alive”) strategies, with corresponding sensitivity analyses aligned to the chosen strategy to ensure transparency and validity of outcome interpretation [34]. Nevertheless, events such as death carry important psychosocial implications, reflecting severe disease progression and profoundly affecting patients and their families [32].

Additionally, procedural and institutional causes—such as data entry errors or voluntary withdrawal—also contributed to missing data. These observations highlight the importance of improving trial design and operational management to optimize data collection, enhance follow-up adherence, and minimize non-clinical sources of missingness. Notably, patient withdrawal may itself reflect psychosocial distress, treatment burden, or preference for disengagement. Understanding these patterns is crucial for developing supportive interventions that reduce attrition among vulnerable patient groups.

Although missingness related to clinical outcomes is often unavoidable, methodological and operational improvements can mitigate its effects. Flexible data collection strategies, such as electronic questionnaires, remote assessments, and proactive communication with participants, may increase data completeness. Furthermore, providing additional support for patients with poor prognosis and transparently reporting missing-data patterns and handling methods can enhance interpretability and reproducibility of study findings.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the statistical methods used to handle missing PROs. Most studies continued to rely on CCA, mixed-effects models, and MMRM, although the use of MI and other advanced approaches is gradually increasing. This trend parallels a 2021 review of RCTs in high-impact journals, which reported that despite growing adoption of MI between 2014 and 2019, CCA remained the dominant approach [36, 37]. The continued prevalence of CCA likely reflects its simplicity and interpretability, as well as its acceptable performance when missingness is minimal and sample sizes are large. However, CCA yields unbiased estimates only under the assumption of data MCAR, a condition rarely met in PRO research [38, 39]. Consequently, its widespread use may overestimate average health status and underestimate the experiences of patients with high symptom or emotional distress.

Given that PRO data are typically longitudinal and correlated with symptom severity and psychosocial factors, they impose higher methodological demands. Mixed-effects and MMRM approaches can accommodate partially observed data under a MAR assumption, offering greater robustness and efficiency in estimating temporal trends. PMM and joint models provide further flexibility under MNAR mechanisms [19]; however, PMMs rely on untestable assumptions regarding how outcomes differ across missing-data patterns. Because these assumptions cannot be verified from the observed data, results from PMMs are highly sensitive to how patterns are defined and modeled, which can lead to bias if the assumptions are misspecified. This limitation underscores the importance of cautious interpretation and the routine use of sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of findings [40].

Beyond these conventional approaches, recent work has proposed population-modeling strategies that characterize longitudinal PRO trajectories by borrowing information across individuals, rather than explicitly imputing missing values. Such likelihood-based frameworks can accommodate informative missingness and have been successfully demonstrated using NSCLC PRO data [41, 42]. In addition, MI allows incorporation of uncertainty arising from missingness and generally improves estimation precision, though bias persists when data are MNAR. Importantly, only a minority of studies assessed robustness through sensitivity analyses, representing a clear gap relative to SISAQOL [35] and CONSORT-PRO recommendation [26]. Limited sensitivity analysis reduces confidence in study findings and weakens their translation into clinical and psychosocial practice.

To strengthen validity, transparency, and patient-centeredness of NSCLC trials, future studies should pre-specify missing data handling strategies at the design stage, incorporate plausible assumptions regarding missing-data mechanisms, and routinely perform sensitivity analyses [43]. Advanced statistical approaches such as joint modeling, PMM (when assumptions are clearly stated and sensitivity analyses performed), and Bayesian methods can simultaneously account for the associations between clinical outcomes and PROs, the non-randomness of missingness, and individual heterogeneity, thereby reducing bias and improving reliability. Moreover, the integration of electronic data capture, real-time monitoring, and artificial intelligence-assisted modeling provides opportunities for dynamic tracking, individualized prediction, and continuous improvement in PRO data quality [35, 44].

Strengths and limitations

This study offers several methodological and conceptual strengths. First, it represents one of the most comprehensive cross-sectional analyses of PRO reporting in NSCLC RCTs to date, incorporating a large sample across multiple continents and publication years. By focusing specifically on the transparency and handling of missing PRO data, this review addresses an underexplored yet critical aspect of methodological quality in oncology research. Second, the use of a structured, CONSORT-PRO-based evaluation framework ensured objective and reproducible assessment of reporting quality. The inclusion of multivariable regression further allowed exploration of study-level factors associated with higher reporting standards, offering empirical insights for improving future trial design and reporting practices.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our analysis relied on information explicitly reported in published articles; unreported methodological details, even if performed, could not be captured, potentially leading to underestimation of reporting quality. Second, because only English-language publications were included, language bias may have limited the representativeness of the findings. Third, the heterogeneity of study designs, PRO instruments, and statistical approaches across trials may introduce variability not fully accounted for in our synthesis. Finally, while this study identified associations between trial characteristics and reporting completeness, causal inferences cannot be drawn.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable evidence to guide methodological improvement and enhance transparency in the use of PROs within oncology trials. Strengthening the rigor and consistency of missing data reporting will be essential for ensuring the interpretability, reproducibility, and patient-centered relevance of future PRO research.

Conclusions

In summary, although most NSCLC trials acknowledged the presence of missing PRO data, critical methodological components, such as assumptions regarding missing-data mechanisms, analytic strategies, and sensitivity analyses, were frequently underreported or inadequately addressed. Conventional approaches, including CCA and LOCF, predominated, while more advanced statistical methods (e.g., MI, mixed-effects models, or pattern-mixture models) were seldom applied. The limited implementation of sensitivity analyses further restricted the evaluation of result robustness under alternative missing-data assumptions. These findings reveal a persistent methodological gap between current research practice and the standards recommended by the CONSORT-PRO extension and FDA guidance on PROs. Encouragingly, the growing emphasis on PRO-specific reporting in recent years suggests a gradual shift toward improved methodological transparency and analytical rigor. Future clinical trials should prospectively plan for missing PRO data through pre-specified strategies, explicit modeling of plausible missingness mechanisms, and the incorporation of robust statistical and sensitivity analyses. Strengthening these methodological elements will enhance the scientific validity, interpretability, and patient-centered relevance of PRO findings, thereby improving the overall credibility and clinical utility of oncology trial evidence.
