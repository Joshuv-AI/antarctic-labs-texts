Persistent genital arousal disorder: A systematic review of diagnosis, etiology, and treatment approaches (2015–2025)
Authors: Prasenjit Bhowmik, Muhammad Umair Shafiq, Layla Sultan, Mustafa Ahmad, Abdulla Khaleel Alawadi, Sachin Yallappa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Persistent genital arousal disorder (PGAD) is a distressing, often debilitating condition of unwanted genital arousal occurring independent of desire and difficult to relieve, with substantial psychosocial burden. We systematically reviewed peer-reviewed studies (2015–2025) to synthesize the evidence for diagnosis, etiology, and treatment. PubMed, Ovid (MEDLINE/Embase), and CINAHL (English-language, human studies; last searched on August 31, 2025) were searched. Two reviewers independently screened the records and extracted data, with disagreements resolved by the consensus and third-reviewer adjudication. Risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I for comparative observational studies and Joanna Briggs Institute tools for case series. The findings were synthesized narratively in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis 2020. The review protocol was registered prospectively in PROSPERO (CRD420251108714). Of 799 records identified, 56 duplicates were removed; 743 titles/abstracts were screened, 197 full texts were assessed, and 14 studies were included. Eligible designs comprised cross-sectional surveys, case–control/cohort studies, case series ≥3 patients, and one clinical review with embedded cases; no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were identified. Owing to clinical and methodological heterogeneity, meta-analysis was not performed. Participants were predominantly women. Diagnostic practice was inconsistent: Most used symptom-based criteria aligned to International Society for the Study of Women’s Sexual Health or Leiblum–Nathan frameworks, variably combined with targeted imaging, neurophysiology, or small-fiber testing. Etiologic signals most often implicated neuropathic/structural mechanisms – sacral radiculopathy, Tarlov cysts, and pudendal or small-fiber neuropathy – alongside central neurobiological and psychosocial contributors. Interventions included pelvic-floor physiotherapy, cognitive-behavioral approaches, pharmacologic agents (e.g., gabapentinoids and benzodiazepines), neuromodulation, and selected surgical decompressions for defined lesions; outcomes were mixed and follow-up/safety reporting limited. Overall, PGAD appears multifactorial and under-standardized in assessment. It should be prioritized to include a consensus, multimodal diagnostic pathway and prospective, comparative studies using validated patient-reported outcomes to inform individualized, evidence-based care.

INTRODUCTION

Persistent genital arousal disorder (PGAD) is a distressing and often debilitating condition characterized by unwanted, persistent sensations of genital arousal such as tingling, throbbing, or engorgement, that occur in the absence of sexual desire or stimulation.[1] Unlike typical arousal, these sensations can last for hours or even days and may not be relieved by orgasm. PGAD predominantly affects females, although cases in males have also been reported.[23] The condition has profound psychosocial implications including anxiety, depression, social withdrawal, and impaired quality of life.[34]

Since its formal recognition in the early 2000s, the understanding of PGAD has evolved considerably. Initially considered a psychosexual anomaly, the condition is now recognized as multifactorial, with emerging evidence implicating neurological, pharmacological, psychological, and structural etiologies.[5] A growing body of neuroimaging research has identified abnormalities such as Tarlov cysts and pudendal nerve entrapments in affected individuals, suggesting a neuropathic basis in many cases.[67] In addition, associations with psychotropic medication use, particularly withdrawal from serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SRIs), have been documented.[8]

Diagnostic criteria for PGAD remain heterogeneous, although the International Society for the Study of Women’s Sexual Health (ISSWSH) has proposed working definitions to guide research and clinical practice.[9] The absence of standardized diagnostic tools complicates prevalence estimation, but recent literature suggests that the condition may be underdiagnosed due to stigma, patient reluctance, and clinician unfamiliarity.

Treatment of PGAD remains challenging. While pharmacological agents[10] such as selective SRIs (SSRIs), gabapentinoids, and benzodiazepines have shown varying efficacy, nonpharmacological approaches, including pelvic floor physical therapy, cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), and neuromodulation,[11] have also been explored. Surgical decompression of pudendal nerves or drainage of Tarlov cysts has been attempted in refractory cases but with inconsistent outcomes.[1213] There is currently no universally accepted treatment algorithm.

Although prior narrative reviews have discussed PGAD and the ISSWSH consensus[9] (2021) broadened the terminology with delineation of biopsychosocial, stepwise approach to evaluation and management, yet the empirical literature is fragmented – dominated by small observational studies and case series with heterogeneous diagnostics and outcome reporting. A focused, study-level, risk-of-bias-informed synthesis is needed to collate current diagnostic modalities, plausible etiologic signals, and reported treatment outcomes in one place. Given the complexity of PGAD and the variability in its presentation and management, this systematic review aims to synthesize peer-reviewed evidence from the past decade (2015–2025) on the diagnosis, etiology, and treatment of PGAD.

Therefore, our primary objectives are to figure out more accurate diagnostic pathways, identify the most plausible etiological factors, evaluate treatment outcomes, and any adverse events to guide evidence-based clinical practice. We also aim to establish symptom recurrence, patient reported outcomes, and quality of life impact as secondary objectives.

To avoid ambiguity arising from variable nomenclature in the literature, we adopt ISSWSH-aligned definitions[9] and apply them consistently throughout the text, tables, and figures. These are described in Supplementary Table 1.

METHODS

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search for studies on PGAD published between January 1, 2015 and August 31, 2025. Major databases such as PubMed, Ovid (MEDLINE and Embase), and CINAHL were searched (last searched on August 31, 2025). The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH) with free-text keywords related to PGAD. Database-specific adaptations were applied to ensure optimal coverage. Filters were applied where available to restrict results. In addition, the reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify any additional eligible studies [Supplementary Table 2].

The review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 statement. A protocol was developed in advance and is also registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251108714).

Inclusion criteria

Eligible studies were peer-reviewed, English-language articles published between 2015 and 2025 on PGAD in humans of any age/sex that primarily addressed diagnosis, etiology/pathophysiology, treatment, epidemiology/prevalence, or psychosocial impact. We included observational (cross-sectional and cohort), nonrandomized interventional, imaging/neurophysiological, qualitative, and case series (≥3 patients) designs, provided they directly examined PGAD/Genito-Pelvic Dysesthesia (GPD) and reported at least one of diagnostic approaches, underlying mechanisms, treatment outcomes/adverse events, or disease burden. For articles combining narrative clinical review with embedded original case-series data, only the original patient-level case-series data were extracted and analyzed.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded single-patient case reports, clinical image vignettes, and studies not specific to PGAD/GPD (e.g., general sexual dysfunction, unrelated pelvic pain, or indirect evidence such as Tarlov-cyst epidemiology without PGAD assessment). We also excluded editorials, letters, commentaries without original data, conference abstracts lacking sufficient detail, animal/in vitro studies, non-English publications, systematic review, and articles outside 2015–2025.

Study process

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Ovid (EMBASE and MEDLINE), and CINAHL identified 799 records. After removing duplicates (n = 56), 743 titles/abstracts were screened; 197 full texts were reviewed. Exclusions included case reports (n = 74), indirect evidence (n = 89), and nonoriginal commentary/editorial/narrative work (n = 20). Ultimately, 14 studies met the inclusion criteria. However, one hybrid study a systematic review with an embedded three-patient case series was retained for the case-series component only while we did not synthesize the whole review narrative. Screening was performed by two independent reviewers (PB, MUS) using screening software EndNote, with disagreements resolved by consensus and when required, third-reviewer adjudication (SY). A PRISMA 2020 flowchart [Figure 1] summarizes the process.

Data extraction and analysis

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a piloted, standardized form. Discrepancies were resolved by the consensus or third-reviewer adjudication. Unclear or missing data were recorded as “not reported” and not imputed. Prespecified data items included study design and setting; sample size and participant characteristics; diagnostic terminology, criteria, and modalities; putative etiologies; interventions and comparators; outcomes (including symptom severity, distress, and quality of life); adverse events; and follow-up duration. Due to heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcome measures, meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, a qualitative narrative synthesis was performed. The synthesis was organized around three domains: Diagnostic modalities, etiological classifications, and treatment modalities. Studies were grouped by design, dominant etiologic hypothesis, and intervention class. Outcomes were summarized using author-reported measures, with narrative synthesis of direction and magnitude where effect estimates were unavailable; heterogeneity exploration and sensitivity analyses were not performed due to the sparse data.

Effect measures: For dichotomous outcomes, we planned risk ratios (RRs); for continuous outcomes, mean difference (MD) or standardized MD, each with 95% confidence intervals; where meta-analysis was not feasible, results were summarized narratively.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated using the design-appropriate tools. Comparative observational studies (cohort/case–control) were appraised using ROBINS-I. Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists were applied to prevalence studies, analytical cross-sectional studies, diagnostic observational studies, and case series, as appropriate. Assessments were performed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved by the consensus and, if required, third-reviewer adjudication.

Certainty assessment: We planned to rate certainty for the key outcomes using GRADE, but given the absence of comparable data across studies, a GRADE assessment was not performed (to be undertaken if quantitative synthesis becomes feasible).

RESULTS

Study characteristics

A total of 14 studies published between 2015 and 2025 met the eligibility criteria. These comprised two epidemiological/clinical characterization studies, three psychosocial investigations, five studies examining neurological or neurophysiological mechanisms, three interventional case series, and one hybrid clinical review with case series data. Study designs ranged from cross-sectional surveys and qualitative interviews to neuroimaging analyses, clinical cohorts, and surgical outcome reports.

Across studies, participants were predominantly adult women, although some prevalence surveys included male respondents.[14] Sample sizes varied widely – from small surgical case series with as few as three patients[15] to large community-based prevalence cohorts exceeding 2500 individuals.[14] PGAD/GPD most commonly affected women in their third to fifth decade, with mean ages generally between the early 30s and early 40s. Age-related trends were noted: Younger and midlife women (~30–40 years) more frequently appeared in psychological and relational cohorts,[1617] whereas older individuals (>45 years) more often demonstrated structural etiologies such as lumbosacral radiculopathy, Tarlov cysts, or pudendal neuropathy.[181920] Table 1a and b summarize the characteristics of the included studies.

Diagnostic modalities

Diagnostic strategies across the included studies were heterogeneous, reflecting evolving definitions and the lack of standardized criteria. Most investigations combined clinical history with symptom-based criteria and selective imaging or neurophysiological testing. Many prevalence and psychosocial studies applied validated self-report tools aligned with ISSWSH[9] or the Leiblum and Nathan criteria,[1] identifying spontaneous, distressing genital arousal without desire through online or questionnaire-based screening.[1416]

Neuroimaging was utilized in multiple studies to explore structural or functional correlates. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) was applied by Dalkeranidis et al.[21] to identify altered resting-state connectivity patterns, while Kümpers et al.[4] combined neuroimaging with clinical phenotyping to delineate the neurological subtypes of PGAD. Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbosacral spine and pelvis was used in surgical and neuropathic case series[1819] to detect sacral meningeal cysts, annular tears, or nerve root compression. Neurophysiological modalities – including somatosensory-evoked potentials and small-fiber nerve assessment – confirmed peripheral neurogenic involvement in selected studies.[722]

Psychological and relational studies operationalized PGAD within a fear-avoidance and distress framework using standardized psychometric instruments.[1723] Only one study implemented a comprehensive multimodal protocol combining pelvic floor evaluation, imaging, and psychological screening.[15]

Across the 14 included studies, diagnostic approaches varied: 3/14 (~21%) relied primarily on self-reported screening tools, 4/14 (~29%) reported targeted neuroimaging, and 2/14 (~14%) incorporated formal neurological examination; smaller numbers reported neurophysiology and/or explicitly multimodal, biopsychosocial assessment pathways, underscoring the need for standardized assessment [Figure 2].

Etiology

Across included studies, PGAD demonstrated a multifactorial etiology spanning neurogenic, psychological, and biopsychosocial pathways. Approximately 45%–40% of studies supported neuropathic mechanisms, with sacral radiculopathy, Tarlov cysts, and pudendal or small-fiber neuropathy identified on imaging or neurophysiology.[718192022] fMRI and case–control data revealed altered resting-state connectivity and heightened sensory network activation,[421] indicating central neurobiological involvement.

Psychological and relational contributors accounted for 25%–30% of findings, with anxiety, catastrophizing, fear-avoidance, and interpersonal distress associated with heightened symptom burden and functional impairment.[1617]

A subset of studies highlighted biopsychosocial interactions (~14%), emphasizing overlaps between pelvic floor dysfunction, neuropathic sensitization, and emotional distress.[15] Idiopathic presentations (~21%) were also reported, particularly in smaller cohorts where no clear structural or neurological abnormality emerged.[142324]

Together, evidence suggests PGAD arises from interacting peripheral, central, and psychosocial mechanisms rather than a single causal pathway, reinforcing the need for individualized, multimodal evaluation [Figure 3].

Treatment modalities and outcomes

Therapeutic strategies for PGAD varied substantially, reflecting its heterogeneous etiology and the absence of standardized protocols. Overall, ~60% of studies evaluated surgical or neurointerventional treatments, ~7% examined pharmacologic approach, ~15% investigated psychobehavioral or pelvic floor therapy, and ~7% employed multidisciplinary care [Figure 4].

Treatment methods used in included studies (n = 14)

Surgical and neurointerventional approaches demonstrated the most robust, sustained benefit in patients with confirmed neurogenic pathology. Sacral meningeal cyst decompression achieved symptom resolution in up to 90% of cases,[18] pudendal nerve neurolysis provided near-complete relief in most patients,[20] and minimally invasive spine surgery improved symptoms in ~65%–80% with maintained benefit at a long-term follow-up.[1922]

Neuromodulatory and pharmacologic treatments yielded more variable results. Pudendal neuromodulation produced durable improvement in most cases,[24] while clomipramine demonstrated partial benefit in refractory presentations.[25]

Pelvic floor and psychobehavioral interventions produced modest yet meaningful reductions in distress and symptom burden. Online pelvic floor training showed small improvements,[23] while fear-avoidance-focused psychological strategies supported improved coping and relationship functioning.[1617]

Other modalities, such as pharmacotherapy, provided significant symptom relief in most patients, although long-term efficacy remains unclear.[25] Multidisciplinary care integrating pelvic floor therapy, psychological support, and targeted medical/surgical intervention offered clinically meaningful but often partial benefit.[15]

Across all treatments, success rates ranged from ~50% to >90%, with the greatest benefit observed in surgically treated neurogenic PGAD and more modest but valuable improvements following conservative and psychosocial therapies.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review synthesizes the evidence from 14 peer-reviewed studies published between 2015 and 2025, summarizing diagnostic approaches, etiological theories, and therapeutic strategies for PGAD. The findings underscore the complexity of this underdiagnosed condition, which remains clinically challenging due to its multifactorial origins, heterogeneous presentation, and limited evidence-based guidelines.

Diagnostic challenges

PGAD, more recently reframed as GPD, remains a rare and clinically challenging condition due to evolving definitions, heterogeneous presentation, and limited standardized assessment pathways. It was first described by Leiblum and Nathan[1] in 2001 as persistent, intrusive genital arousal unrelated to desire and resistant to orgasm relief. The definition was later refined to emphasize distress and psychological burden.[26] Increasingly viewed as a neuro-psychosomatic condition rather than a hypersexual disorder, PGAD is frequently misdiagnosed with vulvodynia, anxiety, or hypersexuality.[5]

The ISSWSH consensus statement[9] [Figure 5] now provides the most accepted diagnostic framework, highlighting persistent, unwanted arousal sensations independent of desire, lasting hours to days, causing distress, and not reliably relieved by orgasm. Despite this, diagnostic practice remains inconsistent. Survey-based studies rely on self-report tools aligned with early criteria,[14] while clinical cohorts employ structured interviews and validated scales.[4] Objective modalities – including skin biopsy for small-fiber neuropathy,[7] electrophysiology in Tarlov cyst cases,[20] spinal MRI,[19] and exploratory fMRI studies showing altered connectivity[2127] – have provided mechanistic insights but lack uniform application.

Qualitative studies underscore significant diagnostic barriers, with patients reporting stigma, dismissal, and frequent misclassification within broader sexual dysfunctions.[23] PGAD currently remains a clinical diagnosis of exclusion, requiring careful sexual, neurological, pelvic floor, and medication history (particularly SSRI/SNRI changes) alongside psychosocial assessment. However, no validated diagnostic protocol exists.

Overall, while PGAD diagnostic criteria have advanced from descriptive origins[1] to consensus guidelines,[9] clinical implementation remains fragmented. A standardized, multimodal diagnostic pathway integrating structured interviews, validated tools, and targeted neuro-urological and psychosocial assessment is critically needed for accurate identification and holistic care.

Etiological complexities

PGAD/GPD has a multifactorial etiology, involving interacting neuropathic, structural, central neurobiological, and psychosocial mechanisms. Early descriptions noted that symptoms arise not from heightened libido but from paradoxical genital arousal with central distress.[12628]

Neuropathic and structural factors are most consistently reported. Small-fiber neuropathy has been demonstrated in PGAD patients[7] and is more prevalent among women with Tarlov cysts,[2029] which can compress sacral nerves and trigger genital dysesthesia.[13] Multiple surgical series show symptom improvement after cyst excision or sacral decompression,[181920] supporting a peripheral nerve–irritation mechanism.

Central neurobiological contributions include altered resting-state connectivity in arousal networks[21] and abnormal limbic–somatosensory activation on fMRI.[27] Serotonergic/dopaminergic dysregulation is supported by PGAD cases linked to SSRI initiation/withdrawal[10] and antiepileptic agents.[30]

Psychological and relational influences modulate symptom severity. Fear-avoidance, catastrophizing, and relational stress worsen distress and functioning,[161731] while qualitative accounts emphasize stigma, misdiagnosis, and emotional burden.[28]

PGAD has also been associated with restless legs syndrome and bladder,[32] eating disorders,[33] pregnancy-related pelvic changes,[34] and posttraumatic onset after motor-vehicle injury,[35] reinforcing its heterogeneous pathophysiology.

Consensus reviews advocate a biopsychosocial model,[92628] wherein structural or neurophysiological triggers interact with central sensitization, psychological factors, and pelvic neuromuscular dysregulation. Multidisciplinary evaluation incorporating neurological, pelvic floor, and psychosocial assessment is therefore recommended.[1536]

In a nutshell, PGAD/GPD appears to arise from peripheral nerve dysfunction, central arousal-network dysregulation, and psychosocial amplification, necessitating integrated, multimodal diagnosis and management.

Treatment modalities

Management of PGAD/GPD remains heterogeneous and mechanism-guided. Pharmacological strategies (SSRIs/SNRIs, clomipramine, gabapentin/pregabalin, and dopaminergic and hormonal agents) show variable, often partial benefit and tolerability issues, with no robust controlled evidence to date.[10112637]

Surgical intervention offers the most durable relief in patients with confirmed structural pathology. Meningeal/Tarlov cyst excision, sacral decompression, and lumbar endoscopic surgery demonstrate high improvement rates in selected cases,[131819] although risks include cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak, infection, and recurrence.

Neuromodulation and neurolysis (pudendal/sacral nerve stimulation and nerve release techniques) show promising results in neuropathic PGAD[121524] but remain invasive and supported by small case series.

Pelvic floor therapy (education, manual therapy, and relaxation/biofeedback) reduces symptoms, particularly in hypertonicity-associated and postpartum PGAD,[2334] although evidence remains preliminary.

Psychological interventions (psychoeducation, CBT, and mindfulness) address distress, avoidance, and relational burden, improving coping and well-being.[16173138] They form a core component of treatment within a biopsychosocial model.

Multidisciplinary care integrating neurologic, pelvic floor, and psychological expertise is consistently recommended[91536] and provides the most comprehensive and individualized outcomes.

Recurrence occurs commonly after surgery or neuromodulation[13] and symptom return is also common if medication or psychological support is stopped, highlighting the chronic, relapsing nature of PGAD.

Adverse events are generally mild with medications (e.g., sedation and gastrointestinal upset) and infrequent but possible with surgery (e.g., CSF leak and infection).

Emerging options include anti-androgens,[37] clomipramine for refractory cases,[25] duloxetine,[39] botulinum toxin,[40] zolpidem,[41] and shockwave lithotripsy,[42] although evidence remains limited.

The lack of standardized outcome metrics, such as the PGAD Symptom Index (PSI) and distress scales, limits consistency in PGAD research. The PSI assesses symptom frequency and severity, while distress scales capture the emotional and psychological impact on patients. Although valuable for measuring treatment outcomes, these tools have not been widely adopted.

Strengths of this review

This review builds on ISSWSH[9] (2021) and delivers an updated, study-level, risk-of-bias–aware synthesis (2015–2025) across 14 studies. It integrates diagnostic evaluation (including imaging/neurophysiology), putative etiologic signals, and treatment outcomes spanning epidemiology, psychosocial research, neurology, and interventional case series. We highlight substantial heterogeneity in case definitions, work-ups, and outcome reporting, but also note areas of convergence that can inform practice and research. By collating observational, interventional, and patient-reported data in one framework, we identify where evidence is most consistent and where prospective, comparative studies using standardized diagnostic criteria and outcome measures are urgently needed.

Limitations of current literature

The literature on PGAD is limited by several factors. First, the overall evidence base remains limited, with most studies small in size and restricted to single-center or survey-based designs. RCTs are absent, and interventional evidence derives mainly from small case series or surgical cohorts. Second, male patients remain underrepresented, limiting the generalizability across sexes. In addition, limited long-term follow-up and safety data affect the assessment of treatment efficacy. Finally, there is a lack of multidisciplinary management protocols, despite the need for collaboration between urologists, neurologists, gynecologists, and mental health professionals.

Future directions

To advance the field of PGAD, future research should focus on multicenter, prospective cohort studies and RCTs using validated diagnostic tools and outcome measures. Mechanistic investigations should expand beyond peripheral neuropathy to include central neuroimaging, hormonal influences, and vascular pathways. Developing etiology-specific treatment algorithms and utilizing neuroimaging and neurophysiology will help better understand PGAD’s mechanisms. Research should also explore neuroendocrine, hormonal, and genetic factors, while ensuring male PGAD patients and individuals from diverse gender identities are included in studies. Finally, the routine use of Patient Reported Outcome Measures will capture distress and quality of life, providing a more comprehensive assessment of treatment outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

PGAD is a complex, multifactorial condition with significant psychosocial impact. Etiologies span neurological, pharmacological, and psychogenic factors, often overlapping within the same patient, yet diagnostic clarity remains limited due to the absence of standardized criteria and validated tools. Current treatments are diverse and largely empirical: CBT benefits psychogenic cases, gabapentinoids provide relief in neurogenic presentations, and surgical decompression offers improvement for selected patients with structural pathology. However, most evidence are largely nonrandomized and confined to small case series with limited and inconsistent follow-up, our recommendations are necessarily provisional and should be considered as hypothesis-generating and require prospective comparative validation. Future research should prioritize standardized diagnostic criteria, core outcome sets, and adequately powered prospective studies to define phenotypes, clarify mechanisms, and determine which interventions deliver durable, patient-centered benefit.
