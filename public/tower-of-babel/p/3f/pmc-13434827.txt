The effects of splenectomy, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and concentrated growth factor (CGF) on liver regeneration following major hepatectomy in rats
Authors: Yunus Emre Sacin, Esma Kirimlioglu, Ulas Ural, Asuman Kaplan Algın, Ali Avanaz, Mustafa Ormeci, Guliz Avsar, Abdullah Kısaoglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Post-hepatectomy liver failure is a serious complication following liver resection and is associated with high morbidity and mortality. Platelets play a crucial role in liver regeneration due to their rich content of growth factors. However, the comparative effects of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and concentrated growth factor (CGF) on liver regeneration remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the effects of splenectomy (SPx), PRP, and CGF on liver regeneration and function following 70% hepatectomy in rats. Male Wistar rats were randomly divided into six groups: Control, Sham, Hepatectomy (PHx), Hepatectomy + SPx, Hepatectomy + PRP, and PHx + CGF. PRP and CGF were prepared using differential centrifugation and administered via the portal vein after hepatectomy. Liver regeneration was assessed histopathologically, immunohistochemically and biochemically on postoperative day (POD) 5. PRP treatment significantly increased Ki-67 expression, indicating enhanced hepatocyte proliferation. CGF promoted neovascularization and improved hepatocyte morphology but showed lower Ki-67 expression than PRP. SPx did not significantly enhance regeneration compared to PHx alone. Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) expression was highest in the PHx group, whereas the PRP and CGF groups exhibited moderate levels. PRP and CGF improved liver function biochemically, reducing bilirubin levels. Both PRP and CGF promote liver regeneration, albeit through distinct mechanisms: PRP primarily enhances cellular proliferation, whereas CGF facilitates vascularization and modulates the immune response. SPx did not confer additional benefits in this model. These findings suggest that PRP and CGF may have potential therapeutic applications in preventing post-hepatectomy liver failure.

Introduction

Posthepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) is a serious complication that can occur after liver resection and is one of the leading causes of postoperative mortality. It is defined as impaired liver function in maintaining its synthetic, excretory, and detoxification capacities, presenting on or after postoperative day (POD) 5 with an increased international normalized ratio (INR) accompanied by hyperbilirubinemia1.

Although the safety of hepatectomy (PHx) has increased significantly in recent years, postoperative morbidity has been reported to be between 4,09%-47,7% and mortality between 0.24%-9,7%2. The incidence of PHLF varies across different series; however, according to the criteria established by the International Study Group of Liver Surgery (ISGLS), it has been reported to be between 9% and 20%3.

After hepatectomy or partial liver transplantation (PLTx), sinusoidal shear stress is the primary trigger for hepatic regeneration4. Following a reduction in liver volume, an increase in portal venous flow per unit of liver tissue becomes inevitable. This hemodynamic change affects the micromorphology of hepatic sinusoids. In response to increased shear stress, nitric oxide (NO), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and interleukin (IL)-6 are released5–8. In addition, the increase in portal venous pressure and vascular shear stress following PHx leads to increased intestinal permeability, resulting in elevated levels of gut-derived pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), including lipopolysaccharides (LPS)9. Following the recognition of LPS by Toll-Like Receptors (TLRs) on Kupffer cells, these cells secrete key cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and IL-610. Elevated IL-6 levels activate the Janus kinase (JAK) pathway and stimulate the phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), thereby initiating hepatocyte proliferation11. The JAK/STAT3 pathway is a fundamental signaling pathway in liver regeneration. It regulates processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, growth, survival, and apoptosis.

The liver occupies a central position in circulation by receiving blood from the gastrointestinal tract via the portomesenteric system, forming the basis of the gut–liver axis and its extensive metabolic activities. From a physiological perspective, it functions as a dynamic reservoir that regulates venous return from the abdominal organs to systemic circulation. Approximately 1/4th of the cardiac output is directed to the liver, corresponding to an estimated flow of 100–130 mL per minute per 100 g of hepatic tissue. The portal vein delivers approximately 70–75% of the blood, carrying 50–70% of the oxygen requirement, whereas the hepatic artery supplies the rest12. In healthy individuals, approximately 30–40% of the portal venous blood flow is contributed by the splenic vein13. On the other hand, surgical approaches related to the spleen, which is another factor affecting the hemodynamic load of the liver, have also gained importance in the literature. Splenectomy (SPx) is preferred in some centers as a method of portal flow modulation, particularly in living donor liver transplantation (LDLT). This preference is influenced by conditions such as portal hypertension associated with splenomegaly, large portosystemic shunts, high-risk esophagogastric varices, portal pressure exceeding 20 mmHg after reperfusion, severe hypersplenism, A, B, AB and O (ABO) blood group system incompatibility, or the presence of a splenic artery aneurysm14.

In experimental studies involving liver transplantation in rats, SPx has been observed to reduce portal pressure, alleviate liver injury, and positively affect the regeneration process15. Furthermore, in a rat model involving 90% PHx and 90% PHx combined with SPx, it was reported that SPx significantly increased liver regeneration at 48 and 72 h postoperatively16. However, SPx is associated with life-threatening complications such as septic complications, pancreatic fistula, and hemorrhage14,17.

Although various surgical techniques have been described to prevent PHLF, these approaches may lead to undesirable outcomes such as morbidity and even mortality. The search for biological agents to support liver regeneration has led to alternative approaches to prevent this complication. Over the years, platelets have been reported to affect liver regeneration significantly18–20. These effects are thought to result from the rich growth factors within the granules of platelets20. Platelet concentrates are classified as platelet-rich plasma (PRP), plasma rich in growth factors (PRGF), platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), advanced platelet-rich fibrin (A-PRF), and concentrated growth factors (CGF) based on different centrifugation parameters21. The specialized centrifugation technique utilized in the preparation of CGF facilitates the formation of a unique three-dimensional fibrin network and entrapment of CD34 + stem cells, distinguishing its regenerative potential from earlier generations of platelet concentrates22.

Platelets are the smallest circulating blood cells and contain three distinct types of granules: alpha, dense, and lysosomal23. These granules contain a variety of components, including adhesion and coagulation factors, chemokines, immunoglobulins, amines, and protease inhibitors, as well as several growth factors such as angiopoietin 1 and 2, fibroblast growth factor (FGF), epidermal growth factor binding protein (EGF-BP), IL-2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and β, HGF, insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), VEGF, and transforming growth factor-alpha (TGF-α)24,25. The primary function of platelets is to rapidly adhere to the exposed vascular endothelium, seal injured vessel walls and form platelet plugs to achieve hemostasis. During this process, platelet activation triggers the exocytosis of granule contents, the release of newly synthesized mediators, and the emission of membrane-bound transcellular signaling molecules25.

The relationship between platelets and PHLF has been extensively investigated in the literature. Several studies have demonstrated that a low preoperative platelet count is associated with a higher incidence of PHLF26,27. In this context, the effects of platelet concentrations of different generations on the liver have become remarkable. PRP represents the first-generation of platelet concentrates, while PRF is a second-generation product obtained using a fixed centrifugal force without the need for additives. CGF is the most recent generation of platelet-derived product28. Some studies have reported that PRP administration in PHx-induced liver injury models or chemical agents exposure enhances liver regeneration, improves liver function, and reduces the fibrotic response within the liver20,29.

Studies on CGF, developed by Sacco in 2006, are quite limited. There are no experimental or clinical studies on liver regeneration in the literature30. CGF enables the isolation of a larger, denser, and more growth factor-rich fibrin matrix than PRF, owing to its variable centrifugation speed31. There is evidence suggesting that CGF and PRF share similar mechanical properties and growth factor content, and that both are more effective than PRP32,33.

PHLF remains one of the leading causes of mortality following liver resection. Therefore, identifying effective and reliable approaches to promote liver regeneration is critical. These approaches should stimulate liver tissue regeneration in patients who develop or are at risk of developing PHLF through hypertrophy and hyperplasia, without inducing complications and reducing postoperative mortality. In this study, we aimed to compare the effects of SPx, PRP, and CGF, whose impact on liver regeneration has not yet been investigated in the literature, on liver regeneration and function in rat models undergoing 70% PHx.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and experimental groups

The study was conducted using male Wistar rats, aged 8–12 weeks and weighing 200–300 g, obtained from the Akdeniz University Animal Research Unit. The experimental protocol was approved by the Akdeniz University Local Animal Ethics Committee (approval number B.30.2.AKD.0.05.07.00/82, dated 02.11.2022) and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. This study was carried out as part of a project supported by the Akdeniz University Scientific Research Projects Coordination Unit. The rats were housed in a humidity and temperature-controlled room under a 12-hour light/dark cycle and provided food and water ad libitum. In this study, the experimental unit was defined as a single rat. The animals were randomly assigned to seven groups. The rats were allocated to the experimental groups through simple randomization using a lottery method, in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines to minimise selection bias. One group was used solely to prepare PRP and CGF. The remaining six groups were designated as experimental groups (Table 1). The sample size was calculated based on a priori power analyse. Potential confounders such as treatment order, measurement sequence, and cage location, were not controlled in this study. On POD 5, the animals in these groups were sacrificed under anesthesia, and blood and liver tissue samples were collected for further analysis. In addition, group allocation was known to the surgeon during the surgical procedures; the investigators responsible for the histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluations were blinded to the group identity.

Preparation of PRP and CGF

For the preparation of PRP and CGF, approximately 5–10 cubic centimeters (cc) of whole blood was collected from each donor rat via the abdominal aorta under anesthesia. The collected blood samples were transferred into Vacusera (Disera, İzmir, Turkey) tubes containing heparin sodium. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged using a Medifuge MF200 (Silfradent, Forli-Cesena, Italy) automatic centrifuge in PRP and CGF modes, respectively. For PRP preparation, an initial centrifugation was performed at 160 relative centrifugal force (RCF) for 15 min to separate erythrocytes and leukocytes from the plasma supernatant. Following this centrifugation, three layers were formed in the tube: plasma at the top, a middle “buffy coat” layer rich in platelets and leukocytes, and a bottom layer of erythrocytes. The upper two layers were carefully transferred to another tube without an anticoagulant using a blunt syringe. The second centrifugation was performed at 850 RCF for 15 min to concentrate the platelets at the bottom of the tube, forming a visible white pellet. As a result of the second centrifugation, a platelet-rich pellet was formed at the bottom of the tube, while platelet-poor plasma (PPP) remained at the top. The upper two-thirds of the PPP was discarded, and the remaining bottom one-third of the PPP was mixed with the platelet pellet and gently homogenized. Subsequently, 10% calcium gluconate was added to the tubes to activate the PRP34. In the CGF mode, the centrifuge applied a specialized sequence: acceleration for the first 30 s, followed by 2 min at 2700 rpm, 4 min at 2400 rpm, another 4 min at 2700 rpm, 3 min at 3000 rpm, and a final deceleration phase of 36 s. The use of sodium heparin tubes ensured that the layers remained in liquid state. After centrifugation, the following sequential layers were obtained: serum, a fibrin-rich but platelet-poor layer, a fibrin and platelet-rich layer, a leukocyte layer, and a fraction containing mononuclear cells and erythrocytes35. The plasma in the third layer was separated and extracted from the tube.

Surgical procedure

In this study, all rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal administration of 50–60 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride (Ketalar, Eczacıbaşı Warner-Lambert Pharmaceutical Industry, Levent, Istanbul, Turkey) and 10–15 mg/kg xylazine hydrochloride (Rompun, Bayer, Şişli, Istanbul, Turkey). Following anesthesia, a midline laparotomy was performed under aseptic conditions to expose the upper abdominal cavity and liver. 70% PHx was performed as described by Higgins and Anderson, by clamping the pedicles of the median and left lateral lobes and ligating them with 4 − 0 silk sutures36. The resected liver lobes were weighed. The liver-to-body weight ratio was determined by dividing the liver weight on POD 5 by the corresponding body weight and expressing the result as a percentage. The total liver weight was estimated from the weight of the resected liver tissue, assuming a standard 70% partial hepatectomy model was used. Liver regeneration was calculated as the percentage ratio of the remnant liver weight at sacrifice to the estimated total liver weight, as previously described by37. In the PHx + SPx group, following PHx, the spleen was clamped at the hilum and resected after ligation with 4 − 0 silk sutures. In the groups designated for PRP and CGF administration, 1 cc of PRP or CGF was infused via the portal vein using an insulin syringe over approximately one minute following PHx. After the surgical procedures, the abdomen was closed. Six hours after surgery, the animals were allowed to feed ad libitum.

Tissue and blood sample collection

On POD 5, the rats were weighed and re-operated under anesthesia. Blood was collected by cannulating the abdominal aorta at the level of the iliac bifurcation using a syringe, and 5–10 cc of blood was obtained from each rat. The collected blood samples were transferred into yellow-capped biochemistry tubes containing a clot activator and separator gel. Following blood collection, the vena cava superior (VCS) was incised. The heart was exposed, and the aorta was cannulated using a branula inserted into the left ventricle. Perfusion was performed with approximately 20 cc of 0.9% sodium chloride, followed by fixation of tissues with an equal volume of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution. Whole livers (in the C and S groups) and remnant livers (in the other groups) were resected and weighed. Liver tissues were sampled for histological and immunohistochemical examination.

Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining and histopathological examination

Tissue sections with a thickness of 5 μm were incubated overnight at 56 °C. The sections underwent deparaffinization and rehydration through two 10-minutes xylene immersions, followed by sequential immersions in decreasing concentrations of ethanol (100%, 90%, 80%, 70%) and finally in distilled water for 10 min. Sections were stained with hematoxylin (AMRESCO-0701, AMRESCO Inc., Ohio, USA) for 45 s, and excess stain was removed with water. Subsequently, the sections were dipped in eosin (AMRESCO-0109, AMRESCO Inc., Ohio, USA) and again rinsed with water to remove excess dye. After staining, the sections were passed through increasing concentrations of alcohol and cleared in xylene for 10 min each. The slides were mounted using Entellan® (107961, MERCK, Darmstadt, Germany). The stained sections were examined under a Zeiss Primo Star light microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany) and evaluated histopathologically with photomicrographs captured using the ZEN 2.5 lite software (Blue Edition, Version 2.5.75.0).

Immunohistochemical analysis

To assess liver regeneration, the immunolocalization of Ki-67 and STAT3 proteins was examined in liver sections. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections, 5 µm thick, were mounted on Superfrost Plus slides, deparaffinized, and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series (100%, 90%, 80%, 70%). For antigen retrieval, citrate buffer (pH 6) was used for STAT3, and Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9) was used for Ki-67. Washes were performed using Tris-buffered saline (TBS). To block endogenous peroxidase activity, BLOXALL (Vector, SP6000) was applied, followed by a 30-minute incubation with 5% normal goat serum (NGS, Vector, S-1000) for protein blocking. Primary antibodies (Rabbit anti-Ki-67, Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-19462; Rabbit anti-STAT3, Cell Signaling, 9139) were incubated overnight at + 4°C. For isotype control, rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG, Vector, I-1000) of the same species was used. For detection, sections incubated with the Ki-67 primary antibody were treated with a biotinylated Goat anti-Rabbit secondary antibody (Vector, BA-1000) and an avidin-biotin complex (VECTASTAIN Elite ABC Reagent, Vector, PK-6100) for 30 minutes. For STAT3, SignalStain® Boost IHC Detection Reagent Horse Radish Peroxidase (HRP) (Mouse, Cell Signaling, 8125S) was applied. All sections were developed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB)(Vector, SK-4100) as the chromogen. Counterstaining was performed with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Vector, H-3404), and slides were mounted using Entellan. Photomicrographs were captured using a Zeiss Primo Star light microscope (Zeiss) and ZEN 2.5 Lite software at 40X/0.65 magnification. Image exposure was set at 90% intensity and 9.5 milliseconds.

Analysis of blood samples

The collected blood samples were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min. From the centrifuged blood, 0.5 cc of serum was drawn using micropipettes and transferred into tubes compatible with the biochemical analyzer. For biochemical evaluation, the DRI-CHEM NX500i (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) automatic dry chemistry analyzer and DRI-CHEM SLIDES (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) colorimetric measurement slides were used. Serum samples were analyzed for alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin, and direct bilirubin levels.

Analysis and statistical evaluation

Staining intensities were quantified using the ImageJ Fiji image analysis software and subsequently transferred to GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad, version 10; Boston, MA, USA) for data analyses. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess whether the data from the two groups followed a normal distribution. For datasets with a normal distribution, differences in group means were analyzed using one-way ANOVA for weight parameters, biochemical parameters, and immunohistochemical data of Ki-67 and STAT3. Post hoc Holm-Sidak testing was applied for pairwise comparisons in Ki-67 data, while post hoc Tukey testing was used for weight parameters, biochemical parameters, and STAT3 expression analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Results were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Animal and liver weights

The preoperative weights of the rats ranged from 200 to 300 g, whereas their weights on POD 5 ranged from 160 to 275 g. No statistically significant differences in body weight were observed between the groups (p = 0.35 and p = 0.71), respectively. In the experimental groups that underwent PHx, the weights of the resected liver tissues ranged from 4.5 to 6.8 g, and the weights of the fully resected livers on POD 5 ranged from 5.6 to 10.3 g. In both cases, no statistically significant differences were found between the groups (p = 0.68 and p = 0.44), respectively.

Liver regeneration rates were calculated for all experimental groups based on the ratio of remnant liver weight at POD 5 to the estimated original liver weight. The mean regeneration rates were 103% in the PHx group, 101% in the PHx + SPx group, 104% in the PRP group, and 109% in the CGF group.

Although the CGF group demonstrated the highest regeneration rate, the differences among groups did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.534). Regeneration rates exceeding 100% in some animals may be attributed to perioperative factors such as sinusoidal congestion, tissue edema, or minor deviations from the assumed 70% resection ratio, rather than true hyperplastic overgrowth.

Regenerative features following partial hepatectomy with or without splenectomy: morphological, immunohistochemical, and biochemical findings

In the PHx group, prominent regenerative activity was evidenced by the presence of binucleated cells and increased mitotic figures (Fig. 1, blue arrow). However, this regenerative response was accompanied by concurrent pathological alterations, including cellular swelling, vacuolization, connective tissue accumulation, and perisinusoidal fibrosis, suggesting incomplete or dysregulated regeneration. Marked mononuclear cell infiltration in both portal and lobular areas, along with bile duct proliferation, further indicated an ongoing inflammatory and reparative process. Additionally, microcirculatory disturbance, such as nuclear atypia and karyomegaly, were observed, potentially reflecting cellular stress and impaired tissue remodeling during regeneration.

Importantly, these morphological findings collectively indicate that although regeneration is initiated in the PHx group, it is accompanied by significant tissue injury and inflammatory responses, suggesting an inefficient regenerative process.

In the PHx + SPx group, hepatocytes exhibited a more uniform size and shape, indicating a more organized regenerative response compared to the PHx group. This was accompanied by increased lymphoid cell presence and sinusoidal dilation, suggesting active immune involvement and improved microcirculatory adaptation during regeneration. Notably, the absence of fibrosis and steatosis further supports a more effective and balanced regenerative process, resulting in overall improved histological architecture compared to the PHx group (Fig. 1).

Taken together, these histopathological improvements in the PHx + SPx group suggest a more coordinated regeneration process with reduced cellular injury compared to the PHx group.

In immunohistochemical evaluations, Ki-67 expression in the PHx group was observed predominantly in Kupffer cells, with limited expression in hepatocytes (Fig. 2A). Ki-67 expression showed a significant increase compared to the C and S groups. In the PHx + SPx group, Ki-67 expression was observed in a substantial portion of lymphoid cells and to a lesser extent in hepatocytes, with no expression detected in Kupffer cells. Although Ki-67 expression in the PHx + SPx group was significantly higher than in the C and S groups, no significant difference was observed when compared to the PHx group (Fig. 2A).

These findings indicate that, despite similar overall proliferation levels between PHx and PHx + SPx groups, the cellular sources of proliferation differ, potentially reflecting a more physiologically coordinated regenerative response in the PHx + SPx group.

STAT3 expression was predominantly observed in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes and bile duct epithelium across all groups (Fig. 2B). The highest level of STAT3 expression was detected in the PHx group. STAT3 expression in this group showed a statistically significant increase compared to the C, S, PHx + SPx, and CGF groups. In contrast, the PHx + SPx group exhibited lower STAT3 expression compared to the other groups that underwent PHx (Fig. 2B). This elevated STAT3 activation in the PHx group may reflect a stress-associated or compensatory signaling response, consistent with the observed histological evidence of tissue injury and inflammation.

In the biochemical analyses, ALT and AST levels were highest in the PHx group. Total and direct bilirubin levels were significantly elevated in the PHx and PHx + SPx groups compared to the other groups (Fig. 3).

Overall, when morphological, immunohistochemical, and biochemical findings are considered together, regeneration occurs in both groups; however, the PHx group is characterized by a more injury-associated and dysregulated process, whereas the PHx + SPx group demonstrates a more balanced and coordinated regenerative response.

Regenerative effects of PRP: morphological, immunohistochemical, and biochemical findings

Histopathological examination of liver tissues revealed that hepatocytes in the C and S groups had a normal polygonal shape, uniform size, and large, round or oval nuclei. The cytoplasm exhibited an eosinophilic and granular appearance, with no evidence of bile pigment or bile plugs (Fig. 1). In the PRP group, liver tissue demonstrated increased mitotic activity along with reduced cellular swelling and vacuolization. No fibrosis was observed, and hepatocytes exhibited uniform size and shape. Sinusoidal architecture was preserved, and a decrease in apoptosis and necrosis rates was noted (Fig. 1). The proliferation marker Ki-67, which exhibits nuclear localization, was expressed at the highest level in the PRP group compared to all other groups (Fig. 2A). Ki-67 expression was predominantly observed in hepatocytes surrounding the central vein and exhibited a distinct staining pattern in both lymphoid and Kupffer cells (Fig. 2A). Additionally, although STAT3 expression was lower than in the PHx group, it showed a statistically significant increase compared to the S group (Fig. 2B). In the biochemical analyses, ALT and AST levels in the PRP group were higher than those in the C and S groups but lower than in the PHx and PHx + SPx groups. In contrast, bilirubin levels were significantly lower compared to the PHx and PHx + SPx groups (Fig. 3). Taken together, these morphological, immunohistochemical, and biochemical findings indicate that PRP administration enhances liver regeneration and promotes a coordinated recovery process.

Regenerative effects of CGF: morphological, immunohistochemical, and biochemical findings

In the CGF group, an increase in mitotic activity and a higher proportion of binucleated hepatocytes were notable. This increase was more pronounced than in the PHx group but lower than in the PRP group. While vacuolization decreased in hepatocytes, Kupffer cell proliferation and more widespread and regular neovascularization, especially in the portal triad areas, were noted. Additionally, the sinusoidal structures appeared well-organized and dilated (Fig. 1). In the immunohistochemical evaluation, Ki-67 expression in the CGF group was increased in hepatocytes, Kupffer cells, and lymphoid cells. While Ki-67 expression was significantly higher compared to the C and S groups, no statistically significant difference was observed when compared to the PHx and PHx + SPx groups (Fig. 2A). However, Ki-67 expression in the PRP group was significantly higher than in the CGF group. STAT3 expression in the CGF group was substantially higher compared to the S group, but considerably lower than in the PHx group (Fig. 2B). In the biochemical analyses, bilirubin levels in the CGF group were significantly lower than the PHx and PHx + SPx groups (Fig. 3). Taken together, these findings suggest that CGF administration supports liver regeneration through modulation of immune cell activity and improvement of vascular organization, as evidenced by both histological and biochemical data.

Comparative analysis of regeneration across experimental groups

PRP administration emerged as the most effective approach regarding mitotic activity and cellular proliferation during liver regeneration. It supported histological and biochemical recovery by reducing signs of tissue injury. In contrast, CGF administration enhanced immune cell proliferation and organized vascular structures. Notably, neovascularization in the portal areas and the integrity of sinusoidal structures showed marked improvement. Ki-67 expression in the CGF group was observed to be lower compared to the PRP group, while no significant difference was detected in STAT3 expression between the two groups. Both interventions supported liver regeneration but promoted recovery through distinct mechanisms. PRP enhanced cellular proliferation and tissue organization, whereas CGF exerted its effects primarily through immune cell proliferation and vascular remodeling.

Although the CGF group demonstrated a higher mean regeneration rate, this apparent trend did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that the observed differences should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect limited sample size and inter-individual variability rather than a definitive biological effect.

Importantly, when all experimental groups are considered together, functional regeneration data are consistent with histological and biochemical findings, indicating that although the CGF and PRP groups exhibit improved regenerative features and more coordinated structural and cellular responses compared to the PHx group, these differences are not sufficiently robust to produce statistically significant changes in overall liver mass restoration.

Discussion

PHLF is generally managed conservatively; however, in progressive cases, liver transplantation may be required. Small-for-size syndrome (SFSS), which can occur following transplantation, shares a similar pathophysiology with PHLF and is explained by the theory of portal hyperperfusion. A relative increase in portal flow due to reduced parenchymal volume leads to decreased hepatic arterial flow, sinusoidal dilation, increased shear stress, hemorrhagic infiltration, centrilobular necrosis, prolonged cholestasis, impaired synthetic function, and inhibition of cellular proliferation38,39.

The histopathological findings of our study provide structural support for the molecular and proliferative changes observed in the experimental groups. The PHx group demonstrated marked parenchymal injury, including cellular swelling, vacuolization, and microcirculatory alterations, consistent with an intense regenerative response accompanied by tissue damage. In contrast, the intervention groups exhibited relatively improved morphological features. The PHx + SPx group showed more uniform hepatocyte architecture and absence of fibrotic alterations than the PHx group, along with sinusoidal dilation and increased lymphoid cell presence. These findings suggest that the reduction in portal inflow following splenectomy may facilitate a relative enhancement in hepatic arterial flow, potentially mitigating the hepatic arterial buffer response and decreasing sinusoidal shear stress, thereby contributing to a more favorable and sustained regenerative process40,41. The PRP group exhibited increased mitotic activity and reduced cellular injury, as evidenced by decreased vacuolization and preservation of hepatocyte morphology. This pattern supports a strong proliferative response, accompanied by relative structural preservation. The CGF group demonstrated a distinct histological profile, with evidence of neovascularization, Kupffer cell proliferation, and an organized sinusoidal architecture. Although the proliferative activity appeared lower than that of the PRP group, the observed structural features suggest a regenerative response that may involve vascular remodeling and tissue organization. Overall, the absence of histological features suggestive of fibrosis in H&E evaluation compared to the PHx group indicates a shift toward a less injury-dominant regenerative pattern; however, further studies are required to determine the functional implications of these morphological findings. CGF-treated livers exhibited superior cellular morphology, neovascularization, and a more organized and preserved three-dimensional architecture. In contrast to PRP, the unique fibrin scaffold and CD34 + stem cell content of CGF are hypothesized to play a pivotal role in maintaining organized morphological integrity and facilitating neovascularization31.

As an established marker of cellular proliferation, increased Ki-67 expression reflects the transition of hepatocytes from the quiescent G0 phase to the active phases of the cell cycle (G1, S, G2, and M)42. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that Ki-67 expression was significantly increased in all hepatectomized groups. PHx + SPx group showed no clear superiority over the PHx group in terms of Ki-67 expression. Conversely, Eipel et al. reported favorable outcomes with splenectomy in 85% PHx models, including higher Ki-67 expression, increased hepatic oxygen pressure, and greater liver weight. These discrepancies may be related to portal flow dynamics, which increase 2.3-fold following 70% PHx and up to 4-fold after 90% PHx41. These findings suggest that the beneficial effects of splenectomy may be directly related to the extent of the liver resection. In contrast, Liu et al. demonstrated that in groups undergoing 70% PHx and 70% PHx + SPx, Ki-67 expression peaked at 48 h in the splenectomy group and at 72 h in the hepatectomy-only group; however, no significant differences were observed between the groups at later time points43. Also, this suggests that although the total amount of regeneration may not differ significantly, splenectomy may accelerate the regenerative process. In addition, among the groups, expression peaked in the PRP group. The significantly higher Ki-67 proliferation index observed in the PRP group could be attributed to the rapid activation and secretion kinetics of pre-synthesized growth factors. Following the clotting process, the biological effects of PRP are initiated within 10 min, with over 95% of these factors being secreted within the first hour44. This surge in mitogenic signals may facilitate the initiation of the hepatocyte cell cycle. In the CGF group, Ki-67 expression was comparable to that in the PHx and PHx + SPx groups but remained significantly lower than that observed in the PRP group. In contrast to PRP, the differential centrifugation steps utilized in CGF preparation involve constant platelet collisions and ruptures, which further optimize the release profile45. Accordingly, CGF may be interpreted as a fibrin scaffold and an integrated reservoir, suggesting the possibility of a more sustained regenerative stimulus than the potentially more rapid but transient action of PRP. While the literature currently lacks studies investigating CGF in liver regeneration, our findings suggest that CGF may trigger cellular responses maximally over a different timeframe than POD 5.

The spatial and cell-specific localization of Ki-67 expression in our study may provide important insights into the distinct regenerative kinetics and microenvironmental changes associated with each intervention. In the PHx group, the predominance of Ki-67 staining within Kupffer cells may reflect a state of sustained inflammatory priming and mechanical stress, potentially related to ongoing portal hyperperfusion. In contrast, the lack of prominent Kupffer cell presence in the PHx + SPx group on histological evaluation may suggest that splenectomy attenuates sinusoidal shear stress, possibly facilitating a transition from an inflammatory-dominant phase to a more stabilized regenerative state, as supported by the observed intrahepatic lymphoid proliferation. PRP and CGF groups demonstrated more specialized regenerative patterns. While liver regeneration is classically initiated in the periportal regions (Zone 1) and progresses toward the pericentral areas, the predominance of Ki-67 expression in perivenous hepatocytes (Zone 3) in the PRP group on POD 5 may indicate a shift in the spatial dynamics of proliferation. This pattern may be related to the rapid availability of exogenously delivered growth factors. Conversely, the diffuse and multicellular Ki-67 expression observed in the CGF group, encompassing hepatocytes, Kupffer cells, and lymphoid cells, may reflect a more coordinated regenerative response involving not only proliferation but also immune modulation and tissue remodeling. This pattern may be associated with the sustained-release properties of CGF within the fibrin matrix. Taken together, these findings suggest that although all interventions increase proliferative activity, they may differentially influence the spatial distribution and cellular contributors to liver regeneration.

Activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway is a well-established component of the priming phase of liver regeneration, primarily mediated through the IL-6 axis, essential for the transition of hepatocytes from quiescence to cell cycle entry, and a pivotal regulator during liver injury and regeneration46. In a murine model, Li et al. reported that hepatocyte DNA synthesis at 40 h post-hepatectomy was reduced to approximately one-third of the control levels in STAT3-deficient mice47. In our study, the highest STAT3 expression was observed in the PHx group, consistent with a robust physiological priming response following liver resection. Notably, the PHx + SPx group demonstrated the lowest STAT3 activation while maintaining a relatively higher Ki-67 index than the PHx group. The attenuated STAT3 expression observed in the PHx + SPx group may be attributed to the direct removal of the spleen, a systemic source of IL-6, and the simultaneous reduction of portal hyperperfusion-induced shear stress and reduced buffer response48. This alleviation of mechanical stress curtails the stress-mediated release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-6, from the sinusoidal endothelium, thereby downregulating the subsequent JAK/STAT signaling cascade49. In contrast, relatively low STAT3 levels in the PRP and CGF groups, despite evidence of proliferative activity, suggest that hepatocyte proliferation may also be supported by mechanisms that are not exclusively dependent on the classical IL-6/STAT3 pathway. This hypothesis is partly supported by several experimental models that demonstrate the redundancy of regenerative signaling. Moh et al. proposed that hepatocyte proliferation is not exclusively dependent on STAT3, suggesting that compensatory mechanisms, including alternative pathways such as STAT1, may be activated in its absence50. Haga et al. further corroborated this view, demonstrating that liver regeneration in liver-specific STAT3-deficient mice proceeds through hepatocellular hypertrophy mediated by the activation of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) / protein kinase B (PKB) signaling pathway11. Also, it may be hypothesized that the exogenous provision of growth factors in the PRP and CGF groups facilitates a more direct transition into the proliferative phase, may reducing the relative dependence on classical priming mechanisms such as STAT3 activation. However, given that only a single time point and a limited set of signaling pathways were evaluated, this interpretation should be approached with caution, and further studies are required to clarify the temporal dynamics of priming and proliferation in this context.

On the other hand, in addition to its classical role in liver regeneration, STAT3 has also been shown to be involved in processes related to aging and fibrosis. As a result, its role in the liver has become a subject of debate, with reports suggesting that STAT3 may exert profibrotic effects, particularly through hepatic stellate cells (HSCs)51,52. In repeated or chronic liver injury, quiescent HSCs become activated and differentiate into myofibroblasts that secrete extracellular matrix (ECM) components; this leads to fibrotic healing and excessive, persistent ECM accumulation instead of balanced regenerative repair53. Additionally, in cases of severe acute liver injury, hepatocyte senescence, which is characterized by a persistent cell cycle arrest, is strongly induced54–56. Pang et al. reported that STAT3 induces hepatocyte senescence and may serve as a biomarker for cellular aging in liver fibrosis57.

Considering the opposing roles of STAT3 in liver regeneration and hepatocyte senescence, the lower STAT3 expression observed in the CGF group may suggest that the high concentration of growth factors delivered via CGF could have mitigated the senescence-inducing effects of STAT3, thereby supporting regeneration. However, this hypothesis requires validation through further experimental studies.

In conclusion, although STAT3 is recognized as a key pathway initiating liver regeneration, there are also studies demonstrating its opposing effects. In our research, while high proliferation was observed in the PRP and CGF groups based on Ki-67 expression, similar results were not reflected in STAT3 analyses. These findings suggest that the magnitude of STAT3 activation may not directly correlate with proliferative activity at later time points, highlighting the complexity and redundancy of regenerative signaling networks. This discrepancy suggests that the effects of STAT3 may be context and time-dependent, highlighting the need for further research focusing on the role of STAT3 in liver regeneration.

Regarding the clinical potential of regenerative biological agents, although PRP and CGF applications have not yet become part of routine practice, their ease of preparation and autologous nature contribute to a low side effect profile. These features make them promising candidates for preventing or treating PHLF in LDLT donors, extensive resections for benign indications, and major hepatectomies performed in tumor patients. However, further studies involving different postoperative time points and molecular biomarkers are needed to clarify the effects of CGF on liver regeneration.

Conclusions

In this experimental study, both PRP and CGF applications were shown to support liver regeneration following 70% hepatectomy; however, these effects appeared to be mediated through distinct biological characteristics. PRP administration was associated with a proliferative response, as reflected by increased Ki-67 expression, whereas CGF application was characterized by a more organized histological architecture, enhanced neovascularization, and more balanced biochemical findings. Splenectomy appeared to modulate the regenerative microenvironment without markedly increasing proliferative indices at the evaluated time point.

Both treatment approaches contributed to the regenerative process through different aspects. Immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated that Ki-67 and STAT3 expression play roles in liver regeneration and proliferative processes. The elevated Ki-67 expression observed in the PRP group supports a more pronounced proliferative activity in this group. In contrast, CGF application was associated with findings related to vascular organization and immune cell responses.

These findings indicate that PRP and CGF support liver regeneration through distinct cellular processes. Different regenerative strategies may influence the structural and temporal characteristics of liver regeneration. Further studies incorporating multiple time points and advanced molecular analyses are needed to better elucidate these effects.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was limited to a single POD 5, which did not fully capture the dynamics of liver regeneration. Second, the experimental design relied primarily on histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation, limited to only two markers, Ki-67 and STAT3 expression, without assessing phosphorylated STAT3 or broader molecular pathways. Third, fibrosis, apoptosis, and necrosis were not evaluated using specific histochemical or quantitative methods; therefore, the findings were based on qualitative observations. In addition, cell-specific identification in immunohistochemical analyses was based on morphological features rather than on double staining. Furthermore, direct measurements of portal hemodynamics and comprehensive functional parameters such as coagulation indices were not included. Finally, this study is limited to an animal model. Advanced clinical studies are necessary to determine the applicability and validity of the findings in humans.
