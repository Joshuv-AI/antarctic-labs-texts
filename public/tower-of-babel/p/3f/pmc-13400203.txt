“I need to find ways, and I've not yet been successful”: Exploring adaptive challenges and adaptive leadership as experienced by patients with gastrointestinal malignancy
Authors: Michael Sanatani, Morgan Black, Elena Tsvetkova, Kyra Harris-Schulz, Shiraz Malik, Shannon Sibbald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the advent of shared-decision making, person-centered care, and early palliative care, many oncology patients and oncologists may face challenges that are not easily solved within these existing paradigms. Adaptive healthcare has been proposed as a framework with which to understand complex problems and guide physician actions. However, it is unknown to what extent patients would endorse this framework as applicable to their own experience.MethodSemi-structured interviews were conducted with patients selected through purposive sampling. Interviews were analyzed both deductively using Heifetz’ adaptive leadership framework, and inductively for new insights in a qualitative descriptive approach.ResultsTwelve interviews were conducted. Despite good conceptual understanding of adaptive challenges, some participants struggled to analyze own experiences using this lens. Nonetheless, numerous adaptive challenges and responses to them were identified.InnovationDivergent from the original adaptive leadership framework, some patients deliberately chose not to adapt in some domains as a means of maintaining autonomy in the midst of upheaval. Our results suggest that consideration of patient autonomy be integrated into the adaptive healthcare if it is to be applied to oncology.ConclusionsPatients with advanced colon or pancreatic cancer reported facing numerous adaptive challenges, confirming the applicability of the conceptual framework to the patient experience. The highly individual nature of the adaptive challenges faced, and the individual variability in responses to challenges, calls for oncologists to carefully explore challenges faced by their patients, as well as patients' preferred way to address them, in order to optimize and individualize adaptive.Keywords: Adaptive challenges, Adaptation, Patient support, Coping, Leadership

Introduction

The concept of adaptive work and leadership has been applied to healthcare primarily in one of two ways: as a guide for understanding, or intervening in, a clinical scenario from the point of view of the healthcare provider, or as a lens through which to examine healthcare systems. Thygeson et al. [1] proposed the use of the adaptive leadership framework to help healthcare address some of the gaps between its aspirations and its reality. They envisioned an improvement in the doctor-patient relationship, increased patient autonomy, and a reduction in the over-use of costly technical services (in the futile pursuit of trying to solve adaptive challenges by technical means). Bailey et al. described an adaptive challenge in healthcare as any situation where there exists “disparity between the capabilities of familiar methods, habits, or values and the demands of the present [and future] clinical circumstances” [2]. Adaptive healthcare has been used as a lens to examine physician-patient interactions in various challenging situations, including end-of-life discussions in the intensive care unit [3] chronic illness [4], and discussions around hepatitis care [5]. McKechnie et al. [6] interviewed parents of children born with life-threatening conditions and used the adaptive leadership framework to make sense of the parents' struggles to adapt to the new reality of caring for their children. These studies also focus on the need for both healthcare providers and patients to undergo adaptive work in such scenarios, shining a spotlight on issues around physician self-awareness which could play a significant role in how challenging situations are addressed. Kuluski et al., synthesizing the existing literature, have developed a pragmatic translation of Heifetz’ original adaptive leadership actions to specific components of person-centred care. For example, “identifying and discussing the underlying reasons for people's resistance to change”, was mapped to “explicitly asking about fears and concerns, demonstrating compassion and empathy” [7].

However, a gap exists around the patient perspective on the processes articulated by this framework, specifically in oncology. It is not known if patients see adaptation in the same way that oncologists and palliative care providers do. For instance, Anderson et al. assert that “the aim of adaptive leadership is to facilitate the patient/family to do the work necessary to close the gap created by adaptive challenges by encompassing a holistic approach to patients within their life contexts” [4]. However, if adaptive healthcare and adaptive leadership are to be effective instruments in person-centred care, their application should ideally be based on a deep understanding of how the people being cared for view adaptive work, adaptive challenges, and adaptive leadership. Understanding how patients themselves experience and conceptualize the challenges they face is arguably just as important as framing these challenges as adaptive or technical from the viewpoint of the physician, if our goal is to support patients in the best way possible through the challenges they experience. Ten years ago, Anderson et al. called for “collaborative work” as a component of adaptive healthcare, and patient-physician collaboration in this sense can only be furthered by a deepened understanding of the patient perspective.

We therefore explored how patients with gastrointestinal malignancies viewed and experienced the challenges they faced during their illness, using the adaptive healthcare framework. Our research questions were:

(1)Which experiences do patients with colorectal or pancreatic cancer perceive as adaptive challenges during their illness journey?

Material and methods

Methodology, epistemology, and study design

This qualitative descriptive study was grounded in a constructivist epistemology, whereby social meaning is co-created though the interaction between researcher and subject [8]. The positionality of the interviewers was considered as contributing to the data development, as the interviewers were physicians involved in cancer care, but not the research participants' individual physicians. This was a deliberate choice as it was hoped interviewers then would be best positioned to explore the challenges faced by patients. The follow-up questions asked by the interviewers in the semi-structured interviews were likely guided by their experience as physicians.

Reflexivity

The research team comprised a multidisciplinary group, primarily clinicians (palliative care physicians SM, KHS and oncologists MS, ET) alongside a researcher with advanced training and expertise in qualitative methodology. The lead author had prior familiarity with the adaptive leadership framework, which informed its use as an initial sensitizing concept. Consistent with a constructivist approach, the team engaged in ongoing reflexive practice, recognizing that clinical training and prior assumptions could shape data collection and interpretation. Regular team discussions were used to examine these influences, particularly in relation to coding decisions and framework development. The inclusion of diverse disciplinary perspectives and methodological expertise supported critical reflection.

Recruitment

Patients were recruited at a tertiary cancer care centre (London Health Sciences Centre in London, Canada). Participants were selected and approached by MB using purposive sampling of patients [9] with colorectal or pancreatic cancer to ensure broad representation of patients with both curable and incurable illnesses, and who were at various phases of the illness journey. The study excluded participants who could not converse in English or were under 18.

Data collection

Patients were familiarized with the adaptive leadership framework in a separate interview and provided graphic materials (appendix 1). The framework was presented as one possible way for participants to start thinking about adaptive challenges, however, interviewers also explicitly encouraged participants to describe challenges in their own words. Probing questions further helped explore participants' interpretations of the challenges they experience. Data collection interviews were carried out virtually and then recorded and transcribed with Microsoft Teams and sonix.ai, and proofread for accuracy by MB. Interview guides were iteratively developed and refined based on interviewee feedback and researcher experience with the existing questions [10]. Any changes made were documented as part of the audit trail. Interviewers were MS, KHS, and SM.

Sampling

Sampling was guided by the principle of data sufficiency [2], [11], [12], [13] whereby data collection and analysis proceeded iteratively until additional interviews did not contribute substantively new insights to the developing themes or refinement of the adaptive leadership framework [2], [11], [12], [13]. Consistent with qualitative descriptive methodology, a priori sample size calculation was not applied.

Data analysis

Data was analyzed using an integrative analytic approach, combining framework analysis with thematic analysis within a qualitative descriptive methodology. Interview transcripts were anonymized and analyzed both deductively and inductively, each by two authors (two of SS, KHS, SM, MS).

Coding combined deductive and inductive approaches. An initial structure informed by the adaptive leadership framework guided early coding, while open coding allowed new concepts to emerge directly from the data. Coding was conducted in iterative batches of transcripts. Codes were compared and grouped based on conceptual similarity.

The deductive analysis initially utilized Heifetz et al.'s original adaptive leadership process framework to guide early iterative coding. This was done for 3–4 interviews at a time, after which the research team met to discuss and refine the framework until consensus, based on insights gathered from the analysis.

Inductively, open thematic coding was used simultaneously (both semantic and latent) to identify patterns across participant accounts and allow new concepts to emerge. The evolving themes of this integrated analysis were discussed by the research team at regular intervals. [14], [15].

This iterative process allowed emerging data from participant accounts to expand and refine the framework. Rather than strictly applying the adaptive leadership framework, analysis sought to explore how participants' experiences extended the framework. The modified framework was then used going forward for the next interviews in a cyclical nature until subsequent interviews did not yield any more perspectives which suggested a change in the iteratively modified adaptive leadership framework. [14], [15]

This study was approved by Western's Human Subjects Research Ethics Board. All participants provided informed consent before participation. Confidentiality was ensured by de-identifying transcripts.

Results

Seventeen patients had been screened (participant numbers in the text are based upon patients approached), and a total of 12 interviews were conducted with consenting patients. Participants included 8 patients with colorectal cancer and 4 with pancreatic cancer, mean age was 58.5 years, 8:4 male/female split, and 1 stage II patient, 1 stage III patient and 10 stage IV patients.

Research question 1: which experiences are perceived as adaptive challenges?

All participants indicated that they understood the concept of an adaptive challenge, as opposed to a technical one, after being provided with explanatory diagrams and definitions prior to the interviews. However, some still then found it difficult to use Heifetz's adaptive leadership framework to analyze their own experiences.

Deeper exploration by the interviewers of potential adaptive challenges identified a wider range of adaptive challenges. As indicated in Table 1, adaptive challenges fell into two categories, deemed collective if mentioned by several participants, and distinct (arising from patient-specific habits, strengths, or attitudes). It is possible that in a larger sample population, adaptive challenges labelled “distinct” in our study, may be experienced by multiple patients. Their connection to very specific patient contexts, conditions, and personal patient dispositions, however, suggest their identification requires specific inquiry and reflection.

Adaptive challenges identified.

Collective adaptive challenges reflect a complex interplay of adaptive challenges, responses, and leadership dynamics. Participants consistently described the personal nature of coming to terms with life-changing diagnoses. Many articulated a profound emotional journey characterized by grief, denial, and anger before reaching any semblance of acceptance. One recurring theme was the fear of losing autonomy and becoming dependent on others, reflecting how illness threatened participants' core identities and sense of self-determination.

The complexity of the healthcare system itself presented a flashpoint for experiences which could be identified as adaptive challenges. Participants expressed confusion about the roles of various healthcare professionals, and frustration with systemic inefficiencies and having to wait. Many described prolonged waits for appointments, challenges in communication regarding treatment plans, and difficulties accessing alternative treatment options. Patients coming from positions of control, or having always been efficient, found it especially difficult to adjust to delays and being passive.

The psychological and emotional impact of illness extended beyond participants to encompass their loved ones. Many described a delicate balancing act—managing their own suffering while simultaneously attempting to shield family members from distress. This frequently led to shifting family dynamics, particularly when previously independent individuals found themselves increasingly reliant on caregivers and struggled with assuming the new role.

Perhaps most profound was the challenge of redefining personal identity and purpose. Participants described the loss of identities tied to professional roles, caregiving responsibilities, or physical capabilities. As illness progressed, many struggled to find new ways to remain engaged and maintain a sense of purpose when their physical capacities diminished.

Modifications to the adaptive leadership framework

Mixed technical/adaptive scenarios

While technical and adaptive challenges, and work, were originally conceived of as a binary classification, it became clear that the patient experience was more nuanced. Certain situations combined both technical difficulties and required inner transformation. For example, this clergyman tried to address physical weakness with physical supports, but also inwardly struggled with the meaning of having to apply a technical solution:

“And it meant a lot to me and I think it meant a lot to them for me to perform this baptism of the newest little member of their family. Well, I found on that day that I was so weak and so ill that I couldn't even hold the baby. There was a weight that was a technical issue. There was an easy technical solution that, you know, seemed, I think, transparent to the congregation because it was just something that I asked for in an adjustment to the liturgy. The parents would hold the baby while I poured the water in and all that kind of stuff. So that was a nice, easy solution. The hard part was for me, I, I literally went back to my office and cried because I wasn't able to to do that the way that I had been used to and wanted to do.” (P 8).

Non-adapting (“Protective reformation”)

Another significant difference noted was around instances where adaptive work, the learning of new skills and outlook, did not occur. This appeared to fall into two scenarios: either the participants were able to adjust to their new circumstances with pre-existing skills and approaches, or they chose to maintain consistent personal narratives and routines for the sake of maintaining a constant in a changing lifeworld. This participant describes a pre-existing realist mindset which was protective:

“I've always been kind of a realist like. Like I've had my CAT scan yesterday and after three months of chemo and people say to me, oh, you know, are you hoping it's going to be okay? I don't think that way…. It is what it is. I can't change what it is. So, whatever it is, I will deal with that” (P 3).

In contrast, this participant acknowledged that an adjustment of his role could have been undertaken by him, but he chose to maintain his routine without denying the threat it was under:

“I think for the most part, I choose to leave it and just pretend if necessary. And it's not a big pretense, but it's, you know, just pretend that I'm not sick and I do all the things that I would do as a normal retired person affected by a sort of this physical condition. So, I mean, I maintained my household activities. I still do all the cooking.” (P 8).

In adaptive leadership, there is focus on identifying resistance to change. For some patients with advanced GI malignancies, resistance to change in the face of life-threatening disease was reported, in contrast, as a coping mechanism.

Adaptive leadership by the healthcare team and others

In exploring how others impacted participants' adaptive work and engaged with the challenges the patients faced, it was remarkable how few reports there were of oncologists explicitly addressing transformation and challenges per se. Recognizing the relativist research paradigm, we note that this is the truth as understood by the patients, and as relayed to the research team, who were not the patients' physicians. Nonetheless, there were several behaviours which patients recognized as being helpful in supporting them through challenges indirectly (Table 1).

Many participants acknowledged the impact of their oncologist's communication on how prepared they felt for the road ahead. Participants used information provided by the oncologist to help them work through decisions and challenges. Any absence of preparation added to the challenges patients faced, because they experienced the uncertainty (“sense of disequilibrium”) and did not know the significance of what they observed.

“Because when that started, it worried us all to hell. Right. And it's like everything, every bump in the road kind of scared us a bit. … Uh, where I don't want to use Doctor Google. You know, but, sometimes he's very helpful.” (P 17).

The “holding environment” in the adaptive leadership framework refers to the creation of a space which is simultaneously safe and controlled but also where the participants are confronted with the issues they are to address. This mapped directly to the clinic visits, as patients reported being confronted with the facts which forced them to consider new ways forward.

“And she said, ‘You know, you can do that if you like, but if you do and something happens, know that no insurance company will cover you. And that's going to be a challenge.’ And we kind of thought about that and said, well, we're now on a fixed income and probably it wouldn't make sense” (P 8).

Another participant recognized the oncologist's role in confronting him with the implications of his diagnosis in a responsible way:

“Dr. _____ is more concerned about the condition than other docs. You know, they will get fairly positive. Almost the same kind of reaction that I have, which is to say the cancer is just there is not an issue. So we'll just let it be there and not get too excited about it. Dr. ______, on the other hand, resists.” (P8).

Patient family and friends also provided adaptive leadership in this sense of a safe holding area where the challenges were discussed and worked on:

“He has this unique ability to to bring me back to the present. So he creates a space where I am able to, I can say any fears, any thoughts without any sort of judgment. If I want to talk about dying. Okay, let's sit, let's sit down. Let's open that conversation and and talk about it” (P16).

Kindness and approachability made patients feel they had a resource to help them navigate both inner and outer challenges, whereas the lack of a supportive relationship with their physician, similar to the absence of explanations, made patients feel alone. The engagement of the physician (“Deploy yourself” aspect of the original framework) could determine whether they were experienced as supportive:

“The bedside manner was completely lacking, and it really was just sort of a cold. Can't do this. We're not going to try. And I felt like a statistic”.(P16).

“But I noticed the biggest thing that they, they did, and I was really appreciative of it, is they listened. Um, so it wasn't always about maybe what they had proposed or what they had thought”(P13).

Both the healthcare team and others in the patients' social circle contributed to a sense of stability, which helped them feel less overwhelmed by all the imminent changes:

“My life experience has said to me: People don't need a stable house, but they need a stable connection somewhere. Whether it's a school, whether it's a church, whether it's going to a friend's house. Yeah, okay. And that stability can get them through the hard part” (P1).

Inductive findings

Through thematic analysis of participant interviews several additional themes became apparent which were present throughout the data set.

Reliance on technical healthcare

Some participants placed complete trust in medical interventions while actively resisting discussions about palliative care or quality-of-life considerations. This sometimes manifested as seeking multiple medical opinions without addressing the underlying need to accept certain limitations of their condition.

Delegation

An undercurrent of delegation existed where participants entrusted healthcare system navigation entirely to family members, avoiding personal engagement in critical decision-making processes.

Personal growth

Perhaps most striking was the emotional growth described by some participants. Many found new meaning through mentorship, education, or focusing on meaningful time with loved ones. Engagement with supportive communities—including online forums, support groups, and inspirational resources—helped many reframe their experiences in a more positive light. Several participants emphasized the importance of cultivating humor, gratitude, and appreciation of small daily joys.

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

In this study examining the cancer experience through the lens of adaptive healthcare, patients revealed the breadth of challenges they face. In applying the adaptive leadership framework to GI oncology, there were several areas where the framework appeared to be directly applicable to the patient experience and the healthcare team actions. However, there were also several areas of discrepancy, some due to the different nature of healthcare compared to a corporate situation, and some possibly related to the fact the situation was examined only from the patients' perspective.

While adaptive challenges assumed to be faced by patients have been previously articulated [2], [11], [12], [13], our results highlight a significant variability in patients' views around adaptive challenges, adaptive work, and adaptive leadership. Not only were the challenges experienced as adaptive very individually specific, but the attitude towards adaptation in general was also heterogeneous. Many of the challenges identified by patients as adaptive were so individual, that they could not possibly have been predicted by the oncologist without specific inquiry of the patient. This suggests that in order to apply the adaptive leadership framework to person-centred care, it is vital that it is explicitly explored with the patient which challenges they face for which their existing coping repertoire is insufficient.

In contrast to other recent qualitative studies examining patient experiences using the adaptive leadership framework [16], [17], we chose to first familiarize participants with the difference between technical and adaptive challenges explicitly. We acknowledge that this may have impacted the challenges relayed to the study team, however, we were interested in exploring how the adaptive challenge framework would be applied by patients as they reflected on their own journey. Understanding this may inform future research around operationalizing adaptive leadership as communication and counselling tool, not just a research lens. Indeed, while participants in our study could all clearly describe the differences between adaptive and technical challenges in theory, most participants struggled to discern which of their difficult experiences called for technical solutions and which would require difficult learning. This suggests that starting with the lived experience of a challenge, not further specified or categorized, might be a more reliable way to initiate conversations around adaptation – even if the oncologist is using an adaptive healthcare lens in the conversation. Patients may not immediately recognize which challenges could be overcome by adapting and new learning, and which areas it is important to maintain as is. An initial open discussion around this may enable adaptive leadership and work to focus on areas of priority for the patient.

The literature on stress appraisal and coping in cancer may be relevant to the themes uncovered in our study. Patient responses to cancer involve non-linear processes of appraisal and coping [18], [19], [20]. Appraisal consists of attaching personal significance to the stressor as representing harm/loss, future threat, or challenge to be overcome. Coping follows appraisal, including various strategies directed at the source of stress (problem-focused) or the psychological distress (emotion-focused) associated with the stressor. An alternate way of viewing coping responses emphasizes the direction of response towards (approach) or away (avoidant) from the stressor [21]. Certain strategies such as taking direct action, seeking social support, and acceptance are generally seen in the literature as positive (adaptive). Maladaptive strategies include denial and disengagement [22]. Franks and Roesch found that patients who perceived their cancer as a threat, harm/loss, or challenge were more likely to use problem-based, avoidant, and approach-oriented strategies respectively [23]. In our study, patients describing experiences that fell under the “delegation” theme may have viewed their cancer as a harm/loss and accordingly inclined to adopt maladaptive coping such as distancing or disengagement. Patients who described adaptive challenges that followed the themes of “personal growth” and “reliance on technical healthcare”, may have appraised their cancer as a threat and challenge. Accordingly, they would have adopted problem-based and approach-oriented strategies such as positive reinterpretation, medical compliance, and active problem solving. Avoidant and emotional focused coping styles such as denial, disengagement, and distancing, are associated with worsening quality of life and increased anxiety, and depression [23]. These patients are more likely in need of adaptive leadership versus patients who are employing adaptive coping strategies.

Innovation

As the goal of the adaptive leadership framework in its original context is unequivocally to induce movement in a certain direction, the absence of adaptive work in the face of what would appear to be an adaptive challenge would be considered an obstacle. Initially, the framework assumed a linear progression of adaptation, where patients recognized challenges, engaged in problem-solving, and ultimately adjusted to new health realities [23]. However, participant narratives illustrated noticeable departures from this model. As outlined in Table 2, several individuals described phases of deliberate non-adaptation, where maintaining pre-existing routines or resisting imposed changes were experienced as beneficial. Patients described routines as a vital anchor amidst the destabilizing experience of cancer affecting multiple domains of their lives. These cases challenge the prevailing assumption in adaptive healthcare literature [2], [7], [12] that patients must always adapt to optimize their care experiences – and brings up a response which is not considered in the original adaptive leadership framework, where it is assumed that change is beneficial. Although due to the relativist research paradigm we can make no claim to broad transferability across contexts different from our healthcare centre and study team, the conviction with which our respondents emphasized how much non-adaptation meant to them confirms this as an important finding. Non-adaptation could represent the use of coping strategies such as “maintaining normal routines”. This is probably not denial, as participants fully recognize elements within their lifeworld which they choose to keep unchanged while fully acknowledging the threat to personhood that cancer represents.

Lending additional credence to the relevance of non-adaptation in the context of challenges and coping, the Dual Process Model (DPM) of Coping with Bereavement and the closely related Two-track Model of Bereavement are built around analogous findings in the grief setting of loss. These theories highlight the simultaneous adherence to fixed memories and bonds, and a more searching, adaptive process around finding a way forward [24]. It may well be that the original adaptive leadership framework, when applied to oncology, should be expanded to a “two track adaptive response” framework. Just as the DPM model has led to novel counselling approaches, our finding of deliberate non-adaptation could inform new ways for oncologists to support patients through change, if this finding is consistently demonstrated in future research studies.

One area we did not explore was patients' sense of identity in relation to their illness trajectory. Seeing oneself as a ‘survivor’, independent of cancer recurrence, may lead to a different attitude towards challenges – both technical and adaptive – than if one acknowledges imminent end of life. Creating distance to one's illness has been described as one strategy in maintaining a “survivor” mindset, and this may impact patients' willingness to engage in adaptation as well [25], [26]. Conversely, “attention reorientation”, as described in a framework describing breast cancer patients' re-alignment of their identity and the realities of their illness challenges, could also be seen as a form of adaptive work, albeit focused on meaning and purpose [27].

The element of patient autonomy and identity thus appears to be an innovative modification as the adaptive leadership framework is applied to person-centred care. Contrary to the existing adaptive healthcare literature, which tends to be somewhat physician-centric and assumes that adaptation is inherently beneficial, our findings indicate that deliberate non-adaptation may reflect necessary coping strategies that serve a protective or empowering function. Some participants described moments where resisting change allowed them to maintain stability and a sense of control over their health journey. The innovative insight our study provides is that non-adapting could be seen as a positive coping strategy, even if one considers the patient-oncologist interaction through the lens of adaptive leadership. Oncologist and patient can both explore the illness experience faced by the patient using the concept of adaptive healthcare in order to gain deeper understanding of the unique challenges faced by the patient, but the next step should possibly not be a universal approach of supporting adaptation around every challenge. Rather, our data suggest that a differentiated view would be experienced as most supportive. Instead of assuming the immediate task is to support adaptation, our findings suggest the first step should be exploring patients' lived experience of challenges, and engaging in discussion around which domains are ready for new learning, and which should be maintained. Having understood the adaptive challenges faced by this particular patient in their context, the next step should be a discussion: ‘Which challenges should we work on together? And where do you wish to hold onto your existing skillset, employing it to your best ability, as you have always done, as an anchor in times of overwhelming change?’

We therefore suggest that to facilitate operationalizing adaptive healthcare in the oncology setting, the three key components of adaptive leadership could indeed inform supportive counselling by oncologists, but while deliberately searching for and noticing opportunities for patient autonomy and “dual-track adaptation/non-adaptation” to occur. Observation of patient or family description of psychological distress offers a first step. Curiosity regarding patient appraisal and coping can aid interpretation. Are patients viewing their cancer as a threat or challenge? What adaptive challenges are they uniquely struggling with? Are they investing in medical solutions, supportive relationships? Where might non-adapting and celebration of an existing skillset or view actually be preferable to a change, for this particular patient? Intervention entails creating a holding space where compassionate communication can help address malignant beliefs and behaviours and discuss unique challenges and how they will be addressed. This approach may help providers and patients navigate the central adaptive challenge to identity and personhood posed by cancer.

Limitations

Although the interviewers were physicians, they were not the patient's primary physicians. Participants may not have felt comfortable disclosing details they might be concerned could reach their own physicians. This possibility is supported by the observation that when the interviewers asked their own patients very similar questions as part of routine care within a physician-patient relationship, it appeared to be much easier for patients to articulate their struggles. The positionality of the researchers may thus have limited the depth of disclosure.

Another limitation stems from the dual task requested of participants to first understand the adaptive challenge construct and then examine their own journey reflexively through that lens. While the framework was presented as a guiding construct to assist participants in articulating their experiences, and interviewers encouraged further reflection, this may have limited the breadth of examples reported. If adaptive healthcare is to be integrated into person-centred care, in-depth education around the constructs involved will be needed both for physicians and patients.

The sample is another potential limitation to consider, as it included a higher proportion of participants with advanced-stage disease. Although this reflects our clinical population and aligns with the study focus on adaptive challenges, and purposive sampling ensured inclusion of varied diagnoses and illness trajectories, it may also shape the transferability of findings.

Finally, the oncologist perspective was not explored. It may well be that oncologists see adaptive leadership as a relevant task, or have experienced adaptive work themselves, perhaps even being led by patients – as adaptive work is certainly bidirectional, and there is increasing evidence that oncologists struggle with challenges as well such as maintaining hope or addressing futility, and may learn from patients in this regard [28], [29].

Conclusions

Participants in this single-centre study of patients with gastrointestinal malignancies experienced and recognized adaptive challenges, and certain actions of their oncologists and social circle could be seen as aligned with elements of the adaptive leadership framework and adaptive work being done. However, the individually variable way in which participants experienced challenges in the context of their overall cancer situation suggests that simultaneously acknowledging patient autonomy may also be crucial. The reported importance of selectively choosing areas within which the patient may choose not to adapt even though the physician might think it advisable, should possibly inform enacted adaptive leadership in the oncology domain more broadly. Future research should explore connections between adaptive leadership theory and existing survivorship, identity, and coping frameworks in cancer care, to optimize and individualize supports oncologists can provide their patients as they navigate challenges together. In addition, future studies should explore the patient experience of adaptive challenges, adaptation, and deliberate non-adaptation in other disease sites and healthcare settings.
