Impending rupture in the apparent healing phase after chimney endovascular aneurysm repair for a Bacillus Calmette-Guérin-related paravisceral infective aortic aneurysm
Authors: Takumi Miwa, Takuro Shirasu, Satoshi Kitaura, Masaya Sano, Toshio Takayama, Katsuyuki Hoshina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe a 75-year-old man with a paravisceral infective native aortic aneurysm secondary to intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin therapy for bladder cancer. He initially underwent chimney endovascular aneurysm repair without antituberculosis therapy. After referral to our hospital, Mycobacterium bovis was isolated from retroperitoneal abscess and antituberculosis multidrug therapy was started. Despite clinical improvement and radiological appearance of abscess liquefaction, a computed tomography scan at 3 months demonstrated endograft migration and rapid enlargement of the aneurysm. Emergent total radical excision of infected paravisceral aorta, all endografts and retroperitoneal abscess was performed with extra-anatomical revascularization, with no evidence of reinfection to date.Keywords: Bacillus Calmette-Guérin, Infective native aortic aneurysm, Chimney endovascular aneurysm repair, Left medial visceral rotation, Antituberculosis therapy

Infective native aortic aneurysms (INAAs) after intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) therapy for bladder cancer are rare.1,2 We report a paravisceral INAA owing to Mycobacterium bovis BCG initially treated with chimney endovascular aneurysm repair (Ch-EVAR) that required radical open resection after aneurysm destabilization during the apparent healing phase.

Case report

A 75-year-old man with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer had received intravesical BCG instillations 4 years before presentation, with no history of immunosuppressive therapy or immune deficiency. Because he developed chills during the first treatment, subsequent treatments were carried out with epirubicin. Two years later, a follow-up computed tomography (CT) scan revealed periaortic fat stranding around the paravisceral aorta (Fig 1A and B), which had appeared normal previously. After 3.5 years, CT scan at the referring hospital demonstrated a saccular juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm 32 × 20 mm in diameter with inflammatory changes (Fig 1, C). At that time, infective and autoimmune-related etiologies were considered; however, comprehensive evaluation, including repeated blood and urine cultures, T-SPOT, sputum acid-fast bacilli testing, and autoimmune markers were consistently negative, and the aneurysm was presumed noninfectious.

Ch-EVAR was selected considering the short and diseased proximal neck. A proximal aortic cuff was deployed below the superior mesenteric artery (SMA); a covered stent graft was placed in the left renal artery, and a bare-metal chimney stent was deployed in the right renal artery after inadvertent ostial coverage. Antituberculosis therapy was not given, and the postoperative course was uneventful. Inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein, remained below 1.0 mg/dL. Six months after Ch-EVAR, surveillance CT scan revealed progressive enlargement of the periaortic soft-tissue mass and new retroperitoneal abscesses (Fig 1, D). Because no definitive treatment was considered there, the patient was referred to our tertiary center.

Based on the clinical course and imaging, we regarded a BCG-related INAA as likely and performed open biopsy and abscess debridement. Intraoperatively, firm caseous abscess tissue and whitish, thickened lymphatic channels were noted (Fig 2). Samples from the abscess cavity were positive for M bovis BCG on polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing and culture, confirming a BCG-related INAA.

Findings on surgical biopsy. (A) Open surgical biopsy revealed a thick whitened lymphatic channel (circle). (B) Periaortic abscess was resected (dashed circle).

Triple antituberculosis therapy with isoniazid, rifampicin, and ethambutol was initiated. Although the patient remained afebrile and inflammatory markers improved, serial CT scans showed enlargement and liquefaction of the retroperitoneal abscesses. Radical resection of the residual abscess was planned. However, contrast-enhanced CT scan 3 months after antituberculosis therapy initiation demonstrated a new type Ia endoleak and stent graft migration with rapid aneurysm expansion from 39 × 24 mm to 48 × 32 mm in 1 month, suggesting impending rupture (Fig 3). Therefore, on the same day as the CT scan emergency surgery was undertaken, instead of a two-stage surgery of reconstruction and graft excision for fear of rupture during the interval.

Computed tomography (CT) images of abdominal aorta after chimney endovascular aneurysm repair (Ch-EVAR). (A) Right after Ch-EVAR. (B) Six months after Ch-EVAR and 2 months after initiation of antituberculosis therapy showed distal neck enlargement. (E) Serial CT scans showed enlargement and liquefaction of the retroperitoneal abscesses.

The operation consisted of a left axillofemoral bypass followed by midline laparotomy and radical resection of the infected paravisceral aorta and all endografts. Using left medial visceral rotation, the entire length of the abdominal aorta from the supraceliac level to the iliac arteries was exposed. To avoid mesenteric and renal ischemia, the SMA and left renal artery were reconstructed with great saphenous vein grafts, using left common iliac artery as the inflow site (Fig 4, A). The supraceliac aorta was then clamped, and the origins of the SMA and both renal arteries were resected en bloc to the level of the inferior mesenteric artery. The right renal artery was sacrificed because it was involved in the retroperitoneal abscess. After right-sided medial visceral rotation, extensive debridement of the retrorenal retroperitoneal abscess was performed. Last, the omentoplasty was performed to the retroperitoneum. Total operative time was 12 hours, and blood loss was 4325 mL. Mycobacterial cultures for the abscess tissue, aortic wall, and explanted stent grafts were negative (Fig 4, B-D).

The postoperative course was complicated by retroperitoneal hemorrhage owing to systemic unfractionated heparinization requiring vasopressor support, along with acute kidney injury necessitating 10 days of hemodiafiltration and ischemic colitis requiring a diverting transverse colostomy, and cardiopulmonary arrest owing to airway obstruction, but he ultimately recovered without permanent neurological deficits. Six months after surgery, a CT scan demonstrated no residual abscess or aneurysm, and the patient was transferred to a rehabilitation facility. The Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of the University of Tokyo approved the current study (approval number 3316-(6), approval date 1/26/2024). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient regarding case presentation.

Discussion

Intravesical BCG instillation is an established adjuvant therapy for non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer, but systemic complications such as infective aortic aneurysm can be fatal.3 BCG may reach the aortic wall through hematogenous dissemination, lymphatic spread, or contiguous extension.4 In our case, an early CT scan suggested a hematogenous route, whereas thickened lymphatic ducts at biopsy suggested lymphatic involvement.

Identification of causative agents is important in INAAs treatment.5 However, it is often challenging in BCG-related INAA owing to slow disease progresses and frequently negative blood or urine cultures.1 Although PCR may be useful, specimens are rarely obtained during endovascular intervention.6 In our patient, the aortic wall was not sampled during Ch-EVAR, leading to misclassification as noninfectious and delayed antituberculosis therapy.

Optimal management of BCG-related INAA remains controversial. Open surgery with radical debridement and in situ or extra-anatomical reconstruction is considered definitive, but carries substantial operative risks.7 Endovascular repair offers an alternative in high-risk patients by avoiding thoracoabdominal aortic clamping and enabling rapid rupture control8; however, persistent or recurrent infection and the need for secondary open conversion are common.9,10 In our case, a more extensive endovascular approach might have achieved better proximal sealing, but inadequate infection control and large abscess cavities mandated radical debridement. We, therefore, selected open supramesenteric aortic resection with extra-anatomical axillofemoral bypass and autologous visceral revascularization, accepting substantial perioperative morbidity in exchange for complete removal of infected tissue.

A distinctive feature of this case is the timing and mechanism of aneurysm destabilization. Previous reports have mainly associated rupture or progression with uncontrolled infection.9 In contrast, our patient appeared to improve clinically under antituberculosis therapy, and inflammatory changes on CT scans did not clearly worsen. Impending rupture occurred where the native aortic wall had been replaced by abscess tissue at open biopsy. By the time of radical surgery, the abscess had become liquefied, and cultures were all negative despite positive PCR results, suggesting that structural wall weakening during the apparent healing phase of the abscess, rather than active uncontrolled infection, contributed to aneurysm instability after Ch-EVAR.

This observation has several practical implications. In patients with BCG-related INAA treated by EVAR, careful longitudinal CT assessment of not only aneurysm size but also wall composition and periaortic tissue characteristics is essential, even when systemic signs of infection improve. When large abscess cavities replace the native aortic wall, early radical resection should be considered, and EVAR should be regarded as a temporary bridge rather than a permanent solution. Surgeons should recognize that rupture can occur not only during the progressive phase of infection, but also during the remodeling and healing phases.

Conclusions

We report a rare case of BCG-related paravisceral INAA initially managed with Ch-EVAR that later developed impending rupture during the apparent healing phase of antituberculosis therapy, necessitating radical supramesenteric aortic resection with visceral revascularization. Given the unpredictable course of BCG-associated infective aneurysms, close surveillance and timely conversion to radical open repair are crucial, especially in patients with hostile neck anatomy and extensive periaortic abscess formation.
