Single–particle rotational microrheology enables pathological staging of macrophage foaming and antiatherosclerotic studies
Authors: Jinhui Shang, Yuan Ma, Xixuan Liu, Shijie Sun, Xiayun Pang, Rui Zhou, Shuangyan Huan, Yan He, Bin Xiong, Xiao-Bing Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMacrophage foaming plays critical roles in the initiation and development of atherosclerotic plaques and unstable plaque evolution. To achieve effective treatment of atherosclerotic diseases, it is important to uncover the pathological evolution dynamics of macrophage foaming and underlying regulatory mechanisms. Here, we introduce a single–particle rotational microrheology method, which enables the pathological staging of macrophage foaming and antiatherosclerotic studies from the dynamic analysis of the lysosomal viscous feature over the pathological evolution progression. We uncovered three distinct pathological stages in the evolution dynamics, accompanied by a pathological stage–dependent activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome–involved positive feedback loop. We validated this positive feedback loop as a promising therapeutic target and revealed the time window–dependent efficacy of NLRP3 inflammasome–targeted drugs against atherosclerotic diseases.

Atherosclerotic disease, including myocardial infarction and stroke, is a major cause of human mortality worldwide. As the pathological hallmark at the early stage of atherosclerotic disease, macrophage–derived foam cells play critical roles in the initiation and development of atherosclerotic plaques as well as unstable plaque evolution (1–4). Macrophages can endocytose various kinds of modified lipoproteins (e.g., oxidized low-density lipoprotein, oxLDL) with the aid of several kinds of scavenger receptors (5–9). However, the modified lipoproteins are poorly degraded and can be deposited within macrophage lysosomes, leading to lysosomal storage disorders and cellular dysfunction, which in turn triggers the progression of macrophage foaming and subsequent formation of atherosclerotic plaques (10–13). Previous investigations have revealed the molecular mechanisms and biological consequences of disorders in lipid metabolisms, signaling pathways, and inflammatory responses in macrophage–derived foam cells by taking advantage of various biological techniques (14–19). These studies provided critical information for understanding the formation and characteristics of macrophage–derived foam cells, which greatly advanced the development of therapeutic strategies against atherosclerotic diseases (20–22). However, the evolution dynamics of macrophage foaming and underlying regulatory mechanisms remain obscure (23, 24).

With the advances of optical imaging techniques and data processing methods, tracking the translational and rotational behaviors of single biomolecules or biological particles has become a powerful approach for the fundamental and mechanistic studies of diverse physiological and pathological processes (25–30). Such tracking techniques not only enable us to resolve the dynamic localization, conformation, and rotational information of biomolecules but also provide the opportunity to uncover complicated biomolecular interactions (31, 32). For example, Fang et al. reported a three–dimensional single–particle orientation and rotational tracking (3D–SPORT) technology to uncover the coordinated cargo–motor interactions and dynamin-action mediated vesicle twist during the clathrin–mediated endocytosis in live cells (33, 34). Our group developed a data–mining integrated tracking microscopy to measure the allosteric modulation of cell receptors in the native state (35), and to uncover the detailed dynamics of receptor–mediated endocytosis of anisotropic nanocargoes (36). As dictated by the Stokes–Einstein (SE) and Stokes–Einstein–Debye (SED) expressions, tracking the translational or rotational movement of nano-objects allows the determination of the viscous feature of surrounding microenvironments (37). Moreover, lysosomes are the terminal endpoint of nano–scaled particles after endocytosis (38–41). Taken together, it is possible to use robust single–particle microrheology techniques for dynamic analysis of disease–related viscous feature variations within cell lysosomes (42).

In this work, we present a robust single–particle rotational microrheology method to uncover the pathological evolution dynamics of macrophage foaming and the underlying positive feedback mechanism, which enables us to evaluate the efficacy of drugs targeting the positive feedback loop for antiatherosclerotic studies (Fig. 1). The core idea of this method is to track the out–of–plane rotation–caused scattering brightness fluctuations of the gold nanorod (AuNR) probe–based microrheometer, followed by the determination of rotational relaxation time for the dynamic analysis of the lysosomal viscous feature during macrophage foaming. With this technique, we demonstrated the lysosomal viscous feature as a robust pathological reporter for macrophage-derived foam cells. More importantly, we uncovered three distinct pathological stages underlying the evolution trajectory of macrophage foaming, which are highly correlated with a pathological stage–dependent activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome–involved positive feedback loop. By using a pharmacological inhibitor and clinical drug candidates targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome, we validated the capability of this positive feedback loop as a promising and general therapeutic target against atherosclerotic diseases. Moreover, we found that the drug efficacy is superior when applied prior to the activation of the positive feedback loop, while treatment at later stages results in poor efficacy, suggesting the pathological stage–dependent efficacy of NLRP3 inflammasome–targeted drugs. These findings provide insights into understanding the pathological progression of macrophage foaming and underlying regulatory mechanisms, which can advance the development of early treatment strategies against atherosclerotic diseases in preclinical studies.

Results

Principle of Single–Particle Rotational Microrheology with the AuNR Probe–Based Rotational Microrheometer.

As nonphotobleaching and nonphotoblinking plasmonic probes, anisotropic AuNRs have been extensively used for long–term imaging and rotational tracking applications (43–47). Previously, Alivisatos et al. and our group have independently established the single–particle scattering imaging techniques for 3D orientation imaging and rotational tracking of anisotropic AuNRs based on the analysis of polarized scattering signals with the aid of birefringent crystals (48, 49). However, the introduction of birefringent crystals in the light–collecting path complicates the imaging system, and the splitting of scattering light by birefringent crystals is prone to produce signal overlap for simultaneous imaging of massive particles (Fig. 2A), which could hamper the broad applications of such imaging techniques. To address this issue, we proposed a simple and robust imaging strategy that enables us to track the rotational dynamics of massive AuNR probes without the aid of birefringent crystals (Fig. 2 B and C). According to previous literature (50), AuNR probe can be simply approximated as a dipole along its longitudinal diameter. For the electromagnetic wave scattered by the dipole, the propagation direction is perpendicular to the plane determined by the vectors of electric and magnetic fields. Because the camera can only capture the light with a propagation direction along the optical axis (defined as z axis), the scattering brightness solely depends on the vector component in the x–y plane of the wave field scattered by the dipole located within the depth of focus of the imaging system. Such kind of single–particle imaging method, which selectively detects the out-of-plane vector component of AuNR probes, can be named as out–of–plane polarization imaging (Fig. 2B). With this imaging technique, the fluctuating scattering brightness of AuNR probes could be determined for the analysis of out–of–plane rotational dynamics driven by the out–of–plane vector component of transient Brownian torque (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). We thus propose the single–particle rotational microrheology method to measure the viscous feature of surrounding microenvironment from the analysis of rotational dynamics of AuNR probes. This includes two steps in the analytical workflow, 1) tracking the time courses of out–of–plane rotation–caused scattering brightness fluctuations and 2) conducting the autocorrelation analysis of scattering brightness fluctuations to calculate the rotational relaxation time (τ) for the viscous feature analysis.

To examine the feasibility of the single–particle rotational microrheology method, we first prepared the fetal bovine serum (FBS) coated AuNRs (~118 nm × 50 nm, SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3). Using the AuNR probe–based rotational microrheometer (51, 52), we tested the disturbance of fluid flow on the accuracy of single–particle rotational microrheology for the analysis of rotational relaxation time. After dispersed in viscous glycerol solution, the scattering brightness fluctuations of individual AuNR probes were tracked after the introduction of lateral fluid flow with the aid of a pipette. Interestingly, the degree of fluctuations in the time courses of scattering brightness of AuNR probes in the presence of fluid flow was almost the same as that observed without disturbance (Fig. 2D). Moreover, there were no significant differences in the rotational relaxation time of AuNR probes with or without the interference of fluid flow (Fig. 2 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). As revealed by theoretical considerations, this method is not prone to suffer the disturbances from fluid flow and lateral sample drift when the out–of–plane rotation of AuNR probe is dominantly driven by random thermal force (SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S5). This elegant characteristic can benefit the accurate analysis of viscous features in the presence of passive diffusion of lysosomes and lateral sample drift. Moreover, we applied the single–particle rotational microrheology to measure the rotational relaxation times of AuNR probes in aqueous glycerol solutions with viscosities comparable to the effective viscosity in cell lysosomes at different physiological or pathological conditions (SI Appendix, Figs. S6 and S7). In line with the theoretical derivation (τ=πL3ℓℓ+13kBTlnLD+σ·η, Eq. 4), the rotational relaxation time is linearly proportional to liquid viscosity (Fig. 2G), suggesting the capability of single–particle rotational microrheology for the analysis of the viscous feature of microenvironment surrounding the rotational microrheometer. According to the theoretical derivation, the variations in the length (L) and diameter (D) of this rotational microrheometer would result in enlarged deviations in the analysis of the viscous feature (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Therefore, monodisperse AuNR probes with similar time–averaged scattering brightness are important for the accurate determination of viscous feature changes. In addition, the addition of oxLDL in aqueous glycerol solutions can be sensitively determined by single–particle rotational microrheology (SI Appendix, Fig. S9), suggesting the potential for the analysis of changes in the lysosomal viscous feature caused by aberrant lipid deposition.

Probing Lysosomal Viscous Feature in Macrophage–Derived Foam Cells by Single–Particle Rotational Microrheology.

Because the formation of foam cells is tightly associated with the development of lipid storage disorders and subsequent cell damage induced by loss of lysosome function (10, 41), we applied the single–particle rotational microrheology method to determine the lysosomal viscous features in macrophage–derived foam cells. The prepared AuNR probe–based rotational microrheometer is highly biocompatible when incubated with macrophages (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). For the single–particle analysis of viscous features in cell lysosomes, the AuNR probes were first delivered into the macrophage lysosomes via endocytosis after the incubation at an optimized concentration to minimize the interference from probe aggregation (SI Appendix, Figs. S11 and S12). Subsequently, the macrophages were subjected to continuous oxLDL stimulation over 72 h to produce macrophage–derived foam cells (Fig. 3A) (53). The continuous oxLDL stimulation yields clear lipid accumulation in AuNR probe–loaded lysosomes and cell morphology changes (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Figs. S13–S16), leading to the increased viscous features in cell lysosomes. As the loading of multiple AuNR probes within single lysosomes might generate probe aggregates (SI Appendix, Fig. S17), it is important to distinguish the lysosome loaded with single AuNR probe in the following imaging studies. To this end, we calculated the time–averaged scattering brightness of single AuNR probes and aggregated AuNR probes caused by salt treatment. Thanks to the plasmonic coupling effect, the time–averaged scattering brightness of single AuNR probes is significantly less than that of aggregated AuNR probes (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). Next, we used the single–particle rotational microrheology method to measure the lysosomal viscous feature in macrophage-derived foam cells. The single–particle imaging studies revealed the notable variations in color and scattering brightness of individual AuNR probes caused by out–of–plane rotation within macrophage lysosomes (Fig. 3 C–E). In contrast, the scattering brightness of the AuNR probes in macrophage–derived foam cells remained largely unchanged over time, indicating a highly restricted rotational degree of freedom due to aberrant lipid accumulation. The autocorrelation analysis of scattering brightness fluctuations yielded a rotational relaxation time (τ) of ~378 ms and ~1,383 ms for the AuNR probes in macrophages and macrophage–derived foam cells (Fig. 3F), respectively. This means that the lysosomal effective viscosity is significantly increased because of the aberrant lipid accumulation in the lysosomes of macrophage–derived foam cells, leading to a remarkable decrease in the rotational degree of freedom for the AuNR probes. The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, which is well characterized by the upregulation of NLRP3 and IL-1β expressions, has been recognized as a typical molecular feature in macrophage–derived foam cells (54–56). Therefore, we examined the difference in the molecular feature between macrophages and foam cells by measuring the expression of NLRP3 and IL-1β. The significantly up–regulated expression of NLRP3 and IL-1β indicated the activation of the NLPR3 inflammasome in macrophage–derived foam cells (Fig. 3G), which is in line with the changes of the lysosomal viscous feature measured by single–particle rotational microrheology. These findings suggested that the lysosomal viscous feature might be used as a pathological reporter for the investigation of evolution dynamics of macrophage foaming and underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Uncovering the Evolution Trajectory and Pathological Stage–Dependent Activation of the NLRP3 Inflammasome–Involved Positive Feedback Loop.

Next, we attempted to probe the kinetic changes of the pathological reporter during macrophage foaming by single–particle rotational microrheology. After the delivery of AuNR probes to macrophage lysosomes through endocytosis and consequent oxLDL stimulation, the rotational behaviors of AuNR probes were then monitored continuously by out–of–plane polarization imaging (Fig. 4A). To uncover the dynamic changes of the pathological reporter and determine the evolution trajectory of macrophage foaming, we recorded the out–of–plane rotation–caused scattering brightness fluctuations of AuNR probes at different timepoints after oxLDL stimulation. The degree of fluctuations in scattering brightness of AuNR probes gradually reduced over time (SI Appendix, Fig. S19), but remained almost unchanged in the control groups (without oxLDL treatment in SI Appendix, Fig. S20, with NLRP3 activation by LPS in SI Appendix, Fig. S21). The rotational relaxation time of the AuNR probes increased remarkably from 376 ms to 1,539 ms upon oxLDL stimulation (Fig. 4B), while only slight variations in the rotational relaxation time were detected in the control groups (SI Appendix, Figs. S22 and S23). Plotting the rotational relaxation time as a function of time yielded the evolution trajectory of macrophage foaming, which revealed that the changes of pathological reporter follow a characteristic sigmoidal growth model (Fig. 4C), compared to the slight changes in control groups (SI Appendix, Figs. S24 and S25). As indicated by the pathological reporter, the evolution trajectory of macrophage foaming can be divided into three pathological stages, 1) an early stage (0 to ~24 h, with little lipid accumulation); 2) a middle stage (~24 to ~72 h, with a remarkedly accelerated rate of lipid accumulation); 3) a late stage (after ~72 h, approaching to the saturation of lipid storage within cell lysosomes). With the oil red O staining assay, we confirmed the differences of lipid accumulation in macrophages at the three pathological stages after cell fixation (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). To further validate the pathological stages revealed by single–particle rotational microrheology and uncover the underlying biological characteristics, we examined whether the molecular features were pathological stage dependent over the macrophage foaming progression. We found that the NLRP3 expression was slightly increased at the early stage, but the release of IL-1β could not be detected (Fig. 4D), implying the occurrence of NLRP3 inflammasome priming at this stage (57–59). Nevertheless, both the expression of NLRP3 and IL-1β were significantly up–regulated at the middle stage and maintained at the late stage (Fig. 4D), which suggested that the NLRP3 inflammasome was activated at the middle stage and overactivated at the late stage (60, 61). Interestingly, the temporal regulation of NLRP3 inflammasome activation is highly consistent with the pathological stage–dependent upregulation of oxLDL receptors (including CD36 and LOX-1) and the enhancement of oxidative stress over macrophage foaming (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Figs. S27 and S28). The upregulation of oxLDL receptors was completely consistent with the gradual acceleration of lipid uptake and accumulation in lysosomes at the middle stage of macrophage foaming. These pieces of evidence suggested that the three pathological stages underlying the macrophage foaming progression as indicated by the pathological reporter are reliable and biologically significant for in–depth understanding of foam cell formation in atherosclerotic diseases.

According to the uncovered evolution trajectory of macrophage foaming, the middle stage represents a critical window for foam cell formation because of the dramatically enhanced rate of lipid accumulation and the pathological stage–dependent NLRP3 inflammasome activation. However, the dependence of the evolutionary feature on NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the corresponding regulatory mechanism remains obscure. To reveal this mechanism, we investigated the impact of NLRP3 inflammasome activation on the pathological evolution dynamics of macrophage foaming. For this purpose, the AuNR probes–loaded macrophages were incubated with a typical NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor MCC950 (for 1 h at a safety concentration of 0.5 μM, SI Appendix, Fig. S29) (62) and then subjected to the continuous oxLDL stimulation. The pathological evolution trajectory after NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition was then measured within 96 h by single–particle rotational microrheology (Fig. 4F). As indicated by the pathological reporter (Fig. 4 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S30), the evolution dynamics was significantly impeded by MCC950 treatment. Only the extended early stage (0 to ~48 h) and middle stage (~48 to 96 h) could be observed within 96 h (Fig. 4H), as compared to that without the pretreatment of MCC950 (Fig. 4C). The delayed pathological stage transition after the short-term inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome was also confirmed by the reduced lipid accumulation in macrophages (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). These findings suggested that the pathological stage–dependent NLRP3 inflammasome activation is not only a biological consequence of oxLDL stimulation but also might play a positive feedback regulatory role at the middle stage of macrophage foaming. To validate such role of the NLRP3 inflammasome, we examined changes in the oxLDL receptors and IL-1β after the short–term MCC950 pretreatment (Fig. 4I). Compared to the significant upregulation of IL-1β and oxLDL receptors in macrophages upon oxLDL stimulation, both the IL-1β and oxLDL receptors were down–regulated in the presence of MCC950 pretreatment and oxLDL stimulation. The inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome can attenuate the upregulation of oxLDL receptors, which accounts for the delayed evolution dynamics of macrophage foaming caused by MCC950. All these pieces of evidence support the idea that the temporally regulated activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome can act as a feedback trigger to up–regulate the expression of oxLDL receptors and then significantly promote the formation of macrophage-derived foam cells. Therefore, by integrating this regulatory role of the NLRP3 inflammasome with the reported pathways of oxLDL–stimulated NLRP3 inflammasome activation (16, 57, 63), we can figure out the distinct biological features at different pathological stages and a pathological stage–dependent positive feedback regulation underlying macrophage foaming (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S31). During the evolution progression of macrophage foaming, the priming and activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes are regulated in a time–dependent manner, which highly depends on the lysosomal lipid accumulation and consequent cellular dysfunction at different pathological stages. In turn, the pathological stage–dependent activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome triggers the upregulation of oxLDL receptors, which could greatly boost the lipid accumulation within lysosomes at the middle stage. After transforming into lipid–laden foam cells at the late stage, the NLRP3 inflammasome is overactivated, accompanied by the extremely increased oxidative stress. Given the specific regulatory role on the pathological evolution dynamics of macrophage foaming, the NLRP3 inflammasome–involved positive feedback loop might act as a possible target for drug screening and development of therapeutic strategies against atherosclerotic diseases.

Evaluating the Antiatherogenic Therapeutic Potential by Targeting the NLRP3 Inflammasome.

To examine the potential of the positive feedback loop at the middle stage of macrophage foaming as a therapeutic target against atherosclerotic diseases, we used a pharmacological inhibitor of the NLRP3 inflammasome as a model drug and measured the dynamic changes of the pathological reporter upon drug treatment by single–particle rotational microrheology. As a conceptual demonstration, rapamycin, a pharmacological inhibitor which can attenuate the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome via blocking mTORC1 signaling (64, 65), was used in our experiment (Fig. 6A). With the classical MTT assay, the safety concentration of rapamycin was estimated at 40 mg/L in the analysis of antiatherogenic efficacy (SI Appendix, Fig. S32). Then, we monitored the dynamic changes in the pathological reporter in macrophages upon oxLDL stimulation and rapamycin treatment simultaneously. The autocorrelation analysis of scattering brightness fluctuations produced the time course of changes in rotational relaxation time and the tendency after rapamycin treatment (Fig. 6 B and C). Interestingly, the elevation of rotational relaxation time could not be detected within 48 h with the treatment of rapamycin, when compared to that in the control group with oxLDL stimulation only. This evidence indicated that the early–to–middle stage transition during macrophage foaming was suppressed after rapamycin treatment. Moreover, we examined the changes in NLRP3 inflammasome activation and oxLDL receptor expression after rapamycin treatment (Fig. 6D). The remarkable downregulation of NLRP3 and oxLDL receptors (CD36 and LOX-1) suggested that both the NLRP3 inflammasome activation and oxLDL receptor upregulation were efficiently inhibited by rapamycin. Furthermore, we confirmed the antiatherogenic efficacy of rapamycin treatment with the oil red O staining assay (SI Appendix, Fig. S33). It is evident that the aberrant lipid accumulation in macrophages upon continuous oxLDL stimulation was impeded in the presence of rapamycin treatment. These findings demonstrated that the NLRP3 inflammasome–involved positive feedback loop underlying macrophage foaming can act as a promising therapeutic target for effective treatment of atherosclerotic diseases.

Revealing the Time Window–Dependent Efficacy of NLRP3 Inflammasome Inhibition.

Because of the pathological stage–dependent activation of the positive feedback loop, it can be assumed that treatment efficacy might be dependent on the therapeutic time window for antiatherosclerotic drugs targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome. To validate this hypothesis, we used the single–particle rotational microrheology method to evaluate the antiatherosclerotic efficacy of rapamycin treatment at three different time windows (selected as 24 h, 60 h, and 84 h after oxLDL stimulation, respectively, Fig. 7A). From the single–particle imaging studies (Fig. 7 B and C), we found that the rotational relaxation time of AuNR probes gradually decreased after rapamycin treatment at the beginning of the middle stage (window 1), when compared to the gradual increase of rotational relaxation time with drug treatment at the later period of the middle stage (window 2) or at the late stage (window 3). These results suggested that the pathological evolution of macrophage foaming can be clearly reversed via rapamycin treatment prior to the activation of the positive feedback loop, while the rapamycin treatment at latter stages after the activation of the positive feedback loop did not effectively block the pathological evolution progression. The remarkable difference in therapeutic efficacy at different time windows is reasonable because the elevated expression of oxLDL receptors triggered by the activation of positive feedback regulation could not be disrupted after the latter stages. The time window–dependent treatment efficacy also supported the reliability of uncovered pathological stage–dependent activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome–involved positive feedback loop underlying the macrophage foaming progression. Additionally, we investigated cellular oxidative stress and cell viability after the rapamycin treatment and found that cellular homeostasis can be maintained only with early–stage drug treatment targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome (SI Appendix, Figs. S34 and S35). Therefore, the time window is a critical factor for the development of antiatherosclerotic therapeutics strategies targeting the positive feedback loop.

Evaluating Clinical Drug Candidates for Antiatherogenic Applications.

Finally, we applied the single–particle rotational microrheology to determine whether the NLRP3 inflammasome could serve as a general target for the early treatment of atherosclerotic diseases with clinical drug candidates. As a demonstration, two kinds of clinical drug candidates, CY-09 and C172 (Fig. 8 A and B) (66), were selected in this work. According to the results of MTT analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S36), the drug concentration in the antiatherogenic treatment was selected as 5 μM in the subsequent drug efficacy analysis. After the lysosomal delivery of AuNR probes by incubation, the macrophages were subjected to oxLDL stimulation and then treated with CY-09 or C172, respectively. From the analysis of rotational relaxation time by single–particle rotational microrheology (Fig. 8 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S37), we found that the lipid accumulation–caused increment of the lysosomal viscous feature was evidently suppressed when drug treatment was applied at the stage before the activation of the positive feedback loop (window 1). This evidence suggested the great potential of the two clinical drug candidates targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome to block the pathological evolution of macrophage foaming. As expected, the therapeutic efficacy was reduced when the drug treatment was conducted after the activation of the positive feedback loop (window 2, Fig. 8 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S37). These findings indicated that the inhibition of the positive feedback loop with the drugs targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome could be used as a reliable and general strategy for the early treatment of atherosclerotic diseases.

Discussion

We report an out–of–plane polarization imaging–based single–particle rotational microrheology method, which enables us to analyze the viscous feature of the localized microenvironment by tracking the dynamics of the AuNR probe–based rotational microrheometer. Compared to the birefringent crystal–based orthogonal polarization imaging techniques (48, 49), our method allows simultaneous detection of massive AuNR probes without suffering signal overlap. In addition, the measurement of rotational relaxation time with our method is not prone to suffer the interference from lateral sample drift, making it favorable for the analysis of subcellular microrheology in the presence of lateral lysosome movement and sample drift. Moreover, we demonstrated the single–particle rotational microrheology as a simple and robust tool for uncovering the evolution dynamics of macrophage foaming and the underlying regulatory mechanism for antiatherosclerotic therapy explorations. From the analysis of rotational behaviors of individual rotational microrheometers in cell lysosomes delivered by endocytosis, we validated that the lysosomal viscous feature can be used as a pathological reporter for macrophage–derived foam cells due to aberrant lipid accumulation. As compared with traditional techniques for the evaluation of cellular lipid accumulation that require cell fixation (e.g., oil red O staining, TEM characterization, and immunohistochemical analysis) (67, 68), the proposed single–particle rotational microrheology strategy allows dynamic analysis of lipid accumulation in multiple lysosomes of live macrophages simultaneously over extended periods of time. With this technique, we uncovered three distinct pathological stages underlying the evolution dynamics of macrophage foaming, which highly correlate with the temporal regulation of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, oxLDL receptor upregulation, and oxidative stress responses.

To uncover the regulatory mechanism of pathological stage–dependent molecular features, we investigated the impact of NLRP3 inflammasome activation on the dynamics of macrophage foaming. We found that the pathological evolution and the upregulation of oxLDL receptors can be clearly impeded after the inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome with MCC950, suggesting that activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome at the middle stage can act as a positive feedback trigger to promote the development of macrophage foaming. By using a pharmacological inhibitor and clinical drug candidates targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome, we demonstrated the potential of the positive feedback loop as an effective and general therapeutic target for the treatment of atherosclerotic diseases. Furthermore, we revealed the evident differences in the antiatherosclerotic efficacy before and after the activation of the positive feedback loop, implying the pathological stage–dependent treatment efficacy of NLRP3 inflammasome–targeted drugs. We believe that these findings provide insights into the understanding of evolutionary features and underlying regulatory mechanisms of macrophage foaming, which can advance the development of early treatment strategies against atherosclerotic diseases in preclinical studies.

Currently, this method is limited to extract in vivo information of macrophage foaming because of the short working distance of the imaging system and the interference from tissue scattering. This is of course a common shortcoming for most single–particle and single–molecule imaging techniques. Nevertheless, this limitation might be partly or fully addressed with the advancement of vascular vessel organoids or zebrafish–based atherosclerosis models, and imaging system integrating objectives with super long working distances and machine learning based–methods for reducing the interference of tissue scattering in the future. It can be anticipated that these collaborative innovations could advance the application of single–particle rotational microrheology to uncover more valuable insights into macrophage foaming in organoids and in vivo.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Chemicals.

The chemicals including HAuCl4, NaBH4, HCl, AgNO3, sodium oleate, ascorbic acid, 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT), and cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical (Shanghai, China). Phorbol-12-tetradecanoate-13-acetate (PMA), isopropyl alcohol, oil red o, lipopolysaccharides (LPS), rapamycin, MCC950, CY-09, and CFTR (inh)-172 (C172) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). RPMI-1640 medium, Dulbecco’s Phosphate–Buffered Saline (DPBS), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin/streptomycin were obtained from Procell (Wuhan, China). The oxLDL was obtained from Yuanye Bio-Technology (Shanghai, China). All the primary antibodies used in our experiment include NLRP3 antibody (Bioss), IL-1β antibody (Bioss), CD36 antibody (Abcam), LOX-1 antibody (Absin), and β-tubulin antibody (AbClon). The secondary HRP–conjugated antibody (anti-rabbit IgG) was obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). All the commercial chemicals were used without further purification unless specified.

Methods

Preparation and Characterization of AuNR Probes.

The anisotropic gold nanorods (AuNRs, ~118 nm × 50 nm) used in our experiment were synthesized using a seed–mediated growth method according to the procedures as reported previously (69). After the growth process was completed, the residual regents in solution were removed by centrifugation (4,000 rpm for 8 min), and the AuNRs were redispersed in water. For the surface functionalization, the as–prepared AuNRs (2 mL) were centrifugated for three times (4,000 rpm for 8 min). The precipitate was redispersed in 1 mL of water. Then, 50 µL of FBS was added into the AuNR solution and incubated for 2 h. After removing the excessive proteins by centrifugation, the FBS–functionalized AuNR probes were resuspended in 500 µL of water and stored at 4 °C before use. The preparation and modification of AuNR probes were characterized with an ultraviolet-visible spectrometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan) and TEM (JEM 1230, JEOL, Japan), respectively.

Cell Culture and Cell Imaging.

The THP-1 cells (obtained from Procell) were cultured in the cell culture flasks and maintained with RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C and 5% CO2. To obtain the differentiated macrophages, the THP-1 cells suspended in 1640 medium containing 3% FBS were subjected to the treatment of PMA (200 ng/mL) for 48 h. The culture medium was refreshed until the differentiated macrophages were attached to substrates. To induce the progression of macrophage foaming, the oxLDL at a final concentration of 80 mg/L was added to the culture medium (53). The incubation was continued according to the imaging requirements. For the analysis of oxidative stress responses during macrophage foaming, the oxLDL–stimulated macrophages were treated with DCFDA for 30 min and then detected with the flow cytometry and confocal laser scanning microscope. For the colocalization imaging of AuNR probes and cell lysosomes, the macrophages were incubated with the as-prepared AuNR probes for 12 h and then, the lysosomes were stained with Lyso–Tracker Green (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 30 min. After removing the residual nanoprobes and fluorescent probes by washing them with culture medium, the cells were observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope. The resonance light scattering imaging of AuNR probes was achieved by setting the excitation and emission wavelength at 640 nm, simultaneously. For the fluorescence imaging of lysosomes with Lyso–Tracker Green, the emission (500 to 550 nm) was collected upon the excitation of light with a wavelength of 488 nm.

MTT Assay.

To examine the biocompatibility of the nanoprobes and drugs targeting NLRP3 inflammasome–targeted drugs, the macrophages were treated with different amounts of nanoprobes or drugs including MCC950, rapamycin, CY-09, and C172 for an incubated of 24 h, respectively. After washing with DPBS three times, 20 mg/L MTT was added for an incubation of 4 h. The cell viability was tested by measuring the absorbance at 490 nm with a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, USA). For the analysis of the impact of drug treatment on long–term cellular homeostasis, cell viability at different times (from 0 to 72 h) after rapamycin treatment at the early stage was measured.

Oil Red O Staining Assay.

Oil red O (500 mg) was dissolved in 100 mL of isopropanol and stored at 4 °C in the dark. To conduct the oil red O staining assay (67), 4 mL of water was mixed with 6 mL of oil red O isopropanol solution, and then, the solution was filtered. The macrophages were rinsed with DPBS three times and fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 20 min. After removing the paraformaldehyde solution by rinsing the cells with DPBS, the oil red O solution was added for incubation of 30 min at 37 °C. The excessive oil red O was removed by washing with 60% isopropanol for 5 s. Finally, the cells were washed twice with DPBS and observed under a bright–field microscope.

TEM Characterizations of Cell Lysosomes.

To prepare the cell sample for TEM characterization, 8 mL of THP-1 cell suspension with a density of ~1 × 106 cells/mL was transferred into three culture dishes (with a diameter of 10 mm). After the induction of cell adhesion by PMA treatment, 100 µL of AuNR probes was added into the cell culture medium for an incubation of 2 h. After removing the residual AuNR probes by washing with culture medium, macrophages were treated with oxLDL at a final concentration of 80 mg/L for the induction of macrophage foaming. The cells were harvested after 72 h and washed with DPBS buffer for three times. Then, the cells were treated with fixative solution at 4 °C for 4 h. The cell sections were prepared according to the standard protocols and observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Servicebio).

Western Blotting Assay.

The expressions of NLRP3, IL-1β, oxLDL receptors (CD36 and LOX-1), and tubulin were analyzed with the western blotting assay according to the protocols as reported previously (62). Typically, the cells were lysed by adding 100 µL of RIPA lysis buffer containing 1 mM of PMSF and the cell lysates were subject to centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 4 °C) for 10 min. After the collection of supernatants, the protein samples were resolved on 10% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked in 5% (wt/vol) dried milk in 1×TBS-T at room temperature for 90 min. For the detection of protein contents, the membranes were incubated with primary antibody solutions diluted with 5% (wt/vol) dried milk in TBS-T overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBS-T buffer three times, the membranes were treated with the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibody solutions diluted with 5% (wt/vol) dried milk in TBS-T for 1 h. The residual antibodies were removed by washing with TBS-T buffer three times, and then, the membranes were detected under a Bio–Rad ChemiDoc XRS imaging system after the treatment of a commercially available enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Dark–Field Imaging and Data Processing.

The out–of–plane polarization imaging was conducted under a dark–field microscope (Nikon 80i, Japan) equipped with an oil–immersed 60× objective and a color CCD camera (Olympus DP72, Japan). The orthogonal polarization imaging was conducted by adding a birefringence crystal in the light–collecting path and a 40× objective was used for the observation of AuNR probes (49, 70). The orthogonal polarization imaging and out-of-plane polarization imaging were conducted to detect the same sample containing AuNR probes with a dense distribution for the comparative analysis of disturbance from signal overlap. To examine the correlation between the rotational relaxation time and liquid viscosity, the AuNR probes were dispersed into the glycerol–water mixtures at different glycerol concentrations from 95 to 99% (with corresponding viscosity from 366 cp to 775 cp). The AuNR probes were then tracked by out–of–plane polarization imaging. The scattering brightness of anisotropic nanoprobes over rotational movement in viscous liquids was recorded continuously for the autocorrelation analysis. The exposure time was set as 70 ms. The scattering brightness of nanoprobes was extracted using ImageJ software to obtain their intensities over 200 consecutive frames. To obtain the optimal probe concentration for single–particle imaging, different volumes of as–prepared AuNR probes (from 5 to 80 µL) were separately incubated with macrophages for 12 h). The dependence of probe aggregation on probe concentration was examined and analyzed by single-particle imaging. With the optical probe concentration, the analysis of probe aggregation on incubated time (from 12 to 120 h) was also conducted by single–particle imaging. Because the active lysosome transport might result in the deformation of the lysosome membrane and unwanted constraint of probe rotation, the AuNR probes exhibiting the characteristics of active transport were not counted for the statistics analysis. Meanwhile, the passive diffusion in the z direction would cause the AuNR probe to deviate from the focal plane, leading to the evident decrease of scattering brightness. To ensure the accuracy of autocorrelation analysis, the nanoprobes that stayed on the focus plane longer than 14 s (over 200 frames) were selected and counted for the analysis of statistical distributions. For the single–particle analysis in different viscous solutions, the averaged rotational relaxation time of 100 AuNR probes was calculated.

To evaluate the interference of lateral particle movement on the accuracy of rotational relaxation time analysis, the lateral fluid flow was introduced by generating a slight pressure with the aid of a pipette. Then, the rotational relaxation time in the cases with and without the presence of lateral fluid flow was calculated. For each case, the averaged rotational relaxation time of 100 AuNR probes was analyzed. Because of the possible interference of probe aggregation on the accuracy of statistical analysis of rotational relaxation time, we proposed the discrimination of monodispersed AuNR probes from the probe aggregates according to the time–averaged scattering brightness. To obtain the sample containing AuNR probe aggregates, the prepared AuNR probe solution was treated with NaCl solution (1 mol/L). Then, the raw AuNR probes and probe aggregates were separately monitored under a dark–field microscope and the time–averaged scattering brightness of 300 particles was calculated for each case. Due to the plasmonic coupling effect, the monodisperse and aggregated AuNR probes exhibited clearly separated distributions of time–averaged scattering brightness. To avoid the interference of probe aggregation on the analysis of the lysosomal viscous feature in live cells, the AuNR probes with time–averaged scattering brightness less than critical threshold were analyzed and counted (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). For the visualization of evolution dynamics of macrophage foaming stimulated by oxLDL stimulation, the AuNR probes were delivered into macrophages, and then, the scattering brightness fluctuations of individual AuNR probes were monitored by out–of–plane polarization imaging. The control groups were set as the macrophages without oxLDL stimulation and with the LPS treatment (1 μg/mL), respectively. The drug efficacy was evaluated by analyzing the monitoring the changes in the fluctuations of scattering brightness of AuNR probes in oxLDL stimulated–macrophages at different times after drug treatment. For the calculation of rotational relaxation time of AuNR probes in cell lysosomes, 70 AuNR probes in more than 10 cells were counted in our experiment. All the results were expressed as the mean ± SD. Statistical significance (ns. P > 0.05) was performed by one–way ANOVA.

Theoretical Considerations for the Autocorrelation Analysis of Time Course of Scattering Brightness Fluctuations.

For the continuous tracking of rotational movement of an anisotropic AuNR probe on the focal plane by out–of–plane polarization imaging, the averaged scattering brightness fluctuation as a function of lag time Δt can be given by[1]⟨I(t+Δt)-It⟩=Imax-IminfDrΔt,

where Imin and Imax are the scattering brightness of the nanorod probe at θ=90∘ and θ=0∘ (θ is the polar angle as shown in Fig. 2), respectively. The rotational diffusion coefficient (Dr) of a rod–like nanoparticle around the longitudinal axis can be obtained according to the Stokes–Einstein–Debye equation, Dr=3kBTπηL3lnLD+σ, where L and D represent the length and diameter of AuNR probe, σ is an end effect correction and can be obtained by the equation σ=-0.662+0.917·DL-0.05·(DL)2, η is the liquid viscosity (49).

Then, the autocorrelation function of scattering brightness variations of AuNR probe can be calculated with the equation,[2]Ct=∑t=0TI′tI′t+Δt∑t=0TI′tI′t,

in which scattering brightness variations I′(t) is defined as I′(t)=I(t)-⟨I⟩, and ⟨I⟩ represents the averaged scattering brightness. According to previous literature, the autocorrelation curves exhibit the Kohlrausch–Williams–Watts decay (71, 72),[3]Ct=C0×exp-tτβ,

where τ is the rotational relaxation time and β is the corresponding stretching parameter. Because the rotational diffusion coefficient Dr can be determined as Dr=1τℓ(ℓ+1), (ℓ is the order of the Legendre polynomials, ℓ = 1, 2, 3, or 6 for different cases), we conclude that the rotational relaxation time (τ) is linearly proportional to the viscosity (η) of the surrounding microenvironment of the AuNR probes as[4]τ=πηL3ℓℓ+13kBTlnLD+σ.

Therefore, the viscous feature of localized microenvironment surrounding the AuNR probes can be measured from the analysis of rotational relaxation time by single–particle rotational microrheology.
