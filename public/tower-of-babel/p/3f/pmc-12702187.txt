PKA orchestrates long-range lysosomal vesicle transport during synaptic maintenance
Authors: Kerriann K. Badal, Yibo Zhao, Bindu L. Raveendra, Sebastian Lozano-Villada, Kyle E. Miller, Sathyanarayanan V. Puthanveettil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBidirectional long-distance transport of organelles is crucial for communication between the cell body and synapses, yet how this transport is regulated during synapse formation, maintenance, and plasticity remains unclear. In the Aplysia sensory neuron (SN)-motor neuron system, which mediates the gill-siphon withdrawal reflex, we demonstrate that synapse maintenance coincides with a sustained decrease in retrograde lysosomal vesicle (LV) transport in SNs. This reduction becomes evident three days after synapse formation, in contrast to anterograde mitochondrial transport, which increases within 12 h. Serotonin-induced, learning-related synapse formation further suppresses retrograde lysosomal transport within 24 h, while boosting anterograde mitochondrial transport as early as 1 h post-exposure. A pharmacological screen implicates PKA as a key regulator of retrograde LV transport during synapse maintenance. These results reveal organelle-specific, temporally distinct regulation of long-distance transport in synapse formation, maintenance, and plasticity.

Introduction

Synapse formation involves multiple specializations requiring the coordinated regulation of molecular and biochemical pathways in pre- and postsynaptic neurons.1,2,3 In presynaptic neurons, these include active zone formation, vesicle clustering, neurotransmitter synthesis and release, and mitochondrial enrichment.4,5,6,7 In postsynaptic neurons, they include post-synaptic densities and neurotransmitter receptor clustering.8,9 Additionally, specialized cell adhesion complexes, transsynaptic signaling proteins, and growth factors are required.1,2,3,10,11 Retrograde signaling from postsynaptic neurons also modulates presynaptic signaling.12,13,14 While these studies illuminate synapse formation, little is known about how synapses are maintained.15,16

Recent research using models like Drosophila, Caenorhabditis elegans, and mice has identified key players in synapse maintenance, such as ATAT-2 tubulin acetyltransferase, the RPM-1 signaling hub,17 IP3 signaling,18 Shank 3 phosphorylation,18 polarity proteins,19 calcium channels,20 Piccolo,21 and cell adhesion molecules.22 Together, these findings describe local mechanisms governing synapse maintenance.

Decades of research have revealed the highly dynamic bidirectional transport of cargoes and signals during synapse function and plasticity; yet, whether and how the long distance bidirectional axonal transport is modulated during basal and activity-dependent synapse maintenance is widely unknown. Given the highly polarized architecture of neurons, with the soma communicating with synapses through axons and extensive dendritic arbors, it is likely that soma-synapse communication is essential for synapse maintenance. Several studies, including our own, have demonstrated that long-distance delivery of gene products from the soma to the synapse via molecular motor proteins along microtubule tracks23 ensures proper synapse function and plasticity.24,25,26,27,28,29,30,31 Kinesin mediates anterograde transport to deliver proteins, RNAs, and organelles to synapses,32,33,34 while dynein/dynactin-facilitated retrograde transport is crucial for cellular signaling, material recycling, and neuronal homeostasis.31,35,36 However, how this bidirectional transport is modulated to maintain synapse function remains unclear, representing a critical gap in our understanding of neural circuit function and plasticity.

To assess the role of long-distance transport in synapse maintenance, we utilized the Aplysia gill-siphon withdrawal reflex model. Unlike vertebrate neurons that form autapses, Aplysia sensory neurons (SNs) and motor neurons (MNs) form synapses exclusively with specific neuron types.37,38,39,40 In the presence of L7 motor neuron (L7MN), a target postsynaptic MN in the abdominal ganglia, SNs form functional synapses that remodel and grow in response to neuronal stimulation.41,42 We recently reported that maintaining SN-L7MN synapses involves a bidirectional increase in mitochondrial transport flux, with anterograde transport significantly enhanced within 12 h of synapse formation.43 However, it is unclear whether this increase is specific to all cargos or depends on the cargo type, and what mechanisms modulate transport during synapse maintenance and plasticity.

Using the SN-L7MN cell culture system, we examined whether synapse maintenance affects transport in a cargo-specific manner and whether synapse formation and plasticity uniquely modulate different cargos. We imaged retrograde transport of lysosomal vesicles (LVs) during synapse maintenance, formation, and plasticity. LVs, a group of acidic organelles involved in the endolysosomal system, autophagy, and macromolecule recycling, are essential for neuronal connectivity, sensory-motor functions, mood, learning, memory, and aging.44,45,46,47,48,49,50,51,52 Defects in LV axonal transport and signaling impair axonal homeostasis and are linked to neurodegenerative diseases.49,53,54 Our quantitative live imaging of LV transport revealed direction-specific modulation, with a significant reduction in retrograde transport observed 72 h after synapse formation and further reduction within 24 h after induction of plasticity. Pharmacological inhibition studies suggest that spatial compartmentalization of PKA activity may underlie the modulation of LV transport.

Results

Reduction in the long-distance retrograde transport of lysosomal vesicles during the maintenance of SN-L7MN synapses

To examine whether synapse maintenance is associated with a general enhancement in the bidirectional transport of organelles, such as mitochondria in presynaptic SNs,43 or with organelle-specific and direction-specific modulation of transport, we imaged long-distance transport of LVs. In SN and postsynaptic L7MN co-cultures (SN-L7MN), synapses begin to form within 6 h of plating. In these cultures, the excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs), a measure of synaptic communication, increase until three days after in vitro culture (DIV3) and then remain relatively stable. Therefore, SN-L7MN cultures over three days old are considered to be in the maintenance phase.

In DIV6 SN-L7MN co-cultures, we conducted quantitative live imaging of the bidirectional transport of LVs in SNs (Figures 1A and 1B). SN and SN-L7MN kymograph analyses revealed very few LVs transported in the anterograde direction; therefore, we focused our studies exclusively on quantitative analyses of transport in the retrograde direction. We found that LV flux is reduced in SNs during basal synapse maintenance compared to transport in SNs without a functional synapse (flux: SN retrograde LV = 0.153 LV/μm min ± 0.022 SEM, n = 20 neurons, SN-L7MN retrograde LV = 0.09 LV/μm min ± 0.01 SEM, n = 19 neurons, unpaired two-tailed t test, p value = 0.046; velocity: SN retrograde LV = 0.32 μm/s ± 0.01 SEM, n = 20 neurons, SN-L7MN retrograde LV = 0.25 μm/s ± 0.02 SEM, n = 17 neurons, (pooled average) unpaired two-tailed t test, p value = 0.205; Figures 1C and 1D; Table S1; Videos S1, S2, and S3). Next, we assessed the stationary, moving, and total LVs in SNs and presynaptic SN-L7MN neurons to determine whether a reduced retrograde LV flux is due to a change in density or a change in transport kinetics. We found that the number of moving and total lysosomal particles is significantly reduced in presynaptic SN-L7MN neurons, but no change in the number of stationary particles (moving: SN average LV: 8.38 number of LV ± 0.74 SEM, n = 33; SN-L7MN average LV: 4.57 number of LV ± 0.58 SEM, n = 35, unpaired two-tailed t test, p value = 0.00014; total: SN average LV: 13 number of LV ± 0.67 SEM, n = 33, SN-L7MN average LV: 9.08 number of LV ± 0.75 SEM, n = 35, unpaired two-tailed t test, p value = 0.00028; Figures 1B, 1C, and 1E; Table S1; Videos S1, S2, and S3). These results suggest that synapse maintenance results in a significant and persistent reduction in the number of retrograde LVs in motion and the density of lysosomal particles within presynaptic axons.

Kymograph analyses revealed that LV retrograde flux is significantly reduced (72-h flux: SN retrograde LV = 0.824 LV/μm min ± 0.06, n = 12; SN-L7MN retrograde LV = 0.441 LV/μm min ± 0.044, n = 13; unpaired two-tailed t test, p value = 0.00008. Differential modulation of LV and mitochondrial transport during long-term facilitation of SN-L7MN synapses

Building on our findings that basal synapse formation and maintenance differentially modulate LV transport (Figures 1 and 2), we next investigated whether activity-dependent, learning-associated synaptic remodeling similarly influences organelle transport. The SN-L7MN synapses undergo long-term facilitation (LTF) in response to serotonin (5HT), a modulatory neurotransmitter critical for learning in Aplysia.58 In vivo, repeated tail shocks trigger long-term sensitization (LTS)—a form of non-associative learning—by inducing 5HT release from serotonergic neurons onto SN-L7MN synapses, promoting the formation of new synaptic connections.59,60 In vitro, 5HT exposure likewise induces robust, long-lasting enhancements in synaptic transmission and remodeling, characteristic of LTF. The induction and persistence of LTF require coordinated molecular and structural changes that strengthen synaptic connections.40,61

To model learning-induced plasticity, we applied five spaced pulses of 5HT (5 × 10 μM) to SN-L7MN co-cultures and assessed bidirectional transport of LVs and mitochondria at two time points: 1 h and 24 h post-5HT. The 1-h time point captures early, initiation-phase responses, while the 24-h time point reflects maintenance-phase changes. Prior to transport analysis, we confirmed successful induction of LTF by recording EPSPs. Consistent with previous reports, EPSPs were significantly elevated 24 h after 5X5HT treatment (baseline = 12.12 mV ± 1.32 SEM, n = 17; post-5X5HT = 16.88 mV ± 1.8 SEM, n = 17; paired t test, p = 0.0009; Figures 3A–3C; Table S3; Videos S8, S9, S10, S11, S12, S13, S14 and S15). At 1-h post-5HT exposure, retrograde transport of LVs remained unaltered, while anterograde mitochondrial flux significantly increased. Specifically, retrograde LV flux was 0.426 LV/μm·min ± 0.05 SEM (baseline) versus 0.35 ± 0.06 LV/μm·min (post-5HT), p = 0.185. Velocities were similarly unchanged (0.74 μm/s ± 0.05 SEM vs. 0.867 μm/s ± 0.07 SEM, p = 0.18). In contrast, mitochondrial anterograde flux increased from 0.479 mito/μm·min ± 0.06 SEM to 0.806 mito/μm·min ± 0.1 SEM (p = 0.01). Retrograde mitochondrial flux remained unchanged (1.25 vs. 1.31 mito/μm·min, p = 0.7), as did velocities (Figures 3D–3I; Table S3; Videos S8, S9, S10, S11, S12, S13, S14 and S15). These data suggest a rapid, organelle- and direction-specific enhancement in mitochondrial delivery to the synapse during the early phase of LTF, without immediate effects on lysosomal retrieval.

At 24 h after LTF induction, retrograde LV transport was significantly reduced, consistent with long-term synaptic remodeling. Retrograde flux decreased from 1.57 LV/μm·min ± 0.16 SEM in vehicle controls to 0.91 LV/μm·min ± 0.101 SEM post-5HT (p = 0.001), while velocity increased modestly (0.71 μm/s ± 0.03 SEM to 0.82 μm/s ± 0.03 SEM, p = 0.02; Figures 3J–3L; Table S3; Videos S8, S9, S10, S11, S12, S13, S14 and S15). Anterograde mitochondrial transport remained elevated at this later stage: flux rose from 0.414 ± 0.06 mito/μm·min (control) to 0.746 ± 0.105 mito/μm·min (p = 0.01), while retrograde flux and velocities were unchanged (Figures 3M–3O; Table S3; Videos S8, S9, S10, S11, S12, S13, S14 and S15).

Together, these results reveal a temporally coordinated, direction-specific, and organelle-specific modulation of axonal transport during learning-associated synapse formation and maintenance. Anterograde mitochondrial transport is rapidly and persistently enhanced during both the initiation and maintenance phases of LTF, while retrograde LV transport is selectively suppressed during the maintenance phase. These findings support a model in which activity-dependent remodeling of SN-L7MN synapses is accompanied by long-distance transport mechanisms that dynamically allocate organelles to meet the evolving demands of synaptic plasticity.

Role of the postsynaptic neuron in modulating presynaptic LV transport during synapse maintenance

In our search for mechanisms underlying the reduction in long-distance retrograde transport of LVs during synapse maintenance, we first considered the possibility that persistent signals from the L7MN modulate presynaptic transport (Figure 4A). To investigate the contribution of L7MN cell body-generated signals in regulating presynaptic LV transport, we microdissected the L7MN during synapse maintenance and then imaged presynaptic axonal transport. An increase in presynaptic LV transport after L7MN cell body removal (CBR) would suggest a role for L7MN cell body-generated signaling in modulating presynaptic transport. Therefore, we microdissected the L7MN cell body and measured LV transport 24 h later (Figure 4B). To investigate whether postsynaptic glutamatergic signaling modulates presynaptic retrograde transport of LVs, we first examined the roles of AMPA (α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid) and NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) receptors at SN-L7MN synapses. We began by blocking AMPA receptor (AMPAR)-mediated transmission using cyanquixaline (CNQX), an AMPAR antagonist known to reduce excitatory synaptic transmission.43 SN-L7MN synapses were imaged 30 min after CNQX bath application to assess changes in LV transport (Figures 4A and 4D). Quantitative analyses revealed no significant differences in retrograde LV flux or velocity following CNQX treatment (flux: pre-CNQX = 0.66 LV/μm·min ± 0.07 SEM, post-CNQX = 0.598 LV/μm·min ± 0.06 SEM, n = 14 neurons, p = 0.44; velocity: pre-CNQX = 0.583 μm/s ± 0.02 SEM, post-CNQX = 0.639 μm/s ± 0.02 SEM, p = 0.14; Figures 4E and S2B; Table S4). These results suggest that acute inhibition of AMPA receptor signaling does not impact presynaptic LV transport.

Next, we assessed the role of NMDA receptor (NMDAR) signaling by applying the competitive NMDAR antagonist (DL)-2-amino-5-phosphonovaleric acid (APV) for 15 min (Figures 4A and 4D). Unlike CNQX, APV treatment led to a significant reduction in LV retrograde flux, although velocity remained unchanged (flux: pre-APV = 0.948 LV/μm·min ± 0.219 SEM, post-APV = 0.5539 LV/μm·min ± 0.159 SEM, n = 9 neurons, p = 0.02; velocity: pre-APV = 0.608 μm/s ± 0.01 SEM, post-APV = 0.542 μm/s ± 0.05 SEM, p = 0.143; Figures 4F and S2C; Table S4).

Contrary to expectations based on general inhibition of synaptic transmission, APV did not enhance LV retrograde transport. To rule out the possibility that this effect was due to a direct action of APV on SNs independent of synaptic signaling, we examined retrograde LV transport in isolated SNs before and after APV application. In this context, APV had no significant effect on LV flux or velocity (flux: pre-APV = 0.787 LV/μm·min ± 0.07 SEM, post-APV = 0.758 LV/μm·min ±0.38 SEM, n = 11 neurons, p = 0.811; velocity: pre-APV = 0.786 μm/s ± 0.03 SEM, post-APV = 0.842 μm/s ± 0.05 SEM, p = 0.401; Figure 4G; Table S4). These findings indicate that the reduction in LV transport observed in SN-L7MN synapses is not a direct consequence of APV acting on SNs but rather may arise from downstream signaling cascades engaged by NMDA receptor blockade.

Given that NMDA receptors are glutamate-gated cation channels with high calcium (Ca2+) permeability, and that their inhibition may reduce postsynaptic Ca2+ influx, we hypothesized that intracellular calcium signaling could play a regulatory role in presynaptic LV transport. To test this, we chelated intracellular calcium using 1 mM BAPTA-AM (1,2-Bis(2-aminophenoxy)ethane-N,N,N',N'-tetraacetic acid tetrakis (acetoxymethyl ester)) for 30 min in SN-L7MN synapses and monitored changes in transport (Figure 4H). BAPTA treatment significantly reduced retrograde LV flux, while velocity remained unchanged (flux: pre-BAPTA = 0.853 LV/μm·min ± 0.136 SEM, post-BAPTA = 0.346 LV/μm·min ± 0.07 SEM, n = 10 neurons, p = 0.0008; velocity: pre-BAPTA = 0.559 μm/s ± 0.04 SEM, post-BAPTA = 0.514 μm/s ± 0.039 SEM, p = 0.309; Figures 4I, 4J, and S2D; Table S4). Notably, mitochondrial transport was unaffected under these conditions. Together, these results indicate that presynaptic retrograde transport of LVs during synapse maintenance is sensitive to intracellular calcium levels.

The role of plasticity-related signaling pathways in LV transport

Quantitative analyses of organelle transport during synapse formation, maintenance, and plasticity revealed a consistent reduction in retrograde LV transport that correlates with synaptic state. One possible explanation is that maintaining synaptic connections imposes increased metabolic demands, requiring neurons to optimize resource allocation. In this context, fewer vesicles may return to the soma because materials are more efficiently utilized locally at the synapse. Alternatively, active mechanisms may suppress retrograde transport to prioritize local consumption. Based on this reasoning, we hypothesized that signaling pathways known to mediate synaptic plasticity may regulate LV transport dynamics following synapse formation. To test this hypothesis, we examined whether pharmacological activation or inhibition of intracellular signaling pathways—including protein kinase C (PKC), inositol trisphosphate receptor (IP3R)-mediated calcium release, and cyclic AMP (cAMP)/protein kinase A (PKA) signaling—could modulate LV transport in isolated SNs lacking synaptic connections. If these pathways contribute to the persistent reduction in retrograde transport observed after synapse formation, their activation in isolated SNs should elicit similar transport changes.

We treated SNs with: phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; 50 nM, 15 min), an activator of PKC signaling; adenophostin A (ADA; 10 μM, 15 min), which stimulates IP3R-mediated calcium release; forskolin (FK; 50 μM, 30 min), a cAMP pathway activator; and 14–22 amide (PKAi; 360 nM, 30 min), a selective PKA inhibitor. These concentrations and durations were chosen based on previous studies, including our earlier work demonstrating that PKA activity is critical for mitochondrial transport43 (Figures S3A and S3B).

Surprisingly, none of these treatments significantly altered retrograde LV transport in SNs lacking synapses. Specifically, PMA treatment did not significantly affect flux (pre-PMA: 1.41 LV/μm·min ± 0.11 SEM, n = 29 neurons; post-PMA: 1.17 LV/μm·min ± 0.11 SEM, n = 26 neurons; paired t test, p = 0.13). Similarly, ADA showed no significant effect (pre: 0.361 LV/μm·min ± 0.089 SEM, n = 17; post: 0.274 LV/μm·min ± 0.067 SEM, n = 17; p = 0.354), nor did FK (pre: 0.316 LV/μm·min ± 0.04 SEM, n = 19; post: 0.31 LV/μm·min ± 0.05 SEM, n = 12; p = 0.964), or PKAi (pre: 0.795 LV/μm·min ± 0.08 SEM, n = 16; post: 0.716 LV/μm·min ± 0.08 SEM, n = 16; p = 0.533) (Figures S3C–S3F; Table S4). Furthermore, mitochondrial transport was also unaffected by PKAi treatment in SNs without synapses (Figure S3G; Table S4).

Collectively, these results indicate that PKC, IP3R-mediated calcium release, cAMP, and PKA signaling do not modulate retrograde lysosomal transport in isolated SNs. Thus, the persistent reduction in LV transport observed during synapse formation likely requires synaptic context or additional activity-dependent signaling not engaged in isolated neurons.

Neither LV nor mitochondrial flux or velocity was significantly affected, indicating that short-term suppression of mTOR signaling does not impair organelle transport in isolated SNs (Figures 5E, 5F, S4H, and S4I).

We next asked whether synapse maintenance imposes a demand on membrane availability due to the continual formation and recycling of synaptic vesicles. This membrane demand may alter intracellular membrane homeostasis, thereby reducing retrograde LV transport. To test this, we supplemented SN-L7MN cultures with docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), a polyunsaturated fatty acid critical for neuronal membranes. DHA contributes to plasma membrane composition, mitochondrial integrity, and vesicle fusion/fission processes,63,64 but it remains a limiting component of membrane biosynthesis. If limited membrane availability constrains retrograde LV transport, DHA supplementation may restore or enhance organelle transport. Conversely, since DHA is also known to promote glutamatergic synaptic transmission and neurite growth, its effects may instead mimic activity-dependent plasticity and further reduce LV transport.

To distinguish between these possibilities, we treated SN-L7MN neurons with DHA (50 μM) for either 3 or 24 h. At 3 h, we observed a significant reduction in retrograde LV flux (pre-DHA: 2.19 LV/μm·min ± 0.155, n = 11; post-DHA: 1.83 LV/μm·min ± 0.08, n = 11; p = 0.033; Figures 7D and S6C; Table S7; Videos S22, S23, S24, and S25), while mitochondrial transport was unaffected (pre: 1.04 mito/μm·min ± 0.08; post: 1.09 mito/μm·min ± 0.09; p = 0.63; Figures 7E and S6D; Table S7; Videos S22, S23, S24, and S25). These findings suggest that acute DHA exposure reduces retrograde LV transport, consistent with enhanced synaptic activity or membrane incorporation rather than relief from material limitation.

Taken together, these findings support the idea that acute DHA supplementation mimics synaptic growth signals, reducing retrograde LV transport and enhancing anterograde mitochondrial delivery. This profile closely resembles the effects of 5HT, a known inducer of synaptic plasticity and new synapse formation, further linking LV transport dynamics to activity-dependent synaptic remodeling.

We next assessed the impact of PKA inhibition on organelle transport in SN-L7MN cocultures. PKAi significantly reduced retrograde LV flux (pre-PKAi: 0.353 LV/μm·min ± 0.04 SEM, n = 7; post-PKAi: 0.091 LV/μm·min ± 0.02 SEM, n = 7; p = 0.0008; Figures 8D, 8E, and S7A; Table S8; Videos S26, S27, S28, and S29), demonstrating that PKA activity is required for sustained LV trafficking during synapse maintenance. In parallel, PKAi also markedly reduced bidirectional mitochondrial transport. Anterograde flux decreased from 0.345 mito/μm·min ± 0.06 SEM to 0.077 mito/μm·min ± 0.01 SEM (p = 0.008), and retrograde flux from 0.324 mito/μm·min ± 0.06 SEM to 0.074 mito/μm·min ± 0.03 SEM (p = 0.003) (Figures 8D, 8F, and S7B; Table S8; Videos S26, S27, S28, and S29). These findings corroborate our previous report that PKA signaling is essential for mitochondrial transport.43

Taken together with our earlier observations—namely, that synapse maintenance enhances mitochondrial transport43 while persistently reducing LV transport (this study)—these results raise an intriguing possibility: PKA may function in a compartmentalized manner to differentially regulate organelle transport. Given that basal synaptic function depends on PKA activity, it is unlikely that global PKA levels are reduced during synapse maintenance. Instead, we hypothesize that PKA activity is selectively enhanced in regions promoting mitochondrial transport (e.g., axons or synapses) and suppressed in compartments such as the Golgi apparatus, where vesicle budding for lysosome formation occurs. This model aligns with prior studies demonstrating spatial compartmentalization of cAMP/PKA signaling.65,66

To test this hypothesis, we performed biochemical fractionation to examine PKA phosphorylation in Golgi-enriched membrane fractions. PKA activation is marked by phosphorylation at Thr197,67 and prior studies have shown that Golgi-associated PKA activity is essential for budding vesicles required for lysosome biogenesis.68 Moreover, the kinase STK25 can inhibit PKA signaling by phosphorylating PRKAR1A, a regulatory subunit of PKA.69 We therefore asked whether activity-dependent learning alters compartment-specific PKA phosphorylation.

Using a LTS paradigm in Aplysia, a well-established model of non-associative learning and long-term memory, we first assessed total PKA activity in CNS homogenates. Western blot analyses revealed a robust increase in phospho-PKA (Thr197) levels within 1 h of LTS training (Figures 8G and 8I; N = 24 animals; Table S8), consistent with enhanced global PKA activation. Next, we isolated Golgi-enriched membrane fractions from the CNS using a commercial enrichment protocol. Golgi fractions were verified by the enrichment of GM130, a well-characterized Golgi marker. Remarkably, western blot analysis revealed a decrease in phospho-PKA levels specifically in the Golgi fraction following LTS training (Figures 8G and 8I; N = 24 animals; Table S8), despite the increase observed in total homogenate. This compartment-specific downregulation supports our hypothesis that PKA activity is differentially regulated across subcellular domains.

Together, these findings suggest an intriguing possibility that during synapse maintenance and activity-dependent plasticity, PKA activity is upregulated in regions supporting mitochondrial transport and downregulated in the Golgi apparatus, where it may limit vesicle budding and retrograde LV flux. Such spatial modulation of PKA signaling could provide a mechanistic basis for the bidirectional and organelle-specific regulation of axonal transport.

Discussion

The proper functioning of neural circuits and the persistence of memories depend on the maintenance of synaptic connections. While local signaling mechanisms governing synapse maintenance are well documented, the role of long-distance soma-to-synapse communication in this process remains poorly understood. Thus, whether and how bidirectional signaling between the soma and synapse contributes to the maintenance of neural circuits represents a fundamental, unresolved question in neurobiology.

To address this, we focused on the long-range axonal transport of two critical organelles: mitochondria, which support neuronal energetics, and LVs, which mediate cellular recycling. Studies across various model systems have demonstrated that vesicles associated with autophagy and endocytosis are typically concentrated at axon terminals, growth cones, and varicosities.70,71 These vesicles share molecular features and trafficking pathways with synaptic vesicles and endoplasmic reticulum components,34,72,73,74 and their movement is mediated by microtubule-based motor proteins. Several studies, including ours, have shown that motor protein-driven long-distance transport is essential for soma-synapse communication during synaptic development and plasticity.24,26,28,75,76

Using Aplysia sensory-motor neuron cultures, which form well-defined synapses devoid of autapses,77 we precisely quantified bidirectional transport of mitochondria and LVs.43,57 We previously reported that mitochondrial flux increased in both anterograde and retrograde directions within 24 h of synapse formation between SNs and L7MNs.43 This raised the important question: is this enhancement specific to mitochondria or a general feature of all organelle transport during synapse maintenance?

To explore this, we quantified LV transport and found a striking and significant reduction in retrograde LV transport in SNs during synapse maintenance, in contrast to the increased bidirectional mitochondrial transport. These results reveal that synapse maintenance differentially regulates organelle trafficking, highlighting a previously unappreciated level of specificity in long-range transport.

The reduction in retrograde LV flux likely reflects changes in LV biogenesis and dynein motor engagement during synapse maintenance.78,79,80,81,82,83 We propose that increased mitochondrial transport reflects the elevated metabolic demands required to sustain synaptic activity, while the reduced retrograde LV transport may result from more efficient synaptic component utilization, generating less waste requiring lysosomal degradation. During periods of low synaptic activity, presynaptic precursors not incorporated into the membrane may be targeted for degradation via retrograde LV transport. In contrast, active synapses preferentially utilize these precursors, reducing the need for lysosomal clearance.

To test this, we examined organelle transport during distinct stages of synapse formation, maintenance, and plasticity (Figure 9A). Synapse formation in SN-L7MN pairs proceeds through clearly defined phases of target recognition, stabilization, development, and maturation. Within 12 h of plating, anterograde mitochondrial transport increased significantly, becoming bidirectional by 24 h and persisting thereafter.43 During this same period, LV transport remained unchanged. However, by 72 h—when EPSPs stabilize—retrograde LV transport was significantly reduced. We next asked whether activity-dependent synapse plasticity further modulates organelle transport. Exposure to 5×5HT, which mimics the modulatory effects of serotonin and induces learning-related synapse formation, increased anterograde mitochondrial transport within 1 h and led to persistent transport changes (Figure 3). In contrast, the flux of retrograde LV transport was reduced 24 h post-stimulation. Since 5HT elevates intracellular Ca2+ and activates PKA, both of which regulate organelle biogenesis and motor protein dynamics,84,85,86,87,88,89 our findings suggest that serotonin modulates transport through organelle- and direction-specific mechanisms (Figure 9A). However, further mechanistic studies are required to establish causal links between these signaling pathways and transport dynamics.

Because 5×5HT-induced plasticity and synapse formation are established cellular models for long-term memory,41,75 our results imply that learning triggers distinct, time- and cargo-specific transport adaptations. Newly formed synapses that persist for days41,90,91 likely require mitochondrial supply and lysosomal remodeling tailored to their state of activity. This is further supported by the activity-dependent upregulation of the anterograde kinesin motor ApKHC1 within 1 h of 5×5HT exposure,24 and the known requirement for mitochondrial support near dendritic spines during synaptic plasticity.92,93,94,95,96,97 Lysosomal function is also essential for synaptic remodeling.98,99 The reduction in retrograde LV transport from distal processes suggests the presence of local regulatory mechanisms that limit vesicle formation after synapse stabilization.

To probe potential mechanisms underlying reduced LV retrograde transport, we conducted targeted pharmacological manipulations of (1) postsynaptic signaling (including L7MN soma dissection), (2) learning-related pathways, and (3) protein degradation, autophagy, and ER-Golgi trafficking. Our rationale was that synapse formation may inhibit specific pathways in the presynaptic neuron to reduce LV transport. In SNs lacking synapses, pharmacological inhibition that mimics these suppressive effects should reveal the relevant signaling cascades. Conversely, pharmacological enhancement or suppression of LV transport in SNs with synapses could uncover mechanisms normally altered during synapse maintenance. These experiments implicated roles for Ca2+ signaling, autophagy, and ER-Golgi trafficking in modulating LV transport. However, given the pleiotropic effects of pharmacological agents, future experiments using live-cell imaging and genetic perturbations will be essential to dissect the specific contributions of these pathways.

One particularly notable result from these manipulations is the direction-selective effect on organelle transport. Inhibition of autophagy using bafilomycin (Baf) disrupted retrograde, but not anterograde, mitochondrial flux in isolated SNs (Figures 5I and 5J). This observation is consistent with the idea that dysfunctional or depolarized mitochondria are transported retrogradely for degradation,100 linking autophagy to the quality control of mitochondrial transport.

We also tested whether LV biogenesis, which depends on precursors derived from the trans-Golgi network, could be regulated by ER-Golgi communication. Inhibition of ER-Golgi trafficking using brefeldin A (BFA) had no measurable effect on LV transport, suggesting that this pathway is not a major contributor to lysosomal trafficking under these conditions. Interestingly, BFA enhanced anterograde mitochondrial flux, which may reflect ER stress-induced apoptosis.101 This interpretation aligns with prior observations that increased mitochondrial transport and fragmentation occur during apoptotic signaling.

In search of mechanisms, we considered whether the availability of membrane components following synapse formation, along with changes in compartmentalized signaling pathways, contributes to the observed reduction in retrograde LV transport. A major structural change during presynaptic development is the formation of the active zone and the generation of synaptic vesicles—processes that require substantial membrane resources.102 This increased demand for membrane material could deplete pools available for lysosomal precursor formation, thereby reducing retrograde LV transport.

To test this hypothesis, we exposed SN-L7MN synapses to DHA, a lipid known to enhance neuronal growth and synaptic function. As expected, within 3 h of DHA treatment, we observed a significant decrease in retrograde LV transport and a concomitant increase in anterograde mitochondrial transport (Figure 7). These findings suggest that neuronal growth and synaptic maturation modulate organelle transport in a direction- and cargo-specific manner, similar to the effects observed with 5×5HT stimulation.

Several studies have identified PKA as a key regulator of autophagy and vesicular trafficking.103,104,105 In our study, pharmacological inhibition of PKA during synapse maintenance led to a decrease in anterograde mitochondrial transport and further suppression of retrograde LV transport (Figure 8). Given the essential role of cAMP/PKA signaling in synapse function, and our observation that synapse maintenance is associated with increased mitochondrial and decreased LV transport, these results suggest that compartmentalized PKA activity may underlie these divergent transport patterns. In this model, PKA activity is selectively sustained or suppressed in distinct subcellular compartments during synapse maintenance.

Such compartmentalized cAMP signaling has been well described.65,66 Notably, PKA has been localized to the trans-Golgi network68,106 a known source of lysosome precursors. Moreover, recent studies show that the regulatory subunit PRKAR1A can be phosphorylated by the kinase STK25, resulting in local PKA inhibition.69 Although it remains to be tested whether Golgi-resident STK25 directly phosphorylates Golgi-localized PKA to suppress LV biogenesis, our data are consistent with a working model in which Golgi-specific PKA inhibition reduces vesicle budding for lysosome formation, leading to reduced retrograde LV transport. In contrast, cytosolic PKA activity, preserved or enhanced during synaptic maintenance, supports mitochondrial transport (Figure 8).

Biochemical evidence supports this model. Following LTS in adult Aplysia, we observed increased phosphorylation of PKA at Thr197—a known regulatory site associated with PKA activation.67 Subcellular fractionation of the CNS post-LTS revealed decreased Thr197 phosphorylation in Golgi-enriched fractions, while phosphorylation in the cytosolic fraction was increased. This compartment-specific shift in PKA activation suggests that localized PKA signaling could indeed differentially modulate organelle transport during synapse maintenance (Figure 9B). Additionally, PKA may influence organelle transport by regulating adaptor protein loading, cargo-motor interactions, or by phosphorylating molecular motors to alter their velocity and directionality.

It is important to note that our biochemical assays were performed on CNS tissue from intact Aplysia, which lacks cell-type specificity. Therefore, future experiments using cell-type-specific genetic and imaging tools will be essential to validate this model and clarify the mechanisms underlying compartmentalized PKA signaling during synapse formation and plasticity.

Conclusion and implications

Together, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized relationship between long-distance organelle transport and synapse states. Specifically, we demonstrate that the long-distance transport of LVs is modulated in a direction-specific manner during synapse formation, maintenance, and plasticity. These transport dynamics are shaped by compartmentalized signaling events—particularly those involving autophagy, membrane availability, and localized PKA activity. Given that both importantly, mitochondrial transport is also modulated in direction specific manner during synaptic plasticity. Mitochondria and LVs rely on dynein for retrograde transport, the specificity of transport regulation likely arises at the level of organelle biogenesis, cargo selection, and adaptor protein recruitment. Our data support a model in which spatially distinct signaling domains within the neuron fine-tune soma-synapse communication, enabling synapses to adapt their metabolic and degradative machinery in response to activity and plasticity. A deeper mechanistic understanding of how long-distance organelle transport is dynamically regulated by compartmentalized signaling will be crucial for elucidating how neural circuits are stabilized and modified during learning and memory—and may reveal new therapeutic targets for synaptic dysfunction in neurological disease.

Limitations of the study

Our quantitative live imaging experiments revealed a reduction in retrograde transport of LVs during synapse maintenance and in the plasticity-associated growth of synapses. However, the precise molecular mechanisms driving these reductions, as well as the physiological rationale for attenuating long-distance communication between the synapse and soma/nucleus, remain to be determined. In particular, identifying the signaling pathways that modulate LV transport during synapse maintenance will be essential for uncovering the processes that support the stabilization of synaptic connections.

As this study directly follows our earlier work on activity-dependent modulation of mitochondrial transport, we initially hypothesized that synapse formation triggers PKA signaling, which in turn promotes enhanced mitochondrial transport. Interestingly, when we examined the role of PKA in LV transport, our findings suggested a potential compartmentalization of PKA activity. While our biochemical fractionation experiments on Golgi-enriched fractions are consistent with this possibility, definitive evidence—such as demonstrating Golgi localization of PKA, potentially mediated by A-kinase anchoring proteins (AKAPs), and assessing compartment-specific modulation of PKA activity following synapse formation—will require further investigation. Finally, a central unresolved question remains: why should LV transport be reduced during synapse maintenance? Addressing this will be critical for understanding how selective modulation of organelle transport contributes to the long-term stability and plasticity of neural circuits.

Experimental model and study participant details

Sexually mature Aplysia californica (6–9 months old) ranging from sizes of 1–4 g and 80–120 g were obtained from the National Resource for Aplysia at the University of Miami’s Rosenstiel School of Marine, Atmospheric, and Earth Science, and were housed in artificial saltwater tanks on-site at the Herbert Wertheim UF Scripps Institute for Biomedical Innovation & Technology. Animals were allowed at least five days to acclimate before being used for neuronal cultures or behavioral analysis.

Method details

Aplysia neuronal cultures

Aplysia neuronal cultures were prepared following the protocol described by Montarolo et al. (1986). Briefly, motor neurons were isolated from the abdominal ganglia of Aplysia weighing 1–4 g, while sensory neurons were isolated from the pleural ganglia of 80–120 g 6-month-old Aplysia. The ganglia were digested with dispase II dissolved in modified L15 at 34.5°C.75,107 After digestion, the pleural and abdominal ganglia were rinsed and transferred to a solution consisting of 25% hemolymph (isolated from wild Aplysia) and 75% modified L15 with L-glutamine.

In a silicone-bottom plate, the ganglia were pinned down and desheathed. The L7 and L11 motor neurons (MNs) were then identified, extracted, and plated in a 35 mm glass-bottom dish. Sensory neurons (SNs) were similarly identified, isolated, and extracted, and then either plated with motor neurons or cultured in isolation on glass-bottom dishes. These cultures were stored at room temperature overnight. The following morning, the neuronal cultures were transferred and incubated at 17°C–18°C.

Synapse formation and maturation measurements

In Aplysia in vitro systems, measurements of electrical connectivity, specifically excitatory postsynaptic potentials, between co-cultured sensory and motor neurons indicate that synapses form as early as 6 h after plating. Additionally, presynaptic axonal mitochondrial bidirectional trafficking is enhanced 24 h after plating.43 Consequently, given the dynamic changes within the first 24 h of plating, days in vitro (DIV) 0–1 are classified as the “synapse formation” stage. Between DIV 2–3, synapses are maturing, referred to as the “synapse maturation” stage. After DIV 3, the majority of the SN-L7MN connections are mature and maintained, leading to the classification of DIV 4–6 as the “synapse maintenance” stage.75

Intracellular electrophysiology measurements

Excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs) were measured in SN-L7MN between DIV 4–6 as described in Miniaci et al. (2008) and Martin et al. (1997). Briefly, a stimulating glass electrode filled with modified L15 media was used to stimulate the presynaptic SN cell body with a 2-millisecond current input. A recording glass electrode, with a resistance of 5–14 Megaohms and filled with 2.3M KCl, was used to impale the postsynaptic L7MN soma and measure membrane potential responses. This setup allowed for the assessment of basal synaptic communication within the SN-L7MN circuitry as a result of presynaptic stimulation.

Microdissection

After electrophysiological confirmation of synapse formation in SN-L7MN at DIV 3–4, the L7MN cell body was manually microdissected using a glass electrode to eliminate postsynaptic cell body-generated signals. Following the microdissection, the cultures were rinsed with modified L15 media, and the culture media was replaced with fresh media. The cultures were then incubated at 17.8°C for 24 h before live imaging.

Quantitative live imaging of bidirectional transport

One hour before imaging, LysoTracker Deep Red and MitoTracker Green FM were used to fluorescently label lysosomal vesicles and mitochondria.57 Live transport images were collected using a Zeiss 880 confocal microscope at the Max Planck Florida Institute. Axonal regions, with a distance of at least 300 μm from the SN cell body, were imaged using a 63x oil objective lens within a 512×512 pixel frame. The imaging parameters were: scan speed of 13 (maximum), scan time of 314 ms, pixel dwell time of 0.51 ms, for 500–600 cycles, with an image size of 45.0 μm × 45.0 μm. A 488 nm laser with power set to 1.1 and a gain of 560 was used to visualize lysosomal vesicle transport, while a 633 nm laser with power set to 1.5 and a gain of 650 was used to visualize mitochondrial transport. The live transport images were saved as.czi (Carl Zeiss Image) files.

Quantitative analysis of time-lapse image data

To analyze transport, movies were rotated, cropped, separated by channel, and kymographs were constructed and analyzed using ImageJ as described (Badal et al., 2022) and briefly detailed below. Because Aplysia axons are exceptionally wide, typically 5 to 10 μm in diameter, multiple kymographs with a width of ∼1 μm were generated for each movie and were pooled for each neuron. Transport was analyzed by drawing a line across the center of the kymographs and counting the number of particles that crossed the line over time, as well as their slopes as they crossed the line, to determine the flux (F) and instantaneous velocity (VINT), respectively. Dividing the flux by velocity yielded the concentration of moving organelles (CMOV) using the relationship F = VINT CMOV. Averages, standard errors, and significance were calculated based on the data from individual neurons in Excel.

Pharmacology experiments

One hour before live transport imaging, neurons were treated with LysoTracker Deep Red [75 nM] (ThermoFisher Scientific L12492) dissolved in DMSO to visualize lysosomal vesicles, and MitoTracker Green FM [300 nM] (ThermoFisher Scientific M7514) dissolved in DMSO to visualize mitochondria. The following pharmacological reagents/vehicles were used to treat SN/SN-L7MN: Phorbol 12-Myristate 13-Acetate (PMA) [50 nM]/DMSO for 15 min; Adenophostin A (ADA) [10 μM]/water for 15 min; Forskolin (FK) [50 μM]/DMSO for 30 min; 14–22 Amide (PKAi) [360 nM]/water for 30 min; D(−)-2-Amino-5-phosphonopentanoic acid (AP5) [100 μM]/water for 30 min; 6-cyano-7-nitroquinoxaline-2,3-dione (CNQX) [50 μM]/water for 15 min; BAPTA-FM [1 mM]/DMSO for 30mins; Lactacystin (Lact) [50 μM]/water for 30 min; Chloroquine (CQ) [50 μM]/water for 30 min; Bafilomycin A (Baf) [10 nM]/DMSO for 30mins; Brefeldin A (BFA) [10 μg/mL]/DMSO for 30 min; Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) [50 μM]/DMSO for 3 and 24 h; serotonin (5HT) [20 μM]/L15 for 5 min pulses.

Long-term facilitation and bidirectional transport measurements

Long-term facilitation (LTF) is the cellular plasticity underlying sensitization in Aplysia and has been thoroughly studied to enhance our understanding of learning and memory. Five interspaced pulses of serotonin (5HT) [10–50 μM] induce LTF in the SN-L7MN cultures. LTF is characterized by enhanced synaptic transmission, increased synaptic vesicle release, an increased number of varicosity sites, and the upregulation of transcription and translation, all of which contribute to the long-term strengthening of synaptic communication.75,108,109,110,111 To induce LTF in SN-L7MN cultures, we applied 20 μM 5HT to the cultures for 5 min, followed by a 15-min rinse, and repeated this 5HT stimulation five times (5X5HT). We measured excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs) 24 h later to verify the enhanced EPSPs induced by 5X5HT (Figure S3; Table S3). To investigate mitochondrial and lysosomal vesicle transport during LTF, we imaged transport 1 h and 24 h after 5X5HT.

Long-term sensitization training

To investigate the role of PKA in regulating lysosomal vesicle distribution in the cytosol and on the Golgi-complex membrane, we examined phospho-PKA and PKA protein expression in the Aplysia nervous system after long-term sensitization (LTS) training. Following established sensitization protocols in Aplysia, we trained the animals for LTS as we described recently,112 collected the central nervous system 1 h after training, isolated pleural, pedal, and abdominal ganglia from the central nervous system, fractionated the Golgi membrane and cytosolic materials, and conducted western blotting to evaluate PKA protein expression. Long-term sensitization experiments were conducted on 8-month-old (sexually mature), 80–120 g Aplysia. Each Aplysia was individually housed for five days, with feeding halted 24 h before the pre-test. On the day of the pre-test, the animals received four siphon touches, and the duration of siphon withdrawal (in seconds) was observed, averaged, and used to divide the animals into behavioral groups. On the test day, animals in the sensitization group received four tail shocks, each spaced 30 min apart. Each tail shock consisted of four trains, each lasting 1500 ms, with a shock rate of 0.33 pulses per second (PPS). The control group received mock tail shocks. Post-test, four siphon touch withdrawal assessments were performed 1 or 24 h after LTS or control training before tissue isolation. The pleural, pedal, and abdominal ganglia were collected 1 h after LTS or control training.

Preparation of total homogenate and golgi-enriched fractions for western blotting analysis

Total aplysia homogenates

Ganglia (plleural, pedal and abdominal) from both control and sensitized Aplysia were homogenized in SDS lysis buffer (two ganglia per sample). After homogenization, the resulting lysates were cleared of cell debris by centrifugation at 8000g for 10 min at 4°C. To quantify and analyze total and phosphorylated PKA proteins, equal amounts of protein from these cleared lysates (determined by BCA assay) were subjected to Western blotting. We used Phospho-PKA alpha/beta (Thr197) Recombinant Polyclonal Antibody (Invitrogen, 711615) for detecting phosphorylated PKA, while PKA alpha Polyclonal Antibody (Invitrogen, PA5-17626) for total PKA. The ratio of phosphorylated to total PKA protein was determined using ImageJ software.

Golgi-enriched fractions

To isolate Golgi-enriched fractions from Aplysia ganglia, we used the Minute Golgi apparatus Enrichment Kit (Invent Biotechnologies Inc, GO-037), following the manufacturer’s protocol with modifications to the centrifugation steps.

The ganglia from control and sensitized animals were homogenized in lysis buffer (Buffer A), and the resulting cell lysate was centrifuged at 900g for 5 min to remove cell debris. The cleared lysate was then centrifuged at 5000g for 10 min to obtain a supernatant free of less soluble proteins, such as lipids and plasma membrane proteins. The supernatant was subsequently centrifuged at 16,000g for 30 min, yielding a pellet that showed the highest signal for Golgi matrix proteins (GM130 antibody) in western blot analysis.

All centrifugation steps were performed at 4°C. Protein concentration in the fractions was quantified by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein from both the Golgi-enriched fractions and total homogenates of control and sensitized animals were loaded for western blotting to assess the levels of phosphorylated and total PKA.

Quantification and statistical analysis

p-values for unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, paired two-tailed Student’s t test, or one-way ANOVA were calculated in either Microsoft Excel or GraphPad Prism. Fisher or Tukey tests were used for post hoc analyses. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. The number of replications and statistical tests are described in each figure.
