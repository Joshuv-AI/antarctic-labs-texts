Single-Dose Oral Administration of Theophylline in Institute of Cancer Research Mice: A Central Nervous System Safety Pharmacology Evaluation using the Modified Irwin Test
Authors: Sung-Hyun Cho, Yeon-Woo Kim, Caglar Doguer, Youngseung Lee, Jung-Heun Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Theophylline, a methylxanthine-class bronchodilator that has anti-inflammatory properties, is clinically used to manage chronic respiratory disorders such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Although theophylline demonstrates therapeutic efficacy, it has a narrow therapeutic index and central nervous system (CNS) activity, which requires a detailed safety assessment. This study investigated the acute neurobehavioral and thermophysiological effects of orally administered theophylline in male Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) mice using the modified Irwin test. Single doses of 10, 30, or 100 mg/kg of theophylline were administered, followed by structured behavioral observations at 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 24 h post-dosing. Administration at concentrations of 30 and 100 mg/kg induced transient hyperthermia, increased spontaneous locomotion, and reversible motor coordination deficits between 1 and 6 h, with full recovery by 24 h. Tail elevation was observed only at concentrations of 30 mg/kg, whereas partial eyelid closure occurred exclusively in mice receiving 100 mg/kg administered doses. However, doses of 10 mg/kg did not produce measurable changes in any of these parameters. No notable alterations were detected in the abdominal tone, tremors, convulsions, respiration, righting reflex, salivation, lacrimation, skin color, or exophthalmos, thereby indicating the absence of systemic or generalized CNS toxicity. The modified Irwin test captured the subtle, regionally selective neurofunctional responses induced by theophylline, thus emphasizing its relevance in CNS safety pharmacology and supporting continued mechanistic investigations under clinically relevant exposure conditions.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic inflammatory respiratory diseases, such as bronchial asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), are major health concerns worldwide because of their progressive impairment of pulmonary function and considerable disease burden (McGeachie et al., 2016; GBD Chronic Respiratory Disease Collaborators, 2020). Bronchial asthma is characterized by reversible airflow obstruction as well as eosinophilic inflammation, whereas COPD involves irreversible airway narrowing accompanied by neutrophilic inflammation (Dey et al., 2023). The effective pharmacological management thereof requires agents that induce bronchodilation and attenuate pulmonary inflammation (Martinez et al., 2015). Among the pharmacological candidates, methylxanthine derivatives have been investigated for their bronchodilatory and anti-inflammatory potential, with theophylline exhibiting clinical efficacy for the management of obstructive pulmonary disorders (Rogliani et al., 2019). Despite theophylline’s demonstrated clinical benefits, it exhibits a narrow therapeutic index and interacts with multiple physiological systems (Hopkins and MacKenzie-Ross, 2016). Therefore, comprehensive neuropharmacological and systemic evaluations are required to define the safety profile of theophylline within therapeutic applications.

Theophylline is a dimethylated xanthine alkaloid of natural origin and the primary component of Camellia sinensis and Theobroma cacao (Attar and Altikatoglu Yapaoz, 2023; Sánchez et al., 2023). The clinical use of theophylline as a bronchodilator has previously been established in the treatment of obstructive pulmonary disorders, including asthma and COPD (Urbanova et al., 2016). The pharmacological mechanisms of theophylline include the nonselective inhibition of phosphodiesterase isoenzymes and antagonism of adenosine receptors as a competitive agent (Pardo et al., 2020). Moreover, phosphodiesterase inhibition elevates the levels of intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), thereby facilitating bronchial smooth muscle relaxation and sustained airway patency (Esneault et al., 2015). In addition, antagonism of adenosine receptors reduces the bronchoconstrictor responses and downregulates the proinflammatory signaling cascades within pulmonary tissues (Cho et al., 2025a). Although theophylline demonstrates therapeutic effectiveness, its narrow therapeutic range (10－20 mg/L) increases the risk of concentration-dependent toxicity (Cooney et al., 2016), in which supratherapeutic doses may result in neurotoxic outcomes, including psychomotor agitation, skeletal muscle tremors, generalized seizures, and coma (Zarrindast et al., 2002). Furthermore, the stimulation of medullary respiratory centers may further induce abnormal increases in respiratory activity (Cho et al., 2025b). Therefore, toxicological investigations using preclinical models are crucial to characterize neurophysiological safety under clinically relevant conditions.

A previous study conducted by Cho et al. (2025b) evaluated the respiratory pharmacodynamics of theophylline in male Sprague-Dawley rats. Theophylline was administered at doses of 0, 2.5, 5, and 10 mg/kg, and the respiratory rate, tidal volume, and minute ventilation volume were measured using whole-body plethysmography. The pharmacodynamic data revealed substantial increases in respiratory rate and minute ventilation at 5 and 10 mg/kg, without the corresponding changes in tidal volume, which suggests that theophylline elicits centrally mediated stimulation of respiratory activity. However, the absence of neurobehavioral assessments in the experimental protocol limited the interpretation of potential central nervous system (CNS) effects. Furthermore, the use of intravenous administration did not accurately reflect the pharmacokinetic characteristics associated with the oral delivery thereof, which is a clinically relevant route of exposure. This study addressed these previous limitations by orally administering theophylline in Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) mice and employed the modified Irwin test to evaluate the acute neurofunctional and behavioral alterations indicative of CNS activity.

The original Irwin test was developed in the 1960s and served as a broad observational method for identifying neuropharmacological activity in rodents by recording their spontaneous and stimulus-evoked behaviors following drug exposure (Irwin, 1968). Although the original Irwin test yielded qualitative data, the absence of standardized scoring criteria and defined observation intervals limited its reproducibility and cross-study comparability (Irwin, 1968). Therefore, the modified Irwin test was subsequently developed to address these limitations by incorporating a semi-quantitative scoring system and structured time points for the behavioral assessment (Mathiasen and Moser, 2023). This refined experimental protocol of the modified Irwin test enhanced the methodological consistency and regulatory applicability (Redfern et al., 2019). The modified experimental protocol included the systemic evaluation of locomotor activity, autonomic responses, reflexes, muscle tone, body temperature, and general appearance in a time-dependent manner. Furthermore, to improve functional interpretation and facilitate domain-specific analyses, each behavioral and physiological endpoint was classified into one of four categories, namely survival and general conditions, neuromuscular and motor responses, autonomic and physiological responses, and reflexes and sensory responses (Redfern et al., 2019). In addition, regulatory authorities, including those adhering to the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) S7A guidelines, recommend the use of the modified Irwin test for safety pharmacology assessments targeting CNS function (Food and Drug Administration, HHS, 2001). Given that theophylline exerts stimulatory effects on the central neural circuits, particularly via the antagonism of adenosine receptors, and induces systemic pharmacological effects beyond the respiratory system, the modified Irwin test enables the sensitive detection of neurobehavioral alterations that may not be identified using conventional respiratory or physiological endpoints.

This study evaluated the acute neurofunctional effects of oral theophylline administration in ICR mice. The modified Irwin test was employed as a structured behavioral assessment to evaluate the CNS responses, including thermoregulation, spontaneous locomotor activity, motor coordination, and autonomic indicators such as palpebral closure and tail elevation (Mathiasen and Moser, 2023). To ensure regulatory compliance, the experimental protocol followed the safety pharmacology framework established by the ICH S7A guidelines (Food and Drug Administration, HHS, 2001). The current investigation expands upon previous research that emphasized respiratory outcomes by providing insights into the specific neurobehavioral and autonomic effects under conditions of the oral administration of theophylline.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of 32 male ICR mice, 6 weeks of age, were supplied by Koatech for use in the experimental protocol. The animals were housed at CentralBio under controlled environmental conditions, which included a 12-h light/dark cycle (150－300 lux), ambient temperatures of 21.5°C－22.7°C, relative humidity of 47.6%－54.0%, and 15-18 air exchanges/h (Yoo et al., 2023). The environmental indices were automatically monitored every 10 min and recorded hourly to ensure stability. The experimental mice were provided ad libitum access to ultraviolet (UV)-sterilized filtered tap water and irradiated standard rodent chow (1314 IRR, Altromin Spezialfutter GmbH & Co. KG) throughout the study duration unless specifically fasted. Upon completion of the study, the experimental mice were humanely euthanized using CO2 gas inhalation in accordance with the institutional ethical guidelines. All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of CentralBio Co., Ltd. (approval number CBIACUC_22-0410SPI).

The study design involved the evaluation of systemic pharmacodynamic responses following the gastrointestinal absorption of orally administered theophylline. A single oral dose of theophylline was administered to each ICR mouse using a stainless-steel gavage needle. The experimental mice were fasted for 3－4 h prior to dose administration, with continuous access to UV-sterilized filtered water. Standard chow was reintroduced 3 h post-dosing. The administration volume of the theophylline solution was fixed at 20 mL/kg and individually adjusted based on the mouse’s body weight measured immediately before treatment.

Theophylline (Sigma-Aldrich) was weighed using an analytical balance and suspended in 0.5% (w/v) carboxymethyl cellulose sodium salt dissolved in triple-distilled water. To ensure uniform dispersion, vortex mixing was employed, and the suspension was diluted to final concentrations of 0.5, 1.5, and 5 mg/mL. All formulations were freshly prepared on the day of administration. Theophylline was administered at doses of 10, 30, or 100 mg/kg. The dose selection was guided by previously reported pharmacodynamic studies in Sprague-Dawley rats and translated to mice using interspecies scaling based on the body surface area normalization principles (Nair and Jacob, 2016). The relative Km factors for rats and mice (6 and 3, respectively) were employed to guide the estimation of equivalent mouse doses. The highest dose (100 mg/kg) was selected based on previous studies, which demonstrated significant thermoregulatory changes in mice without resulting in lethality (Zarrindast et al., 2002). The 30 mg/kg dose represented an intermediate range, which allowed for the assessment of pharmacologically relevant neurobehavioral modulation, whereas the 10 mg/kg dose approximated the low-dose threshold below which systemic or CNS effects are typically absent (Cho et al., 2025b). This stepwise dose design was implemented to capture the transition from physiological to pharmacological responses under controlled oral exposure conditions.

Body temperature was measured using a lubricated rectal probe that was connected to a digital thermometer (Kent Scientific Corp.). The measurements were performed by inserting the probe approximately 2 cm into the rectum and recording the stable readings under standardized environmental conditions to minimize variability.

To assess the neurobehavioral effects of theophylline, the modified Irwin test was used, which is a validated protocol for evaluating CNS activity following acute pharmacological exposure in rodents. The assessment consisted of 18 predefined observational endpoints including autonomic, somatic, and neurophysiological domains, namely mortality, diarrhea, piloerection, exophthalmos, catalepsy, limb traction, pinna reflex, spontaneous locomotion, tremor, convulsion, eyelid closure, tail elevation, abdominal tone, righting reflex, peripheral skin color, salivation, lacrimation, and respiratory rate (Cho et al., 2025a). All behavioral observations were performed at six time points, including at baseline (0 h) and at 1, 2, 4, 6, and 24 h following theophylline administration (Cho et al., 2025a). A summary of the behavioral endpoints and scoring criteria is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 25, IBM Corp.). A one-way analysis of variance was used to identify statistically significant differences among the treatment groups, and the homogeneity of variance was verified using Levene’s test. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. The findings were interpreted in accordance with previously established methodological references (Amelly et al., 2023; Cho et al., 2024; Cho et al., 2025c).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

A single oral administration of theophylline at doses of 30 and 100 mg/kg in male ICR mice induced dose-dependent, transient, and reversible alterations in thermoregulation and neurobehavioral parameters. Administration of 30 mg/kg of theophylline elevated the core body temperature to 36.4±0.6°C at 1 h and was maintained at an elevated level of 36.3±0.5°C at 2 h. Moreover, the administration of 100 mg/kg of theophylline resulted in a statistically significant increase in body temperature to 36.6±0.4°C at 4 h (P<0.05; Table 1). This theophylline-induced hyperthermia between 1 and 6 h post-dosing likely reflects central thermogenic activation mediated by the pharmacological antagonism of adenosine A1 and A2A receptors, which are localized in the hypothalamic and brainstem nuclei (Devereaux and Pamenter, 2023). Antagonism of the adenosine receptors facilitates sympathetic outflow and metabolic activation, thereby promoting heat generation (Zarrindast et al., 2002). Previous studies have reported that blocking adenosine receptors enhances sympathetic tone and increases energy expenditure, which is consistent with the observed elevations in body temperature (Dall’Igna et al., 2003). Furthermore, nitric oxide (NO) signaling is known to modulate theophylline-induced temperature responses. Previous studies have shown that NO synthase inhibitors reduce hyperthermia, whereas NO precursors promote hypothermic effects at high doses, which suggests that NO acts downstream or in parallel to adenosine receptor antagonism (Zarrindast et al., 2002). The core body temperature returned to baseline by 24 h in all treatment groups, thus indicating that the theophylline-induced thermophysiological alterations were reversible and temporally constrained.

Administration of theophylline at a dose of 10 mg/kg significantly decreased the core body temperature at 24 h (35.3±0.3°C, P<0.05; Table 1). However, the absence of a dose-dependent trend and the lack of an early-phase effect suggest that this hypothermic response at 10 mg/kg may reflect a nonspecific physiological fluctuation rather than a meaningful pharmacological action. The delayed onset and low magnitude of the temperature reduction imply that the administration of 10 mg/kg of theophylline failed to engage the central thermoregulatory mechanisms. This view that low-dose theophylline lacks pharmacologically meaningful thermoregulatory effects is supported by previous studies, which demonstrated that low doses of methylxanthines did not consistently alter core body temperature in rodents (Katyal and Gupta, 2012). Collectively, this evidence suggests a threshold effect for central thermophysiological modulation of theophylline. The hyperthermia observed at doses of 30 and 100 mg/kg, accompanied by behavioral activation, appears to reflect the coordinated neurochemical responses involving adenosinergic and NO-dependent pathways. The present findings highlight theophylline’s dose- and time-dependent central effects and support the use of core body temperature as a complementary endpoint alongside neurobehavioral parameters in safety pharmacology studies.

Administration of 30 mg/kg of theophylline elicited a marked increase in spontaneous locomotor activity in six animals during the 1－2 h period after dosing, with an additional animal exhibiting hyperactivity at 4 h post-dosing. Administration of 100 mg/kg of theophylline induced comparable hyperactivity in three animals, although the frequency was lower than that observed at 30 mg/kg. Grade 6 hyperactivity was fully resolved after 6 h in both groups (Table 2). The increased locomotion observed following the administration of 30 and 100 mg/kg of theophylline likely resulted from dopaminergic and noradrenergic stimulation, which was mediated by phosphodiesterase inhibition and elevation of intracellular cAMP concentrations (Katyal and Gupta, 2012; Devereaux and Pamenter, 2023). These motor coordination deficits were concurrently observed in six animals from the 30 and 100 mg/kg groups, as evidenced by the traction test failure during the 1－2 h period. Full recovery of motor performance occurred by 4 h post-dosing (Table 2). Coordination impairments may result from disruption of the postural control mechanisms that involve the cerebellar, vestibular, or somatosensory integration pathways (Horak et al., 1990). Moreover, adenosine receptor antagonism and the subsequent alteration of intracellular signaling could further contribute to destabilized motor output (Clark and Dar, 1988). Heightened motor drive combined with insufficient proprioceptive stabilization likely exacerbated functional instability, and acute fatigue or dysregulated arousal states may serve as additional modulators of performance.

Dose-dependent alterations in the locomotor and coordination behaviors were evident following theophylline administration, revealing a distinct pattern in the CNS. Neurobehavioral excitation was characterized by hyperlocomotion and impaired motor coordination, which was most pronounced at 30 mg/kg of theophylline and consistent with the previously established stimulant profile of methylxanthines in rodent models (Nehlig et al., 1992). Phosphodiesterase inhibition and subsequent cAMP accumulation potentiate dopaminergic and adrenergic neurotransmission, thereby enhancing the motor output and behavioral arousal state. The administration of 100 mg/kg of theophylline, despite representing a higher exposure, induced fewer instances of hyperactivity, which suggests a biphasic dose-response pattern. Compensatory mechanisms, such as presynaptic autoreceptor activation, receptor desensitization, and recruitment of inhibitory neural networks, may attenuate the behavioral expression at higher doses. The absence of excitation in the 10 mg/kg group further supported the identification of a dose threshold below which central activation was not elicited under current experimental conditions. Previous preclinical studies have reported similar phenomena, wherein the intermediate doses of methylxanthines enhanced exploratory behavior and seizure susceptibility, but high doses disrupted the motor coordination and induced sedation or ataxia (Mohammad et al., 1993; Sbaraglia et al., 2017). The findings of this study align with previous observations and confirm the dose-dependent, nonlinear behavioral effects of theophylline in mice.

Distinct autonomic and physiological signs emerged in a dose- and parameter-specific manner following the administration of theophylline. Administration of 30 mg/kg of theophylline induced tail elevation (Grade 2) in four animals at 1 h post-dosing, which suggests increased excitability of the spinal motor neurons or heightened spinal reflex gain (Table 3). The absence of tail elevation in the 100 mg/kg group indicated suppression of reflex-mediated activity, potentially because of impaired motor coordination or central fatigue. Tail elevation serves as a behavioral indicator of spinal motor system excitability (Nantwi and Goshgarian, 2002); however, the absence of electrophysiological or pharmacological validation may limit the current interpretation. Administration of 100 mg/kg of theophylline resulted in partial eyelid closure corresponding to Grade 2 ptosis (approximately 50% palpebral narrowing) in four animals at 1 h, which was not observed in the lower-dose groups (Table 3). This transient narrowing of the palpebral fissure may reflect oculomotor inhibition, neuromuscular fatigue, or sedation associated with increased central excitatory activity. The involvement of cholinergic dysfunction or generalized CNS inhibition requires further exploration with receptor-selective antagonists or electromyography.

Autonomic and physiological responses to the administration of theophylline manifest in a dose- and region-specific manner, thus reflecting the selective modulation of spinal and cranial motor circuits. The tail elevation observed at 30 mg/kg likely resulted from increased spinal excitability, whereas the absence of this reflex at 100 mg/kg supports a functional suppression model, which is consistent with excessive central stimulation that led to motor dysregulation. Eyelid ptosis at 100 mg/kg suggests the transient impairment of oculomotor function or central neuromuscular fatigue, potentially linked to the methylxanthine-induced disturbances in calcium signaling or ATP availability within the brainstem motor nuclei (López-Cruz et al., 2014). Assessment of observed items across all functional domains, namely survival and general condition (death, skin color); neuromuscular and motor responses (tremors, convulsions, catalepsy, traction, abdominal tone); autonomic and physiological responses (respiration rate, salivation, lacrimation, piloerection, diarrhea, exophthalmos); and reflexes and sensory responses (righting reflex, pinna reflex), indicates that theophylline did not produce generalized CNS toxicity (Supplementary Table 2). Instead, theophylline selectively modulated discrete neurofunctional pathways, which aligns with the established sensitivity of the modified Irwin test for detecting subtle central effects mediated by adenosinergic and monoaminergic signaling.

The administration of 30 mg/kg of theophylline produced the most consistent neurobehavioral and thermoregulatory alterations in male ICR mice, including hyperthermia, increased locomotion, tail elevation, and impaired motor coordination, without inducing systemic toxicity, as indicated by the absence of mortality and abnormal findings in the general clinical signs. The administration of 100 mg/kg of theophylline induced similar effects but at lower frequencies, potentially because of receptor saturation, feedback inhibition, or reduced neural responsiveness. The administration of 10 mg/kg of theophylline did not induce measurable behavioral or physiological changes. The 30 mg/kg dose elicited distinct behavioral phenotypes, which suggests a favorable balance between the pharmacological response and CNS tolerability. Notably, hyperactivity and elevated core body temperature were concurrently observed at 30 and 100 mg/kg, thus suggesting coordinated activation of the central excitatory pathways. The alignment between behavioral stimulation and the thermogenic response implies shared underlying mechanisms, including adenosine receptor antagonism and the downstream modulation of dopaminergic and sympatho-excitatory systems. This concurrent manifestation of hyperactivity and increased core body temperature reinforces the interpretation that theophylline induces a dose-dependent stimulation of the CNS.

The present findings underscore the necessity for a detailed neuropharmacological evaluation of theophylline, particularly when administered at elevated or prolonged exposure levels. Although administration at low doses effectively supports respiratory function with minimal CNS involvement, the clinical use of theophylline remains limited by its narrow therapeutic index. The experimental limitations of this study include the absence of neurotransmitter quantification, receptor assays, and electrophysiological data, which preclude mechanistic validation. The semiquantitative behavioral scoring lacks high temporal precision and is susceptible to observer bias. Despite these constraints, this study employed clinically relevant oral dosing and a validated behavioral test system with time-structured observations. The observed dose- and domain-specific alterations support the classification of theophylline as a selective modulator of central neural circuits. Future studies that incorporate chronic exposure models, neurochemical markers, and region-targeted recordings will clarify these mechanisms and identify the long-term neurotoxic thresholds. Extending the dosing regimens may help determine whether the thermophysiological alterations persist, adapt, or intensify over time, thereby providing insight into the chronic impact of methylxanthine-induced adenosinergic modulation.
