Knowledge, attitudes, and practice of infection control in radiation therapy departments
Authors: Dania Abu Awwad, Sarah Lewis, Suzanne Hill, Marlin Karzon, Peter Kench, Yobelli Jimenez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Radiation therapy staff showed very high infection control knowledge and positive attitudes.•High IPC knowledge did not correlate with consistent clinical practice.•Time pressure was the main barrier to standard IPC precaution adherence.•Communication failures limited adherence to transmission‑based precautions.•Radiation therapy departments reported strong organisational support for IPC practices.

Introduction

Infection prevention and control (IPC) in healthcare settings is important, and healthcare workers are responsible for maintaining the highest standards of patient safety during the delivery of care [1]. Medical radiation practitioners play a vital role in patients’ diagnostic and treatment journeys, often managing a high workload and rapid patient throughput [2]. Patients attend radiation therapy departments for scanning and treatment, and adequate IPC measures are necessary to prevent cross-contamination between patients. This is particularly important as patients presenting to the radiation therapy department with infectious diseases cannot be isolated or placed in negative-pressure rooms, and disease transmission can occur at any point during a scanning or treatment session, both within and outside the examination rooms [3].

Previous studies on radiographers have found high IPC knowledge scores but lower practice adherence [4]. Common cited barriers were the high workload within radiology departments [5], which is a common barrier among other health professionals, including nurses, doctors, and allied health professionals [6], [7]. Radiation therapy has similar challenges to radiology, but the overall workflow, patient interactions, and equipment used present unique challenges and IPC requirements [2], [8]. Many patients present for cancer treatment are immunocompromised, and radiation therapists operate multiple equipment across the radiation therapy department [1], [8], [9]. Additionally, immobilisation devices may be shared across multiple patients, and radiation therapists have close physical proximity to patients during procedures such as tattooing and adjusting patient positioning [8], [9].

While a study among healthcare workers found that those with more patient contact had higher IPC knowledge [10], little is known about IPC knowledge among radiation therapists. Recent literature on infection control and radiation therapy has focused on Covid-19, and were often commentary in nature [1], [2], [8]. Ku et al., [2] reported a lack of consistent infection control procedures across different departments from different states in Australia, whilst Shanahan & Akudjedu [9], reported on work-related stress associated with increased IPC requirements during the Covid-19 pandemic. Hence, there remains a lack of understanding of radiation therapy staff’s IPC knowledge and the challenges radiation therapy departments face in adhering to optimal IPC practices. The aim of this study was to explore the barriers and facilitators to adherence to proper IPC practices in radiation therapy departments and to assess the IPC knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of radiation therapy staff.

Methods

A national, cross-sectional survey was developed targeting infection control practices and knowledge among staff in computed tomography (CT) departments, using the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) IPC guidelines [11], [12]. Our team developed this survey and adapted it for the current study by replacing all references to CT with radiation therapy and updating the focus on radiation therapy-specific equipment. The survey and a participant information statement with study details were distributed to radiation therapists across Australia through clinical sites offering radiation therapy services, where educators were asked to share it with their staff and colleagues. The study was also broadcast through the Australian Society of Medical Imaging and Radiation Therapy (ASMIRT) newsletter, the national society for radiation therapists in Australia. The survey remained open between November 2024 and March 2025. The study received ethics approval from the University of Sydney’s Human Research Ethics Committee (2022/493) and all participants were required to provide informed consent before accessing the survey.

The first part of the survey covered demographics, including participants’ age and role in their current radiation therapy department. Questions regarding the radiation therapy department, such as who works there and the number of patients treated, were also posed. Participants were subsequently asked to reflect on the IPC information available to them, the types of support present, and the workplace culture. Using open-ended questions, participants could reflect on the circumstances that impact their ability to adhere to standard and transmission-based precautions.

The last section of the survey included a series of questions on knowledge, attitudes and practice. Knowledge questions followed a ‘true’ and ‘false’ format, and a score was allocated for each correct response, with no score for incorrect responses. Each participant’s total knowledge score was then calculated. Attitude questions ranged from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’ on a 5-point Likert scale. Practice questions ranged from ‘always’ to ‘never’ on a 5-point Likert scale. Two questions ranged from ‘between every patient’ to ‘never’ on a 7-point Likert scale to assess the frequency of IPC practices, including ‘once a day’ and ‘once a week’. There were nine questions for each KAP component, and these questions were designed to correspond with one another across the components. For example, under knowledge, participants were presented with the statement “Hand hygiene is indicated after the removal of gloves”, and the corresponding statement in attitudes and practice was “I consider hand hygiene to be warranted after removing gloves” and “I use hand hygiene after removing gloves” respectively.

Open-ended questions were analysed using content analysis. Responses from each participant were coded, and the frequency with which each item was mentioned was calculated and presented descriptively. For each KAP component, scores were generated and compared using Spearman’s correlation. Scores above 0.5 indicate a strong correlation, 0.3–0.5 a moderate correlation, and 0.1–0.3 a weak correlation [13]. The p-value was set to 0.05, and Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 31 was used for statistical analysis (IBM Corp, 2025) [14].

Results

Demographics and workplace practice

A total of 58 staff working in radiation therapy departments completed the survey. Participants were predominantly female (85%), had more than 10 years of experience (66%), and worked part-time (60%) (Table 1). Regarding the participants’ roles, five held leadership positions, while the remaining cohort included two magnetic resonance radiographers, four nurses, one clinical educator, and one staff member in quality improvement. Overall, 51 participants had a radiation therapy background. All participants were registered health professionals and worked in public hospitals, 95% of whom worked in New South Wales (NSW). Most participants reported that their workplace has a dedicated IPC team (76%), while 9% were unsure. The majority received IPC training through their workplace within the past year (86%), while the remainder had training in the past 1–3 years (12%) or more than five years ago (2%). Policies and guidelines were the most common source of IPC information (97%), followed by colleagues (72%) and workshops or courses (59%). Less popular sources of information included product manuals (29%), internet searches (19%), and application specialists (9%).

Regarding departmental IPC practices, 98% of participants agreed that hand hygiene products are easily accessible in their department (Fig. 1). This was consistent between staff in metropolitan and rural sites. There was also strong agreement that participants’ departments emphasised the importance of IPC, supported staff in staying home when unwell, and allowed workers to determine the most appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE). There was lower consensus regarding the availability of equipment with in-built safety features such as retractable needles. Compared to metropolitan sites, staff from rural sites had slightly higher overall agreement (strongly agree and agree responses) regarding feeling supported in staying home when unwell (95% vs 87%), determining what PPE to use (84% vs 77%), and having equipment with in-built safety features (26% vs 15%).Fig. 1Infection control and safety practices in radiation therapy departments.

In terms of practice, 83% of participants reported that they do not find it difficult to prioritise IPC practices when busy. However, this figure drops to 53% for tasks involving the insertion and maintenance of invasive devices using aseptic technique. Through open-ended responses, participants were asked when they were more likely to not adhere to standard precautions and transmission-based precautions (Fig. 2). While 22% would always adhere to correct standard precautions, many participants gave examples of scenarios that might influence their practice. The most common factor that could impede adherence to standard precautions was time, including being busy, overbooked, or running behind schedule. Participants shared personal factors such as loss of focus, personal choice, or their work sequence, which means they aren’t using hand hygiene between touching the patient and the machines. For emergency scenarios, participants shared scenarios explaining their reasoning, with one participant stating “if something happens quickly and your natural reaction is to help the patient”. PPE availability was less of an issue, but participants noted it would be a limiting factor if the stock ran out but was not yet refilled. The biggest reported challenge influencing transmission-based precautions was poor communication, where participants shared examples of being unaware that a patient required additional infection precautions. This stemmed from a lack of verbal handover, insufficient patient notes, with participants stating that “I was not notified verbally and/or in patient records that this is required” and “I am not aware of the need to, i.e. outpatients have no investigations alerts on precautions required”.Fig. 2Factors influencing lack of adherence among participants for standard and transmission-based precautions.

Knowledge, attitudes, and practice

Participants scored highly for knowledge, with an average of 98% (±5.3%), ranging from 78% to 100%. Across all the knowledge questions, more than 90% of participants selected the correct response. In terms of attitudes, scores were also high, with the lowest agreement for “I consider patients to be a source of infection regardless of their diagnosis” at 86%, while the rest of the attitude statements achieved more than 90% agreement. Simultaneously, practice scores were high, with more than 90% of participants selecting ‘always’ and ‘frequently’ for all items except “I clean custom patient immobilisation devices,” which was 88%. Breaking down the data further, 69% of participants ‘always’ used standard precautions for all patients and 79% ‘always’ cleaned radiation therapy equipment. Knowledge and attitude scores had a significant moderate correlation, where higher knowledge scores were correlated with higher attitudes scores (rho = 0.432, p < 0.001). Discussion

Participants in this study from radiation therapy departments demonstrated strong knowledge of infection control and have supportive structures in place at their workplaces to help adhere to proper IPC practices. While time pressures may impact adherence to standard precautions, communication, or lack thereof, was a more pertinent issue when it came to implementing transmission-based precautions. Overall, staff had positive attitudes towards IPC, and the majority of participants had recent IPC training.

Most radiation therapy staff relied on policies and guidelines for IPC information, while colleagues still played a large role. This can include colleagues in senior positions and underscores the need for all staff to have accurate knowledge, as peer influence strongly shapes behaviour. This was also seen among radiographers, where colleagues were the primary source of information for contrast injector use and problems with contrast administration in CT departments [15]. Additionally, modality-specific IPC training is also often conducted by colleagues, which has long-term influences on the adherence and accuracy of staff IPC practices [16], [17]. A positive finding among staff in the radiation therapy departments was that most had received IPC training within the past year. This aligns with national IPC guidelines, which emphasise that IPC education should be ongoing, and encourages facilities to provide IPC training annually to ensure staff maintain current knowledge and skills [12].

Participants in this study reported positive workplace cultures within their departments, with most participants agreeing that their departments emphasise the importance of IPC, are supportive when unwell staff take leave, and support staff autonomy in selecting appropriate PPE. This was higher than that reported by radiographers and nurses in another Australian study [4]. Staff working in radiation therapy departments are caring for a higher proportion of cancer patients than radiographers, and patients are often immunocompromised, which may explain the higher level of support for IPC practices within radiation therapy departments [2]. This is essential, as departmental leadership play an important role in streamlining communication on safety and infection control, which would otherwise lead to inconsistent practices and confusion among staff [2]. During the surge of COVID-19, Wei et al. [8] reported that their departmental leadership enforced changes to tackle the risk of infection spread, and there was no transfer of COVID-19 within the department after implementing modified workflows, cleaning procedures, and staff training [8]. The lessons learned during this time, such as strong leadership, diligent cleaning between patients, and staff training, can and should be implemented within departments irrespective of COVID-19.

The main challenge in adhering to proper IPC for standard precautions, as reported by study participants, was time. Additional challenges included personal factors, such as the workflow staff follow and distractions, as well as emergency scenarios, such as a patient needing immediate attention. Time was still a challenge for transmission-based precautions, but communication was identified as the main issue. This included written communication such as the lack of patient’s infectious status on hospital information systems, or poor handover between ward staff and the radiation therapy department. Time was similarly noted as a challenge in radiography settings with additional pressures from poor staffing [5]. Although emergencies are less common in radiation therapy, staff’s instinct to respond quickly to patients in urgent situations, combined with time pressures, has been noted in previous studies [18], [19]. Communication is also a common issue among radiology departments and heavily impacts staff’s ability to implement additional PPE precautions for patients with transmission-based precautions when there is no note or associated communication [5]. As with any healthcare worker, radiation therapists are at risk of exposure to infections, particularly when a patient’s infection status is not communicated or the patient is unaware they have an infectious disease [8]. Hence, it is imperative that strategies are implemented to reduce or eliminate the number of occasions where radiation therapy staff were informed of a patient’s infectious status during or after a patient’s attendance to the department.

Overall, staff in radiation therapy departments had near-perfect knowledge scores and high agreement across attitude statements. While, most IPC practices should ‘always’ be performed to align with national guidelines [12], more than 90% of participants reported that they always or frequently applied these IPC practices. Of note, practice was not correlated with either knowledge or attitudes. The strongest practice performance was applying standard precautions when handling radiation therapy equipment, but the lowest adherence was observed in cleaning the equipment. This may indicate that participants consistently performed hand hygiene before and after patient contact, which is part of standard precautions and helps prevent the transfer of infections from patients to equipment. If correct IPC practices are followed and equipment is only touched with sanitised or washed hands, this would help maintain surface cleanliness [20]. However, this does not account for equipment that is directly touched by patients. Therefore, caution is needed when interpreting these results, as it remains unclear whether the lower rates of equipment cleaning reflect reliance on good hand hygiene or gaps in cleaning protocols.

Limitations

While the study sample is heavily skewed towards public departments in NSW and has a small sample size, it covers a variety of workplaces and experiences, including a third from rural locations, many seniors or radiation therapists in leadership roles, and workplaces with a wide range of daily patient numbers. Staff other than radiation therapists were included in the study, which may have influenced the results. It is unclear whether their responses reflect practices shaped by the culture and protocols of radiation therapy departments or their original professional training. This introduces potential variability in the data, as different professional backgrounds may have distinct infection control priorities and experiences. However, including these staff is important because they work closely with radiation therapists and contribute to infection control practices within the radiation therapy department.

The majority of this survey was quantitative, and there was some misalignment between the KAP factors, with practice not always matching participants’ knowledge or attitudes. Further qualitative studies could help clarify these findings by enabling participants to expand on their experiences and expectations, including how and when IPC training is delivered within their departments. Further studies could also facilitate a deeper exploration of organisational IPC processes across a broader cross‑section of Australian radiation therapy services, including the private sector, to better capture national variation in IPC practice.

Conclusion

Surveyed staff in radiation therapy departments demonstrated high knowledge of IPC and reported positive attitudes towards IPC practices. However, despite strong knowledge levels, there were some inconsistencies in practice, where the frequency of implementing IPC practices can be improved. Communication breakdown has been identified as a challenge in adhering to proper PPE when caring for patients with infectious diseases, but overall, participants had supportive structures in place within their workplaces that fostered a strong IPC culture.
