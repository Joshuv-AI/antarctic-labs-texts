Student-led teaching and learning initiatives in nutrition education for medical and health students: a scoping review
Authors: Sean Yong, Lauren Ball, Jørgen Torgerstuen Johnsen, Breanna Lepre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnhealthy diets are a leading cause of death, yet nutrition education for medical and healthcare professionals remains inadequate internationally. Beyond ‘top-down’ curriculum and accreditation initiatives, student-led teaching and learning initiatives typically commence to fill curricular gaps. This scoping review aimed to synthesise evidence on student-led nutrition education initiatives for medical and health students.MethodsFollowing JBI methodology for scoping reviews, MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, ERIC and Web of Science were searched for studies from any country and year that related to student-led nutrition education initiatives for medical and health students. A grey literature search ensured a comprehensive overview of available literature. Data were synthesised narratively.ResultsTwenty articles published between 2012 and 2024 were included, and most were from the USA (n=15). Student-led nutrition education initiatives included culinary education (n=11, 55%), placement opportunities (n=10, 50%), lecture series (n=7, 35%) and student interest groups (n=6, 30%). Twelve studies (60%) included more than one type of initiative. Initiatives mostly targeted medical students (n=13, 65%), and four were interdisciplinary (20%). Studies included students from undergraduate or preclinical years through to practising health professionals. Initiatives were delivered by students, physicians, dietitians, and other health professionals, mostly in-person (n=12, 60%) within university (n=12, 60%) or community (n=7, 35%) settings. Thirteen studies evaluated the initiative(s) using questionnaires (n=10, 50%), informal feedback (n=2, 10%) or focus groups (n=1, 5%). Of these, seven studies (54%) reported improved confidence and competence in nutrition among the students involved.ConclusionsStudent-led nutrition education initiatives are likely to be well received and may improve nutrition competence. These initiatives are a promising approach to address the persistent gap in nutrition education for medical and health students.

Introduction

Nutrition plays an important role in supporting health and well-being.1 Poor nutrition contributes to mortality and disability worldwide, with 11 million deaths each year associated with dietary risk factors,2 and diet plays a major role in the development and management of chronic disease.3 4 While dietitians have specialist knowledge and skills in nutrition, other health professionals, particularly those with regular patient contact, can support individuals to eat well. For example, general practitioners are well placed within the community to provide nutrition advice and are regarded as the preferred provider of nutrition care by patients.5 Practice and hospital nurses play an important role in supporting nutrition care based on their frequency of time spent with patients, and role in recording dietary intake.6 7 Other community-based health professionals, such as pharmacists and dentists, are also ideally placed to support healthy diets for their patients, given their frequent engagement with the community.8–10 The delivery of nutrition advice by these health professionals would have a positive downstream impact on individual dietary behaviours and therefore population health.11

Despite the potential role of health professionals in nutrition care, medical students and physicians report a low level of nutrition competency.12–14 Low knowledge, confidence and competence in nutrition care have also been reported among other health professionals including nurses,15 16 pharmacists17 18 and physiotherapists.19 Health professionals have reported uncertainty as to what basic nutrition care comprises and low self-efficacy in facilitating patients’ dietary behaviour change.12 The low nutrition competency and confidence in delivering nutrition care can be attributed in a large part to inadequate nutrition education and training.13 20 A lack of nutrition education and assessment contributes to low competence and confidence in delivering nutrition care, low prioritisation of nutrition during time-pressured consults, confusion as to when to refer to a dietitian, and lack of confidence managing conversations with patients about diet and nutrition.12 13 21 Ultimately, the inadequacy of nutrition education for health professionals is a barrier to the delivery of nutrition care in practice.

Previous attempts to increase or improve nutrition education within medical and health professions’ curricula have resulted in modest success.12 22 23 Barriers to the inclusion of nutrition education in curricula for health professions include an already crowded curriculum, a lack of suitable faculty to teach nutrition, a focus on therapeutic rather than preventative care (ie, the biomedical model), and a lack of incentive, as accreditation standards globally have historically neglected to include nutrition.20 21 24 25 Nutrition education for medical and health professionals remains inadequate regardless of country, setting, and year of study,20 warranting further exploration.

Student-led teaching and learning initiatives, defined as any initiative initiated and/or conducted primarily by students and occurring outside of formal course requirements,26 have shown promise in filling learning gaps in other health fields, such as pharmacy and medicine, including student-led community clinics, peer-led education on topics not covered in the curriculum (eg, lecture series or tutorials) and advocacy efforts for diversity, inclusion and student well-being among health students.27 28 Medical and pharmacy students participating in student-led teaching and learning initiatives reported outcomes including meaningful learning experiences, increased knowledge and improved student well-being and transition into the workforce.27–30 Given the barriers to nutrition education for health professionals, there is a need to explore other routes of education outside of formal curricula or training. Understanding how student-led teaching and learning initiatives may play a role in nutrition education can provide an insight into opportunities to address current barriers. However, the literature on student-led nutrition education initiatives for medical and health students is yet to be synthesised. This scoping review aimed to synthesise literature on nutrition education initiatives led by students within medical and health disciplines.

Methods

Given the variety of different methods a student-led teaching and learning initiative could involve, scoping review methodology was opted for over a systematic review in order to provide a comprehensive picture of the available literature on this topic.31 This scoping review was conducted in alignment with Arksey and O’Malley32 and the Joanna Briggs Institute,33 and data were reported in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting guidelines.34 The protocol for this review was registered prospectively with Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/gznpj/).

Eligibility criteria

This scoping review aimed to identify and describe the characteristics of student-led nutrition education initiatives for medical and health students internationally. The PCC (Population, Concept, Context) framework was used to identify the main concepts for this review.35

Population

To provide a defined inclusion criteria for professions, the target population for student-led nutrition education initiatives was health disciplines accredited by the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA) and self-regulating health disciplines including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Practice, Chinese Medicine, Chiropractic, Dental, Medical, Medical Radiation Practice, Nursing and Midwifery, Occupational Therapy, Optometry, Osteopathy, Pharmacy, Physiotherapy, Podiatry, Psychology, Nutrition and Dietetics, Exercise and Sports Science, Audiology, Speech Pathology and Social Work. These disciplines provide patient care and could play a role in delivering and/or supporting nutrition care as part of a multidisciplinary team.36 For studies outside of Australia, we mapped reported disciplines to the closest AHPRA-recognised profession.

We included student-led nutrition education initiatives in all settings, including the community, university, clinical settings, placement and others. Student-led teaching and learning initiatives at any level or stage of education for the above health disciplines were included.

Concept

We included two concepts in this review: (1) student-led teaching and learning initiatives, and (2) nutrition education initiatives for medical and health students.

Initiatives that were designed, initiated, delivered and/or governed by students in the included medical and health professional disciplines were eligible for inclusion in this review. Initiatives where students volunteered or opted for an elective or extracurricular course to drive their own nutrition education were included as this was still considered student-initiated education. Nutrition education initiatives that were delivered as part of formal learning or curricula were excluded.

Context

Studies which described and/or evaluated a student-led nutrition education initiative for medical and health students in any setting were included. The outcome of the study needed to: (1) describe the student-led nutrition education initiative; (2) list the organisers and/or participants and/or (3) describe the outcomes of the initiative.

Any type of primary study with any sample size was included. Reviews related to student-led nutrition education initiatives for medical and health students were excluded, but reference lists were hand-searched for additional articles that met the inclusion criteria. Grey and white literature were included to ensure that we provided a comprehensive synthesis of the available literature on this topic. No restrictions were placed on date or geographical region; however, only articles published in English were included.

Search strategy

Reviews related to the concepts of this review were searched to create a preliminary list of search terms.20 26 37 An initial search strategy was developed in conjunction with an experienced health librarian and the search strategy was subsequently reviewed and adjusted by the entire research team.

A search strategy, including MeSH terms, was used to search MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, ERIC and Web of Science. The search strategy was translated for other databases using The Polyglot Search Translator.38 The full search strategy is available in online supplemental materials.

To identify grey and white literature, a Google search using the search terms ‘student-led initiative’, ‘nutrition education’, ‘medical’ and ‘healthcare’ as a single query was undertaken. The first five pages of results were reviewed for relevant articles to include.

Screening and selection

Covidence (Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) was used for screening and selection of articles for this review. The first 100 articles were screened using title and abstract in triplicate by three researchers (BL, LB and SY), and revisions to the inclusion criteria were discussed. The remaining titles and abstracts were screened in duplicate. All researchers reviewed full texts in duplicate in alignment with the inclusion criteria. Any conflicts were resolved through discussion.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data extraction was conducted using Covidence. The research team met to reach an agreement on the information and level of detail to be extracted. Extraction was split evenly among all researchers, who conducted extraction independently, then each article was checked by a second researcher.

The data extraction template included study design and details, characteristics of the nutrition education initiative (name, duration, mode (eg, online, face-to-face), setting, organisers, people delivering the initiative, participants, aim, rationale, details of the initiative, activities and methods, topics and content covered), and outcome measures for the initiative. Extracted data were synthesised narratively and grouped by similar types to summarise the use of different initiatives, as well as those which included an evaluation of the outcomes of the initiative to describe a picture on if the initiatives have been working to improve nutrition competency among medical and health students.

Study characteristics

Initiative characteristics

Student-led nutrition education initiatives included culinary education (n=11), shadowing and placements (n=10), a lecture series (n=7), student interest groups (n=6) and elective or cocurricular courses (n=5). Studies included between one and four initiatives, with 12 studies (60%) involving multiple (more than one) initiatives. Initiatives were most frequently delivered by students (n=13, 65%), 11 were developed by students (55%), eight were student-governed (40%), while only six were student-initiated (30%) (table 2). One study described an initiative that was student-initiated, developed, delivered and governed.41 Initiatives that were student-governed were primarily student interest groups. A summary of each study is included in online supplemental materials.

Electives and cocurricular courses

Five studies41–45 (25%) included an elective or cocurricular course, with the number of students ranging from 20 to 35 students. Disciplines included medicine, nursing and other unspecified health degree students. The courses were delivered by a range of health professionals, including physicians, dietitians and psychologists and dietetic students. Four studies used a blended (in-person and online) approach to delivery. For example, Olson et al42 described a 10 week elective course which consisted of weekly virtual lectures, a journal club and shadowing opportunities, delivered to medical students in their first, second and fourth years of study.

Lecture series and presentations

Seven studies40 42 46–50 (35%) described nutrition-related lecture series, delivered to between 20 and 2000 students, all of which were medical students. The lecture series involved 5 to 11 lectures, delivered by physicians, dietitians, psychologists, pharmacists and in some cases, the medical students themselves, such as in Baute et al,48 where students created five 1 hour lectures in collaboration with a faculty mentor to deliver to other students at a graduate medical school. Three studies hosted the lectures online, allowing for greater attendance and participation. For example, Dumm et al40 advertised an online lecture series to 39 universities and received attendances of up to 2000 students per event.

Shadowing and placements

Ten studies43–46 51–56 (50%) included student-led placement opportunities. These initiatives were mostly targeted at medical and dietetic students, though there was one43 interdisciplinary (medical, nursing and other unspecified health professional students) placement experience. Nine studies described the delivery of group education in nutrition to the community as a placement experience.

Culinary education

Eleven studies39 44 46 50–55 57–59 (55%) described student-led culinary education initiatives. These initiatives were undertaken by between 1039 and 12551 students. In most cases, the initiative was student-delivered, whereby students delivered the culinary education (ie, to community or to their peers). For example, in the study of Al-Saedi et al,39 we can see that undergraduate nutrition students provided culinary education to other unspecified undergraduate students in collaboration with a chef. In some cases, the culinary education initiative was developed by students in collaboration with health professionals such as dietitians, physicians, chefs and lactation consultants. In one study, the students governed (co-ordinated) the culinary education programme.52

Student interest groups

Six studies41 44 46 47 50 57 (30%) described the formation of a student-initiated and/or governed group or council with a nutrition focus. Most (n=5) of the student interest groups were comprised of medical students, and student interest groups included between 3550 and 19057 students in total. Student groups provided a platform for other initiatives including nutrition-related lectures, culinary education, community outreach opportunities and the development and/or implementation of elective courses. In four studies, the student interest group was initiated by students in partnership with faculty, allowing for greater influence in promoting activities.

Evaluation

Thirteen studies39 40 42 45 48–54 57 58 (65%) evaluated the student-led nutrition education initiative. Most studies which evaluated the initiative (n=12, 92%) explored self-reported outcomes including acceptability of the initiative and self-reported changes in nutrition-related confidence or competence in skills such as counselling. One study (8%) evaluated improvements in nutrition knowledge using a questionnaire prelecture and postlecture series.40 Evaluation was conducted via surveys and questionnaires (n=10, 50%), informal qualitative feedback (n=2, 10%) or focus groups (n=1, 5%). Seven studies39 40 49 51–54 (54% of studies which evaluated the initiative) reported that students’ self-assessed nutrition competence improved. For example, Dumm et al40 reported an increase in self-assessed competence to counsel in nutrition following a lecture series. Similarly, medical students (n=31) in Rothman et al52 reported increased confidence in their ability to counsel patients to change their dietary habits following participation in a culinary medicine initiative. In the five studies40 42 48–50 (25%) which evaluated a nutrition-related lecture series, students reported that the lectures were engaging and increased their nutrition knowledge. For example, in the study of Pojednic and Frates,50 medical students who were surveyed reported that the lectures were helpful as they covered information not provided in the formal curriculum.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this scoping review is the first to synthesise the literature pertaining to student-led nutrition education initiatives for medical and health students internationally. This study identified 20 articles from the USA, the UK, Germany and Israel, which describe student-led nutrition education initiatives, including student interest groups, lecture series, elective courses, placements and culinary education. In this review, student-led nutrition education initiatives appeared to improve students’ self-reported nutrition competence. Of note, two studies also reported additional benefits in the initiatives being student-led.60 61

To address the double burden of malnutrition, we need to ensure the integration of nutrition actions in the health system and a health workforce that can deliver them. However, it has been proven difficult to increase nutrition education in a crowded curriculum, and the curricula remain inadequate,20 21 24 25 with typically less than 20 hours of nutrition training.20 62 63 The findings from this scoping review reiterate the desire for more nutrition training among medical and health students20 and the potential for student-led initiatives to fill this gap. Nevertheless, we need to recognise that this approach has its limitations. As with most students, assessment drives learning, and therefore student-led initiatives in nutrition may not be prioritised or selected by all students. This aside, student-led teaching initiatives may support a much-needed paradigm shift by encouraging the development of nutrition champions in their respective fields who can voice the need for training in nutrition as an essential component of medical and health education.

Resourcing, including funding, was a clear enabler of student-led nutrition education initiatives for medical and health professionals, particularly in the development of student interest groups. For example, Stiegmann et al46 and Lintvedt et al44 describe how funding received from a lifestyle medicine award led to the delivery of a greater number of high-quality activities. On the contrary, in Kaye et al,58 a culinary education programme had to be adapted to a smaller group of students due to a lack of resources. Partnerships or positive relationships, such as those with clinics, community centres, schools and other health professionals, were another key enabler of the delivery of student-led nutrition education initiatives in this review. For example, faculty support enabled Olson et al42 to deliver a lecture series with assistance from physicians, a dietitian and other multidisciplinary facilitators.

In this review, studies that evaluated the initiative reported that students preferred practical or skills-based initiatives, such as culinary education, shadowing other health professionals and student-led clinics. For example, in four studies that included a skills-based initiative,39 51 52 54 students self-reported improved confidence in nutrition counselling. This finding aligns with previous research, which identifies a preference for and benefits of practical nutrition-related training among medical and health students.64 65 Service learning and simulation-based training in nutrition and culinary medicine have previously been identified as leading to improvements in nutrition knowledge and confidence in discussing nutrition with patients.64–67 Future initiatives in nutrition education for medical and health students should ideally be skills-based to support application to practice.

This novel study may provide an emerging pathway for future student-led initiatives. However, this study has limitations that must be noted. Studies not published in English were excluded from this review, which may have led to the exclusion of further articles that meet the inclusion criteria. This review lacks the perspectives from low- and middle-income countries, and further studies or student-led nutrition projects are needed to identify if the same trends can be found in medical and health students beyond high-income countries. Furthermore, articles may have used different terms to describe student-led initiatives, which may have led to further articles being excluded. Future work could examine preferences for student-led nutrition education initiatives and implement and evaluate such initiatives to identify what is most effective.

Conclusion

This scoping review provides a summary of the available literature on student-led nutrition education initiatives for medical and health students internationally. Student-led initiatives, particularly those which are skills-based, offer diverse learning models and promote collaboration among health professionals. Self-reported changes in nutrition competence highlight the potential for these initiatives to improve the current status quo of nutrition education. Adequate funding and strong partnerships were identified as enablers of the success of student-led nutrition education initiatives. Expanding initiatives to include other health disciplines and leveraging innovative approaches, such as virtual or hybrid models, could broaden reach and impact.
