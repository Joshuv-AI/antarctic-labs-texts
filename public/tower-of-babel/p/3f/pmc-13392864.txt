Tuberostemonine ameliorates Alzheimer’s disease pathology by suppression of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway
Authors: Yingzhou Li, Xiaoxia Xu, Ziyao Meng, Linjie Chen, Qin Yu, Ruya Wang, Yiyu Ren, Fan Chen, Guang Liang, Xia Zhao, Houming Yu, Jurui Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a multifactorial neurodegenerative disorder with limited therapeutic options. Here, we report that tuberostemonine (Tub), an alkaloid from Stemona tuberosa, exerts neuroprotective effects in AD models. In Aβ1-42-treated PC12 cells, Tub reduced cytotoxicity, apoptosis, and oxidative stress while restoring mitochondrial function. In APP/PS1 transgenic mice, Tub administration improved cognitive performance, reduced amyloid-β plaque deposition, attenuated microglial activation, and attenuated neuronal loss, with efficacy superior to donepezil. Mechanistically, Tub selectively inhibited p38 MAPK phosphorylation without affecting ERK or JNK pathways, as confirmed by pharmacological inhibition and activation experiments. These findings identify Tub as a promising multi-target natural compound for AD intervention through p38 MAPK pathway modulation.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder among the elderly, characterized clinically by progressive memory loss, global cognitive impairment, language dysfunction, and alterations in mood and personality.1 The core neuropathological hallmarks of AD include the abnormal deposition of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques, the hyperphosphorylation of Tau protein leading to neurofibrillary tangles, and the selective loss of cholinergic neurons.2,3 The hippocampus, a brain region critical for learning and memory, is particularly vulnerable to metabolic and oxidative stress. The disruption of synaptic plasticity and structural integrity within the hippocampus is considered an anatomical basis for the progressive cognitive decline observed in AD.4 At the molecular level, the accumulation of extracellular Aβ plaques not only disrupts the neuronal microenvironment but also induces significant oxidative stress, resulting in the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and triggering lipid peroxidation.5 Concurrently, intracellular hyperphosphorylated Tau protein impairs neuronal cytoskeleton stability and intracellular transport through the formation of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), thereby collectively driving AD pathogenesis.6

Oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases (NDs), representing an imbalance between the production of ROS and the cellular antioxidant defense capacity.7 The brain is particularly susceptible to oxidative damage due to its high metabolic rate and oxygen consumption.8 Excess ROS can damage crucial biomolecules, leading to cellular dysfunction and ultimately neuronal death.9,10 Mitochondria, as the primary source of intracellular ROS, generate a surplus of free radicals under oxidative stress conditions, disrupting redox homeostasis.11 This, in turn, induces a cascade of oxidative damage, promotes Aβ production, facilitates abnormal Tau protein modifications, and alters the generation or function of other key biomolecules, thereby initiating or exacerbating AD pathology. Consequently, the modulation of oxidative stress is considered a promising therapeutic strategy for AD. The limitations of current AD pharmacotherapy, which offers only symptomatic relief with limited efficacy, have prompted researchers to explore natural products with multi-target intervention potential. A substantial body of preclinical evidence indicates that natural products hold great promise for AD drug development.12 Given that AD is a complex disease involving multiple interconnected mechanisms (including Aβ deposition, Tau pathology, and neuroinflammation) single-target drugs often yield suboptimal outcomes. Plant-derived natural products, with their structural diversity, multi-target mechanisms of action, and favorable safety profiles, have emerged as a significant avenue for novel AD drug discovery.13

Stemona is a traditional Chinese medicinal herb with abundant resources and extensive clinical applications. Extracts derived from Stemona have been demonstrated to possess various pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antiviral, and lung-moistening cough-suppressing effects.14,15,16,17 As an important species within the Stemona genus, Stemona tuberosa Lour. holds a significant position in traditional Chinese medicine theory. In the classical cough-suppressing formula “Zhisou San,” S. tuberosa serves as a key component of the core herb pair “Baibu-Ziwan” (Stemona-Aster), often used in combination with Aster.18,19 Modern pharmacological studies have confirmed its efficacy in regulating the cough reflex and alleviating airway inflammatory responses. The tuberous roots of S. tuberosa are rich in various bioactive constituents, among which tuberostemonine (Tub) is one of its principal active alkaloids.20 Existing research indicates that Tub can significantly ameliorate cigarette smoke-induced acute lung inflammation in mice, improve airway histopathological damage, reduce inflammatory cell infiltration, and inhibit the production of pulmonary chemokines.21 Furthermore, Tub has been shown to enhance the chemotherapeutic efficacy in leukemia by downregulating NF-κB expression, reverse multidrug resistance in K562/ADR chronic myeloid leukemia cells,22 and function as a glutamate receptor inhibitor at the neuromuscular junction.23,24

Based on this, the present study aimed to investigate the protective effects of Tub against neuronal damage and AD pathology using both in vitro and in vivo models. We compared its efficacy with donepezil, a clinically approved AD drug, and explored its molecular mechanisms through target sequencing technology. Our results demonstrate that Tub possesses significant potential for AD intervention, with efficacy superior to that of donepezil in our experimental systems. Therefore, Tub represents a promising candidate drug worthy of further investigation for the treatment of AD.

Results

Tub attenuates Aβ1-42-induced apoptosis in PC12 cells

Prior to investigating the neuroprotective effects of Tub (Figure 1A), we first evaluated its safe concentration range in PC12 cells. MTT assays revealed that treatment with Tub did not significantly affect cell viability, indicating that the concentrations used in subsequent experiments were non-cytotoxic (Figure S1). The accumulation of Aβ is considered an early event in AD pathogenesis. Aβ1–42, a well-established model for neuronal injury in AD, has been widely implicated in triggering various cytotoxic responses, including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction.25 In this study, we used 20 μM Aβ1–42 as the working concentration for subsequent experiments.26 We then examined the protective effect of Tub against Aβ1–42-induced neuronal damage. PC12 cells were pretreated with different concentrations of Tub for 2 h, followed by co-incubation with Aβ1–42 for 24 h. MTT results demonstrated that Tub significantly alleviated Aβ1–42-induced cytotoxicity in PC12 cells (Figure 1B). We further measured lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release in the culture supernatant. The results showed that Aβ1–42 exposure markedly increased LDH release, whereas Tub pretreatment reversed this effect (Figure 1C). To explore whether Tub attenuates Aβ1–42-induced oxidative stress, we performed JC-1 staining and ROS fluorescence assays. The results indicated that Tub significantly reduced intracellular ROS levels and restored mitochondrial membrane potential (Figures 1D–1F). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis revealed that Tub pretreatment significantly decreased the proportion of apoptotic cells compared to the Aβ1–42-only group (Figures 1G and 1H). Consistently, western blot analysis showed that Tub treatment significantly downregulated the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved-caspase 3, while upregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 (Figures 1I and 1J). Tub ameliorates learning and memory deficits in APP/PS1 mice

To evaluate the cognitive-enhancing effects of Tub, APP/PS1 transgenic mice were administered Tub at doses of 1 mg/kg or 5 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection once daily for one month. Donepezil (5 mg/kg), a clinically approved AD drug, was used as a positive control,27 while WT and APP/PS1 model groups received an equal volume of vehicle. Behavioral tests were conducted following the one-month treatment period (Figure 2A). The novel object recognition (NOR) test was first performed to assess nonspatial memory. Representative movement trajectories of mice from each group are shown in Figure 2B. Compared with WT mice, APP/PS1 model mice exhibited significantly reduced exploration of the novel object, both in terms of frequency and duration. In contrast, Tub-treated mice showed a marked increase in novel object exploration, indicating a significant improvement in recognition memory (Figures 2C–2E).We further evaluated spatial learning and memory using the Morris water maze (MWM). Swimming paths of each group are illustrated in Figure 3A. During the acquisition phase, APP/PS1 model mice displayed significantly longer escape latencies compared to WT mice, whereas Tub treatment substantially shortened the latencies over the training days (Figures 3B and 3C). In the probe trial, after removal of the platform, Tub-treated mice spent more time in the target quadrant (Figure 3D) and crossed the former platform location more frequently than APP/PS1 model mice (Figure 3E). Tub treatment alleviates neuropathology in APP/PS1 mice

Aβ plaque deposition, neuroinflammation, and tau hyperphosphorylation are the main neuropathological features of AD.28,29,30 Immunofluorescence staining revealed that both Tub- and donepezil-treated APP/PS1 mice exhibited significantly reduced Aβ deposition and decreased microglial aggregation in the hippocampus and cortex compared with vehicle-treated APP/PS1 mice (Figures 4A–4C). Western blot analysis of brain homogenates further showed that Tub or donepezil treatment significantly reduced APP/β-amyloid expression and tau phosphorylation (P-Tau), while increasing the protein level of postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95)(Figures 4D–4G).To quantitatively validate these observations, we measured the concentrations of different Aβ species in mouse hippocampal homogenates using specific ELISA kits. The results demonstrated that Tub or donepezil treatment significantly reduced the levels of β-secretase-cleaved fragments, particularly Aβ40 and Aβ42 (Figures 4H and 4I). Neuronal loss is a direct cause of cognitive impairment in AD. Inhibiting neuronal apoptosis has been shown to be an effective strategy to prevent neuronal loss and thereby improve cognitive function. As shown by IF imaging, Tub or donepezil treatment reduced neuronal loss in the hippocampus and cortex of APP/PS1 mice (Figures 4J and 4K). We subsequently evaluated the expression levels of apoptosis-related proteins by western blot. The results revealed that Tub significantly increased the Bcl-2/Bax ratio and decreased cleaved caspase-3 levels (Figures 4L–N), indicating protection against neuronal loss. We next examined oxidative stress markers in brain tissues. APP/PS1 model mice showed significantly reduced SOD activity and elevated MDA content, indicating impaired antioxidant capacity and enhanced lipid peroxidation. Neuroprotective effect of tub involves the p38 MAPK signaling pathway

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the neuroprotective effect of Tub in PC12 cells, we first identified potential molecular targets by cross-referencing Tub-binding targets with known AD-related targets, yielding 205 overlapping candidates (Figure 5A). Subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of the MAPK signaling pathway among these shared targets, suggesting its potential involvement in Tub-mediated neuroprotection (Figure 5B). The MAPK pathway serves as a critical mediator transducing extracellular signals into intracellular responses and plays a pivotal role in regulating cellular stress, proliferation, and apoptosis.31 In mammals, three major MAPK subfamilies—c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK)—are closely associated with the regulation of cell survival and apoptosis. To determine which MAPK subtype is primarily affected by Tub, we measured the phosphorylation levels of these kinases by western blot. The results showed that Aβ1–42 stimulation markedly increased the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK, whereas Tub pretreatment significantly suppressed this activation. In contrast, Tub had minimal effects on the phosphorylation levels of ERK and JNK (Figures 5C and 5D). Tub ameliorates neuronal apoptosis by suppressing the p38 MAPK signaling pathway

We performed immunofluorescence staining to analyze the spatial distribution of p38 MAPK protein in brain tissues. The results showed that treatment with either Tub or donepezil significantly reduced p38 MAPK expression in both the hippocampal and cortical regions of APP/PS1 mice (Figures 6A and 6B). To further validate whether Tub acts through the p38 MAPK pathway in vivo, we measured the phosphorylation levels of p38 MAPK in these brain regions. Western blot analysis revealed a marked upregulation of phosphorylated p38 (P-p38) in APP/PS1 model mice, which was effectively suppressed by Tub treatment (Figures 6C and 6D). These in vivo findings are consistent with the results obtained in vitro, reinforcing the conclusion that Tub exerts its neuroprotective effects via modulation of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. To further verify this mechanistic hypothesis, we pretreated PC12 cells with SB203580, a specific p38 MAPK inhibitor, to block the pathway and examined the expression of apoptosis-related proteins by western blot (Figure 7A). In the presence of SB203580, the Aβ-induced increase in the P-p38/p38 ratio was attenuated (Figure 7B). Notably, no statistically significant differences were observed in the Bcl-2/Bax ratio or lysated caspase-3 levels between the Tub+Aβ group, SB203580+Aβ group, and the combined Tub+SB203580+Aβ treatment group (Figure 7C), indicating that Tub’s regulatory effect on these apoptosis markers depends on the functional p38 MAPK signaling pathway. We further assessed intracellular ROS levels and mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) using JC-1 and DCFH-DA staining, respectively (Figure 7D). Aβ treatment significantly increased ROS accumulation and reduced ΔΨm. In contrast, Tub or SB203580 intervention effectively suppressed ROS generation and restored ΔΨm (Figures 7E and 7F).To further support this mechanism, we next used gemcitabine, a specific p38 MAPK activator, to pretreat PC12 cells and examined the expression of apoptosis-related proteins. The results showed that gemcitabine pretreatment reversed the protective effects of Tub on Aβ-induced apoptosis, as evidenced by increased P-p38/p38 ratio, decreased Bcl-2/Bax ratio, and elevated cleaved caspase-3 levels compared to the Tub+Aβ group (Figures 7G–7I). These gain-of-function experiments provide strong causal evidence that Tub ameliorates Aβ-induced neuronal apoptosis specifically through suppression of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. Microglia and astrocytes are essential for maintaining CNS homeostasis and play critical roles in response to injury and in AD pathophysiology. We therefore induced an inflammatory response in microglial and astrocytic cell lines by treatment with Aβ and collected samples for RT-qPCR to detect the expression of inflammatory cytokines. The results showed that Aβ led to a significant increase in the expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 in both microglia and astrocytes. Notably, these inflammatory markers were significantly reduced in the Tub+Aβ group, the Tub+SB203580+Aβ group compared to the Aβ-alone group (Figures S3 and S4). Discussion

In this study, we provide the first evidence demonstrating the therapeutic potential of Tub in AD. Our findings indicate that Tub effectively mitigates Aβ1–42-induced damage in PC12 cells, restores mitochondrial membrane potential, and reduces intracellular ROS levels, highlighting its potent antioxidant and neuroprotective properties. KEGG enrichment analysis suggested the involvement of the MAPK signaling pathway in Tub-mediated neuroprotection. Subsequent experimental validation confirmed that Tub specifically suppresses the activation of p38 MAPK without significantly affecting the ERK or JNK pathways. In APP/PS1 transgenic mice, Tub treatment markedly improved cognitive deficits, reduced Aβ plaque deposition, decreased neuronal apoptosis, and suppressed microglial activation. In conclusion, our results strongly suggest that Tub is a promising candidate drug for the treatment of AD, acting through p38 MAPK suppression to alleviate key pathological and behavioral impairments.

AD is a complex neurodegenerative disorder involving multiple interconnected pathological processes. Beyond the classic hallmarks of Aβ deposition and Tau hyperphosphorylation, AD pathogenesis also entails neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and synaptic dysfunction, which interact to drive disease progression. Current AD therapeutics face two major challenges: first, single-target drugs often fail to address the multifaceted pathological network of AD32; second, existing treatments offer limited efficacy and are frequently associated with adverse effects.33 In this context, natural products have emerged as promising candidates for AD drug development due to their multi-target capacity, favorable safety profiles, and neuroprotective potential.34 For instance, diosgenin has shown potential to improve cognitive function in patients with mild AD in clinical trials.35 Corilagin reduces toxic Aβ generation by inhibiting glutaminyl cyclase, thereby ameliorating AD-like pathology in APP/PS1 mice.36 The flavonoid strobopinin acts as a potent Nrf2 activator with dual anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities,37 while bergapten alleviates Aβ pathology via modulation of the MAPK pathway.38 These natural products, with their diverse mechanisms of action, collectively open new avenues for developing multi-target therapeutic strategies for AD.

Tub exhibits strong antioxidant capacity. Elevated Aβ levels are closely associated with increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS, exacerbating neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress in AD.39 In this study, we found that Aβ1–42 induced mitochondrial membrane depolarization and elevated intracellular ROS levels, both of which were significantly suppressed by Tub treatment. Similarly, intraperitoneal administration of Tub to APP/PS1 mice enhanced SOD activity and reduced MDA levels in the brain. These results suggest that the antioxidant properties of Tub may underlie its neuroprotective effects both in vitro and in vivo.

In recent years, the MAPK pathway has emerged as a plausible therapeutic target in various NDs, including AD, Parkinson’s disease, and stroke.40,41,42 MAPKs function as critical signaling nodes and have been shown to regulate pathology across multiple disease models.43 In neurons, JNK, ERK, and p38 MAPK represent the principal MAPK subfamilies.44 These serine/threonine kinases form an essential intracellular signaling network that responds to diverse extracellular stimuli—such as growth factors, cytokines, and hormones—as well as intracellular stress signals like oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress, thereby regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis.45 Aberrant Aβ accumulation activates the MAPK pathway, which in turn mediates cellular stress responses, inflammation, and apoptosis in neurons.46 Among these pathways, p38 MAPK serves as a critical link between Aβ-induced toxicity, Tau hyperphosphorylation, and neurofibrillary tangle formation,47 collectively contributing to neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuronal death.

As a key member of the MAPK family, p38 MAPK plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of NDs. This pathway can be activated by Aβ accumulation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signals, and is involved in regulating core AD pathological processes such as Tau hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, and apoptosis.48,49,50,51 Studies have shown that p38 MAPK activity is significantly elevated in the hippocampus and cortex during early AD stages and is present in both neurons and glial cells,52,53,54 suggesting that inhibition of p38 MAPK signaling may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for AD. In this study, KEGG enrichment analysis and subsequent experimental validation revealed that Tub selectively inhibits p38 MAPK phosphorylation without affecting ERK or JNK activity, indicating that its antioxidant and neuroprotective effects may be mediated through specific modulation of the p38 MAPK pathway. Previous reports have shown that inhibition of p38 MAPK signaling alleviates oxidative stress and toxicity in AD models.55,56 Consistent with this mechanism, we found that Tub administration significantly ameliorated AD-related pathology in APP/PS1 mice, accompanied by reduced levels of p-p38 in the brain, suggesting that Tub may attenuate neuronal apoptosis and oxidative damage by suppressing p38 MAPK phosphorylation. Further validation using the p38 MAPK-specific inhibitor SB203580 and the p38 MAPK-specific activator gemcitabine supported this conclusion. Tub exerts antioxidant-mediated neuroprotection through inhibition of the p38 MAPK pathway, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for AD. Furthermore, the current behavioral characterization is limited to spatial and recognition memory, future studies should include open field and elevated plus maze tests to rule out potential confounding effects on locomotor activity or anxiety.

In summary, this study demonstrates that Tub ameliorates AD pathology by inhibiting the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. Mechanistically, we found that the beneficial effects of Tub in both PC12 cells and APP/PS1 mice are associated with suppression of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. Furthermore, we illustrated the therapeutic role of Tub mediated through inhibition of p38 MAPK signaling. Collectively, these findings reveal the considerable potential of Tub as a novel candidate drug for the treatment of AD.

Limitations of the study

As a natural product, Tub may have multiple targets. Its direct binding target remains unknown, and whether it inhibits p38 MAPK phosphorylation directly or indirectly is unclear. Thus, other signaling pathways may also contribute to its neuroprotective effects. Nevertheless, the correlation between Tub-induced p38 MAPK suppression and neuroprotection supports the involvement of this pathway in AD amelioration. Future studies using DARTS, CETSA, or SPR will identify direct targets and clarify the mechanism of action. Additionally, Tub’s ability to cross the BBB requires further investigation. Despite these limitations, this study establishes Tub as a promising candidate for AD therapy and provides a foundation for future mechanistic research.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

The 8-month-old APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (APP/PS1) transgenic mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory in the United States, while C57BL/6J wild-type mice were sourced from the Experimental Animal Center of Hangzhou Medical College; all animals were housed at the Hangzhou Medical Experimental Animal Center. Only 8-month-old female mice were used in this study. During experiments, mice were kept in groups of five per cage under controlled conditions with a temperature range of 24°C–26°C, relative humidity of 60–70%, and a 12-h light-dark cycle, with adequate food and water provided. Animals were randomly assigned to either an experimental group or a control group, and group assignments were blinded during data collection and analysis. All experiments and procedures were performed in accordance with the Hangzhou Medical College Animal Care Guidelines and approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Pharmacy, Hangzhou Medical College (202502030, China).

Cell culture

PC12 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL). The cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The culture medium was refreshed every two days. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, the cells were passaged. Briefly, they were detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 3 min, resuspended in fresh complete medium (DMEM with 10% FBS), and subsequently seeded into new culture dishes for continued propagation or experimental use57

Method details

MTT assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. Briefly, the MTT reagent was reconstituted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a final concentration of 5 mg/mL and allowed to dissolve completely at room temperature protected from light. After the PC12 cells underwent the designated treatments, the original culture medium was carefully removed from each well. Then, 20 μL of the prepared MTT solution was added to each well containing 100 μL of fresh medium, and the plate was incubated at 37°C for 3 h in the dark. Following incubation, the supernatant was aspirated, and 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well to solubilize the formed formazan crystals. The plate was placed on an orbital shaker and incubated at room temperature for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution. The absorbance of each well was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using a microplate reader. The cell viability of the control group was set as 100%, and the relative viability of the treated cells was calculated accordingly.

Lactate dehydrogenase release assay

Cellular cytotoxicity was evaluated by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release into the culture medium using a commercial LDH assay kit. Following the designated treatments, the culture supernatant from PC12 cells was carefully collected from each well and transferred to separate centrifuge tubes. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove any cellular debris. Subsequently, 120 μL of the resulting supernatant from each sample was transferred into a new 96-well plate. The LDH working solution was prepared strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions, thoroughly mixed, and then 60 μL was added to each well containing the supernatant. The plate was wrapped in aluminum foil to protect it from light and incubated on a horizontal shaker at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at a primary wavelength of 490 nm, with a reference wavelength of 600 nm (or above) used for background correction. The percentage of cytotoxicity or cell death was calculated using the following formula: Cytotoxicity (%) = [(Absorbance of treated sample − Absorbance of sample background control)/(Absorbance of maximum enzyme activity control − Absorbance of sample background control)] × 100.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) measurement

The mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using the JC-1 fluorescent probe. After the designated treatments, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed three times with ice-cold PBS. The JC-1 staining solution was prepared by diluting the stock solution in serum-free medium at a ratio of 1:500 to achieve a final working concentration of 10 μg/mL. The cells were then incubated with the JC-1 solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to thoroughly remove any unincorporated dye. JC-1 exhibits fluorescence emission shift dependent on ΔΨm: it forms aggregates emitting red fluorescence (Ex/Em = 585/590 nm) in healthy mitochondria with high membrane potential, while it remains in monomeric form emitting green fluorescence (Ex/Em = 514/529 nm) in depolarized mitochondria. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, and this ratio was used as an indicator of mitochondrial membrane potential.58

Reactive oxygen species measurement

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were detected using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). After the designated treatments, PC12 cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS. A DCFH-DA working solution was prepared at a final concentration of 10 μM by diluting the stock solution in serum-free medium at a ratio of 1:1000. The cells were then incubated with this solution at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to thoroughly remove any extracellular probe. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 488 nm and 525 nm, respectively. The intensity of the green fluorescence, which is proportional to the intracellular ROS levels, was quantified and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was quantitatively assessed by flow cytometry using an Annexin V-FITC/PI double-staining kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, after treatment with the designated concentrations of Tub, approximately 5 × 104 PC12 cells were collected and centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 5 min. The supernatant was carefully removed, and the cell pellet was gently resuspended in 195 μL of Annexin V binding buffer. Subsequently, 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC conjugate was added, followed by gentle mixing and the addition of 10 μL of propidium iodide (PI) staining solution. The cell suspension was incubated at room temperature for 15 min in the dark to allow for specific binding. The stained cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 h to determine the percentage of cells in early apoptosis (Annexin V-FITC positive, PI negative) and late apoptosis/necrosis (Annexin V-FITC and PI double-positive). All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure statistical reliability.

Animals and drug treatment

A total of fifty 8-month-old female APP/PS1 (APPswe/PSEN1dE9) double-transgenic mice (weighing 30–32 g) and age-matched wild-type (WT) controls were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. All mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions at the Animal Facility of Hangzhou Medical College, maintained at 25 °C with a 12 h light/dark cycle, and provided with free access to food and water. The experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hangzhou Medical College (Approval No. 202502030).

The mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 10 per group): (1) WT group, (2) APP/PS1 model group, (3) APP/PS1 + Tub (1 mg/kg), (4) APP/PS1 + Tub (5 mg/kg), and (5) APP/PS1 + Donepezil (5 mg/kg). Tub (1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg) and donepezil (5 mg/kg) were dissolved in 1× PBS containing 2% DMSO as the vehicle. All drug treatments were administered daily via intraperitoneal injection according to individual body weight. Mice in the WT and APP/PS1 control groups received an equal volume of the vehicle solution (1× PBS with 2% DMSO). After one month of continuous treatment, behavioral tests were conducted to evaluate cognitive and memory functions.

Morris water maze test

The spatial learning and memory capabilities of the mice were evaluated using the MWM test. The experiment consisted of two consecutive phases: the acquisition training phase and the spatial probe trial. During the acquisition phase (days 1–4), mice underwent four training trials per day. In each trial, the mouse was gently released into the water facing the pool wall from one of four different quadrants, with the location of a hidden platform remaining constant. Each mouse was allowed a maximum of 60 s to locate the submerged platform. If a mouse failed to find the platform within the allotted time, it was gently guided to the platform and allowed to remain there for 15 s to reinforce spatial memory. Inter-trial intervals were 30 min, and after each session, the mice were carefully dried and returned to their home cages. All swimming trajectories were recorded by an automated video tracking system, and the escape latency (time taken to find the platform) was analyzed using VisuTrack software as a measure of spatial learning. On day 5, the spatial probe trial was conducted to assess memory retention. The platform was removed from the pool, and each mouse was released from a novel starting quadrant and allowed to swim freely for 60 s. The number of crossings over the original platform location and the time spent in the target quadrant were recorded and analyzed, serving as indicators of spatial reference memory.

Novel object recognition test

The NOR test was employed to evaluate recognition memory in mice. The testing apparatus consisted of a white, square open-field arena (100 × 100 × 100 cm), with a video camera mounted above to record all activities. The experiment was conducted under uniform lighting and in a quiet environment to minimize external disturbances. The procedure included two phases: habituation/training and testing. During the training phase, two identical objects (denoted as A1 and A2) were placed symmetrically in the arena. Each mouse was allowed to freely explore the objects for 5 min, and the exploration time and frequency for each object were recorded. After a 24-h retention interval, the testing phase was conducted, in which one of the familiar objects (A) was replaced with a novel object (B). The mouse was again allowed to explore for 5 min, and the exploration time directed toward the novel and familiar objects was recorded. Between trials, the arena and objects were thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol to eliminate any residual olfactory cues. Recognition memory was evaluated by comparing the time spent exploring the novel object versus the familiar one, with a significant preference for the novel object indicating intact recognition memory.

SOD and MDA assays

SOD Activity: SOD activity in brain tissues was measured using a commercial WST-1-based assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissue homogenates were prepared by homogenizing brain samples in ice-cold physiological saline (0.9% NaCl), followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant. A 20 μL aliquot of the appropriately diluted supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate. Then, 20 μL of enzyme working solution and 200 μL of WST-1 substrate solution were added to each well. After gentle mixing, the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. SOD activity was expressed as units per milligram of protein (U/mg prot).

MDA Content: The extent of lipid peroxidation was evaluated by determining the MDA content using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method as described in the assay kit. Freshly prepared MDA working solution was added to each tissue homogenate sample, followed by thorough vortex mixing. The mixture was incubated in a 95 °C water bath for 40 min. After cooling to room temperature, the samples were centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min. Subsequently, 200 μL of the supernatant from each sample was transferred to a new 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm. MDA concentration was calculated based on a standard curve and expressed as nanomoles per milligram of protein (nmol/mg prot).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, and frozen at −80°C. Coronal sections of 15 μm thickness were prepared using a cryostat and air-dried at room temperature for 2 h. The sections were washed three times with PBS, 5 min each, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature. After another three washes with PBS, the sections were blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding. The blocking solution was removed, and the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies diluted in PBS: anti-P38 (1:200), anti-NeuN (1:200), anti-Iba1 (1:200), and anti-APP (1:200). The following day, the primary antibodies were retrieved, and the sections were washed three times with PBS. They were then incubated with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. After final washes with PBS, the sections were counterstained with DAPI-containing anti-fade mounting medium for 5 min to visualize nuclei. Coverslips were applied, and images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Western Blot analysis

Cultured PC12 cells or brain tissue samples from different treatment groups were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The protein concentration of each sample was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher, #23225). Equal amounts of protein from each sample were separated by 12.5% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto a 0.22 μm PVDF membrane at 300 mA for 90 min. The membrane was then blocked with 3% BSA in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. After blocking, the membrane was incubated with specific primary antibodies (diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer) overnight at 4°C. The following day, the membrane was washed three times with TBST and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and imaged with a Bio-Rad Gel Doc XR system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Aβ40 and Aβ42 enzyme-Linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Brain tissues were washed with pre-chilled PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) to remove residual blood, weighed, minced, and homogenized on ice in PBS. The homogenate was centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 5 min to obtain the supernatant. Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit, and equal amounts of protein were used per well. Samples or standards (100 μL per well) were added, covered, and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. After washing twice, biotin-labeled antibody working solution (100 μL/well) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Following three washes (1 min each), HRP-streptavidin conjugate working solution (100 μL/well) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After five washes (1–2 min each), TMB substrate solution (90 μL/well) was added and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 10 min. The reaction was stopped with 50 μL/well of stop solution, and the absorbance was read at 450 nm.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells and brain tissues using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher, 15596026) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For RT-PCR analysis, cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, RR037A). Subsequently, real-time PCR amplification was performed on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Singapore) using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, RR820A). Relative expression levels were calculated by the 2ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH. Gene primers were purchased from Thermo Fisher

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experimental data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). To determine statistically significant differences among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by the Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For experimental designs involving repeated measurements within the same subjects, two-way repeated measures ANOVA was applied, with Tukey’s test used for post hoc analysis where appropriate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0).
