PROTOCOL: Evidence and Gap Map of Studies of the Effects of Health Interventions to Reduce Out-of-Pocket Expenses
Authors: Xiuxia Li, Junliang Tao, Yongsheng Wang, Yanfei Li, Rui Li, Nan Chen, Jieyun Li, Huijuan Li, Meixuan Li, Lili Zhang, Howard White, Kehu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This is a protocol for a Campbell Evidence and Gap Map (EGM) of the studies of the effect of health interventions to reduce out-of-pocket expenses (OOPE). The objective is to identify existing gaps in the available evidence where further primary studies and systematic reviews are needed.

Background

The Problem, Condition, or Issue

Out-of-pocket expenses (OOPE) refer to costs that individuals pay from their own cash reserves (Kagan, 2019). It includes both formal and informal expenses that are directly related to the cost of seeking care (World Health Organization & World Bank, 2021).

This definition incorporates informal payments (unofficial payments) under OOPE. This includes providing tips to health staff, bribing for access to higher quality healthcare services, or being requested to make additional payments by health staff or establishments (Zandian et al., 2019). Informal payments may be in cash or in-kind.

In terms of health insurance, OOPE refers to policyholder’s share of covered healthcare costs, including the money for deductibles, copays, and coinsurance (Kagan, 2019). As in the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS), OOPE also includes cash outlays for health-related services, supplies, and other items that are not covered by health insurance (Cohen, 2005; Paez et al., 2009).

Generally, when the proportion of OOPE drops to 15–20% of total expenditure on health (TEH), the impact of economic shocks is manageable (World Health Organization, 2013). However, most countries have not yet reached this level. In 2015, the median OOPE as a share of TEH was 31% in upper-middle-income countries, 38% in low-income countries, and 43% in lower-middle income countries (World Health Organization, 2018).

There is an inverse relationship between the share of OOPE in TEH and poverty: poorer people pay a greater share of their income on OOPE (Wagstaff et al., 2020). OOPE can impose a significant financial burden on individuals with limited resources, preventing them from accessing necessary care, and so exacerbating inequity in health outcomes (Falkingham, 2004; World Health Organization, 2000). Since 2015, the proportion of the population who spend more than 10% of their household budget on OOPE has worsened, reaching 13.5% in 2019 (approximately 1 billion people). In 2019, 4.9% of the global population (381 million people) fell into or further fell into extreme poverty due to OOPE (World Health Organization, 2023). For example, over one-third (35%) of U.S. adults experienced financial difficulties in paying medical bills, including 28% who were currently paying off medical debts, and almost 14% of the respondents reported delayed or missed necessary medical treatments (Choi, 2018). Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic may exacerbate the economic difficulties faced by individuals who rely on OOPE for healthcare payments.

An increasing number of countries are adopting interventions to reduce OOPE in healthcare services. These interventions vary from country to country. Evidence suggests that well-designed interventions can be successful in reducing OOPE and their negative impacts (World Health Organization, 2019). Examples of successful interventions include:• In China, OOPE accounted for nearly 60% of TEH in 2003. However, through a series of governance measures such as the implementation of a social health insurance scheme and the provision of free public health services, the proportion of OOPE was reduced to 28.8% in 2016 (Zhang et al., 2019).• In Turkey, the Green Card scheme is a non-contributory insurance scheme for the people living in poverty. A survey conducted in 2002 revealed that 93.3% of beneficiaries of the Green Card scheme had to forego treatment due to financial constraints (Tatar et al., 2007). In response, the Turkish government expanded the Green Card scheme from 2003 to 2006, and reduced health spending by beneficiaries by one-third during 2005–2006 (Tirgil et al., 2019).• Vietnam is among the countries which have adopted a Universal Health Care Coverage scheme in order to reduce inpatient and outpatient OOPE. Consequently, we plan to conduct a global Evidence and Gap Map (EGM) of studies of the effectiveness of all reported health interventions intended to reduce OOPE. This map will provide a scientific basis for developing evidence-informed health strategies and social policies to address OOPE.

The Intervention

We include interventions primarily aimed at reducing OOPE. These interventions include abolishing fees, providing fee waivers or exemptions, and interventions to limit informal charges. They also involve prescribing practices, particularly the use of generic drugs. Health insurance is also included. However, we do not include preventive medicine or health systems reforms to increase efficiency which may reduce OOPE, but that is not their primary purpose. Additionally, we do not consider interventions like unconditional cash transfers (UCTs) that may help people to pay health expenditures; such interventions may reduce the financial burdens, but they do not directly reduce OOPE. Why it is Important to Develop the Evidence and Gap Map (EGM)

Universal Health Coverage (UHC) is a global call for ensuring that everyone can access comprehensive and quality health services when and where needed, without suffering financial hardship. Unfortunately, the 2023 UHC Global Monitoring Report highlights that billions of people worldwide are left behind on the path to UHC (World Health Organization & World Bank, 2023). High levels of OOPE remain a huge obstacle to achieving progress toward UHC, hence reducing OOPE through health interventions is in line with this global call. There is clear evidence indicating that OOPE impose significant financial burdens on individuals with limited resources.

Additionally, unofficial payments often pose a major barrier to accessing necessary healthcare, leading to substantial out-of-pocket costs and financial difficulties for families, particularly in the context of lower-middle income countries (Paredes-Solís et al., 2011; Zandian et al., 2019). In settings with limited resources, health service providers are frequently underpaid, making unofficial payment a primary income source for some health workers. This creates unofficial payment incentives to ensure the delivery of health services (Falkingham, 2004).

The dependence on out-of-pocket payments varies globally, but a strong correlation exists between the level of out-of-pocket payment and poverty (World Health Organization, 2000). The role of enhanced health interventions to reduce OOPE in reducing poverty, decreasing household health spending, and promoting health for all is gaining prominence on government and policy agendas. Currently, there is a lack of evidence synthesis providing practitioners and researchers with available evidence on interventions to reduce OOPE and the effectiveness of such evidence.

An evidence and gap map (EGM) is an intuitive, visual, and interactive tool designed to provide an overview of the existing evidence on a specific topic, theme, or domain (Saran & White, 2018). The EGM can serve to prevent unnecessary research duplication and assists identifying the most relevant evidence for guiding the development of systematic reviews and evidence-based decision-making products (EBDMPs) in the field (White, 2021). Our EGM will identify relevant research, presented by interventions and outcomes, to show the current status of the evidence based on interventions to reduce OOPE. This map will enhance the discoverability of evidence for users, aiding policy practitioners and researchers in accessing high-confidence evidence, identifying research hotspots and research gaps, and offering references and direction for future research.

Existing EGMs and/or Relevant Systematic Reviews

To our knowledge, there is currently no EGM on the topic of health interventions to reduce OOPE. There are, however, systematic reviews of interventions to reduce OOPE in general and of health insurance in particular.

Jalali et al. (2021) conducted a scoping review categorizing measures to reduce direct OOPE based on the health system functions framework. The measures were classified into four categories: health system stewardship, creating resources, health financing mechanisms, and delivering health services (Jalali et al., 2021). As a scoping review, the review did not have the comprehensive search we are proposing (specifically, they only searched four databases and no grey literature sources). Zandian et al. (2019) considered strategies to reduce informal payments only, emphasizing measures such as increasing public awareness and changing cultural norms as key policies to combat informal payments (Zandian et al., 2019). A review by Sugunan et al. (2023) is restricted to lower-middle income countries, and does not present a typology of interventions (Sugunan et al., 2023).

Numerous reviews on health insurance have been published, analyzing the effects of OOPE (Acharya et al., 2012; Erlangga et al., 2019; Prinja et al., 2017). Some insurance plans provide significant protection against high OOPE. One systematic review assesses the effects of the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) on OOPE and catastrophic health expenditures (CHEs), in Ghana, which shows that the uninsured paid 1.4 to 10 times more in OOPE and were more likely to incur CHEs than the insured (Okoroh et al., 2018). A systematic review protocol has been developed to explore the effect of performance-based financing interventions on OOPE for maternal health services in Sub-Saharan Africa (Nkangu et al., 2022).

These systematic reviews focus on a specific aspect of reducing OOPE, and their scope is limited. For example, they may include a small number of studies, only focus on a specific intervention, or concentrate only on lower-middle income countries.

This EGM will provide an overview of the evidence on the effects of health interventions to reduce OOPE. The specific methods will be detailed in the methods section.

Methods

Evidence and Gap Map: Definition and Purpose

An EGM is a systematic review tool that visually summarizes the of availability of evidence and knowledge gaps within a particular policy domain (Howard White et al., 2020; Saran & White, 2018; Sparling et al., 2021). EGM summarize what evidence there is, not what the evidence says (Saran & White, 2018). The interventions and outcomes are graphically presented in row lists and column lists respectively in the map, indicating the density and scarcity of the available evidence. Additionally, confidence ratings for included studies may be provided. The primary dimensions of the map are its rows and columns, which are intervention categories and outcome categories, respectively. Secondary dimensions such as country, population, and research design will be included as filters.

EGMs are used as a global tool that attempts to give access to research evidence to practitioners, policymakers, the public, and research commissioners. EGM can serve as a guiding framework for future research, aimed at addressing both the “absolute gaps” – an absence of primary studies within specific domains, and the “synthesis gaps” – the absence of strong confidence systematic reviews in those specific domains (Snilstveit et al., 2016). Our EGM will collect evidence on the effectiveness of all reported health interventions intended to reduce out-of-pocket costs.

Framework Development and Scope

This EGM will provide an overview of the evidence on the effects of interventions to reduce OOPE on health. The initial interventions and outcomes framework was developed and adapted through review of relevant reports, literature, and consultations with stakeholders. In August 2023, after discussing and receiving suggestions from stakeholders, the main classification framework related to various interventions and outcomes was determined. The framework will follow a standard intervention and outcomes matrix in which the rows are interventions and the columns are outcomes. A preliminary framework is given in Table 1. We will further refine the framework during piloting.

This EGM has a geographically broad scope. It will encompass studies from all regions. The geographic categorization will be based on world geographical regions and World Bank country classifications by income level (World Bank, 2024).

Its rigorous, transparent, and reproducible methods aim to minimize bias, thereby enhancing the reliability of the conclusions (Clarke, 2011).(2) Randomised controlled trials (RCTs)

(2) Randomised controlled trials (RCTs)

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are employed to assess the effects of an intervention on the study subjects. Participants are randomly assigned to either the experimental or control group (which may involve multiple groups) to ensure baseline comparability in factors other than the intervention.(3) Quasi-experimental designs

(3) Quasi-experimental designs

(3) Quasi-experimental designs

Quasi-experimental design refers to a type of research design in which groups are not randomly assigned but are instead pre-existing or formed based on non-random criteria. Quasi-experimental designs will be included due to the difficulties in conducting RCTs in a wide range of people, especially in implementing health interventions to reduce OOPE. The quasi-experimental designs should contain the pre-test and post-test. The study will include both active and passive controlled studies. We will exclude designs without a comparison group. The quasi-experimental designs eligible for this EGM include:• Difference-in-difference (DID) analysisDID analysis, also referred to as the “double differences” approach, is a design that estimates the impact by comparing the changes in outcomes over time between the treatment and the control group (White & Sabarwal, 2014).• Propensity score matching (PSM)PSM involves estimating the probability for each individual to be assigned to either receive or not receive an intervention, known as the propensity score. Subsequently, individuals who receive the intervention are matched with those who do not, based on similar propensity scores. PSM ensures that the average characteristics of the intervention and control groups are comparable, which is considered sufficient for obtaining unbiased estimates (White & Sabarwal, 2014).• Regression discontinuity designs (RDD)RDD utilizes discontinuity of a variable near a threshold or cutoff point to categorize individuals into two groups: treated and untreated. This design is grounded in a fundamental assumption that, in the vicinity of the cutoff point, differences among individuals are primarily due to the discontinuity of the cutoff variable rather than other confounding factors (White & Sabarwal, 2014).• Instrumental variable (IV)When a controlled experiment is not feasible, or in cases where it is not possible to successfully deliver treatment to every unit in a randomized experiment, IV methods are used to estimate causal relationships (White & Sabarwal, 2014).• Interrupted time series (ITS) analysisITS is used to evaluate the impact of an intervention, such as policy changes or treatment interventions, on time series data. This method focuses on observing the trend changes in the time series before and after the intervention occurs.• Natural experimentsIt is commonly understood that natural experiment refers to a type of experiment that occurs in specific contexts where researchers are unable to control the implementation of intervention measures (Leatherdale, 2019).• Other matching designsWe will not include qualitative research or cross-sectional studies in the EGM. Types of Intervention/Problem

We will include interventions that are explicitly designed (or intended) to reduce OOPE. We will exclude preventive medicine interventions not explicitly designed to reduce OOPE, health system reforms that lack direct impact on OOPE reduction, and UCTs that do not directly lower OOPE. The categories of included interventions are as follows: (1) Reducing service charges, (2) Reducing expenditure on drugs, (3) Financial support, (4) Reducing demand, (5) Preventive measures aimed at reducing OOPE. Other Eligibility Criteria

We will include studies reported in English or Chinese. No language restrictions will be applied at the search stage. However, screening will be conducted in English and Chinese only, reflecting the team’s language expertise and ensuring accurate study selection. To ensure comprehensive coverage of the evidence base, we will impose no restriction on publication date. Both published and unpublished studies will be eligible for inclusion. There will be no restrictions on the scale of the intervention. Therefore, both pilot and full-scale interventions will be included in the EGM. Likewise, interventions at any stage of implementation, including ongoing and completed interventions, will be eligible for inclusion.

Dependency

The unit of analysis will be individual studies. If a single study has multiple reports, the most recent report will be utilized. If previous publications include different outcomes, they will be used only to report missing outcomes. Systematic reviews may encompass primary studies included in the map, and it is possible that more than one systematic review may involve the same primary study. Primary studies meeting our eligibility criteria will be included in the map, irrespective of their inclusion in a systematic review.

Data Collection and Analysis

Screening and Study Selection

Two researchers will independently conduct screening, divided into two parts: initial screening based on title and abstract, followed by a comprehensive review of the full text according to inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure 2). Any conflicts will be resolved by a third member of the research team. Refer to Appendix 1 for detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria. Before the formal screening, we conducted a pilot study to ensure the feasibility of the criteria. The results of the pilot study are documented in Supplemental document 1.Figure 2.Screening Tool

Screening Tool

For studies with multiple intervention groups, include interventions that meet the eligibility criteria. If a study contains multiple eligible interventions, they will be presented separately in the map. If our screening finds that any ongoing research meets our selection criteria, but the research results have not been published, we will also include these studies and label them as ongoing. As the review is updated, we will track the progress of these studies. For grey literature with existing results, we will label them as grey literature and perform data extraction.

Data Extraction and Management

Two researchers will independently code eligible studies, and any conflicts will be resolved by a third member of the research team. We will extract and encode data for all included studies, including country, population, interventions, main outcomes, study design, and other relevant details. Refer to Appendix 2 for the specified coding categories. The pilot results of the coding are documented in Supplemental document 1.

Tools for Assessing Risk of Bias/Study Quality of Included Reviews

We will assess the confidence for systematic reviews and primary studies using appropriate tools. Systematic reviews will be assessed using the AMSTAR-2 (Shea et al., 2017). AMSTAR-2 comprised 16 evaluation items, among which seven were critical items. “Y” represented conformity, “N” represented non-conformity, “PY” indicated partial conformity, and “NP” indicated not applicable due to the absence of meta-analysis. Assessment was conducted using an online tool (https://amstar.ca/Amstar_Checklist.php), and the overall study confidence will be automatically generated after the assessment is completed.

We will assess the primary studies using the critical appraisal tool developed by Howard and Saran (Appendix 3) (Apunyo et al., 2022; H. White et al., 2020). The tool consists of seven items, with four of them being crucial for decision-making. Each item will be assessed as having high confidence, medium confidence, or low confidence. The overall confidence in the study will be determined by the lowest rating among these four key items.

The risks of bias of all included studies will be independently assessed by two reviewers, and conflicts will be resolved by a third member of the study team.

Methods for Mapping

We will use the EPPI-reviewer to screen the literature, extract data, and use EPPI-mapper to create the EGM (Eppi-Centre, 2019).
