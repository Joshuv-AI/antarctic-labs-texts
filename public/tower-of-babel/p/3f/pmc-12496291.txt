GmAP2 enhances plant tolerance to aluminum toxicity and phosphorus deficiency in Arabidopsis
Authors: Cheng Li, Pengxin Du, Lidan Wang, Xin Xu, Hongying Zhong, Yanbo Cheng, Tengxiang Lian, Lu Li, Qibin Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKey messageOverexpression of the GmAP2 gene regulates several aspects of gene expression in Arabidopsis, including acid–aluminum stress, low-phosphorus stress, ERF family transcription factors, ABA signaling, and genes related to lateral root development. The overexpression of GmAP2 can help plants mitigate the effects of aluminum toxicity and increase their tolerance to low-phosphorus stress.AbstractEthylene response factors (ERFs) represent one of the largest transcription factor families in plants and are involved in regulating biotic and abiotic stresses, as well as growth and development in higher plants. In this study, the soybean GmAP2 gene, encoding the AP2/ERF transcription factor, is a downstream gene of GsMYB7, which enhances soybean tolerance to aluminum toxicity under acidic conditions, referred to as acid-aluminum stress. The expression pattern of GmAP2, which is constitutive and rich in roots, was upregulated under acid-aluminum and low-phosphorus stress. The resistance phenotypes of the wild-type and GmAP2-overexpressing lines were investigated by treatment with acid-aluminum plates and low-phosphorus plates, respectively. The results revealed significant differences between the GmAP2 transgenic lines and the wild type, and compared with the wild-type plants, the genetically modified plants presented significantly better physiological indicators, such as fresh weight, root length, and number of lateral roots. Under acid-aluminum stress, changes in weight, MDA content, and proline content in Arabidopsis thaliana were evaluated. The genes associated with these pathways, including genes related to the acid-aluminum response (AtALMT, AtALS3, AtSTOP1, and AtMATE), ERF family (AtERF6, AtERF15, AtERF5, and AtERF109), and/or ABA response (AtABI5, AtRD29A, AtRAP2.6, AtABI1, and AtABI2), are significantly regulated. In the context of low-phosphorus stress, a comprehensive analysis of the regulatory landscape revealed significant alterations in genes involved in these pathways, including low phosphorus-responsive genes (AtPHT1; 1, AtPHO1, AtPHR1, and AtMGD2), ERF family genes (AtERF1, AtERF6, AtERF13, and AtERF109), and/or genes associated with lateral root development (AtKCS16). These regulatory changes occurred in response to low-phosphorus stress. These results provide a basis for breeding soybean varieties that can increase their tolerance to the stresses of aluminum toxicity and low phosphorus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00299-025-03622-7.Keywords: GmAP2, Acid-aluminum, Low phosphorus, Soybean, Arabidopsis

Introduction

The AP2/ERF family of transcription factors (TFs) is a class of plant-specific TFs that consists of four functional regions, including the DNA-binding domain, the transcriptional regulatory domain, the oligomerization site, and the cytosolic localization signals. Among the four regions, the AP2/ERF-binding domain is highly conserved (Abiri et al. 2017). The AP2/ERF TFs can bind the cis-elements of promoter regions specifically to regulate biotic and abiotic stress responses in plants (Ma et al. 2024a, b). The AP2/ERF structural domains contain a three-stranded β-sheet recognizing the target sequence at the N-terminus and an α-helix at the C-terminus (Allen et al. 1998). Based on the number of AP2/ERF structural domains, the AP2/ERF TFs can be categorized into five subfamilies: AP2, ERF, DREB, Soloist, and RAV. Current studies have focused on the AP2, DREB, and ERF subfamilies, whereas there have been few reports on the Soloist subfamily, whose nucleotide sequences are highly conserved in most plants (Li et al. 2016).

AP2/ERF TFs regulate various abiotic stress response processes in higher plants. In terms of drought stress, GmDREB1 can form heterodimers with ERF-like TFs, regulating the expression of downstream stress-related genes and thereby enhancing drought tolerance. ZmERF21 influences drought tolerance in maize seedlings by directly controlling the expression of genes involved in hormone signaling and stress responses (Chen et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022c). The AP2/ERF transcription factor OsERF52 and the OsSRO1c-OsDREB2B complex increase cold tolerance in rice through protein phase separation, providing a new strategy for developing cold-resistant rice varieties. The ethylene signaling pathway negatively regulates CBF/DREB1-mediated cold responses in soybean, thereby affecting its cold tolerance (Robison et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2024). Under salt stress, GmERF13 in soybean mediates the inhibition of nodule formation through its interaction with GmLBD16a. GsSnRK1 interacts with the transcription factor GsERF7 in wild soybean, jointly regulating the plant's resistance to salt stress. The AP2/ERF transcription factor OsRAV1 significantly enhances seed vigor and salt tolerance in rice (Feng et al. 2020; Gao et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2025). Furthermore, under heavy metal stress, the double knockout of AtERF13 and AtERF6 enhances cadmium tolerance in Arabidopsis thaliana by finely regulating plant growth and the transcriptome (Chen et al. 2024).

Aluminum (Al) is the most abundant metal element in the earth’s crust, second only to oxygen and silicon. In its natural form, aluminum is stable and does not interact with the biological processes of biological organs (Nidhi et al. 2013). However, when the soil pH is less than 5.5, aluminum exists mainly in the form of Al3+, which is the most toxic form and is harmful to plants (Ranjan et al. 2021). Currently, acidic soils are distributed mainly in northern temperate and tropical regions worldwide (Von Uexküll and Mutert 1995). Aluminum toxicity frequently occurs in acidic soils and is among the most important factors limiting plant growth in acidic soils (Kochian et al. 2015). The physiological and molecular mechanisms through which soybeans respond to aluminum stress include key Al-tolerance pathways such as organic acid efflux, cell wall modification, antioxidant system activation, and microbial interactions (Li et al. 2025). Previous research has demonstrated that plant aluminum (Al) activates the Arabidopsis malate transporter ALMT1, thereby mediating the efflux of malate to chelate Al in acidic soils, revealing the structural basis of ALMT1-mediated aluminum resistance in Arabidopsis (Wang et al. 2022b). The GmABR1 and GsERF1 genes increase Arabidopsis tolerance to aluminum toxicity through an ethylene-mediated pathway (Li et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023a).

Numerous studies have shown that aluminum toxicity and phosphorus deficiency often coexist in acidic soils and are two key factors limiting crop growth. This is because iron and aluminum oxides in acidic soils readily undergo complex reactions with soil phosphorus and externally applied phosphorus, forming complexes that are difficult for plant roots to absorb and resulting in low available phosphorus content in the soil (Zheng 2010). Phosphorus is an important nutrient element required for plant growth and development. It is an important component of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), RNA (ribonucleic acid), plasma membranes, proteins, and phosphates, and phosphorus participates in metabolic processes such as photosynthesis and signaling in plants (Clarkson and Marschner 1995). Recent studies have investigated the synergistic mechanism of GmERF1 and GmWRKY6 in the response of soybean to low-phosphorus stress to regulate root development and the expression of genes related to phosphorus uptake. In Arabidopsis, AtERF070 regulates root development and the maintenance of phosphorus homeostasis in response to phosphorus starvation, thereby affecting the plant's phosphorus absorption and utilization efficiency. AP2 family TFs play key roles in the symbiotic process with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi by regulating lipid metabolism and transport, thus influencing the plant's phosphorus uptake (Zhang et al. 2023; Ramaiah et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2023b). Organic acid transport is a key mechanism through which plants efficiently acquire phosphorus. In Arabidopsis roots, AtALMT3 may be involved in malate secretion induced by low phosphorus. Under phosphorus-deficient conditions, the AtALMT3 gene plays an important role in helping roots secrete organic acids to activate insoluble phosphorus in the soil (Maruyama et al. 2019).

Soil acidification and phosphorus deficiency have long been serious constraints on soybean quality and yield, impeding the healthy development of agricultural systems. Extensive research on acid-aluminum and low-phosphorus stress tolerance has been reported in plants. As noted earlier, genes such as AtALMT1, GmABR1, and GsERF1 contribute to plant resistance to acid-aluminum stress. The genes encoding AtERF070, GmERF1, and AtALMT3 help plants cope with low-Pi stress. Among the AP2/ERF family genes, GmABR1 and GsERF1 are involved in acid-aluminum stress, whereas AtERF070 and GmERF1 are associated with low-phosphorus stress. However, there have been few reports on ERF family genes that simultaneously regulate plant aluminum toxicity and low-phosphorus stress. In this study, we compared the expression profiles of acid-aluminum-induced upregulated genes in transgenic lines overexpressing GsMYB7 with those in wild-type plants. A significantly upregulated EBP gene was identified (Online Supplemental Table 1), which was named GmAP2 (Wang et al. 2022a, b, c). The regulatory role of GmAP2 was subsequently investigated in acid-aluminum and low-phosphorus environments through experiments involving both Arabidopsis heterologous transformation and soybean hairy root-induced transformation.

Materials and methods

Gene cloning and sequence analysis

The GmAP2 gene was analyzed by bioinformatics through the NCBI (the National Center for Biotechnology Information), Phytozome, and SoyBase databases. DNAMAN and MAGE11 software programs were applied to the GmAP2 gene for amino acid sequence comparison and evolutionary tree construction, and the tree was embellished using the iTOL online website. The relevant gene information can be found in Supplementary File 2.

The GmAP2 gene sequence was obtained from the NCBI database. Based on the sequence information of the soybean genome, specific primers were designed (forward primer: 5´-ACTCTACTCATTCCACACCCA-3´; reverse primer: 5´-CTGTG CATCCCTGCCAAAAC-3´) to amplify the full length of GmAP2 using a cloning reaction system (refer to the Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase kit from Nanjing Novozymes Biotechnology). The PCR product was detected by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The purified PCR product was then inserted into the pLB vector to form the fusion plasmid, which was subsequently transferred into the DH5α competent cells, after which the positive clones were sent to Bioengineering for sequencing (Zero Background Rapid Cloning Kit, Tiangen, China).

Plant materials and stress treatments

The seeds of soybean (cultivars BX13 and Huachun 6), Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0), and Ben’s tobacco were obtained from the Guangdong Subcenter of the National Center for Soybean Improvement (Guangzhou, China). To cultivate the soybean seedlings of Huachun 6, culture pots filled with vermiculite were placed in trays supplemented with an appropriate amount of water. When the vermiculite was completely soaked, 25 seeds of Huachun 6 were planted in each pot and covered with a thin layer of vermiculite. The pots were then placed in an incubation room (26 °C, 16 h/8 h of light/dark) (Zhu et al. 2014). When the soybean seeds germinated and grew cotyledons, the soybean seedlings with main root lengths of approximately 6 cm (after the vermiculite was removed) were transferred to 0.5 mM CaCl2 solution (pH = 5.8) for 24 h of preculture.

To investigate the tissue expression pattern of GmAP2, leaves, root tips of 1–2 cm, stems of the seedlings, flowers, and young pods of Huachun 6 were taken as samples, which were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

Various concentrations (0, 25, 50, 75, and 100 µM) of AlCl3 solution (pH = 4.5) were used to incubate precultivated soybean seedlings. After being treated with the aluminum solution for 8 h, 1–2 cm long roots of the soybean plants were taken as samples, which were also frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. The precultured soybean seedlings were incubated in a solution of 50 µM AlCl3 (pH = 4.5) for 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, or 24 h. Root tips (1–2 cm) were taken as samples, which were then frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction (Han et al. 2022). All the experiments were performed with three biological replicates.

Soybean seeds of the variety BX13 were germinated on wet vermiculite using the same method. Soybean seedlings with fully expanded cotyledons and a main root length of approximately 6 cm were selected, after which the plants were washed with vermiculite. The seedlings were subsequently transferred to modified 1/2 strength Hoagland nutrient solution supplemented with 500 µM KH₂PO₄ (NP) for 1 day. Half of the seedlings were then transferred to a modified 1/2 strength Hoagland nutrient solution containing 5 µM KH₂PO₄ (LP) (with additional K ions supplemented using potassium chloride) and maintained for 14 days. A completely randomized block design was employed, with soybean plants cultivated in hydroponic chambers containing nutrient solution (pH = 5.8), which was replaced every 3 days. Roots were collected as samples at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 h, as well as at 3, 7, and 14 days after they were transferred to low-phosphorus conditions and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction. All the experiments were performed with three biological replicates (Zhang et al. 2017).

The same method was used to grow Huachun 6 soybean seedlings. After 24 h of prehydroponic cultivation, the soybean seedlings were transferred to a 100 μM ABA solution and treated for 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, or 24 h. Roots that were 1–2 cm long were taken as samples, treated with liquid nitrogen, and subsequently frozen at −80 °C for RNA extraction. All the experiments were performed with three biological replicates.

RNA extraction and real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR analysis

The RNA was extracted from the samples using a kit specifically designed for this purpose, in accordance with the instructions provided in the RC411 Nanjing Novozymes RNA Extraction Kit. The RNA concentration was measured using an RNA concentration meter. cDNA synthesis was performed using a reverse transcription kit from Baorui Medical Biotechnology Co., Ltd. cDNA was used as the template for qRT‒PCR (refer to the TaKaRa Fluorescent PCR Kit). Relative expression data were analyzed using the 2^−△△CT method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Actin3 from soybeans was selected as the internal reference gene for data normalization. All the primers used for qRT‒PCR are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Subcellular localization analysis

The location of the GmAP2 protein was predicted using the UniProt online website. Two restriction sites, NcoI and SpeI, were selected using CE DesignV1.03 software to linearize the pCAMBIA1302 vector by double restriction. Recombinant cloning primers were designed (Supplementary Table 1), and recombinant cloning was carried out using the correctly sequenced GmAP2-pLB recombinant vector plasmid as the cDNA substrate. The recombinant cloned product was purified, and the linearized recombinant pCAMBIA1302 vector was ligated to the recombinant cloned product. The ligation product of GmAP2-pLB was transformed into DH5α cells. After electrophoresis and sequencing confirmation, the ligated GmAP2-pCAMBIA1302-GFP plasmid was extracted from DH5α competent cells and transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 competent cells (refer to the GV3101 transformation protocol in the product manual provided by Shanghai Weidi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Agrobacterium GV3101(P19) containing the recombinant plasmid GmAP2-pCAMBIA1302-GFP and the control Agrobacterium GV3101 (P19) containing the plasmid pCAMBIA1302-GFP were cultured to maximum bacterial viability and then centrifuged to retain the bacterial suspension. Sediments of each Agrobacterium were resuspended in 15 mL of suspension solution, mixed with the suspension, and incubated at room temperature for 3–4 h. Nicotiana benthamiana plants were thoroughly watered prior to injection and exposed to white light for 1 h to open the stomata. Using a 1 mL sterile syringe, the bacterial suspension was slowly injected into the leaves until they were fully saturated. After the leaves were covered with plastic wrap for 3 days, the lower epidermis was peeled off, and the leaves were observed under a laser confocal microscope. Fluorescent signals were detected using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8) with laser excitation wavelengths set to 488 nm (GFP) and 552 nm (chlorophyll autofluorescence).

Transactivation assay of the GmAP2 protein

Yeast vector construction was performed using pGBKT7 and pGADT7 vectors. Recombinant connection primers were designed using CE DesignV1.03 software (Supplemental Table 1), and the cloned products were purified. The pGBKT7 vector was linearized by double digestion with EcoRI and BamHI restriction enzymes. The restriction enzyme products were subsequently ligated to the amplified fragments to construct the GmAP2-pGBKT7 recombinant plasmid. The ligation products were then transferred into E. coli, and the positive clones were sequenced (Sheng Gong Bioengineering Co., Ltd).

The GmAP2-pGBKT7 recombinant plasmid and the pGADT7 vector were cotransformed into Y2HGold yeast receptor cells to detect self-activation. The pGBKT7-lam plasmid and pGADT7-T vector were cotransformed into Y2HGold competent cells as a negative control for self-activation. The pGBKT7-53 plasmid and pGADT7-T vector were cotransformed into Y2HGold competent yeast cells as a positive control for self-activation. Y2HGold yeast competent cells were prepared according to the Y2HGold transformation method provided by Wei di Biotechnology Company. Positive control, negative control, and single colonies of yeast cells cotransformed with the GmAP2-pGBKT7 recombinant plasmid or the pGADT7 empty vector were resuspended in sterilized ddH₂O. SD/-Trp-Leu/X-α-Gal and SD/-Leu-Trp-His-Ade/X-α-Gal plates coated with chromogen were separately delineated and incubated at 28 °C for 36–48 h. The presence of blue coloration is indicative of the GmAP2 protein exhibiting self-activating activity.

Construction of overexpression vectors and gene editing vectors

To insert the coding sequence of GmAP2 into the pTF101 vector, XbaI and SacI were selected as restriction enzyme sites to construct the overexpression vector. The intermediate vector pUC19 contains the Arabidopsis AtU6 promoter, and the Cas9 protein is derived from Streptococcus pyogenes. The target primers for the sgRNAs were designed using the online software CRISPRPv2.0 (Lei et al. 2014). CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing technology was used to edit the open reading frame of the GmAP2 gene. Target site selection was performed on the CRISPR-P2.0 website with four high-scoring target sites. Four of the target sites with spanned introns had high on-score rates, and they were all located on exons. The targets were designated GmAP2-sgRAN-13, GmAP2-sgRAN-15, GmAP2-sgRAN-20, and GmAP2-sgRAN-21, and the primers were synthesized through target design. After oligonucleotide dimers were formed with forward and reverse primers, they were integrated into the intermediate vector pUC19. Following sequencing confirmation in DH5α competent cells, the four target sites were inserted into small vectors and connected to the pCas9 vector to construct the gene editing vector.

Arabidopsis transformation and soybean root hair transformation

Transformation was performed using the Col-0 ecotype of Arabidopsis thaliana. The constructed overexpression vector of GmAP2-pTF101 was subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, after which the target gene was transformed into Arabidopsis plants via the flower immersion method (Huang et al. 2022). Five-day-old seedlings with expanded cotyledons were used for soybean hairy root transformation. The vectors of GmAP2-pTF101 and GmAP2-Cas9 were then transformed into Agrobacterium strain K599, after which plant cell transformation was performed via hypocotyl injection. The plants transformed with the pTF101 vector were used as a control (Kereszt et al. 2007).

Phenotypic analysis of aluminum-treated and phosphorus-treated transgenic Arabidopsis lines

Three transgenic lines with the highest relative expression levels of GmAP2 and WT were selected for phenotypic identification. Concentration gradient phenotype plates (containing 0, 50, 100, and 200 µmol/L AlCl₃) were prepared using 1/2 strength MS solid medium with a pH of 4.5. Briefly, after the 1/2 MS medium solution was autoclaved, filtered AlCl₃ solution (100 µmol/L) was added (to different volumes) to form the phenotype identification plates. WT and transgenic Arabidopsis plants with consistent root lengths were selected from the germination plates and transplanted to the phenotype plates for vertical cultivation for 14 days. Phenotype identification of low phosphorus tolerance was performed using modified 1/2 Hogland solid medium with a pH of 5.8. After high-temperature and high-pressure sterilization, different concentrations of 1 mol/L KH₂PO₄ (the additional K ions were supplemented with potassium chloride) were added to set up concentration gradient phenotype plates at 500, 5, and 0 µmol/L. WT and transgenic Arabidopsis plants with consistent root lengths were selected from the germination plates and transferred to the phenotype plates for vertical culture for 11 days. The phenotypes of the Arabidopsis root systems were analyzed and observed under different AlCl₃ and KH₂PO₄ treatments (Li et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2023a).

Determination of aluminum ion contents in root tips

Approximately 1–2 cm of hairy root tips treated with AlCl₃ were taken as samples with a weight of 0.05 g and placed in a centrifuge tube containing 2 mol/L hydrochloric acid. Three replicates were designed for each treatment. We incubated them in a shaking chamber for approximately three days, after which we filtered the samples, diluted them to a final volume of 6 ml with 2 mol/L hydrochloric acid, and determined the aluminum ion content in the soybean roots using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP‒AES) (Osawa and Matsumoto 2001).

Determination of malondialdehyde and proline contents in transgenic Arabidopsis plants under aluminum stress

To measure physiological parameters, wild-type and transgenic Arabidopsis seedlings were grown in 1/2 MS medium (pH = 5.8). After 3–4 days, they were transferred to 100 µM AlCl₃ solid medium (pH = 4.5) and grown for one week. Four whole plants were then harvested for analysis of their malondialdehyde (MDA) and proline (Pro) contents. The MDA content was determined using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction method (Schmedes and Hølmer 1989), and the proline content was measured using the sulfosalicylic acid method (Ábrahám et al. 2010).

Determination of dry weight and phosphorus content

Fresh plant tissue samples were placed in a 105 °C oven for 1 h to obtain a green color, after which the samples were dried in a 65 °C oven for 3 days. After drying, the samples were ground and digested using concentrated H₂SO₄ and H₂O₂, and the phosphorus concentration was determined using the molybdate-blue colorimetric method (Murphy and Riley 1962). The phosphorus content in plant tissues is expressed as the amount of phosphorus (g/kg) contained in each kilogram of plant material. The total phosphorus content per plant was calculated by multiplying the total plant weight by the phosphorus content (mg/plant).

Hematoxylin staining

To prepare the hematoxylin staining solution, 0.6 g of hematoxylin and 0.06 g of potassium iodate were weighed, 300 mL of distilled water was added, and the solution was stirred overnight (24 h) with a magnetic stirrer.

To analyze the expression of the GmAP2 gene in root hairs, samples of root hairs were selected for subsequent experiments. Root hairs were transferred to a CaCl₂ solution containing 50 µM AlCl₃ (pH = 4.5) and treated for 24 h. The acid-aluminum-treated root hairs were placed in hematoxylin staining solution for approximately 30 min and then soaked in ultrapure water for 1 h, with the water changed every 15 min. The staining of the root hairs was observed and photographed under a microscope (Rincón and Gonzales 1992).

Low-phosphorus hairy root hydroponics

HC6 soybean seeds were sterilized with chlorine gas (Cl₂) for 5–6 h and then placed in vermiculite and allowed to germinate under normal light. After 5 days, K599 Agrobacterium cells containing the constructed GmAP2-pTF101 overexpression vector or the empty pTF101 vector were inoculated into the hypocotyl region near the cotyledon node. Seedlings were then cultivated following infestation with the GmAP2-pTF101 vector or the pTF101 vector in a high-humidity greenhouse for a period of two to three weeks. When the root hairs reached approximately 5–10 cm in length, the main roots were removed, and the chimeric plants containing root hairs were transferred to 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution containing 500 µM KH₂PO₄ (NP) or 5 µM KH₂PO₄ (LP) for further cultivation (Kereszt et al. 2007). Hydroponic cultivation was conducted for 14 days (additional K ions were supplemented with potassium chloride), and the water was replaced every 3 days. The genomic DNA of the seedlings was extracted to identify positive plants, and the RNA expression levels of the GmAP2 gene in the wild-type and overexpression plants were quantified via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR). After cultivation, the gathered data were analyzed to determine the presence of phenotypic disparities in hairy-rooted plants under various phosphorus concentration treatments (Li et al. 2014).

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 22.0, GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 software, and ImageJ software. The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. Comparisons between groups were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by t tests. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance (*: P < 0.05; **: P < 0.01).

Results

Isolation and bioinformatics analysis of GmAP2

The GmAP2 gene was obtained by screening the acid-aluminum-induced gene expression profiles using the overexpressing transgenic line of GsMYB7 and searching the NCBI database (Wang et al. 2022a). The gene name of GmAP2 is GLYMA_02G016100, with the gene accession number LOC100819416 and the protein accession number XP_001241423.1. The GmAP2 gene contains two exons and is located on chromosome 2, with a total sequence length of 1,586 bp and a CDS length of 903 bp, which produces a protein that is 300 amino acids in length. The GmAP2 protein contains an AP2 domain located in the 75–134 amino acid region and belongs to the ERF subfamily of the AP2/ERF transcription factor family (Supplemental Fig. 4). Specific primers were designed to amplify the cDNA of GmAP2 from Huachun 6, and PCR was performed to obtain a 1211 bp target fragment (data not shown). The purified PCR product was ligated into the pLB vector, after which the fusion vector was transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α. After PCR identification of clones with a band size of 1335 bp, the bacterial cultures were subjected to sequencing, and the sequence information was confirmed.

Measurement of transcriptional activation activity of GmAP2 protein. a Schematic diagram of self-activation colonies. b Verification of transcriptional activation activity in yeast cells, SD/-Trp-Leu/X-α-Gal medium and SD/-Leu-Trp-His-Ade/X-α-Gal medium

A total of 181 AP2/ERF family members with high homology to the GmAP2 gene were obtained from the NCBI database, including 112 genes from soybean and 69 genes from Arabidopsis (Fig. 1a). Homologous protein sequence alignments were performed for nine members from the same branch as GmAP2, including four members from soybean and five members from Arabidopsis (Fig. 1b). Amino acid sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree analysis revealed that the GmAP2 protein formed a sister branch (a minimal unit tree clade) with AtERF71 (At2g47520) in Arabidopsis thaliana on the evolutionary tree. GmAP2 and AtERF71 are closely related, orthologous genes. Previous studies have shown that AtERF71/HRE2 promotes root growth and development by positively regulating root cell expansion. It activated the expression of related genes by recognizing and binding specific cis-acting elements such as the GCC box and DRE/CRT, which led to increased root cell expansion and ultimately resulted in the elongation of primary and lateral roots. Therefore, it is speculated that GmAP2 may perform a similar function (Lee et al. 2015a, b).

Expression patterns of GmAP2

qRT-PCR was carried out to investigate the expression pattern of GmAP2 in different tissues. The results revealed that GmAP2 exhibited a constitutive expression pattern. Compared with that in the stems, the expression level of GmAP2 in the roots was 1.2 times greater, whereas its expression in flowers was 0.1 times lower (Fig. 2a).

Analysis of the expression patterns of GmAP2 in Huachun 6 and BX13 soybeans. a Tissue expression patterns of GmAP2. The samples of stems, roots, leaves, flowers, and pods were collected from Huachun 6 during the pod-setting stage. b Expression of the GmAP2 gene under the treatments of different aluminum concentrations of 0, 25, 50, 75, and 100 μM AlCl₃ (0.5 mM CaCl₂, pH 4.5) for 8 h. c Expression of the GmAP2 gene under the treatment of 50 μM AlCl₃ (0.5 mM CaCl₂, pH 4.5) for 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h. d Expression of the GmAP2 gene under the treatments of low phosphorus (5 µmol/L KH₂PO₄) and normal phosphorus (500 µmol/L KH₂PO₄) in modified Hoagland nutrient solution for 0 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, 3 d, 7 d, and 14 d. e Relative expression levels of the GmAP2 gene under the action of 100 μM ABA. All data in the figure were replicated three times. “*” indicates a significant difference compared to the control (*, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01)

To investigate the response of GmAP2 to acid-aluminum stress, various aluminum concentrations and time gradients were used to measure the expression levels of GmAP2 under various aluminum conditions. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression levels of GmAP2 tended to first increase but then decrease with increasing concentrations of AlCl₃. The expression level of GmAP2 was greatest in the group treated with 50 µM AlCl₃, which was 6 times greater than that in the control group (Fig. 2b). In response to treatment with 50 µM AlCl₃, the initial increase in GmAP2 expression was followed by a decrease in expression over time, with the highest expression level being 6 times greater than that of the control after 8 h of treatment (Fig. 2c). Therefore, the results suggested that GmAP2 expression is significantly upregulated under acid-aluminum conditions.

To investigate the response of GmAP2 to low-phosphorus stress, different concentrations and time points were used to measure the expression levels of GmAP2 after treatment with 500 µM and 5 µM KH2PO4, respectively. The qRT‒PCR results revealed that under treatment with 5 µM KH2PO4, the expression level of GmAP2 was greatest on Day 14, which was 8.1 times greater than that of the control (Fig. 2b). Under the treatment with 500 µM KH2PO4, the expression level of GmAP2 reached a maximum at 36 h, which was 3.35 times greater than that of the control (Fig. 2b).

Under ABA treatment, the expression of the GmAP2 gene increased and reached a maximum transcript level of 30.2 times that of the control at 24 h (Fig. 2e). These results indicated that GmAP2 responded strongly to ABA.

Subcellular localization analysis of GmAP2 protein

To verify the location of the GmAP2 protein, GmAP2-pCAMBIA1302-GFP and pCAMBIA1302-GFP fusion vectors were separately transformed into GV3101 (P19) for injection into tobacco epidermal cells using a syringe. Under laser confocal microscopy, GFP was detected throughout the cells, whereas the GmAP2-GFP fusion was detected in the nucleus, consistent with the staining pattern of the nuclear dye DAPI. These results indicate that the GmAP2 protein is localized in the cell nucleus (Fig. 3).

Assay of GmAP2 protein transcription activation activity

The recombinant plasmid pGBKT7-GmAP2 and the empty vector pGADT7 (AD) were cotransformed into Y2HGold yeast cells. Yeast cells cotransformed with pGBKT7-53 and pGADT7-T were used as positive controls, and those cotransformed with pGBKT7-lam and pGADT7-T served as negative controls. Phenotypic characterization of GmAP2 transgenic Arabidopsis under aluminum stress and low-phosphorus stress

Three GmAP2 transgenic lines of the T4 generation and wild-type Arabidopsis thaliana were selected for phenotypic characterization (Supplemental Fig. 1). After the seedlings were treated on acid-aluminum plates for 14 days, a phenotypic tolerance analysis was conducted to evaluate the acid-aluminum stress. The results revealed that under 0 µM AlCl₃ (pH = 4.5), there were no significant differences in primary root length, fresh weight, or aluminum ion content in the root tips between the wild-type and transgenic lines. In the presence of AlCl₃ (pH = 4.5) solutions of 50, 100, and 200 µM, compared with those in the control, the relative root elongation rates were 72%, 66%, and 25%, respectively, for the wild type and 86–93%, 78–82%, and 39–50%, respectively, for the transgenic lines (Fig. 5b). The fresh weights of the 6 wild-type plants were 28.9 mg, 27.2 mg, and 23.2 mg, respectively. The fresh weights of the transgenic lines ranged from 39.3–41.2 mg, 33–34.1 mg, and 27.6–29.3 mg, respectively (Fig. 5d). The Al3+ ion contents in the roots of the wild-type plants were 223.9 µg/g, 391.1 µg/g, and 501.2 µg/g, respectively. The Al3+ concentrations in the roots of the transgenic lines ranged from 126.1–182.8 µg/g, 174.4–206 µg/g, and 329.6–436.7 µg/g, respectively (Fig. 5c). The results of the measurement of proline (Pro) and malondialdehyde (MDA) contents indicated that under 0 µM AlCl₃ treatment, the MDA accumulation levels in the aboveground parts of the wild-type and the transgenic lines were 13.66 nmol/g and 11.2–13.26 nmol/g, respectively. Under the AlCl₃ treatment, the MDA contents in the aboveground parts of both the wild-type and the transgenic plants significantly increased from 53.42 nmol/g and 30.55 to 38.01 nmol/g (Fig. 5e), respectively. In the transgenic lines grown on medium without AlCl₃, the Pro accumulation in the aboveground parts of the wild-type plants was 39.37 nmol/g, whereas the accumulation levels in the transgenic lines ranged from 37.51–43.68 nmol/g. Under the AlCl₃ treatment, the Pro contents in the aboveground parts of both the wild-type and the transgenic plants significantly increased from 45.03 nmol/g and 45.57 to 53.25 nmol/g, respectively (Fig. 5f). These results indicated that after 14 days of treatment with 100 µM AlCl₃ (pH = 4.5), compared with wild-type Arabidopsis, GmAP2-transgenic Arabidopsis experienced less acid-aluminum toxicity. These results demonstrate that GmAP2 may increase the tolerance to acid-aluminum stress in Arabidopsis.

The results indicated that under KH₂PO₄ (pH = 5.8) concentrations of 500 and 5 µM, the phosphorus contents of the wild type were 8.47 g/kg and 3.44 g/kg, respectively, whereas those of the transgenic lines were 9.37–10.44 g/kg and 3.90–4.22 g/kg, respectively (Fig. 6e). Molecular mechanism of GmAP2 tolerance to Al stress

To investigate the mechanism of GmAP2 under conditions of acid-aluminum stress, the expression levels of marker genes, including AtALMT, AtALS3, AtSTOP1, and AtMATE, were determined under AlCl₃ treatment (pH = 4.5). qRT-PCR analysis revealed that under acid-aluminum stress, the transcript levels of AtALMT, AtALS3, AtMATE, and AtSTOP1 in the wild type and three transgenic lines were greater than those in the 0 μM AlCl₃ control. In response to treatment with 100 μM AlCl₃ (pH = 4.5), the expression levels of all the marker genes increased with increasing transcript levels in the three transgenic Arabidopsis lines (Fig. 7a–d). The expression levels of AtMATE and AtALS3 were the highest, reaching 6.0-fold greater than those in the control (Fig. 7b, d).

qRT-PCR analysis was carried out to investigate whether the ERF genes in Arabidopsis are regulated by the GmAP2 gene under acid-aluminum stress. The results revealed that the expression levels of AtERF6 (Meng et al. 2013), AtERF15 (Lee et al. 2015a), AtERF5 (Fujimoto et al. 2000), and AtERF109 (Bahieldin et al. 2018) significantly changed in response to aluminum stress. Under treatment with 100 µM AlCl₃, the transcript levels of AtERF6, AtERF15, AtERF5, and AtERF109 in GmAP2 transgenic lines were significantly greater than those in the wild type.

As reported in previous studies, AtERF15 is a member of the ERF family within the genus AtERF and functions as a positive regulator of the abscisic acid (ABA) response (Fig. 7f). These findings were further corroborated by the observation that the GmAP2 gene is induced by ABA and responds to abscisic acid (Fig. 2e). Consequently, a selection of genes associated with abscisic acid regulation, including AtABI5 (Fan et al. 2019), AtRD29A (Nakashima et al. 2009), AtRAP2.6 (Zhu et al. 2010), AtABI1 and AtABI2 (Merlot et al. 2001), were identified. The qRT‒PCR results indicated that under aluminum stress conditions, the expression levels of the AtABI5, AtRD29A, and AtRAP2.6 genes increased approximately 4.4-, 2.4-, and 18.2-fold, respectively, in the three transgenic Arabidopsis lines. Among these genes, the expression of the AtRD29A gene substantially increased exclusively in the L4 line. In the three transgenic lines, the expression levels of AtABI1 and AtABI2 were significantly reduced by approximately 0.5-fold (Fig. 7i–m). It is hypothesized that GmAP2 may increase acid-aluminum tolerance in plants by regulating the expression of genes associated with the acid-aluminum response, the ERF family in Arabidopsis and/or the abscisic acid pathway.

Molecular mechanism of GmAP2 tolerance to LP stress

To further investigate the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of plant tolerance to low phosphorus by the GmAP2 gene, four marker genes, AtPHT1; 1, AtPHO1, AtPHR1, and AtMGD2, were identified for the analysis of expression levels. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression levels of these four genes were upregulated in both wild-type and transgenic Arabidopsis plants under low-phosphorus treatment, with greater upregulation in the former. Among these genes, the expression of the key gene AtPHR1, which positively regulates the response to phosphate starvation, tended to increase in both the wild-type and the transgenic lines after phosphorus treatment. Under low-phosphorus conditions, AtPHR1 expression was upregulated 1.65-fold compared with that under normal conditions. Compared with those in the WT plants, the expression levels of AtPHR1 in the GmAP2 transgenic lines increased 2.84-, 2.74-, and 2.88-fold, respectively, under low-phosphorus conditions (Fig. 8c). Compared with those in the control, the expression levels of AtPHT1; 1, AtPHO1, and AtPHR1 in the transgenic lines were approximately 3.84-, 3.40-, and 2.88-fold greater, respectively (Fig. 8a–c).

Among the ERF gene family members in Arabidopsis, the expression levels of the genes AtERF1 (Cheng et al. 2013), AtERF6, AtERF13, and AtERF109 significantly differed under low phosphorus stress. Compared with those in the wild type, the expression levels of the AtERF1, AtERF6, and AtERF109 genes significantly increased in the transgenic lines (Fig. 8e–h). AtERF13 was upregulated both in the transgenic lines and in the wild type under low-phosphorus stress. However, compared with that in the wild type, AtERF13 was significantly downregulated in the GmAP2 transgenic line (Fig. 8g).

Further validation indicated that the expression levels of the Arabidopsis ERF family genes AtERF6, AtERF13, and AtERF109 were significantly upregulated in transgenic Arabidopsis and associated with the promotion of lateral root development. Previous studies revealed that the AtKCS16 gene is downstream of the AtERF13 gene (Lv et al. 2021). The qRT‒PCR results revealed that under low-phosphorus stress, the expression levels of the AtKCS16 gene were significantly greater in the GmAP2 transgenic lines than in the WT, with increases in expression of 4.08-, 3.26-, and 4.92-fold, respectively (Fig. 8i). Therefore, it is speculated that the GmAP2 gene may increase the low-phosphorus tolerance of Arabidopsis by regulating the expression of low-phosphorus-responsive genes, the relevant responsive genes of the Arabidopsis ERF family, and the downstream genes related to lateral root development directly regulated by the ERF family.

Discussion

Members of the AP2/ERF family play key roles in plant growth and development, stress response, and hormone regulation (Vogel et al. 2012; Ma et al. 2024a, b). Comparative analysis of the conserved domains of AP2/ERF superfamily genes in soybean and Arabidopsis revealed structural differences between two major types of AP2/ERF domains—RAV-type and ERF-type—particularly in the C-terminal regions of their conserved domains. However, their primary (amino acid) structures are highly conserved. The AP2 subfamily comprises genes that regulate plant growth and development (Elliott et al. 1996; Chuck et al. 1998; Boutilier et al. 2002); the ERF subfamily of genes mediates important functions in the stress response (Yamaguchi-Shinozaki and Shinozaki 1994; Sakuma et al. 2002); and RAV subfamily genes are influenced primarily by signals such as ethylene and brassinosteroids (Alonso et al. 2003; Hu et al. 2004). Studies have demonstrated that ERF subfamily members play a role in soybean stress resistance; however, few studies have explored how these genes mediate tolerance to acid-aluminum (Al) and low-phosphorus (LP) stress. Here, we report that the GmAP2 gene is induced by acidic Al and LP stresses and that it increases the resistance of genetically modified plants to Al and LP stresses.

The GmAP2 gene belongs to the ERF subfamily of the AP2/ERF superfamily and contains a conserved AP2 domain. The GmAP2 protein is a TF that is localized in the nucleus and exhibits transcriptional self-activation activity (Figs. 3, 4). The GmAP2 gene is orthologous to genes that respond to biotic and abiotic stresses, such as Glyma.03G263700, Glyma.07G044300, Glyma.09G041500, AT1G53910, AT1G72360, AT2G47520, AT3G14230, and AT3G16770 (Fig. 1b). Among these genes, GmERF3 (Glyma.03G263700) enhances salt, drought, and pathogen resistance in transgenic tobacco (Zhang et al. 2009). As a transcriptional activator in the cell nucleus, GmERF7 (Glyma.07G044300) participates in multiple signal transduction pathways by binding to the GCC-box to activate the expression of stress-related genes, and its overexpression can increase the salt tolerance of tobacco plants (Zhai et al. 2013). Under stress conditions involving ET, ABA, Phytophthora, salt, and drought, the promoter activity of the GmERF5 (Glyma.09G041500) gene in tobacco leaves significantly increased, indicating that the GmERF5 gene may play a crucial role in ABA-mediated stress resistance (Dong et al. 2015). Oxygen sensing mediated by AT1G53910 regulates central metabolic processes such as respiration, the TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism, as well as the levels of immune-regulatory metabolites (Paul et al. 2016). The overexpression of AT3G14230 enhances the resistance of transgenic plants to gray mold disease, whereas T-DNA insertion mutants exhibit reduced resistance. Further studies revealed that the overexpression of AT3G14230 in ein2, ein3, and ein3-like1 mutants restored the disease resistance conferred by the gene. AT3G14230 plays important regulatory roles in gray mold resistance and the ethylene response (Zhao et al. 2012).

A promoter analysis of the GmAP2 gene revealed 14 cis-acting elements in the region 2000 bp upstream of the ATG start codon (Supplemental Table 2). Among all the elements, ABRE, CGTCA-motif, TGACG-motif, TGA-element, and P-box are plant hormone signal response cis-elements; AT1-motif, Box 4, G-Box, GT1-motif, and anaerobic response element (ARE) are light response elements; CAT-box is a meristem expression response element; and the protein-binding site of HD-Zip 3 is a defense and stress response element consisting of TC-rich repeats. All these cis-acting elements respond to abiotic stress (Hao et al. 2024). These results suggest that GmAP2 may increase plant tolerance to abiotic stress by regulating abscisic acid synthesis, promoting lateral root development, and/or through other potential mechanisms.

Hematoxylin staining has been widely applied in aluminum stress studies because hematoxylin reacts with Al3⁺, enabling the observation of aluminum distribution in roots. Darker staining indicates greater accumulation of Al3⁺ in root tips and more severe aluminum toxicity in the root system (Rincón and Gonzales 1992). In this study, the GmAP2 gene was transformed into soybean root hairs. The results of hematoxylin staining and root tip aluminum ion measurements were consistent with previous findings. The deepest staining and highest aluminum ion content in the root tips were observed in GmAP2-edited plants. Conversely, the lightest staining and lowest aluminum ion content were detected in the root tips of the GmAP2-overexpressing plants (Supplemental Fig. 2, 5).

It is well known that the method of hairy root infection has been widely used in studies verifying soybean gene-mediated low-phosphorus stress (Li et al. 2014). In this study, after 14 days of phosphorus starvation treatment, the plants in the control group presented phosphorus deficiency symptoms, such as relatively small, narrow leaves with a deep green color. Transgenic composite plants with hairy roots overexpressing GmAP2 remained relatively normal. Additionally, under phosphorus-deficient conditions, overexpression of GmAP2 in soybean root hairs led to significant increases in above-ground dry weight, phosphorus content, and total phosphorus concentration in transgenic composite plants, as well as significant increases in below-ground dry weight and phosphorus content. However, under phosphorus-sufficient conditions, compared with the control, GmAP2 overexpression resulted in increased phosphorus content and total phosphorus concentration only. Furthermore, regardless of environmental phosphorus levels, overexpression of GmAP2 significantly increased the root length, lateral root length, and lateral root number of the composite plants (Supplemental Fig. 3).

In this study, GmAP2 overexpression increased plant tolerance to acid-aluminum stress, with the root length significantly longer than that of the wild-type plants (Fig. 5). Previous studies have indicated that malondialdehyde is a peroxidation product of cell membranes and that its levels reflect the extent of cell membrane damage (Luan et al. 2018). Under stress conditions, proline is also an important stress-resistant substance that can interact with reactive oxygen species (ROS) in plants to alleviate peroxidation damage to cell membranes (Natarajan et al. 2012). In the present study, in response to treatment with 100 μmol/L AlCl3, compared with that in the control, the proline contents in the GmAP2 transgenic lines significantly increased, whereas the accumulation of malondialdehyde significantly decreased (Fig. 5e, f). These findings suggest that GmAP2 transgenic Arabidopsis plants exhibit increased tolerance to aluminum toxicity. In the context of low-phosphorus stress, compared with those of the wild type, the root length and lateral root length of the GmAP2-overexpressing lines significantly increased. Regardless of the presence of low phosphorus or normal phosphorus, the phosphorus contents of the GmAP2 transgenic lines were significantly greater than those of the wild type. Therefore, the results suggest that GmAP2 contributes to the plant’s resistance to acid-aluminum and low-phosphorus stresses.

Previous studies have shown that under aluminum stress, members of the MATE transporter family and ALMT protein family located on the plasma membrane secrete citrate and malate to confer resistance to aluminum through the formation of nontoxic chelates (Sasaki et al. 2004). In Arabidopsis, the C2H2 zinc finger transcription factor STOP1, which is located in the nucleus, plays a key role in aluminum tolerance (Iuchi et al. 2007). STOP1 can directly mediate aluminum tolerance by binding the conserved sequence in the promoter region to regulate the expression of AtALMT1; it can also control the expression of AtMATE and AtALS3 (Tokizawa et al. 2015; Kochian et al. 2015). In this study, the expression levels of genes such as AtALMT1, AtMATE, AtALS3, and AtSTOP1 were significantly increased in the GmAP2 transgenic lines after AlCl3 treatment (Fig. 7a–d). AtPHT1;1 is a member of the phosphate transporter PHT family. Under low-phosphorus stress, AtPHT1;1 activity is activated to help plants acquire available Pi (Li et al. 2019). In Arabidopsis, AtPHO1 mediates the long-distance transport of phosphate from the roots to the xylem (Fang et al. 2025). AtPHR1 is a MYB-CC family transcription factor that increases the transcriptional activity of the transcription factor GLK1 through physical interactions under low-phosphate stress; therefore, AtPHR1 can regulate multiple responses to Pi starvation (Hammond et al. 2003). AtMGD2 replaces phospholipids by synthesizing monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG), thereby reducing the plant’s phosphorus requirements to adapt to low-phosphorus environments (Kobayashi et al. 2006). In this study, the expression levels of AtPHT1;1, AtPHO1, AtPHR1, and AtMGD2 were significantly increased in the GmAP2 transgenic lines under low-phosphorus conditions (Fig. 8a–d). It is speculated that GmAP2 may increase tolerance to aluminum and low phosphorus by regulating the responses of genes to acid-aluminum and/or low-phosphorus stresses.

ABA is a widely distributed stress hormone in higher plants (Finkelstein et al. 2002). Abscisic acid (ABA) regulates the resistance of soybeans to aluminum (Li et al. 2025). The GmAP2 gene is induced by ABA and responds to abscisic acid, with its expression significantly increasing over time (Fig. 1e). AtERF15 has been reported to positively regulate the abscisic acid hormone in Arabidopsis. In this study, under AlCl3 treatment, the expression of AtERF15 in the transgenic line significantly increased. The expression levels of the Arabidopsis abscisic acid (ABA) regulatory genes AtABI5, AtRD29A, AtRAP2.6, AtABI1, and AtABI2 also changed significantly (Fig. 7i–m). Therefore, based on the current findings, it was hypothesized that the GmAP2 gene in transgenic Arabidopsis would positively regulate the AtERF15 gene, which is responsive to ABA, and would regulate genes directly related to ABA regulation in Arabidopsis when it encountered acid-aluminum stress. In this case, the AtABI5, AtRD29A, and AtRAP2.6 genes were positively regulated in the transgenic lines, whereas the AtABI1 and AtABI2 genes were negatively regulated. In essence, modification of the GmAP2 gene using transgenic technology can ameliorate the damage caused by acid-aluminum stress independently.

Phosphorus is among the essential mineral nutrients required for plant growth and development; however, its availability is often low in many soils. To adapt to low-phosphorus environments, plants regulate their growth through a series of physiological and morphological changes during lateral root development, which play crucial roles under low-phosphorus stress. Previous studies have shown that AtERF109 mediates the interaction between jasmonic acid signaling and auxin synthesis by directly regulating the expression of the key auxin synthesis enzyme-encoding genes ASA1 and YUC2, thereby controlling lateral root formation (Cai et al. 2014). AtKCS16 is a key enzyme in VLCFA synthesis, and its expression is inhibited by AtERF13, leading to decreases in the contents of C18:1, C22:0, and C24:0. VLCFAs are involved in cell wall modification and intercellular signaling, and their deficiency impairs the process of lateral root primordia (LRP) breaking through the endodermis, resulting in the stagnation of LRP development at stages IV–V. Exogenous supplementation with VLCFAs or overexpression of AtKCS16 can reverse lateral root development defects in AtERF13-overexpressing plants, confirming that VLCFAs are direct downstream signaling molecules of AtERF13 (Lv et al. 2021). In this study, the expression of AtERF109 in the GmAP2 transgenic lines significantly increased under low-phosphorus treatment compared with that in the wild type (Fig. 8h). The expression of AtERF13 significantly decreased (Fig. 8g), whereas the expression of the AtKCS16 gene, which is regulated by AtERF13, was significantly increased in the GmAP2 transgenic lines (Fig. 8i). Under low-phosphorus conditions, a substantial increase in the elongation of lateral roots and primary roots was observed in the transgenic Arabidopsis plants (Fig. 6a, e). Therefore, when confronted with low-phosphorus stress, GmAP2-transgenic Arabidopsis may control lateral roots by activating the regulatory pathway of the AtERF109 gene and the long-chain fatty acid biosynthesis pathway, which is negatively regulated by AtERF13. These two processes promote the development of lateral roots, thus facilitating the resistance of GmAP2-transgenic Arabidopsis to low-phosphorus stress.

Under acid-aluminum and low-phosphorus treatments, compared with those in the wild type, the expression levels of genes in the Arabidopsis ERF family also changed significantly in GmAP2 transgenic Arabidopsis. In the presence of acid-aluminum stress, the expression levels of the AtERF6, AtERF15, AtERF5, and AtERF109 genes significantly increased to aid in resistance to aluminum toxicity in the transgenic lines. When plants were subjected to low-phosphorus stress, the expression levels of the AtERF1, AtERF6, and AtERF109 genes significantly increased, and the expression level of the AtERF13 gene decreased to help alleviate low-phosphorus stress. Notably, the AtERF6 and AtERF109 genes in transgenic Arabidopsis played positive roles under both stress conditions. Therefore, we speculate that overexpressing the GmAP2 gene may help increase the tolerance of Arabidopsis under acid-aluminum and low-phosphorus stresses by altering the expression levels of genes in the ERF family.

Conclusion

In summary, the expression pattern of GmAP2, which is enriched in soybean roots, is upregulated by acid-aluminum and low-phosphorus stresses. The GmAP2 protein is an AP2/ERF transcription factor with nuclear location and self-activation activity. Our results indicate that GmAP2 may function as a regulator of stress-response pathways. The overexpression of GmAP2 increased the tolerance of the transgenic plants to aluminum toxicity and low-phosphorus (LP) stress, as evidenced by improved root traits, increased proline accumulation, and reduced malondialdehyde content. Molecular mechanism analysis revealed that the expression levels of the genes changed significantly in the transgenic lines under acid-aluminum stress, which was related to acid-aluminum stress and the ABA response. Under low-phosphorus stress, the expression levels of several LP-tolerant genes and genes that promote lateral root development were significantly altered in the GmAP2 transgenic lines. Under both Al and LP stresses, the genes of the Arabidopsis ERF family were also significantly activated in the GmAP2 transgenic lines. The results of this study provide insight into the molecular mechanisms by which GmAP2 affects Al and LP stress tolerance in plants and offer a molecular basis and gene source for the breeding of resistance traits in soybean.
