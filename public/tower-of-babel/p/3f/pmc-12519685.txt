Mental health of professional football referees in Cameroon: exploring challenges and developing support interventions
Authors: Leonard Tanko Tankeng, Christian Eyoum, Gilbert Mua Akwa, Randol Tsowa Vouomene, Veronique Ange Ngo Bilong, Livingstone Dziany, Narcisse Tinkeu, Maurice Douryang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the mental health status of referees in Cameroonian professional football and identify associated risk factors.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among referees from the Cameroon Elite One, Elite Two and Female Super League during the 2024–2025 season. The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 was used to assess mental health status after officiating a match, classifying scores as normal, mild, moderate, severe or extremely severe for depression, anxiety and stress symptoms. Data were analysed using R Studio (V.4.2.2). Logistic regression identified associations with statistical significance set at p<0.05.ResultsOut of 179 professional referees in Cameroon, 150 (83.8%) participated. Ages ranged from 18 to 45 years (mean=30±5.86 years). Depression symptoms were observed in 28%, with higher rates in assistant referees (32.4%) than in central referees (23.7%). Anxiety symptoms were present in 42.7% of participants, while 12% exhibited stress symptoms. Lower level referees had higher levels of all symptoms. Notably, 90.5% of referees with symptoms of depression had at least one additional mental health symptom. Severe symptoms of depression co-occurred with severe anxiety and stress symptoms. Having another profession significantly increased risks of depression symptoms (p=0.007, OR 7.7) and anxiety (p=0.003, OR 4.2). Performance concerns were the main psychological stressor (74.0%).ConclusionCameroonian referees experience high levels of depression, anxiety and stress symptoms, with assistant referees more affected. Holding another profession increases mental health risks. Targeted interventions are needed to address psychological stressors and improve referees’ well-being.Keywords: Football, Depression, Anxiety, Stress, Athlete

Introduction

Football referees face significant physical and psychological stress during matches.1 The WHO defines mental health as a state of well-being, in which an individual can cope with stress, work productively and contribute to the community.2 While mental health initiatives for athletes have grown, referees remain largely overlooked.3 Referees make critical decisions under high pressure while ensuring fair play and safety but often face pressure and abuse from players, coaches, media, fans, assessors and football federations.4

A study among European football referees found a 4-week prevalence of 6% for distress and 12% for anxiety/depression, with a 12-month incidence of 10% for distress and 16% for anxiety/depression.5 A similar study in Turkey revealed 24.5% of referees had mild depression, 7.6% moderate and 2.5% severe. Anxiety was mild in 18.7%, moderate in 4.4% and severe in 9%, while 38.8% reported moderate stress and 1.8% severe stress.6 In Africa, El Bakry et al found that 15.9% of referees from the African Confederation of Football (CAF) experienced mild anxiety, 1% had moderate anxiety and only two showed mild signs of depression.7

Referees continue to face abuse and deliberate acts of aggression, intensifying the demands for accurate decision-making in an already high-pressure environment. In England, 93% of referees reported abuse, whereas in France, 68% reported abuse.8 Verbal and physical abuse are more frequent and intense at the grassroots level. While it seems referees are expected to endure such mistreatment as part of their role, these stressors may have serious consequences on their mental health. This study assesses the mental health of Cameroonian football referees, shedding light on the mental health aspects of refereeing in a low- and middle-income country (LMIC) context focusing on their post-match mental health. It investigated the prevalence of symptoms of depression, anxiety and stress, as well as the stressors and factors associated with these symptoms. By contributing to the limited literature on this topic in LMICs, it offers insights for policymakers to improve practices in Cameroon and globally.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted from 5 April 2024 to 1 March 2025, and is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Study setting and sampling

The study targeted all licensed referees for the ongoing football season in the Cameroon Elite One, Elite Two and Female Super League. A consecutive, non-exhaustive sampling method was applied. We included all referees who held a valid referee licence and consented to participate in the study. The Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21)9 was administered to the referees 1 week after they officiated a match following the guidelines of this mental health assessment tool. Data collection took place between match days 1 and 5 of the Cameroon Elite One, Elite Two and the Female Super League. Questionnaires with incomplete responses were excluded from the analysis. Referees who withdrew consent during or after data collection were also excluded.

Statistical analysis

Completed questionnaires were analysed in R Studio (V.4.2.2). Quantitative variables were summarised as means, frequencies and percentages. The DASS-21, validated for athletes and non-athletes, was used as the mental health assessment tool.10 The DASS-21 consists of 21 items, subdivided into three subscales for depression, anxiety and stress. Responses were scored and then classified as normal, mild, moderate, severe or extremely severe based on the cut-off values for each subscale. Logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with depression, anxiety and stress. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Participants completed the DASS-21 by rating 21 statements on a 0–3 scale: 0 (Never—did not apply at all), 1 (Sometimes—applied to some degree or occasionally), 2 (Often—applied considerably or frequently) and 3 (Almost Always —applied very much or most of the time). Data collected included sociodemographic details (age, sex, education, experience, demotion history, promotion status and referee rank). The DASS-21 questionnaire ended with a multiple-choice question on perceived emotional stressors.

Equity, diversity and inclusion statement

We included all referees, regardless of gender, race, disability, ethnicity or socioeconomic background.

Public involvement statement

Our study included referees and assessors, some with past abuse experiences, while others embraced advocating for mental health. They contributed to questionnaire design, data collection and committed to supporting future research.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Out of 179 referees and assistant referees registered across the concerned leagues, 150 completed questionnaires were retained, yielding a response rate of 83.8%. We excluded 13 incomplete questionnaires, while 16 referees refused or withdrew consent. Among the respondents, 34 (22.7%) were women and 116 (77.3%) were men. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 45 years, with a mean age of 30±5.86 years. Participants had an average of 4 years of experience. Additionally, 118 respondents (78.7%) reported being engaged in another professional activity alongside refereeing. The study sample consisted of 76 referees (50.7%), including 18 women and 58 men, and 74 assistant referees (49.3%), comprising 16 women and 58 men. Regarding marital status, 88 (58.6%) were single, while 62 (41.3%) were either married or cohabiting. In terms of educational attainment, 102 (68%) had attended university, and 48 (32%) had completed secondary education. The distribution across leagues was as follows: 52 (34.7%) were Elite One referees, 64 (42.7%) were Elite Two referees and 34 (22.7%) were referees in the Female Super League.

Depression in referees

Out of the total participants, 42 (28.0%) exhibited symptoms of depression ranging from mild to severe. Among these, 18 (12.0%) were Elite One referees, 16 (10.7%) were Elite Two referees and 8 (5.3%) were Female Super League referees. Within the Elite One referees subgroup, 18 (34.7%) showed symptoms of depression, with 14 (26.9%) experiencing mild and 4 (7.7%) moderate symptoms. In Elite Two, 16 (25.0%) of referees had symptoms of depression, with 6 (9.4%) classified as mild, 6 (9.4%) as moderate and 4 (6.3%) as severe. In the Female Super League, 8 (23.5%) had depression symptoms, with 1 (2.9%) classified as mild and 7 (20.6%) as moderate (table 1).

Symptoms of stress in referees

All referees who reported severe symptoms of stress, depression or anxiety also had at least one additional mental health symptom. Among all participants with symptoms of depression, 90.5% had at least one coexisting mental health symptom. Severe depressive symptoms were consistently accompanied by extremely severe anxiety and severe stress symptoms. All referees with severe stress symptoms also reported symptoms of extremely severe anxiety and severe depression.

Emotional stressors of referees

A total of 111 referees (74.0%) reported that concerns about their performance during matches were a source of emotional stress. A total of 46 referees (30.7%) reported verbal abuse occurring during the game and via online platforms in the days following the match as an emotional stressor (table 4).

Associated factors

Referees are exposed to multiple stressors that may contribute to anxiety, depression and stress. Several factors have been identified as potential contributors to these conditions. In this study, univariate logistic regression analysis revealed that having another profession was significantly associated with both depression (p=0.007, OR 7.7, 95% CI 1.74 to 33.83) and anxiety (p=0.003, OR 4.2, 95% CI 1.61 to 10.92). However, after multiple logistic regression analyses, the only statistically significant factor associated with depression was having another profession (p=0.013, OR 7.1, 95% CI 1.51 to 33.65).

Discussion

Sociodemographic characteristics

Refereeing demands consistent, precise decision-making under physical and psychological stress, potentially contributing to mental health disorders.11 This study explores referees’ mental health, assessing 150 referees and assistant referees for depression, anxiety and stress. Of these, 34 (22.7%) were women and 116 (77.3%) men, with ages ranging from 18 to 45 years (mean: 30±5.86). This suggests that Cameroonian referees are relatively young, likely due to frequent training programmes promoting competent youth referees. In contrast, CAF referees had a higher mean age of 40±3.35 years, and Spanish first-division referees averaged 38.7±3.5 years.7 The difference may stem from CAF’s structured pathway requiring extensive experience and multiple evaluations, whereas Cameroon’s league integrates qualified younger referees more quickly.

Additionally, 118 (78.7%) of referees reported having another professional activity. Since refereeing in Cameroon is not a full-time profession and pay is match-based, many seek additional employment to meet financial needs. Similar trends are observed in Europe, where over 90% of referees work an average of 36 hours per week alongside officiating.5 A study on German Bundesliga referees also noted that many are not full-time employees of the football association and depend on other jobs for financial stability.12

Depression

Our findings indicate that the overall prevalence of symptoms of depression among referees was 28%. Within our study population, 14% exhibited mild symptoms of depression, 11.3% moderate and 2.7% severe symptoms of depression. Notably, the four (2.7%) participants with severe depression were assistant referees officiating in the lower league (Elite Two). This may be attributed to the high performance demands placed on referees in Cameroon’s professional leagues, despite limited access to officiating resources. The resulting errors contribute to psychological distress, including dysphoria, self-deprecation, lack of interest and anhedonia, thereby increasing susceptibility to depression.6 13 We also found a difference in the prevalence of depressive symptoms between the men (34 (29.3%)) and the women (8 (23.5%)) referees. This may be linked to the socioeconomic and cultural burdens of men in the local context.11

The prevalence of depressive symptoms was slightly higher among assistant referees (32.4%) compared with central referees (23.7%). One possible explanation is that central referees are positioned closer to the ball and players, allowing for more accurate decision-making, whereas assistant referees, who often lack essential technological support such as referee communication headsets or goal-line technology, are more prone to errors. Also, assistant referees are closer to fans, substitute players and coaching staff who often verbally abuse or contest decisions. These challenges may foster mental health symptoms during and after the game.

Our findings differ from those reported among referees officiating in the CAF, where depression rates were below 5%.7 The lower prevalence in CAF referees may be explained by greater access to technological resources and officiating support, which improve decision-making accuracy and reduce stress levels.14 Additionally, the CAF study population had a mean age approximately 10 years older than that of our participants, suggesting greater mental resilience and relative financial stability, which serve as a protective factor against mental health disorders.15

In European professional leagues, the prevalence of depression over a single season was 16%, with a 1 month baseline prevalence of 12%.5 Our results closely align with findings from the Turkish professional league, where 34.6% of referees reported depressive symptoms, with 24.5% classified as mild, 7.6% as moderate and 2.5% as severe. However, it is important to note that the mental health of Turkish referees in that study was significantly impacted by COVID-19 and the overall psychological burden of officiating during the pandemic.16 Comparing may be difficult as different assessment tools are used. Moreover, genetic predisposition, cultural influences, environmental stressors, societal pressures and variations in coping strategies all play a role in shaping the mental health outcomes of referees in different settings.11 17

Anxiety

In our study, 42.7% of participants exhibited symptoms of anxiety, categorised as mild (16%), moderate (21.3%), severe (4.0%) and extremely severe (1.3%). Overall, 14 (41.2%) of the female referees showed anxiety symptoms. A similar result to that found in the men referees showed that 50 (43.1%) manifested anxiety symptoms. The prevalence of 42.7% in this study is notably higher than the 24% anxiety reported among Turkish referees and the findings by El Bakry et al, who reported 15.9% mild and 1% moderate anxiety among CAF referees. This difference may be attributed to the heightened performance pressures and the frequent occurrence of officiating errors due to limited resources. Additionally, the proximity to fans in stadiums in our context may amplify the impact of verbal abuse from fans, coaches and players, further exacerbating stress and anxiety. This aligns with prior literature saying performance concerns and the fear of making mistakes have been identified as key triggers of stress and anxiety in referees.18 Furthermore, our study population was relatively young (mean age 30±5 years) with an average of only 4 years of experience, which contributes to increased decision-making pressure and performance anxiety in referees.19

Stress

Only 18 (12%) of all participants were reported to have stress symptoms, with 14 (9.3%) experiencing mild stress, 2 (1.3%) moderate stress and 2 (1.3%) severe stress. Overall, stress symptoms were observed in 14 male referees (12.1%) and 4 female referees (11.8%), indicating a similar prevalence. This finding differs from the 40.6% prevalence reported in Turkish referees. Although different assessment scales were used, it is important to note that the COVID-19 pandemic significantly contributed to increased stress levels among referees in the Turkish study.16 Referees who met the cut-off scores for stress included 2 (1.3%) Elite One referees, 12 (8%) Elite Two referees and 4 (2.7%) referees from the Female Super League.

Match referees who had cut-off scores for stress were 2 (1.3%) Elite One referees, 12 (8%) Elite Two referees, while 4 (2.7%) were referees of the Female Super League. Additionally, two assistant referees from Elite Two exhibited symptoms of severe stress. This aligns with existing evidence suggesting that lower level referees are associated with higher stress.19

Emotional stressors

In this study, 111 referees (74%), comprising 90 men and 21 women, identified performance-related concerns as their primary emotional stressor. This was followed by fear of physical aggression, reported by 68 referees (45.3%), including 48 men and 20 women. Verbal abuse from players, fans, coaches and online sources was reported by 46 referees (30.7%), of whom 37 were men and 9 were women. Additionally, 46 referees (30.7%), comprising 15 women and 31 men, identified referee assessors as a source of stress. These findings align with studies on Turkish referees, where performance concerns were also the most common stressor.6 13 Refereeing demands precise decision-making, and the career progression of referees depends on their performance. This explains the pressure related to performance, as it is directly linked to their progress. Fear of physical aggression is particularly high in lower leagues like Cameroon due to crowd proximity, inadequate barriers and low security.20 Verbal abuse, a significant stressor, is more noticeable in quieter settings, particularly in lower professional leagues with smaller crowds, as reported by Turkish referees.20 Studies suggest that despite being present at all levels, abuse is more frequent at lower levels, explaining its impact on referees’ mental health,8 21 which explains our results. Referee assessors, present during matches to evaluate officials, were also a notable stressor (46, 30.7%). Their reports influence promotions, adding pressure on referees seeking career advancement. Similar stress has been reported in Turkish professional leagues.22

Associated factors

Having an additional profession was significantly associated with depression (p value=0.007, OR 7.7, 95% CI 1.74 to 33.83) and anxiety (p value=0.003, OR 4.2, 95% CI 1.61 to 10.92). Psychological well-being has been reported to be poorer among individuals with multiple jobs, with a higher occurrence of mental health disorders.23 This is primarily due to the increased stress and overload that come with holding multiple positions. The role strain theory suggests that multiple social roles heighten the obligations an individual must fulfil, leading to greater difficulty in meeting the expectations of various jobs, which in turn negatively impacts mental well-being.24 Additionally, numerous studies have shown that holding multiple jobs increases the risk of both work-related and non-work-related injuries, driven by fatigue, sleep deprivation and the mental and physical strain caused by switching between different roles.25,27

Clinical and policy implications

This study highlights the urgent need for mental health support for referees, especially in LMICs such as Cameroon. At the central level, governing bodies should adopt and enforce policies that protect referees, prevent abuse and promote structured mental health and performance programmes. At the peripheral level, referees should be encouraged to engage in resilience training, relaxation techniques and regular professional development to enhance well-being and performance. Policymakers must also strengthen regulations against abuse and expand access to technologies such as video assistant referees, communication headsets and goal-line systems to reduce performance-related stress. Further research in diverse contexts is needed to identify protective factors and guide effective interventions.

Limitations

This cross-sectional study limits the ability to explore changes in mental health over time. Additionally, the use of self-report assessments may result in underreporting or overreporting of symptoms, depending on referees’ subjective perceptions. Furthermore, the absence of clinical evaluations limits the diagnostic accuracy for identifying potential mental health disorders among participants.
