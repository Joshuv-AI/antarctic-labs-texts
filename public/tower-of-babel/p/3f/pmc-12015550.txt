Oxytocin influences parental care in male threespine stickleback across multiple time scales
Authors: Usan Dan, Meghan F Maciejewski, Emma Schwaiger, Alison M Bell
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxytocin (OXT) and its homologs are known to regulate parental care in vertebrates, but it is unknown what role these neuropeptides may play in the evolutionary loss of care. Here, we compared two recently diverged ecotypes of threespine stickleback (Gasterosteus aculeatus) that differ in parental care. Males of the common ecotype provide obligate, uniparental care to their offspring, whereas males of the white ecotype abandon their offspring after fertilization. To test if OXT plays a role in the loss of care, we manipulated OXT in males of both ecotypes via intraperitoneal injection of a vehicle control, OXT single- or double-dose, or an OXT antagonist. We observed the behavioral response to injection at two time points for commons (0 and 4 days post-fertilization (dpf)) and one for whites (0 dpf). Our results suggest that, in commons, OXT promotes the onset of care but not its maintenance. Notably, commons that ultimately terminated their clutches did not respond to OXT at 0 dpf, which may have contributed to their failure to transition to a state of care. Whites responded to OXT manipulation in a different manner than commons, suggesting that the loss of care in whites is not due to a loss of sensitivity to OXT, or insufficient levels of OXT ligand, but rather an evolutionary change to the underlying parental circuit that OXT is acting on. These results provide evidence that ancient hormonal systems like OXT can contribute to losses of care over multiple timescalesKeywords: Isotocin, Paternal care, Evolutionary loss, Gasterosteus aculeatus, Neuropeptide, Social behavior, Nonapeptide

Introduction

The nonapeptide family of neuropeptides, which includes arginine-vasopressin (AVP), oxytocin (OXT), and their relatives, has a deep evolutionary history in both form and function (reviewed in Goodson, 2008; Johnson and Young, 2017). Nonapeptide systems are highly variable across multiple time scales, allowing for flexibility in the behaviors they regulate (reviewed in Goodson, 2008). Across shorter time scales, nonapeptides can facilitate behavioral responses to changes in season, social context, and internal physiological state (reviewed in Goodson, 2008). For example, in mammals, OXT can contribute to changes in female responsiveness to offspring cues as they transition across reproductive stages (reviewed in Valtcheva and Froemke, 2019). Across longer time scales, nonapeptides can facilitate behavioral divergence among populations and species. For example, differences in AVP receptor distribution underlie the divergence in pair bonding and affiliative behaviors among species of Microtus voles (Young et al., 1997, 1999; Pitkow et al., 2001; Lim et al., 2004).

The work in Microtus voles provides a classic example of how natural variation can be leveraged to understand the contributions of nonapeptides to behavioral diversity. However, apart from work in Microtus (e.g., Young et al., 1999), Peromyscus (Bendesky et al., 2017), and dark-eyed juncos (e.g., Kimmitt et al., 2020), few studies have manipulated hormones in a comparative context to find causal links between hormones and behavior (Jourjine and Hoekstra, 2021). Manipulative studies allow us to establish causal links between mechanisms and behavior to address outstanding questions about how behavior evolves more directly. For example, nonapeptide mechanisms can be tweaked by changing the amount of hormone ligand being produced or the number of receptors expressed in a given brain region. One outstanding question is which of these may be easier to change over different time scales (e.g., see Goodson, 2008; O’Connell and Hofmann, 2012).

To better understand how nonapeptides contribute to behavioral evolution, in this study, we manipulate OXT in two divergent populations (ecotypes) that differ in their parental care. Parenting behavior is particularly useful for understanding the role of nonapeptides in the evolution of behavior because nonapeptides have been repeatedly implicated in parenting behavior. Previous work has shown that oxytocin and its homologs mesotocin and isotocin promote parental care across vertebrate taxa in mammals (reviewed in Veenema and Neumann, 2008; Taylor and Grieb, 2022), birds (Thayananuphat et al., 2011; Chokchaloemwong et al., 2013), frogs (Fischer et al., 2019), and fishes (O’Connell et al., 2012; DeAngelis et al., 2017, 2018, 2020). While different taxa have different homologs of oxytocin, such as isotocin in teleost fishes, hereafter, we refer to oxytocin and its homologs as oxytocin (OXT) in light of their shared evolutionary history and in the interest of facilitating cross-taxa discussion (Theofanopoulou et al., 2021). Generally, the effects of oxytocin on parental care can be categorized in terms of the initiation and maintenance of care. For example, oxytocin receptor (Oxtr) knockout mice show increased pup abandonment but no differences in maternal care behavior once care is initiated (Rich et al., 2014), demonstrating that in mice, oxytocin is critical for the initiation but not the maintenance of parenting behavior. Alternatively, in rats, females treated with Oxtr antagonist several days post-partum spend less time licking and grooming pups, suggesting that oxytocin influences the levels of parenting behavior shown during the maintenance of care (Champagne et al., 2001).

While there is a rich literature on the role of OXT in mammalian maternal care (reviewed in Pedersen, 2013), less is known about OXT’s role in paternal care (but see work on California mice (Guoynes and Marler, 2022), voles (Bales et al., 2004; He et al., 2021), titi monkeys (Baxter et al., 2020), and humans (Feldman et al., 2010)) because in mammals males always provide care alongside females. This makes it difficult to tease apart the mechanisms regulating paternal care (reviewed in Maciejewski and Bell, 2022). In contrast, teleost fishes show male care in both uniparental and biparental forms (Gross and Sargent, 1985; Mank et al., 2005), offering opportunities to gain new insights into paternal care.

Threespine stickleback (Gasterosteus aculeatus) are especially good subjects for studying the proximate mechanisms and evolution of paternal care because they exhibit sole male care, as well as extensive behavioral variation among closely related populations. Once male stickleback establish a territory, build a nest, and spawn with a female, they care for their embryos primarily by fanning their pectoral fins over the nest to oxygenate the embryos (Van Iersel, 1953). Fanning behavior increases over time and peaks as the embryos approach hatching four to five days post-fertilization (Van Iersel, 1953). Males continue to care for and defend their fry for up to a week post-hatching (Van Iersel, 1953).

Interestingly, there is an atypical ecotype of threespine stickleback that does not provide care (Blouw and Hagen, 1990). This non-caring ecotype, known as the “white” stickleback due to the males’ conspicuous white breeding coloration, occurs in Nova Scotia, Canada (Blouw and Hagen, 1990). White stickleback fathers do not fan their embryos or defend their fry. Instead, after fertilization, whites abandon their embryos by spitting them out of the nest (i.e., “dispersing” the embryos) (Blouw, 1996). This embryo dispersal behavior begins rapidly, typically within 2–10 min after fertilization (Blouw, 1996). The white ecotype is thought to have recently diverged from the typical parental “common” ecotype, <12 KYA (Samuk, 2016). These ecotypes provide a unique opportunity to explore the hormonal basis of divergence in parenting behavior in a pair of closely related populations rather than comparing species that are often millions of years diverged. Comparing stickleback populations allows us to explore differences across shorter evolutionary time scales, which has the potential to be particularly powerful for homing in on mechanisms associated with behavioral divergence in its early stages, when there has been less time to accumulate differences that may be confounded with the behaviors of interest (reviewed in Jourjine and Hoekstra, 2021).

In addition, the white-common system provides the opportunity to study a population that has lost parental care, which can tell us how losses at the behavioral level may relate to losses at the mechanistic level. How behaviors can be lost is a particularly perplexing question, given that the hormonal systems regulating behaviors are likely robust, with redundancies and compensatory mechanisms. For example, Berendzen et al. (2023) recently demonstrated that prairie voles without functional Oxtr still maintain wild-type levels of pair bonding and parenting behaviors. This suggests that losing a behavior like parental care could be difficult because several aspects of a hormonal pathway likely need to change for care to be lost. Perhaps this resilience of hormone-mediated behaviors contributes to the rarity of losses of care relative to gains (Reynolds et al., 2002).

Although parenting behavior has been studied in stickleback (e.g., Van Iersel, 1953; Wootton, 1976), and there has been some work on the hormonal basis of these parenting behaviors (e.g. Borg and Mayer, 1995; Páll et al., 2002a, 2002b; Kleszczyńska et al., 2012), little is known about the evolution of the hormonal mechanisms relevant to parenting behavior in the white-common system. A recent study found that oxytocin gene expression was upregulated in the brains of males actively caring for offspring relative to males without offspring (Bukhari et al., 2019), suggesting a promising candidate for further experimentation. Thus, to investigate the hormonal basis of divergent parental care of white and common stickleback, we pharmacologically manipulated male white and common stickleback via intraperitoneal (IP) injection with a single dose of oxytocin (OXT), double dose of oxytocin (OXT2), oxytocin antagonist (ANT), or vehicle control. We used these data to address two overarching questions. (1) In the common stickleback, what is OXT’s role in the initiation and/or maintenance of parenting behavior? (2) In the white stickleback, what is OXT’s role in the loss of parental care?

To address whether and how OXT plays a role in initiating and/or maintaining parenting behavior in commons, we injected individual male commons at two stages of care: at the onset of care immediately after spawning (0 days post fertilization, dpf), and again at 4 dpf when their parenting behaviors peak (Van Iersel, 1953; Behrens et al., 2024). If OXT promotes the initiation of care, we expect that commons treated with OXT at 0 dpf will provide more paternal care, i.e., fanning the embryos more than controls, and ANT-treated males will do the opposite. If OXT is important for the maintenance of care, we expect that commons treated with OXT at 4 dpf will fan their embryos more than controls, and ANT-treated males will do the opposite.

To evaluate OXT’s role in the evolutionary loss of parental care in whites, we injected each male white once immediately after spawning (0 dpf) when males typically disperse their embryos (Blouw, 1996; Behrens et al., 2024). This allowed us to discriminate among three hypotheses for the evolutionary loss of care in whites. If whites lost care because they produce insufficient levels of OXT ligand, despite maintaining the underlying “parental circuit” that is present in commons, then by increasing circulating levels of OXT via injection, we should be able to make whites behave like commons and show more parenting behaviors like fanning (the ligand hypothesis). Alternatively, whites might have lost care because their “parental circuit” changed. We propose two ways that the “parental circuit” may have changed in whites. First, they may have become insensitive to oxytocin, and therefore, their behavior will not change in response to treatment with OXT, OXT2, or ANT (the insensitivity hypothesis). Insensitivity to OXT manipulation could result from insufficient expression of OXT receptors to respond to the ligand. Alternatively, whites might have lost care not because their “parental circuit” is insensitive to OXT, but because there has been some other change to the underlying “parental circuit”, which results in a behavioral response to OXT in whites that is different from commons (the neural substrate hypothesis). This could suggest that whites have sufficient OXT receptors to respond to manipulation, but they have a different pattern of receptor distribution than commons, resulting in a different behavioral response.

Methods

Animal husbandry

The adult threespine stickleback used in this experiment were derived from wild populations. Fish of the common, parental ecotype originated from Cherry Burton Road, New Brunswick, Canada (N 46° 01.516′, W 64° 06.150′), and fish of the white, non-parental ecotype originated from Canal Lake, Nova Scotia, Canada (N 44° 29′54.0″, W 63° 54′09.1″). The ecotypes were collected from different locations because they are generally not sympatric at these two sites (commons do occur at low frequency at Canal Lake), and we wanted to avoid potential hybrids as these ecotypes are suspected to be experiencing ongoing hybridization (Blouw and Hagen, 1990; Samuk, 2016). Fish were collected under the Canadian Department of Fisheries and Oceans Maritime permit #343930 held by Dr. Anne Dalziel and Dr. Laura Weir. Embryos of each ecotype were generated in the field in the summer of 2022 via artificial fertilization and shipped to the University of Illinois. Fish were subsequently reared in the lab for approximately one year before beginning our experiments in the summer of 2023. Fish were housed in single-family, mixed-sex groups in tanks with gravel and artificial plants. Experiments were conducted from May to August 2023. During this period, fish were maintained at summer conditions (17 °C, 16 h light: 8 h dark photoperiod) in a brackish water (10 ppt) recirculating system with 10 % daily automatic water changes. Fish were fed once daily after 14:00 CST when experiments had concluded for the day with a mixture of live brine shrimp, frozen bloodworms, mysis shrimp, cyclops, and spirulina. All procedures were approved by the University of Illinois Institutional Care and Use Committee (Protocol #21031).

Scoring behavior

Males of each ecotype were visually identified by their breeding coloration (red throat, blue eyes), removed from group housing, weighed, and relocated to new tanks where they were housed alone (36L × 33W × 24H cm). Each tank contained gravel, an artificial plant, and nesting materials (a box of sand and filamentous algae). We allowed males to acclimate to their new tanks overnight and begin nest construction before beginning injections and behavioral observations.

We exposed males to gravid females of the same ecotype to induce courtship and spawning. A gravid female was placed in a refuge in the male’s tank. The refuge consisted of a cylindrical PVC container (10.5 cm in diameter, 8 cm high) filled with water from the male’s tank with a rubber plug to keep the female inside. After a five-minute acclimation, the plug was removed, and the female was allowed to exit the refuge into the male’s tank. If a spawning did not occur after 15 min, the female was removed and returned to her home tank. Males were allowed at least 30 min before introducing a new female. Typically, we waited until the next day before attempting again. If a spawning was observed (i.e., the male swam through the nest after the female deposited eggs), the male and female were removed immediately. The female was returned to her home tank and the male was injected (see Pharmacological manipulation).

Following injection, males were returned to their tanks and placed in a refuge. Males were held in the refuge for 30 min to allow them to recover from the injection and handling and to allow the pharmacological manipulations to take effect. This 30-min recovery time was determined based on previous work in teleosts (DeAngelis et al., 2017) and our own pilot studies (see Supplement). Then, the male was released from the refuge and allowed to enter his tank. We began a 15-minute behavioral observation when the male exited the refuge. We recorded two behaviors using Jwatcher (Blumstein et al., 2006; see Supplementary Table 1 for ethogram): time fanning and dispersals. Later, latency to nest, fan, and disperse were extracted from the Jwatcher data files by retrieving the first instance that the respective behaviors were performed in a trial. Behaviors were scored by an observer blind to treatment.

Male whites typically remove their embryos from the nest soon after fertilization and disperse them by picking them up from the nest and spitting them out into the surrounding area (Blouw, 1996). In the lab, males perform anywhere from 10 to 110 dispersals within 15 min of fertilization (Behrens et al., 2024). However, in our study, seven out of the 40 male whites we observed did not show any dispersal behavior within the first 15-minute observation period. Our pilot data suggest that failure to disperse may result from holding male whites after injection, preventing them from immediately returning to their nests (see Supplement), but not from the treatment itself (see Section 3.4). We observed these seven males for a second 15-minute period immediately after finishing the first (for a total of 30 min) to determine if they would disperse if provided more time. Males that did not disperse in the second trial (five of seven males) were categorized as “non-dispersers.”

Four days after fertilization, male commons were injected again with the same treatment as 0 dpf to test for an effect of OXT manipulation on the maintenance of parenting behavior. 4 dpf is typically when the primary parenting behavior (fanning) peaks in parental stickleback (Van Iersel, 1953). Males were again held in the refuge for 30 min post-injection to allow them to recover from the injection and handling and to allow the pharmacological manipulation to take effect. When the male exited the refuge, we began a 15-minute behavioral observation identical to the observation described for 0 dpf. Nests were checked for developing embryos at the end of the observation period.

Pharmacological manipulation

We tested the effect of oxytocin on behavior by administering one of four treatments via intraperitoneal (IP) injection with a syringe (BD syringe, 31 gauge, 8 mm) to each male: 1× PBS (a vehicle control), 2 μg/g b.w. oxytocin (referred to as “OXT”, (Ser4, Ile8)-Oxytocin, 966.15 molecular weight, Bachem, Torrance, CA, USA, Product Number: 4030890), 4 μg/g b.w. oxytocin (referred to as “OXT2”), or 200 μg/g b. w. non-peptide oxytocin receptor antagonist (referred to as “ANT,” L-368,899 hydrochloride, 627.68 molecular weight, Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, United Kingdom, Catalog Number: 2641). OXT, OXT2, and ANT were dissolved in 1× PBS so that all males received the same volume (40 μL/g b.w.). IP injections are a well-established method for manipulating neuropeptide hormones and behavior in fishes (e.g., see O’Connell et al., 2012; Reddon et al., 2012; DeAngelis et al., 2017, 2018, 2020). These studies typically assume that hormones administered peripherally via IP injection cross the blood brain barrier and act centrally to affect behavior. However, we acknowledge that peripheral administration of nonapeptides may affect unintended targets, and it is possible that these manipulations affected the oxytocin system as a whole, not just the brain. The antagonist was selected because it has been shown to block the activation of both OXT receptor subtypes and affect social behavior in zebrafish (Landin et al., 2020). A high dosage of ANT was chosen because only high doses were found to affect social behavior in zebrafish (Landin et al., 2020). Dosages of OXT and OXT2 were chosen from within the range shown to affect social behavior in other teleost species (Reddon et al., 2012; Reddon et al., 2014; Lindeyer et al., 2015). The OXT2 treatment was included to test for a dose-dependent effect of OXT.

Whites were injected once, on 0 dpf. Commons were injected twice, on 0 dpf and 4 dpf, with the same treatment each time. We chose to manipulate OXT signaling at 0 dpf, immediately after fertilization, because that is the point when males are transitioning either to a state of paternal care (commons) or are dispersing their embryos (whites). It is important to note that it is possible that endogenous OXT was released in response to spawning. Therefore, because we injected after spawning, the results at 0 dpf might have been affected by the presence of endogenous OXT. To test the effects of OXT on the maintenance of care, we chose to manipulate OXT signaling again at 4 dpf in commons because the effects of nonapeptide manipulation are transient; the antagonist has a 2-h half-life in mammals (Thompson et al., 1997) and the effects of oxytocin manipulations are typically most effective within an hour or two after injection (e.g., see review in rhesus macaques, Freeman et al., 2016).

All males were injected between 9:00 and 13:30 CST. After each injection, we waited 30 min before beginning a 15-min behavioral observation (see Scoring behavior).

In total, we injected and observed the behavior of 98 males. 74 of these males dispersed (whites) or parented (commons) as expected (PBS: N = 10 commons, N = 11 whites; OXT: N = 11 commons, N = 9 whites; OXT2: N = 12 commons, N = 7 whites; ANT: N = 6 commons, N = 8 whites). The 24 males that did not behave as expected included 5 whites that did not disperse within 30 min (PBS: N = 1, OXT: N = 3, OXT2: N = 0, ANT: N = 1) and 19 commons that terminated their clutches early (i.e., males whose nests did not contain live embryos at 4 dpf) (PBS: N = 1, OXT: N = 7, OXT2: N = 5, ANT: N = 6). The whites that did not disperse within 30 min were excluded from our behavioral analyses because there were not enough non-dispersing individuals for analysis. The commons that terminated their clutches early were included in our analyses as clutch “terminators.” The commons that behaved as expected (i.e., did have embryos in their nests at 4 dpf) were categorized as “carers.” Clutch termination occurred at similar rates in the lab in previous years (see Supplement), and male stickleback are known to readily consume their own eggs (FitzGerald and Van Havre, 1987), therefore the existence of these “terminators” was not unusual. We leveraged the data on these terminators to investigate how the success of a male’s clutch might affect and be affected by their responsiveness to OXT manipulation.

Statistical analysis

To address OXT’s role in parenting behavior across stages in commons, we tested for an effect of treatment on behavior in commons at 0 dpf and 4 dpf. Because some males terminated their clutches early, we also tested for an effect of parental trajectory (carer vs. terminator). To address OXT’s role on a longer time scale in the evolutionary loss of parental care, we tested for an effect of treatment on behavior in whites at 0 dpf. In our results, we primarily focus on patterns in the control (PBS) and OXT treatments. A section of the Discussion touches on the patterns in the OXT2 and ANT groups, which showed several unexpected patterns.

Although there were no significant differences in latency to nest between treatment groups (see Results), before testing for the effects of treatment (and parental trajectory in commons) on each behavior, we first adjusted for latency to nest to account for individual differences in recovery from injection, handling, and return to reproductive behavior. We defined latency to nest as the time it took for the focal male to approach his nest after emerging from the refuge post-injection. To correct latency behaviors (latency to fan and latency to disperse), we subtracted latency to nest so that adjusted latencies represented the time it took a male to perform the behavior after first visiting the nest. To adjust the duration (time fanning) and count data (number of dispersals), latency to nest was subtracted from the total length of the trial (15 min for most individuals, see Section 2.4.4 for exceptions) to produce an adjusted trial length. Duration and count data were then divided by the adjusted trial length to produce proportion or rate data, respectively (time fanning became the proportion of trial spent fanning, number of dispersals became dispersals per second). After behaviors were adjusted for latency, they were transformed, if necessary, to better fit the assumptions of normality.

After behavioral data were adjusted for latency to nest and normalized, we ran linear models using the lm function in the stats package. We ran separate models for each behavior. When significant main effects were detected, we performed post hoc pairwise comparisons using the emmeans function in the emmeans package (Lenth, 2023), and the resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the FDR method. We calculated effect sizes (Cohen’s d) for each pairwise comparison using the eff_size function in the emmeans package. Results of the pairwise comparisons and effect sizes are reported in Supplementary Tables 3, 4, 5, and 6. The specifics of our analysis for each ecotype are detailed below. All analyses and data visualizations were performed using R Version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, 2023).

Effect of OXT manipulation on behaviors 4 dpf, separated by parental trajectory in commons

Because carers and terminators were in different behavioral states at 4 dpf (carers were fanning their embryos and terminators were fanning empty nests), we separated the males by parental trajectory to further investigate treatment effects on fanning behavior in the two different contexts. We ran models on each parental trajectory separately testing for the main effect of treatment. Time fanning was arcsine transformed and latency to fan was square-root transformed.

Effect of OXT manipulation on behaviors 0 dpf in whites

To gain insight into the role OXT plays in the loss of parental care, we tested for an effect of pharmacological manipulation on white behavior at 0 dpf. Behavior on 4 dpf was not investigated in whites because after dispersal, whites return to nest maintenance. For each behavior, we ran linear models testing for the main effect of treatment (PBS, OXT, OXT2, ANT). Latency to disperse, the number of dispersals, and latency to fan were square-root transformed, latency to nest was log-transformed, and time fanning was arcsine transformed. For the males that did not disperse in the first trial but did disperse in the second trial (two of seven males), we combined the two trials into one 30-min trial for analysis; behaviors were adjusted as described above (see Statistical analysis) except the unadjusted trial length was 30 min instead of 15 min.

In the white ecotype, 5 of the 40 males injected were “non-dispersers”, i.e., they did not disperse their embryos after 30 min of observation. Using Fisher’s exact test we tested for an association between treatment and the likelihood of dispersing (or not). This was done using the fisher.test function in the stats package. We performed two tests: a Fisher’s exact test in which each treatment group was considered separate (PBS, OXT, OXT2, and ANT) and a test in which all pharmacological manipulation groups (OXT, OXT2, ANT) were pooled into a single “treated” group and compared to controls injected with PBS. This second test was performed to see if perturbing the OXT system (but not necessarily a specific treatment) was associated with a higher likelihood of failing to disperse.

Results

In this study, we address two overarching questions. First, in the common, parental ecotype, what is OXT’s role in the initiation and/or maintenance of parenting behavior? Second, in the white, non-parental ecotype, what is OXT’s role in the evolutionary loss of parental care? Below, results addressing these two overarching questions are presented in turn.

In the common, parenting ecotype, what is OXT’s role in the initiation and/or maintenance of parenting behavior?

At the initiation of care (0 dpf), single-dose OXT suppressed parenting behavior in commons on a carer trajectory, but not those on a terminator trajectory

33 % of commons did not have embryos in their nests at 4 dpf. These males were categorized retroactively as “terminators” because they terminated their clutches; males with embryos in their nests at 4 dpf were categorized as “carers” because they provided care for the duration of the experiment.

We predicted that OXT would promote parenting behaviors like fanning in commons. While OXT manipulation did not affect how quickly males began fanning embryos (Fig. 1A, Table 1), it did affect how long males fanned their embryos and the effects of OXT depended on whether males were on a carer or terminator trajectory (Fig. 1B, Table 1).

In carers, OXT manipulation at the onset of care (0 dpf) suppressed fanning behavior. Carers treated with OXT spent less time fanning than carers in all other treatment groups (Fig. 1B, PBS-OXT: p = 0.023, OXTOXT2: p = 0.034, OXT-ANT: p = 0.045).

Terminators did not experience this OXT-mediated decrease in fanning behavior shown by carers (Fig. 1B); OXT-treated carers spent less time fanning (Fig. 1B, p = 0.006) than OXT-treated terminators. This was interesting because, on 0 dpf, both terminators and carers had recently fertilized eggs in their nests, so we might expect them to respond similarly to OXT. We did not detect any differences in fanning behavior among treatment groups in terminators, possibly due to limited statistical power, as the sample size of terminators was small, most notably for the PBS group (n = 1).

To investigate the role of OXT in the decision to terminate care, we asked whether commons in certain treatment groups were more likely to terminate their clutches. This was not the case (Fisher’s exact test p = 0.180), but there was evidence that manipulating OXT in general may have contributed to the relatively high rate of clutch termination. Commons with manipulated OXT systems (OXT, OXT2, and ANT treatments combined) terminated their clutches 38.30 % of the time relative to 9.09 % in commons that did not have manipulated OXT systems (Fig. 2), a suggestive but not statistically significant pattern according to Fisher’s exact test (p = 0.082).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of oxytocin in mediating parenting behavior and in the evolutionary loss of parental care using two behaviorally divergent threespine stickleback ecotypes, commons and whites. We set out to address two overarching questions. (1) In the common, parental ecotype we asked, what is OXT’s role in the initiation and/or maintenance of parenting behavior? (2) In the white, non-parental ecotype we asked, what is OXT’s role in the evolutionary loss of parental care?

The effects of OXT on parenting behavior depended on stage (whether they were just starting to initiate parental care or were days into the process), parental trajectory (whether they went on to terminate or care for their clutch) and ecotype (whites or commons). Perturbing the OXT system when male commons were just beginning the transition to a parental state (0 dpf) affected the fanning behavior of individuals that ultimately cared for their clutches (carers, 0 dpf); however, OXT perturbation had no effect on commons that were already in a parental state (carers, 4 dpf), or on commons that ultimately terminated their clutches (terminators, 0 dpf). These findings suggest that in commons, OXT is important for the initiation of parenting behavior but not its maintenance. Moreover, OXT manipulation affected whites and commons differently; manipulating OXT soon after spawning caused male whites to start interacting with their embryos sooner but suppressed fanning behavior in carer commons.

Together, these findings provide support that OXT signaling is important in paternal care in stickleback across multiple time scales (e.g., based on parental stage, trajectory and ecotype), and that its effects on behavior differ across these time scales. Our results also demonstrate that the role of OXT has changed during the evolutionary divergence of the two ecotypes.

Oxytocin promotes the initiation but not the maintenance of care in commons

Consistent with the hypothesis that oxytocin is involved in the initiation but not the maintenance of care in commons, the behavioral effects of OXT depended on the males’ behavioral state, and males seemed especially sensitive to perturbation of the oxytocin system soon after fertilization.

In our experiment, 33 % of male commons terminated their clutches, presumably by eating them. Our results suggest that manipulating the OXT system might have prevented the successful onset of care behavior in some males. Specifically, there was a suggestive pattern that hormone-treated commons (OXT, OXT2, ANT) terminated their clutches more often than PBS-treated commons. Oxytocin has been linked to infanticide and breeding success in other species. For example, oxytocin is thought to play a role in preventing pup-directed aggression and infanticide in both male and female rodents (McCarthy et al., 1986; McCarthy, 1990; Inada et al., 2022). Additionally, in our experiment, terminators, unlike carers, did not respond to OXT at 0 dpf, which suggests that individual differences in the sensitivity and responsiveness to OXT early in the transition to fatherhood could contribute to males’ decisions to ultimately care for or terminate their clutches. In rats, individual females that begin caring sooner and provide more care show higher levels of oxytocin receptors in certain brain regions (Champagne et al., 2001). In stickleback, individual-level variation in OXT responsiveness may contribute to the likelihood that they will parent or terminate their clutches. It is worth noting that terminator males did respond to OXT at 4 dpf after they had terminated their clutches and presumably returned to a “pre-spawn” state, demonstrating that terminators were not “duds” and were in fact capable of responding to OXT. Perhaps terminators did not respond at 0 dpf because they were not primed to respond at that time. While there are several other potential drivers of whole-clutch termination, such as male condition and clutch size (reviewed in Manica, 2002), these factors did not seem to contribute to clutch termination in our study (see Supplement). Overall, this suggests to us that the decision to terminate may be related to differences in how carers and terminators respond to OXT. Further work is needed to explore this interesting possibility.

In direct contrast to terminator males, which responded to OXT at the later stage of care (4 dpf) but not at the onset of care (0 dpf), commons on the carer trajectory responded to OXT at the onset of care, but not at later stages of care. This is interesting because immediately after fertilization, both terminators and carers were arguably in the same behavioral state; both had just successfully courted a female, spawned, and had young embryos in their nests. Males that ultimately cared for their embryos behaviorally responded to OXT treatment at that point, while males that went on to terminate their embryos did not. This suggests that males must be responsive to OXT to transition into care successfully. These results are reminiscent of work in mice showing that females that are insensitive to oxytocin (Oxtr-knockouts) have higher rates of pup abandonment than controls, but once females initiate care, Oxtr-knockout and control mice do not differ in the levels of care they provided to their pups (Rich et al., 2014).

Generally, it is thought that once parental care has been established, its maintenance is reinforced by interactions with offspring or the environment and is less dependent on hormones (Adkins-Regan, 2005). In fact, threespine stickleback fathers have been shown to modify their parenting behavior in response to external cues. Males increase their fanning over time as embryos develop and the oxygen needs of offspring increase (Van Iersel, 1953). They also adjust their fanning behavior in response to external factors such as temperature (Hopkins et al., 2011), dissolved oxygen (Reebs et al., 1984), and predation risk (Stein and Bell, 2014).

Although the effects of OXT on terminator commons are consistent with a care-promoting role of OXT, carer commons did not show increased parenting behavior when injected with OXT. We expected that treating males with OXT would promote more parental fanning behavior because previous work that has manipulated OXT (via treatment with a peptide antagonist) in fishes found that blocking OXT receptors leads to a reduction in care behaviors (cichlids: O’Connell et al., 2012; clown-fish: DeAngelis et al., 2017, 2020). However, in our study, commons treated with single-dose OXT fanned less than controls on 0 dpf. This could suggest that in stickleback, OXT inhibits parenting behavior; however, given the unexpected results with OXT2 and ANT treatments (further discussed in Section 4.3), there are other explanations.

Changes to the underlying parental substrate could contribute to the loss of parental care in whites

Unlike most stickleback, where fathers provide extensive paternal care, the white ecotype does not provide paternal care. Instead, they disperse the embryos from the nest soon after fertilization (Blouw, 1996). The white and common ecotypes are closely related and recently diverged (Samuk, 2016); therefore, an outstanding question is how this dramatic difference in behavior originated so quickly. If OXT promotes parenting behavior, then we hypothesized that the evolutionary loss of parental care involved changes in OXT signaling. Consistent with this general hypothesis, whites responded differently to OXT manipulation relative to commons. Specifically, males of the white ecotype that were injected with OXT showed a reduction in their latency to disperse and fan their embryos. The fact that OXT decreased the latency of both fanning and dispersal but did not significantly affect the levels of these behaviors, suggests that in whites OXT may not be associated with promoting care, but rather with activating the motivation to interact with embryos. This is consistent with work in mammals showing that oxytocin interfaces with the dopaminergic reward pathway to regulate social motivation (reviewed in Gordon et al., 2011) and can modulate sensory processing to improve neural responses to offspring signals (Marlin et al., 2015). In cichlids, OXT injection has been shown to increase attentiveness to social cues in the context of territorial aggression (Reddon et al., 2012), but see (Lindeyer et al., 2015).

These results suggest that whites have not lost parental care due to an insensitivity to OXT because they do behaviorally respond to OXT manipulation or solely due to low levels of OXT ligand because replacing OXT via injection does not recover parenting behavior. Instead, these results illustrate how whites respond to OXT perturbation differently than commons, which could be caused by an ecotypic difference in the set-up of the neural substrate OXT is acting upon, i.e., the “parental circuit.” For example, the neuroanatomical pattern of OXT receptors in the brain of whites could differ, such that when OXT is administered in whites, it activates a different pathway and behavioral response than in commons.

Hormonal receptors have been implicated in the loss of parental care in at least one other study, in an avian brood parasite, the only other work that we are aware of that has investigated mechanisms of the evolutionary loss of parental care. Lynch et al. (2020) found that the evolutionary loss of parental care in brown-headed cowbirds is associated with changes in the pattern of prolactin receptor distribution in the brain. Compared with parental species, cowbirds have a reduced density of prolactin receptors. When treated with prolactin, cowbirds respond with an increased aversion to nestlings instead of the expected increase in parenting behaviors like nest building and egg incubation (reviewed in Lynch, 2021). This suggests that cowbird behavior is not completely insensitive to prolactin but rather that prolactin has a different effect; it may suppress parenting behavior instead of promoting it (reviewed in Lynch, 2021). This is possibly mediated by changes in the responsiveness of cowbirds’ “parental circuit” to prolactin. Future work is needed to determine if a similar mechanism, i.e., a change in the number and/or distribution of OXT receptors in the brain, also underlies the different behavioral responses of whites and commons to OXT. Although our results do not suggest that lower levels of OXT ligand in the white ecotype can fully explain the loss of parental care, future work should investigate differences in OXT ligand between the ecotypes, for example, by characterizing potential ecotypic differences in the number or activation of OXT neurons or expression levels of OXT in the preoptic area, where these neurons are found in fishes (Cerdá-Reverter and Canosa, 2009).

Finally, the commons that terminated their clutches offer an opportunity to compare the loss of parental care in whites to terminator commons, which also “lost” care in a sense, to gain insights into the causes of the loss of care over different time scales. At 0 dpf, whites responded to OXT by starting to fan and disperse sooner, but the amount of fanning did not respond to treatment. In contrast, unlike whites, terminator commons failed to respond to OXT manipulation at 0 dpf across all behaviors measured. Terminators did not show a change in the latency to fan or the amount of fanning. It is important to note that it is difficult to draw direct comparisons because of the low number of control males in the terminator group (only one male treated with PBS terminated his clutch) and because there is no equivalent to dispersal behavior in commons. Still, consistent with the idea that the “parental substrate” may differ between whites and commons, these results suggest that there is something different about the loss of parental care in whites that is not the same as a common that has failed to fully transition into fatherhood.

Unexpected results and future directions

Pharmacological manipulations are a powerful method for testing causal links between hormones and behavior and for offering new insights into hormonal mechanisms. For example, our results suggest that the relationship between oxytocin and behavior is highly dose-dependent and potentially nonlinear: in cases where OXT treatment had an effect, OXT2 treatment often had no effect. Interestingly, in one case, the OXT-antagonist treatment caused the same direction and magnitude of effect as the OXT treatment. This could indicate a nonlinear dose-response curve, which has been found in other work investigating nonapeptides in teleosts (e.g., Santangelo and Bass, 2006; Reddon et al., 2014), or cross-reactivity with the AVP receptor (Chard, 1973).

Although we chose to focus this study exclusively on OXT because it is known to promote parental care in other fishes (O’Connell et al., 2012; DeAngelis et al., 2017, 2018, 2020) and has been implicated in paternal care in stickleback (Bukhari et al., 2019), we would be remiss if we did not consider the myriad ways other hormones may interact with our focal hormone. OXT is unlikely to be acting in isolation. Indeed, hormones typically act in concert with one another to regulate behaviors. For example, estrogen and progesterone function synergistically in pregnancy (reviewed in Courrier, 1950), and in male voles alloparenting is inhibited by the administration of a combination of oxytocin and vasopressin antagonists, but not one or the other (Bales et al., 2004). In addition, other recent work in voles underscores the system’s resilience; despite its known importance for pair bonding and parental care, knocking out the oxytocin receptor does not alter those behaviors (Berendzen et al., 2023). In light of this work, it is unlikely that a change in a single hormone, like OXT, would be sufficient to cause such a stark behavioral change as the evolutionary loss of care. Future work utilizing single-cell and spatial transcriptomic technologies will allow us to investigate more broadly the various molecules associated with parenting (e.g., other neuropeptides, steroid hormones, and their receptors), their interactions with each other, and their potential roles in the loss of care in this unusual stickleback ecotype.

Conclusions

This work adds to our understanding of nonapeptides’ role in the evolution of parental care, especially the evolutionary loss of care. Our study also highlights how the effects of hormones on behavior can be very state-dependent; we found that the behavioral responses of commons to oxytocin manipulation depended on whether the males were early or late in the parental cycle and whether they had decided to care for or terminate their offspring. We provide evidence for OXT’s role in promoting the onset of care in the common ecotype. There appears to be a window of sensitivity before spawning and immediately after spawning when commons are responsive to OXT manipulation. This window closes later in the parental cycle when OXT manipulation no longer influences the levels of parenting behavior shown by commons. Further work is needed to clarify how OXT influences parenting behavior during this sensitivity window. The intriguing effects of OXT, OXT2, and ANT suggest the mechanism is not a simple dose-dependent response.

We also provide evidence for OXT’s role in the loss of parental care across multiple time scales. In commons, our findings suggest that a lack of responsiveness to OXT at the onset of care may contribute to early clutch termination; in whites, our findings suggest that the evolutionary loss of parental care may have occurred via a change in the underlying parental circuit that OXT is acting on, not simply a change in the levels of OXT ligand. Future work is needed to characterize the density and distribution of both nonapeptide receptors and nonapeptide-producing cells in the brains of whites and commons, as well as examine the potential contribution of other hormones to this recent loss of parental care.
