Pseudomonas aeruginosa affects Acinetobacter baumannii’s growth, gene expression and antibiotic resistance in in vitro co-culture system
Authors: May Thet Paing Phoo, Thitaporn Dechathai, Kamonnut Singkhamanan, Sarunyou Chusri, Rattanaruji Pomwised, Monwadee Wonglapsuwan, Kazuya Morikawa, Komwit Surachat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A. baumannii’s growth and gene expression are affected by P. aeruginosa.•P. aeruginosa induces energy metabolism and iron acquisition genes in A. baumannii.•P. aeruginosa modulates A. baumannii’s antibiotic resistance.•This is the first transcriptomic study of co-cultured P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii.Keywords: Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Co-culture, Siderophores, Transcriptome, Antibiotic resistance, Fosfomycin, Amensalism

Introduction

P. aeruginosa, as well as A. baumannii, is listed as a member of critical antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) bacterial species termed ESKAPE pathogens (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species) (Rice, 2008; Tacconelli, 2017). P. aeruginosa is associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cystic fibrosis, bacteremia, and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). P. aeruginosa produces several virulence factors, such as pyocyanin, 4‑hydroxy-2-heptylquinoline-N-oxide (HQNO), siderophores such as pyoverdine, and pyochelin (Serra et al., 2015). A. baumannii can cause nosocomial infections, including wound infections, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and bacteremia. It can survive in hostile environments such as hospital environments and persist for long periods of time (Chapartegui-González et al., 2018). This ability is a key factor in its widespread dissemination and makes the infection control challenging (Howard et al., 2012).

Polymicrobial infection shows an increasing trend: approximately 10 % in 2017, 14 % during the COVID pandemic, and 13 % in 2024 (UKHSA, 2025). The mortality rates are significantly higher in patients with polymicrobial infections (11 %) compared to those with monomicrobial infections (6 %) (Cillóniz et al., 2025). Moreover, co-infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) and carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CRPA) have synergistic effects on clinical outcomes (Tadese et al., 2022).

There are some studies on co-infections involving P. aeruginosa or A. baumannii, examining how their interactions impact bacterial physiology. For example, A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae interact to promote mutual survival through cross-feeding and cross-protection, leading to increased biofilm formation, antibiotic resistance, and virulence (Semenec et al., 2023). Widely studied P. aeruginosa-S. aureus co-culture increases antibiotic resistance and internalization into host cells (Briaud et al., 2019).

In terms of the co-infection by P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii, it is frequently observed in intensive care unit (ICU) patients (Wang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018). One-fifth of bloodstream infections with A. baumannii are polymicrobial, and P. aeruginosa is the second most common co-pathogen with A. baumannii (Qian et al., 2022). One case study showed that co-infection of multidrug-resistant A. baumannii with P. aeruginosa significantly affected mortality in surgical patients, while other co-infecting species had no apparent effect (Dent et al., 2010).

P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii are both Gram-negative bacteria and can interact through the quorum-sensing signaling molecule, N-acylhomoserine lactones (AHL), to increase the co-culture biofilm mass (Bhargava et al., 2012). The interaction is also exemplified by the reduced imipenem susceptibility of P. aeruginosa (Wang et al., 2021), pyocyanin-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in A. baumannii and increased persistence against antibiotics (Bhargava et al., 2014). However, this interspecies interaction has not been studied by a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis, which is a useful way to understand bacterial interspecies interactions (Tognon et al., 2019). In the present study, we aimed to elucidate the interaction of multidrug-resistant clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii in an in vitro co-culture model.

Methodology

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

In this study, multidrug-resistant clinical isolates Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA01 (distinct from the standard laboratory strain PAO1) and Acinetobacter baumannii SK005 were used. P. aeruginosa PA01 was initially isolated from the rectal swab of a patient who was admitted to Songklanagarind Hospital in Southern Thailand in 2017 (Whole genome sequence accession number NZ_JANTQX000000000) (Chukamnerd et al., 2022). A. baumannii SK005 was isolated from sputum from a patient admitted to Songkhla Hospital in 2019 (NZ_JAIGUK000000000) (Chukamnerd et al., 2023). The glycerol stocks were prepared with Trypticase soy broth (TSB) (Beckton Dickinson, USA) and 20 % glycerol and kept at −80 °C.

Competition growth assay

Glycerol stocks of P. aeruginosa PA01 and A. baumannii SK005 were streaked on TSA, and a single colony was grown in TSB broth to mid-exponential phase. The co-culture was prepared by mixing a 1:1 ratio of monocultures of P. aeruginosa PA01 and A. baumannii SK005, each adjusted to ∼5 × 105 CFU/ml final concentration, and incubated at 37 °C in a static condition. At 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h, 10-fold serial dilutions of the culture were made in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), spread on TSA, and incubated overnight at 37 °C to determine the colony-forming units (CFU). P. aeruginosa PA01 and A. baumannii SK005 colonies were distinguished by their colony morphology, which was confirmed by cytochrome oxidase test (PA01: positive, SK005: negative).

Assessing the interaction between P. aeruginosa PA01 and A. baumannii SK005 in a co-culture (mathematical comparison)

The competitive index (CI) of co-culture was calculated as described by (Macho et al., 2007), in which the ratio of the log10 CFU/mL of co-cultured PA01 to the log10 CFU/mL of co-cultured SK005 at the endpoint time (t = n) divided by the initial ratio of the log10CFU/mL of co-cultured PA01 to the log10 CFU/mL of co-cultured SK005 at the initial timepoint (t = 0).

The relative increase ratio (RIR) for monocultures was similarly calculated, in which the ratio of the log10 CFU/mL of monoculture PA01 to the log10 CFU/mL of monoculture SK005 at the endpoint time (t = n) divided by the initial ratio of the log10CFU/mL monoculture PA01 to the log10 CFU/mL monoculture SK005 at the initial timepoint (t = 0).

If the CI was statistically different from the RIR at the same timepoint, it is considered that there is an interaction between the two species.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The bacterial suspensions of both monocultures and co-culture, adjusted to 0.5 McFarland in TSB, were loaded into a 12-well plate with a glass coverslip placed in the well (2 mL per well). The cultures were incubated for 0, 6, and 12 h. At each timepoint, the cell suspensions were removed, and the samples were washed twice with PBS and distilled water for 15 min. The glass slides, with attached bacteria, were fixed in 2.5 % (w/w) glutaraldehyde for 2 h. The samples were then dehydrated in an ethanol series (50 %, 60 %, 70 %, 80 %, 90 %, and 100 %), with each step repeated twice for 15 min. After dehydration, the samples were critical-point dried (CPD) and coated with gold-palladium. The samples were observed using a Hitachi SU3900 scanning electron microscope SEM3 (Hitachi, Japan).

Antibiotic resistance assay

MICs of antibiotics of P. aeruginosa PA01 and A. baumannii SK005 were determined by broth microdilution using Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) media and Mueller Hinton Broth (MHB) media (Supplementary Tables 1,2). For testing the fosfomycin resistance, the BHI was supplemented with 25 µg/mL glucose-6-phosphate according to CLSI criteria (CLSI, 2024).

Antibiotic resistance assay was carried out as previously described in Briaud et al. (2019) with some modifications (Briaud et al., 2019). SK005 and PA01 were grown for 5 h in BHI broth and were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.002 in fresh BHI broth. For co-culture, they were mixed at a 1:1 ratio. The monoculture and co-culture were treated with MIC concentration of antibiotics (fosfomycin, cefoxitin, imipenem and erythromycin) and incubated for 5 h at 37 °C at 150 rpm. CFUs were counted by using Mueller Hinton (MHA) agar. The percentage of survival was determined by dividing the CFU/ml after antibiotic treatment by the CFU/ml before treatment. For testing the fosfomycin resistance, the BHI was supplemented with 25 µg/mL glucose-6-phosphate.

Transcriptomic analysis

P. aeruginosa PA01 and A. baumannii SK005 were grown in TSB both individually and in co-culture by mixing in a 1:1 ratio at 37 °C for 6 h and 12 h. The OD600 of each culture was adjusted to 1.0, and cells were harvested by centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The bacteria pellets were treated with 50 mg/mL lysozyme (Himedia, India) and 20 mg/mL proteinase K (Invitrogen, USA) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Total RNA was extracted from three independent biological replicates per condition using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Germany). Genomic DNA was removed by digestion with DNase I (Qiagen, Germany). The RNA concentration was measured using a Nanodrop 1000 spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific, USA). The extracted RNA was stored at −80 °C.

RNA sequencing was carried out by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea) using TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Sample Prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 100 pair-ends. All samples passed the sequencing quality control by FastQC. Reads were mapped against its own genome using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2019) using the index file. Gene-level read counts were generated using STRINGTIE 2.2.1 (Shumate et al., 2022).

Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis was conducted using edgeR v4.4.2 package using R v4.4.3 (Chen et al., 2020). DGEList was created by raw gene counts, and lowly expressed genes were filtered according to the criteria (1) a minimum raw count of 10 in at least one sample, (2) a total count of 15 in all samples, and (3) genes must be expressed in at least 70 % of the population. A cutoff of false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an average log2 fold change (log2FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ −1.5 was used to determine significantly differentially expressed genes between co-culture and monocultures. Volcano plots of the DEGs were generated using the ggplot2 package in RStudio (Xia et al., 2018). To verify gene identity and putative protein names, BLASTp searches were performed against the NCBI non-redundant protein database using translated amino acid sequences as queries. For functional annotation, we aligned the gene sequences of upregulated and downregulated genes to KEGG A. baumannii AC2 database using BlastN and assigned abw IDs to submit to STRING v12.0 (Szklarczyk et al., 2025) for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. The Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) against Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) and BLAST search were used to identify AMR genes in SK005 genome (Alcock et al., 2023). Iron acquisition system differential gene expression was visualized using ggenes (https://github.com/wilkox/gggenes.git). AMR differential gene expression was visualized using ComplexHeatmap Package in R (Gu, 2022).

Reverse transcription and quantitative real-time PCR

The cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the QuantiNova Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the qPCRBIO SyGreen® Mix Separate-ROX (PCR Biosystems, London, UK) in a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2(-ΔΔCt) method with normalization to A. baumannii SK005 reference gene rpoB. The target genes and primers were listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Results

A. baumannii SK005 growth was unilaterally reduced by P. aeruginosa PA01

Growth kinetics of P. aeruginosa PA01 and A. baumannii SK005 were tested in monocultures and co-culture to assess interspecies interaction (Fig. 1A). The growth of A. baumannii SK005 in co-culture was comparable with its monoculture at 6 h but significantly decreased from 12 h to 72 h. P. aeruginosa PA01 growth was not affected by A. baumannii SK005. Thus, P. aeruginosa PA01 unilaterally exerts a negative effect on the growth of A. baumannii SK005. Fig. 1B confirms statistical significance between CI and RIR of monocultures and co-culture of P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii, suggesting significant interaction rather than growth rate differences. SEM results showed no remarkable morphological change in both species in co-culture (Fig. 1C).

Data are shown as the mean values obtained from three independent experiments. The error bars represent standard deviations of the means from triplicate data. P. aeruginosa PA01 induces increased expression in energy metabolism and metabolic adaptation of A. baumannii SK005

RNA-seq analysis was performed in biological triplicates for each of three conditions: monoculture of P. aeruginosa PA01, monoculture of A. baumannii SK005, and co-culture of PA01 & SK005 in TSB. The 6 h timepoint was selected because it represents an early phase of change in the growth curve of A. baumannii, and we intended to capture the transcriptional responses preceding this detectable interaction. Table 1 shows differentially expressed genes in co-culture compared with the monoculture (FDR ≤ 0.05 and log2 FC ≥1.5 or ≤ −1.5). P. aeruginosa PA01 had no differentially expressed genes in the presence of A. baumannii (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, 202 genes and 169 genes were up- and down-regulated, respectively, in A. baumannii SK005 (Supplementary Fig. 1). KEGG pathway analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2A) highlighted several pathways such as “Biosynthesis of siderophore group nonribosomal peptides”, “C5-Branched dibasic acid metabolism (Branched chain amino acids: Val, Leu, Ile, Ala, and Pan)”, “One carbon pool by folate”, “Citrate cycle (TCA cycle)”, and others. Overall gene expression demonstrated increased expression in energy metabolism, nucleotide synthesis, amino acid metabolism, and siderophore biosynthesis in A. baumannii in co-culture. Thus, it was suggested that A. baumannii SK005 undergoes metabolic reprogramming in response to P. aeruginosa PA01. Further details (Supplementary Result) and the overall gene expression changes in A. baumannii SK005 are mapped in Fig. 2.

In both KEGG and GO analysis, siderophore biosynthesis, iron ion homeostasis, and iron ion transport were top-enriched pathways in the co-culture condition (Supplementary Figs. 2A,3). Regarding the iron acquisition system, almost all siderophore-related genes in acinetobactin cluster, baumannoferrin cluster, acinetobactin precursor, 2,3-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHBA) were upregulated (Fig. 3). Fimsbactin siderophore cluster that is found in less than 10 % of sequenced A. baumannii strains is not present in our SK005 strain (Bohac et al., 2019). The ferrous iron transport system B gene, feoB, which encodes the main membrane transporter of ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) was also upregulated (log2FC 5.17) and the downstream feoA gene was also significantly differentially expressed (log2FC 1.49). In line with the upregulated iron acquisition system, iron-storage bacterioferritin genes bfr1 and bfr2 were downregulated. Overall, these results suggested that P. aeruginosa PA01 induces iron starvation in A. baumannii SK001 (see Discussion).

To address the change in the transcriptome of A. baumannii, and the largely unchanged status of P. aeruginosa are also observed at a different timing, we conducted additional RNA seq analysis for the timepoint of 12 h. Again, P. aeruginosa showed no DEGs except single gene (rcsC encoding sensor histidine kinase). In A. baumannii, we detected 835 DEGs at 12 h (Table 1). The number of DEGs shared between 6 h and 12 h was 179, corresponding to 48 % of DEGs detected at 6 h (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Tables 5,6). Strikingly, iron-homeostasis genes were included in the DEGs shared by 6 h and 12 h timepoints, suggesting that these responses are stably sustained in the co-culture system (Fig. 3). The expression changes in 5 iron-homeostasis genes (basF, basE, basB, bfr1, bfr2) and one additional gene (cinA) were confirmed by RT-qPCR analysis (Fig. 6).

P. aeruginosa PA01 modulates A. baumannii’s antibiotic resistance

Among the DEGs at 6 h, we identified 6 genes related to antibiotic resistance (Fig. 4). The upregulated genes were amvA encoding multidrug efflux MFS transporter, emrA and emrB, co-transcribed as EmrAB operon encoding MFS efflux pump, adeB gene of adeABC operon encoding a multidrug efflux RND efflux pump and aph(6)-Id aminoglycoside resistant gene. In contrast, we observed the downregulation of fosB, a gene responsible for fosfomycin resistance, which was the only DEG shared between 6 h and 12 h (Fig. 4).

We further investigated the resistance of some antibiotics such as fosfomycin, cefoxitin, imipenem and erythromycin (Fig. 5). Co-culture of A. baumannii with P. aeruginosa led to a significant decrease in survival of SK005 under the MIC concentration of fosfomycin compared to monoculture. Conversely, the survival of SK005 in co-culture under cefoxitin and imipenem was significantly increased. No significant change was observed under erythromycin. We could not test aminoglycoside resistance level since MIC of SK005 was very high (>16,384 µg/ml) (Supplementary Table 1). PA01 survival under these antibiotics did not significantly change between monoculture and co-culture. (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Discussion

Microbial interspecies interactions are generally categorized into cooperation and competition, with many co-culture studies demonstrating those reciprocal interactions (Alford et al., 2022; Semenec et al., 2023). In contrast, the present study revealed that P. aeruginosa has the pleiotropic effects on A. baumannii, but not vice versa, reminiscent of ‘amensalism’, where one bacteria alone suffer from the inhibitory effect while another is unaffected. During our study was in progress, Ababneh et al. reported the reduction of growth of all tested A. baumannii isolates in the presence of P. aeruginosa (Ababneh et al., 2025), which is consistent with our present results. In contrast, they also indicated that A. baumannii decreases the growth of K. pneumoniae and S. aureus. Thus, the ‘amensalism’-like relationship between P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii is a species-specific phenomenon. We firstly revealed that A. baumannii SK005 undergoes drastic transcriptional changes in co-culture, while P. aeruginosa PA01 remained largely unaffected. Such resilience is not specific to the interaction between P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii; similar resilience of P. aeruginosa growth and gene expression was also seen in the co-culture with Gram-positive S. aureus (Filkins et al., 2015; Miller et al., 2017).

In transcriptomic data, we observed upregulation of siderophore-related genes such as acinetobactin and baumannoferrin in A. baumannii in co-culture. In terms of acinetobactin synthesis (see Fig. 2 left side), it is driven by a non-ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) complex, encoded by the bas operon. This complex assembles the mixed catechol-hydroxamate type acinetobactin molecules, which contain the catechol ring (from DHBA via entA and entB) and the hydroxamate tail (from N-hydroxyhistamine via basG and basC). (Hasan et al., 2015). Our results showed that A. baumannii's response to iron starvation involves significant upregulation of not only almost all the bas genes but also entA and entB genes (Fig. 3). Moreover, an increased level of gene expression of baumannoferrin siderophores-related genes and decreased expression of the iron storage system were observed in the co-culture condition. This would be attributed to the distinct abilities of the iron acquisition between the two species: iron affinity of pyoverdine of P. aeruginosa (1032 M-1) (Meyer and Hornsperger, 1978) is higher than A. baumannii’s siderophores, such as acinetobactin (1026 M-1) (Bohac et al., 2019), baumannoferrin (unknown) (Sheldon and Skaar, 2020), and fimsbactin (1027 M–1) (Shapiro and Wencewicz, 2016). It is also known that acinetobactin and other siderophore clusters were upregulated in A. baumannii in iron-limited conditions (Eijkelkamp et al., 2011). The upregulation of ferric iron transport protein with MotA/TolQ/ExbB proton channel family, as well as the downregulation of iron storage proteins, also supports the idea that A. baumannii SK005 is facing iron starvation in the presence of P. aeruginosa.

P. aeruginosa is a highly adaptable bacteria that frequently outcompetes other bacteria in polymicrobial communities (Tashiro et al., 2013). In polymicrobial environments, iron availability is often limited, and it leads to competition in microbial communities. P. aeruginosa uses several strategies to access the iron (Schalk and Perraud, 2023), such as acquisition of iron and heme from various uptake pathways, production of its own siderophores, and the use of catecholamine neuromediators (released during host infection) (Perraud et al., 2022), and plant-derived mono catechols (Luscher et al., 2022) as siderophores. Additionally, P. aeruginosa has a siderophore piracy mechanism that allows it to steal siderophores produced by other species (Perraud et al., 2020). To note, siderophores produced by A. baumannii are mixed catechol-hydroxamate type, which is the same type as pyoverdine (Cox and Adams, 1985; Proschak et al., 2013). Since both bacteria have the same siderophore type, siderophore piracy by P. aeruginosa may be more feasible with A. baumannii.

In the case of co-culturing with S. aureus, P. aeruginosa completely suppressed S. aureus’s siderophore production (Rajapitamahuni et al., 2023) and utilized iron released from lysed S. aureus (Mashburn et al., 2005). Another study showed that siderophore production in P. aeruginosa decreased when exogenous iron was supplied but increased in the presence of S. aureus. It suggested that iron competition still occurs between two species (Harrison et al., 2008). Conversely, in our study, P. aeruginosa could not inhibit the siderophore-related genes in A. baumannii; instead, those genes were upregulated in A. baumannii. Upregulation of siderophore biosynthesis genes in A. baumannii was also seen in co-culture with K. pneumoniae or Enterobacter hormaechei (Chan et al., 2018). Thus, in co-culture conditions, iron competition is a major factor in the interaction between different species, as is often the case in the survival within the infected host environment.

In our RNAseq analysis for the 6 h timepoint, many efflux pumps were upregulated in A. baumannii in co-culture, which may act as a defense mechanism against P. aeruginosa. The upregulated amvA gene encoding multidrug efflux MFS transporter is associated with resistance to antimicrobials and disinfectants (Rajamohan et al., 2010; Short et al., 2021). The adeB gene that encodes multidrug RND (resistance/nodulation/division) family transporter was also upregulated. Among the AdeABC operon, adeB is mainly involved in aminoglycoside resistance, and it mediates several antibiotic sensitivity levels (Xu et al., 2019). Upregulation of emrA and emrB genes that encode EmrAB efflux pump is associated with colistin resistance. These transporter genes were not included as DEGs at 12 h timepoint, suggesting that the regulation of a series of transporter genes is a part of the initial adaptation of A. baumannii to the co-culture settings.

Many studies have shown that co-culture conditions can affect antibiotic resistance. For example, the interaction of P. aeruginosa and S. aureus leads to increased antibiotic resistance of S. aureus (Briaud et al., 2019). Indole produced by Escherichia coli helps Salmonella typhimurium increase antibiotic tolerance (Vega et al., 2013). Interaction among different species in polymicrobial infection can lead to new antibiotic resistance patterns. This study found the higher survival of co-cultured A. baumannii under β-lactam antibiotics, concomitant with the upregulation of peptidoglycan synthesis genes (Supplementary Result) and efflux pump genes (Fig. 4). Conversely, the efficiency of fosfomycin was increased in the co-cultured A. baumannii, which has not previously been reported. Fosfomycin is a bactericidal antibiotic inhibiting the initial step of the cell wall synthesis by targeting MurA, and it is used in combination for the treatment of carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) infections. Our finding has implications for healthcare settings, and further studies of this phenomenon would contribute to better treatment decisions. The mechanism behind the downregulation of fosB gene in SK005 needs to be clarified in future studies. In Gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus and Bacillus species, fosB is under the control of the extracytoplasmic-function sigma factor SigW and is regulated in response to extracellular signals through the function of the cognate anti-sigma factor (Cao et al., 2001). It remains unknown whether A. baumannii has such a regulatory system.

Limitation

This study tested the interaction of multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa PA01 and A. baumannii SK005 strains. While growth inhibition of A. baumannii was demonstrated to be general among many clinical isolates (Ababneh et al., 2025), and the iron-related response also seems to be a general response against P. aeruginosa that produces strong siderophores (see Discussion), further studies including multiple isolates, particularly high-risk international clones of P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii, are required to generalize the present findings of the change in antibiotic susceptibilities and the transcriptome profile.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the transcriptomic response of P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii in co-culture conditions. Our study showed that P. aeruginosa is a dominant player in this co-culture condition, remaining largely unaffected by A. baumannii. A. baumannii SK005 was forced to adapt to the condition generated by P. aeruginosa through the drastic change in expression of genes for metabolic pathways and iron acquisition, concomitant with the altered antibiotic resistance. This study broadens the understanding of microbial ecology by highlighting the fact that mixed pathogens do not always undergo interaction to a mutually equivalent extent and also underscores the necessity to optimize antibiotic therapy specific to polymicrobial infections.
