Workforce shortages and supported access to medical care for hospital employees: a scoping review
Authors: Vera Beulen, Cindy Noben, Martijn Smits, Helen Mertens, Aggie Paulus, Walther Van Mook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis scoping review synthesises peer-reviewed and grey literature on supported access to medical care for hospital employees with existing physical health complaints to understand how it may reduce sickness absenteeism by promoting sustainable employability and to identify gaps and opportunities to inform the design and implementation of organisational supported care frameworks.DesignWe conducted this scoping review following the Arksey and O’Malley framework and Levac et al’s methodological recommendations, reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extensions for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).Data sourcesMEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Elsevier), CINAHL (EBSCO) and Web of Science were searched from database inception to 8 November 2024. The search was updated on 18 May 2026. This was supplemented by forward and backward citation tracking of included peer-reviewed articles and targeted hand searching of Dutch grey literature in Medisch Contact, a leading Dutch professional journal for the medical profession, focusing on clinical practice, medical policy and professional issues.EligibilityEnglish-language or Dutch-language peer-reviewed publications and well-argued opinion pieces on healthcare workers’ access to care (COVID-19 and beyond) in hospital or transferable settings were included. Sources focused on mental health or health promotion, financial aspects, personal protective equipment, patient access or healthcare worker utilisation, as well as non-English/Dutch sources, webpages and papers without full text, were excluded.Data extractionASReview was used for title and abstract screening following the SAFE procedure and full-text screening was conducted with team consensus.Results27 publications were included from 36 685 identified records. The literature was predominantly qualitative in nature and COVID-19-focused, yielding four themes (ethical considerations, multilevel challenges, target groups and strategies and outcomes). It also highlighted limited evidence on evaluated supported-access interventions and workforce outcomes beyond crises.ConclusionsAlthough supported access to medical care for hospital employees appears a promising multilevel, system-embedded strategy to reduce sickness absenteeism and promote sustainable employability in general, the evidence to substantiate and justify such strategies beyond acute crises is limited. Moreover, current literature lacks clear conceptualisation, operationalisation as well as robust implementation and evaluation frameworks. Addressing these gaps is a priority for future research to scientifically scaffold policies and frameworks to sustain a healthy and stable future hospital workforce.Keywords: Health policy, COVID-19, Health Services, Health Services Accessibility, Hospitals, Health Equity

Background

The global healthcare sector faces a significant workforce shortage, with an estimated shortfall of 11 million health workers by 2030 across all income levels.1 The constant and increasing demand for care, combined with an ageing population and a tightening labour market, further exacerbate workforce challenges.2 Hospitals are particularly vulnerable as providing 24/7 patient care requires sustained staffing and resources to safeguard accessibility and quality of services.3 These pressures have profound consequences not only for the delivery of healthcare services but also for the overall well-being of hospital employees.

One important way in which this strain becomes visible is through higher rates of sickness absenteeism and presenteeism.4 Sickness absenteeism refers to employees missing work due to health issues, whereas sickness presenteeism describes employees being present at work but unable to perform effectively due to those same issues.5 Both phenomena reduce staffing capacity and labour productivity,6 creating a vicious cycle in which remaining staff face heavier workloads, further undermining their own mental and physical well-being.

Although workforce challenges are observed internationally, their magnitude and impact vary across healthcare systems due to differing policies, workforce structures and care standards. In the Netherlands, despite international recognition as a high-performing healthcare system,7 workforce shortages are becoming increasingly urgent, with projections rising from nearly 4% in 2030 to over 20% by 2060.8 Signs of these workforce shortages are already visible, as waiting times exceed the maximum acceptable waiting-time limits for non-urgent care set out in agreements between providers and insurers. In 2024, the outpatient waiting times in the Netherlands exceeded these standards in 49.1% of outpatient consultations, 43.6% of medical specialist treatments and 28.9% of diagnostic procedures.9 In addition, sickness absenteeism among hospital employees in the Netherlands remains high. The most recent provisional data indicate that sickness absence in hospitals was 6.2% in the third quarter of 2025, which is in line with the high levels observed throughout 2024.10

Hospital employees play a crucial role in maintaining the accessibility and quality of care, and their health is therefore central to the sustainability of healthcare systems. To date, most initiatives and research have focused on the mental health of hospital employees, particularly in response to the COVID-19 pandemic.11 With respect to physical health, research has predominantly focused on preventive measures such as ergonomic adjustments,12 fitness activities13 and health screenings.14 However, initiatives that support hospital employees in accessing timely medical care for existing physical health complaints remain underexplored, even though supported access to medical care may be crucial for reducing sickness absenteeism and promoting sustainable employability (SE).15

One promising but underexplored approach is to facilitate supported access to medical care for hospital employees. In this article, this type of access is defined as fair, readily available medical care that supports the health of hospital employees, in line with existing ethical and legal frameworks. This can, ultimately, benefit both employees’ well-being and patient outcomes. The COVID-19 pandemic already demonstrated the value of supported access, as Dutch healthcare workers (HCWs) were prioritised for testing and vaccination, measures that reduced sickness absenteeism and kept essential staff on duty.16 Despite these lessons, little is known about the broader application and long-term impact of supportive medical care access outside acute crises.

Therefore, this scoping review provides a comprehensive overview of existing peer-reviewed and grey literature about facilitating supported access to care for hospital employees with existing physical health complaints. This review aims to provide a synthesis of what is currently known in the literature and an integrated understanding of how supported access can reduce sickness absenteeism by promoting SE of the hospital workforce. Additionally, it aims to inform future research on the design and implementation of organisational frameworks of supported access to medical care.

Methods

We performed a scoping review to examine supported access to medical care for hospital employees, identifying and describing recurring themes and temporal trends in the literature, and briefly describing existing interventions where mentioned. The guiding research question was: ‘What is known in the peer-reviewed and grey literature about the concept, definitions, interventions, strategies and policies related to facilitating supported access to medical care for hospital employees, and what lessons can be drawn from this?’ The core concepts guiding this review were derived from this research question and refined through iterative discussions within the research team, informed by preliminary scoping of the literature.

This scoping review followed the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley, considering the recommendations made by Levac, Colquhoun and O’Brien.17 18 We applied the first five stages of the framework; the optional sixth stage, stakeholder consultation, was herein not included. To improve transparency and reproducibility, we conducted and reported this scoping review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extensions for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (online supplemental material 1). This scoping review was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) (10.17605/OSF.IO/CMU4V).

Search strategy

The literature search consisted of a database search and a grey literature search. This combined approach was chosen to ensure a comprehensive overview of supported access initiatives regarding medical care for hospital employees.

Database search

In collaboration with an experienced academic librarian, the lead researcher (VB) developed a search strategy and conducted a comprehensive literature search in Medline (via OVID), Embase (via Elsevier), CINAHL (via EBSCO) and Web of Science, covering all records up to 8 November 2024. The search was updated on 18 May 2026 using the same search strategy. These databases were selected to ensure broad coverage of clinical, nursing, allied health and multidisciplinary research, including social sciences and organisational research related to healthcare workforce and access to care. The search combined three core concepts: (1) healthcare personnel (eg, nurses, clinicians, front-line workers, HCWs), (2) access to care and related factors (eg, health services accessibility, priority access, equity, sick leave, presenteeism, workforce sustainability) and (3) healthcare personnel/sickness absence (eg, nurses, clinicians, HCWs, front-line workers, absenteeism, absence, sick leave). We used Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) and proximity operators (eg, adj2, adj6) to connect terms within and between these concepts, optimising sensitivity and specificity of the search. We excluded publication types such as reports, letters, editorials and comments using filters to maintain feasibility of the screening process. The complete search strings for all databases are provided in online supplemental material 2.

Grey literature search

We reviewed grey literature to capture practical, contextual and policy-relevant information that may not have been available in the traditional academic databases. To identify relevant grey literature, we performed hand searching, specifically targeting opinion and research reports published in Medisch Contact, a reputable Dutch medical professional journal and written by experts in the field. The inclusion of this journal was based on a preidentified article that met our inclusion criteria and was directly relevant to the research topic. Other Dutch nursing and allied health journals were not searched manually due to scope and feasibility constraints, but relevant publications could still be captured through the systematic search strategy applied.

The grey literature was limited to Dutch opinion and research reports and policy documents because employee-support interventions are highly context-specific and Dutch regulatory and organisational structures differ from those in other countries. Non-Dutch grey resources were therefore unlikely to provide transferable or policy-relevant insights.

In contrast, peer-reviewed literature was not geographically restricted in order to capture the broader international evidence base on interventions relevant to HCW access to care. Differences in context were accounted for during synthesis by identifying the country and healthcare system context of each study and interpreting findings within their original setting, with a focus on their potential transferability to the Dutch hospital setting.

Eligibility criteria

The articles were included based on the following criteria: articles (1) addressing access to care during the COVID-19 pandemic and beyond, reflecting the period in which healthcare systems experienced a structural shift characterised by increased workforce pressure and the accelerated development and implementation of employee-support interventions. Although the review primarily focused on literature published during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, relevant pre-pandemic studies meeting the eligibility criteria were also included; (2) consisting of either peer-reviewed scientific publications or well-argued opinion pieces, as we were interested both in well-described, methodologically sound intervention approaches as well as in policy, ethical and legal considerations; eligibility was assessed by the research team through discussion, with inclusion decisions made by consensus; (3) focusing on vaccination as a strategy to give HCW access to care, insofar as these publications offered relevant ethical, organisational or practical implementation insights that could inform the design of supported access interventions in our context and (4) examining hospital contexts or other contexts, where findings could be potentially transferable to hospital-based care; and published in the English or Dutch language.

We excluded articles if their primary focus fell outside the scope of this review, such as those addressing mental health, general well-being or health promotion, or financial aspects of healthcare provision. Although mental health interventions addressing absenteeism or presenteeism may offer relevant insights into access to healthcare for HCWs, these articles were excluded to maintain a focused and feasible scope, as mental health-related interventions were considered to involve different mechanisms and contextual factors compared with access to care for physical health complaints. We also excluded articles that did not align with the target population or the concept of supported access to medical care for hospital employees, such as those examining HCW utilisation rather than facilitation, or those addressing patient access instead of HCWs’ access. Additionally, we excluded articles with irrelevant content, including those focused on personal protective equipment (PPE) or not explicitly concerned with HCWs’ access to care. Articles published in languages other than English or Dutch, articles without available full text, as well as webpages, were also excluded.

The eligibility criteria were further refined through independent screening of 30 articles randomly selected from the initial pool of studies identified through the search strategy. Each team member independently screened these articles based on title and abstract using preliminary inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, VB also independently screened the same set of articles. Any discrepancies in inclusion and exclusion decisions were discussed within the research team until consensus was reached, leading to refinement of the eligibility criteria.

Selection and data extraction

The article selection process involved an open-source artificial intelligence (AI) tool ‘AS Review’ (V.1.6.3) for title and abstract screening,19 following the SAFE procedure.20 The full dataset was imported into ASReview. Title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening, were conducted by VB based on the predefined eligibility criteria. ASReview was used as an AI-assisted prioritisation tool, ranking records according to predicted relevance based on VB’s screening decisions and by continuously re-ordering the remaining unscreened records. All initial inclusions were made by VB, with final inclusion determined through consensus-based discussion within the research team. After that, we performed data extraction to capture study characteristics and key information relevant to the research question.

ASReview

ASReview, an open-source tool originally developed to accelerate title and abstract screening in systematic reviews, was used to support and speed up the title and abstract screening process in our scoping review, which followed a comparable screening procedure. The tool applies an active researcher-in-the-loop machine-learning algorithm, using text mining to rank articles from high to low probability of eligibility for inclusion. We first imported search results from all databases into EndNote (V.21.5) for automated deduplication, followed by a manual verification by VB to ensure accuracy prior to initiating the AI-assisted screening process.

SAFE procedure

We applied the SAFE procedure to guide the screening of titles and abstracts using ASReview. The SAFE procedure provides a systematic approach to determine the stopping point for title and abstract screening in AI-assisted reviews, in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. It is designed to be a conservative approach that is accessible to non-experts and aims to identify a reasonable percentage of relevant records in the dataset rather than striving for complete retrieval.

The SAFE procedure consists of four phases. In the first phase, we screened a random set of training data to provide prior knowledge for the machine-learning model. For this review, we established this prior knowledge by screening 1% of the total dataset (n=36 685), equating to 367 articles, using predefined eligibility criteria agreed on with the research team. The decisions made during this initial screening formed the basis for the model’s training.

The second phase involved an active learning process in which the algorithm continuously ranked the remaining unscreened articles according to their estimated relevance. Based on the prior knowledge generated in the previous phase, ASReview ranked the unscreened articles in order of predicted relevance, continuously presenting the article with the highest estimated probability of inclusion at the top of the list. The reviewer assessed each article’s relevance based on its title and abstract, guided by the predefined eligibility criteria. In cases of uncertainty, we retrieved and read the full text to avoid unnecessary noise in the model. This feedback was incorporated into the algorithm, which then updated the ranking accordingly. To enhance model reliability and minimise the risk of omissions, we screened 40% of all records, substantially exceeding the 10% minimum advised in the SAFE procedure.

Phase 3 of the SAFE procedure, which involves applying alternative machine learning models to identify potentially missed relevant records, was not conducted. This decision was based on the exploratory nature of the review and the considerable resources required compared with the limited expected gain in reliability and validity. In addition, we observed a plateau in the ASReview relevance ranking curve (online supplemental material 3) suggesting that the majority of relevant articles had likely already been identified by the applied model.

We considered phase four, which entails evaluating the quality of the screening process, not applicable, as it falls outside the scope of our scoping review.

Full-text screening and consensus-based inclusion

A preliminary selection based on title and abstract screening using the ASReview tool was made, after which the full texts of the selected articles were retrieved and reviewed. In case of uncertainty regarding inclusion, the articles were discussed within the research team, and decisions were made through consensus.

Following this initial full-text review, we compiled a refined selection of articles and distributed it among the research team for further evaluation. Each team member independently assessed the assigned articles based on full-text screening. Disagreements regarding inclusion were resolved through discussion between the lead researcher and the specific team member who had reviewed the article until consensus was achieved.

Forward and backward citation searching

We subsequently used the articles selected through full-text screening to conduct forward and backward citation searching with the Citation Chaser tool.21 This process was carried out in two rounds to allow for an additional round of citation searching. In addition, we manually screened the reference lists of included articles to identify any further relevant studies and performed targeted manual searches in the Dutch medical journal Medisch Contact. In cases of uncertainty regarding the inclusion of newly identified articles, decisions were made through discussions with the research team.

Subsequently, the identified articles from both the scientific and grey literature were transferred into a data charting form to systematically organise the extracted information. This form, developed by the researcher and adapted from the Cochrane data collection template,22 included general study characteristics and key information relevant to addressing the research question. The research team reviewed and approved the final version of the data charting form.

Data analysis

We performed a thematic analysis following the guidelines outlined in AMEE Guide No. 131 and using the six-step framework of Braun and Clarke.23 The analysis was based on qualitative data extracted from the results and findings sections of the included studies, consisting of narrative descriptions of experiences, interventions, and outcomes reported within these studies. An inductive approach was used to identify patterns and themes directly from the data, without being guided by pre-existing categories or theoretical constructs.

All extracted data were systematically coded using ATLAS.ti software (V.25). The initial coding was conducted by the lead researcher, who independently coded all included studies and developed preliminary codes and emerging themes. These were subsequently discussed within the research team, where discrepancies and uncertainties were resolved through iterative discussion and consensus. This process resulted in the refinement of codes and the development of the final overarching themes.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

We initially identified 36 685 publications through a comprehensive electronic database search, of which 23 met the eligibility criteria. Additionally, we identified two publications via forward and backward citation and two publications from a grey literature search in the medical journal Medical Contact (Dutch: Medisch Contact), resulting in a final selection of 27 publications. Detailed information on the included publications is provided in online supplemental material 4. These publications covered a broad geographical range, with a predominant focus on the United States and the United Kingdom. The studies described in the publications were conducted across diverse settings, primarily within hospital and healthcare system contexts and related to COVID-19. Methodologically, the evidence base was almost entirely qualitative, supplemented by a single randomised controlled trial that quantitatively evaluated the effects of an intervention, which was included because its protocol article was already part of the review and it was expected to yield additional relevant information. More than half of the publications included ethical analyses exploring whether and under what conditions HCWs should be prioritised for access to care, particularly in the context of resource scarcity. Most publications primarily focus on preventive care, followed by treatment, with post-treatment care receiving limited attention.

This scoping review aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of existing peer-reviewed and grey literature about facilitating supported access to medical care for hospital employees to develop an integrated understanding of how supported access can potentially reduce sickness absenteeism by promoting SE of the hospital workforce. Accordingly, we first present the key themes that emerged from the literature search, followed by a description of the observed temporal trends. Finally, we discuss lessons learnt from both the identified themes and interventions.

Ethical considerations

This theme emerged as a frequently addressed topic in the included literature and focused on the ethical considerations regarding access to care for HCWs. These discussions were often centred on whether and to what extent HCWs, particularly front-line workers, should be prioritised for access to care during public health emergencies, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Across the included publications, ethical justification for prioritising HCWs could be divided into internal justification, which emphasises HCWs’ own motivation and sense of duty, and external justification, which refers to societal or functional considerations.

The internal justification concerned moral obligation. Moral obligation was described as the internal motivation to act based on ethical conviction rather than legal requirement, highlighting the personal duty many HCWs feel.24 Whereas external justifications focus on the societal reasons for prioritising HCWs, moral obligation draws attention to HCW’s own sense of duty, and thus complements rather than replaces these external ethical arguments.

Among the external justifications, reciprocity was most prominent. Reciprocity refers to the notion that HCWs, due to their increased risk through direct patient contact, should receive priority access to care as a form of societal return.25 It is often invoked as an ethical justification grounded in fairness and mutual obligation. Social value, defined as the contribution of an individual or group to the overall functioning and well-being of society,26 while reciprocity emphasises mutual exchange between individuals and society, social value focuses more broadly on the societal benefit an individual provides and is often used to argue for prioritisation based on an individual’s role or impact.

Within this broader framework of social value, several instrumental and utilitarian considerations were also described as external justifications. Instrumental value frames HCWs as a means to achieving a broader goal, underscoring their critical functional role in maintaining the delivery and continuity of medical care,26 and was the second most frequently discussed concept. The utilitarian principle includes prioritising those who are in a position to save others, often summarised as ‘the greatest good for the greatest number’.27 Closely related is the multiplier effect, emphasising the broader impact of HCWs through their ability to support patients.28 Finally, the principle of narrow social utility was used to describe the short-term, crisis-specific value an individual brings to the community during emergencies.29 To contextualise these findings, figure 3 summarises the number of included publications in which each of these ethical justifications was identified.

Multilevel challenges in healthcare

This theme encompasses both organisational-level and individual-level challenges, which often act as underlying causes driving the need for interventions regarding SE in healthcare. Organisational challenges refer to systematic and structural factors that influence the working environment, whereas individual challenges concern the personal experiences and responses of HCWs within this context. These two categories are closely interrelated, as organisational factors often contribute to individual challenges, which in turn, can affect organisational functioning.

Organisational-level challenges

A recurring organisational challenge highlighted is the substantial financial burden associated with sickness absenteeism.30 The National Health Service (NHS) reported that sickness absenteeism costs approximately 2.4 billion dollars per year with an annual average of just under 10 absence days per employee. A greater potential cost is that of presenteeism with 56% of NHS staff in 2009 reporting pressure to attend work when feeling unwell.14

Another organisational challenge identified concerns the mental well-being of the healthcare workforce. Several publications highlighted a decline in overall mental well-being largely attributed to organisational factors such as excessive workloads, insufficient staffing, lack of psychological safety and sustained operational pressure.1424 30,32 These organisational stressors not only affect the individual health outcomes but also affect the stability and functioning of the healthcare system as a whole. Therefore, maintaining a healthy workforce is considered a priority for healthcare organisations, as employers and management have a responsibility to protect the health and rights of their workers in order to ensure that the public can benefit from optimal health services.33 34

In addition to health-related challenges, limited awareness of existing health initiatives among staff is an issue that management need to address. According to Lawrence et al, many HCWs reported that they have limited or no knowledge of the health support initiatives available. This finding highlights the importance of effective communication between management and staff to ensure that existing initiatives are not only implemented but also clearly communicated and accessible to all employees.30 35 36

A lack of trust in healthcare management also has been identified as a significant organisational challenge. According to Lawrence et al, HCWs often express cynicism towards their management.30 This mistrust may be reinforced by predominantly developing health interventions from the organisation’s perspective, with limited attention to employees’ views on workplace health culture and the relevance of health interventions for staff. Actively involving staff in the development of workplace health interventions is essential, not only to foster trust in management but also to ensure that interventions align with the actual needs of the workforce.

Individual-level challenges

A consistent individual challenge, across publications over the past two decades, is the high prevalence of burnout, with approximately one-third of doctors worldwide experiencing burnout at any given time, regardless of their specialty.37 Key contributing factors include high workload, persistent demand for care and limited structural support.2426 27 32,34 36 38 Thereby, the medical profession is intellectually demanding and involves complex decision-making, often requiring HCWs to navigate emotionally and psychologically challenging clinical situations.37 These factors, further exacerbated by stressful working conditions and the ongoing risk of becoming personally infected, give a substantial physical and emotional distress, leading to fear, anxiety, depression, posttraumatic stress disorder, ultimately increasing the risk of burnout and absenteeism among HCWs.38 40

Moreover, the structural pressures within healthcare do not only contribute to psychological strain but are also associated with physical health consequences. High workloads and chronic stress can result in physical health complaints such as fatigue and sleep disturbances. Additionally, these work conditions often lead to neglect of self-care among HCWs, including poor nutrition and limited physical activity, further exacerbating their physical vulnerability.30

Target groups

The literature consistently focuses on HCWs as the primary group addressed in ethical discussions and healthcare interventions, defined as individuals directly involved in the delivery of health services.35 Broader terms, such as health workers, were used less frequently and encompass a wider range of professions (eg, health policy professionals, bioethicists). The inclusion of individuals in these target groups often depends on their perceived risk of infection or level of exposure.

In this context, the term front-line workers is commonly applied to those with direct patient contact, as they are generally considered at higher risk of both exposure to infectious agents and work-related physical strain, and are therefore prioritised for such health interventions. Supportive staff, who often work in non-clinical roles, are inconsistently included in the target groups; some publications justify inclusion due to indirect exposure (eg, cleaning patient rooms or performing physically demanding logistical tasks), while others exclude them based on the absence of direct patient contact, reflecting varying definitions of exposure risk. Consequently, the classification of supportive staff within the target group is not uniform and often depends on context-specific evaluations.

Various terms are used to describe healthcare target groups, differing in specificity and scope. The broadest term, health workers, as used by the International Council of Nurses,43 includes roles from clinical staff such as nurses, pharmacists, physicians and dentists to assistive, kitchen and maintenance personnel. Within this broad category, HCWs typically refers to those more directly involved in healthcare delivery, encompassing both paid and unpaid individuals who may be exposed to patients or infectious materials, as defined by Yunihastuti.38 A more specific subset is front-line workers, often understood as those with direct patient contact or who handle infectious materials, which is expanded by Golden et al to include technical and support staff performing essential tasks such as sanitation and material transport.35 Varvayannis et al further emphasise that front-line workers can also include personnel involved in maintaining critical infrastructure necessary for public health functions.24

In summary, the included publications describe multiple target groups with varying terminology and definitions. The inclusion of specific groups, particularly supportive staff, depends largely on differing assessments of exposure risk and contextual factors. Clear and consistent terminology is essential to define the target group for specific interventions and to transparently communicate the criteria guiding these inclusion decisions.

Strategies and outcome measures

Strategies were mainly described in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, focusing on triage framework for vaccination strategies, ventilator allocation and PPE.27 29 35 44 While some of these strategies included practical approaches, their scope in this regard was relatively limited, with a predominant emphasis on offering ethical frameworks as a foundational guide for prioritisation decisions.

Outside the COVID-19 pandemic, the focus shifted predominantly to occupational health measures aimed at maintaining workforce well-being and safety.14 33 43 45 This focus provides practical insights into the design and implementation of interventions, extending beyond traditional occupational health to encompass broader support for employees’ physical health, such as a walk-in clinic for female employees addressing women-specific health concerns.46

In our research context, the reported outcome measures included return-to-work rates, absenteeism rates, staff engagement and staff retention. These outcomes were frequently mentioned; however, the reported data were often inconclusive or limited, with many publications recommending their measurement but not providing actual methods or results.

In summary, these four key themes illustrate the multidimensional nature of the included literature, from evaluations of healthcare interventions to normative ethical debates. The emphasis on the ethical debates highlights that practical decision-making in healthcare is inherently intertwined with ethical considerations regarding access to care.

Temporal trends

Figure 2 presents a timeline of all relevant publications included in this review, covering the years 2005–2025. The figure shows the number of publications published per year, indicated by black dots below each year. Each theme is represented using a distinct colour and symbol, giving a visualisation of the relative prevalence of the themes per year. The timeline is structured into three phases: pre-COVID-19 (2005–2019), during COVID-19 (2020–2021) and post-COVID-19 (2022–2025). In the absence of an official start and end date for the COVID-19 pandemic, this study follows the timeframe commonly referenced in the literature to ensure consistency.40

The pre-COVID-19

During the pre-COVID-19 period (2005–2019), the number of relevant publications was relatively limited, with only four identified papers, compared with the higher number of publications during COVID-19 and post-COVID-19 phases. The publications were distributed evenly across the years, with a notable exception of 2008 and 2009, during which no relevant publications were published. Regarding the identified key themes, strategies and outcome measures were consistently addressed in all identified publications. In contrast, ethical considerations received limited attention, being discussed in only one out of the four publications. The themes of multilevel challenge in healthcare and target groups were equally represented, appearing in two of the four publications. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that no relevant publications were found between 2011 and 2019, indicating a potential research gap during this period.

The COVID-19 period

The COVID-19 period (2020–2021) represents a short but critical timeframe. From 2020 onwards, an increased number of relevant publications is observed, with a peak in 2021. This trend suggests that the topic gained significant relevance during this period. Regarding the thematic focus, the majority of publications in 2020 addressed ethical considerations, a focus that further intensified in 2021. The theme of multilevel challenges in healthcare was present in all of the publications during this period. Additionally, the theme of target group was also frequently represented, appearing in a significant portion of publications. Similarly, strategies and outcome measures were widely discussed across both years.

The post-COVID-19 period

In the post-COVID-19 period (2022–2025), the number of published publications is clearly lower than during the COVID-19 period, despite the post-COVID-19 period being shorter. At the beginning of the post-COVID-19 period, publications predominantly focused on ethical considerations, whereas this focus diminished toward the end of the period. The theme of multilevel challenges in healthcare was prominent in 2022 and 2023 but showed a decline in subsequent years. The theme of target group remained a consistent focus throughout this period. Ethical considerations continued to be a key theme in 2022 and 2023 but were absent in 2024 and 2025.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to synthesise literature on supported access to medical care for hospital employees. Previous research on healthcare workforce sustainability has focused on occupational health, burnout, and preventive strategies, with much less attention to direct access to medical care for existing physical health problems as a strategy to reduce sickness absenteeism, indicating an important but underexplored area for future research. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, publications were scarce, suggesting limited scholarly and policy attention; a temporary surge between 2020 and 2021 reflected the urgent need to safeguard HCWs’ health and ensure continuity of care through prioritised testing, treatment and vaccination. Thereafter, publications declined markedly, with very few studies on non-acute contexts. This scoping review developed an integrated understanding of how such access may promote SE and identified gaps and opportunities to inform future research and the design of organisational frameworks in this area.

Four overarching themes emerged: ethical considerations, multilevel challenges, target groups and strategies and outcome measures. While overlap between physical and mental health-related content was observed across the included literature, an analytical distinction was made between these domains, as they commonly differ in underlying determinants, course and types of interventions. Mental health problems are often multifactorial and strongly influenced by psychosocial and organisational factors, whereas physical health problems are more often understood and classified within a biomedical framework. This distinction clarifies differences in mechanisms, intervention types and related policy and implementation approaches within healthcare systems, without implying a strict separation in practice. Insights from occupational mental health interventions further suggest that mechanisms such as early access, reduced barriers to care, and proactive support may also be relevant for improving outcomes and reducing work absence among HCWs with physical health complaints, providing conceptual parallels for supported access models.

These findings do not yet translate into immediate, evidence-based recommendations for hospital practice, as systematically evaluated interventions are largely absent. Nevertheless, this review underlines the importance of involving hospital employees from the earliest stages of designing and implementing supported access initiatives, so that they are closely aligned with their needs and have a greater chance of being effective. For policymakers, particularly hospital leaders, the findings highlight a gap and an opportunity: to consider whether, and how, supported access to medical care for hospital employees could be incorporated into occupational health policies or collective labour agreements, with future research providing input for measures aimed at SE and workforce well-being.

Although evidence in hospital settings remains limited, adjacent literature from other sectors, such as the business sector, suggests that workplace initiatives aimed at reducing sickness absenteeism and presenteeism, such as occupational health services, preventive health checks, ergonomic interventions and well-being programmes, may support employee health and productivity. However, transferability to healthcare settings is not straightforward due to the complexity of clinical environments, where access to care is mediated and constrained by patient-facing responsibilities and staffing pressures. These interventions may therefore serve as inspiration, but require careful adaptation rather than direct adoption in the development of supported access models in healthcare.

Future research should further specify the concept of ‘supported access’ by, for example, conducting surveys and focus groups with hospital employees to identify barriers to access and perceived needs, and to translate these findings into design requirements for interventions. Comparative qualitative research is needed to examine how ethical considerations described mainly in US and UK literature apply in the Dutch context and how differing ethical frameworks shape organisational and national policies, and whether findings in these contexts are generalisable to Dutch and/or European contexts. Finally, because supported access initiatives are multilevel and system-embedded, involving multiple stakeholders with sometimes conflicting interests, future research should include a qualitative stakeholder analysis to clarify key stakeholders, their roles, influence and main areas of tension and alignment.

A major strength of this review is its broad and systematic scope. By including peer-reviewed and grey literature from national and international sources, it integrates diverse perspectives, highlights key themes and policy interventions, and reveals gaps in the evidence base. Use of the open-source AI tool ASReview improved efficiency and transparency of screening, while adherence to the SAFE procedure ensured methodological consistency. Involving multiple reviewers in defining inclusion and exclusion criteria further reduced the risk of selection bias and strengthened the credibility of the thematic analysis.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The stopping criterion in ASReview may have led to omission of relevant publications, although this risk was mitigated by extensive, iterative screening to improve relevance ranking. The third SAFE step, identifying additional records using a different model, was not applied, based on guidance that most relevant articles are typically identified by this stage and that further iterations rarely yield substantial new results. The fourth step, a formal quality assessment of included articles, was not performed because this falls outside the remit of a scoping review. In addition, only a limited number of Dutch nursing and allied health journals were searched manually, which may have resulted in some relevant publications being missed if they were not indexed in the selected databases. However, given the comprehensive database search and the exploratory nature of this study, these limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, although their overall impact may be limited.

Against the backdrop of increasing pressure on the HCW, particularly in hospital care, and projected structural staff shortages in the coming decades, workforce sustainability has become a critical policy priority, with the ratio of HCWs expected to shift from approximately 1 in 7 to 1 in 3 by 2060, according to the Dutch Scientific Council for Government Policy (Wetenschappelijke Raad voor het Regeringsbeleid).8 Within this context, interventions that support HCWs’ access to appropriate medical care for physical health problems may be considered a potentially underused strategy to reduce sickness absenteeism and presenteeism and to strengthen workforce sustainability. Overall, these findings highlight the relevance of further developing, evaluating and implementing supported access models for physical health complaints in hospital settings.

Conclusions

The increasing workforce shortage in hospital care, exacerbated by high levels of sickness absenteeism and presenteeism, poses a serious and long-term threat to healthcare delivery. Our scoping review shows that supported access to medical care for hospital employees has received little structural attention after the acute crisis of the COVID-19 pandemic subsided, particularly in relation to long-term workforce sustainability. Existing literature, predominantly from the United States and the United Kingdom, offers valuable ethical reflections herein but provides limited concrete strategies and systematically measured outcomes. Additionally, supported access to medical care was mainly described in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic as a strategy to reduce sickness absenteeism and maintain staffing levels and continuity of care beyond acute crises.

In contemporary hospital practice, supported access to medical care for hospital employees can contribute to SE by lowering barriers to accessing care, which could enable employees to obtain medical assessment and treatment at an earlier stage. Earlier detection and management of health problems may then prevent minor or moderate complaints from progressing into more severe conditions that require prolonged sickness absenteeism. Support of the physical well-being and SE of hospital employees can thus potentially contribute to a healthier and more stable workforce, which in turn can safeguard the quality and accessibility of patient care.

To move beyond crisis-driven, ad hoc pandemic initiatives, future research should first further specify the concept of supported access to medical care for hospital employees. This includes a clear focus on existing physical health complaints, rather than the predominant emphasis in the current literature on mental health and general preventive or occupational health measures, and the definition of specific target groups and concrete design criteria. Building on this conceptual groundwork, qualitative studies can then explore how supported access initiatives can be designed, implemented and evaluated in contemporary (non-crisis) hospital care and in non-US/UK contexts, such as Dutch and other European healthcare systems, within their legal, ethical and organisational frameworks. Insights from such studies will provide a more robust empirical and conceptual basis for the design, implementation and rigorous evaluation of supported access initiatives in hospital practice.

Overall, supported access to medical care for hospital employees appears to be a promising multilevel, system-embedded strategy to reduce sickness absenteeism and promote SE. However, it currently lacks clear conceptualisation and operationalisation, as well as implementation and evaluation frameworks. Addressing these gaps should be a priority for future research and policy aimed at sustaining a healthy and stable hospital workforce.
