Effects of dietary probiotics and their combination with copper-loaded montmorillonite on growth performance, intestinal digestive function and nitrogen metabolism in yellow-feathered broilers
Authors: Binke Chen, Hanyue Zhang, Yibin Xu, Jiaqi Chen, Runzi Tang, Jiayue Zhao, Aikun Fu, Xiuan Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This experiment was conducted to investigate the effects of dietary compound probiotics and their combination with copper-loaded montmorillonite (Cu-MMT) on growth performance, intestinal digestive function and nitrogen metabolism in yellow-feathered broilers. A total of 720 1-day-old male broilers were randomly assigned to 3 groups with 6 replicates per group and 40 birds each. The control group (CON group) was fed with a corn-soybean meal basal diet, the probiotic group (P group) was supplemented with 500 mg/kg compound probiotics in the basal diet, and the combined group (PM group) was further supplemented with 1000 mg/kg Cu-MMT in the diet of P group. The experiment lasted for 60 days. The results showed that both treatment groups, particularly PM group, significantly reduced the feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) compared with the CON group in the late growth stage (22 to 60 d) and the whole growth period (P < 0.05). Moreover, PM group showed enhanced the apparent metabolizability of crude ash, both P and PM groups promoted the development of intestinal villi morphology, increased duodenal trypsin and lipase activities, and simultaneously inhibited the activity of serum xanthine oxidase (XOD), thereby reducing the contents of urea nitrogen (UN) and ammonium nitrogen (NH₄⁺-N) in feces. Additionally, PM group significantly decreased fecal pH value (P < 0.05) compared to the CON group and P group. These results indicated that probiotics and their combination with Cu-MMT can reduce F/G, improve intestinal digestive function, and regulate nitrogen metabolism in yellow-feathered broilers.

Introduction

According to the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), global poultry production was reported to reach 138 million metric tons in 2024, which is expected to rise to 210 million metric tons, accounting for more than 40% of total meat production and becoming the largest meat category (Sandström and Kummu, 2023). Expanding intensive poultry farming generates enormous amounts of poultry excreta. Large quantities of nitrogen substrates in such excreta are subsequently mineralized the environment, producing massive scale of ammonia nitrogen that can be further converted into nitrous oxide (N2O) through nitrifier denitrification (Hristov et al., 2013). However, as a greenhouse gas, N2O contributes to global warming 296 times as much as carbon dioxide (Fukumoto et al., 2006), which aggravates the environmental burdens (Adegbeye et al., 2019).

Intestinal microbiota contributes substantially to nitrogen transformation and recycling in poultry. In the hindgut, ammonia can be generated by microbial degradation and deamination of undigested proteins, amino acids, and urinary nitrogenous compound (Dai et al., 2011). In addition, intestinal and excreta-associated microorganisms produce enzymes such as uricase and urease that mediate the sequential degradation of uric acid and urea, derived nitrogen ultimately contributing to ammonia formation (Pewan et al., 2025). Therefore, targeting intestinal nitrogen metabolism enzymes offers a promising approach for optimizing ammonia nitrogen conversion in poultry manure.

Probiotics, as effective bioactive agents, have been widely employed to enhance the absorption and utilization of dietary nutrients (Alqahtani et al., 2024; Kogut and Fernandez-Miyakawa, 2022). These additives optimize nitrogen utilization efficiency by fostering a favorable microbial environment. The proliferation of beneficial bacteria enhances intestinal nutrient uptake, which directly translates to a reduction in nitrogenous waste excreted into the environment (Pewan et al., 2025). For instance, Bacillus velezensis is known for producing antimicrobial metabolites, including lipopeptides and other bioactive compounds to suppress pathogenic bacteria and optimize the intestinal micro-environment (Fan et al., 2018). Bacillus subtilis is a spore-forming probiotic species with strong environmental tolerance and enzyme-producing capacity, and it has been reported to improve intestinal morphology, digestive enzyme activity, nutrient utilization, and gut microbial balance in broilers, which may enhance protein digestion and amino acid absorption and consequently decrease the flow of undigested nitrogenous substrates into the hindgut (Jha et al., 2020). Bacillus licheniformis can produce extracellular enzymes such as proteases and amylases, which may further promote protein degradation and nitrogen utilization efficiency (Deng et al., 2012; Divakaran et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2012).

Montmorillonite (MMT) is a layered aluminosilicate clay mineral which is characterized by a large specific surface area, high ion-exchange ability, and strong adsorption capacity (Szczerba et al., 2020). By loading copper ions onto the MMT surface, the resultant copper-loaded montmorillonite (Cu-MMT) composite combines the adsorption properties of MMT with the antibacterial functions of copper ions (Tong et al., 2005). Specifically, Cu-MMT adheres to bacterial cell walls through electrostatic adsorption and releases bioactive Cu2+ to inhibit the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia coli, thereby improving intestinal health (Hossain et al., 2023). Beyond its antibacterial role, MMT can also utilize its inherent adsorption capacity to sequester NH₄⁺ in aqueous environments, thereby mitigating ammonium nitrogen (NH₄⁺-N) accumulation (Wei et al., 2025). Moreover, the hydroxyl groups on its surface can coordinate with cations to form surface complexes to reduce intestinal NH₄⁺-N levels (Li et al., 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that Cu-MMT enhances nutrient digestibility, nitrogen metabolism, intestinal integrity and regulate nitrogen metabolism in broilers (Shi et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2022; Xia et al., 2004). However, while the individual benefits of probiotics or Cu-MMT have been documented, the comparative efficacy and potential combined effects on nitrogen metabolism and ammonia nitrogen excretion remain unexplored. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate these dietary interventions to identify the optimal strategy for mitigating fecal nitrogen emissions.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with animal welfare guidelines established by China and was approved by the Animal Care and Welfare Committee and the Scientific Ethical Committee of Zhejiang University (Approval No. ZJU20260300).

Experimental materials

Compound probiotics containing Bacillus velezensis (3 × 107 CFU/g), Bacillus subtilis (3 × 107 CFU/g) and Bacillus licheniformis (3 × 107 CFU/g) were produced by Zhejiang Kangwan Dechuan Technology Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China.

Cu-MMT was prepared using calcium-based montmorillonite and copper sulfate from Chifeng, Inner Mongolia, China, via liquid-phase ion exchange method (Hu et al., 2005). The raw ore contained over 95% calcium montmorillonite. After purification, the chemical formula of the montmorillonite was determined as [Na0.158K0.082Ca0.256Mg0.063][Mg0.376Fe2+0.014Fe3+0.13Al1.474] [Si3.87Al0.13]O10(OH)2·nH2O. The resulting Cu-MMT had a copper mass fraction of 1.5%, a cation exchange capacity is 1.45 mol (+)/kg, and a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area of 305.7 m2/g.

Experimental design and diets

In this experiment, a total of 720 one-day-old male yellow-feathered broilers with similar initial body weights were randomly allocated into 3 dietary treatment groups. Each group consisted of 6 replicates, with 40 broilers each. Broilers in the control group (CON group) were fed a basal diet; those in the probiotic group (P group) received the basal diet supplemented with 500 mg/kg of the compound probiotics; and those in the combined group (PM group) received the basal diet supplemented with 500 mg/kg of the compound probiotics and 1000 mg/kg Cu-MMT. All experimental diets were formulated to meet the nutritional requirements for yellow-feathered chickens as specified in the Chinese standard Nutritional Requirements of Yellow Chicken (NY/T 3645-2020). The composition and calculated nutrient levels of the experiment diets are presented in Table 1. The experiment lasted for 60 days, which was divided into an early phase (1–21 d of age) and a late phase (22–60 d of age). Apparent nutrient metabolizability

The apparent metabolizability of nutrients was determined using an indigestible indicator. On day 29 of the experiment, 4 healthy yellow-feathered broilers with similar body weights were selected from each replicate and placed in metabolic cages. After a 3-day adaptation period, fresh feces were collected from each replicate for 4 consecutive days. One portion of the fecal sample was acidified with 10% hydrochloric acid for nitrogen retention, and the other portion was used for analysis of other components. Both portions were stored at −20°C. At the end of the collection period, total feed consumption was recorded, and feed samples were collected for subsequent laboratory analysis. Fecal samples were dried in a forced-air oven at 65°C, ground, and then passed them through a 40-mesh sieve to obtain air-dried samples. The contents of crude protein (CP), crude ash (Ash), calcium and phosphorus in the feed and dried fecal samples were analyzed to calculate the apparent nutrient metabolizability. All chemical analyses were performed according to the methods of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 2012). Apparent nutrient metabolizability was calculated using the following formula.%Metabolismrate=100−100×%acid−insolubleashinfeed%acid−insolubleashinfeces×%nutrientinfeces%nutrientinfeed

Measurement of growth performance and sample collection

On day 1, 21 and 60, broilers were weighed per replicate. Body weight and feed intake for each replicate were recorded at the end of each phase, and average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI) and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) were calculated for the early growth phase (1–21 d), the later growth phase (22–60 d), and the overall period.

On day 60 of the experiment, 4 broilers with similar body weights were selected from each replicate sample for slaughter and subsequent sampling. Blood (10 mL) was collected from each bird into a vacuum tube containing a clot activator and then centrifuged at 3,500 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Serum samples were transferred to 1.5 mL sterilized Eppendorf tubes and stored at −80°C for further analysis. Meanwhile, 0.5 cm segments of duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were collected and placed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The remaining intestinal segments along with their contents were retained and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis (Chen et al., 2026).

Intestinal morphology

Duodenal, jejunal and ileal segments were fixed and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections were then prepared and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) for morphological evaluation of villi. The stained sections were observed using an optical microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and images were captured and documented. Villus morphology was further examined using CaseViewer software (3D HISTECH Kft., Budapest, Hungary). For each section, three fields of view were selected for analysis. Within each field of view, villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured from randomly selected villi, and the ratio of VH/CD was calculated (Ding et al., 2024).

Intestinal digestive enzyme activity

The activities of trypsin, lipase, and amylase in the duodenum were determined using commercial assay kits provided by Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Intestinal permeability

Serum concentrations of D-lactate (D-Lac) and diamine oxidase (DAO) were measured using test kits obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Endotoxin (ET) levels were determined using kits from Xiamen Limulus Reagent Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xiamen, China).

Nitrogen metabolism indexes

Serum xanthine oxidase (XOD) activity, uric acid (UA) and blood ammonia (BA) concentration were measured using the commercial assay kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). In addition, fecal contents of UA, urea (UN), NH₄⁺ -H and urease activity were determined using the corresponding reagent kits.

Fecal pH

Fresh fecal samples were thoroughly mixed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a ratio of 1:8 (w/v) and then centrifuged at 2,500 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected, and the pH value of the supernatant was measured using a PHS-2F pH meter (Leici, Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS statistical software (Version 26.0 for windows; SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Differences among treatment means were compared using the Tukey’s multiple range test. Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), calculated from the means of experimental replicates. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05, which was considered significant, and 0.05 < P < 0.10 was considered a tendency. Values of P < 0.01 were considered highly significant. Graphs were visualized utilizing GraphPad Prism software (v8.0, San Diego, CA, USA).

Discussion

Precise regulation of nitrogen metabolism is considered important for improving nitrogen utilization efficiency in poultry production. Excessive release of nitrogen in the form of NH3 and N2O may contribute to air pollution, soil acidification, and other environmental issues, which could potentially hinder the sustainable development of the poultry industry (Adegbeye et al., 2019). This study aimed to investigate the effects of the combined supplementation with compound probiotics and Cu-MMT on nitrogen metabolism and fecal nitrogen excretion in yellow-feathered broilers. The results showed that supplementation with compound probiotics, either alone or in combination with Cu-MMT, not only significantly reduced the feed-to-gain ratio, but also reduced fecal nitrogen content, particularly ammonia nitrogen levels, suggesting a potential role in improving nitrogen utilization from poultry production.

Previous studies have demonstrated that Bacillus spp. may improve nitrogen utilization efficiency in poultry. Mechanistically, Bacillus spp. could optimize intestinal morphological structure and enhance digestive enzyme activity, which facilitates the digestion and absorption of dietary protein. Li et al. (2023) reported that Bacillus velezensis can improve nutrient utilization in broilers by enhancing intestinal morphology, secreting digestive enzymes, and increasing the absorption of dietary protein and other nutrients. Chen et al. (2024) demonstrated that dietary supplementation with Bacillus subtilis significantly increased digestive enzyme activities in broilers. In addition, Bacillus licheniformis can produce extracellular enzymes such as proteases and amylases (Xu et al., 2018), thereby potentially improving feed utilization efficiency and promoting nitrogen-efficient utilization in broilers. This may reduce the deamination of undigested amino acids in the intestinal lumen and consequently decrease ammonia production (Zhao et al., 2026). Consistent with these findings, our results indicated that compound probiotics were associated with increased villus height in the duodenum, jejunum and ileum and elevated activities of duodenal trypsin and lipase (Wang et al., 2025a; 2025b). Furthermore, both probiotics and the combined application of probiotics with Cu-MMT significantly decreased serum D-Lac concentration and DAO activity, indicating improved intestinal barrier function (Chen et al., 2023). Notably, the combined addition of probiotics and Cu-MMT showed more pronounced effects on intestinal morphology and barrier integrity than probiotics alone, which is in line with previous observations (Guo et al., 2020).

As a functional clay mineral, Cu-MMT may help optimize the intestinal microenvironment, alleviate mucosal irritation, and exert anti-inflammatory actions to maintain intestinal barrier stability (Hu et al., 2025; Nadziakiewicza et al., 2019). These advantages may allow Cu-MMT to provide additional benefits when combined with probiotics, potentially improving intestinal health and crude protein digestion and absorption. This combined effect is also supported by the apparent nutrient metabolizability results observed in the present study.

In poultry, uric acid serves as the primary end product of nitrogen metabolism due to the relative deficiency of arginase activity (Chen et al., 2025). Hepatic and renal XOD play important roles in uric acid biosynthesis in broilers (Yan et al., 2024). In the present study, serum UA and BA concentrations showed no significant differences among treatments, which is consistent with Leal et al. (2023). Although combined supplementation with probiotics and Cu-MMT markedly reduced serum XOD activity, circulating UA and BA remained unchanged. This may be attributed to the fact that serum XOD may not fully reflect the XOD activity in liver and kidney tissues, and its alteration alone may be insufficient to affect the systemic steady-state levels of UA and BA. Similar findings have been reported by Zhang et al. (2021).

The treatment significantly decreased fecal UN and NH₄⁺-N concentrations, suggesting a regulatory effect on intestinal nitrogen metabolism rather than systemic circulating nitrogen metabolism. It was inferred that probiotics improve nitrogen utilization by enriching beneficial intestinal microbiota (Ahmed et al., 2014). Previous studies have shown that Bacillus velezensis, Bacillus licheniformis, and Bacillus subtilis can modulate the intestinal microbiota, promote the growth of beneficial bacteria, and enhance nutrient absorption (Pei et al., 2022; Zhu La et al., 2024), thereby potentially reducing ammonia production in feces (Bastos et al., 2020). Meanwhile, Cu-MMT has been reported to possess adsorption and ion-exchange capacities, enabling it to adsorb intestinal ammonia nitrogen (Morton et al., 2001). In addition, copper ions loaded on MMT may inhibit the proliferation of ammonia-producing harmful bacteria, thereby reducing intestinal ammonia generation. The absence of a significant decrease in fecal NH₄⁺-N content in the PM group compared with the P group may be attributed to the NH₄⁺ adsorption capacity of Cu-MMT. Cu-MMT may retain partial intestinal NH₄⁺ in feces via ion exchange or physical adsorption, which may limit the conversion of bound NH₄⁺ into NH₃. Accordingly, ammonia volatilization may be reduced without significantly decreasing the concentration of NH₄⁺-N in feces (Mazloomi and Jalali, 2017; Redding, 2013).

The combined supplementation of probiotics and Cu-MMT was associated with decreased fecal UN and NH₄⁺-N excretion, further suggesting that their combined treatment may help regulate intestinal nitrogen metabolism and may reduce nitrogen-related excretion in yellow-feathered broilers.

However, this study did not directly measure ammonia or nitrogen oxide gas emissions. Future studies should include direct measurements of nitrogen gas emissions and gut microbiome analysis to further elucidate the specific effects and underlying mechanisms of this combined treatment on environmental nitrogen emissions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study showed that both probiotics alone and their combination with Cu-MMT improved duodenal digestive enzyme activity, promoted nitrogen metabolism, and reduced fecal ammonia nitrogen content, while also improving growth and intestinal health in yellow-feathered broilers. Although the combined treatment did not outperform probiotics alone in terms of growth performance, it significantly reduced fecal NH₄⁺-N levels, indicating potential environmental benefits. These findings suggest that the combination of probiotics and Cu-MMT may represent a potential nutritional strategy for reducing nitrogen excretion in poultry production.
