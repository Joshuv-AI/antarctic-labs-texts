Metabolic and hepatic differences among biochemically euthyroid individuals with distinct thyroid hormone sources: a comparative study
Authors: Enver Ciftel, Filiz Mercantepe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimThis study aimed to evaluate the associations between thyroid hormone profiles and derived ratios (particularly the T3/T4 ratio), body composition parameters, and markers of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in biochemically euthyroid individuals with distinct thyroid physiologies. Specifically, we sought to compare patients without endogenous thyroid hormone production following total thyroidectomy, patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and healthy controls.MethodIn this retrospective, cross-sectional study, three groups with serum TSH levels within the reference range were included: (i) patients with hypothyroidism due to Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT, n = 123), (ii) patients who had undergone total thyroidectomy (TT, n = 103) for benign conditions, and (iii) healthy control subjects (C, n = 83). Thyroid hormone levels and ratios (T3/T4, T3/TSH, T4/TSH), anthropometric measurements, body composition parameters, and non-invasive indices of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis (Fatty Liver Index (FLI) and Fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4)) were assessed in all participants.ResultsThe T3/T4 ratio differed significantly across groups (controls > HT > TT; p < 0.001). Both patient groups exhibited higher FLI (p = 0.019) and FIB-4 (p = 0.002) scores than controls. In correlation analyses, the T3/T4 ratio was positively associated with FIB-4 exclusively in the TT group (r = 0.263, p = 0.007). In hierarchical multiple regression restricted to TT patients, age independently predicted FIB-4 (β = 0.553, p < 0.001), whereas visceral fat rating was not significant (p = 0.476). After adjustment for age and visceral adiposity, the T3/T4 ratio remained an independent predictor of FIB-4 (β = 0.201, p = 0.016). Although TT patients showed less favorable body composition (higher fat mass and visceral adiposity; lower muscle and bone mineral percentages), these parameters did not independently predict FIB-4.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that thyroid hormone balance is not homogeneous even under biochemically euthyroid conditions and that differences in functional thyroid capacity may translate into distinct metabolic and hepatic consequences. Thyroid hormone ratios may therefore provide complementary clinical information, supporting a more tissue-oriented approach to the evaluation of the thyroid–liver axis.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02421-9.

Introduction

Thyroid hormones are fundamental endocrine regulators that are essential for normal growth and development, energy homeostasis, mitochondrial function, and metabolic regulation from embryogenesis onward [1, 2]. However, a substantial proportion of these broad biological effects are too complex to be explained solely by circulating levels of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), thyroxine (T4), and triiodothyronine (T3), and are instead predominantly determined by tissue‐specific thyroid hormone signaling [2, 3]. Serum thyroid hormone concentrations are regulated through the negative feedback mechanisms of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis [4, 5]. As early studies from the late nineteenth century demonstrated, surgical removal of the thyroid gland results in severe physical and cognitive impairment, underscoring that the thyroid produces indispensable substances required for normal human physiology [6].

T4 is the hormone predominantly secreted by the thyroid gland, whereas T3 represents the biologically active form [1, 7]. T4 primarily functions as a prohormone, and its biological activity depends on conversion to T3 in target tissues, deiodinase enzyme activity, and intracellular receptor responsiveness [1, 6]. The majority of circulating T3 is not directly secreted by the thyroid gland but is generated via local conversion of T4 to T3 in peripheral tissues through the action of type 1 and type 2 deiodinases. Tissue-specific expression of deiodinases, thyroid hormone transporters, and thyroid hormone receptor isoforms enables fine regulation of thyroid hormone action at the tissue level. Target organs possess nuclear T3 binding sites, with the highest density observed in the pituitary gland, followed by the liver and heart [1, 6]. Consequently, the biochemical and physiological responses to T3 may vary substantially depending on the target tissue involved [6].

Functional thyroid capacity and relative T3 production are among the key determinants regulating the sensitivity and feedback control of the HPT axis. This concept suggests that glandular T3 secretion plays a critical indirect regulatory role in maintaining thyroid hormone balance beyond its quantitative contribution alone. From a clinical perspective, accumulating evidence indicates that in conditions where thyroid reserve is reduced or entirely absent, TSH alone may insufficiently reflect tissue-level thyroid hormone action and thus have limited diagnostic value [8].

Local T3 production within target tissues may determine thyroid hormone effects independently of circulating T3 concentrations [9]. This observation highlights the critical role of local T4–T3 conversion in shaping tissue-specific thyroid hormone activity [10]. Accordingly, even when serum hormone levels fall within reference ranges, impairments or interindividual differences in tissue‐level thyroid hormone signaling may still occur [9]. Previous studies have shown that under levothyroxine (LT4) therapy, TSH does not always reliably reflect peripheral thyroid hormone action, and that free T3 (FT3) levels may be more closely linked to tissue‐level biological outcomes [11]. In this context, TSH has been described as a “regulatory variable” rather than an outcome variable, suggesting that during LT4 treatment it may not serve as a standalone, reliable physiological indicator of homeostasis [12, 13].

Thyroid dysfunction exerts multifaceted effects on abdominal obesity, hyperglycemia, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels, hypertriglyceridemia, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome components, and overall body composition [4, 7, 14–16]. The liver is a major target organ for T3, with thyroid hormones regulating key metabolic processes such as β‐oxidation, gluconeogenesis, and cholesterol metabolism [17–19]. Hepatic T3 signaling mediated through thyroid hormone receptor β1 (TRβ1) is a critical regulator of lipogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and cholesterol clearance [17, 20, 21]. Consequently, even subtle alterations in thyroid hormone signaling may lead to pronounced effects on hepatic metabolism [18].

Hypothyroidism is among the most common endocrine disorders worldwide, particularly affecting middle-aged women and frequently occurring in the context of autoimmune thyroid disease [22]. Given the central physiological roles of thyroid hormones, LT4 replacement therapy represents the standard treatment for individuals with thyroid hormone deficiency or insufficiency [23]. Nevertheless, several studies have shown that a subset of patients continues to experience persistent symptoms such as fatigue, weight gain, cognitive slowing, and metabolic dysfunction despite having TSH levels within the reference range. Moreover, cardiometabolic risk profiles may fail to fully normalize under complete biochemical euthyroidism [22, 23]. These findings have raised concerns that serum TSH concentrations may not always accurately reflect tissue‐level thyroid hormone effects [24].

The T3/T4 ratio is considered an indirect marker of peripheral deiodinase activity, and interest in its relationship with metabolic conditions has increased in recent years [25]. Several studies have reported associations between altered T3/T4 ratios and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) as well as various metabolic disorders [25–28]. With the updated nomenclature referring to this condition as metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), the interaction between thyroid hormone signaling and hepatic steatosis has emerged as an important focus of contemporary research [16, 17, 20, 29].

In addition, body composition is strongly influenced by both thyroid function and hepatic metabolism [7]. Skeletal muscle and bone tissue are highly sensitive to thyroid hormones, and tissue-level T3 deficiency has been implicated in the development of sarcopenia and reduced bone mass [7]. Accordingly, even subtle differences in thyroid hormone signaling may affect not only metabolic parameters but also overall body composition.

A review of the available literature indicates that studies directly comparing patients who have undergone total thyroidectomy—representing a unique physiological state in which circulating T3 is entirely dependent on hepatic and peripheral deiodinase activity—with patients who retain endogenous thyroid tissue due to Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and healthy euthyroid individuals under identical biochemical euthyroid conditions are scarce [30]. Despite representing fundamentally different thyroid physiologies, these three groups are frequently classified similarly as “euthyroid” in routine clinical practice.

In the present study, three distinct populations with serum TSH levels within the reference range were evaluated: (i) patients who had undergone total thyroidectomy for benign indications and were receiving LT4 replacement therapy, (ii) patients with hypothyroidism due to Hashimoto’s thyroiditis rendered euthyroid by LT4 treatment, and (iii) healthy control subjects. These groups were compared in terms of anthropometric measurements, biochemical parameters, markers of hepatic steatosis, body composition, and metabolic indices. Through this approach, we aimed to elucidate whether different thyroid physiologies are associated with distinct metabolic and hepatic outcomes despite biochemical euthyroidism.

In summary, this study represents the first direct comparison of three populations—total thyroidectomy patients, patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and healthy individuals—who are biochemically euthyroid yet fundamentally differ in terms of thyroid hormone source and tissue-level hormone signaling. By jointly evaluating patients whose circulating T3 is fully dependent on peripheral deiodinase activity and those with preserved endogenous thyroid tissue, this study provides an opportunity to assess tissue‐specific metabolic and hepatic consequences beyond serum TSH normalization. This approach addresses a clinically relevant and insufficiently explored question: whether biochemical euthyroidism guarantees uniform metabolic and hepatic outcomes across individuals with distinct thyroid physiologies.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study was designed as a single-center, cross-sectional, retrospective observational study based on the review of hospital electronic medical records of patients who attended the Endocrinology and Metabolism Outpatient Clinic of Recep Tayyip Erdoğan University Training and Research Hospital between January 2023 and June 2025. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Board of Recep Tayyip Erdoğan University (approval date: 09 October 2025; approval number: 2025/412). Due to the retrospective design of the study, informed consent was waived.

A total of 309 participants who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were allocated into three groups:

Hashimoto thyroiditis group (n = 123): Biochemically euthyroid individuals receiving levothyroxine (LT4) replacement therapy, with preserved thyroid tissue, positivity for anti–thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) and/or anti–thyroglobulin (anti-Tg) antibodies, and thyroid ultrasonography findings compatible with Hashimoto thyroiditis.

Surgical group (n = 103): Biochemically euthyroid individuals who had undergone total thyroidectomy, with benign histopathological results, and who had been receiving a stable dose of LT4 for at least three months.

Healthy control group (n = 83): Individuals with normal hormone levels and no history of thyroid disease, chronic liver disease, metabolic disorders, or autoimmune diseases.

In all participants, biochemical euthyroidism—defined as serum TSH and free T4 levels within the laboratory reference ranges—was required to have been present for at least three months. Among all individuals receiving LT4 therapy, no dose adjustment was allowed during the preceding three months. In all participants receiving LT4 therapy, treatment consisted exclusively of LT4 monotherapy. No patient in either the Hashimoto thyroiditis or the surgical group was receiving combined levothyroxine and liothyronine (T3 + T4) therapy at the time of the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adult individuals aged 18 years and older were eligible for inclusion if they had been in a biochemically euthyroid state for at least three months. In participants receiving levothyroxine (LT4) therapy, a stable LT4 dose without any adjustment during the preceding three months was required. For inclusion in the Hashimoto thyroiditis group, positivity for anti–thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) and/or anti–thyroglobulin (anti-Tg) antibodies was mandatory. Participants assigned to the surgical group were required to have a history of total thyroidectomy with benign histopathological findings; individuals with a pathological diagnosis of thyroid malignancy were explicitly excluded.

Participants were excluded if they had a known chronic liver disease, including hepatitis B or C infection, autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson disease, or hemochromatosis. Additional exclusion criteria comprised excessive alcohol consumption, defined as > 30 g/day in men and > 20 g/day in women; a history of any malignancy; pregnancy or lactation; uncontrolled diabetes mellitus or hypertension; and the use of medications known to influence thyroid function or deiodinase activity, including systemic glucocorticoids, amiodarone, antiepileptic agents, statins, or other drugs affecting thyroid hormone metabolism. Individuals with renal insufficiency, defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m², were also excluded. Furthermore, participants with acute illnesses potentially affecting thyroid function, neuromuscular disorders influencing body composition, a body mass index (BMI) < 18.5 kg/m², or incomplete or inconsistent clinical data were not included in the final analysis.

Biochemical measurements

Venous blood samples were obtained from all participants between 08:00 and 10:00 a.m. after an overnight fasting period of at least 8 h. Hormonal and biochemical parameters were measured in the hospital’s laboratory using standardized methods. In participants receiving LT4 therapy, blood samples were collected prior to the morning dose of levothyroxine, in accordance with the standard pre-analytical protocol of our clinic, in order to avoid post-dose elevations in circulating free T4 levels. Thyroid function tests included serum free triiodothyronine (fT3), free thyroxine (fT4), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). Serum fT3 levels were measured in pg/mL (reference range: 2.13–4.12 pg/mL), fT4 levels in ng/dL (reference range: 0.89–1.76 ng/dL), and TSH levels in µIU/mL (reference range: 0.34–4.78 µIU/mL). Thyroid autoimmunity was assessed by measuring serum anti-thyroglobulin antibodies (anti-Tg) and anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies (anti-TPO). Anti-Tg levels were expressed in IU/mL (reference range: 0–4.5 IU/mL), and anti-TPO levels were expressed in U/mL (reference range: 0–13.8 U/mL). All laboratory analyses were performed using standard automated immunoassay methods in the central laboratory of our institution. Serum glucose, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels were measured using an automated analyzer (Roche Diagnostics).

Assessment of thyroid hormone-related indices

To evaluate thyroid hormone metabolism and pituitary–thyroid axis activity, several hormone-derived indices were calculated. The T3/T4 ratio was used as an indirect marker of peripheral deiodinase activity. In addition, the T3/TSH and T4/TSH ratios were calculated to assess pituitary–thyroid axis sensitivity and relative thyroid hormone activity. This approach is based on methodologies previously described in the literature for the indirect assessment of tissue-level thyroid hormone signaling [8, 11, 30].

Body composition analysis

Body composition was assessed using a Tanita SC-330 Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis (BIA) device. Prior to the measurements, standardized pre-assessment conditions were ensured for all participants, including fasting status, abstinence from alcohol consumption, heavy meals, and intense physical activity for at least 12 h before the assessment. Participants were measured without shoes or metallic accessories and while wearing light clothing.

The BIA device provided measurements of body fat percentage (FM%), skeletal muscle percentage (MM%), bone mineral percentage (BM%), visceral fat area (VFA), visceral fat rating (VFR), and basal metabolic rate (BMR).

Non-invasive hepatic and metabolic indices

All non-invasive hepatic and metabolic indices used in the study were calculated according to previously validated formulas.

The Fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) was calculated using the following formula: FIB-4 = (Age [years] × AST [U/L]) / (Platelet count [10⁹/L] × √ALT [U/L]) as originally described by Sterling et al. [31].

The Fatty Liver Index (FLI) was calculated according to the equation proposed by Bedogni et al. [32]: FLI = e^(0.953 × ln(TG) + 0.139 × BMI + 0.718 × ln(GGT) + 0.053 × WC − 15.745) / (1 + e^(…)) × 100.

The Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI) was calculated as described by Lee et al. [33] using the following formula: HSI = 8 × (ALT/AST) + BMI + (2 if female) + (2 if diabetes).

The Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR) was calculated according to the method proposed by Bello et al. [34]: METS-IR = ln [2 × fasting glucose (mg/dL) + triglycerides (mg/dL)] × BMI / ln [HDL cholesterol (mg/dL)]

Insulin resistance was also assessed using the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) [35], calculated as follows: HOMA-IR = (fasting glucose × fasting insulin) / 405.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed for normality by examining skewness and kurtosis values, with values between − 2 and + 2 considered indicative of a normal distribution [36]. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using the chi-square test. For comparisons of continuous variables among more than two independent groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied when the assumption of normality was satisfied. Following ANOVA, post hoc analyses were conducted using the Bonferroni test when variances were homogeneous, and the Tamhane’s T2 test when the assumption of homogeneity of variances was not met. Relationships between quantitative variables were evaluated using correlation analysis and hierarchical multiple linear regression analysis. Pearson’s correlation test was used to assess associations between two continuous variables when the normality assumption was fulfilled. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 309 participants were included in the study, comprising 123 patients with Hashimoto thyroiditis, 103 patients who had undergone total thyroidectomy with benign histopathological findings, and 83 healthy control subjects. The three groups were comparable in terms of sex distribution (p > 0.05). Mean age differed significantly among groups (F = 18.618, p < 0.001), with both patient groups being significantly older than the control group, while no difference in age was observed between the Hashimoto and surgical groups (p > 0.05).

Significant differences among groups were observed for serum T3 (p < 0.001), T4 (p < 0.001), TSH (p = 0.006), fat mass percentage (FM%, p = 0.017), muscle mass percentage (MM%, p = 0.012), bone mineral percentage (BM%, p = 0.015), visceral fat rating (VFR, p = 0.011), FIB-4 score (p = 0.002), fatty liver index (FLI, p = 0.019), visceral fat area (VFA, p < 0.001), T3/T4 ratio (p < 0.001), T3/TSH ratio (p = 0.002), and T4/TSH ratio (p < 0.001).

Serum TSH levels differed between the patient groups, being significantly higher in the Hashimoto thyroiditis group. Serum T3 levels were similar between the two patient groups but were significantly lower compared with healthy controls. Serum T4 levels differed among all three groups, with the highest levels observed in the surgical group and the lowest levels in the control group.

Body composition parameters revealed that FM%, MM%, and BM% were similar between the Hashimoto and surgical groups but differed significantly between the surgical group and healthy controls. MM% and BM% were significantly higher in the control group compared with the surgical group, whereas FM% was significantly lower in controls.

FIB-4 scores were comparable between the two patient groups but were significantly higher than those observed in healthy controls. The T3/T4 ratio differed significantly across all three groups, with the highest values observed in healthy controls and the lowest values in the surgical group. The T3/TSH ratio differed significantly between the patient groups and was higher in the surgical group. Similarly, the T4/TSH ratio was significantly higher in the surgical group compared with the other groups.

No significant differences were observed among the three groups with respect to basal metabolic rate (BMR), hepatic steatosis index (HSI), HOMA-IR, TyG index, or METS-IR. Correlation and regression analyses

When the relationships between thyroid hormone–derived indices and clinical and laboratory parameters were evaluated separately in the Hashimoto and surgical groups, a moderate and statistically significant positive correlation was observed only between the T3/T4 ratio and FIB-4 score in the surgical group (r = 0.263, p = 0.007). No such association was detected in the Hashimoto group. Discussion

This study comparatively evaluated thyroid hormone profiles (T3, T4, and derived ratios), body composition parameters, and markers of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in patients with Hashimoto thyroiditis and in patients receiving levothyroxine (LT4) replacement therapy after total thyroidectomy, in comparison with healthy controls. The main findings demonstrate that even among individuals classified as biochemically euthyroid, thyroid hormone balance is not homogeneous and that this heterogeneity is closely related to the functional capacity of the thyroid gland. The distribution of the T3/T4 ratio across the three groups (healthy controls > Hashimoto thyroiditis > surgically thyroidectomized patients) suggests that the presence of endogenous thyroid tissue and preserved hormone production capacity plays a decisive role in maintaining systemic thyroid hormone balance.

The metabolic effects of thyroid hormones cannot be restricted to serum TSH levels alone. Deiodinase activity, cellular transporters, and the tissue-specific distribution of thyroid hormone receptor isoforms are fundamental determinants of tissue euthyroidism [37]. The literature clearly demonstrates the broad effects of thyroid hormones—particularly T3—on energy homeostasis, lipogenesis, lipolysis, hepatic glucose metabolism, and mitochondrial function [38]. The beta isoform of the thyroid hormone receptor (TRβ) is the predominant receptor in the liver, while deiodinase type 1 and type 2 play critical, tissue-specific, and adaptive roles in the conversion of T4 to T3 under metabolic stress conditions [10, 37]. Within this physiological framework, relative tissue-level T3 deficiency (tissue hypothyroidism) can occur despite normal serum TSH levels under LT4 replacement therapy. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced in patients who have undergone total thyroidectomy, in whom all circulating T3 is produced exclusively via peripheral deiodinase activity, with complete loss of physiological glandular T3 secretion [9]. Therefore, the T3/T4 ratio is considered a unique bioindicator reflecting tissue-level thyroid hormone balance independently of absolute hormone concentrations. Recent studies have indeed shown that the fT3/fT4 ratio may be associated with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, inflammation, sarcopenia, and mortality [15, 38]. In a study of obese children, Bilgin et al. reported significantly higher fT3/fT4 ratios in the NAFLD group and suggested that this finding might reflect increased deiodinase activity as a compensatory mechanism against hepatic fat accumulation [25]. Gokmen et al. identified the fT3/fT4 ratio as an independent predictor of NAFLD regardless of thyroid functional status, while hypothyroidism itself was not directly associated with NAFLD in their cohort [26]. In contrast, Turker et al. reported that although low T3/T4 ratios were associated with NAFLD, patients who developed fibrosis exhibited higher T3/T4 ratios compared with healthy individuals [39].

In the present study, T3 levels were lower in both the Hashimoto and surgical groups compared with controls, whereas T4 levels were higher, particularly in the surgical group. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that LT4 monotherapy increases total T4 levels while failing to fully restore physiologic T3 concentrations [9, 11, 40]. It should be noted that the T3/TSH and T4/TSH ratios are mathematically sensitive to variations in the TSH denominator, and the lower mean TSH observed in the surgical group may partly contribute to the elevation of these ratios. However, it is important to emphasize that statistically significant between-group differences were also observed in absolute T3 and T4 concentrations independently of TSH, suggesting that the elevated T3/TSH and T4/TSH ratios in the surgical group are not solely attributable to differences in pituitary feedback set-point or LT4 dosing practices, but may genuinely reflect altered peripheral thyroid hormone availability and conversion dynamics in this group.

The distinctive feature of our study, however, lies in the observation that although the T3/T4 ratio was lowest in the surgical group, increases in this ratio were independently associated with higher FIB-4 scores. While seemingly paradoxical at first glance, this finding is biologically plausible from a hepatic physiology perspective. Progressive liver fibrosis has been shown to induce compensatory activation of hepatic deiodinases, leading to increased local T4-to-T3 conversion [39]. Under conditions of elevated energy demand, oxidative stress, and inflammation, the liver may enhance local T3 production as an adaptive response, resulting in a relative increase in the T3/T4 ratio despite low absolute T3 levels. In addition, liver fibrosis may reduce hepatic T4 clearance, thereby increasing circulating T4 levels and further contributing to variability in measured hormone ratios. These complex interactions become particularly evident in thyroidectomized patients, in whom the metabolic burden of thyroid hormone conversion is entirely dependent on peripheral deiodinases. In contrast, in the Hashimoto group, partial preservation of endogenous thyroid tissue—even in the presence of autoimmune damage—likely allows continued T3 production, biologically explaining the absence of a similar association.

Beyond these mechanisms, the fibroblast growth factor-21 (FGF-21) pathway provides a plausible intermediary link. FGF-21 is a key regulator of multiple metabolic pathways, controlling hepatic fatty acid oxidation, enhancing glucose uptake in adipose tissue, and contributing to brown adipose tissue–mediated thermogenesis. Its expression is known to be regulated by thyroid hormones, particularly T3 [41, 42]. In a damaged liver, increased deiodinase activity may represent part of a compensatory response aimed at elevating FGF-21 levels.

Thyroid hormones are among the principal regulators of hepatic lipid metabolism [14]. Hypothyroidism promotes lipogenesis, reduces cholesterol clearance, and exacerbates insulin resistance [4, 43]. Accordingly, the role of thyroid dysfunction in the pathogenesis of MASLD has been well established [17, 27]. In our study, both Hashimoto and surgical groups exhibited higher FLI and FIB-4 scores compared with healthy controls, indicating that liver health may not fully normalize even after achieving biochemical euthyroidism. The independent association between the T3/T4 ratio and FIB-4 in the surgical group further suggests a specific role for thyroid hormone sensitivity in the development of hepatic fibrosis.

Recent experimental evidence challenges the traditional view of thyroid hormone signaling as uniformly hepatoprotective. Mendoza et al. demonstrated that thyroid hormone signaling in hepatocytes simultaneously activates lipogenesis and fatty acid oxidation, with carbohydrate-responsive element–binding protein (ChREBP) playing a central role in this process [19]. Thyroid hormone–induced lipogenesis associated with ChREBP activation highlights the potentially dual and context-dependent hepatic effects of T3. Within this framework, the association between higher T3/T4 ratios and increased FIB-4 scores observed in our study may reflect disproportionate intrahepatic T3 signaling. Enhanced T3 availability could promote ChREBP-mediated de novo lipogenesis, hepatocellular lipid accumulation, and downstream fibrogenic pathways [18, 19].

Previous studies have shown that serum T3/T4 ratios are highest in healthy euthyroid individuals and progressively decrease in patients receiving LT4 monotherapy in the absence of endogenous thyroid hormone production [24]. The T3/T4 distribution observed in our study is consistent with these findings. Importantly, the fact that the association between the T3/T4 ratio and FIB-4 was observed exclusively in the surgical group suggests that its clinical relevance emerges primarily under conditions of complete dependence on peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion. In contrast, preserved endogenous T3 production in individuals with intact thyroid tissue may buffer fluctuations in circulating hormone ratios, thereby attenuating this relationship.

These findings support the concept that serum euthyroidism does not necessarily equate to hepatic metabolic euthyroidism. Following thyroidectomy and LT4 replacement, thyroid hormone regulation appears to shift toward an individual set-point model, with TSH–FT4–FT3 relationships losing their fixed, log-linear characteristics [12, 44]. After loss of endogenous hormone production, TSH may no longer reliably reflect tissue-level thyroid hormone effects [8, 13, 30]. The metabolic and hepatic differences observed particularly in the surgical group in our study suggest that TSH may lose its reliability as a “tissue signal” following loss of thyroid capacity.

Furthermore, the surgical group exhibited lower muscle mass and bone mineral percentage, along with higher fat mass and visceral adiposity, indicating that post-thyroidectomy metabolic alterations cannot be explained by hormone levels alone. Skeletal muscle and bone tissue are highly sensitive to thyroid hormone signaling, and chronic tissue-level T3 deficiency may lead to sarcopenic tendencies and reduced bone mass [45]. Nevertheless, the lack of independent associations between body composition parameters and FIB-4 in regression analyses suggests that hepatic fibrosis cannot be attributed solely to visceral adiposity, reinforcing the role of altered thyroid hormone metabolism.

Finally, the hypothesis that physiological T3 signaling may, under certain conditions—particularly in the absence of endogenous production—be associated with increased fibrotic burden aligns with the clinical effects of liver-selective TRβ agonists such as resmetirom [29]. TRβ-selective agonists have been shown to preserve metabolic benefits while bypassing the lipo­genic and fibrogenic components of thyroid hormone signaling. Our findings underscore the importance of evaluating thyroid hormone balance qualitatively rather than quantitatively and of considering tissue-specific effects.

Collectively, these results indicate that the classical definition of “biochemical euthyroidism” may be clinically insufficient, particularly in patients receiving LT4 monotherapy after total thyroidectomy. The independent association between the T3/T4 ratio and FIB-4 in this group suggests that thyroid hormone balance should be assessed not only through TSH and free hormone levels but also through hormone ratios and tissue-level T3 availability. Accordingly, endocrinologists and hepatologists may need to move beyond considering TSH as a sole “safe” biochemical target and consider earlier and more systematic MASLD/fibrosis screening in surgically thyroidectomized patients treated with long-term LT4.

The observation that both Hashimoto and surgical groups exhibited higher FLI and FIB-4 scores indicates that liver health in patients with thyroid disease may require closer monitoring even after laboratory euthyroidism is achieved. It should be emphasized, however, that the mean FIB-4 values in all three groups (ranging from 0.58 to 0.74) remain well below the conventionally accepted threshold of 1.30 associated with advanced fibrosis risk. The between-group differences, while statistically significant, therefore occur within a low absolute fibrosis-risk range and should not be interpreted as indicating clinically significant hepatic fibrosis in these populations. Rather, these findings may reflect early or subclinical alterations in hepatic homeostasis that warrant longitudinal follow-up. In particular, the coexistence of unfavorable body composition (higher fat mass and visceral adiposity, lower muscle and bone mass) and disrupted thyroid hormone balance in the surgical group highlights the need for a holistic assessment of cardiometabolic and hepatic risk. In clinical practice, approaches incorporating the T3/T4 ratio alongside simple noninvasive scores may facilitate earlier identification of thyroid–liver axis dysfunction.

Our findings suggest that hepatic outcomes related to thyroid hormone balance—particularly the T3/T4 ratio—should be explored further in prospective and mechanistic studies. Future research in larger, multicenter cohorts with longitudinal designs may clarify causality between thyroid hormone profiles and the development of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis. Beyond indirect scores such as FIB-4 and FLI, tissue-level validation using liver ultrasound elastography, magnetic resonance elastography, or selected liver biopsies will be critical.

Mechanistically, translational studies examining the interplay between the T3/T4 ratio, deiodinase activity, intrahepatic T3 signaling, ChREBP, and the FGF-21 axis would strengthen the biological plausibility of the observed associations. From a therapeutic perspective, clinical trials evaluating combined T4/T3 treatment protocols or liver-selective TRβ agonists as interventions targeting hepatic steatosis and fibrosis may inform more rational strategies for managing the thyroid–liver axis. In the future, artificial intelligence– and machine learning–based models integrating thyroid hormone profiles, body composition parameters, and hepatic indices may enable individualized fibrosis risk prediction.

One of the main strengths of this study is the simultaneous comparison of three distinct groups—healthy controls, patients with Hashimoto thyroiditis, and individuals receiving LT4 replacement after total thyroidectomy—under biochemically euthyroid conditions. The integrated evaluation of thyroid hormone levels and ratios, body composition parameters (FM%, MM%, BM%, VFR), and noninvasive hepatic steatosis and fibrosis scores (FLI, FIB-4) allowed a multidimensional assessment of the thyroid–liver relationship. Notably, the independent association between the T3/T4 ratio and FIB-4 observed exclusively in the surgical group highlights a subgroup-specific relationship linked to loss of thyroid capacity.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Due to its cross-sectional design, causal inferences regarding the relationship between thyroid hormone ratios and hepatic steatosis or fibrosis cannot be established. The sample size, particularly for subgroup analyses, was limited and warrants validation in larger cohorts. Hepatic steatosis and fibrosis were assessed using derived indices such as FLI and FIB-4, without imaging-based or histopathological confirmation. Potential confounders, including dietary habits, physical activity, and certain metabolic comorbidities, could not be fully controlled. Furthermore, a formal multivariable regression analysis was not performed for the FLI, and the between-group differences in this index should therefore be interpreted with caution in the context of potential residual confounding. The single-center design and relatively homogenous population may limit generalizability. Finally, although the T3/T4–FIB-4 association was demonstrated only in the surgical group, the possibility that weaker associations in other groups were missed due to limited statistical power cannot be excluded. Additionally, although patients in the surgical group underwent total thyroidectomy for benign indications, the specific underlying diagnoses — which may have included toxic multinodular goiter or Graves’ disease — were not systematically recorded and analyzed as separate subgroups. Given that these conditions may be associated with distinct metabolic histories, this represents an additional limitation that should be considered when interpreting the findings of the surgical group.

Conclusion

This study suggests that thyroid hormone balance may not be homogeneous even among individuals who meet biochemical criteria for euthyroidism, and that this balance may vary according to thyroid functional capacity and the T3/T4 ratio. The observation that T3/T4 ratios were highest in healthy controls, intermediate in patients with Hashimoto thyroiditis, and lowest in patients receiving LT4 monotherapy after total thyroidectomy is consistent with the notion that preservation of endogenous thyroid hormone production may support systemic hormone balance and tissue euthyroidism. The independent association between the T3/T4 ratio and FIB-4 in the surgical group raises the possibility that subtle disturbances in thyroid hormone signaling may be associated with hepatic fibrosis markers, particularly in individuals who are fully dependent on peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion. However, given the cross-sectional and associative nature of this study, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and prospective studies in larger cohorts are needed to confirm these relationships.

In summary, serum euthyroidism may not necessarily equate to hepatic or metabolic euthyroidism. These findings raise the possibility that loss of thyroid capacity may be associated with altered TSH reliability as a tissue-level signal, though this interpretation requires longitudinal validation. Our results suggest that a more holistic and individualized approach integrating the thyroid–liver axis, hormone ratios, and non-invasive liver scores may warrant further investigation, particularly in thyroidectomized patients treated with LT4 monotherapy.
