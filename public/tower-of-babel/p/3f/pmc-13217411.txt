Occult primary prostate lymphoma in a young male: A case report
Authors: Rod Maverick Jacinto, Michelangelo Cobangbang, Maria Anna Vanessa Geron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary diffuse large B-cell prostate lymphoma (DLBCL) is an extremely rare malignancy, particularly in the young, with only a limited number of cases recorded in the literature. This rarity often poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, as its clinical features can mimic those of more common prostatic conditions. Misdiagnosis can result in inadequate management especially when initial evaluations do not show suspicious findings. Given the rarity of prostate DLBCL, there is currently no consensus on the optimal treatment regimen. This case underscores the need for awareness among clinicians to provide prompt treatment offering a favorable outcome with timely intervention.Keywords: Prostate lymphoma, Lymphoma, Prostate malignancy, Diffuse large B cell lymphoma

Introduction

Primary lymphoma of the prostate constitutes an exceptionally rare and diagnostically complex malignancy, frequently escaping early clinical recognition due to vague symptoms and the infrequent occurrence of lymphomatous invasion in the genitourinary tract.1 Lymphoma primarily arising from the prostate gland is exceedingly rare accounting for only 0.1% of all lymphoma cases and 0.09% of all prostatic malignancies, with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma being the most common histological subtype.2 This kind of extranodal lymphoma is uncommon in young adults and is almost always secondary.2 This rarity is complicated by its tendency to resemble more prevalent benign prostatic conditions, such as benign prostatic hyperplasia or prostatitis, resulting in delayed diagnosis and possibly inadequate treatment especially when not readily apparent.1,3 Because of its uncommon appearance in clinical settings and owing to its nonspecific presentation, this condition is typically not part of the usual differential diagnosis. The infrequency of this condition means that arriving at a definitive diagnosis typically requires a high index of suspicion, thorough histopathological assessment, and advanced immunohistochemical analysis. The aggressive behavior and poor short-term outcomes associated with prostatic lymphoma necessitate early and accurate diagnosis to facilitate timely treatment and improve patient prognosis.4

We report a rare clinical case of primary lymphoma of the prostate in which the diagnostic workup was notably obscure. This case report aims to elucidate the diagnostic challenges, management considerations, and unique presentation of this rare entity in a young male, contributing to the limited body of literature on primary prostatic lymphoma.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old Filipino male presented with gross, painless initial hematuria with no associated clots spontaneously resolving and recurring for 1 month, which was accompanied by urinary frequency and intermittency. There was no associated fever, chills, vomiting, flank pain, pelvic pain, or other lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). The patient had a medical history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and allergic rhinitis, with a prior surgical history of anterior cruciate ligament repair, and was a former smoker with a two-pack-year smoking history. He has significant work-related second- and third-hand smoking exposure. Physical examination was unremarkable, and digital rectal examination (DRE) revealed a smooth, nonnodular, nontender prostate measuring approximately 20 g. Complete blood count was within normal limits, with a hemoglobin level of 15.9 g/dL, hematocrit of 47.1%, white blood cell count of 6250/mm3 with 52% neutrophils, and a platelet count of 358,000/mm3. Urinalysis was negative for nitrites and demonstrated 1 red blood cell per high-power field (hpf), 9 white blood cells per hpf, and 9 bacteria per hpf. Serum creatinine, sodium, and potassium levels were within normal limits. Urine cytology revealed the presence of atypical urothelial cells, classified as Paris System category 3. Prostate specific antigen (PSA) was at 0.7 ng/mL. Due to the recurring gross hematuria, a CT urography was done and did not reveal suspicious findings to suggest a pathology along the genitourinary tract. The kidneys and their parenchymal thickness were normal in size without lithiasis and hydronephrosis. The urinary bladder had no suspicious wall thickening or mass, and the prostate gland was normal, measuring 27 g (Fig. 1). All other organs were grossly normal, and no lymphadenopathy was seen.

Delayed-phase CT urography demonstrating a prostate of normal size and contour (red arrow), with preserved morphology and no focal irregularity, nodularity, or mass lesion identified.

Diagnostic cystoscopy was subsequently performed and revealed a friable and hyperemic mucosa of the prostatic urethra with areas of sloughed tissue and mild pinpoint bleeding (Fig. 2). The bladder neck appeared hyperemic with edematous mucosa and similar pinpoint bleeding. The bladder walls were smooth, with no evidence of intravesical masses or lithiasis, and the bilateral ureteral orifices were grossly normal.

Cystoscopic view demonstrating an area of suspicious mucosal abnormality at the bladder neck characterized by irregular appearance and altered surface texture, which was biopsied.

Subsequently, transurethral resection of the bladder neck mucosa was performed using a bipolar resectoscope, along with fulguration of pinpoint bleeders. Initial histopathologic analysis of the specimen revealed atypical lymphoid proliferation. The immunohistochemistry panel showed positive for CD20 (Fig. 3a), Bcl6 (40-50%) (Fig. 3b), MUM1 (70-80%) (Fig. 3c), c-myc (80-90%) (Fig. 3d), and Bcl2 (50-60%) (Fig. 3e). Staining for Ki-67 showed a high proliferation index of more than 80% (Fig. 3f). This confirmed the diagnosis of dual expressor diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) with a non-germinal center B-cell (non-GCB) profile by Hans classifier. Thereafter, an F-18-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) scan was done to evaluate the extent of disease which showed increased FDG uptake limited to the prostate gland and prostatic urethra with no suspiciously FDG-avid systemic involvement including the lymph nodes (Fig. 4a).

Microscopic view under high-power objective (HPO) of immunohistochemical staining showing positivity for CD20 (3a), Bcl6 (40–50%) (3b), MUM1 (70–80%) (3c), c-Myc (80–90%) (3d), and Bcl2 (50–60%) (3e), and a high Ki-67 proliferation index (>80%) (3f).

Axial PET CT scan images of the prostate demonstrating FDG avidity at different time points. Fig. 4a shows increased FDG uptake within the prostate at the time of diagnosis. Fig. 4b demonstrates complete resolution of FDG avidity at 2 months following chemotherapy. Fig. 4c shows interval reappearance of FDG uptake at 8 months post-chemotherapy. Red arrow per panel points to the prostate gland.

The patient was then advised semen cryopreservation to preserve fertility prior to further oncologic treatment. He subsequently received six cycles of chemotherapy consisting of rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP)—administered over four months. Overall, the chemotherapy regimen was well tolerated with only fatigue as the complaint of the patient every session. The patient did not develop any major adverse effects or treatment-related toxicities. At follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of LUTS, with no recurrence of gross hematuria. Laboratory parameters showed normal values apart from mild neutrophilia (ranging 81-84%) and transaminitis (AST ranging 36-101 U/L and ALT ranging 62-211 U/L) which spontaneously resolved after the chemotherapy. After completion of the R-CHOP regimen, 3-month follow-up FDG PET-CT scan showed complete metabolic response (Fig. 4b). At the 8th month post-chemotherapy, repeat FDG PET-CT scan showed mildly increased FDG uptake at the prostatic apex (Fig. 4c). Consequently, multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed normal-sized prostate gland with no suspicious nodule or irregularity (Fig. 5). In the interim, the patient has no recurrence of LUTS and gross hematuria.

Axial T2-weighted MRI of the prostate demonstrating a normal-sized prostate gland with homogeneous signal intensity and no suspicious nodules or focal lesions (red arrow).

Discussion

Primary prostatic lymphoma is a rare entity defined by disease confined to the prostate without nodal or systemic involvement at diagnosis, with fewer than 100 reported cases and no standardized management guidelines.2,5,6 In this case, disease was localized to the prostate with obstructive symptoms and no systemic involvement, fulfilling established diagnostic criteria.6 The incidence of primary prostatic lymphoma is immensely low, limiting the availability of standardized diagnostic and treatment guidelines derived from large clinical studies.7 Although typically diagnosed in older adults, this presentation in a young patient is atypical and highlights the need to consider rare etiologies in similar cases.2,5

Patients with primary prostatic lymphoma most commonly present with nonspecific LUTS, including urinary frequency, intermittency, urgency, and dysuria. These nonspecific manifestations often lead to diagnostic delay or misdiagnosis, as radiologic findings may be indistinguishable from those of more prevalent prostatic conditions, particularly adenocarcinoma.3,8 Hematuria, the initial presenting symptom in this patient, has been reported in only 17.19% of documented cases of primary prostatic lymphoma.2 DRE typically reveals prostatic abnormalities such as enlargement or nodularity in up to 97.92% of reported cases.2 However, no such abnormalities were identified in this patient. Serum PSA levels remain within normal limits (<4 ng/mL) in approximately 89.5% of cases,2 consistent with the findings of this report. Furthermore, most cases described in the literature demonstrate abnormalities on imaging studies, including CT and MRI, which frequently prompt diagnosis through prostate biopsy or surgical intervention.2,5 In contrast, the present case was characterized by inconclusive imaging findings, nonspecific symptoms, and a normal physical examination, thereby further complicating the diagnostic process. Consequently, diagnosis was established via cystoscopic biopsy, a diagnostic approach reported in only 1 of 63 cases (1.59%) in the series by Yuan et al.2

Immunohistopathology is essential for diagnosis and subtype classification, as clinical and radiologic findings are nonspecific. Microscopically, primary prostatic DLBCL demonstrates diffuse infiltration of large atypical lymphoid cells characterized by vesicular chromatin, prominent nucleoli, frequent mitotic figures, and occasional necrosis, often leading to disruption of the normal prostatic architecture.5,7 CD20 expression confirms B-cell lineage and supports the utilization of anti-CD20-directed therapy, such as rituximab-containing regimen.7,9,10 Additional markers like CD10, BCL6, and MUM1 allow for subclassification based on the Hans algorithm into germinal center B-cell (GCB) and non-germinal center B-cell (non-GCB) phenotypes, where the non-GCB subtype is typically linked to a more aggressive clinical behavior and worse outcomes.7,9,10 Furthermore, evaluation of MYC and BCL2 expression is essential, since concurrent overexpression characterizes the double-expressor phenotype, a high-risk biologic group associated with treatment resistance, elevated relapse risk, and reduced overall survival.10 The presence of non-GCB subtype along with double-expressor status indicates particularly aggressive disease pathology and could affect treatment intensity and surveillance strategies. In specific instances, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analyses for MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 alterations may also be conducted to rule out high-grade B-cell lymphomas characterized as “double-hit” or “triple-hit,” which carry even poorer prognosis and could necessitate more aggressive treatment strategies.11

The treatment of primary prostatic DLBCL is mainly extrapolated from systemic DLBCL management guidelines due to its infrequency and the lack of disease-specific clinical trials.7 R-CHOP chemotherapy continues to be the standard treatment and has demonstrated improved disease control and survival rates compared to surgery or radiotherapy alone by targeting both localized and microscopic systemic disease.2,11 Radiotherapy can serve as a consolidative or palliative option for localized conditions or obstructive symptoms, while surgery is typically limited to diagnostic or palliative uses.2,12 High-risk biologic subtypes, particularly non-GCB and double-expressor phenotypes, tend to have poorer responses to standard therapy and might necessitate closer surveillance or enhanced protocols, incorporating newer therapies like polatuzumab vedotin 13. The prognosis is typically unfavorable because of the aggressive nature of the disease, with historical data indicating around 64% 1-year and 33% 5-year overall survival rates.2,7,14 Adverse biological markers like MYC and BCL2 overexpression further negatively impact survival outcomes, emphasizing the necessity of immunohistopathologic risk assessment, multidisciplinary management approaches, and evaluating clinical trial participation to improve patient outcomes.15

Conclusion

Despite its rarity, it is crucial for urologists and oncologists to maintain awareness of primary prostate lymphoma due to its aggressive nature and the potential for rapid progression, particularly since delayed diagnosis can lead to advanced disease leading to mortality. This limited incidence necessitates careful consideration of every reported case to enhance the collective understanding of its presentation, diagnosis, and optimal therapeutic strategies, which often include chemotherapy, surgery, and/or radiotherapy. Primary lymphoma of the prostate lacks standardized therapy; hence, early-stage combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy are justified, while advanced disease relies on systemic therapy, including R-CHOP.
