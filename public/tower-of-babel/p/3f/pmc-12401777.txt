Optimizing dosimetry in Y-90 microsphere radioembolization: GPU-accelerated Monte Carlo simulation versus conventional methods for high-volume setting
Authors: Liu Hongming, Liang Ziwei, Hu Ziyi, Qu Shuiyin, Hu Ankang, Yan ShuChang, Wu Zhen, Zhang Hui, Li Junli, Qiu Rui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYttrium-90 (90Y) microsphere radioembolization has shown unique advantages in treating both primary and metastatic liver cancer and was introduced into China in 2022. Despite the development of various dosimetric models—ranging from empirical to voxel-based approaches—practical implementation remains challenging. With over 370,000 new liver cancer cases annually and limited access to certified 90Y treatment centers, Chinese interventional oncology departments face increasing pressure to balance dosimetric accuracy with clinical efficiency. This study aims to develop a GPU-based fast Monte Carlo project for accurate voxel-level dose calculation and to evaluate its performance alongside existing dosimetric strategies, with the goal of supporting optimized clinical workflows in high-volume settings.MethodsA fast Monte Carlo simulation algorithm was developed using Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) acceleration and applied retrospectively to eight patients diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma or metastatic colorectal cancer. The dosimetric performance of the GPU-based approach was compared against direct Monte Carlo (MC) simulations, the Medical Internal Radiation Dose (MIRD) formalism, the Voxel S-value (VSV) method, and the Local Energy Deposition (LED) model. Voxel- and organ-level dose accuracy were quantified using metrics such as Mean Absolute Relative Error (MARE), Relative Standard Deviation (RSD), and D95 in dose volume histogram. Statistical comparisons were conducted using Shapiro-Wilk normality tests and repeated measures ANOVA to assess inter-method differences.ResultsThe GPU-based Monte Carlo code demonstrated high accuracy and computational efficiency. Using direct MC simulation as the reference, the GPU-based approach yielded the lowest voxel-level variability, with median RSDs in high-activity transverse regions reaching − 1.13%, indicating superior consistency. Corresponding MARE were 4.53% for the GPU method, compared to 6.71% for VSV and 49.36% for LED, confirming its dosimetric reliability. At the organ level, the GPU-based method achieved RSDs of 0.35% ± 0.80% (tumor), -0.45% ± 0.76% (liver), 1.41% ± 4.45% (lung), and − 1.43% ± 1.23% (spleen), significantly outperforming alternative models. Notably, VSV and LED substantially underestimated lung dose (-52.19% ± 23.87%, -53.71 ± 22.17%), highlighting their limited applicability in heterogeneous regions. In contrast, the dose of spleen (F = 3.26, p = 0.069) and kidneys (F = 3.22, p = 0.071) did not show statistically significant differences between methods. In terms of computational performance, the GPU-based code delivered a remarkable 1,296-fold speed-up over traditional MC simulations, enabling efficient voxel-level dosimetry suitable for clinical workflows.ConclusionThe GPU-based fast Monte Carlo simulation provides a highly accurate and computationally efficient tool for voxel-level dosimetry in 90Y radioembolization. It enables precise estimation of tumor and lung doses with significantly reduced processing time and hardware demands, offering clear clinical advantages in minimizing radiation pneumonitis risk and supporting high-throughput workflows. Importantly, a stratified approach to dosimetric modeling—selecting simplified methods such as VSV or LED for small, well-contained lesions, and reserving GPU-based Monte Carlo for anatomically complex or heterogeneous cases—may optimize the balance between accuracy and efficiency. Future work will focus on large-scale validation and formalizing model selection criteria tailored to tumor morphology and treatment scope, with the aim of advancing personalized dosimetric planning in liver-directed therapies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Nuclear medicine, 90Y, Fast Monte Carlo simulation, Dosimetry, GPU

Introduction

Yttrium-90 radioembolization (Y-90 RE) targets liver tumors by exploiting the hemodynamic characteristic between tumor (hepatic artery-dependent) and normal liver (portal venous perfusion), allowing microspheres selectively embolize in the tumor microvasculature. To prevent radiation-induced complications by ectopic embolization, notably radiation pneumonitis with a 40% mortality rate in severe cases [1], REBOC [2] and EANM [3] guidelines recommend hepatic angiography and 99mTc-MAA imaging before treatment. Despite rigorous patient selection, multicenter studies from the United States [4], Canada [5], and South Korea [6] have documented multiple cases of RE-induced complications including radiation pneumonitis, gastric ulcers, and cholecystitis, particularly with resin microspheres. From a dosimetric perspective, the Medical Internal Radiation Dose (MIRD) model remains the clinical standard due to its computational efficiency and procedural simplicity. However, the investigation by Bozkurt et al. [7] revealed that the MIRD method systematically overestimates tumor and pulmonary doses by approximately 20% and 11%, attributable to interindividual tissue heterogeneity. This systematic bias not only compromises the reliability of pulmonary dose evaluations but may also lead to misguiding activity planning, resulting in deviations from the therapeutic window and ultimately affecting clinical efficacy.

With the aim of balancing tumor response and organ at risk (OAR) toxicity, precise dose prediction and personalized dosimetric planning are essential. The direct Monte Carlo (MC) simulation considered the “gold standard”, relys on patient-specific molecular imaging data. However, even with optimized simulation parameters, MC still require approximately 60 h for calculations [8], which fails to meet the demands of clinical practice. Rapid voxel-level dose evaluation methods, such as Voxel S-value (VSV) and Local Energy Deposition (LED), have been researched and applied. These methods enhance efficiency by simplifying physical processes, allowing for accurate dose calculation in tumors and the liver under specific anatomical conditions [9]. However, Chen et al. [10, 11] assessed multiple density-correction algorithms for VSV, demonstrating that current combinations fail to balanced accuracy between lesions and pulmonary tissues. The LED does not account for cross-fire effects, leading to inaccurate estimations near the liver-lung interface, which increases the risk of under-treatment and side effects. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and deep learning (DL), have offered a new pathway for efficiently and accurately estimating radiopharmaceutical therapy doses [12]. For example, Lee et al. [13] trained a CNN using PET/CT images and MC–calculated dose distributions from 10 patients, achieving a mean prediction error of only 1.09% across eight organs compared to MC results. However, the accuracy of such models heavily depends on the training dataset, which in turn requires extensive MC simulations—resulting in high computational costs and resource demands. Moreover, limited generalizability remains a major drawback of current AI-based approaches.

Contemporary rapid-dose algorithms struggle with efficiency-accuracy tradeoffs, and the clinical practice of Chinese liver cancer treatment further amplifies this contradiction. According to the 2024 annual cancer registry report [14], there are approximately 367,000 new cases of primary liver cancer each year. With the inclusion of RE in the recommended guidelines for primary liver cancer (2024 edition) [15], the clinical demand is expected to show a significant growth trend. For instance, at the Tsinghua Changgung Hospital in Beijing, a national demonstration center, the number of RE treatments has reached 300 cases over the past two years. In their treatment protocol, the time interval between 99mTc-MAA imaging and Y-90 RE treatment typically does not exceed five days, making the lengthy computation time of precise Monte Carlo simulations unacceptable. This situation underscores the urgent need to enhance the efficiency of dose planning, as optimizing dosimetry calculation speed has become a critical breakthrough for improving the accessibility and scalability of clinical services.

Against these dilemmas, developing a novel computational model that balances dosimetric accuracy with time efficiency has become a key breakthrough for addressing the challenges posed by the imminent large-scale clinical application of Y-90 RE. Thus, a GPU-based fast Monte Carlo project was developed. To the best of our knowledge, this technique is being applied to Y-90 RE therapy for the first time, enabling the rapid and accurate calculation of not only tumor and liver doses but also the doses to surrounding OAR. The reliability of model in simulating physical processes was validated, and it was subsequently employed to estimate doses in specific clinical cases, with its computational efficiency and accuracy compared against four other methods.

Materials and methods

Patient data acquisition and characteristics

In this retrospective analysis, the post-therapy imaging data of eight patients with liver malignancy including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) were examined from the Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital. The patients underwent 90Y RE with SIR-Sphere® (Sirtex Medical, Sydney, Australia) and were subjected to PET/CT imaging between May 2023 and July 2024. Patients demographic (age: 59.38 ± 13.06) and tumor characteristics are shown in Table 1. The median injected activity is 1.7 GBq and average activity is 1.69 ± 0.88 GBq. The selection of these patients did not include additional clinical or demographic data, as such information was deemed unnecessary for evaluating and comparing dosimetric methods. The research data was obtained with the patient’s informed consent and approved by the Medical Ethics Review Board of Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital.

Patient specific phantom

The PET/CT imaging was performed using the GE Discovery MI system. For the CT component, a helical scan was employed with a rotation time of 0.5 s and a full rotation length. The tube voltage was set to 120 kV, and the current was manually adjusted, with a reference mA of 200 and an actual mA of 200. For the PET component, the scan time was 20 min. Reconstruction was performed using the VUE Point FX method, while quantitation was conducted with the QClear4000 algorithm. The voxel size and dimension of the CT images are 0.98 × 0.98 × 2.79 mm3 and 512 × 512 × 71, respectively, providing patient-specific structural information for the simulation. The distribution of 90Y is determined based on the PET/CT image, the original voxel size and dimension of which are 3.65 × 3.65 × 2.79 mm3 and 192 × 192 × 71. Since the PET and CT scans were performed on the same device, the data from both modalities are considered pre-registered. To facilitate the dose calculation and the sampling of the 90Y source, the CT images were down-sampled to match the resolution of the PET using MATLAB script. Within the reconstructed voxel phantom, the organ types and material compositions for each voxel were determined based on the HU values, following the methodology reported in previous research [16]. This voxelization process was implemented using a C + + program developed on the Visual Studio 2022 platform.

Tumor and organ segmentation

The open-source software TotalSegmentator [17], developed by University Hospital Basel, was used for organ segmentation. In this study, the lungs, liver, kidneys and spleen were segmented, as shown in Fig. 1(c)-(d). These binary masks were further refined with the assistance of a nuclear medicine physician with over five years of clinical experience. The tumor and healthy liver parenchyma exhibit similar tissue densities, which makes recognition challenging. Therefore, tumors with high uptake values were automatically segmented based on the PET images using the thresholding function of the 3D Slicer 5.6.2 software. Pixels with a value greater than 10% of the maximum pixel value on the PET images, which is consistent with clinical practice, were considered as the tumor region. The segmentation results were then reviewed and manually modified by the physician, particularly to remove any abnormal intrahepatic shunts. A 3D representation of the tumor is shown in Fig. 1(e)-(f).

Dosimetry approaches

Direct Monte Carlo simulation

The direct Monte Carlo simulation is employed to model the transport and interactions of particles, which is regarded as the “Gold Standard”. Using the personalized phantom described in Sect. 2.2, individualized calculations are performed. The non-uniform distribution of the radioactive source, was sampled in proportion of the intensity of pixel values, which served as weights for position sampling. Simulations were conducted using Geant4 to obtain the three-dimensional dose distribution. To ensure that the results of the MC were within an acceptable range of statistical uncertainty, it was imperative to simulate 108 particles [18, 19].

Isotropic uniform sampling was applied within the voxels. The decay of 90Y consists of three components: gamma, beta particles, and electron, which sourced from the ICRP Publication No.107 [20]. MIRD method

Assuming a uniform distribution of activity, the MIRD method calculates the average organ dose by multiplying the accumulated activity in the organ by the S-value, which represents the average absorbed dose in the Region of Interest (ROI) per unit accumulated activity. Given that the position of microspheres remains relatively fixed, the accumulated activity is directly related to the initial activity and the physical half-life. GPU-based Monte Carlo

The GPU-based Monte Carlo simulation employed in this study is an optimized version of our previously developed platform [26], designed to simulate photon-electron coupled transport for internal irradiation dosimetry. The core modules include the GPU Monte Carlo engine and the internal source sampling system. The simulation incorporates the Livermore low-energy electromagnetic model to enhance the accuracy of low-energy photon transport, and accounts for key electron processes such as bremsstrahlung, ionization, multiple scattering, and positron annihilation.

The program is developed using C + + and CUDA, enabling high-performance parallel computing on an RTX 3090 GPU. The procedural implementation flow is illustrated in Fig. 3. Simulation parameters—including source definitions, cross sections, material properties, and random seeds—are initialized on the CPU and transferred to GPU memory. Transport routines are executed in parallel using pre-defined thread blocks and grids. Particle histories are processed based on a last-in-first-out logic until all primary and secondary particles are simulated. Qt and VTK are integrated for user interface development and dose visualization. Final statistical results, including absorbed dose distributions, are returned to the host for post-processing and analysis.

Criteria for benchmark data selection

To ensure the accuracy of the geometry, source spectrum, and physical process parameters in voxel-level Monte Carlo simulations, the S-value for soft tissues (3 × 3 × 3 mm³) was selected as the evaluation metric. MIRD Pamphlet No.17 provides S-value for convolution kernels across various dimensions. However, discrepancies were noted between its data and results from using EGSnrc, particularly at source-to-detector distances beyond 10 mm (Fig. 4). In this Figure, the horizontal axis represents the distance from the voxel center to the source, while the vertical axis depicts the normalized dose. To investigate the discrepancies, Geant4 was used to model the energy deposition from the decay of 90Y within the voxel. Analyzing the MIRD Pamphlet suggests that the overestimation of energy deposition beyond 10 mm may stem from the use of the F6 tally card of MCNP4c 17. This distance corresponds to the maximum electron range in soft tissue, where photon-matter interactions have not yet reached equilibrium with charged particles. Thus, EGSnrc results were used as a benchmark to validate the accuracy of program.

Voxel-level dose verification

This study compared the performance of four voxel-level dose evaluation methods using 3D dose distribution maps in the axial and coronal planes. Figure 6(a)-(d) show the axial dose distribution maps for case_1, all displayed with a uniform color scale for easy comparison. The results reveal that the dose distribution trends are highly consistent. To further quantify the agreement with MC results, the gamma passing rates were calculated. The γr values for all voxels were: 99.65% (GPU), 99.45% (VSV), and 99.36% (LED); and for non-zero dose voxels: 99.65% (GPU), 99.36% (VSV), and 95.04% (LED). Figure 6(e)-(f) provide quantitative analysis of voxel doses along the A and B directions in Fig. 6(a). In the tumor regions where 90Y accumulates, the dose trends of the various methods almost overlap. However, as activity decreases, the LED method shows significant distortion due to the lack of the cross-fire effect, as highlighted in the red dashed region in Fig. 6(f). A further quantitative analysis was conducted on the RSD of voxel doses within the green dashed box region, using MC results as the reference. The GPU method demonstrated a significant advantage in accuracy over the other two methods, particularly in terms of the lower dispersion of RSD. This indicates that the GPU method exhibits better robustness to variations in voxel composition, density, and other properties. Additionally, the MARE for GPU, VSV, and LED in this high-dose region were 4.53%, 6.71%, and 49.36%, respectively.

This indicates a substantial dose underestimation by VSV, potentially due to its inability to account for low-density tissue heterogeneity and particle range effects. The LED also deviates from the MC baseline, though to a lesser extent. In the tumor, characterized by high local uptake, all methods maintained acceptable error levels, but GPU again showed the most stable performance (0.35% ± 0.80%), as reflected by the narrow RSD range. For low-density regions like the lung, GPU showed a small positive bias (1.41% ± 4.45%), whereas both LED and VSV significantly underestimated dose values, exhibiting larger variability across cases (Fig. 9c). This underperformance was particularly evident in cases 1, case 2, and case 6, suggesting a limitation of these methods under conditions of low radioactivity concentration. For peripheral organs such as the kidneys, all methods showed relatively consistent trends in RSD, and differences among methods were less pronounced. While some variations existed, particularly in extreme cases, the overall performance appeared similar, indicating the need for further quantitative analysis to detect subtle distinctions.

To quantitatively assess the differences in accuracy among the three methods, statistical analysis was performed using repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Prior to ANOVA, the Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed that the RSD data for each organ followed a normal distribution. As shown in Table 3, significant differences were observed among the methods for the liver (F = 272.00, p < 0.001), lung (F = 41.70, p < 0.001), and tumor (F = 19.20, p < 0.001), indicating that inter-method RSD differences are systematic and indicative of fundamental divergences in computational modeling and dose estimation mechanisms. These findings suggest that the choice of estimation method substantially impacts absorbed dose calculations in regions with either high microsphere accumulation (liver and tumor) or high heterogeneity (lung). In contrast, the spleen (F = 3.26, p = 0.069) and kidneys (F = 3.22, p = 0.071) did not show statistically significant differences between methods, implying comparable performance across all three approaches in these organs. The absence of statistical significance difference, coupled with the marginally superior performance of LED in predicting spleen and kidney doses, is more likely due to potential statistical fluctuations, rather than fundamental algorithmic discrepancies.

Discussion

Voxel-level dose accuracy across methods

The current study ported the MC method to GPU hardware, achieving rapid computation of the three-dimensional dose distribution in 90Y microsphere radioembolization therapy. This was compared with other commonly used dosimetric methods, including VSV, LED, and MIRD. By comparing the voxel dose variations from two different views, it is evident that in regions of concentrated 90Y, the differences in calculation accuracy among the various methods are less than 15%. The VSV method, using a convolution kernel at a single density, exhibits significant dose discrepancies in areas with large tissue density differences, as illustrated in Fig. 7(e). This is highly consistent with the findings of others [27], who compared VSV with MC and reported a 60% difference at the liver-lung boundary, with even larger discrepancies in lung ranging from − 56 to 70%. Similarly, the LED method, by simplifying photon and high-energy electron energy deposition, falsely assumes no energy deposition in regions of minimal 90Y accumulation in the liver and lungs, introducing large computational errors. In terms of overall voxel-level dose accuracy, the GPU-based approach demonstrates strong performance, as evidenced by both MARE and DVH metrics. In both test case_1 and case_2, it yielded the lowest MARE values and achieved D95 values that most closely matched the reference MC results.

Organ-level dosimetric performance

The dose to the organ of interest is consistent with the voxel dose distribution. MIRD simplifies the physical processes, which inevitably introduces some error. However, a qualitative analysis of tumor geometry reveals that when the tumor coverage is extensive, MIRD provides a highly accurate estimation. This is likely because such a scenario closely aligns with the assumption of a uniformly distributed source. This result aligns with the findings of Bozkurt [7], who reported a dose difference of approximately 5%. The GPU-based Monte Carlo method exhibited absolute mean dose errors of less than 4%, with a notably low calculation deviation for lung doses, measured at 1.41% ± 4.45%. In contrast, both the VSV and LED methods substantially underestimated lung doses by -52.19% and − 53.71%, respectively, which may significantly elevate the risk of radiation-induced pneumonitis. Furthermore, for adjacent critical organs such as the kidneys and spleen, the overall accuracy of dose estimation was limited across all methods. However, analysis of variance (ANOVA) did not reveal statistically significant differences in these organs, suggesting that the observed variations are more likely attributable to statistical fluctuations rather than methodological discrepancies. This can be explained by the relatively minor density differences between the liver and these organs, as well as the limited irradiated regions confined to their boundaries. Given the short range of β-particles, such factors likely minimize inter-method variance.

In contrast, for primary target regions—including the liver, tumor, and lung—ANOVA results in Table 3 yielded p-values below 0.05, indicating significant differences in dose estimation accuracy. Among all tested methods, the GPU-based Monte Carlo consistently demonstrated the highest agreement and stability relative to the reference Geant4 results. These findings underscore the reliability of the GPU implementation in organ-level dosimetry, particularly in heterogeneous or low-density tissues where VSV and LED methods may fail to provide clinically dependable estimates. Overall, the GPU-accelerated Monte Carlo simulations exhibit robust and accurate performance, making them a preferable choice for clinical dose reconstruction where both precision and efficiency are critical.

Computational efficiency, trade-offs, and clinical relevance

In terms of computational efficiency, although direct MC simulation offers the highest accuracy, their extensive runtime remains a critical barrier to clinical application. For instance, Piston [8] demonstrated that even with optimizations—such as increasing voxel volume eightfold and applying secondary particle cutoff ranges of 0.1–0.5 mm—computation time was reduced by only 55%, which remains suboptimal. In contrast, the GPU-accelerated MC implementation achieved a remarkable 1,300-fold increase in computational speed, rendering it far more practical for clinical workflows. Simplified methods such as the VSV and LED models further reduce runtime, with execution speeds reaching the second scale better than reported [28]—occasionally even outperforming the minute runtimes reported for GPU-based methods. This level of efficiency becomes particularly advantageous in anatomically favorable conditions, such as when tumors extensively infiltrate the liver while remaining spatially distant from lung tissue, thereby minimizing low-density tissue interference.

A qualitative review across all test cases revealed that tumor volume and its anatomical location relative to the liver and lungs significantly influence the consistency among different dose calculation methods. Specifically, in cases involving small tumors located in the anterior inferior segment of the right hepatic lobe, VSV-based estimates closely approximated those from both MC and GPU methods. Given VSV’s superior speed, this observation suggests that stratifying patients based on tumor geometry and anatomical context could serve as a practical framework for selecting dose calculation algorithms.

From this perspective, a tailored approach—one that balances efficiency and accuracy by matching computational models to tumor characteristics—may optimize both clinical decision-making and research workflows. For small, well-contained tumors situated away from low-density regions, VSV and LED methods are likely to provide clinically acceptable accuracy (e.g., < 5% deviation). Conversely, in anatomically complex or heterogeneous scenarios—such as those involving the lungs or irregular tumor margins—GPU-based MC remains the preferred choice due to its robustness and high spatial fidelity.

Future work should focus on further validation through larger case series and statistical evaluation. Ongoing efforts in our group are applying this stratified methodology to diverse clinical scenarios, including segmental and lobar liver-directed therapies, with the aim of formalizing model selection criteria based on tumor morphology and treatment scope. Additionally, comparative studies with AI algorithms should be incorporated into future investigations. Existing research [13] suggests that, despite limitations in generalizability and the high computational cost of training, CNN can achieve accuracy and efficiency comparable to GPU-based MC. Moreover, leveraging GPU-based simulations to generate training datasets offers a promising strategy to facilitate the development and deployment of AI models.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that GPU-based fast Monte Carlo technique was applied to 90Y-RE therapy and a comprehensive comparison of its computational characteristics with current dosimetric methods was conducted. Based on the accurate estimation of tumor and liver doses, GPU-based Monte Carlo simulation is a powerful tool for the precise and rapid assessment of lung and other OARs doses. The robustness, accuracy, and computational efficiency of this approach hold great potential for incorporating Monte Carlo-based evaluations into treatment planning prior to therapy. This can undoubtedly assist physicians in optimizing dose plans and reducing their workload, while also minimizing the impact of changes in the hepatic vascular tree structure during the waiting period before treatment. Moreover, the comprehensive three-dimensional dose evaluation enhances the flexibility of dose optimization. Estimating doses to surrounding organs, such as the lungs, gallbladder, stomach, diaphragm, and kidneys, offers significant clinical value in preventing radiation-induced complications, including pneumonitis, cholecystitis, gastritis, and diaphragm injury.
