Tetralogy of Fallot (ToF) patients frequently present with significant tricuspid regurgitation (TR), which has been associated with adverse clinical events.1 Transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) with the Evoque system (Edwards Lifesciences) is now commercially available. Case Presentation

A 52-year-old male with prior history of repaired ToF was lost to follow-up until he presented with cardiac arrest due to ventricular arrhythmia subsequently treated with catheter ablation and subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation. At this timepoint, the right ventricle (RV) was found markedly dilated, and the patient underwent percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation (PPVI). His prior medical history was also notable for iron-deficiency anemia due to antral vascular ectasia and a documented protein-losing enteropathy, more likely related to his chronic right-heart failure.

One year after pulmonary valve intervention, he was admitted for recurrent right-sided heart failure signs and shortness of breath on minimal exertion. Multimodality imaging workup was positive for an isolated torrential TR, with a well-functioning PPVI device and no left-sided disease. Valve morphology main characteristic was the presence of a large coaptation gap (10.5 mm), unsuitable for edge-to-edge repair (Figure 1). The multidisciplinary team decided to proceed with TTVR given with TTVR given the high risk associated with the surgery, and despite the relatively young age. During the screening process, the anatomy was considered eligible for Evoque implantation, with however some concerns regarding a possible risk of ventricular migration given the unusually severe leaflet tethering (19.5 mm height) (Figure 1). The patient was admitted 5 days before the procedure to undergo aggressive prehabilitation with intravenous diuresis and levosimendan infusion, in order to reduce RV dimensions as much as possible and to optimize the adaptation of the ventricular function after TR suppression. Finally, a 56-mm Evoque was successfully implanted with no residual TR afterward. The patient was discharged home on day 7, after an uneventful postinterventional hospital stay. The prolonged length of stay was justified given the concerns for access-related complications, conduction monitoring, and heart failure assessment in the setting of advanced right heart failure. At the 30-day visit, he was NYHA functional class I, with a considerable increase in Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire score from 55 to 95. Follow-up 6-minute walk test distance was 420 m, in a patient who was unable to climb 2 flights of stairs on a row before the intervention. RV reverse remodeling assessed on computed tomography (Figure 2) was progressive along with structural interventions: −64 mL in end-diastole volume (EDV) after PPVI, and −88 mL after TTVR. Total reduction in RV EDV was 152 mL, corresponding to a 26% reduction compared to baseline. Along with RV EDV decrease, RV end-systolic volumes increased after TTVR, resulting in a deterioration in RV ejection fraction postprocedurally, from 48.3% to 27.7%. Those changes in RV structure and function translated on transthoracic echocardiography into a significant increase in left ventricular (LV) outflow stroke volume, from 67 mL (heart rate [HR] 64 beats/min) pre-TTVR to 122 mL (HR 68 beats/min) at 30 days and 139 mL (HR 58 beats/min) at 3 months. Interestingly, LV EDV rose, from 151 to 213 mL, showing an inverse and reciprocal relationship between both ventricles. Discussion

TTVR in a ToF patient has been recently reported.2 Late after placement of the ventricular septal defect patch, scarring and restriction of the septal leaflet is frequently observed in those patients,3 making the Evoque implantation procedure particularly challenging given the difficulty to engage leaflets. To minimize the risk of the procedure in those cases, prehabilitation seems crucial in order to limit leaflet tethering and optimize device anchoring. Whether pretreatment should be diuretics only or in combination with inotropes remains to be clarified. Despite those considerations, the benefits of this intervention may be relevant, with a major symptomatic improvement expected. In this case, presenting with a very advanced RV dilatation, the improvement in functional status and organ damage seemed to be directly associated with cardiac reverse remodeling found on RV EDV, that correlated well with an increase in LV EDV with improved LV hemodynamics reflected in LV stroke volume rise. Percutaneous structural interventions offer a unique opportunity for optimized lifespan management in the specific population of adults with congenital heart disease. Heart team discussions are key to minimize repeated surgeries in those individuals frequently displaying a high burden of complex prior interventions and in whom reoperation is at risk.4 Newly approved technologies, such as the Evoque valve, provide promising perspectives in this underserved group of patients.

Conclusions

ToF patients frequently present with significant TR. TTVR procedure in this population may be more challenging due to particular valve anatomies. However, after successful implantation this younger subgroup of TR patients may benefit the most from a mini-invasive approach, with early reverse remodeling and major symptomatic improvement.
