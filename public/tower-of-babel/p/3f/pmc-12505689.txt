Time worked in shifts and occurrence of drowsiness events while driving: attenuating effect of physical activity
Authors: Luiz Antônio Alves de Menezes-Júnior, Fernando Luiz Pereira de Oliveira, George Luiz Lins Machado-Coelho, Fausto Aloisio Pedrosa Pimenta, Raimundo Marques do Nascimento Neto, Sílvia Nascimento de Freitas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShift work disrupts circadian rhythms and is associated with adverse sleep outcomes, such as daytime sleepiness and drowsiness while driving. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between history of shift work, drowsiness events during driving, and the potential moderating effect of physical activity.MethodsA cross-sectional investigation involved 1,413 male shift workers in an iron ore mining firm. Heavy machinery essential to the mining process was operated. A specific question from the Berlin Questionnaire was used to evaluate drowsiness while driving, asking whether participants had ever felt drowsy or fallen asleep while driving, regardless of whether the episode occurred during work-related or leisure-time driving. The main explanatory variable was the shift in working hours. The levels of physical activity were classified as low, moderate, and high using the IPAQ. Multivariate and descriptive logistic regression analyses were conducted.ResultsThe majority of workers were aged 20 to 34 (45.1%), self-identified as non-white (77.2%), and had completed high school (76.0%). The average history of shift work time was 9.56 years (95% CI: 9.23–9.90), with 76% working for five years or more. Drowsiness events while driving were reported by 17.3% of workers. Multivariate analysis showed a significant association between history of shift work and drowsiness events, indicating a dose–response relationship, where longer history of shift work time increased the likelihood of drowsiness events (OR: 2.93 for 10–15 years; OR: 4.34 for > 15 years). However, physical activity acted as a moderating factor, demonstrating a dose–response effect in reducing this chance, with a reduction of 27.6% and 30.9% for moderate and high levels, respectively, compared to participants with a low level of activity.ConclusionA longer history of shift work time increases the chance of drowsiness events during driving, but higher physical activity levels mitigate them.Keywords: Circadian rhythm, Sleep, Occupational risks, Physical activity, Sedentary behaviors

Introduction

Rotating shifts is a common practice in many industries that require continuous operations, such as mining, where the need to maintain production 24 h a day, seven days a week imposes a shift structure. However, this practice can have significant implications for workers' health and safety [18]. The disruption of circadian rhythms caused by varying working hours can lead to a range of health problems, including sleep disorders and chronic fatigue [32]. The longer the accumulated time of circadian disruption, the more vulnerable we become to these sleep disorders [28, 35]. These problems, meanwhile, increase the risk of brief drowsiness events and involuntary periods of sleep that occur during waking activities, such as driving [15]. Episodes of drowsiness are particularly dangerous in work environments that require high vigilance, such as operating heavy machinery in mining [6].

Previous studies suggest that the accumulated time spent working alternating shifts is associated with a worsening in sleep quality and an increase in the incidence of drowsiness events [9]. However, regular physical activity has been identified as a potentially effective intervention for improving sleep quality and reducing fatigue. Physical activity (PA) can help regulate circadian rhythms and improve general health, offering a mechanism to mitigate the adverse effects of shift work on sleep. PA improves sleep quality through dual mechanisms: circadian regulation and homeostatic reinforcement. As a circadian zeitgeber, PA synchronizes biological rhythms by modulating core body temperature and melatonin secretion, enhancing alignment between sleep–wake cycles and environmental cues [32]. PA mitigates metabolic dysregulation caused by sleep deprivation. For instance, a randomized trial demonstrated that high-intensity interval exercise (HIIE) during sleep restriction preserved mitochondrial function while stabilizing peripheral circadian rhythms [32]. This underscores PA’s role in buffering the metabolic and circadian disruptions inherent to shift work. By promoting deeper, more restorative sleep, physical activity can reduce daytime sleepiness and improve alertness, potentially reducing the occurrence of drowsiness events [20, 29, 34, 35].

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between time worked in alternating shifts and the occurrence of drowsiness events while driving, as well as to examine the mitigating role of moderate or vigorous physical activity in this relationship.

Methodology

Design and participants

The present study was conducted within the framework of the Strengthening Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. We concentrated on shift workers from a Brazilian company that extracts iron ore in Minas Gerais and Pará. Under the 'Fatigue Management Project,' three cross-sectional panels were carried out to collect comparable data from workers in various parts of the nation.

We carried out panels in three different regions: the Iron Quadrangle in 2012, with the participation of 337 workers from four mines; the southern region of Pará in 2018, with 932 workers; and in 2015 in the same region again, with 192 workers from another mine. To improve internal validity and eliminate possible confounding factors related to gender, we excluded 38 women from the sample, and 10 workers were excluded due to incomplete data. This resulted in a final sample of 1,413 rotating shift workers.

The workers followed rotating shift schedules specific to their work sites. In Minas Gerais, participants followed a 4 × 1 shift rotation, consisting of four consecutive 6-h work shifts, each followed by 12h of rest, and one day off after the cycle. The shifts rotated through four periods: 7 a.m. to 1 p.m., 1 p.m.to 7 p.m., 7 p.m.to 1 a.m., and 1 a.m.to 7 a.m. In Pará, participants followed a 5 × 2 shift rotation, with five consecutive 8h shifts, each followed by 24h of rest, and two days off after the cycle. In both locations, workers rotated between morning, afternoon, and night shifts, ensuring continuous production coverage.

Data collect

The approach for gathering data was to follow Menezes-Júnior's framework [11, 12]. Three cross-sectional studies involved face-to-face data collection by trained professionals. This included completing questionnaires, taking anthropometric measurements, and collecting biological samples.

Explanatory: History of shift work time

Time spent working shifts was assessed through interviews with the participants, who were asked about the total number of years they had worked night or alternate shifts during their working lives. Shift work duration was evaluated as continuous and categorized into four groups (< 5, 5–9.9, 10–14.9, ≥15 years) in the primary analysis to enhance interpretability and detect potential non-linear associations. For the moderation analysis involving physical activity, shift duration was modeled in 5 and 10-year increments. This choice was based on prior evidence demonstrating a 7.1% increase in cardiovascular risk per 5 additional years of shift work [34] and literature suggesting five years as a threshold for the development of shift work-related health problems [26, 31]. This approach also prevented dilution of the sample across small subgroups, ensuring more stable estimates.

Outcome: Drowsiness events

The Berlin Questionnaire was used to collect data on drowsiness events. This validated tool for diagnosing sleep disorders includes a question asking participants whether they had ever felt drowsy or fallen asleep while driving [2, 21]. The question did not specify whether the event occurred during occupational or non-occupational driving, thus capturing any driving-related drowsiness regardless of context. Responses were recorded in binary format (yes/no). Participants were asked if they had ever experienced sleep episodes while driving. The response to this query was given in binary (yes/no) form.

Moderator: Physical activity

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) short form, version 8, was used to assess the participants' level of physical activity. With this validated tool, people can measure how much physical activity they have done in the last few weeks. Participants were divided into three categories of physical activity based on their IPAQ responses: low (< 600 MET-minutes/week), medium (600–3000 MET-minutes/week), and high (> 3000 MET-minutes/week) [10, 16]. This classification examined the effects of physical activity on drowsiness events and the moderating effect of physical activity on the association between the duration of alternating shift work and the incidence of drowsiness events.

Covariates

Covariates in the fields of sociodemographics, medicine, behavior, and anthropometry were taken into account. The sociodemographic variables were, seasonality (winter, spring, summer, and autumn), education (full primary, secondary, technical, and higher education), geographic location (Minas Gerais and Pará), age (20 to 29.9 years, 30 to 39.9 years, 40 to 65 years), and self-declared skin color. The self-reported skin color was based on the categories proposed by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE: White, Brown, Black, Yellow, and Indigenous) [9]. BBYI is an acronym for Black, Brown, Yellow, or Indigenous. Occupational position was categorized based on whether the participant held a job involving the operation of heavy machinery. Workers were classified as “machine operators” if they operated mining equipment or vehicles as part of their routine occupational duties. All other job roles were categorized as “non-operators”. This variable was included as a proxy for occupational driving exposure, given that machine operators are more likely to engage in prolonged seated tasks involving vehicle or equipment operation.

Alcohol intake and smoking were the two behavioral variables that respondents self-reported and entered into the survey. The participants' smoking status was divided into two categories: non-smokers (those who never smoked or stopped more than six months ago) and smokers (those who smoked until the study date or quit less than six months ago). In addition, nicotine dependence was assessed using the Fagerstrom Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND), which has six questions that result in a score ranging from 0 to 10 points [13]. Two methods were used to analyze alcohol consumption: self-reported frequency of alcohol intake, which classified people as alcohol consumers if they reported any frequency, and the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) questionnaire, which stratifies risks into no risk, low risk, and high risk, the use of both parameters aimed to minimize underreporting [3].

Waist circumference, weight, and height were among the anthropometric measurements made; qualified experts did these to reduce measurement errors. A waist circumference greater than 102 cm was deemed high, indicating a higher chance of obesity-related morbidities in males (WHO, 2000). Based on weight and height measurements, BMI was computed and categorized using the WHO stratification: 18.5 to 24.9 kg/m2 was considered normal weight, 25 to 29.9 kg/m2 was considered overweight, and ≥ 30 kg/m2 was considered obesity (WHO, 2000). Furthermore, self-reported medical diagnoses for cardiovascular, respiratory, chronic kidney diseases, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes were assessed. Those who reported a medical diagnosis or use of medications to control these conditions were classified as having comorbidities.

Statistical analysis

First, we evaluated the data descriptively, showing the mean with confidence intervals for the continuous variables and the absolute and relative frequencies for the categorical variables. We explored associations between variables using Pearson's chi-square test. Additionally, we calculated Cramér's V coefficient as a measure of effect size for these associations. Cramér’s V quantifies the strength of association between categorical variables on a scale from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating stronger associations [8]. This provides complementary information to the p-value, which only tests for statistical significance without indicating the magnitude of the association.

Subsequently, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were carried out to assess the relationship between history of shift work time and the occurrence of drowsiness events while driving. We employed two analytical strategies to strengthen the robustness of our findings. The first was based on a causal inference framework using a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), which allowed us to identify a minimal sufficient adjustment set to control for confounding while avoiding unnecessary adjustment for mediators or colliders [9]. According to the DAG, the selected covariates included age, education, geographic location, self-reported skin color, and occupational position (machine operator), as these variables sufficiently blocked backdoor paths between exposure and outcome (Fig. 1).

Variables in white are the unobserved variables

In parallel, we conducted a second model using a full adjusted approach that included additional sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical variables not specified in the DAG. This expanded model contained the variables from the initial model, plus tobacco use, alcohol consumption, comorbidities, and medication use, based on their potential impact on sleep quality and sleepiness. Age was included due to its association with both sleep physiology and exposure to shift work schedules. Education was considered an indicator of socioeconomic status, and geographic location was included to account for regional differences in shift patterns. Tobacco and alcohol use were included due to their known effects on sleep regulation, while comorbidities and medication use were incorporated because of their possible direct influence on sleep quality and daytime alertness [27]. This dual-model approach allowed us to evaluate the consistency of our findings across theoretically driven and empirically enriched adjustment strategies, providing more comprehensive insights into the relationship under investigation.

Furthermore, an interaction analysis was performed to examine the relationship between history of shift work and drowsiness events, accounting for the participants' varying degrees of physical activity. Physical activity was divided into three categories: low, medium, and high. The history of shift work time was regarded as an ordinal variable and was divided into intervals of five or ten years. An additive interaction term was employed in this analysis to examine how the participants' varying levels of physical activity affect the impact of shift work hours on drowsiness events. The interaction effects were plotted in a forest plot (Fig. 2), using a logarithmic scale for the X-axis. This visualization was constructed in Microsoft Excel by setting the odds ratio axis to a log10 scale (base 10), allowing for symmetric representation around the null value (OR = 1) without transforming the original OR values.

Results

Among the 1,413 rotating shift workers included in the study, 17.6% (n = 249) reported having experienced a drowsiness episode while driving. The occurrence of these events varied according to several sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics. Workers aged 40 years or older had a higher frequency of drowsiness events (35.7%) compared to those aged 20–29 years (7.6%) (p = 0.001). Participants with longer histories of shift work were also more likely to report drowsiness (p < 0.001). Lower levels of education were associated with higher prevalence, as 77.9% of those with only secondary education reported drowsiness events, versus 14.1% among those with technical education (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

A significantly higher proportion of those reporting drowsiness episodes were not employed as machine operators (82.3% vs. 30.5%; p < 0.001), and a greater percentage reported low physical activity levels (57.4% vs. 46.9%; p = 0.008). Smokers or former smokers were also more prevalent among participants who reported drowsiness (17.3%) compared to those who did not (11.2%) (p = 0.008). No statistically significant differences were observed in skin color, BMI, and alcohol use (Table 1).

The mean duration of shift work was 9.56 years (95% CI: 9.23–9.90), with 23.8% of participants reporting less than 5 years, 36.7% reporting 5 to 9.9 years, 31.3% reporting 10 to 14.9 years, and 8.3% reporting more than 15 years of shift work experience (Fig. 2). Concerning sleepiness, we found that 17.6% of participants reported experiencing a drowsiness episode while driving a vehicle, and it was more prevalent among older workers, those who had worked shifts longer, those who had less physical activity, and smokers (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Levels of physical activity were inversely associated with the occurrence of drowsiness events while driving. Compared to participants with low levels of physical activity, those with moderate or high physical activity had a lower chance of reporting drowsiness events, according to the adjusted models. Furthermore, the history of shift work time exhibited a dose–response relationship with drowsiness events while driving, with longer durations correlating with increased odds. Participants with 10–14.9 years of shift work had nearly twice the odds of reporting drowsiness (OR: 1.90; 95% CI: 1.13–3.17), and those with more than 15 years had 2.45 times higher odds (95% CI: 1.15–5.21), compared to those with less than five years (Table 2).

Discussion

The results of this study confirm that time spent working alternating shifts is significantly associated with an increase in the occurrence of drowsiness events while driving. We observed a clear dose–response gradient, in which workers with more years of experience on alternating shifts had a higher likelihood of drowsiness events. These findings are consistent with existing literature, which suggests that prolonged exposure to alternating shifts can deregulate circadian rhythms, leading to a worsening of sleep quality and increased daytime fatigue [9].

This disruption in circadian and homeostatic sleep regulation mechanisms compromises alertness, particularly during monotonous tasks such as driving. Shift work affects the biological clock by altering melatonin production and impairing sleep continuity, leading to accumulated sleep debt. As a result, cognitive performance and vigilance decline, elevating the risk of drowsiness episodes [1, 4, 5, 7, 14].

In addition to neurophysiological changes, shift work is associated with increased stress and metabolic dysregulation, which further affect energy levels and alertness. For instance, poor sleep affects glucose metabolism and immune function, and irregular light exposure may impair vitamin D synthesis, which plays a role in neuromuscular function [11, 12, 22, 24]. These overlapping mechanisms contribute to a physiological environment more prone to drowsiness and fatigue among shift workers.

Other behavioral and psychosocial factors also play a role. Unhealthy eating patterns, excessive caffeine intake, social stress, and physical inactivity are common among shift workers and negatively affect sleep quality and daytime functioning [17, 25, 30, 32].

However, regular physical activity can mitigate some of the negative effects of chronic sleep restriction caused by prolonged shift work [20]. In our study, we found that physical activity can moderate this adverse relationship. Workers who engaged in moderate or vigorous physical activity were significantly less likely to have episodes of drowsiness, regardless of how long they worked alternating shifts. However, no significant difference was found between moderate and vigorous activity levels. Several mechanisms can mitigate the negative effects of shift work on sleep and alertness. Physical activity increases the production of hormones essential for inducing and maintaining sleep, such as melatonin and serotonin [19], facilitating a healthier sleep–wake cycle. In addition, physical activity reduces sedentary behavior, which is often associated with shift work, improving not only overall physical health but also sleep quality [24, 29]. Regular physical activity also provides greater exposure to sunlight and increased synthesis of vitamin D, improving the alignment of the sleep–wake cycle with the natural light–dark cycle [22–24].

As for drowsiness events, physical activity improves cardiovascular and metabolic health by increasing fatigue resistance. Physically active workers tend to have better energy levels and are less likely to feel fatigued during the day [20, 34], which is crucial for avoiding drowsiness events. The increased blood circulation and cerebral oxygenation resulting from regular exercise can improve alertness and cognitive function [19], reducing the occurrence of episodes of uncontrollable sleepiness during monotonous tasks such as driving. Thus, physical activity not only improves sleep quality and reduces fatigue but also increases the ability to maintain attention and alertness during the day, mitigating the risks associated with shift work.

These findings have important implications for occupational health, especially in industries where shift work is common, such as mining. Companies can consider promoting physical activity programs for their workers as a strategy to reduce the risk of drowsiness episodes and improve safety at work. Wellness programs that encourage the regular practice of even moderate physical activity can have a significant impact on reducing fatigue and improving sleep quality for shift workers.

Our study identified an association between the history of shift work and the occurrence of drowsiness episodes while driving, as well as a potential moderating effect of physical activity. However, some limitations should be considered. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of the study prevents the determination of causality. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether promoting physical activity can effectively reduce the incidence of drowsiness episodes in shift workers. In addition, the assessment of physical activity and drowsiness episodes was based on self-report, which may be subject to recall bias and under-reporting. Objective measures, such as the use of accelerometers to monitor physical activity and sleep monitoring devices, could provide a more accurate assessment. Our study did not assess sleep apnea, a potential confounder for drowsiness events, which could be explored in future research. Physical activity was measured using the short version of the IPAQ, which does not differentiate between activity domains (e.g., leisure-time vs. occupational physical activity). Although this limits our ability to distinguish the context in which physical activity occurred, it is worth noting that most participants performed sedentary occupational tasks, such as operating machinery or office-based activities. Therefore, we assume that differences in reported physical activity levels largely reflect non-occupational activity. Moreover, the sample was composed exclusively of rotating shift workers, with no inclusion of individuals working fixed daytime or administrative schedules. This limits the possibility of comparing outcomes between shift and non-shift workers. Although all participants were exposed to night shifts as part of their alternating schedules, we did not collect information on the specific frequency of night work, which restricts more detailed stratified analyses based on the intensity of night shift exposure.

Future research should explore specific interventions to increase physical activity among shift workers, such as structured exercise programs and incentives for regular physical activity. In addition, further studies are needed to investigate other possible mediating and modulating factors in this relationship, such as diet, the quality of the sleep environment, and the use of sleep hygiene techniques. Exploring interactions between physical activity and other lifestyle factors may reveal important insights for promoting sleep health and preventing occupational accidents.

Conclusion

A longer history of shift work was associated with an increased likelihood of drowsiness events during driving, while higher levels of physical activity were associated with a reduced likelihood of such events. These findings suggest that physical activity may play a protective role in the context of shift work. However, given the cross-sectional nature of this study, causality cannot be inferred. Future longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these associations and to better understand the potential benefits of physical activity for improving alertness and safety among shift workers.
