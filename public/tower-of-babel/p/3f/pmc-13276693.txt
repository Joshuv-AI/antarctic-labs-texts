Autistic Children With Speech Onset Delay Show Reversed Bias in Spectral Versus Temporal Auditory Processing
Authors: Luodi Yu, Shuyu Xie, Li Wang, Laurent Mottron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA recent “hierarchical” reinterpretation of the neurological basis of autism suggests that in autism with early language delay, perceptual processing may be favored over the integration of transmodal information. This model is largely based on neuroimaging findings relating to visual processing, but predicts a corresponding reorganization in the auditory modality. The present study provides the first direct psychophysical evidence of a reversed auditory processing bias in children with prototypical autism, characterized by impaired temporal resolution and enhanced spectral sensitivity. Using two matched psychoacoustic paradigms—temporal gap detection and low‐frequency frequency modulation (FM) detection—we assessed auditory thresholds in 21 autistic and 23 typically developing (TD) children. Compared to TD peers, autistic children exhibited significantly higher temporal thresholds and significantly lower spectral thresholds, yielding a markedly elevated Auditory Bias Index (ABI) that quantifies a preference for spectral over temporal cues. In the group of autistic children investigated here, enhanced spectral resolution correlated positively with receptive language and nonverbal IQ, whereas temporal resolution was associated with age‐related gains and language outcomes. These findings support the hypothesis of an atypical sensory‐perceptual hierarchy in autism with speech onset delay and early behavioral atypicalities, with potential implications for alternative, nonsocial pathways to language acquisition.

Autistic Children Show Reversed Bias in Spectral Versus Temporal Auditory Processing

The Enhanced Perceptual Functioning model (Mottron et al. 2006) and its sequels, the Sensory symptoms theory (Robertson and Baron‐Cohen 2017), and the Cascading Effects Theory (Cascio et al. 2016) posit that atypicalities in early sensory processing can propagate through higher‐order domains, including social cognition and language. A recent update of this model suggests that autism may involve a fundamental reorganization of cortical processing hierarchies, shifting the balance between unimodal sensory systems and integrative, transmodal networks (Bernhardt et al. 2025). This model is largely supported by neuroimaging findings relating to visual processing. In the auditory modality, arguments favoring this series of theoretical accounts are limited to narrative reviews highlighting a potential dissociation between relatively enhanced spectral (frequency) processing and diminished temporal (timing) processing (Haesen et al. 2011; Yu and Wang 2021). Empirical support remains scarce, with one exception of Samson et al. (2011), which reported increased activity in primary auditory cortex but diminished activity in nonprimary auditory cortex in response to temporally, but not spectrally, complex sounds. However, direct empirical evidence for this reversed hierarchical auditory system remains absent.

Human speech perception is a complex process that relies on the brain's capacity to interpret both temporal and spectral features of sound. Temporal properties refer to the time‐based characteristics of sound, such as duration, onset, and offset, which are essential for perceiving consonants, timing cues, and amplitude modulations of speech. Spectral properties, on the other hand, relate to the frequency makeup of sounds, including the distribution of energy across different frequencies, such as pitch, formants, and spectral envelope. Deficits in an individual's ability to process auditory temporal information may hinder the development of clear and stable phonological representations and are implicated in developmental language disorders such as dyslexia and specific language impairment (Boets et al. 2011; Hämäläinen et al. 2013).

In the spectral domain, a higher prevalence of absolute pitch (AP), the ability to label a musical note without reference, has been documented in musically untrained autistic individuals compared to the general population (DePape et al. 2012; Heaton, Williams, et al. 2008; Mottron et al. 2013). Empirical studies conducted across diverse regions, linguistic backgrounds, and experimental paradigms have reported enhanced pitch discrimination abilities in autism (Bonnel et al. 2003; Heaton et al. 1998; Järvinen‐Pasley, Wallace, et al. 2008; Lepistö et al. 2005; Yu et al. 2015), although not all findings converge (e.g., Boets et al. 2015). Bonnel et al. (2003) reported enhanced pitch discrimination in autistic participants compared to typically developing (TD) peers using a series of psychophysical tasks; however, this effect was absent in autistic individuals without speech onset delay (Bonnel et al. 2010), suggesting that atypical pitch processing may be more closely tied to certain developmental profiles within autism. Some evidence points to a negative relationship between superior pitch discrimination and native language acquisition, regardless of the direction of causality (Eigsti and Fein 2013; Jones et al. 2009). As a mechanistic example, Mandarin‐speaking autistic children show enhanced discrimination of nonspeech pitch contrasts but poorer discrimination when pitch is integrated into phonemes of their native language, that is, lexical tones in Mandarin (Yu et al. 2015). Such findings could reflect that heightened pitch perception may “hijack” the extraction of meaning‐bearing units in spoken language (Eigsti and Fein 2013; Järvinen‐Pasley, Pasley, and Heaton 2008; Thye et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2015). At the same time, enhanced pitch perception has also been linked with alternative, nonsocial pathways to language learning in some individuals. Dumont et al. (2024), for example, found that autistic individuals with unexpected bilingual abilities—acquired outside typical communicative contexts—demonstrated superior pure‐tone discrimination despite lower native language scores relative to their peers.

Conversely, temporal auditory processing appears often impaired in autism. Psychophysical studies using auditory gap detection paradigms have reported that children on the autism spectrum exhibit elevated detection thresholds (the minimum inter‐stimulus interval [ISI] required to perceive a disruption), indicating reduced auditory temporal resolution (Bhatara et al. 2013; Foss‐Feig et al. 2017). Other investigations using simultaneity and temporal order judgment (TOJ) tasks have revealed a wider temporal integration window for auditory and multisensory input (e.g., flash‐beep, audiovisual speech), such that greater asynchrony between two signals is treated as simultaneous (de Boer‐Schellekens et al. 2013; Foss‐Feig et al. 2010; Stevenson et al. 2014). Although some studies have reported mixed or task‐dependent effects (Meilleur et al. 2020), the overall literature indicates robust difficulties in fine‐grained temporal resolution in autism. Such limitations in temporal precision may compromise fundamental aspects of speech perception such as consonant perception (Chen and Peng 2021; Kuhl et al. 2005) and listening in background noise (Callejo and Boets 2023). Cortical responses to sounds are delayed and oscillatory synchrony is reduced in autism spectrum disorder (ASD), highlighting disruptions in neural timing (Gandal et al. 2010; Yu et al. 2018). These impairments may cascade into broader difficulties in speech‐in‐noise comprehension and phonemic representation, possibly due to increased internal neural noise or diminished efficiency in temporal entrainment (Alcántara et al. 2012; Foss‐Feig et al. 2018). Importantly, temporal processing deficits in autism appear modality‐specific, predominantly affecting audition rather than vision (Foss‐Feig et al. 2017).

These atypicalities in auditory processing are consistent with a shift in hemispheric dominance in autism, depending on the type of information processed. In neurotypical individuals, well‐established hemispheric asymmetries exist in auditory processing: the left hemisphere is specialized for representing rapidly changing, temporally‐based acoustic features (e.g., voice onset time, VOT), while the right hemisphere is more sensitive to slowly changing, spectrally‐based features such as fundamental frequency (F0) contours (Poeppel et al. 2008; Zatorre 2022). In most right‐handed, neurotypical individuals, left‐hemispheric dominance for language is a hallmark of functional specialization, and this leftward lateralization is thought to be supported by fine‐grained temporal processing capacities (Albouy et al. 2020).

In contrast, autistic children often exhibit atypical lateralization during auditory and language processing, including reduced or reversed (rightward) lateralization (Jouravlev et al. 2020; Kleinhans et al. 2008; Lai et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2023). Developmental trajectories of brain lateralization also diverge significantly between autistic and TD children. While TD children shift from bilateral to left‐dominant activation patterns with age, autistic children tend to transition from bilateral activation toward increasing right hemisphere dominance (Eyler et al. 2012; Li et al. 2025).

These findings suggest that biases in temporal versus spectral auditory processing may participate in the atypical cortical organization observed in autism, together with other indications of inverse hierarchy evident in the visual modality. However, a detailed characterization of the psychophysical biases of auditory processing in autism is essential for extending the alteration of typical hierarchical organization of information processing to the auditory modality. Few studies have examined temporal and spectral domains concurrently, and those that have often yield inconsistent findings. For instance, Boets et al. (2015) and Bhatara et al. (2013) reported impairments in both spectral and temporal processing in autistic individuals, challenging the idea of a reversed bias. In Bhatara et al. (2013), participants completed two psychoacoustic tasks: one to determine frequency discrimination thresholds and another to assess temporal gap detection (TGD) thresholds. While the study confirmed deficits in gap detection among autistic participants, it also found impaired frequency discrimination at higher frequencies (4000 Hz). Similarly, Boets et al. (2015) investigated autistic children with a history of early language delay and reported no evidence for an asymmetric pattern of enhanced spectral but impaired temporal processing. Instead, both domains showed some degree of impairment. Notably, both studies employed a three‐alternative forced choice (3AFC) paradigm for frequency discrimination, potentially confounding perceptual acuity with executive demands (Sutcliffe and Bishop 2005). Moreover, studies reporting enhanced pitch processing often employ speech‐based or complex melodic stimuli, raising the possibility that such findings reflect not enhanced perceptual acuity per se, but rather a tendency toward over‐focus on perceptual features and a reduced susceptibility to distraction from melodic or linguistic content (Boets et al. 2015).

In sum, existing research has yet to provide conclusive evidence regarding atypical biases in temporal and spectral auditory processing in autism. The inconsistency in findings is likely attributable to heterogeneity in both study populations and methodologies. First, many prior studies did not take into account the possible effect of the developmental pathway of language acquisition in the interpretation of their results. However, some evidence suggests that perceptual atypicalities may be specific to autistic individuals with early language delays (Bonnel et al. 2010) that can be referred to as prototypical autism. Second, studies have employed a range of task designs including 3AFC tasks, two‐alternative forced choice (2AFC) tasks, and yes/no paradigms. These paradigms differ in their psychometric decision points and impose varying cognitive demands. Third, the extended duration and monotonous nature of psychophysical tasks may lead to reduced motivation and attentional engagement among autistic participants, potentially compromising the accuracy of data collected from this group. There is a critical need for controlled, psychophysically rigorous studies in well‐defined populations to clarify the nature and extent of auditory processing biases in autism.

The current study addresses these gaps by examining auditory temporal and spectral resolution in children with prototypical autism. Using two psychophysical paradigms—TGD and frequency modulation (FM) detection—we seek to obtain accurate individual thresholds in perception of timing and frequency difference. TGD is a well‐established psychoacoustic paradigm for measuring auditory temporal resolution by determining the minimum silent interval at which a listener can detect a discontinuity—serving as a classic paradigm for capturing millisecond‐level auditory temporal sensitivity. It has been widely applied across both human and animal studies, including in populations with dyslexia, specific language impairment, and ASD. In FM detection tasks, pure tones with modulated frequencies are presented, and the frequency deviation (Δf) from the carrier wave is systematically varied to determine the listener's sensitivity to spectral change. FM detection was selected over frequency discrimination tasks to minimize confounds related to auditory working memory and other cognitive demands (Sutcliffe and Bishop 2005). By comparing detection thresholds across domains, we aimed to quantify individual differences in temporal and spectral processing to reflect the relative dominance of spectral versus temporal resolution. This approach offers a direct test of the hypothesis that autistic children exhibit a reversed auditory processing bias and provides new insights into how these perceptual tendencies may inform language learning trajectories.

Methods

Participants

A total of 71 children were initially recruited for this study, including 44 autistic children and 27 TD children. Inclusion criteria for the autism group were as follows: (1) a clinical diagnosis of ASD by a licensed pediatrician with at least 10 years of diagnostic experience, based on DSM‐5 criteria; (2) observable atypical developmental features before the age of two, identifiable by nonspecialists; (3) delayed language development, defined as the absence of two‐word phrases by age two; (4) exclusion of individuals with Asperger's syndrome, global developmental delay (e.g., walking independently after 15 months), syndromic autism (e.g., those with neurological or genetic syndromes indicated by dysmorphic features, delayed motor development, or epilepsy), and other neurodevelopmental disorders within the autism spectrum. These criteria represent a proxy for what we have defined as “prototypical autism” (Mottron and Gagnon 2023). Among the 44 autistic children composing the initial group, one was reported by caregivers to have hearing impairment, and two had known genetic disorders, and all three were excluded. During data collection, 9 children in the autism group did not complete the psychophysical tasks; 11 autistic children and 4 TD children refused assessments, did not pass practice trials, or withdrew from the study. The final sample consisted of 44 participants: 21 autistic children (1 girl, 20 boys) and 23 TD children (1 girl, 22 boys). The autism and TD groups were matched on chronological age (t(42) = −0.35, p = 0.729). The study procedures were approved by the local research ethics committee and written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardian of each participant.

Participants' nonverbal IQ (NVIQ) was assessed using the Combined Raven's Progressive Matrices. Social responsiveness was measured using the validated Chinese version of the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS; Cen et al. 2017), completed by parents based on the child's behavior over the past 6 months. Autistic traits in the TD group were assessed using the Short Self‐reported Adolescent Version of Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ‐SSAV) (Hu 2021) to screen for possible ASD, and none scored above the cutoff for suspected ASD. Receptive language was evaluated using the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test‐Revised (PPVT‐R) (Sang and Liao 1990).

Given that participants in the autism group had significant language challenges, assessment designed for early stages of language development were employed to estimate their expressive language abilities. The assessment was adapted from the “Language Ability Test for Preschool Children (18–36 Months)” developed by the Tianjin Normal University Institute of Linguistics (2012) to measure expressive language at four levels: real words, nonwords, phrases, and sentences. During the test, participants were required to repeat the words or sentences spoken by the examiner. For the real word, nonword, and phrase levels, scores were computed by summing the repetition completion score (1/0), syllable accuracy (0%–100%), and phoneme accuracy (0%–100%). At the sentence level, the scoring included four components: completion (1/0), core syntactic elements (1/0), error reverse scoring (0–3), and syllable accuracy (0%–100%). The core syntactic elements of the current sentence items were identified using the Language Technology Platform (LTP) for Chinese natural language processing (Che et al. 2021), which annotates core words and their directly related syntactic constituents. A sentence repetition received 1 point if all core elements were reproduced accurately and in the correct order. The error reverse scoring was: 3 points for fully accurate repetition, 2 for one error, 1 for two or three errors, and 0 for four or more errors. The maximum scores for each level were as follows: real words—108 points, nonwords—9 points, phrases—27 points, and sentences—92 points. The maximum total score for the expressive language assessment was 236 points, with higher scores indicating better expressive language abilities.

TD group scored significantly higher than the autism group on NVIQ (t(42) = 6.68, p < 0.001), receptive language (t(42) = 14.25, p < 0.001), and expressive language (t(42) = 4.62, p < 0.001). The autism group was significantly higher on the SRS; all individuals scored above the suggested cutoff for ASD (t(42) = −12.56, p < 0.001). Stimuli

Temporal Gap Detection Task

Two types of stimuli were created: a continuous 1000‐ms Gaussian noise and a 1000‐ms Gaussian noise containing a silent gap at the temporal midpoint of the stimulus (Figure 1). The ISI within each trial was fixed at 1000 ms. Stimulus intensity was RMS standardized at 70 dB SPL. The initial gap duration was 50 ms—a duration that is perceptually salient for young children.

Illustrations of auditory stimuli in the frequency modulation (FM) detection and gap detection tasks.

FM Detection Task

This task employed frequency‐modulated tonal stimuli. By manipulating the frequency deviation (Δf) within the modulated signal (Figure 1), it allowed for the measurement of participants' frequency discrimination thresholds. Each trial involved a comparison between a 1000‐ms unmodulated pure tone and a 1000‐ms FM tone, both centered at a carrier frequency of 1000 Hz. The FM signal was sinusoidally modulated at 2 Hz. The parameters align with prior studies (Boets et al. 2015; Moore and Sek 1995), which primarily engage sensitivity to temporal fine structure (TFS)—rapid fluctuations in the phase of the waveform that support fine‐grained frequency discrimination (Moore and Ernst 2012). The initial Δf of the FM signal was 100 Hz.

Procedure

Temporal Gap Detection Task

Participants completed the task individually in a quiet room with the support of a trained experimenter. The task employed a 2AFC paradigm. Each trial presented two noise stimuli in random order—one continuous and one with a gap—separated by an ISI of 1000 ms. Participants were asked to identify which one contained a gap. To enhance comprehension and engagement, a cartoon‐based visual aid was incorporated into the task interface: the two sounds were represented by two cartoon televisions, with the gapped sound associated with a “broken television.” Children were asked to select the broken television based on what they heard. A two‐down, one‐up adaptive staircase procedure was used to adjust the gap duration between trials (Levitt 1971; Soranzo and Grassi 2014). The initial gap duration was set at 50 ms. Step sizes were scaled multiplicatively: the first two reversals employed a 1.5 factor, and the subsequent eight used a 1.2 factor. The final threshold was defined as the mean gap duration across the last four reversal points.

The task comprised a training phase and a testing phase. During training, the experimenter introduced the task rules using slides and demonstration videos. Children were told they would hear two TV sounds—one “normal” (continuous noise) and one “broken” (gapped noise)—and learn to identify the broken TV by its sound (Figure 2). The experimenter introduced this concept by playing each sound while clicking on the corresponding TV image, repeating this pairing to build stable associations. In guided practice trials, children heard two sounds and pointed to the one that was broken. Gaps were set at 50 ms to ensure success, and correct responses were reinforced with visual rewards aligned with autistic interests. To proceed to the test phase, participants had to correctly identify the gapped stimulus in at least six out of eight trials, with gap durations randomly drawn from 30, 40, or 50 ms. In the test phase, each trial displayed an on‐screen prompt and played the auditory stimuli. Participants were required to select the cartoon television corresponding to the gapped stimulus. Each child completed two runs of the task, and the final gap detection threshold was calculated as the average across the two runs.

Trial procedure of the psychoacoustic tasks. Participants heard two auditory stimuli and indicated via button press which stimulus contained a temporal gap/FM. Detection thresholds were determined using a two‐down, one‐up adaptive staircase procedure.

FM Detection Task

The FM detection task followed a similar structure to the gap detection task, employing a 2AFC paradigm with a one‐down, two‐up adaptive staircase procedure. The initial Δf was 100 Hz. As in the gap task, the threshold was determined as the mean of the last four reversals in the adaptive sequence. In each trial, participants heard two tones: a steady pure tone and a modulated tone, presented in random order with an ISI of 1000 ms. Participants were asked to identify the modulated tone, using a visually supported interface similar to the gap detection task. In this case, the two sounds were represented by two cartoon robots, with the modulated tone corresponding to the “broken robot.”

During training, participants learned to distinguish between the pure and modulated tones with the help of visual demonstrations following similar procedures as the gap detection task (Figure 2). The formal test mirrored the structure of the gap detection task: two runs were administered, and the final FM detection threshold was calculated as the average of both runs.

Statistical Analysis

First, independent‐samples t‐tests were conducted to compare the gap detection thresholds and FM detection thresholds between the autism and TD groups. Then, to enable direct comparison between performance on the temporal (gap detection) and spectral (FM detection) tasks, both sets of threshold data were normalized (Figure 3). This step was necessary because the two tasks were measured on different scales (milliseconds vs. Hertz). Specifically, the TGD threshold was converted into a Temporal Gap Detection Index (TGDI) by dividing each individual's threshold by the adult reference threshold of 2.5 ms (Formby and Muir 1989). A TGDI value below 1 indicates better temporal resolution than the adult benchmark, while a value above 1 indicates inferior to adult‐level. Higher index values reflect higher detection thresholds and thus lower temporal resolution. Similarly, the FM detection threshold was converted into a Frequency Modulation Detection Index (FMDI) by dividing each individual's threshold by the adult reference threshold of 2 Hz (Grose and Mamo 2012). A value below 1 indicates superior spectral resolution compared to adults, while a value above 1 suggests poorer resolution relative to adults. Higher index values correspond to higher FM detection thresholds and thus lower spectral resolution. An Auditory Bias Index (ABI) was then computed by dividing each participant's TGDI by their FMDI. This approach produces theoretically interpretable indices that enable cross‐domain and cross‐individual comparisons, which quantifies the relative dominance of spectral versus temporal processing. A value near 1 indicates balanced development across temporal and spectral domains. An ABI greater than 1 indicates a relative advantage in spectral over temporal processing (i.e., a spectral bias), whereas an ABI less than 1 suggests a temporal processing bias. Finally, Bayesian correlation analyses were conducted to explore the relationships between auditory processing indices (gap threshold, FM threshold, ABI) and other individual‐level variables, including IQ, receptive language, expressive language, and social responsiveness scores in each group. This approach is particularly well‐suited for small samples and evaluates evidence through posterior distributions rather than through repeated significance tests. Consequently, inference is based on the strength of evidence (Bayes Factor, BF10) for each association rather than p values, reducing the need for post hoc corrections for multiple comparisons (Gelman et al. 2012; Wagenmakers et al. 2018).

Results

Group Comparisons of Detection Thresholds

Independent‐samples t‐tests showed that the TD group had significantly lower gap detection thresholds than the autism group, t(42) = −6.98, p < 0.001, indicating that autistic children required longer temporal gaps to reliably detect discontinuities (Figure 4; Table 2). In contrast, for FM detection, the TD group exhibited significantly higher thresholds than the autism group, t(42) = 4.86, p < 0.001, suggesting that autistic children were able to detect smaller Δf than their peers (Figure 4; Table 2). These findings suggest that autistic children exhibit reduced temporal but enhanced spectral auditory resolution compared to TD peers.

Both FM detection threshold and temporal gap detection threshold differed between groups, with the autism group performing better (p < 0.001) at FM but poorer (p < 0.001) at gap detection compared to the TD group.

Auditory Temporal–Spectral Processing Bias

This section further evaluates the hypothesis that autistic children exhibit a relative bias favoring spectral over temporal processing. For TGD, both groups showed TGDI values greater than 1 (Figure 5). The TD group did not differ significantly from 1 (t(44) = 1.54, p = 0.130), indicating performance comparable to adult reference. In contrast, the autism group showed a TGDI significantly greater than 1 (t(40) = 7.69, p < 0.001), indicating performance poorer than adult reference. These results suggest that the TD children had largely reached adult‐level temporal resolution, whereas the autistic children remained developmentally delayed in this domain.

Autistic children showed significantly lower FMIs than TD peers (upper left), suggesting better spectral processing. Conversely, their TGIs were significantly higher (upper right), reflecting poorer temporal processing. The autism group exhibited significantly higher ABIs (bottom left), indicating a reversed bias between temporal and spectral processing favoring the spectral aspect of sounds. Density distributions of ABI (bottom right) further illustrate this shift in bias and show minimal overlap between groups.

In the FM detection task, both groups had FMDI values above 1 (Figure 5), with significant deviation from adult norms (TD, t(44) = 7.48, p < 0.001; autism, t(40) = 3.03, p < 0.01). The results indicate that although neither group had yet achieved adult‐level performance, the autistic children were approaching adult levels more closely than their TD peers.

Finally, group comparisons of the ABI—calculated as the ratio of TGDI to FMDI—revealed a striking divergence in processing profiles. The TD group exhibited an ABI of 0.58, whereas the autism group showed a substantially higher value of 1.69, a difference that was highly significant (t(42) = −7.98, p < 0.001; Figure 5). These findings indicate that the two groups exhibited opposite auditory processing biases: autistic children showed a relative preference for spectral over temporal information (ABI > 1), while TD children demonstrated a bias toward fine‐grained temporal processing (ABI < 1).

Correlational Results

This section reports Bayesian correlation analyses between psychoacoustic measures and cognitive‐linguistic variables. Evidence strength was interpreted following commonly used guidelines, with Bayes Factor (BF10) values greater than 3 taken to indicate at least moderate evidence for an association (Kass and Raftery 2012; Wagenmakers et al. 2018). Associations showing meaningful evidential support (BF10 > 3) are reported here with the posterior mean correlation coefficient (ρ) and its 95% credible interval (CI). For measures associated with age, partial correlations were conducted controlling for age, and only associations that remained supported are highlighted. A summary of all correlation values is presented in the supporting information (Table S1).

Gap Detection

In the autism group, gap detection thresholds were negatively correlated with age (ρ = −0.41, BF10 = 5.64, 95% credible interval [−0.69, −0.04]; Figure 6), indicating that auditory temporal resolution improves with age in autistic children. In contrast, age was not correlated with gap detection performance in the TD group, suggesting that TD children's temporal resolution may have reached maturity within the studied age range.

Scatter plots illustrating the associations between age and psychoacoustic performance, as well as between psychoacoustic performance and receptive language.

Among measures of NVIQ, ASD symptomology, and language abilities, a strong negative correlation was found between gap detection thresholds and receptive language scores in the autism group (ρ = −0.48, BF10 = 14.65, 95% CI [−0.74, −0.15]; Figure 6). This relationship remained supported after controlling for age (ρ = −0.38, BF10 = 3.60, 95% CI [−0.67, 0.00]). These findings suggest that autistic children with better auditory temporal resolution tend to exhibit more advanced language skills. No evidence of associations was observed in the TD group.

FM Detection

FM detection thresholds were significantly correlated with age in both groups (autism: ρ = −0.45, BF10 = 8.83, 95% CI [−0.72, −0.11]; TD: ρ = −0.44, BF10 = 7.67, 95% CI [−0.70, −0.08]; Figure 6), indicating a developmental improvement of spectral resolution in both TD and autistics. As a result, partial correlations were computed to assess relationships between threshold and other variables while controlling for age. In the autism group, thresholds were negatively correlated with NVIQ (ρ = −0.45, BF10 = 8.98, 95% CI [−0.72, −0.09]) and receptive language ability (ρ = −0.37, BF10 = 3.63, 95% CI [−0.67, −0.02]). These results support the link between better spectral resolution and more advanced cognitive and language abilities in autistics. No evidence for correlation was observed for the TD group aside from the aforementioned age effect.

No correlation was observed between gap detection and FM detection thresholds in either group (autism: ρ = 0.14, BF10 = 0.61, 95% CI [−0.25, 0.48]; TD: ρ = 0.12, BF10 = 0.55, 95% CI [−0.25, 0.46]), suggesting that the temporal and spectral measures reflect largely independent dimensions of auditory processing.

ABI

The ABI reflecting relative bias in spectral versus temporal processing was positively correlated with age in the TD group (ρ = 0.35, BF10 = 3.12, 95% CI [−0.00, 0.64]), suggesting that as TD children grow older, their auditory processing becomes more spectro‐temporally balanced. While ABI was initially correlated with receptive language in the TD group (ρ = 0.47, BF10 = 14.14, 95% CI [−0.05, 0.62]), this association disappeared after controlling for age. No evidence of age effect was observed in the autism group, implying that the atypical spectral bias may not change with development. ABI in the autism group showed no evidence of correlations with language or cognition.

Discussion

By employing two parallel psychoacoustic paradigms in a homogeneous sample, the present study provides direct evidence of a reversed auditory processing bias in autistic children compared to their TD peers. Autistic children with early language delay and autistic manifestations, and without overt “syndromic” associated conditions, exhibited impaired temporal resolution, as reflected in elevated gap detection thresholds, alongside enhanced spectral resolution, as indicated by lower FM detection thresholds. This dissociation was quantitatively captured by the ABI, which revealed a marked preference for spectral over temporal processing in the autism group—a pattern opposite to that observed in TD children. Spectral resolution was positively associated with receptive language and nonverbal cognitive abilities in the autism group. Unlike their TD counterparts, autistic children showed no age‐related modulation of this processing bias, suggesting that the observed spectral preference may be developmentally stable or that this modulation, if any, occurs later in the autistic group.

Reduced Temporal Resolution in Autistic Children

A primary finding of this study is that autistic children exhibit poorer auditory temporal processing compared to their TD peers. Specifically, the autism group showed elevated gap detection thresholds, a result that aligns with our initial hypothesis and previous findings (Bhatara et al. 2013; Foss‐Feig et al. 2017). Additional evidence for differences in temporal processing comes from studies using the TOJ task, which requires individuals to determine the sequential order of two rapidly presented sounds. Autistic individuals often require longer ISIs to accurately identify the order of sounds (Chan et al. 2023; Stevenson et al. 2014). Notably, although autistic children demonstrate deficits in auditory gap detection and TOJ tasks, their performance on visual gap detection and visual TOJ tasks is generally intact (Foss‐Feig et al. 2017; Kwakye et al. 2011). This suggests that the observed temporal processing challenges are not due to a general impairment in timing perception, but rather reflect a modality‐specific pattern within the auditory domain—a visual versus auditory dissociation profile that distinguishes autism from other neurodevelopmental conditions, such as developmental dyslexia (Meilleur et al. 2020).

In the current study, gap detection thresholds in the autism group showed a negative correlation with age, indicating continued developmental improvements during the school years. This age‐related pattern was not observed in the TD group, whose performance closely resembled the adult benchmark (TGDI ≈1), consistent with findings that auditory temporal resolution typically matures by 7 years of age (Shinn et al. 2009). These results suggest that while auditory temporal acuity in TD children stabilizes early, its development in autism may follow a more protracted trajectory.

In the autism group, lower gap detection thresholds were associated with better receptive language, even after controlling for age. This finding is consistent with prior research indicating that elevated temporal thresholds are associated with greater difficulties in speech perception and comprehension (Bhatara et al. 2013; Foss‐Feig et al. 2017). Differences in auditory temporal sensitivity may have downstream consequences on higher‐order language and social functions, especially in complex listening environments. For instance, Groen et al. (2009) found significantly poorer “dip‐listening” abilities in autism—the ability to understand speech in noisy environments by taking advantage of brief, quiet gaps in background noise, indicative of an insufficient capacity to access rapid temporal cues. Neurophysiological data also support this link as Foss‐Feig et al. (2018) found that autistic children exhibited attenuated P2 amplitudes in response to near‐threshold auditory gaps, and this neural response was significantly associated with greater social communication symptoms.

Enhanced Spectral Resolution in Autistic Children

A second major finding is that autistic children demonstrated superior auditory spectral resolution relative to their TD peers evidenced by the lower FM detection thresholds. This enhancement aligns with prior reports of heightened pitch discrimination in autism (Bonnel et al. 2010, 2003; Heaton, Hudry, et al. 2008; Järvinen‐Pasley, Wallace, et al. 2008; Yu et al. 2015). Spectral resolution in the auditory system is supported by two complementary neurophysiological mechanisms. The first is the place mechanism, which relies on the tonotopic organization of the cochlear basilar membrane and auditory cortex and involves frequency‐specific resonance patterns corresponding to the spectral content of incoming sounds. The second is the phase‐locking mechanism, which involves the temporal alignment of neural excitation patterns with the fine temporal structure of the sound waveform. In humans, pitch perception at low frequencies (below ~4 kHz) is predominantly mediated by phase‐locking, whereas at higher frequencies it depends on place mechanisms (Moore and Sek 1996). The FM stimuli in the present task fell within the low‐frequency range; thus, the observed enhancement likely reflects increased sensitivity in phase‐based spectral processing in autistic children.

This pattern of results partially replicates prior findings. For example, Bhatara et al. (2013) found no group differences between autistic and TD adolescents in frequency discrimination at 500 Hz and 1000 Hz—frequencies relying on phase‐locking. However, this pattern contrasts with Boets et al. (2015) who observed poorer performance in the ASD group under similar low‐frequency conditions (500 Hz). However, the use of cognitively demanding paradigms, such as 3AFC tasks and variable reference tone for discrimination, may complicate perceptual performance with executive function limitations. Variations in sample composition, such as the inclusion of more heterogeneous autism phenotypes, may obscure the observation of enhanced spectral processing abilities. For example, Bonnel et al. (2010) reported enhanced pitch sensitivity only in autistic individuals with early language delay, but not in those with Asperger's syndrome. Given that our study focused on the autistic phenotype with delayed speech onset, the observed spectral advantage might be more prevalent in this subgroup—which happens to correspond to the autistic children diagnosed with the highest clinical certainty (Rødgaard et al. 2024).

In both groups, FM thresholds improved with age. In the autism but not TD group, lower (better) detection thresholds were positively correlated with cognitive and language abilities, even after controlling for age. This suggests that enhanced spectral processing in autism may have an intricate relationship with higher‐order, transmodal abilities. In the broader literature, heightened pitch perception in autism has often been characterized as unrelated to, or detrimental to language development (Eigsti and Fein 2013; Hisaizumi and Tantam 2024; Rotschafer 2021). Several studies have argued that this enhanced sensitivity might hinder the development of native phonological representations (Wang et al. 2017; Yu et al. 2015). In contrast, our finding is consistent with the possibility that enhanced pitch perception may represent an intrinsic strength that facilitates unconventional language learning pathways, hereby supporting Dumont et al.'s (2024) interpretation of pitch processing atypicalities in autism.

One striking example of unconventional language learning pathways is “unexpected bilingualism” or UB, a phenomenon describing the spontaneous acquisition of a foreign language through nonsocial channels, typically not used in daily communication or directly taught by others. Notably, all documented cases of UB involve autistic individuals and span diverse languages (Dumont et al. 2024; Kadiri and Anasse 2023; Kissine et al. 2018; Vulchanova et al. 2012; Zhukova et al. 2023). Gagnon et al. (2025) recently demonstrated that in a large sample comprising 53% minimally verbal autistic children, these children were 4.38 times more likely than TD children to show UB, defined as familiarity with at least one language for letters and numbers not identified as part of the languages spoken in the child's social environment. Dumont et al. (2024) reported that autistic individuals with unexpected bilingual abilities performed significantly better in pure‐tone discrimination tasks than both autistic individuals without such abilities and TD controls, despite having lower scores in native language assessments. Based on this, the authors proposed that enhanced pitch perception—reflecting enhanced perceptual precision—may support language learning in autistic individuals by enabling precise tracking of prosodic and phonological regularities in constrained, nonsocial contexts (e.g., screen‐based speech), where reduced input variability and increased repetition facilitate structural encoding without the demands of real‐time social interaction. This interpretation gains further support from recent EEG data: acoustically exaggerated speech sounds‐ analogous to screen‐based speech‐ significantly improved neural syllable decoding accuracy in autistic toddlers, reaching performance levels comparable to TD children (Yu et al. 2025). Notably, this benefit was more pronounced in the autism group and was complimented by a selective increase of neural responsivity to pitch contour exaggeration, suggesting a greater reliance on spectral features in autistic speech encoding.

Collectively, these findings suggest that enhanced pitch perception may present both costs and benefits in autism. On one hand, it may interfere with the acquisition of socially mediated, real‐time communicative language. On the other hand, it may potentially facilitate alternative language learning pathways through static, noninteractive modalities such as screen‐based speech or digital games that are often preferred and better tolerated by autistic children.

Reversed Auditory Bias and Atypical Hemispheric Asymmetry in Autism

The present study provides the first quantitative, albeit preliminary, evidence of a reversed bias in temporal versus spectral auditory processing among autistic children, as indicated by the ABI. For TD children, auditory timing perception was dominant and reached maturity earlier; by contrast, in autistic children, spectral processing was dominant and more advanced, at least at the level supported by phase‐locking mechanisms. This reversed bias remained stable across school‐age development in the autism group.

The observed profile of enhanced spectral resolution but reduced temporal sensitivity may be related to differences in hemispheric asymmetry in autism (Haesen et al. 2011). In neurotypical populations, auditory processing is functionally lateralized, with the right and left auditory cortices differentially specialized for spectral and temporal features, respectively. By contrast, neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies have reported reduced or reversed lateralization in autistic individuals, with increased reliance on right‐hemispheric processing and diminished specialization in the left hemisphere (Haesen et al. 2011; Li et al. 2023; Yu and Wang 2021). This pattern of atypical hemispheric organization might represent a plausible neural basis for the observed auditory bias in autism. Enhanced right‐hemisphere engagement might underlie enhanced spectral processing, while concomitant left‐hemisphere inefficiencies might be associated with challenges in processing fine temporal features critical for speech and language analysis.

Implications

This study holds significant implications for understanding broader aspects of information processing in autism. Recent theoretical perspectives have advocated for a “shifted hierarchy” framework to conceptualize cognitive processing in individuals with prototypical autism (Bernhardt et al. 2025). Specifically, a shift away from socially biased information processing—and a corresponding enhancement in the autonomy, salience, and precision of perceptual processing—may underlie the broader behavioral, cognitive, computational, and neural features observed in autism. In the present context, a shifted hierarchy in auditory spectral‐temporal processing may be a consequence of a modality‐specific reorganization of cortical processing hierarchies in autism. Such a reversed hierarchical configuration within the auditory system may provide insight into certain autistic savant phenomena, including unexpected bilingualism and exceptional musical abilities.

Moreover, our findings challenge intuitive assumptions by revealing a potential positive association between enhanced spectral processing and language as well as cognitive abilities in the autistic group. This aligns with the hypothesis that auditory hypersensitivity, particularly in the spectral domain, may serve as a compensatory mechanism supporting atypical pathways of language development. For instance, atypical right‐hemispheric dominance in the language network—typically involved in the processing of prosodic and spectral features—may enable some autistic individuals to bypass conventional left‐hemisphere‐driven speech decoding pathways, which rely on temporal integration. Instead, they may engage high‐fidelity pitch analysis to encode linguistic structure in low‐social, highly repetitive, noninteractive contexts, such as screen‐based speech input (Dumont et al. 2024). Such “spectral‐oriented” perceptual bias may simultaneously hinder adaptation to dynamic, socially embedded native language input as an adaptive cost. These findings imply that altered perceptual functioning in autism may not merely impede language acquisition but may also reshape the cognitive architecture supporting alternative learning trajectories.

Limitations

Our study results should be interpreted with caution in light of several limitations. First, to maximize sample homogeneity, the study employed a relatively small sample size of a limited age range. Notably, individuals with Asperger‐like phenotypes—those without language disorders—were excluded. As a result, the findings are specifically applicable to “prototypical” autistic children characterized by delayed or disordered language development, early autistic manifestations (here before 2 years of age), and may not be generalizable to older individuals, those diagnosed later on, or the broad autism spectrum as currently defined. Second, the FM detection task assessed spectral resolution only within the low‐frequency range, which primarily relies on phase‐locking mechanisms, thereby excluding contributions from place‐based processing dominant at higher frequencies. Similarly, the measurement of temporal resolution focused exclusively on gap detection without incorporating other dimensions of temporal auditory processing such as temporal sequencing or order judgment. Finally, while our findings suggest that enhanced spectral and reduced temporal resolution observed in autistic children are associated with language abilities, these associations do not establish causality. It remains possible that the observed perceptual profiles may, at least in part, reflect downstream consequences or “spill‐over” effects of early language delay, such as reduced exposure to temporally complex speech input or increased reliance on nonsocial auditory experiences. These experiential differences could shape auditory system development without necessarily representing causative endophenotypes. Longitudinal studies are needed to disentangle whether potential perceptual biases precede, co‐develop with, or emerge as adaptations to delayed language acquisition.

Conclusions

This study provides the first preliminary psychophysical evidence that children with prototypical autism show enhanced spectral but reduced temporal resolution relative to TD peers. Importantly, this dissociation is consistent with a reversed processing bias: autistic children tend to rely more on pitch‐based than timing‐based cues, opposite to the pattern observed in TD children. The observed positive correlation between pitch sensitivity and language in the autism group supports the hypothesis that enhanced pitch processing may be intricately linked with language development in this group. These findings provide further insights into current understanding of information processing hierarchies in autism, and support the future hypothesis that rather than representing a mere deficit, auditory atypicalities in autism may reflect compensatory mechanisms that shape divergent cognitive and linguistic development.
