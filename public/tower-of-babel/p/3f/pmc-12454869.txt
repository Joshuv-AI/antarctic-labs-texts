Topological optimization method “MMA-BP” for photoacoustic resonator and implementations in ppt-level gas sensor using miniaturized vase-type photoacoustic cells
Authors: Wenzhe Wang, Zhiyu Feng, Yachao Jiang, Jie Zhang, Shiyu Yan, Xiaohong Cao, Ping Lu, Chaotan Sima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA topological optimization method for photoacoustic resonators is proposed as the Method of Moving Asymptotes with Bernstein Polynomials (MMA-BP). This method is demonstrated in a ppt-level ultra-sensitive photoacoustic spectroscopy gas sensor using miniaturized vase-type photoacoustic cell (V-PAC). The V-PAC has a volume of merely 5 mL and achieves a minimum detection limit of 281 ppt for C2H2 with an integration time of 768 s, corresponding to a normalized noise equivalent absorption of 4.46 × 10⁻9∙cm⁻1∙Hz⁻1/2 with a single optical path. It represents an improvement of approximately 14 times beyond that of using the conventional T-type PAC. We experimentally investigated consequent frequency shifts within conventional PACs with thin resonator tubes, and determined the influence of losses on the resonant frequency shift. The geometric contraction effect in vase-type PAC is also investigated. The proposed topological MMA-BP method and implementations provide a universal approach to establish optimized PAC structures for photoacoustic gas detection.Keywords: Photoacoustic spectroscopy, Method of moving asymptotes, Bernstein polynomials, Vase-type resonant photoacoustic cell, Resonant frequency

Introduction

Trace gas detection technology is in wide demand across various fields such as environmental monitoring, power systems, healthcare, and food safety [1], [2], [3], [4]. Currently, common gas detection techniques based on absorption spectroscopy include Tunable Diode Laser Absorption Spectroscopy (TDLAS) [5], [6], [7], [8] and its variant, Light-Induced Thermoelastic Spectroscopy (LITES) [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], Raman Spectroscopy [15], Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) [16], Photoacoustic Spectroscopy (PAS) [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], etc. Compared to other optical gas sensing technologies, PAS has gained widespread attention due to its advantages of high sensitivity, linear amplification with optical sources, no need of photodetectors and immunity to background light interference [25], [26], [27], [28]. Its core principle is based on the photoacoustic effect, where the concentration of a gas is detected by collecting the acoustic waves produced by gas molecules after absorbing modulated light [29], [30].

The sensitivity of a PAS-based gas detection system is generally determined by the hardware of light sources, microphones, and photoacoustic cells (PACs). Essentially, the structural design of the PAC plays a vital role for the photoacoustic effect enhancement. Compared to non-resonant PACs, resonant PACs could effectively amplify generated acoustic signals through the resonance effect, hence significantly improving the detection sensitivity. In 2019, Gong et al. proposed a semi-open T-PAC with a halved resonant frequency than that of a conventional H-PAC [31]. In 2022, Huang et al. proposed a spherical PAC that achieved simultaneous measurement of CO2, H2O, and CH4 using the three radial resonant frequencies [32]. In 2024, Wang et al. proposed a trapezoidal compound ellipsoidal resonant PAC [33]. Wu et al. reported a sphere-cylinder coupled T-PAC, achieving a volume of only 0.8 cm3 and a minimum detection limit (MDL) of methane at 1 ppm [34]. Zhao et al. introduced a hyperbola to design an microcone-curved PAC that achieved an acetylene detection limit of 1.41 ppb with an integration time of 290 s [35]. Feng et al. proposed a T-PAC with a rounded transition cross-section (RT-PAC), which can effectively reduce flow noise and enhance the SNR [36]. In 2025, Zhao et al. reported an embedded T-type multi-pass PAC, achieving an MDL of methane at 0.4 ppb [37]. Chen et al. proposed an E-type photoacoustic cell, which can effectively improve the gas response speed [38]. Over the years, high sensitivity and miniaturization have always been the goals for PACs. Although the literatures have continuously reported versatile PACs to improve performance beyond traditional H-type or T-type PACs, a universal and effect method for optimizing the resonant PAC structure has yet to be investigated.

Topology optimization offers a computational method to achieve optimal structural performance by enabling the optimal distribution of structural materials within the design domain, while considering the loads and boundary conditions [39]. In 1987, Svanberg proposed the Method of Moving Asymptotes (MMA) [40], which has since been widely applied in bridge design and automotive contour design, etc. [41], [42]. Additionally, Bernstein Polynomials were primarily introduced by Bernstein for solving function approximation problems, which also finds implementations in geometry and structural design [43]. Building upon these methodologies, it is feasible to combine and apply these methods for the optimized structural design of PACs.

Here, a ppt-level PAS gas sensor using miniaturized vase-type PAC with MMA-BP optimization was proposed and demonstrated. Inspired by the structural topology optimization, we design the MMA-BP method based on the Method of Moving Asymptotes algorithm and use Bernstein polynomials to construct the boundary of the photoacoustic resonant tube. Hence, a novel and miniaturized Vase-type photoacoustic cell (V-PAC) was obtained for the detection of C2H2, and fabricated by 3-D printing. Experiments show that the V-PAC based gas sensor has an MDL of 281 ppt with an integration time of 768 s, and an NNEA of approximately 4.46 × 10−9 W∙cm−1∙Hz−1/2 with a single optical path. Simulation and experimental results verified that the overall performance of the V-PAC is improved by about 14 times compared to a conventional T-PAC without the MMA-BP optimization. Furthermore, we investigated and experimentally verified the variation behavior of resonant frequency and acoustic signals with the changed resonator tube radius for T-type and H-type photoacoustic cells. It is found that the losses of acoustics waves propagating longitudinally dominate the resonant frequency shift in such PACs, while the acoustic waves benefit from the geometric contraction effect in the proposed V-PAC. This technique provides a universal and effective approach for the structural optimization for resonant PACs to enhance PAS-based gas sensing.

Photoacoustic simulation of the designed PAC

Photoacoustic simulation of the T-PACs

To study the photoacoustic performance of the V-PAC, simulations based on finite element analysis were conducted and compared with the T-PACs. The thermoviscous acoustics module was used to simulate the photoacoustic effect. A frequency response scan was performed on the final optimized V-PAC obtained after 35 iterations, the initial T-PAC, a trapezoidal photoacoustic cell (TR-PAC) obtained after 5 optimization iterations, similar to the design in literature [52], and a microcone-curved photoacoustic cell (MCR-PAC) obtained after 10 optimization iterations [35]. These results that coincide remarkably with the abovementioned literatures strongly validate the correctness of this MMA-BP approach.

As shown in Fig. 3, the scanning range of the resonant frequency for the V-PAC was set between 6250 and 7750 Hz, 3250–4750 Hz for the T-PAC, 4750–6250 Hz for the TR-PAC and 5250–6750 Hz for the MCR-PAC, with a frequency scanning step of 2.5 Hz comprehensively. Fig. 3(a), (b), (c), and (d) represent the acoustic pressure distributions at the resonant frequency for the T-PAC, TR-PAC, MCR-PAC, and the V-PAC, respectively. The results show that the maximum acoustic pressure of the V-PAC is approximately 3 times stronger than that of the standard T-PAC, 2.25 times than that of the TR-PAC, and 1.77 times than that of the MCR-PAC, respectively. Fig. 3(e) shows the frequency response curves of the obtained four PACs. The peak resonant frequencies are 4023 Hz for the T-PAC, 5634 Hz for the TR-PAC, 5978 Hz for the MCR-PAC, and 7089 Hz for the V-PAC. During the optimization process, the shape of the resonator tube tends to exhibit the trapezoidal and microcone-curved design in literatures [35], [52] and the Vase-type geometry ultimately.

Comparison of simulation acoustic pressure distribution for different PAC shapes during the optimization process. (a) The initial standard T-PAC; (b) TR-PAC [52]; (c) MCR-PAC [35]; (d) Finally optimized V-PAC; (e) Frequency responses of the four PACs.

To be noted, the obtained V-PAC should be physically realizable with the minimum resonant tube radius. If without pre-setting radius constraints on the initial and terminal facets of the resonator tube, the initial facet would become wider while the terminal facet would become thinner than 0.5 mm. This would not only hinder the laser beam, but also degrade the acoustic amplification effect. This is because an inadequate neck or terminal radius of the V-PAC significantly increases loss in that region, which diminishes the acoustic amplification effect of the geometric contraction. Conversely, an overly large initial radius of the resonator tube increases its volume, thereby reducing the photoacoustic signal principally.

Experimental setup

The PAS gas sensing experiment was conducted for C2H2 detection, employing wavelength modulation and second-harmonic detection (WMS-2f) to improve the SNR. An original T-PAC and the optimized V-PAC, both consistent with the designed geometry, were fabricated using the 3-D printing setup (Formlabs, Form 4) with high-sensitivity photosensitive resin. The manufacturing error of the 3-D printing setup is ±50 microns, ensuring low-cost fabrication and sufficiently precision. The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 5.

Schematic diagram of PAS gas detection system based on V-PAC.

According to the HITRAN database [53], C2H2 exhibits a strong absorption peak around 1530.37 nm (6534.37 cm−1). Therefore, a 1530.3 nm DFB laser (GYSCIENTECH) with a power of 14 mW was used as the excitation source to excite the photoacoustic signal of C2H2. An arbitrary waveform generator (AWG, RIGOL DG812) outputs a low-frequency triangular wave (20 mHz) superimposed with a high-frequency sine wave (half of the designed PAC resonant frequency) to the laser driver. The laser driver modulates the laser's output wavelength by changing the temperature and voltage bias, set to 19.9 °C and 2.27 V, respectively. An EDFA (Max-ray Photonics, EDFA-C-BA-23-B) amplifies the laser's output power to enhance detection accuracy. The output beam passes through a collimator to reduce its divergence angle and enters the V-PAC through an optical window. C2H2 gas molecules absorb the light and periodically release heat within the PAC, generating an acoustic signal. After resonant amplifications, the photoacoustic signal is captured by a low-cost MEMS microphone fixed at the far end of the resonator tube. A gas blender (GB, MCQ GB100) is used to mix gases of different concentrations and flow rates. A lock-in amplifier (LIA, Zurich Instruments, MFLI 500 kHz) with a second-order filter demodulates the acoustic signal from the microphone. At 1 s integration time, its equivalent noise bandwidth (ENBW) is 0.125 Hz.

Results and discussion

Modulation depth optimization

The amplitude of the 2 f harmonic signal is optimized by changing the modulation depth of the driving voltage. By optimizing the modulation depth, the maximum 2 f signal could be obtained. As shown in Fig. 6, the trend of the 2 f signal amplitude of C2H2 in the V-PAC is displayed as the amplitude of the 5619.5 Hz sine wave varies from 20 mV to 300 mV. It is observed that the 2 f signal amplitude firstly increases and then decreases with the rising modulation voltage. The optimal modulation depth was selected as 122.5 mV for the DFB laser.

Performance evaluation

The frequency responses, harmonics and sensitivity are measured and evaluated in the experiments. Fig. 8(a) shows the frequency response curves of the V-PAC and the T-PAC. Standard 1000 ppm of C2H2 mixed with N2 was passed into the V-PAC and the T-PAC, respectively. The temperature of the PACs was kept to 25 °C to prevent resonant frequency shifts. All measurements were performed under the atmospheric pressure (1 atm). By varying the laser modulation frequency and recording the amplitude of the photoacoustic signal generated by each PAC, their frequency response curves were obtained. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the resonant frequency of the V-PAC is 11.239 kHz, and that of the T-PAC is 3.146 kHz. It can be observed that the actual resonant frequencies of both PACs deviate significantly from the simulation results. For the V-PAC in particular, the resonant frequency shifted by nearly 50 %. A similar issue of resonant frequency shift was also observed in reference [35], but the reason was not explained. We subsequently conducted further investigations on this issue.

To evaluate the performance improvement of the V-PAC compared to the conventional T-PAC, the standard 1000 C2H2 sample gas are employed and mixed. It is indicated that the sensitivity of the V-PAC is about 3 times that of the T-PAC, which basically agrees with the simulation.

Noise figure analysis

To analyze the noise figures of the experiments, pure N2 was injected into each PAC at a flow rate of 100 mL/min, and noise data was continuously collected for a period of 30 min. As shown in Fig. 9(a) and (b), the noise standard deviations (1σ) of the V-PAC and the T-PAC are 165.2 nV and 288.2 nV, respectively. It is evident that the noise of the V-PAC is much smaller than that of the T-PAC. To analyze the reason for the lower noise, the background noise of each PAC was measured again at the resonant frequency of the other PAC. As shown in Fig. 9(c) and (d), the 1σ noise figure of the T-PAC at 11.239 kHz is about 160 nV, similar to that of the V-PAC at its resonant frequency of 11.239 kHz, while the 1σ noise of the V-PAC at 3.146 kHz is 207.4nV, almost 30 % suppressed than that of the T-PAC at its resonant frequency of 3.146 kHz.

Comparative experiments of the noise figure for the T-PAC (blue dots) and the V-PAC (red dots). (a) Background noise of the T-PAC at its own resonant frequency of 3.146 kHz; (b) Background noise of the V-PAC at its own resonant frequency of 11.239 kHz; (c) Background noise of the T-PAC at 11.239 kHz; (d) Background noise of the V-PAC at 3.146 kHz.

The reasons for the improved noise figure of the V-PAC are discussed here. Firstly, the high resonant frequency reduces external background noise, which is widely acknowledged. Secondly, the smooth and rounded-like transition between the resonator tube and buffer contributes to the suppression of noise figure, and similar noise reduction phenomena were reported in literatures [35], [36]. Consequently, the MDLs for the V-PAC and the T-PAC are 19.3 ppb and 96.7 ppb, respectively, showing 5 times improvement with an integration time of 1 s.

Allan deviation analysis was further performed to evaluate the detection limit and long-term stability of the established sensors. As shown in Fig. 10(a) and (b), with Allan deviation analysis, the V-PAC achieved an MDL of 581 ppt with an integration time of 768 s, while the T-PAC had an MDL of 8.1 ppb with an integration time of 806 s. This represents a 14-fold reduction in MDL for the V-PAC relative to the T-PAC.

Influence of resonator tube radius on resonant frequency shift

To investigate the significant resonant frequency shifts in the PACs, we further conducted experiments on T-type and H-type PACs with varying resonator tube radii. According to Eq. (2), the resonant frequencies of PACs are mainly determined by the length of resonant tubes, and this concept was implemented in literatures for multi-resonant PAS sensors [20], [21]. Simulations and theoretical analysis illustrate that changes in the resonator tube radius have negligible effects on resonant frequencies of T-type and H-type PACs, as shown in Fig. 12 (b) and (d). Nevertheless, experiments revealed that excessively decreased resonator tube radii in T-type and H-type PACs would bring significant deviations of resonant frequency from simulation, and this phenomenon was further investigated here.

Analysis of the measured influence of resonator tube radius on resonant frequency. (a) Frequency response of the T-PAC with resonator tube radii ranging from 1.5 mm to 3 mm; (b) Comparison of simulated and experimental resonant frequencies versus resonator tube radius for the T-PAC; (c) Frequency response of the H-PAC with resonator tube radii ranging from 1.5 mm to 3 mm; (d) Comparison of simulated and experimental resonant frequencies versus resonator tube radius for the H-PAC.

As indicated by Eq. (3), reducing the resonant tube radius increases the surface loss (1/Qs), thereby leading to an increase in the energy loss (1/Q). This increase in energy loss (1/Q) leads to the slowed effective acoustic velocity and hence causes a decrease in the resonant frequency f0. The measured frequency responses for T-type and H-type PACs with different resonator tube radii are plotted in Fig. 12(a) and (c), respectively. Fig. 12(b) and (d) show the curves of the experimental and simulated resonant frequencies versus the resonator tube radius for T-type and H-type PACs, respectively. The experimental results are consistent with our hypothesis. In experiments, for both H-type and T-type PACs, as the resonator tube radius decreases, the resonant frequency gradually decreases, while the acoustic signal increases due to the reduced resonator volume. Since the H-PAC has two buffers with minor acoustic pressure, acoustic pressure leakage is less likely, hence the resonant frequency shift is slightly smaller than that of the T-PAC.

Simultaneously, it is observed that even a T-PAC with a small resonator tube of 1.5 mm radius, whose volume is smaller than that of the obtained V-PAC, still exhibits a significantly 60 % weaker acoustic pressure compared to the V-PAC. Furthermore, when the resonant frequency is decreased, the background noise increases, leading to a degraded sensing performance. This compassion further highlights the benefits of the proposed V-PAC.

Conclusion

In summary, we proposed a universal optimization method for photoacoustic resonant cells based on the topological MMA-BP method, obtaining a high-sensitivity Vase-type PAC. The acoustic pressure amplitudes of different PAC shapes obtained during the optimization process were also simulated and compared. The acoustic pressure continuously increased with the iterative optimization, and the final optimized V-PAC achieved the maximum acoustic pressure. In comparative experiments for C2H2 detection, the V-PAC exhibited not only a superior signal but also minor noise than the conventional T-PAC. With an integration time of 1 s, the MDL of the V-PAC was reduced by a factor of 5 compared to the original T-PAC. With an integration time of 768 s, the V-PAC achieved an MDL of 281 ppt for C2H2. Compared to the original T-PAC, the MDL was reduced by nearly 14 times. The NNEA also reached 4.46 × 10−9 W∙cm−1∙Hz−1/2, with a volume of only 5.35 mL and single path. If higher power lasers or more sensitive microphones were used with multi-pass cells, the MDL could be further reduced to few ppt-levels. Additionally, we investigated the phenomena of varied resonant frequency shift in different PACs with thin resonant tubes. The mechanism of the actual resonant frequency deviations in experiments is also explained. This work provides a universal approach for the optimized design of PACs, and offers an innovative enhancement solution for PAS-based trace gas detection.
