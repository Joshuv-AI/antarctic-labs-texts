Synergistic dust suppression using inorganic salt and anionic nonionic complex system
Authors: Tian Wang, Baoyong Zhang, Ximing Liu, Jinghao Lv, Ningjing Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study examines the synergistic effect of an inorganic salt and an anionic nonionic surfactant system on the wettability and moisture retention of bituminous coal dust. Wettability and evaporation behavior were characterized using sedimentation time, contact angle, surface tension, and evaporation loss. The optimized ternary formulation markedly improved wetting performance, reducing the contact angle to 16.17° and shortening the settling time to 6.23 s. Although the salt-free system exhibited slightly lower final evaporation loss, the salt-containing system achieved superior overall performance by coupling rapid wetting with enhanced moisture retention. Mechanistic analysis indicates that the inorganic salt strengthens interfacial interactions and regulates evaporation dynamics. These findings provide a practical strategy for sustained dust suppression and improved secondary dust control in coal mining environments.

Introduction

Bituminous coal is the most widely distributed and abundantly available coal type in nature. In 2023, China’s raw coal production reached 4.65 billion tonnes, of which bituminous coal accounted for approximately 44.09%.1 According to national statistics, a total of 12,087 new cases of occupational diseases were reported in 2023, representing an increase of 8.81% compared to 11,108 cases in 2022. Among these, 8,051 cases were identified as occupational pneumoconiosis, reflecting a 6.26% increase from 7,577 cases in the previous year and accounting for 66.61% of all reported occupational diseases.2,3 These data highlight the persistent and severe impact of dust exposure in coal mining environments. Furthermore, with the progressive transition of coal mining to deeper seams and the rapid development of intelligent mining technologies, dust generation in confined spaces such as fully mechanized mining faces has increased substantially. This exacerbates the risk of coal workers’ pneumoconiosis and poses a major challenge to occupational health protection, thereby significantly constraining the advancement of the “Healthy China” initiative.

At present, dust suppression in coal mines is primarily achieved through water spraying or surfactant solutions.4,5 Considerable research efforts have been devoted, both domestically and internationally, to improving the wettability of surfactant-treated dust. Gan. et al.6 systematically evaluated the effects of four surfactants on the wettability of bituminous coal, revealing the following order of effectiveness: fast-percolating ALES (ammonium lauryl ether sulfate)> SLES (sodium lauryl ether sulfate)> TD (dodecyl triethanolamine salfate) > SDS (sodium dodecyl sulfate). Meng. et al.7,8 combined atomic force microscopy (AFM) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to reveal that sodium dodecylbenzene sulfonate (SDBS) molecules adsorb onto coal surfaces through hydrophobic interactions between alkyl chains and coal organic matter, while hydrophilic groups orient toward water, forming a stable wetting layer. This interfacial arrangement enhances coal-water interactions, particularly through functional groups such as hydroxyls and amine oxides. Wang and Jiang (2020) systematically investigated the performance enhancement of octyl phenol ethoxylate (OP-10) surfactant solutions through experiments on surface tension, reverse osmosis behavior, water retention, and spray dust suppression efficiency. The results demonstrated that when the concentration of the OP-10 solution is below its critical micelle concentration (CMC), the addition of inorganic salt additives has a discernible effect on the surface tension of the solution.9 A novel inorganic salt composite solution composed of SDBS, the nonionic surfactant polyoxyethylene octylphenyl ether (Triton X-100), and Na2SO4 was prepared. Based on analyses of surface tension, contact angle, sedimentation time, wetting rate, as well as changes in the microstructure and functional groups of the coal surface, the wetting behavior of the solution toward coal dust and its adsorption capacity on coal particle surfaces were investigated. The results demonstrate that the inorganic salt composite solution significantly enhances the wettability of coal samples.10 By investigating the synergistic effects of inorganic salts and magnetic fields on the wettability of water, the efficiency of dust suppression can be enhanced. Based on the composition of water from the Chongtai Coal Mine, four inorganic salts—sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), sodium sulfate (Na2CO4), magnesium chloride (MgCl2), and calcium chloride (CaCl2)—were selected to evaluate their effects on the surface tension of aqueous solutions. Furthermore, the combination of these salts with a magnetic field was found to further reduce surface tension and improve wettability. Molecular simulations conducted using Materials Studio were employed to elucidate the dominant ionic mechanisms influencing surface tension, and to assess the adsorption energies of typical coal dust functional groups in salt solutions.11 An alkaline dust suppressant composed of SAS60 and Na2CO3 enhances coal wettability by reducing contact angle, increasing hydrophilic functional groups, and modifying pore structure, with both experimental and MDs results confirming improved water-coal interactions and dust suppression performance.12 The synergistic effect of inorganic salts with anionic surfactants was investigated by Kilau and Pahlman in 1987, and it was found that potassium and sodium salts significantly increased the wetting properties of anionic surfactants.13 Extensive research has been conducted to evaluate the effects of surfactants on wettability by analyzing key parameters such as contact angle, surface tension, and CMC. However, single surfactants often exhibit limited duration of action and variable performance depending on the coal rank. To overcome these limitations, compounding strategies have been widely adopted. In particular, the combination of anionic and nonionic surfactants has been shown to significantly enhance the wettability of bituminous coal and improve dust suppression efficiency. Experimental studies on such composite systems have focused on optimizing surfactant types, mixing ratios, and concentrations to achieve optimal wetting performance.14,15,16,17 Although previous studies have explored the influence of inorganic salts on the wettability of coal dust in the presence of surfactants, most have focused on single salt concentrations or different salts at fixed concentrations. Moreover, the evaluation methods have predominantly relied on surface tension measurements, while studies on the synergistic effects between inorganic salts and surfactant mixtures remain limited.18,19,20,21,22 Sodium chloride (NaCl) was selected in this study as a representative inorganic electrolyte because of its low cost, high water solubility, and practical applicability in dust suppression formulations. As a typical monovalent salt, NaCl can screen the electrostatic repulsion between anionic surfactant head groups, compress the electrical double layer, and promote closer adsorption of surfactant molecules at the coal-water interface. These effects facilitate interfacial tension reduction, mixed micelle formation, and the wettability improvement of hydrophobic coal dust.23,24 As shown in (Table S1), compared with conventional dust suppression approaches such as single surfactant systems, magnetized activated water, and alkaline dust suppressants reported in the literature, the composite dust suppressant developed in this study integrates the rapid wetting capability of surfactants with the moisture-retention function of polymer components, thereby achieving both effective initial dust suppression and prolonged moisture retention. Analysis of wetting properties of monomeric surfactant solutions

Monomer surfactant optimization and compounding

Monomer surfactant preference

Combined with Figures 1 and 2, it can be observed that among the anionic surfactants, both SDBS and SDS exhibited a pronounced decrease in surface tension with increasing concentration. Beyond a certain concentration, further increases resulted in a plateau, with only minor fluctuations and a slight downward trend.

At a mass fraction of 0.1%, the difference in surface tension between the two surfactants was negligible. However, SDBS demonstrated a smaller contact angle, indicating superior wettability compared to SDS at the same concentration. For cationic surfactants, both CTAB and 1227 showed larger contact angles than other surfactant types, suggesting relatively poorer wettability. Among the tested surfactants, CTAB exhibited comparatively superior performance.

For amphoteric surfactants, BS-12 and CAB-35 both showed enhanced surface tension reduction and improved contact angle performance, with BS-12 performing slightly better overall.

Among nonionic surfactants, TWEEN-80 demonstrated a relatively weak effect on surface tension reduction, whereas OP-10 exhibited a more pronounced and stable performance.

Based on the comprehensive evaluation, SDBS, CTAB, OP-10, and BS-12 are identified as the preferred single-component surfactants.

Below the CMC, surfactant molecules adsorb rapidly at the coal-water and air-liquid interfaces, reducing surface tension and contact angle, thereby enhancing wettability and dispersion. As shown in Figure 4, significant differences in droplet spreading and contact angle are observed among the surfactants. OP-10 (Figure 4C) exhibits the smallest contact angle (32.98°), with a notably flattened droplet profile, indicating superior wettability and the lowest surface tension among the tested systems. Combined with Figures 3 and 4, it can be observed that the optimal surfactants selected from each category—anionic SDBS, nonionic OP-10, amphoteric BS-12, and cationic CTAB—exhibit their best performance at concentrations corresponding to their respective CMCs. The optimal mass fractions are 0.05 wt.% for SDBS, 0.10 wt.% for OP-10, 0.05 wt.% for BS-12, and 0.05 wt.% for CTAB.

As shown in Figure 4, the wettability of the surfactants at their CMC follows the order: OP-10 > SDBS > BS-12 > CTAB. This trend is consistent with the observed contact angles and droplet morphologies. Higher surface tension results in larger contact angles, causing droplets to maintain a more spherical shape and limiting their ability to effectively infiltrate the pores of the porous medium. Consequently, pore filling is restricted, leading to reduced effective porosity and permeability. In contrast, lower surface tension leads to smaller contact angles, enhanced spreading, and improved permeability, which facilitates the penetration and transport of droplets within the porous structure.

To further investigate synergistic effects, the four selected surfactants were compounded in binary systems at mass ratios of 5:0, 4:1, 3:2, 1:1, 2:3, and 1:4. The total surfactant concentration in each mixture was fixed at the corresponding CMC level (0.05 wt.% for SDBS, CTAB, and BS-12, and 0.10 wt.% for OP-10), ensuring that all systems were evaluated under optimal interfacial conditions.

Figure 5 summarizes the effect of surfactant compounding on surface tension and coal dust settling behavior. While the impact on surface tension was negligible, compounding clearly improved the wetting performance of coal dust by shortening the settling time and increasing the settling rate, indicating a synergistic effect. The most effective wetting result was achieved with a 2:3 mixture of 0.05% SDBS and 0.1% OP-10, followed by a 2:3 mixture of 0.05% BS-12 and 0.1% OP-10. These results demonstrate that the compounded formulations require less time and facilitate faster coal dust settling compared to single-component solutions. Surfactants evaluate efficacy at the gas-liquid interface. However, sedimentation time and wetting behavior are governed by the combined effects of solid-liquid and gas-liquid interfacial tensions. Based on these experimental outcomes, the anionic-nonionic compounded solution composed of 0.05 wt.% SDBS and 0.1 wt.% OP-10 at a 2:3 ratio is recommended for optimal wetting. Previous wettability experiments demonstrated that the composite surfactant system with an SDBS/OP-10 ratio of 2:3 exhibited superior wetting performance on coal dust.

As illustrated in Figure 5B, the change in surface tension following surfactant compounding was negligible. Overall results indicate that the composite surfactants effectively reduced coal dust settling time compared to their monomer counterparts. Notably, the synergism between SDBS and OP-10 was particularly significant at a volume ratio of 2:3; the resulting wetting time was 9.21 s, representing a reduction of 52.45% and 18.99% compared to single solutions of SDBS (19.37 s) and OP-10 (11.37 s) at the same concentration, respectively.

Tests further demonstrated that mixtures of anionic and nonionic solutions exhibited superior synergistic effects. For instance, at a 2:3 mass ratio of BS-12 to OP-10, the coal dust wetting time was 8.35 s, which is 29.76% and 26.56% lower than that of the single BS-12 (12.04 s) and OP-10 (11.37 s) solutions, respectively. Conversely, composite solutions of CTAB/OP-10 and CTAB/BS-12 resulted in higher coal dust wetting times than their respective monomer solutions, indicating an antagonistic effect between CTAB and both OP-10 and BS-12. Furthermore, the wetting times for BS-12/OP-10 and SDBS/CTAB composites fell between those of their corresponding monomer surfactants. Specifically, the BS-12/OP-10 mixture exhibited an antagonistic effect, resulting in a wetting time lower than that of the single BS-12 solution (12.04 s).

At a mixing ratio of 2:3, the binary mixture of 0.05 wt.% SDBS and 0.1 wt.% OP-10 exhibited the best wetting performance, followed by the mixture of 0.05 wt.% BS-12 and 0.1 wt.% OP-10 at the same ratio. These results indicate that surfactant blending significantly shortens the wetting/settling time of coal dust, leading to faster sedimentation compared with the corresponding single-surfactant (monomer) solutions. Based on the overall compounding tests, the anionic-nonionic composite formulation consisting of 0.05 wt.% SDBS and 0.1 wt.% OP-10 at a 2:3 ratio is hereafter denoted as Solution X.

Moisture-retention performance test and analysis

To eliminate the influence of fluctuations in ambient temperature and humidity on evaporation-related mass loss, and to ensure that the moisture-retention evaluation reflects only the intrinsic differences of the coal powder-solution systems, solution-only blank controls were introduced in parallel with the coal-containing tests under identical conditions (Table S3). Specifically, B1 was the blank control containing the composite surfactant solution without coal powder, and B2 was the blank control containing the composite surfactant solution supplemented with NaCl (also without coal powder). The corresponding coal-containing experimental groups were C1 and C2, respectively.

Mass measurements were performed using an analytical balance with a precision of 0.001 g. Briefly, 3.00 ± 0.01 g of bituminous coal powder (<200 mesh) was weighed and transferred into a glass dish, followed by the addition of 15.0 g ± 0.01 of the test solution. The mixture was homogenized thoroughly and then allowed to dry naturally. The mass of the entire system was recorded every 8 h for a total duration of 48 h. The stage-wise mass-loss data of three parallel replicates for each group are provided in (Table S4).

For quantitative comparison, the cumulative mass loss of the coal-containing system at time ttt is denoted as wt.otal(t), while that of the corresponding solution-only blank is Wblank(t). As shown in Table S3 and S4, both coal-containing systems exhibit positive baseline-corrected retention at 48 h, while the NaCl-containing formulation (C2, paired with B2) yields larger Wnet(t) and Rmr(t) values than the surfactant-only system (C1, paired with B1), indicating improved moisture-retention performance after accounting for baseline solution evaporation.

In the baseline-corrected (paired-control normalized) analysis of Table S4, both coal-containing systems—C1 (SDBS/OP-10, paired with B1) and C2 (SDBS/OP-10/NaCl, paired with B2)—exhibited a positive net moisture retention at 48 h (Wnet(t) > 0). This indicates that, after subtracting the baseline evaporation of the corresponding bulk solutions, the coal-solution systems still retain a measurable amount of water. More importantly, the introduction of NaCl further enhanced this effect: The net moisture-retention rate Rmr for C1 was 1.24 ± 0.37% (Wnet(t) = 0.187 ± 0.055 g), whereas Rmr for C2 increased to 2.52 ± 1.46% (Wnet(t) = 0.377 ± 0.220 g), corresponding to an approximately 2-fold improvement relative to C1. These baseline-corrected results demonstrate that the NaCl-amended formulation delivers a significantly higher net moisture-retention capacity in the coal-containing system, thereby strengthening the robustness and comparability of the conclusion by minimizing environmental interference and accounting for differences in bulk-solution evaporation.

By employing this paired-control normalization approach, we effectively eliminated the interference of intrinsic solution evaporation rates. The consistently positive and significantly higher Wnet(t) values for the C2 system provide robust evidence that the addition of NaCl intrinsically strengthens the coal-water interaction, ensuring superior performance in dust suppression applications where prolonged moisture presence is critical.

Interfacial synergism between anionic/nonionic surfactants and NaCl for improved coal dust wettability

The composite dust suppressant exhibited excellent storage stability (Figure S1), with only minor changes after 90 days at 25°C (surface tension: −1.89%–1.64%; contact angle: −4.29%–2.42%), indicating negligible performance degradation (Table S5). NaCl was selected in this study as a representative inorganic electrolyte because of its low cost, highwater solubility, and practical applicability in dust suppression formulations. As a typical mono-valent salt, NaCl can screen the electrostatic repulsion between anionic surfactant head groups, compress the electrical double layer, and promote closer adsorption of surfactant molecules at the coal-water interface. These effects facilitate interfacial tension reduction, mixed micelle formation, and the wettability improvement of hydrophobic coal dust.28,29,30,31,32

The ternary system (0.05 wt. % SDBS + 0.10 wt. % OP-10 + 0.10 wt. % NaCl) demonstrates significantly enhanced wetting performance. The contact angle decreases dramatically to 16.17 ± 0.44°, and the settling time is reduced to 6.32 ± 0.22 s, indicating rapid wetting and dispersion of coal dust. Although the surface tension (31.57 ± 0.004 mN·m-1) is not the lowest among all systems, the overall wettability is markedly improved. This highlights that surface tension alone is not sufficient to describe wetting performance; instead, interfacial adsorption behavior and spreading dynamics play dominant roles. The superior performance of the ternary system can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between the anionic and nonionic surfactants in the presence of inorganic salt. Specifically, SDBS provides strong electrostatic adsorption onto the coal surface, while OP-10 enhances molecular flexibility and improves surface coverage. Meanwhile, NaCl promotes micelle compaction and interfacial packing through ionic strength effects. This cooperative mechanism facilitates the formation of a more stable and densely packed adsorption film, thereby significantly reducing the contact angle and accelerating particle wetting and sedimentation. Overall, the results demonstrate that NaCl exhibits a pronounced synergistic effect with SDBS but not with OP-10 alone. However, when combined in a ternary system, the interaction between SDBS, OP-10, and NaCl leads to substantial improvements in wettability and dispersion performance. This provides important insights into the design of efficient composite wetting agents for coal dust suppression (Table S6). As shown in Table S7, the RSD values for surface tension are generally low (0.113% for 0.10 wt. % NaCl and below ∼2.2% for all systems), indicating high reproducibility. Similarly, the RSD values for contact angle are mostly within ∼0.2–2.8%, while the sedimentation time shows RSD values below ∼4.2%, with the highest value around 4.15%, demonstrating acceptable variability. Notably, for mixed systems ( SDBS + NaCl or OP-10 + NaCl), the RSD values remain low (0.997% for surface tension and 0.331% for sedimentation time), confirming that the addition of electrolytes does not compromise experimental stability. The data exhibit good reproducibility and reliability.

As shown in Figures 8A–8F and Table S8, the wettability of the tested solution systems strongly depended on solution composition. The NaCl system exhibited the poorest wetting performance, as evidenced by the highest surface tension (51.63 mN·m-1) and contact angle (75.70°), together with the most negative spreading coefficient (−38.84 mN·m-1) and the lowest immersion work (12.79 mN·m-1). After the addition of surfactants, both the surface tension and contact angle decreased markedly, indicating a substantial improvement in wettability. Compared with SDBS, OP-10 showed better wetting performance, with the contact angle decreasing to 32.98 and the immersion work increasing to 26.58 mN·m-1. The SDBS/OP-10 mixed system maintained a low contact angle ( 32.77°) and a relatively high immersion work (27.36 mN·m-1), suggesting a cooperative effect between the anionic and nonionic surfactants. More importantly, after the introduction of inorganic salt into the mixed surfactant system, the wettability was further enhanced. The SDBS + OP-10 + NaCl system exhibited the lowest contact angle (16.17°), the shortest settling time (6.23 s), the spreading coefficient closest to zero (−1.24 mN·m-1), and the highest immersion work (30.26 mN·m-1), demonstrating the best overall wetting performance. The NaCl system is not shown in Figure 8C, since its sedimentation time exceeded 600 s. The relatively small error bars in Figure 8 also indicate good experimental repeatability. These results suggest that NaCl promotes the interfacial adsorption and tighter molecular packing of the SDBS/OP-10 mixed system, thereby reducing interfacial resistance and improving spreading and penetration on the solid surface.

As illustrated in Figure 9, NaCl enhances the wettability of bituminous coal dust through synergistic effects with the anionic-nonionic surfactant system. The raw coal surface is inherently hydrophobic, exhibiting a high contact angle and poor affinity for water. Meanwhile, the nonionic surfactant cooperates with the anionic surfactant to form a more compact and stable interfacial adsorption layer, which promotes the orientation of hydrophilic groups toward the aqueous phase and decreases the contact angle. At concentrations below the CMC, the surfactant mixture mainly adsorbs at the coal-water interface, gradually improving the hydrophilicity of the coal surface.33 When the concentration approaches or exceeds the CMC, excess surfactant molecules increasingly participate in the formation of mixed micelles in the bulk solution. These mixed aggregates improve the solubilization and dispersion of hydrophobic components, while the interfacial adsorption layer continues to enhance water spreading on the coal surface. As a result, the wettability of bituminous coal dust is further improved.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that the combination of anionic and nonionic surfactants yields a pronounced synergistic effect on wettability and sedimentation behavior, surpassing the performance of individual surfactants. The optimal formulation, solution X (SDBS:OP-10 = 2:3), exhibited both minimal contact angle and settling time, indicating that the cooperative interaction between the anionic and nonionic components significantly enhances surface coverage and interfacial activity. This observation aligns with prior studies, which have reported that mixed surfactant systems can reduce the energy barrier at solid-liquid interfaces and improve wetting efficiency by combining the electrostatic contributions of anionic surfactants with the steric and hydrogen-bonding capabilities of nonionic surfactants. In particular, the slight additional improvement over OP-10 alone suggests that even small contributions from anionic surfactants can reinforce interfacial adsorption without compromising the favorable characteristics of nonionic surfactants.

The addition of NaCl to solution X further improved performance, with a 50.66% reduction in contact angle at a 1:4 mass ratio. This ionic strength-dependent enhancement suggests that NaCl screens electrostatic repulsion between surfactant headgroups, facilitating stronger adsorption onto the coal surface and promoting more efficient liquid-solid interactions. This salt-driven synergism has been previously reported in similar colloidal systems and underscores the critical role of ionic strength in modulating surfactant behavior.

Beyond interfacial adsorption, the observed increase in water retention with increasing NaCl concentration suggests that the synergistic mechanism extends to bulk-interface coupling. Enhanced water retention implies stronger liquid-solid affinity and improved capillary penetration within the porous coal structure, which is critical for sustained wetting performance. This behavior is particularly relevant for engineering applications such as dust suppression and slurry transport, where both rapid wetting and long-term moisture stabilization are required. The results, therefore, indicate that tuning ionic strength provides an additional control parameter for optimizing not only interfacial properties but also macroscopic process performance.

In conclusion, this work provides valuable insights into the synergistic interactions between anionic and nonionic surfactants, with NaCl acting as a key enhancer of interfacial properties. The findings provide mechanistic insight into surfactant synergy at solid-liquid interfaces and offer a rational basis for optimizing formulation strategies in coal processing and related particulate systems.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the present work was performed under controlled laboratory conditions using bituminous coal dust, which may not fully reproduce the heterogeneous and dynamic conditions encountered in practical mining environments, such as variations in temperature, relative humidity, ventilation intensity, and dust particle characteristics. Consequently, the field applicability of the optimized ternary system remains to be further validated. Second, the investigation was limited to a specific anionic-nonionic surfactant pair (SDBS/OP-10) and NaCl, and the observed synergistic effects may depend on the physicochemical properties of both the surfactants and the coal substrate. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating the present conclusions to other surfactant-salt combinations or coal ranks. Third, although macroscopic indicators, including contact angle, settling time, surface tension, and evaporation loss, were employed to evaluate performance, the interfacial adsorption behavior and synergistic mechanism were not elucidated at the molecular scale. Advanced spectroscopic characterization and molecular simulation would be valuable for clarifying the adsorption configuration and interaction pathways within the ternary system. Finally, important practical considerations, including long-term stability, environmental compatibility, corrosion risk, and economic viability, were beyond the scope of this study and should be systematically addressed in future work.

Preparation of surfactant and salt solutions

For single-surfactant systems, the required amount of surfactant was dissolved under magnetic stirring until a homogeneous solution was obtained. For mixed systems, selected surfactants were combined at predetermined ratios before dilution. In salt-containing systems, inorganic salts were added after complete dissolution of the surfactants.

All solutions were equilibrated at room temperature prior to measurement.Surfactant solutions were prepared using deionized water at mass concentrations of 0.025wt. %, 0.05wt. %, 0.075wt. %, 0.1wt. %, 0.2wt. %, and 0.3wt. %. Sodium chloride (NaCl) were prepared using deionized water at mass concentrations of 0.1wt. %.

To avoid introducing additional components and to maintain the original pH of the blended reagents, sodium chloride (NaCl) solution was selected as the inorganic salt additive and combined with SDBS/OP-10 at different mass ratios (5:0, 4:1, 3:2, 1:1, 2:3, 1:4, and 0:5). These mixtures were subsequently used for wettability and moisture-retention (humectancy) evaluations.

Surface tension and contact angle measurements

Surface tension measurement

Surface tension was measured using a Krüss K100 automatic tensiometer based on the Wilhelmy plate method at 25±0.2°C.

Each sample was measured in triplicate, and average values were reported. All glassware was thoroughly cleaned prior to measurement to avoid contamination. Measurements showing instability or obvious artifacts were discarded and repeated.

Contact angle measurement

Contact angles were measured using a JCY-14 contact angle goniometer. A droplet (5 μL) of test solution was deposited onto the sample surface using a microsyringe.

Coal dust pellets were prepared by compressing 200 mg of coal powder under a pressure of 30 MPa for 3 min to obtain flat and uniform surfaces. The initial contact angle was recorded after stabilization of the solid–liquid–air interface.

Each condition was tested at multiple positions and repeated in triplicate. Lower contact angles were interpreted as improved wettability.

Evaluation of wettability and dust suppression performance

Coal dust settling test

A fixed volume of test solution (100 g) was added to a transparent container. A fixed mass of coal dust (2 g) was gently placed on the liquid surface.

The time required for the coal dust to become completely wetted and sink below the liquid surface was recorded. All measurements were performed in triplicate under identical conditions.

To ensure consistency, coal dust was added in a standardized manner, and measurements affected by operational errors were excluded and repeated.

Formulation design

To evaluate the synergistic effects of surfactant systems, single-component, binary-component, and ternary-component formulations were prepared.

SDBS was used as the anionic surfactant, OP-10 as the nonionic surfactant, and NaCl as the inorganic salt additive. Deionized water served as the solvent.

All components were weighed accurately and dissolved under continuous magnetic stirring at room temperature until homogeneous solutions were obtained. Fresh solutions were prepared prior to measurement to minimize the effects of storage.

Experimental design and quality control

The experiments were designed to compare the effects of surfactant type, concentration, surfactant compounding, and salt addition on coal wettability. Single-surfactant systems were first evaluated as reference conditions. Mixed-surfactant systems and salt-containing systems were then tested under matched concentration conditions to examine the influence of compositional modification on wettability-related parameters, including surface tension, contact angle, and dust settling behavior.

All experiments were conducted using the same batch of coal dust and freshly prepared solutions. Each measurement was repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility.

Measurement order was randomized where applicable to reduce systematic bias. Data were included only when no experimental abnormalities occurred. Predefined exclusion criteria included sample contamination, instrument malfunction, abnormal droplet formation, defective pellets, and handling errors.

Statistical details of experiments

Statistical analyses in this study were performed using standard deviation (SD) to assess data dispersion. All data are presented as mean values, with n representing the number of observations used to calculate the mean. The statistical software employed is specified in the Methods section. Detailed statistical information, including the statistical tests used and the definition of n, is provided in the figure legends the results section.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All measurements were performed in triplicate unless otherwise stated, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Surface tension, contact angle, and settling time were used as indicators of wettability.

Statistical analysis was performed using Origin 2021. Differences between groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Additional resources

Key generation

In this study, the concept of key generation is adapted to define a structured experimental parameter framework for evaluating coal dust wettability.

Two parameter sets, kn and km, were constructed to represent baseline conditions and controlled perturbation conditions, respectively. Each set integrates key experimental variables, including surfactant type (anionic, cationic, nonionic, and Amphoteric ions), concentration (0.025wt. %∼0.3wt. %), and solution composition (single, compounded, or salt-enhanced systems).

The baseline set kn corresponds to systematically designed conditions for evaluating intrinsic wettability, whereas the perturbed set km was derived from kn by introducing controlled variations, such as concentration shifts and compositional modifications, while preserving overall comparability.

This dual-parameter design enables paired-condition analysis, allowing the direct comparison of wettability responses under closely related experimental conditions. As a result, intrinsic wetting performance can be effectively distinguished from perturbation-induced effects, improving the robustness and interpretability of the experimental system.

Encryption

The “encryption” process corresponds to the experimental construction and physicochemical modulation of surfactant–coal systems, through which wettability characteristics are embedded into measurable responses.

Under these conditions, adsorption, and interfacial energy reduction collectively govern the wetting process, translating experimental variables into observable physicochemical properties. Compounded systems and salt additions act as controlled perturbations, increasing system complexity and enabling evaluation of robustness in wetting performance.

Decryption

The “decryption” process corresponds to the quantitative characterization and extraction of effective wettability behavior from experimental measurements.

Experimental results were comparatively analyzed across parameter sets to distinguish consistent wetting enhancement (reduced surface tension and contact angle, accelerated settling) from variability induced by concentration or compositional effects.

By integrating these parameters, the dominant mechanisms governing coal dust wettability—namely improved spreading, enhanced solid–liquid affinity, and increased penetration—were systematically resolved.
