Prevalence, risk factors, and comparative diagnostic performance of cryptic plasmid and MOMP-based real-time PCR assays for genital Chlamydia trachomatis infection among women of reproductive age in the West Region of Cameroon: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Boris Emmanuel Djoumsie Gomseu, Mahamat Mustapha Anouar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChlamydia trachomatis (C. trachomatis) is a leading cause of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in resource-limited settings. In low-income contexts such as Cameroon, the epidemiology of this bacterium remains poorly documented. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and risk factors of C. trachomatis infection among women of reproductive age in the West Region of Cameroon, and to assess the analytical performance and concordance of cryptic plasmid and major outer membrane protein (MOMP)-based real-time PCR (R-T PCR) assays.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 570 women of reproductive age presenting at four selected health facilities in the West Region of Cameroon. Endocervical swabs were collected and analyzed using R-T PCR targeting the MOMP and cryptic plasmid genes to detect C. trachomatis DNA. Socio-demographic, behavioral, and clinical data were recorded using a structured questionnaire. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with C. trachomatis infection. Diagnostic performance of MOMP and cryptic plasmid R-T PCR was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistic and McNemar’s test, with ompA R-T PCR assay, used as the comparator.ResultsThe prevalence of C. trachomatis infection was 12.98%. Infection was associated with secondary education level (aOR = 7.39, [95% CI]: 2.24–24.41), Bantu ethnicity (aOR = 6.52, [95% CI]: 2.35–18.11), being a secondary school student (aOR = 3.04, [95% CI]: 1.24–7.46), lack of knowledge of transmission routes (aOR = 5.91, [95% CI]: 2.16–16.12), postcoital pain (aOR = 14.86, [95% CI]: 4.12–55.10) and history of miscarriage (aOR = 2.40, [95% CI]: 0.94–6.08).ConclusionsStudy shows that genital Chlamydia trachomatis infection was identified among women of reproductive age in the West Region of Cameroon and was associated with education level, ethnicity, occupation, limited knowledge of transmission routes, postcoital pain, and history of miscarriage. Cryptic plasmid–based R-T PCR showed acceptable diagnostic performance in this setting. These findings support strengthening sexual health education and considering the integration of C. trachomatis testing into reproductive health services in similar contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04673-y.

Introduction

Chlamydia trachomatis (C. trachomatis) is the most common bacterial cause of sexually transmitted infections worldwide, with a disproportionate burden among women of reproductive age. C. trachomatis is associated with a broad range of clinical conditions, including trachoma, infant pneumonia, cervicitis, urethritis, pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy, infertility, and lymphogranuloma venereum [1–3]. In women, most C. trachomatis infections are asymptomatic, allowing the infection to persist undetected and increasing the risk of complications and onward transmission [4]. Chlamydia trachomatis infection represents a substantial global public health burden. In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated 128.5 million new genital infections among adults aged 15–49, with the highest burden observed in sexually active women under 25 years [5, 6]. Sub-Saharan Africa is disproportionately affected, with over 34 million new infections annually among women and prevalence estimates ranging from 8% to 37%, depending on socioeconomic conditions and access to healthcare [7, 8]. The largely asymptomatic nature of the infection contributes to its persistence and its association with adverse reproductive health outcomes.

In low-income countries, C. trachomatis infections frequently remain undiagnosed due to limited awareness and restricted access to reliable diagnostic tools, leading to preventable infertility and other reproductive complications. Unlike high-income countries with national C. trachomatis screening programs [9], Cameroon faces major challenges, including the lack of routine screening, low public awareness, and limited laboratory capacity [9, 10]. The epidemiological burden of Chlamydia trachomatis infection in Cameroon remains poorly characterized. This limitation is mainly attributable to the absence of a structured national surveillance system and the limited integration of reliable diagnostic tools in routine clinical practice. As a result, available epidemiological data are fragmented and insufficient to provide robust estimates across different population groups.

Previous studies conducted in Cameroon have largely relied on serological assays or rapid diagnostic tests, reporting highly variable prevalence rates ranging from 4.3% to 40.8% [11, 12]. However, these approaches are inherently limited, as they detect antibodies rather than active bacterial infection. Consequently, they do not allow clear discrimination between past exposure and current infection, a limitation that is particularly critical for intracellular pathogens such as C. trachomatis.

This methodological constraint may lead to misclassification bias and compromise both the accuracy and comparability of epidemiological findings [13, 14]. In this context, nucleic acid amplification techniques, particularly R-T PCR, represent a more reliable alternative due to their high sensitivity and specificity for the detection of active infection.

Molecular diagnostic methods, particularly R-T PCR assays targeting the cryptic plasmid and the MOMP gene, provide high sensitivity and specificity for C. trachomatis detection [15, 16]. However, access to these techniques remains limited in routine clinical settings in Cameroon. In the context of an absent national screening strategy, limited availability of molecular diagnostics, and persistent high-risk sociocultural practices, a clearer understanding of C. trachomatis epidemiology is required. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of genital C. trachomatis infection among women of reproductive age in West Region of Cameroon, to identify associated risk factors, and to evaluate the diagnostic performance of cryptic plasmid and MOMP-based R-T PCR assays using ompA assay, used as the comparator.

Materials and methods

Study area

The West Region (5.4638 oN, 10.8000 oE) is one of the ten Regions of Cameroon, covering a surface area of 13,892 km2; it is divided into eight divisions as follows: Mifi, Bamboutos, Haut-Nkam, Ménoua, Bandjoun, Noun, Ndé, Koung-Khi, and Hauts-Plateaux. In 2025, the West Region of Cameroon, whose administrative capital is Bafoussam, had an estimated population of 2,232,844 inhabitants [17]. The Region is characterized by a Sudanian tropical climate with two main seasons: a dry season extending from October to early March, and a rainy season from March to mid-November. This Region constitutes a major agro-pastoral zone, with extensive cultivation of crops such as cabbage, carrot, maize, banana, tomato, plantain, and beans [18]. The Region is marked by socio-economic challenges, including high youth unemployment and limited access to comprehensive sexuality education, alongside early or unprotected sexual debut, which increases vulnerability to sexually transmitted infections [19, 20]. Demographic evidence indicates that sexual initiation commonly occurs during adolescence in Cameroon, with many individuals reporting debut before age 18, implying early exposure to C. trachomatis. The West Region counts an estimated 774 health facilities, within a national total of approximately 6.000 public and private facilities, which collectively conduct between 6.000 and 60.000 C. trachomatis screenings annually [21–24].

Study design

Selection of hospitals

A facility-based cross-sectional study using consecutive recruitment of eligible women attending the four hospitals was conducted. A total of 570 endocervical swab specimens were collected from symptomatic and asymptomatic women (aged 18–49) from 15 September 2023 to 13 August 2024 in Mbouda, Mifi, Bangangté District hospitals (secondary-level hospital) and Dschang Annexe Regional hospital (tertiary-level hospital) in the West Region of Cameroon for the investigation of C. trachomatis (Fig. 1). These hospitals, as first-level public health facilities, provide general medical care, maternal and child health services, and routine laboratory testing, including C. trachomatis screening. For the purposes of this study, R-T PCR–based molecular testing was used as a comparative molecular approach for the detection of C. trachomatis, owing to its recognized analytical performance relative to routine screening methods such as rapid tests or syndromic management commonly used in these clinical settings. These health facilities were purposively selected because they are first-level public hospitals offering routine reproductive and sexual health services and serving both urban and surrounding rural populations.

Sample size calculation

The sample size estimation was based on a previously published study conducted in Cameroon, which reported a C. trachomatis prevalence of 40% [12]. This prevalence estimate was used as an input parameter for calculating the expected sample size using Cochran’s formula [25] for cross-sectional studies: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{n}=\mathrm{Z}^{2}\times\mathrm{p}\times\left(1-\mathrm{p}\right)/\mathrm{d}^{2}$$\end{document}.

With a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), an expected prevalence of p = 0.4, and a margin of error d = 0.05, the minimum required sample size was estimated at approximately 368 participants. However, to strengthen statistical power and improve the representativeness of the findings, a total of 570 sexually active women of reproductive age were ultimately enrolled from four hospitals across West Region of Cameroon.

Participant inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants for this study were sexually active women aged 18 to 49 years who visited one of the selected District Hospitals in the West Region of Cameroon for consultation during the study period and provided written informed consent were included.

Women who were menstruating at the time of sampling, receiving antibiotic treatment or had been exposed to antibiotics within the past 15 days, and individuals under 18 years of age were excluded. Also, participants with an incomplete questionnaire or missing responses regarding sociodemographic and behavioral information were excluded.

Participant selection methodology

Eligible women attending the selected health facilities during the study period were consecutively recruited until the required sample size was reached. In each facility, all women meeting the inclusion criteria were approached, regardless of symptom presentation. This approach ensured that every eligible patient had the opportunity to participate and reflected the routine clinical population within the sociocultural context of the study sites. Recruitment was conducted across four hospital sites to capture a diverse participant profile. This strategy allowed inclusion of women from different socioeconomic backgrounds across the various divisions of the Region.

The study objectives were clearly explained to all potential participants, and written informed consent was obtained prior to enrolment. A structured questionnaire was administered to collect sociodemographic and behavioral data (Supplementary file). Participants who were unable to read received assistance from trained medical personnel, and questions were explained in local languages when necessary.

Clinical symptoms potentially related to C. trachomatis infection were assessed by resident doctors through patient interviews and clinical examination. Both symptomatic and asymptomatic women were included to ensure a representative sample of sexually active women of reproductive age.

To minimize social desirability bias, interviews were conducted in private settings under strict confidentiality. Participants were informed that their responses were anonymous and used solely for research purposes.

Endocervical swabs were collected from 570 women for PCR detection of C. trachomatis. Samples were placed in sterile tubes containing 1 ml of saline (0.9% NaCl) and stored at − 20 °C until DNA extraction.

DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from the endocervical samples using the QIAamp mini kit (Qiagen, CA, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions [26]. Samples were removed from the freezer, left at room temperature for 1 h, and centrifuged at 15.000 rpm for 10 min. The pellet was resuspended in 180 µL ATL buffer and homogenized using a vortex mixer (iSwix-VT, Neuation). Proteinase K was added, followed by vortexing and incubation at 56 °C for 3 h, with homogenization 2–3 times during incubation. After incubation, 200 µL buffer AL was added, vortexed for 15 s, and incubated at 70 °C for 10 min. Then, 200 µL ethanol was added and homogenized.

The mixture was applied to minicolumns and centrifuged at 8.000 rpm for 1 min; the filtrate was discarded. Minicolumns were washed sequentially with 500 µL AW1 (centrifuged at 8.000 rpm for 1 min) and 500 µL AW2 (centrifuged at 14.000 rpm for 3 min), discarding filtrates after each wash. Minicolumns were transferred to new tubes and centrifuged at 14.000 rpm for 1 min to remove residual wash buffer. DNA was eluted in buffer AE, incubated at room temperature for 5 min, and centrifuged at 10.000 rpm for 3 min. Extracted DNA was stored at − 20 °C for further analysis.

The quantity and quality of the extracted DNA were analysed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific NanoDrop 2000) [27].

Amplification by R-T PCR

The molecular detection of C. trachomatis was achieved by R-T PCR using two different pairs of primers: cryptic plasmid primer KL1/KL2 (F5’-TCCGGAGCGAGTTACGAAGA-3’; R5’-AATCAATGCCCGGGATTGGT-3’) and MOMP primer (F5’-CCTGTGGGGAATCCTGCTGAA-3’; R5’ GTCGAAAACAAAGTCACCATAGTA 3’) [28, 29]. Briefly, the amplification reactions were set in a 20 µL reaction volume containing, 5 µL of SYBR green (Sigma-aldrich), 0.25 µM of each primer probe mix, and 5 µL of extracted DNA. The amplification conditions were as follows: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 45 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. At the end of the PCR, all samples with a cycle threshold value of ≤ 33 were considered positive. Appropriate positive and negative controls were included in each PCR run to ensure assay validity, monitor performance, and exclude contamination or non-specific amplification. All samples were tested in duplicate to ensure reproduction of the results. Amplification and PCR product detection were done with the BIO-RAD CFX96 R-T PCR System (BIO-RAD, USA).

Validity of MOMP R-T PCR and cryptic plasmid R-T PCR results

Verification of the validity of MOMP- and cryptic plasmid–based R-T PCR results was carried out by confirmatory amplification of the ompA gene in positive samples, as previously described [28, 30]. Specifically, the ompA gene (OMP 1F: 5’-ATG AAA AAA CTC TTG AAA TCG G-3’) and CTOMP 2R: 5’-ACT GTA ACT GCG TAT TTG TCT G-3’) was amplified by R-T PCR in a final reaction volume of 20 µL, consisting of 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix (Invitrogen), 0.5 µM of each primer, and 5 µL of extracted DNA template. The amplification conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min.

In the absence of a universally accepted gold standard for the molecular diagnosis of C. trachomatis, the ompA R-T PCR assay was used as a comparator. This choice was based on its extensive use in molecular epidemiology studies and its well-characterized genetic target. In addition, ompA has shown consistent performance in comparative diagnostic evaluations reported in the literature.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality through visual inspection and formal testing. Given the large sample size (n = 570) and the approximately normal distribution observed, continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Logistic regression with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was used to measure the degree of the association between the infection by CT and sociodemographic parameters, clinical symptoms and past medical history.

Univariate logistic regression analyses were initially performed for all candidate variables. In addition, variables identified from previous literature including age, marital status, condom use, and prior history of STIs infections were also considered as potential confounders regardless of their univariate significance. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values. Model fit was evaluated with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, Cox & Snell R², and Nagelkerke R². Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values are reported for all variables in the final model. In this study p < 0.05 was considered significant. The dependent variable in the logistic regression analysis was C. trachomatis infection status, defined as a binary outcome (1 = PCR-positive; 0 = PCR-negative). The independent variables were demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms and clinical diagnoses.

The Sensitivity, specificity, predictive values and percentage of agreement of cryptic plasmid R-T PCR and ompA R-T PCR, MOMP R-T and ompA R-T PCR were calculated using standard formulas. Cohen’s kappa statistic was used to assess the level of agreement between plasmid and ompA R-T PCR, MOMP R-T PCR and ompA R-T PCR. The exact McNemar test was applied to evaluate the symmetry of discordant results between each assay and the comparator assay.

All these analyses were performed using SPSS version 24 software.

Ethical considerations

All procedures contributing to this study were approved by the Cameroon National Committee for Ethics in Human Health (Approval No. 2018/05/1022/CE/CNERSH/SP) and were conducted in compliance with internationally accepted ethical standards for human experimentation, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1975), as revised in 2008. Administrative authorization was obtained from the West Regional Delegation of Public Health as well as from the administrative authorities of each participating hospital.

Clinical management of C. trachomatis-positive participants

All participants who tested positive for C. trachomatis were promptly informed of their results. Treatment was provided in accordance with the Cameroon National Guidelines for the Management of STIs. Depending on clinical considerations and participant preference, treatment consisted of either a single 1 g dose of azithromycin or a 7-day course of doxycycline. Partner notification was actively encouraged, and partners were offered treatment or referral in line with WHO recommendations. Participants also received individualized counselling on sexual health, infection prevention, and the importance of partner management to reduce the risk of reinfection.

These measures ensured that participants received comprehensive care extending beyond the scope of the research, thereby addressing both ethical obligations and public health priorities. No financial incentives were provided for study participation.

Results

Participant recruitment and final study population

Out of the 750 women initially approached, 180 were excluded. Specifically, 60 refused to provide informed consent, 30 were excluded due to antibiotic use, 27 reported no sexual experience, 30 provided incomplete questionnaires, and 33 had endocervical samples deemed inadequate for analysis (Fig. 2). The overall acceptance rate was 76% (570/750).

Characteristics of the study participants

This study focused on a population of 570 women with an age range between 18 and 49 years and a mean age of 26 ± 7.17 years. In this study, 98.9% (564/570) of participants declared having regular sexual activity. The socioeconomic status of the women was assessed using self-reported activities. About 19.47% (111/570) of the participants were unemployed, 11.05% (63/570) were traders, 21.75% (124/570) were university students, 9.12% (52/570) secondary students and about 12.80% (73/570) were farmers. Many women reported using condoms 48.77% (278/570) and 37.54% (214/570) did not use them. The mean age at first intercourse of participants was 18.12 ± 6.13 (Table 1).

Prevalence of C. trachomatis infection

The overall prevalence of C. trachomatis was 12.98% (10.47–15.99) (74/570) among women of reproductive age using cryptic plasmid R-T PCR. The prevalence in different hospitals was 11.93% (7.10-19.34) (13/109), 15.53% (9.79–23.75) (16/103), 20.33% (14.16–28.28) (25/123), and 14.81% (9.80-21.78) (20/135) in Mifi, Bangangté, Mbouda District, and Dschang Annexe Regional, respectively.

Associated risk factors of C. trachomatis infection

Socio-demographic characteristics

The multivariable logistic regression model was statistically significant (p < 0.001) and demonstrated good fit to the data (Hosmer–Lemeshow test, p = 0.58). The model explained 24% (Cox & Snell R²) to 44% (Nagelkerke R²) of the variance and correctly classified 91% of cases. No evidence of multicollinearity was observed (VIF range: 1.04 to 2.75 and tolerance values range: 0.37 to 0.98). Based on this model, the analysis revealed an association between educational level and C. trachomatis infection. Women with secondary education had higher odds of infection compared with those with no formal education (aOR = 7.39; 95% CI: 2.24–24.41). Women with university education also showed increased odds, although to a lesser extent (aOR = 2.68; 95% CI: 1.15–6.24). Ethnicity was also associated with infection. Bantu women had higher adjusted odds of C. trachomatis infection compared with Sudano-Sahelian women (aOR = 6.52; 95% CI: 2.35–18.11), whereas Semi-Bantu ethnicity was not independently associated after adjustment. Regarding occupation, being a secondary school student was associated with higher odds of infection compared with being unemployed (aOR = 3.04; 95% CI: 1.24–7.46) (Table 2). Lack of knowledge of STI transmission routes was significantly associated with C. trachomatis infection. Women without such knowledge showed higher adjusted odds of infection compared with those who were aware of transmission routes (aOR = 5.91; 95% CI: 2.16–16.12). These associations should be interpreted with caution due to the cross-sectional design and wide confidence intervals, which reflect uncertainty in the estimates.

Performance of MOMP R-T PCR and cryptic plasmid PCR

A comparative assessment of MOMP R-T PCR and cryptic plasmid R-T PCR against ompA R-T PCR revealed differences in their capacity to detect C. trachomatis. The sensitivity, specificity, predictive values of the positive and negative tests were 100% for cryptic plasmid R-T PCR and 97.06%, 96.27%, 62.26% and 99.81% for the MOMP R-T PCR (Table 4). The 2 × 2 contingency tables and diagnostic performance of the assays are shown in Supplementary Table S1. The agreement between the cryptic plasmid R-T PCR and ompA R-T PCR, as well as between MOMP R-T PCR and ompA R-T PCR, was analyzed using Cohen’s kappa test. There was almost perfect agreement between the cryptic plasmid R-T PCR and ompA R-T PCR, (kappa value of 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.93), and the McNemar test was not applicable. Similarly, substantial agreement was observed between MOMP R-T PCR and ompA R-T PCR, with a kappa value of 0.82, 95% CI: 0.77–0.86 (McNemar pvalue < 0.001). The overall percentage agreements were 100% for cryptic plasmid R-T PCR and 96.32% for MOMP R-T PCR, respectively.

Discussion

The present facility-based cross-sectional study conducted among 570 women of reproductive age in four cities of the West Region of Cameroon reported a C. trachomatis prevalence of 12.98%. This estimate is notably higher than the 2% reported by Sobze et al. (2014) in Dschang, yet significantly lower than the 40.80% reported by Fogue et al. (2017) among sexually active Cameroonian women. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in study population, geographical location, and diagnostic methods used. In particular, serological assays may overestimate prevalence by detecting past exposure rather than active infection [11, 12]. The prevalence observed is comparable to reports from East Africa, including Kenya (14%) and Tanzania (12%) [31, 32], suggesting a sustained burden of C. trachomatis across sub-Saharan Africa.

The high prevalence observed is consistent with the predominantly asymptomatic nature of C. trachomatis infection. In low- and middle-income countries, more than 60% of infections in women are asymptomatic, with pooled estimates ranging from 50.4% to 70.5% [33]. Asymptomatic individuals can transmit C. trachomatis at rates comparable to symptomatic cases (20–50% per partnership and 2–4% per sexual act). However, they often remain infectious for longer periods because of the absence of symptoms delays healthcare-seeking [34]. This prolonged duration of untreated infection creates a persistent silent reservoir, facilitating sustained transmission and contributing directly to elevated population-level prevalence. In such contexts, reliance on syndromic management alone may be insufficient in this context supporting consideration of expanded, screening strategies [35].

Targeted interventions could focus on identified high-risk groups and strengthen comprehensive sexuality education to improve STI knowledge and prevention behaviors. Culturally adapted community engagement and improved access to affordable diagnostics are essential to reduce transmission and long-term reproductive morbidity.

After multivariable adjustment, several socio-demographic and behavioural characteristics remained independently associated with C. trachomatis infection. Educational level showed that women with secondary education had approximately sevenfold higher odds of infection, while those with university education had more than twofold higher odds compared with women without formal education. This counterintuitive pattern may reflect unmeasured social and behavioural factors and suggests that higher educational attainment alone may not necessarily translate into to effective sexual health literacy or risk-reducing behaviors. In several low- and middle-income settings, formal education is not always accompanied by comprehensive sexuality education. This gap may contribute to continued exposure to sexual risk despite higher schooling levels. Similar observations have been reported elsewhere, where educational attainment was not consistently protective and was sometimes linked to earlier sexual debut and higher report sexual risk behaviours [36–38].

Ethnicity was associated with C. trachomatis infection. The observed prevalence varied across groups, with 26.02% among Sudano-Sahelian women, 14.77% among Semi-Bantu women, and 6.84% among Bantu women. Although crude prevalence estimates differed across ethnic groups, multivariable analysis identified Bantu ethnicity as dependently associated with infection after adjustment for potential confounding factors. These differences may reflect variation in cultural practices, sexual behaviors, healthcare-seeking patterns, or other contextual factors. Some studies have described sociocultural practices such as widow inheritance, polygamy, or sexual cleansing rituals in the context of STI transmission dynamics in certain settings [39–41]. However, these practices were not assessed in the present study. Therefore, any interpretation linking the observed differences to such factors remains speculative and should be approached with caution.

In addition, structural determinants, including access to healthcare, sexual health education, and negotiation power within relationships, may also contribute to variations in STI exposure and detection [42]. Overall, the observed associations highlight the complexity of contextual influences and should not be interpreted as reflecting inherent differences between ethnic groups.

In the present study, occupation was independently associated with infection. The prevalence of C. trachomatis infection varied according to participants’ occupation. The higher prevalence was observed among women with secondary (32.69%) or university education (20.96%) as well as among unemployed participants. This apparent paradox may reflect inadequate or inconsistently implemented sexuality education within formal curricula in many developing countries. Educational attainment does not always translate into effective knowledge of STI transmission. Unemployment may be associated with increased vulnerability through economic instability and other unmeasured social or sexual behavioural factors [43]. Conversely, Mbah et al. reported higher C. trachomatis prevalence among women in regular employment, suggesting that occupational exposure and social networks may also influence infection risk, underscoring the multifactorial nature of C. trachomatis transmission dynamics [44].

In this study, lack of knowledge about STI transmission routes among women was independently associated with an increased risk of C. trachomatis infection. This finding highlights a key behavioral determinant in a context where prevalence among women ranges from 8% to 22% [45]. Similar gaps have been reported at Nkoldongo District Hospital in Yaoundé, where awareness of C. trachomatis was markedly lower than that of syphilis (91.2%), despite a high seroprevalence of 22.5% and a reinfection rate of 4.94%. These observations indicate persistent deficiencies in sexual health education, even within clinical settings [46].

The absence of anal sex practice was associated with lower outcome. Women who reported not practicing anal sex had approximately 50% lower odds of C. trachomatis infection compared with those who reported this practice. This finding may reflect differences in sexual behavior patterns or exposure to higher-risk sexual networks. It also underscoring the relevance of healthcare provider inquiry into diverse sexual practices, including oral–anal contact for comprehensive STI screening [47]. Rectal C. trachomatis infections are common and may remain undiagnosed in the absence of site-specific testing [48].

Postcoital pain (pain during or after sex) was associated with markedly higher adjusted odds of C. trachomatis infection. Although this finding suggests potential clinical relevance, the wide confidence interval indicates substantial uncertainty and therefore warrants cautious interpretation. Nevertheless, these results highlight the potential importance of considering C. trachomatis testing in symptomatic women, as early detection of frequently overlooked infections may help reduce complications, including pelvic inflammatory disease [37, 49].

A higher prevalence of C. trachomatis infection was observed among women with a history of miscarriage; however, the cross-sectional design does not causal inference. This association may reflect cumulative sexual exposure and the persistence of undiagnosed or inadequately treated infections. Women with a prior history of miscarriage may have experienced increased exposure to C. trachomatis in high-prevalence settings, particularly in contexts of unprotected sexual activity or limited partner screening for STIs [50, 51]. These findings are consistent with previous reports, done by Baud et al. [27], who documented an increased risk of miscarriage among women previously exposed to genital C. trachomatis infection. From a pathophysiological perspective, undiagnosed cervico-uterine C. trachomatis infection can disrupt pregnancy through multiple mechanisms. However, because of the cross-sectional design of the present study, the temporal relationship between C. trachomatis infection and miscarriage history cannot be established. Chronic tubal inflammation may impair ovum and embryo transport. This process may increase the risk of ectopic pregnancy, which is sometimes clinically managed similarly to spontaneous miscarriage [52, 53].

In addition, C. trachomatis infection can weaken fetal membranes, promoting premature rupture and subsequent pregnancy loss [54, 55]. Several studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa have consistently reported associations between C. trachomatis infection and adverse pregnancy outcomes, including spontaneous miscarriage and ectopic pregnancy [44, 51]. Case–control studies further support a significant correlation between C. trachomatis infection and recurrent pregnancy loss, implicating inflammatory damage to the upper genital tract as a key mechanism [56, 57].

Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of considering women with a history of miscarriage as a high-risk group for C. trachomatis infection. Systematic screening using nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) during post-miscarriage and antenatal care, combined with targeted sexual health education, may contribute to reducing reinfection rates, pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, and other adverse reproductive outcomes. Integrating C. trachomatis testing into broader reproductive health services addresses both immediate pregnancy risks and long-term fertility consequences linked to untreated infection [52, 53].

Molecular detection using PCR, as applied in this study, offers superior sensitivity and specificity compared with conventional culture methods, which typically achieve only 75–85% sensitivity [58–60]. Two R-T PCR targets were evaluated: the cryptic plasmid and the MOMP gene. The cryptic plasmid assay demonstrated higher diagnostic performance, likely due to multiple plasmid copies (≈ 7–10 per bacterial cell) versus the single-copy MOMP gene [61, 62]. These results suggest cryptic plasmid–based PCR may represent a useful diagnostic approach for C. trachomatis detection in low-resource settings. The use of ompA-targeted PCR as a confirmatory assay strengthened the molecular diagnosis of C. trachomatis in this study. Cryptic plasmid–based PCR offers excellent sensitivity and is well suited for initial screening in high-prevalence and resource-limited settings. However, plasmid targets may occasionally detect residual DNA from non-viable organisms, which could affect specificity. Confirmation using ompA, a conserved chromosomal gene encoding MOMP, mitigates this limitation and ensures accurate species-level identification. The ompA gene is a reference target for C. trachomatis genovar characterization and reliably distinguishes urogenital strains from trachoma- and lymphogranuloma venereum–associated variants [63, 64]. By combining highly sensitive plasmid screening with ompA confirmation, our diagnostic approach optimizes both sensitivity and specificity, providing a robust and context-appropriate molecular strategy for C. trachomatis detection in Cameroon and similar low-resource settings.

R-T PCR targeting the cryptic plasmid and MOMP gene remains the most sensitive and specific method for detecting C. trachomatis. In our study, the cryptic plasmid R-T PCR showed almost perfect agreement with ompA R-T PCR assay, used as the comparator, with no discordant pairs observed, making the McNemar test not applicable, which reflects its robust reliability. In contrast, the MOMP R-T PCR, showed 21 discordant pairs. The McNemar test was significant (p < 0.001), indicating asymmetry between false-positives and false-negatives [15]. The high level of agreement likely reflects the strong concordance between assays targeting related genomic regions rather than methodological overlap, as the cryptic plasmid and ompA targets are distinct genetic loci. However, scaling this approach in resource-limited settings faces major constraints, including high equipment and reagent costs, dependence on imported consumables, the need for skilled personnel, and limited laboratory coverage [65, 66]. Recent COVID-19 experiences in Cameroon illustrate these limitations, with testing centralized in a few laboratories and dependent on external support [66].

Low-cost and decentralized alternatives, such as miniaturized R-T PCR platforms, sample pooling, and RT-LAMP, can reduce per-test costs and enable targeted screening [57, 67]. Large-scale deployment still requires sustained funding, reliable supply chains, and integration into existing systems. A tiered (pyramid) strategy appears realistic: rapid or isothermal tests at peripheral sites for initial screening, targeted R-T PCR for confirmation and follow-up at regional laboratories, and central facilities for quality control and training [65–69]. This approach balances diagnostic accuracy with operational feasibility while prioritizing high-risk populations.

Our results also highlight a major structural gap in Cameroon’s sexual and reproductive health system. Despite the serious reproductive consequences of C. trachomatis, the absence of a national screening policy and continued reliance on syndromic management may contribute to the under-detection of asymptomatic STIs. This disproportionately affects young women and underserved populations, exacerbating inequities in access to timely diagnosis and treatment. In Cameroon, the persistence of C. trachomatis infection may be influenced by multiple factors. These include early sexual debut, low awareness of transmission modes, limited condom use, and structural weaknesses in the health system. These findings support the potential value of integrating molecular STI screening into reproductive health programs, particularly in high-risk populations.

Strengths

This study employed validated R-T PCR assays targeting the MOMP and cryptic plasmid regions to assess concordance in the molecular detection of C. trachomatis. It included a diverse sample of women of reproductive age recruited from multiple cities in the West Region of Cameroon, thereby improving the representativeness of the findings. By integrating molecular, clinical, and sociocultural data, the study provides a broader understanding of factors associated with C. trachomatis infection in a low-resource setting.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between risk factors and C. trachomatis infection. Second, some adjusted odds ratios had wide confidence intervals, reflecting limited precision due to small subgroup sizes and a low number of outcome events; therefore, effect estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Third, despite measures to reduce social desirability bias, residual bias cannot be excluded. Fourth, the lack of systematic screening for other sexually transmitted infections may have introduced residual confounding in symptom attribution. Fifth, the PPV of the MOMP R-T PCR assay (62.26%) in this low-prevalence setting (12.98%), despite high specificity (96.27%), may have influenced the estimation of infection burden. Sixth, the absence of independent external validation of PCR assays may limit inter-assay comparability, and prevalence estimates may vary according to target gene (cryptic plasmid vs. MOMP). Finally, as a facility-based consecutive sample, selection bias is possible, and women attending healthcare facilities may not fully represent the general population of reproductive-age women, limiting external generalizability.

Overall, these limitations indicate that findings should be interpreted in an exploratory context and do not imply causation.

Conclusion

This study reports a prevalence of C. trachomatis infection of 12.98% among sexually active women in the West Region of Cameroon. Infection was observed to be associated with several sociodemographic and clinical factors, including educational level, ethnic group, occupation, lack of knowledge of STI transmission routes, postcoital pain, and history of miscarriage. In this study, R-T PCR targeting the cryptic plasmid demonstrated high diagnostic performance in this study and may represent a useful molecular approach for detecting of C. trachomatis. In this context, a shift from syndromic management toward molecular-based screening could be considered. Such strategies may help improve surveillance and support better reproductive health outcomes.
