Efficacy of cycloid vibration therapy for arthrogenic muscle inhibition after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction: a study protocol for a prospective, randomised controlled trial
Authors: Wei Cao, Cuiyun Sun, Ke Hu, Xiaofeng Han, Wei Jin, Rong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTIntroductionArthrogenic muscle inhibition (AMI) is a neuromuscular control disorder that occurs following joint injuries such as ligament tears, joint inflammation or postoperative conditions. It is characterised primarily by reflexive inhibition of the muscles surrounding the affected joint, substantially impeding rehabilitation. After anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR), effective rehabilitation is essential to restore joint function, facilitate a return to preinjury activity levels and reduce the risk of reinjury. Vibration therapy may mitigate AMI by modulating somatosensory input to joint and cutaneous mechanoreceptors, thereby activating neuromuscular pathways through stimulation of mechanoreceptors in the skin, articular structures and musculotendinous tissues. Accordingly, this study employs cycloid vibration therapy (CVT) to evaluate its efficacy and safety in the management of AMI post-ACLR. The findings aim to determine whether integrating CVT into postoperative rehabilitation can effectively mitigate AMI, thereby optimising recovery of neuromuscular control and functional outcomes in ACLR patients.Methods and analysisThis prospective, single-centre, randomised controlled trial will enrol 44 patients with AMI who have undergone ACLR. Participants will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either an experimental group, receiving CVT plus conventional rehabilitation or a control group, receiving conventional rehabilitation alone. All participants will complete an 8-week rehabilitation programme consisting of 24 sessions. Outcome measures will be assessed at baseline, at a 4-week interim evaluation and immediately postintervention at week 8. These assessments will include surface electromyography parameters, ultrasonographic muscle thickness, range of motion, knee joint proprioception, Visual Analog Scale pain scores, Lysholm Knee Scoring Scale, Holden Functional Classification and adverse event rates. A follow-up study will evaluate patient satisfaction and long-term outcomes, while all adverse events will be monitored and documented throughout the trial.Ethics and disseminationThe Medical Ethics Committee of Drum Tower Hospital, affiliated with Nanjing University School of Medicine, approved this study (2024-1022-02). The results will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed medical journal.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500096882.Keywords: Knee, Physical Therapy Modalities, Clinical Protocols, Rehabilitation medicine, Musculoskeletal disorders

Introduction

Cycloid vibration therapy (CVT) utilises a motor shaft within a specialised treatment head to rotate an eccentric rotor at a controlled speed, producing mechanical vibrations at predetermined frequencies. Damping elements transmit these vibrations to the treatment head surface, generating periodic directional force components along the X, Y and Z axes. When the treatment cushion contacts the patient, these mechanical forces transfer into the tissue, inducing rhythmic contraction and relaxation of muscle fibres. This process yields therapeutic benefits including improved blood circulation,1 enhanced lymphatic drainage,2 reduced limb oedema, optimised circulatory function3 and increased joint range of motion (ROM).4

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is critical for knee joint stability and is frequently injured during sports involving sudden stops, sharp turns or jumping, such as soccer, basketball and skiing. ACL reconstruction (ACLR) remains the preferred surgical intervention for restoring knee stability and function, enabling a return to athletic activity.5 The procedure aims to re-establish mechanical balance in the lower limb, recover stability after ACL rupture and protect the meniscus.6 Globally, more than 2 million ACL injuries occur annually,7 with over 4 00 000 patients seeking treatment each year.8 The USA reports at least 200 000 ACL injuries annually, approximately 120 000 of which undergo ACLR.9 China performs about 100 000 ACLR surgeries per year, ranking second worldwide in procedure volume behind the USA. Rehabilitation following ACLR continues to be a major focus of clinical research.

Although reconstruction surgery improves functional prognosis after ACL injury, it does not fully restore native ACL function or prevent long-term degenerative changes in the knee joint.10 11 Short-term recovery is often incomplete due to surgical trauma, tissue healing processes and inconsistencies in rehabilitation before and after surgery. Common postoperative issues include localised knee swelling and pain, restricted motion, substantial quadriceps weakness, persistent gait abnormalities and reduced proprioception and balance,12,15 all of which significantly affect patients’ quality of life. Therefore, postoperative rehabilitation is critically important.

Arthrogenic muscle inhibition (AMI), a frequent complication after ACLR, clinically presents as quadriceps weakness, limited contraction and compromised knee extension.16 It represents a reflexive inhibitory muscular response triggered by joint injury.17 The pathogenesis involves knee joint receptors sensing inflammatory cues such as pain and swelling, which initiate a sustained discharge process that diminishes the excitability of quadriceps motor neurons.18 This in turn hinders the central nervous system (CNS)’s ability to engage the quadriceps muscle.19 A study by Sonnery-Cottet et al20 involving 300 patients with acute ACL injuries found that 170 patients (56.7%) developed AMI. Some studies report that the incidence of AMI following ACLR can reach 100%.21 This condition significantly impedes effective rehabilitation after knee injuries and surgical procedures. The inhibition affects patients’ daily activities and elevates the risk of reinjury, particularly among athletes. Without timely intervention, AMI can persist, leading to long-term knee joint dysfunction.

Early rehabilitation following ACLR faces considerable challenges such as rapid muscle atrophy and impaired proprioception.22 AMI represents a major barrier to functional recovery after ACLR. As a non-invasive physical modality, vibration therapy shows promise for early intervention by mitigating muscle inhibition and atrophy, promoting proprioceptive recovery and regulating bone metabolism. It aids in restoring compromised proprioception, improves joint position sense and kinaesthesia, enhances dynamic stability and reduces reinjury risk.23 24 Unlike early high-load training, low-intensity vibration—particularly localised application—imposes minimal shear forces or excessive loads on the graft and joint structures, offering a favourable safety profile.22 Its non-invasive nature and ease of use further support its suitability during the early postoperative period. Previous studies have commonly initiated vibration interventions between 1 and 5 weeks after ACLR.25,27 Current evidence supports the use of vibration platforms operating at frequencies between 20 Hz and 30 Hz for therapeutic benefit.2324 28,30 In a study measuring vastus lateralis EMG responses across vibration frequencies, Cardinale31 reported peak root mean square (RMS) values at 30 Hz.

Multiple interventions exist for managing AMI, each with specific strengths and limitations. Clinical practice often adopts a multimodal strategy to optimise outcomes. Vibration training has proven effective in counteracting AMI and facilitating efficient muscle activation, thereby supporting functional recovery of the knee.22 32 33 Exploring its utility for post-ACLR AMI carries considerable clinical relevance. Evidence indicates that vibration modulates somatosensory input from mechanoreceptors in joint, cutaneous and tendon tissues.34 35 By stimulating muscular and articular receptors, vibration enhances afferent input to the CNS.36 This integrated activation of muscular and neural pathways helps alleviate AMI.37

After ACLR, alterations often occur in knee joint mechanoreceptors and their central connections. These changes can impair neuromuscular control and proprioception and hinder the recovery of preinjury function.38 Quadriceps weakness may stem from adaptations within the CNS at both spinal and supraspinal levels.39 Although cortical areas continuously interpret proprioceptive signals, ACL injury disrupts mechanoreceptor function, reducing sensory input to the brain. This decline in afferent signalling lowers motor cortex excitability and reduces quadriceps activation.40 41 Pamukoff et al42 showed that whole-body or localised vibration applied to the quadriceps immediately affects the CNS by reducing active motor threshold and increasing motor cortex excitability. Using Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (FMRI), Kapreli et al43 observed reorganisation in sensorimotor brain regions during knee movement in ACL-injured patients, marked by decreased activation in primary motor areas and increased activity in regions such as the presupplementary motor area and posterior inferior temporal gyrus. This pattern implies compensatory mechanisms involving greater cognitive effort and visual reliance due to reduced proprioceptive input. Further work by Khanmohammadi et al44 demonstrated that adding vibration to exercise significantly increases motor cortex excitability compared with exercise alone.

Vibration training supplies additional afferent input to the CNS by activating muscular and articular receptors. This enriched sensory facilitation helps reestablish neuromuscular control, improves feedforward and feedback mechanisms and supports proprioceptor recovery.42 ACL injury involves not only musculoskeletal damage but also neurophysiological changes, extending beyond purely biomechanical factors. Interventions targeting CNS reorganisation and enhanced cortical excitability may therefore yield superior outcomes. Because of AMI, exercise therapy alone often fails to achieve adequate muscle activation.45 As noted, increasing motor cortex excitability and restoring quadriceps function are essential for neuromuscular recovery, particularly in athletes seeking to return to sport. Combining exercise with vibration to provide repetitive sensory input to the CNS may enhance excitability at both spinal and supraspinal levels, offering significant therapeutic advantages.44

This study protocol introduces CVT as a novel intervention. Unlike conventional unidirectional vibration, CVT produces dynamic, multidimensional oscillations and accelerations within three-dimensional space via combined rotational and multiaxial linear motion. Its theoretical benefits include the following aspects2: complex multidirectional stimuli better replicate natural joint kinematics. This approach may more effectively activate articular and muscular proprioceptors (such as group Ia afferents), facilitating supraspinal modulation of spinal α-motoneuron excitability and countering descending inhibitory pathways related to AMI.18 Additionally, complex multidirectional perturbations demand advanced neural integration for sensorimotor processing, promoting neuroplastic adaptation within impaired circuits.44

Unlike prior studies, this trial will evaluate not only traditional outcomes (strength, anthropometrics, pain, ROM) but also use surface electromyography (sEMG) to quantitatively assess neurophysiological parameters of bilateral muscle activation—such as average electromyography (AEMG), RMS and central activation ratio. These measures will be combined with isokinetic testing, functional performance assessments and patient-reported outcomes for a comprehensive analysis. We anticipate that CVT will accelerate the resolution of AMI after ACLR more effectively than conventional rehabilitation, providing an efficient and innovative neurophysiology-based strategy to enhance postoperative recovery. This prospective randomised controlled trial will rigorously test that hypothesis. The results are expected to establish new theoretical and practical foundations for helping ACL-reconstructed patients overcome rehabilitation plateaus and achieve full functional restoration.

METHODS

Study design

This prospective, parallel-group, single-centre RCT randomised participants (1:1) by lot to either a control or experimental group for AMI treatment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Drum Tower Hospital, Nanjing University School of Medicine.

Study setting and recruitment

The study will be conducted within the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at Drum Tower Hospital, a tertiary care facility affiliated with Nanjing University School of Medicine in Nanjing, China. Recruitment is planned to occur from January 2025 to January 2027 using various methods, including posters, online platforms and social media.

All patients referred to the department first undergo an initial evaluation by a Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation specialist. Those presenting with clinical features of AMI18 46—specifically impaired muscle contraction, particularly insufficient activation of the vastus medialis (VM) and limited active knee extension—are referred to a physiotherapist for a comprehensive assessment. Eligibility for the study is determined according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients who meet all inclusion criteria, and no exclusion criteria are invited to participate. They receive detailed verbal and written information regarding the study’s procedures. Participants are informed that they will be randomly allocated to different study groups, each receiving a distinct but appropriate treatment technique for their condition, with the aim of identifying the most effective intervention. Willing participants provide written informed consent, after which the recruiting physiotherapist performs the baseline assessment.

Informed consent

Before any study-specific procedures begin, participants must provide written informed consent. The consent form details the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, as further described in the online supplemental file. Participants retain the right to withdraw from the study at any time. The researchers are responsible for obtaining consent and will ensure participants are fully informed and encouraged to complete both the treatment and follow-up phases.

Randomisation and allocation concealment

Using computer-generated randomisation numbers, we assigned patients to groups by means of sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Following each patient’s enrolment, a therapist not involved in generating the allocation sequence opened the corresponding envelope and carried out the intervention according to the group assignment.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed with PASS software, V.2024 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah) using an independent samples t-test. We selected sEMG values of the vastus medialis muscle at 30° of straight leg raise from a prior study (affected side—VM: 131.68±71.90; contralateral side—VM: 73.94±41.95).47 Parameters were configured with α=0.025 and 80% power. Assuming a superiority margin of 0, an initial calculation indicated a minimum requirement of 18 participants per group (n₁= n₂= 18) under a 1:1 allocation ratio. To account for an anticipated 20% dropout rate, the final recruitment target was increased to 22 subjects per group, yielding a total sample size of 44. Participants were consecutively recruited from the Joint Surgery Department of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital between 2025 and 2027. All subjects received standardised surgical procedures performed by the same surgical team under consistent intraoperative conditions to ensure procedural uniformity. Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to minimise potential confounding factors.

Flow

The physiotherapist responsible for recruitment performed all study evaluations while remaining blinded to group assignments. Recruitment followed the inclusion and exclusion criteria detailed in table 1. Eligible participants provided written informed consent. Baseline assessments recorded general patient information, including name, age, sex, height, weight, affected side, operation date, injury date and injury type. Participants were then randomly assigned to groups. After 4 and 8 weeks of intervention, outcome measures including sEMG, muscle thickness, ROM, knee proprioception, VAS score, Lysholm score, Holden Classification and adverse event rates were collected. Any notable occurrences during the intervention and patient dropout rates were documented. Follow-up assessments were conducted at 3, 6 and 12 months postintervention, during which additional patient data were gathered. The experimental procedure is illustrated in detail in figure 1.

Interventions

The rehabilitation protocol integrated the 2021 AAOS Guidelines for Postoperative Orthopaedic Rehabilitation48 with the clinical protocol established by the Rehabilitation Medicine Department of Drum Tower Hospital, affiliated with Nanjing University Medical School. All rehabilitation procedures were administered by a multidisciplinary team of certified therapists to ensure intervention consistency. Prior to treatment, the team provided standardised prerehabilitation education that covered evidence-based exercise protocols, cryotherapy techniques, proper assistive device use, multimodal pain management, activity modification precautions and emergency complication management. Any deviations from approved treatments or the use of discouraged concomitant therapies for symptom relief were documented.

Participants were randomly assigned to either,group A or group B. Group A received standard rehabilitation interventions, while group B was administered conventional treatment supplemented with CVT. All interventions were delivered in 60 min sessions three times per week over an 8-week period. Comprehensive descriptions of the respective intervention protocols are provided in table 2.

Data collection and analysis

Date collection

The same therapist collected data both before and after each indicator assessment, while outcome measurements were tracked and recorded throughout the study by the corresponding specialists.

Baseline data

Baseline data collection entails the comprehensive documentation of patient demographics and pretreatment clinical status. This process includes recording general patient information, acquiring preintervention sEMG signals and measuring muscle thickness of the rectus femoris (RF) and VMs. We further assess knee joint ROM, determine Lysholm knee function scores and evaluate pain levels using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Walking function is classified according to the Holden Functional Ambulation Classification, and all adverse events are systematically recorded.

Outcome measurements

Table 3 details the outcome variables, their respective evaluation time points and the corresponding measurement instruments. The primary outcome consists of sEMG parameters, specifically the RMS and AEMG values. Secondary outcomes include muscle thickness measured by ultrasonography, ROM, knee joint proprioception, VAS pain scores, the Lysholm Knee Scoring Scale, the Holden Functional Classification and adverse event rates. To minimise bias, a single physiotherapist will conduct all assessments and will also oversee participant recruitment.

Surface electromyography

The primary outcome measures derived from the sEMG data were the AEMG and RMS values.31 49 AEMG quantifies muscle electrical signal intensity by averaging the amplitude of instantaneous electromyograms over a specific time interval. This measure reflects the number of motor units recruited, their types and their synchronisation level during muscle activity. RMS represents neural discharge, calculated from fluctuations in the electromyographic signal amplitude. It is frequently associated with motor unit recruitment and the synchronicity of excitation rhythms. RMS variations generally correspond to muscular contraction force, thereby indicating the energy output of the contraction. Data were recorded simultaneously from both the healthy and affected sides in accordance with this principle. Patients maintained a long sitting posture and held an isometric contraction for 20 s, from which the most representative 10 s segment was selected for analysis. Before attaching the wireless sensors, thigh hair was shaved and the skin was cleansed. Alcohol was applied evenly with medical cotton swabs to reduce skin-sensor impedance and was allowed to evaporate fully until the skin was dry. Sensors were placed at precise locations: Sensor 1 on the left RF, sensor 2 on the left VM, sensor 3 on the right RF and sensor 4 on the right VM.50

Muscle thickness

Muscle thickness served as the primary outcome measure for evaluating improvements in muscle atrophy. We measured it using a colour Doppler ultrasound system (UMT-400), selecting the maximum cross-sectional areas of the RF and VM as representative measurement points.51 For the RF, thickness was measured at the midpoint between the superior border of the patella and the anterior superior iliac spine. For the VM, measurements were taken at the medial thigh border, one-quarter of the distance from the patella’s superior border towards the anterior superior iliac spine. An experienced ultrasonographer performed all scans, maintaining the probe perpendicular to the femoral shaft and ensuring full skin contact with coupling gel. Care was taken to apply minimal pressure to avoid compressing the underlying soft tissues. Once a clear image was obtained, muscle thickness was quantified as the perpendicular distance from the femoral shaft at the specified location. Each site was measured three times, and the average value was recorded. This method has been established as a reliable assessment technique.52

Knee proprioception

Knee joint proprioception was assessed using an isokinetic testing and training system (Computer Sports Medicine, CSMI). The passive joint position reproduction method was employed to evaluate this function in patients.53 During testing, patients wore loose-fitting sports shorts with the affected limb exposed and were seated on an adjustable chair. With the hip and knee joints flexed at 90°, the thigh, ankle and waist were secured to prevent compensatory movements. To eliminate visual and auditory cues, patients wore blindfolds and earplugs. The computer programme was configured with instructions, and proprioception was measured at 30°and 60°positions. Each test began with the knee joint flexed at 90°, and the dynamometer arm moved at a constant angular velocity of 2°/s. The angular error between the target and reproduced positions was recorded. Three trials were conducted for each position under quiet conditions, and testers refrained from providing any cues to the patients.

Knee ROM

A goniometer was used to measure the knee joint’s ROM. With the knee passively flexed to its maximum angle, the measurement was repeated three times, and the mean value was recorded.54

VAS score

Knee pain is a key indicator of functional recovery following surgery. Surgical intervention induces vascular and soft tissue injury, triggering the release of inflammatory mediators that activate peripheral nerves and produce pain.55 To evaluate resting pain, we used the VAS, a well-validated and reliable tool for pain assessment.56 The VAS rates pain intensity on an 11-point numerical scale from 0 to 10, where higher scores reflect more severe pain. Patients were instructed to select the number that best represented their current knee pain intensity at rest.

Lysholm Knee Scoring Scale

The Lysholm Knee Scoring Scale, first introduced in 1982 and revised in 1985, is a widely adopted clinical instrument for assessing knee function, particularly after ligament injuries.57 Higher scores indicate better perceived knee stability and function. This subjective questionnaire evaluates knee activity levels through eight items—limping, support, locking, instability, pain, swelling, stair climbing and squatting—totalling 100 points, with results classified as excellent, good, fair or poor. The scale has proven effective in evaluating functional outcomes after ligament reconstruction, especially in ACLR patients, demonstrating an ICC of 0.94 (95% CI).58

Holden Walking Function Classification

The Holden Walking Function Classification provides a standardised method for evaluating ambulatory capacity. It categorises walking ability into discrete levels, enabling clinicians and rehabilitation therapists to objectively assess and monitor patient progress. The classification system ranges from level 0 to 5, with each level corresponding to a specific degree of walking independence and required assistance. Higher levels reflect progressively greater walking stability.59 Level 0 indicates an inability to walk or a need for support from more than two people. Level 1 describes continuous reliance on one person for walking. Level 2 involves constant physical contact with one assistant due to unsafe ambulation. Level 3 requires supervision or verbal cues from one person without physical contact. Level 4 signifies independent walking on flat surfaces but assistance required on stairs or inclines. Level 5 represents complete independence in all environments.

Safety assessment

Throughout the trial, any adverse events will be recorded by patients, physicians and physical therapists using a dedicated questionnaire. Physical therapists and researchers will classify these events within 24 hours of occurrence as either related or unrelated to the treatment. Common adverse events may include infection, swelling or significant pain-related discomfort. Participants who experience trial-related adverse events will receive appropriate medical treatment and compensation.

Other outcomes

Patients’ compliance assessment

Patient compliance will be continuously monitored throughout the entire observation period. Comprehensive records will document all instances of loss to follow-up or withdrawal, along with their respective reasons.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be performed using SPSS V.25.0. Categorical variables will be analysed with the χ2 test. The Shapiro–Wilk test will be applied to assess normality of the data distribution. Normally distributed data will be expressed as mean±SD (X -±S), and independent-samples t-tests will be used to evaluate between-group differences at baseline. A two-way mixed Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) will be conducted to examine both between-group (intervention) and within-group (time) effects, with post hoc analyses performed using the Bonferroni method. Estimated differences and 95% CIs will be reported for planned comparisons. For non-normally distributed data, medians and IQRs will be presented and non-parametric tests will be applied. When assumptions for the two-way mixed ANOVA are violated, linear mixed models will be employed to appropriately model the data. The significance level will be set at p<0.05, with 95% CIs.

Data management and quality control

All data will be recorded using case report forms (CRFs). Before the study begins, all team members will receive comprehensive training on the study objectives, treatment protocols and quality control procedures. Strict confidentiality will be maintained for all study documents, including consent forms, screening records, CRFs and treatment logs. Only authorised research personnel will have access to the data for analysis. A dedicated monitor will perform weekly audits of the CRFs and rehabilitation records to ensure accuracy and completeness. The principal investigator will convene regular meetings to identify, discuss and resolve any issues that arise, thereby maintaining the highest standards of data quality and integrity.

Ethics and dissemination

This study has been granted ethical approval by The Medical Ethics Committee of Drum Tower Hospital, affiliated with Nanjing University School of Medicine (Reference number: 2024-1022-02). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participating patients after a detailed explanation of the study procedures, potential risks and benefits. DISCUSSION

This study investigated the therapeutic effect of CVT on AMI in patients after ACLR. While AMI following ACL injury and reconstruction is well documented,20 21 no prior studies have systematically examined CVT’s role in its management. AMI impairs quadriceps strength to a greater extent than muscle atrophy alone.21 60 Vibration therapy may counteract AMI by modulating somatosensory input from joint and cutaneous mechanoreceptors, engaging both neural and muscular systems.34 37 It has been shown to improve strength in the quadriceps and hamstrings, reduce arthrogenic inhibition and enhance proprioception and balance in ACLR patients.61 We introduced a CVT device for post-ACLR rehabilitation for the first time, aiming to improve neuromuscular control through multiaxial vibratory stimulation. The core pathophysiology of post-ACLR AMI involves disrupted communication between impaired knee proprioception and central motor commands.17 Although conventional rehabilitation—including electrical stimulation and resistance training—can partially restore strength, these methods often fail to fully engage supraspinal pathways regulating motor neurons.62 A key innovation of our approach is the potential for CVT to provide more targeted stimulation of joint capsule mechanoreceptors,63 thereby enhancing proprioceptive feedback loops linking sensory input, motor cortex activity and muscle contraction. The selected vibration frequency of 30 Hz31 64 aims to optimise afferent fibre activation and muscle resonance,65 potentially facilitating both spinal reflex pathways and muscle spindle sensitivity.

Mechanistically, vibration may ameliorate AMI through several pathways. First, high-frequency mechanical oscillation can excite periarticular mechanoreceptors such as Ruffini endings and Pacinian corpuscles,45 improving the transmission of proprioceptive signals to the spinal dorsal horn and primary motor cortex.66 Second, activation of γ-motor neurons may increase muscle spindle sensitivity,67 leading to improved recruitment threshold and synchronisation of α-motor neurons. Third, repetitive vibratory stimulation could promote synovial fluid diffusion and inhibit postoperative inflammatory mediators, indirectly alleviating pain-related inhibition. The three-dimensional circumductive pattern of CVT may offer greater functional specificity compared with conventional vertical vibration, though clinical outcomes must be empirically validated.

This trial differs from previous research in its intervention timing and comprehensive assessment framework. Whereas most vibration studies after ACLR initiate therapy at 4–6 weeks post-surgery,68 our protocol begins at 2 weeks, aligning with the early neuroplasticity window theory when spinal synaptic remodelling and cortical motor reorganisation are most active.69

Several limitations warrant cautious interpretation of our findings. Although the single-centre design ensures protocol consistency, it may limit generalisability due to regional variations in age and physical activity levels. Vibration parameters such as frequency and amplitude require careful adjustment according to individual factors like body mass and muscle composition to maximise efficacy and minimise adverse effects. The 12-month follow-up may be insufficient to evaluate CVT’s long-term preventive effect on ACLR, underscoring the need for longer term imaging biomarker studies. Future work should develop adaptive models for parameter individualisation and incorporate multimodal neuroimaging to elucidate central plasticity mechanisms. Any adverse events, including dizziness or pain, will be recorded throughout the trial. Results will be submitted for publication in accordance with Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines.70 Furthermore, combining CVT with virtual reality—for instance, through synchronous visuomotor feedback—may establish a novel sensorimotor synergy-based rehabilitation paradigm. Subsequent research should prioritise clarifying CVT’s central mechanisms, possibly using fMRI or fNIRS to map motor cortex activation, and evaluating its efficacy across subgroups such as female athletes or patients with concomitant meniscal injuries.
