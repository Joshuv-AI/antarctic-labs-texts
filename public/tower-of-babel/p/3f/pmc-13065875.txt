A characterisation of the immune cells in immunocompetent and immunodeficient mice with orthotopic brain tumors
Authors: Bryan Gardam, Eunwoo Nam, Paris M Kollis, Jasmine Kilyen‐Coles, S Lenin, Briony L Gliddon, Melinda N Tea, Stuart M Pitson, Michael P Brown, Lisa M Ebert, Tessa Gargett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesGlioblastoma is characterised by poor survival with few treatment advances for over 20 years. Immunotherapies, which have transformed treatment of other cancers, have been difficult to apply to glioblastoma because of its highly immune‐suppressive microenvironment, and there is a growing appreciation that the disease also induces a global suppression of the systemic immune system. However, immunotherapy research is hampered by the lack of detailed characterisation of the whole immune system in murine brain tumor models, with studies until now having focussed mainly on tumor‐infiltrating immune cells.MethodsHere, we have studied common murine models of intracranial brain tumors by using high‐parameter flow cytometry and tissue immunofluorescence staining to fully characterise immune cells in the brain, draining lymph node, spleen and bone marrow.ResultsThe highly immune‐compromised NSG mouse models were striking in their many immune perturbations, which extended beyond the known deficits in lymphocytes. Immunocompetent tumor models had significant changes in brain‐resident immune cells compared to controls, as expected; however, systemic effects were also observed with significant reductions in subsets of monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells in the spleen and increases in bone marrow.ConclusionOur extensive and quantitative characterisation of the immune system in murine models of glioblastoma will allow for a better‐informed selection of models and advance the search for new immune‐based treatments for this deadly disease.

Introduction

A diagnosis of glioblastoma comes with a poor prognosis and patient survival of 7–17 months. 1 There have been no life‐prolonging advances in systemic therapies beyond the standard of care in the last 20 years 2 despite research activity that has been well‐reviewed elsewhere. 3 Recently, immune‐based therapies offer an exciting new approach for treating glioblastoma. Clinical trial activity includes immune checkpoint inhibitors, oncolytic viruses, autologous vaccines and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells; but so far, there have been no durable survival benefits, 4 , 5 and it has been proposed that the immune‐suppressive microenvironment of glioblastoma is a significant hurdle. In our previous study, we explored the frequency of dendritic cells (DCs) in patients with brain tumors and found a reduction in the proportion of circulating DCs. 6 Also discovered during this study, we identified a significant reduction in the proportion of circulating T cells and B cells in primary glioblastoma patients compared to healthy donors. 6 This aligns with the systemic immune effects induced by glioblastoma reported by others. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

One key challenge for developing new immunotherapies is finding suitable animal models to test effects of treatment on both targets and bystander cells. While developing GD2‐targeted immunotherapies for brain tumors, 10 we identified a lack of detailed information on the systemic immune compartments in pre‐clinical brain tumor models. This gap in knowledge limits the ability of researchers to select the best model for investigating new immune‐based treatments for brain tumors as all such therapies will have to contend with the endogenous, disease‐perturbed immune system.

The different mouse models of brain tumors, including those generated from CT2A, GL261 and SB28 cells, are well‐characterised elsewhere. 11 Yet although this publication provides a detailed description of the advantages and disadvantages of each model, there is no characterisation of the immune compartments in these mice. Others have performed a detailed characterisation of the changes in the tumor‐localised immune cells present within CT2A and GL261 syngraft models; however, changes in systemic immune compartments such as spleen and bone‐marrow were not characterised in this study. 12 This is despite the fact that glioblastoma is now acknowledged to have profound effects on the systemic immune response. 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 The SB28 model has been investigated along with non‐brain tumor models for effects on the systemic immune system using CyTOF and was described to induce a number of systemic changes despite its intracranial location. 13 The GL261 model has likewise been investigated for systemic effects via an 11‐colour flow cytometry panel and was found to induce systemic effects on bone marrow, spleen and blood. 14 To the best of our knowledge, there is no current literature describing and quantifying the baseline state of the whole immune system in multiple syngeneic glioma models.

The immune‐deficient NOD‐SCID gamma null (NSG) mouse strain has been engineered to lack T, B and NK cells and together with a SIRPA polymorphism enables the successful engraftment of human cells and is frequently used to model human gliomas. 10 These mice are highly immune‐compromised with defects beyond a lack of adaptive immune cells: Interleukin‐2Rγ (IL‐2Rγ) allelic mutations and the NOD genetic background impair lymph node (LN) organogenesis 15 and the function of DCs and macrophages. 16 , 17 As with the syngeneic models, there has been very limited investigation of the immune system in NSG mice with engrafted gliomas.

To advance development of immunotherapies for glioblastoma, we have undertaken to fully define the baseline immune system in mice with brain tumors. Using three immunocompetent models in C57BL/6 mice and two patient‐derived xenograft models in NSG mice, we have applied high‐parameter (21‐colour) flow cytometry to systematically characterise the differences in immune cells in brain, spleen, cervical lymph nodes (LNs) and bone marrow (BM). In the course of this study, we have also investigated the impact of reporter gene expression on immunogenicity, because of reports that these transgenes can be immunogenic in some strains of mice, 18 , 19 , 20 and characterised the impact of whole body irradiation, which is frequently used to allow adoptive cell transfer in immune‐competent models. Together, our findings provide important new knowledge of the baseline immune state in mouse brain tumor models derived from cell lines widely used in the field. This knowledge can be leveraged in future studies to select the most appropriate system for testing novel immunotherapies.

Results

We used a modified high‐parameter flow cytometry panel, based on that described by Liu et al., 21 to identify the immune cells in mice with and without orthotopic brain tumors. 21 In contrast to the individual detailed flow cytometry panels provided by Liu et al., we found that with careful consideration of the available fluorophores, we could combine the individual panels into a single panel for analysis. We found this allowed for the identification of lymphocytes, granulocytes and monocytes (Figure 1a), dendritic cells and macrophages (Figure 1b) in the spleens, LNs, BM and brains of the mice, as well as of microglia in the brain (Figure 1c). Additional indicative gating for all organs in healthy and brain tumor‐bearing mice is provided in Supplementary figures 1–6, to show the dynamic expression of many markers in the presence of an inflammatory brain tumor. CD11b+ myeloid cells are a dominant population in brain tumors, and here, we have broadly defined these as classical monocytes (CD45+ CD11b+ CD172a+ Ly6C+ CD115+), microglia and microglia‐like cells (CD45int/+, CD11b+, Ly6C−) or macrophages (CD45+ Lin‐ CD11bint/+ F4‐80hi), with a recognition that some of the monocyte subset may also comprise myeloid‐derived suppressor cells. 22 Microglia are distinct from infiltrating BM‐derived myeloid lineages, although BM‐derived cells that have taken on a microglia‐like phenotype can also be captured by these markers, and it can be challenging to distinguish these cells especially in the setting of brain inflammation, where activated microglia increase expression of markers such as CD45, CD11b, and F4‐80 (Figure 4). 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Because of our aim of capturing all immune cell types in a single high‐parameter panel, we did not have the opportunity for detailed profiling of T‐cell subsets, which have been performed elsewhere. 28 Instead, these are simply divided into CD8+ and CD8− subsets. In addition, we have used a counting bead approach to enable reporting of both total numbers and proportions of immune cell types. A caveat of our study is that our tissue sampling method involves mechanical dissociation and cryopreservation, which may inadvertently preference more robust cell types, and in the case of the brain, the parenchyma, meninges, tumor and vascular‐associated immune cells will all be captured.

Enumeration of immune cell types in non‐tumor‐bearing C57BL/6 and NSG mice

First, we determined absolute numbers of different immune subsets in sex‐ and age‐matched non‐tumor‐bearing C57BL/6 and NSG mice. Here, and elsewhere throughout the manuscript, absolute numbers are presented on log scale to enable immune cell subsets of differing frequency to be considered side by side, with minimum, maximum, median, mean, 1st and 3rd quartile, interquartile range, standard deviation, standard error of the mean and 95% confidence interval available in Supplementary table 1. As expected, we identified no B or T cells in any tissue of NSG mice (Supplementary figures 7A–D). In the brains, we observed a significant reduction in the number of eosinophils in NSG mice compared with C57BL/6 mice (Supplementary figure 7A). In the other organs, we observed significant changes in every other subset (monocytes, macrophages, DCs, neutrophils and eosinophils) in NSG compared with C57BL/6 mice, apart from the DCs and neutrophils in the bone marrow (Supplementary figures 7B–D). The numerical defects observed for most immune cells of NSG compared with C57BL/6 mice suggest that prior studies of immune system–brain tumor interactions may have been limited in NSG mice, even for cells of the myeloid compartment, which have often been considered as relatively intact in these mice. 29

Immune cell frequency distribution in C57BL/6 and NSG mice with or without orthotopic brain tumors

Next, we investigated immune cells in the brain, spleen, LNs and BM of non‐tumor‐bearing (control) mice compared with mice with orthotopic brain tumors. Tumor cells were injected into the right cerebral hemisphere and allowed to grow for 3–4 weeks. All glioma cell lines were engineered to express the disialoganglioside GD2 (our target antigen of interest) by stable expression of murine GD2 and GD3 synthases. GD2 is conserved between mice and humans and is expressed at low level in normal brain of both species. 10 We compared the survival times of tumor‐bearing mice with the parental CT2A, CT2A‐luc/GFP, GD2‐CT2A, and GD2‐CT2A‐luc/GFP cell lines from previous studies and observed no significant differences in mean survival (Supplementary figure 8, Table 2). In immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice, we assessed the effect of intracranial tumors of GD2‐expressing CT2A, CT2A‐luc/GFP, GL261, GL261‐luc/GFP and SB28‐luc/GFP glioma cell lines on the proportions of different leukocyte subsets. Details of the experimental design are shown in Figure 2a.

First, we considered the relative immune composition of these tissues. This proportional analysis of immune cells as a percentage of total CD45+ cells is visually summarised in Figure 2b–e, with mean, standard error of the mean (SEM) and P‐values shown in Supplementary table 3. As expected, the most dramatic differences in immune cell populations were observed in the brain. Specifically, we detected a significant increase in the proportion of monocytes and macrophages in the brains of mice bearing CT2A, CT2A‐luc/GFP, GL261 and SB28 tumors compared with control mice, and an increase in the proportion of microglia and microglia‐like cells in CT2A tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice.

We observed a significant decrease in the percentage of B cells in the brains of all tumor‐bearing mice compared with the control mice (Figure 2b, Supplementary table 3). We also observed a significant increase in the percentage of dendritic cells in the brains of the CT2A tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice (Figure 2b, Supplementary table 3). Furthermore, we observed an increase in the percentage of T cells in the brains of CT2A‐luc/GFP, and GL261‐luc/GFP, and a significant decrease in the percentage of T cells in SB28‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice (Figure 2b, Supplementary table 3), but with no apparent decrease in the spleen or increase in the BM of T cells in tumor‐bearing mice despite previous reports 8 (Figure 2b,c,e, Supplementary table 3). Of note, we did not see significant changes in proportions of immune cell types in the spleen of the tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice, apart from in the CT2A tumor‐bearing model where we saw an increase in the percentage of T cells, neutrophils, and eosinophils. A reduction in the percentage of splenic macrophages was observed in the CT2A, CT2A‐luc/GFP and SB28 tumor‐bearing models, and a reduction in the percentage of dendritic cells was observed in the SB28‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing model (Figure 2c, Supplementary table 3). There were very few significant changes in the cervical LNs or BM between tumor‐bearing and control mice (Figure 2d and e).

In immunodeficient NSG mice, we assessed the effects of brain tumors generated from two different glioblastoma patient‐derived glioma neural stem (GNS) cell lines, CCB‐G4T‐luc/RFP and CCB‐G5C‐luc/RFP 10 , 30 (Figure 2f–i and Supplementary table 3). We observed an increase in the percentage of macrophages in the brains of mice bearing either of the patient‐derived tumors compared with control mice (Figure 2f). However, one of the most notable differences observed was the increase in ‘other immune cells’ in the spleen and LN of NSG mice, which were not detected in C57BL/6 mice; these are CD45+ cells that do not have other lineage markers and that we consider to be immature or defective cells 31 (Figure 2g–i). Other subtle changes in immune cell populations were observed between mice bearing different tumors and were assessed quantitatively below to determine the absolute numbers of these cells.

Enumeration of immune cell types in C57BL/6 with orthotopic brain tumors with or without luciferase and GFP

As the immunogenic properties of luciferase‐expressing cells have been reported as minimal in C57BL/6 mice 20 and evident in other strains, 18 we first quantified differences in immune cell composition between the luciferase‐expressing and non‐expressing GL261 and CT2A cell lines implanted as orthotopic brain tumor models (Supplementary figure 9, Supplementary table 4). T cells were increased in brains of mice with CT2A‐luc/GFP (Supplementary figure 9A) but not in brains of mice with GL261‐luc/GFP (Supplementary figure 9E), and there were also minor increases of immune cells in the spleen, for CT2A‐luc/GFP compared with the parental tumor (Supplementary figure 9B), indicating the reporter‐expressing CT2A model may be more immunogenic than the reporter‐expressing GL261.

Absolute numbers of immune cell types in C57BL/6 mice with or without orthotopic brain tumors

Although the analysis presented in Figure 3 revealed several changes in immune cell type frequencies between control and tumor‐bearing mice, it is also important to consider changes in absolute numbers. Following our analysis of the differences between the tumors expressing luciferase/GFP and those not expressing luciferase/GFP, we have focused this analysis on luciferase‐expressing tumor models because we believe that the value of longitudinal in vivo monitoring of tumor growth exceeded any potential impact that immunogenic reporter molecules might have on immune cell composition.

Figure 3a quantifies the absolute number of each immune cell type within brains of immunocompetent tumor‐bearing and control mice. First, we observed a significant increase in the number of monocytes, macrophages and microglial‐like cells in brains of CT2A‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice and SB28‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control C57BL/6 mice. Second, we observed a significant increase in T cells in brains of the CT2A‐luc/GFP and GL261‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice. Interestingly, SB28‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice were not observed to have the same increase in T cells. Third, DCs and eosinophils in the brains of SB28‐luc/GFP and CT2A‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice were also significantly increased compared with control mice.

When considering the systemic immune changes, these tumor models tended to have a decrease in total splenic CD45+ cells compared to mice without orthotopic brain tumors, indicating global perturbation of the immune system, a trend that was most evident for the SB28 model (Supplementary figure 10, Supplementary table 5). At the level of specific immune cell subsets, we observed a significant decrease in splenic macrophages of CT2A‐luc/GFP and SB28‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice (Figure 3b). Remarkably, we observed very few significant changes in any of the immune cell subsets in LNs or BM in the brain‐tumor bearing mice compared with control mice, with the exception of an increase in bone‐marrow macrophages, monocytes and DCs in the SB28 model (Figure 3c and 3d).

In summary, these analyses of immunocompetent brain tumor models reveal a significant increase in brain‐infiltrating immune cells compared with control mice, while spleens of tumor‐bearing mice show a trend towards fewer immune cells than in control mice. Of interest, CT2A and SB28 more closely resembled each other in terms of myeloid recruitment to the brain tumor, although they differed significantly in the extent of T‐cell infiltration.

Absolute numbers of immune cell types in NSG mice with or without orthotopic brain tumors

Next, we investigated the immunodeficient models. In the brain, we observed a significant increase in total macrophages and microglia in both tumor‐bearing mouse models compared with control mice (Figure 3e), which is consistent with our findings in immunocompetent models. In contrast, we observed no significant changes in spleen or LNs of immunodeficient models (Figure 3f and 3g). Finally, we observed a significant increase of neutrophils in BM of G5C‐luc/RFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice and no differences in any other cell type (Figure 3h). Overall, the presence of a brain tumor had little effect on immune cell populations in NSG mice.

Enumeration of T cell, monocyte and DC subsets in brain tumor models

Given the diversity of immune cell subtypes, we next investigated the subsets of immune cells in greater detail. We performed sub‐analyses of monocytes to enumerate classical phagocytic monocytes, non‐classical inflammatory antigen cross‐presenting monocytes, and transitional intermediate monocytes. 32 As classical monocytes are considered to be the major population in the brain, 23 , 26 in this tissue we classed CD45+ CD11b+ Ly6C− cells as microglial‐like monocytes. We also subdivided T cells into CD8+ and CD8− (in the absence of the CD4 marker) subsets, and differentiated between anti‐tumor cross‐presenting classical dendritic cells type 1 (cDC1s), pro‐tumor classical dendritic cells type 2 (cDC2s) and monocyte‐derived dendritic cells (mo‐DCs).

In the brains of tumor‐bearing immunocompetent mice (Figure 4a), we observed a significant increase in the number of classical and microglial‐like monocytes in CT2A‐luc/GFP and SB28‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control C57BL/6 mice. When analysing CD8+ and CD8− T cells separately, we observed the same trends as for bulk T cells, where compared to control mice, both subsets were increased in the CT2A‐luc/GFP and GL261‐luc/GFP models, but not in the SB28‐luc/GFP model. In DC subsets, we observed a significant increase in the cDC2s and mo‐DCs in CT2A‐luc/GFP and SB28‐luc/GFP mice compared with control C57BL/6 mice, whereas cDC1 numbers were unchanged.

In the spleen (Figure 4b), we observed a significant decrease in transitional intermediate monocytes in the CT2A‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control C57BL/6 mice. There were no changes to T‐cell subsets in the spleen, and for DCs, we observed a significant decrease in splenic cDC2s in SB28‐lucGFP mice. Within LNs, we observed a significant decrease in both CD8+ and CD8− T cells in CT2A‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control C57BL/6 mice (Figure 4c). Finally, in the BM (Figure 4d), we observed a significant increase in classical monocytes in CT2A‐luc/GFP and SB28‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control C57BL/6 mice.

We then investigated changes to subsets in the immunodeficient models. In the brain, we observed a significant increase in microglial‐like monocytes in both tumor‐bearing models compared with the control NSG mice, and a significant increase in cDC2s and mo‐DCs in the G5C‐luc/RFP mice compared with the control NSG mice (Figure 4e). In contrast, no significant changes were observed in spleen or LNs (Figure 4f and 4g), whereas we observed a significant increase of cDC2s in BM of G5C‐luc/RFP mice compared with the control NSG mice (Figure 4h).

Together, these detailed analyses reveal that most changes in immune cell subsets occur in the brain and spleen of syngeneic tumor‐bearing mice, with minimal changes in the composition of immune cell subsets in LNs and BM. Meanwhile, the NSG mice showed very few changes in extracranial immune cell subsets in the tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice.

Immunofluorescence staining of mouse brains

To confirm the presence and location of immune cells in the brains of the mice, we conducted immunofluorescence staining for CD3 and CD11b (Figure 5). Representative images of the staining include a control mouse brain (Figure 5a) and brains of mice bearing GFP‐expressing tumors (Figure 5b). The fluorescence intensity stained for each antibody was quantified separately for tumor and non‐tumor areas using FIJI (Figure 5c–5d). In all models, CD3+ cells were detected at low levels, and we observed no significant difference in the intensity of the CD3 staining in the tumor region of the brains of the tumor‐bearing mice compared with the non‐tumor area. There was also no significant difference in the CD3 intensity in brains of tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice (Figure 5c). In contrast to CD3, high‐intensity CD11b staining was observed, with a significant increase in the fluorescence intensity of CD11b staining in the tumor areas of all tumor‐bearing mice compared with the non‐tumor areas (Figure 5d).

Effect of whole‐body irradiation on immune cell types

We then considered the effects of sub‐lethal whole‐body X‐ray irradiation (WBI), which includes the brain, on the CT2A‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice. Sub‐lethal irradiation is frequently used to lymphodeplete mice and promotes engraftment of adoptively transferred cells such as CAR‐T cells. 33 , 34 Irradiation is also reported to, at least initially, reduce immunosuppression by depleting pro‐tumor immune cells such as tumor‐associated macrophages as well as increase tumor immunogenicity both by upregulating expression of damage‐associated molecular pattern molecules and inducing tumor cell death that results in release of, and increased presentation of, tumor antigens. 35 Despite routine use in adoptive T‐cell transfer studies, the baseline effects of WBI have not been well‐characterised. In contrast to adoptive cell transfer studies that often use doses of 5 Gy 36 or 6 Gy 37 , studies of the effects of whole‐body irradiation on immune cells have been limited to dose ranges up to 1 Gy (with analysis of spleen and BM) 38 or up to 2 Gy (with analysis only of splenocytes). 39

To evaluate the effects of WBI on non‐tumor‐bearing mice, we irradiated C57BL/6 mice with a range of X‐ray doses from 0.5 Gy to 5 Gy and analysed immune cells 5 days later. For initial analyses, we enumerated bulk CD45+ immune cells in spleen, peripheral blood and BM (Figure 6a) and, as expected, we observed significant decreases in the spleen and bone marrow at all WBI doses, with the most marked decrease seen at the 5 Gy dose. Although we observed no significant changes in peripheral blood numbers of CD45+ cells at any WBI dose compared with control mice, there was a clear trend toward reduced numbers of circulating CD45+ immune cells at higher WBI doses.

Next, we treated CT2A‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice with 5 Gy WBI, 7 days after tumor cell implantation. At the humane endpoint (approximately four weeks after tumor cell implantation), we compared the immune cells in these irradiated mice to CT2A‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice that did not receive WBI. In the brain, we observed continuing immune changes in the tumor‐bearing mice that received 5 Gy WBI with a significant reduction in macrophages, B cells, T cells, DCs and microglia (Figure 6b). In the spleen (Figure 6c), we observed a persistent significant reduction in B cells and T cells while monocytes, macrophages and neutrophils had increased in WBI‐treated mice compared with non‐irradiated tumor‐bearing mice. In the LNs (Figure 6d), we again observed a significant reduction in monocytes, B cells and T cells following irradiation. In contrast, the immune populations of the bone marrow had recovered and were not significantly different in mice that received 5 Gy WBI compared with control mice (Figure 6e). Taken together, this would suggest that not all immune cells have returned to normal levels at the humane endpoint approximately 4 weeks after WBI; this was seen most clearly in the tumor‐bearing brains, where many immune cell populations were still altered, and in B cells and T cells, which appear to take longer to repopulate the spleen and LNs than other immune cells.

Discussion

Although the immunotherapy revolution has transformed the treatment paradigm for many cancers, it is not standard treatment for glioblastoma patients and remains an active area of research. 5 , 40 , 41 Immunotherapy approaches such as immune‐checkpoint inhibitors may be useful in <3% of glioblastoma patients, and although CAR‐T cell therapy may have broader application, early‐phase clinical trial data are limited. 42 , 43 , 44 Further research is required to enable immune therapies to combat features of the glioblastoma tumor microenvironment that limit anti‐tumor immune activity including antigen heterogeneity and a phalanx of immunosuppressive molecules and cell types, particularly those of myeloid origin. 45 , 46 Moreover, in glioblastoma, we and others have shown immune suppression extends system‐wide and includes DC dysfunction, lymphopenia, T‐cell sequestration and elevated circulating myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). 6 , 8 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 To develop effective immune‐based treatments, it is therefore essential that we have appropriate pre‐clinical models that recapitulate these features of glioblastoma. While others have focussed on the tumor‐infiltrating immune cells in mouse brain tumor models,12 we found little information about system‐wide immune compartments, with only limited investigations in single models. 13 , 14 For this reason, we have undertaken detailed studies to examine immune compartments in control NSG and C57BL/6 mice and in the same strains of mice hosting intracranial brain tumors.

One aspect of our studies focused on better defining the immune system of NSG mice beyond the expected loss of T, B and NK cells. Previous reports have described immature macrophages and dysfunctional DCs. 16 , 17 We now build on these previous findings by revealing significant changes in the absolute numbers of several immune cell types in multiple organs when comparing NSG mice to immune competent C57BL/6 mice. In particular, we identified that monocytes, macrophages and DCs were significantly reduced in the spleens and LNs of NSG mice, and eosinophils were significantly reduced in all tissues analysed, compared with C5BL/6 mice. Interestingly, these systemic reductions did not extend to the brain.

As expected, the implantation of brain tumors in C57BL/6 mice resulted in significant changes in brain immune microenvironment, with a significant increase in classical monocytes, microglial cells, macrophages, CD8+ and CD8− T cells, cDC2s, mo‐DCs in the brains of the CT2A‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice. Further, we observed the same significant increases with the exception of T cells in the brains of the SB28‐luc/GFP tumor‐bearing mice compared with control mice. This suggests the SB28‐luc/GFP model may best represent the high myeloid and low T‐cell infiltration observed in human glioblastoma. 53 In immunodeficient NSG mice‐bearing orthotopic xenografts of patient‐derived short‐passage glioma neural stem cell lines, we observed significant increases in populations of microglia and macrophages in the brains of our tumor‐bearing models compared with control mice.

The immunofluorescence staining of the brains revealed an increase in the CD11b staining that was consistent with the increase in monocytes and macrophages detected by flow cytometry in the tumor‐bearing mice, as compared to the control mice. In contrast, the CD3 immunofluorescence staining did not show the same increase that was seen in the flow cytometry data of tumor‐bearing mice compared with the control mice, likely because of the lower sensitivity of this assay compared with flow cytometry.

Given the intriguing findings of glioblastoma‐mediated global immune suppression, 8 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 we investigated lymphoid organs such as the spleen and BM of tumor‐bearing mice. In immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice, we found that the presence of a brain tumor induced some changes in the BM; however, we did not observe the previously reported 8 sequestration of T cells in this site, although a trend toward increased T cells in the SB28 model was observed. In the spleens of tumor‐bearing mice, macrophages, cDC2s, B cells and monocyte subsets were reduced, with other cell subsets also trending downwards, in line with other reports of the systemic effects of brain tumors. 13 , 14 Our study, therefore, supports the concept that brain tumors induce some level of systemic immune suppression in mice. Despite multiple studies identifying systemic immune suppression in glioblastoma patients, the exact mechanism remains unknown and will be an important focus for further research. Others have reported that the immunosuppression is caused by large molecular weight soluble factors, 9 and a recent review proposes systemic factors are produced not only in response to brain tumors but also other intracranial pathologies, indicating a potential homestatic response mechanism to brain injury. 54 Furthermore, this effect was not observed in NSG models, where the presence of a brain tumor did not lead to systemic reductions in any immune cell types beyond the immune defects already present within this strain.

Limitations of our studies in immunocompetent orthotopic brain models include the use only of syngraft tumors, which are acknowledged to be less similar biologically to human glioblastoma than genetically engineered models. 11 Another interesting approach would be to host our human glioblastoma lines in immune competent mice by using anti‐CTLA4 and CD154 to block T‐cell rejection as described in Semenkow et al.; 55 however, such a model may not then be appropriate to test T‐cell‐targeting immunotherapies. Another limitation is the known issues in recovery of fragile subsets such as neutrophils following mechanical dissociation and cryopreservation, 56 , 57 this potential bias in cell recovery could be overcome in future studies through the employment of spatial omics that would provide an overall picture of all immune cells in the brain of tumor‐bearing mice. 58 Additionally, mice were not perfused prior to tissue collection in this study. This approach may allow residual intravascular blood cells to contribute to measured immune cell populations, potentially limiting precise discrimination between circulating and tissue immune components. In these brain tumor models, as with patients, the blood–brain barrier is disrupted, and haemorrhage is a hallmark; 11 thus, the presence of residual blood in tissue is a common feature. Nevertheless, our brain immune cell percentages align with the limited similar studies available 12 , 13 , 14 and absolute numbers were in the range reported in a recent systematic review, which included perfused and non‐perfused studies. 59 This was an endpoint study where tissues were harvested when mice displayed neurological signs, meaning that we could not report on the kinetics of immune changes that a time‐course study would permit. We also did not examine the functional status of the enumerated immune cells. Additional functional studies of immune subsets would be beneficial to determine any correlation between cell numbers and functionality, and our preliminary analysis of DC subsets suggests glioblastoma causes impaired MHCII expression, 6 a finding also identified by others. 60

Our syngeneic models also overexpress the glioblastoma antigen GD2, which is ubiquitously expressed on the GNS lines. 10 GD2 is an important immunotherapeutic target under investigation for the treatment of glioblastoma. 10 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 GD2 is a glycosphingolipid which is conserved in mice and humans, allowing toxicity testing of human‐GD2‐targeting therapeutics in mice, 65 and as such, is not expected to contribute to the immunogenicity profile. Although this was not formally tested in this study, we did not observe significant differences in mean survival times for CT2A models with and without GD2.

Another new finding in this study was the significant effects of WBI on the immune system of C57BL/6 mice. WBI is used routinely in syngraft models to achieve CAR‐T engraftment, 36 , 37 yet the global immune effects are rarely considered. We found that at the humane endpoint, approximately 4 weeks after irradiation, there were still significant perturbations in multiple immune subsets in brain, spleen and LNs of irradiated mice. Importantly, we have shown that the use of WBI in these models has effects beyond transient lymphodepletion and, in fact, in our study, there appears to be broad remodelling of the immune system following WBI, which will have consequences for any immune‐targeted treatment. We have not explored changes in immune cell populations following localised brain irradiation or stereotactic irradiation; however, these have reported effects on the immune microenvironment, 66 , 67 and for GL261 and CT2A models local irradiation also contributes to systemic immune suppression. 52

This study highlights the importance of a broad assessment of immune cell numbers when deciding which pre‐clinical model is appropriate for testing immune‐based therapies. NSG mice are routinely used for xenograft models of CAR‐T therapy because they enable human tumor and human T cells to be assessed. However, as we have shown, there are extensive differences in system‐wide immune cell composition beyond the absence of B and T cells and including a broad reduction in DCs, monocytes, macrophages and eosinophils. Therefore, it will be difficult to adequately assess the endogenous immune effects of approaches that rely on systemic immune engagement, such as cytokine‐enabled CAR‐T 10 , 68 or other combination therapy approaches. 69 , 70 , 71 However, we did observe that system‐wide reductions in myeloid cells did not apply in the brain. These data suggest that, despite numerical and functional impairments at steady state, myeloid cell populations in NSG mice are responsive to the emergency demands imposed by the pathology of a brain tumor. Consequently, these data suggest that NSG brain tumor models may be suitable for studies of immunotherapies targeting myeloid cells and for investigating interactions between CAR‐T cell therapies and the local brain tumor immune microenvironment, which is typically dominated by macrophages and microglia. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to better define the function of these brain‐resident myeloid cells in NSG mice.

In conclusion, this study presents a global overview of immune cell numbers in commonly used pre‐clinical models of glioblastoma. In considering immunocompetent syngraft models, all three models had evidence of immunogenic tumors with extensive immune cell infiltration of the brain tumor, without evidence of extensive systemic immune suppression described by others. 8 The SB28 model did appear most closely to represent human glioblastoma because the levels of T cells in the brain are the lowest of all the tumor models we investigated. Together, these findings will enable researchers in the field of glioblastoma immunotherapy to better interpret their results and we hope will support further development of immunotherapy for this devastating disease.

Materials and methods

This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. The ARRVE essential 10 checklist is included in the Supplementary information.

Patient‐derived glioblastoma cell lines

The patient‐derived GNS cell lines were generated as previously described. 72 Briefly, an aliquot of dissociated glioblastoma cells was resuspended in 3 mL StemPro NSC medium (Thermo Fisher) and transferred to a T‐25 tissue culture flask coated for 30 min at 37°C with Matrigel (Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA) diluted 1/100 in PBS. The following day, the flask was rinsed twice with warm DMEM to remove debris and dead cells, and fresh StemPro NSC was added. Cell culture details are in the Supplementary methods.

C57BL/6 syngeneic brain tumor cell lines

Parental lines CT2A (Merck) GL261 (Division of Cancer Treatment and Diagnosis Tumour Repository, NCI) SB28‐luc/GFP (DSMZ; Leibniz‐Institut DSMZ Deutsche Sammlung von Mikroorganismen und Zellkulturen GmbH) were engineered to express the GD2 antigen by transduction with ecotropic retrovirus encoding murine GD2 and GD2 synthases (MP9956:SFG.GD3synthase‐2A‐GD2synthase was a gift from Martin Pule Addgene plasmid # 75013; http://n2t.net/addgene:75013; 73 RRID:Addgene_75 013; retrovirus generated from the Phoenix‐Eco (ATCC Phoenix‐ECO CRL‐3214) packaging line), with two rounds of FACS sorting to achieve a > 99% GD2+ line. In addition, GD2‐CT2A and GD2‐GL261 were rendered GFP/F‐luciferase positive via lentiviral transduction using pBliv MSCV‐copGFP T2A fLuc and FACS sorting on GFP+ GD2+ cells. Culture details are in the Supplementary methods.

Orthotopic brain tumor models

Animal experiments were conducted under a protocol approved by the University of South Australia Animal Ethics Committee (#32‐22 & #U08‐23). A Stoelting motorised stereotactic alignment and injection unit was used to deliver 2 μL of tumor cells to the brain over a period of 4 min, 3 mm deep into the right hemisphere. Syngraft models were generated by the implantation of GD2‐SB28‐luc/GFP cells (5 × 103 cells), GD2‐CT2A cells (5 × 104 cells), GD2‐CT2A‐luc/GFP cells (5 × 104 cells), GD2‐GL261 cells (1 × 105 cells) and GD2‐GL261‐luc/GFP cells (1 × 105 cells) into 6‐ to 8‐week‐old female C57BL/6 mice (Australian BioResources, Moss Vale). Patient‐derived cell lines G5C‐luc/RFP (2 × 105 cells) and G4T‐luc/RFP (2 × 105 cells) were injected into 6‐ to 8‐weeks old female NSG mice (Ozgene, Perth). Mice received daily clinical checks and weekly bioluminescence imaging (BLI) for mice with luciferase expression to monitor tumor growth. BLI was performed using an IVIS Lumina S5 after intraperitoneal injection of 100 μL of 30 mg/mL luciferin in saline. Defined euthanasia endpoints included loss of > 15% body weight from starting weight, a body condition score of < 2 (under‐conditioned) 74 and neurological signs including head‐tilt, loss of balance and circling movement. Mice were humanely euthanised with CO2, utilising the Quietek CO2 induction system (Next Advance, USA).

Mouse whole‐body irradiation

Whole‐body irradiation was conducted in X‐Rad225 (Precision X‐ray irradiation, Madison, CT). Each dose was calculated using an internal probe to deliver a total effective dose of 0.5 Gy, 1 Gy, 2 Gy, or 5 Gy. The non‐tumor‐bearing mice were analysed 5 days post WBI. Analysis of the tumor‐bearing mice and corresponding control mice was at the humane endpoint.

Murine tissue samples

Spleens, bone marrow (from the left rear leg), superficial cervical (cerebrospinal fluid‐draining) lymph nodes and brains were harvested from euthanised mice and mechanically dissociated utilising 40 μm strainers in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 11875093) + 1% FBS (CellSera, Cat# AU‐FBS/PG). Samples were centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min and resuspended in FBS + 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma‐Aldrich, Cat# D8418) and cryopreserved for later analysis. Alternatively, brains were processed for immunofluorescence analysis by fixation for 1 h in 10% neutral buffered formalin, followed by overnight in 15% sucrose solution and overnight in 30% sucrose solution prior to embedding in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound.

Flow cytometry staining and analysis

Cells were stained with a 21‐colour panel and analysed on a BD FACSymphony A5 (BD Biosciences) with FCS Express v7 Flow Research Edition (De Novo Software, Pasadena, CA, USA). For further detail, see online Supplementary methods; antibodies utilised are in Supplementary table 6.

Immunofluorescence staining and analysis

Staining was conducted as previously described. 10 Briefly, sections were incubated with 14.6 μg/mL rat anti‐CD11b (M1/70.15; Novus Biologicals) and 4 μg/mL rabbit anti‐CD3 (Abcam) for 12 h at 4°C. Sections were washed and then incubated with donkey anti‐rat IgG 647 and goat anti‐rabbit IgG 555 at a dilution of 1:500 for 1 h at RT. For full details, see Supplemental methods.

Statistical analysis

Data were graphed and analysed using Rstudio Version 2023.06.1. The list of packages is in Table S7. Statistical analysis was conducted in Rstudio utilising the Kruskal‐Wallis test and the Pairwise Wilcoxon test with Benjamini–Hochberg multiple comparison post‐tests. P‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
