Albumin in Cardiac Surgery–Associated Acute Kidney Injury: Therapeutic Target or Epiphenomenon?
Authors: Mukul C. Kapoor
Published: 2026

Acute kidney injury (AKI) after adult cardiac surgery remains a persistent clinical challenge, with an incidence of 20% to 40% depending on diagnostic criteria and patient risk profile.[1] Even minor postoperative increases in serum creatinine are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Perioperative fluid strategy, particularly albumin use, plays an important role in its pathogenesis.[2] Hypoalbuminemia is observed in about 27% of cardiac surgery patients, and evidence indicates that it is significantly linked to postoperative AKI.[3]

Albumin correction before surgery has been practiced, but evidence does not clearly show that it reduces the risk of AKI. Albumin is commonly administered at two concentrations: 4–5% (isotonic) or 20–25% (hypertonic). 4–5% albumin is primarily used for volume expansion because it mimics plasma oncotic pressure, whereas 20–25% albumin is used for rapid plasma expansion and to mobilize interstitial fluid into the intravascular compartment, thereby reducing tissue edema. Hypertonic albumin has recently been scrutinized due to concerns about renal safety.

Critical care studies have suggested a potential association with AKI, though this has not been consistently demonstrated in cardiac surgery populations. Contradictory results have been reported between off-pump and on-pump procedures following preoperative albumin infusion in hypoalbuminemic patients.[4]

Beyond the question of whether albumin is beneficial, increasing attention has been directed toward how it is used—specifically, its concentration and the timing of administration (preoperative versus intraoperative) may also be critical.[4] Four Albumin can be administered preoperatively, intraoperatively, in cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) prime, and postoperatively. It is often used in patients with capillary leak and hypoalbuminemia and may improve fluid balance, but its renal impact is questionable. Administration in CPB prime or intraoperatively may help mitigate hemodilution and preserve microcirculation, whereas postoperative administration is more reflective of treatment than of prevention.

Albumin is more than a volume expander. It plays a central role in maintaining oncotic pressure, preserving endothelial integrity, and modulating inflammation.[5] During CPB, hemodilution reduces plasma oncotic pressure, promoting interstitial edema and impairing renal microcirculation.[6] Albumin infusion can counteract this effect and help maintain adequate circulating volume. While albumin’s physiological properties suggest reno-protective potential, clinical evidence remains inconclusive. The question of whether albumin is a modifiable therapeutic target or a marker of illness severity remains unanswered. Similarly, the timing of albumin administration is a relatively underexplored variable compared with concentration.

The integrity of the endothelial glycocalyx is critical for maintaining capillary barrier function. CPB-induced shedding of the glycocalyx contributes to capillary leak and renal interstitial edema. Albumin stabilizes the endothelial glycocalyx and may attenuate this shedding.[7] Albumin enables scavenging of reactive oxygen species generated during ischemia–reperfusion. Albumin can bind circulating toxins in plasma, such as free hemoglobin released during CPB-associated hemolysis, thereby reducing direct tubular injury.[8]

In critically ill patients, hypertonic albumin significantly expands plasma volume by increasing colloid oncotic pressure, thereby recruiting interstitial fluid, primarily via the lymphatic route. It dehydrates the interstitium and the whole body, helping to keep the intravascular space well filled. 5% albumin is more effective than 20% albumin at increasing systemic filling pressure during surgery, whereas 20% albumin increases vascular resistance.[9]

Several studies suggest that albumin may reduce the risk of AKI or improve surrogate renal outcomes by enhancing hemodynamic stability, reducing fluid requirements, and improving microcirculatory flow. Haynes et al.[10] reported that albumin administration led to lower fluid requirements, higher colloid oncotic pressure, and less pulmonary edema and respiratory impairment. The original ALBICS trial demonstrated that 20% albumin had nephroprotective effects, when given before off-pump cardiac surgery, in patients with a preoperative serum albumin level of less than 4.0 g/dl.[11] Wigmore et al.[12] found that a 20% albumin fluid bolus after cardiac surgery resulted in a smaller positive fluid balance, improved hemodynamics, lower cumulative vasopressor doses, earlier weaning from vasopressors, and better renal outcomes. A secondary analysis of the same trial found that 20% albumin did not reduce the incidence of AKI, but did reduce the severity and duration of stages 2 and 3 AKI.[12]

In this issue, Abo et al.[13] present a systematic review and meta-analysis assessing the incidence of AKI within 7 days after cardiac surgery. They conclude that infusion of 20%–25% albumin is associated with a modest but consistent increase in postoperative AKI, independent of infusion timing during the intraoperative-to-postoperative period. Similarly, numerous recent studies have shown that infusion of 20% albumin after cardiac surgery may confer no benefit and may even harm renal outcomes. Zhang et al.,[14] in a large study of over 28,298 patients, showed that 20% albumin infusion during on-pump cardiac surgery may increase the risk of AKI in patients with normal albumin levels.

The recent publication of the results of the ALBICS AKI Randomized Clinical Trial may have sealed the coffin of postoperative 20% albumin infusion in high-risk cardiac surgery patients. This multicenter, multinational, prospective randomized clinical trial had a large sample size. No clear superiority of hypertonic over isotonic albumin in preventing AKI was demonstrated. Postoperative 20% albumin infusion in patients increased the risk of AKI, and this effect was more pronounced in patients with impaired renal function.[15]

Several methodological challenges explain the persistent uncertainty and why the evidence remains inconclusive. Study designs were heterogeneous, with variations in patient populations, AKI definitions, and albumin protocols, limiting comparability. Albumin was often administered to sicker patients, biasing outcomes. Dosing and timing were variable, and there was a lack of standardization. A major reason for the inability to determine the benefit of albumin infusion was that the etiology of AKI is multifactorial, and fluid therapy is only one component among many determinants.

Based on current literature, routine use of albumin to prevent AKI in cardiac surgery is not justified, as clinical evidence remains inconsistent. Neither concentration nor timing alone appears decisive; rather, their effects are likely context-dependent and influenced by patient characteristics and overall fluid strategy. Where indicated, 5% albumin may be used intra- and postoperatively in cardiac surgery; however, 20% albumin use must be restricted.
