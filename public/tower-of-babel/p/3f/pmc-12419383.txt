Comparison of tumor clipping and marking of skin projection methods in patients undergoing breast-conserving surgery after neoadjuvant chemotherapy: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Abdullah Kut, Selman Emiroglu, Neslihan Cabioglu, Mahmut Muslumanoglu, Ravza Yilmaz, Atilla Bozdogan, Mustafa Tukenmez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:In patients scheduled for breast-conserving surgery (BCS) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the primary mass is marked with a metallic clip. A comparative study was conducted to determine the efficacy and safety of tattoo application as an alternative to this invasive procedure.Methods:Forty patients (clip: 20, tattoo: 20) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, in the group marked with clips, nonpalpable patients were marked with wire, and BCS was performed; in the tattoo group, BCS was performed with the skin containing the tattoo. All statistical analyses was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk). The study was ultimately completed with a total of 40 patients, comprising 20 patients in each group. In comparison to the standard method, the effect size of our study in reducing complications was d = 0.5, and the power was 88%. This power analysis demonstrates that the necessary number of targeted patients has been successfully reached.Results:There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of demographic and clinicopathological features in the 40 patients included in the study (P > .05). The radiological distance of the tumor to the skin was significantly lower in the group of patients who were marked with the tattoo technique than in the group of patients who underwent clipping (tattoo: 15.7 ± 7.4 vs clip: 20.7 ± 9.5) (P = .045). Reexcision requirement was lower in the tattoo group (35% vs 15%, P = .273). All 4 patients whose surgical margin closeness of the tumor was found to 1 to 2 mm, were in the clip group (P = .106). Complications (hematoma/ecchymosis) were observed in 6 (15%) patients in the clip group, and pain was observed in 5 (12.5%) patients. All patients with complications were included in the clip-marked group. Clip migration was observed in 3 (15%) of the patients. No complications were observed in the tattoo group.Conclusion:Tattooing, which is an alternative to the method of marking the mass with metallic clips (accepted as the standard today) before neoadjuvant treatment, is an easy and inexpensive method with fewer complications.

1. Introduction

Breast cancer is a heterogeneous disease owing to its biological subtypes, prognosis, and clinical behavior. Neoadjuvant therapy is frequently used to treat modern breast cancer. By applying neoadjuvant chemotherapy, can achieve pathological complete response at a rate of 26% to 65%.[1–3] Up to 30% of patients with clinically complete response do not have pathological complete response.[4] In patients with a clinically complete or near-complete response, the tumor cannot be palpated on physical examination. It is not known exactly which patients respond clinically to neoadjuvant therapy. Therefore, the primary tumor should be marked before administering neoadjuvant therapy. Generally, the standard method is to mark the tumor with a titanium-based metallic clip.[5] Complications, such as dislocation of the clip (migration), implantation of tumor cells, hematoma, ecchymosis, pain, and infection, may develop in markings with clips. In addition, in patients with clips and nonpalpable masses marked with wire on the day of surgery, specimens were used to investigate the presence of clips in the excised tissue. Specimen X-ray graph should be taken. In recent years, alternative tumor localization methods have been investigated to overcome the limitations of metallic clip marking. Skin tattooing has emerged as a viable option, offering advantages such as simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and reduced complication rates. Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of tattoo-based localization, with varying techniques including single-point and multi-spot approaches. While some reports suggest comparable surgical outcomes between tattooing and standard clip methods, differences in excision volumes and long-term efficacy remain areas of exploration.[6,7] This study evaluates a simplified single-tattoo technique applied to the skin projection of the tumor, aiming to further elucidate its clinical utility and safety in the context of breast-conserving surgery (BCS) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

2. Materials and methods

Forty-eight female patients older than 18 years who were diagnosed with cT1-T3, cN ‐/+, M0 breast cancer and were eligible for BCS planned for neoadjuvant treatment were included in the study. A total of 8 patients were excluded from the study because 4 patients underwent mastectomy after neoadjuvant treatment, and 4 patients underwent surgery in another hospital. New patients were recruited to maintain a total of 40 patients. Patient randomization was stopped when the number required to generate statistically reliable data was reached. Patients were randomly divided into 2 groups in the outpatient clinic secretariat, independent of the doctor. Randomization was performed in the form of odd or even numbers in the first application order. The primary tumoral masses in the breast of the odd-numbered ones were marked with a metallic clip marker, and the even-numbered ones were marked with a tattoo.

AC + taxane ± Anti HER- 2 protocols were administered to 98% (n = 39) of the patients as neoadjuvant systemic chemotherapy. After neoadjuvant systemic chemotherapy, wire marking was performed with preoperative imaging in patients in the clip-marked group who developed a complete or nearly complete clinical response. The volume of the piece removed during the operation was measured in cm3 with the help of a water overflow container. If necessary, reexcision was performed. In the final pathological examination of the patients after neoadjuvant therapy, the proximity of the surgical margin and need for secondary surgery were compared between the 2 groups.

Displacement of a clip >1 cm from the tumor center migration and purpura > 1 cm on the skin was defined as ecchymosis. Hematoma was investigated using USG in patients with ecchymosis or pain. A visual analog scale score of >6 points was considered to indicate pain.

Tattoos with permanent skin ink were applied in the tattoo group in the operating position as seen in Fig. 1. No additional procedure was performed in the patient who underwent tattooing before the operation. During surgery, an incision was made on the skin to include the tattoo, and the breast tissue was removed. The volume of play was measured in cm3 in the overflow vessel as seen in Fig. 2.

When evaluating the groups, the statistician did not know which group the patients belonged to. While evaluating the findings obtained in the study, Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk) program was used for the statistical analysis. Descriptive statistical methods (number, percentage, mean, standard deviation, etc) were used to evaluate the study data. The proportional differences between the groups were analyzed using the Chi-square test, and quantitative differences were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The results were evaluated at a 95% confidence interval and the significance level of P < .05.

4. Discussion and conclusions

In the literature, there is not enough consensus to achieve negative surgical margin and good cosmetic results at low cost in nonpalpable breast tumors. Although many innovative surgical techniques are being investigated, more clinical studies are needed for better results.[8,9]

The procedure of placing a metallic clip marker in the center of the tumor, usually in the same session as a tru-cut biopsy, is accompanied by imaging. Complications such as hematoma, ecchymosis, pain, and infection are specific to the clip placement procedure, as well as clip migration and tumoral planting along the tract where the clip is placed. In the mammography of the specimen taken during the surgery, the clip may not be visible in up to 4% of the cases.[10] It should also be noted that the location of the clip thought to be lost can be safely found by ultrasonography (USG) used during surgery.[11] Even without clip migration, the clip may not have remained in the residual tumor after neoadjuvant therapy. Edeiken et al reported a rate of 39%.[12] There have also reported that the clip can be viewed easily before these complications develop, and it is a cost-effective method.[13,14] It should be noted that in 20% of the cases, the clip may be migraine.[5] Marking of the mass before neoadjuvant with more than one radiopaque clip does not appear to be superior to one clip in terms of pathological surgical margins and reexcision rates.[15] If the tumor cannot be palpated in patients with clips after neoadjuvant treatment, the area with the clip is marked with a wire on the day of surgery, and the specimen is removed during surgery. Graph is drawn. It should also be known that in this method, which is safe, inexpensive, and easy to apply, the wire may migrate, come off, break, and the wire extending out of the breast may disturb the comfort of the patient. There are also methods using radioactive core localization (Iodine125 labeled titanium core), nonradioactive radar localization, and magnetic core localization (Magseed, Endomag, Cambridge, UK), where patient comfort is better and the risk of migration is lower. The marked area was detected during surgery using a gamma probe and a special detector. The removed area was confirmed by specimen mammography. However, the necessity of these secondary methods increases the cost and requires a radiologist, a nuclear medicine specialist, and additional equipment.

In this randomized prospective study, the standard method of marking breast tumors with metallic clips in breast cancer patients undergoing BCS after neoadjuvant therapy was compared with the tattooing method. While 15% complications were observed in the clip-marked group, no complications were observed in the tattoo group. There was no statistically significant difference between the 2 methods in terms of reexcision, surgical margin, mean volumes of removed pieces, and secondary surgical intervention. Wire marking was not applied to any patient in the tattoo group, whereas 40% of wire marking in the clip-on group. The unit patient and total cost in the clip-applied group were much higher than those in the tattoo group. These findings showed that the tattoo technique is a less invasive, easily applicable, inexpensive, safe method that does not require a radiologist, compared to the clip marking method.

There were no complications of metallic clips, no secondary marking method before surgery, and no need for a radiologist using the tattoo method. Since local anesthetics and tattoo dyes are used, there may be a risk of developing an allergic reaction in the patient. However, there is no information regarding this in the literature.

Espinosa-Bravo et al compared 118 tattooed patients and 31 metallic marking groups in terms of lumpectomy volume and surgical margins removed by BCS after neoadjuvant chemotherapy. This is the largest study reported in the literature that compares the marking method with tattoos and metallic markers. The tattoo technique in this study was different from our study. Before neoadjuvant chemotherapy, spot tattoo staining was performed around the palpable tumor (superior, inferior, lateral, and medial of the mass). However, in our study, the tattoo was applied to the middle of the skin projection of the palpable tumor, which was smaller than the mass. There was no difference in the metallic clip application techniques. In this study, the rate of pathological complete response was higher than in our study (45% vs 37.5%). The size of the primary tumor in the breast was T2 in most patients in both studies. In this study, the mean lumpectomy volume was 268 cm3 in the tattoo group and 143 cm3 in the clip group. This difference was statistically significant (P < .004). In our study, however, no difference was found between the 2 groups in terms of the volume of extracted play. While the volume of the piece removed in the tattoo group was 89.5 cm3 on average, it was 83.4 cm3 in the clip group. This difference was not statistically significant (P = .87). The fact that the volumes were less in our study and not significantly different between the 2 groups may be related to our tattoo technique. In this study, there was no statistically significant difference in terms of the surgical margins, as in our study. However, Espinosa-Bravo et al reported a positive surgical margin in 7 patients in the tattoo group and in 1 patient in the clip group. Although they removed more volume in the tattoo group, they found more positive surgical margins in the tattoo group. However, no significant differences were observed. In our study, the surgical margin was not positive in any of the patients in the tattoo group, and the surgical margin in all patients was ≥2 mm. On the contrary, in our study, the surgical margin was measured between 1 to 2 mm in 4 patients (20%) in the metallic clip group. However, this difference was not statistically significant (P = .106). Unlike our study, no complications were reported in the group that underwent metallic clips. In our study, while no complications developed in the tattoo group, one of the complications such as hematoma, ecchymosis, and pain developed in 15% of the patients in the metal clip group (P < .05). In this study, it was concluded that marking with metallic clips is a more appropriate method, and no surgical benefit of marking with tattoo has been demonstrated because marking with tattoo involves more lumpectomy volumes. However, in our study, marking with metallic clips did not have any advantage over the marking method with tattooing, and it was a disadvantageous method in terms of complications.[7]

Lannin et al in 34 breast cancer patients. Before neoadjuvant treatment, Espinosa-Bravo et al applied the skin projection of the tumoral mass to the palpable area as a point-based tattoo. They reported that they archived them by photographing them in different ways. Lumpectomy was performed in 81% of the patients. However, they reported that they were able to obtain a negative surgical margin in only 91% of the patients in the first operation. However, in our study, none of the patients with tattoos or metallic clips had positive margins. Since no tumor was observed in pathology in only one patient with metallic clip migration, the radiological findings were reevaluated, and the area thought to be the tumor bed was marked with a wire and excised. In the final pathology, the tumor was fully pathologically responsive to neoadjuvant therapy with regression findings. None of the other patients required a secondary surgery. In this study, it was also reported that mastectomy was performed in 2 patients due to persistent surgical margin positivity. Our study did not include these cases. In their study, clinical and pathological complete responses were reported as 56% and 22%, respectively, and the pathological response rate was found to be lower than our study (37.5%). This study highlights that tattoos can reveal the original tumor boundaries. Multiple tumors occur when the tumor responds to neoadjuvant therapy in a scatter pattern like Swiss cheese, instead of concentric reduction. The residual tumor foci may be radiologically complete. Because the tattoo clearly indicates the location of the original tumor, it was stated that when this marked area is removed, it can be removed in the residual tumoral areas. As a result, it is hoped that good cosmetic results will be obtained and local recurrences will be reduced using the tattoo marking technique in this study by Lannin et al.[6]

In Turkey, the prices of consumables in state hospitals were considered within the framework of social security institution insurance. In the clip group, metal clips were utilized, whereas in the tattoo group, materials such as wiring wire, paint, plastic paint containers, and sterile tattoo needles were employed. This study calculated that the cost per patient was 160 USD for those who underwent clip procedures, while the cost was approximately 3 USD per patient for those with tattoo marking. Procedural fees, personnel costs, and other institutional charges were excluded from this analysis, as the study focused solely on comparing the direct material costs between the 2 localization techniques. Although these findings could not be proven with total cost-effectiveness analysis, it was predicted that the forging method would be more cost-effective. Today, instead of cheaper surgical methods, increasingly costly procedures are remarkable. Artificial intelligence will soon help locate marked tumor areas in breast surgery using augmented reality technology. However, this technology is still expensive and not yet feasible.[16,17] Therefore, this situation can be shown among the aspects that strengthen our work. In addition, the small number of patients and lack of cost-effectiveness analysis are among the important limitations of our study.

If we look at other methods reported in the literature, the radioactive seed marking method was not widely used in our country before neoadjuvant treatment because of the cost, radiation risk, and strict waste management rules after the procedure. Although the radioactive seed localization marking method is cheaper and more practical, it has not been widely used because of radiation and strict waste regulations. If the mass is marked with a sonographically identifiable hygroscopic clip before neoadjuvant therapy, the marked area can be safely removed with intraoperative USG during surgery. In clinics that use intraoperative USG, marking the mass with a hygroscopic clip can be used as an alternative method. In the mapping technique, before neoadjuvant treatment, the location of the mass was marked topographically on the patient’s breast in the operating position, and its photograph was taken, and the image was archived. After neoadjuvant treatment, the drawing was repeated before the operation, and the area with the mass was removed following the principles of BCS Although this method has not yet been reported in the literature, it is known to be used by experienced surgeons. Our study demonstrates that marking can be performed on the skin after brief training.

This study has several important limitations. First, despite being a randomized controlled trial, the modest sample size (n = 40) may have compromised statistical power, potentially obscuring significant differences in key outcomes such as re-excision rates and margin status. Second, the single-center design may limit generalizability, underscoring the need for multicenter validation with larger cohorts. Third, our cost analysis focused exclusively on direct material costs while excluding procedural and personnel expenses, which may affect the comprehensive economic assessment of these techniques. In addition, an allergic reaction in the tattoo technique can lead to serious comorbid conditions. Finally, the study duration did not permit evaluation of long-term outcomes including local recurrence rates and cosmetic results associated with the tattoo localization method. Future prospective studies with extended follow-up periods should address these clinically significant endpoints.

In this randomized controlled trial comparing clip marking and skin projection tattooing for tumor localization in BCS after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the tattoo method demonstrated significant advantages in safety and cost-effectiveness. Our findings indicate that tattooing was associated with fewer complications (0% vs 30% with clips, P = .020), including no instances of hematoma, ecchymosis, or pain, which were observed exclusively in the clip group. While no statistically significant differences were found in re-excision rates (15% vs 35%, P = .273) or surgical margin status (all tattoo margins ≥ 2 mm vs 20% clip margins 1–2 mm, P = .106), the tattoo group required no wire localization (0% vs 40%, P < .001) and incurred substantially lower direct material costs (3 USD vs 160 USD per patient). However, the study’s modest sample size (n = 40) and lack of long-term follow-up limit definitive conclusions about oncologic outcomes such as local recurrence. These results suggest that tattooing is a safe, cost-effective alternative to clips for tumor localization, particularly in resource-limited settings, but further multicenter studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate its non-inferiority in margin control and long-term survival.
