Dissociative Oxygen Adsorption and Incorporation in Co3O4-Dispersed BaZr0.9Sc0.1O2.95 for PCFC Cathode
Authors: Shinnosuke Kamohara, Akihiro Ishii, Itaru Oikawa, Hitoshi Takamura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of air electrodes with superior surface oxygen exchange properties at intermediate temperatures is crucial for improving the efficiency of protonic ceramic fuel cells. This study evaluated the surface exchange properties of Co3O4 dispersed protonic conductors, BaZr0.9Sc0.1O2.95. Although Co3O4 is widely acknowledged as superior dissociative adsorption catalysts, there is still ambiguity regarding the enhancement mechanisms of their surface exchange properties by Co3O4, as well as their optimal composition to achieve high catalytic activity. To overcome these difficulties, this study elucidated the effect of the chemical states and composition of composites on their surface exchange properties by evaluating their chemical states and surface exchange reaction rates with several compositions prepared at different temperature conditions using a vibrating-sample magnetometer and the pulse isotope exchange technique. For samples annealed at a high temperature, it became evident that the surface exchange activity became the most active by adding only 1 vol % Co3O4 and indicated an abrupt decline above this composition despite an increase in the volume of the catalysts. This was attributed to the combined effect of the high dissociative adsorption activity of the Co-containing solid solutions formed at a high temperature and a decrease in oxygen vacancies due to hole compensation. For samples annealed at intermediate temperature, their chemical states remained unchanged from those of the original milled powders, and their surface exchange properties monotonically improved with an increase in the volume of Co3O4. Based on the results, different chemical states of composites derived from different preparation conditions lead to completely different activation behavior of the surface exchange reaction.Keywords: protonic conductors, surface oxygen exchange reactions, composite electrodes, isotope exchange reactions, cobalt oxides, magnetic susceptibility

Introduction

Protonic ceramic fuel cells (PCFC), which utilize a proton-conducting electrolyte, have garnered substantial interest for their potential in future efficient fuel-cell technology.1−6 It offers the possibility of lowering the operating temperature to approximately 400–600 °C by the higher mobility of protons compared with that of oxide ions.2,3,7 Consequently, advantages including reduction in the cost of cell system by the removal of elaborate heat-tolerant materials, shorter startup time, and longer stability of cell materials under operating conditions are expected.1−3,5 However, the reduction in the operating temperature of PCFC compared with that of typical SOFC has a drawback, as it diminishes the cell performance owing to slower cathode reaction kinetics, particularly in the surface oxygen exchange reaction.3,8−12 Therefore, substantial efforts have been made to explore cathode materials that exhibit superior surface exchange properties at intermediate temperatures.

To improve their cathode properties, a variety of functional oxides, including mixed oxide ions, electronic conductors (MIECs),9,10,13−16 and triple conductors,17−20 which have mobile oxide ions, protons, and holes, have been developed. Among the MIECs, the Co-doped perovskite-type oxides such as La1–xSrxCoO3-δ (LSC),15,21,22 La1–xSrxCo1–yFeyO3-δ (LSCF),23,24 and Ba1–xSrxCo1–yFeyO3-δ (BSCF)13,15,25 were reported to show fast ionic diffusion rate and rapid surface exchange reaction rate. Among the triple conductors, BaCo0.4Fe0.4Zr0.1Y0.1O3-δ (BCFZY) exhibited a diffusion coefficient D of 2.9 × 10–5·cm2·s–1 and an oxygen surface exchange coefficient k of 1.85 × 10–3·cm·s–1 at 600 °C, which were approximately 100 and 2300 times higher than those of Ba0.5Sr0.5Co0.8Fe0.2O3-δ (BSCF), respectively.26,27 These excellent electrochemical properties are attributed to the enlargement of the active sites for the exchange reaction from triple phase boundary between the electrode, electrolyte, and gas to the entire surface of the cathode.18,26

Other approaches to improve the performance of PCFC cathodes include the preparation of composite oxides consisting of single-phase protonic, ionic, mixed ionic-electronic, and poor anion conductors.28−32 Murray et al. indicated that the catalytic activity of La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3-δ (LSCF)-50 vol % Ce0.8Gd0.2O2−δ (GDC) surpassed that of bare LSCF, recording the polarization resistance (Rp) of 0.33 Ω·cm2 at 600 °C and the activation energy decreased from 142 to 122 kJ·mol–1.29 In other instances, composites of typical MIECs and poor anion conductors, Ba0.5Sr0.5Co0.8Fe0.2O3-δ (BSCF)-10 vol % Co3O4, achieved Rp of 0.049 Ω·cm2 at 600 °C and the activation energy of 90.3 kJ·mol–1 which are 0.54 and 0.84 times smaller than that of bare BSCF, suggesting the role of Co3O4 as effective catalysts.31 Previously, our group elucidated the origin of the excellent catalytic activity of Co3O4 in the dissociative adsorption of oxygen at elevated temperatures, which was comparable to that of Co-containing MIECs.10

Based on these results, this study focused on whether Co3O4 dispersed composite protonic conductors exhibit superior surface exchange properties that are applicable to efficient PCFC cathodes. However, several issues must be addressed in terms of their application to PCFC cathodes. First, the mechanism by which the presence of Co3O4 in the composites promotes the catalytic activity is ambiguous. Because the oxide cathode requires a high-temperature baking process for cell preparation, the surface exchange property of the original composites could change owing to factors such as the formation of solid solutions, decomposition to other oxide phases, and grain growth and coarsening.9,33−35 Furthermore, there is no consensus regarding the optimal concentration of Co3O4 for achieving a high catalytic activity of the PCFC cathode. Therefore, this study focused on the effect of chemical states and compositions of composites on their surface exchange properties by measuring the surface exchange reaction rate of typical protonic conductors BaZr0.9Sc0.1O2.95 (BZS10) with x vol % Co3O4 (x = 1, 3, 5, and 10) prepared at different temperature conditions, using the pulse isotope exchange (PIE) technique proposed by Bouwmeester et al.36 The chemical states of the Co cations in the composites were identified using a vibrating-sample magnetometer (VSM)37 at low temperatures, which is generally challenging using typical microstructural and compositional analyses for small Co contents, such as 1 vol %.

Materials and Methods

BaZr0.9Sc0.1O2.95 (BZS10) was prepared using a solid–state reaction method with BaCO3 (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, 99.95%), ZrO2 (Kishida Chemical, 99.9%), and Sc2O3 (Kojundo Chemical Laboratory, 99.7%). The raw materials were mixed in isopropyl alcohol using a planetary ball-milling apparatus (Pulverisette 7 classic line, Fritsch) composed of yttria-stabilized zirconia at 300 rpm for 2 h. After solvent evaporation, the powders were compacted using a hydraulic uniaxial hand press and calcined in air at 1300 °C for 10 h at a heating rate of 5 °C/min. After calcination, the pellets were coarsely ground in an agate mortar and finely ground in a ball mill at 300 rpm for 12 h. To prepare the composite powders, BZS10 powders were ball-milled with x vol % Co3O4 (x = 1, 3, 5, and 10) at 300 rpm for 30 min.

To prevent changes in the morphology and composition of oxides during PIE measurement, all powder samples were annealed at a temperature slightly higher than the maximum measurement temperature of the PIE. In this study, annealing conditions of 880 °C for 6 h and 630 °C for 6 h were chosen to evaluate the effect of the heat-treatment process on the surface exchange properties of the composites. The preparation of the composites with stoichiometric ratios was confirmed by ICP–MS analysis (Agilent 8800), as summarized in Table S1. The phases present in the powder samples were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD; D8 Advance, Bruker AXS) using Cu Kα as the X-ray source, with the current and voltage set at 40 mA and 40 kV, respectively. The specific surface area of the powder samples was evaluated using nitrogen adsorption isotherms at 77 K (BELOSORP18Plus, MicrotracBEL). The dispersion of Co3O4 in BZS10 was analyzed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) with field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; JSM-IT800, JEOL). Furthermore, to evaluate the chemical states of the Co cations in the composites more precisely, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; AXIS-ULTRA, Shimadzu) and a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM; PPMS-7DFD, Quantum Design) were utilized. XPS spectra were calibrated by a C 1s reference set at 284.6 eV. Isothermal magnetization measurements were performed at 10 K by varying the applied magnetic field from −30 kOe to 30 kOe. Temperature-dependent magnetization measurements were also performed at an applied magnetic field of 30 kOe by varying the temperature from 10 to 300 K.

The catalytic activity of composites was evaluated using the PIE technique which is the direct evaluation technique for the overall surface exchange reaction rate of either MIECs or ionic conductors, employing the isotope oxygen 18O2 as a marker.10,36,38,39 The process involves the injection of pulse isotope oxygen gas, which then undergoes two elemental steps, i.e., dissociative adsorption (O2 + 2ne ↔ 2Oadn–) and incorporation (Oadn– + (2 – n)e ↔ Olat2–), indicating a change in the oxygen isotope fractions before and after the surface exchange reaction. Hence, analyzing these fractions enables the computation of the overall surface exchange reaction rate of oxide samples, as well as the discussion of the elemental steps involved in the process. PIE measurements were performed in a 10% oxygen atmosphere. For the base gas, N2-10% 16O2 was flowed at a flow rate of 50 sccm. For the pulse gas, 1 mL of He-10% 18O2 was stored in a loop tube. Approximately 50 mg of the powder sample with particle diameters ranging between 106 and 212 μm was filled into a quartz tube with an inner diameter of 3 mm and was sandwiched by quartz wool to fix its position (Figure S1). The PIE measurements were performed during the cooling process with a 25 °C decrement. For the powder samples annealed at 880 °C, the temperature range was 700–200 °C. For those annealed at 630 °C, the temperature range was 600–200 °C. The composition of the oxygen isotope gases was analyzed using a quadrupole mass spectrometer (QMS; QMG422, Pfeiffer).

Results and Discussion

The appearance of the powder samples prepared at intermediate and high temperatures is shown in Figure 1a,b, respectively. While the powders annealed at an intermediate temperature (630 °C) did not show any color change from the original ball-milled powders, those annealed at a high temperature (880 °C) became more brownish. This color change implied the different chemical states of the composites. To clarify these differences, XRD was performed for BaZr0.9Sc0.1O2.95(BZS10)-x vol % Co3O4 (x = 1, 3, 5, and 10) annealed at 630 and 880 °C, as shown in Figure S2a,b. All the samples exhibited peaks assigned to a cubic perovskite structure. At x = 1 and 3, the peaks attributed to Co3O4 were invisible owing to its low loading. In contrast, at x = 5 and 10, these peaks became visible, suggesting the presence of Co3O4 as a secondary phase. The lattice constants of the cubic perovskite phase in the composites were computed as a function of Co3O4 volume fraction (Figure S3). The lattice constants of powders annealed at high temperature exhibited a slight decrease, which is analogous to the profile of Co-containing BZS10 solid solutions investigated by Uehara et al.,40 while those annealed at intermediate temperature displayed no changes. These results may imply the possibility of the dissolution of the dispersed Co3O4 due to annealing at a high temperature. However, since various factors such as the hydration level are intricately involved in the differences in the lattice constants of protonic conductors,40,41 further investigations, especially cautious observation of Co3O4 distribution, are necessary to detect the cause of changes in the chemical states of composites prepared under different temperature conditions.

BZS10-x vol % Co3O4 composites prepared at (a) 630 °C for 6 h and (b) 880 °C for 6 h. x increases in chronological order from the left to the right side of the figure.

Morphologies and particle sizes of the composites affect the surface exchange properties; therefore, it was observed for all powder samples using FE-SEM. SEM images of composites with various compositions and annealing conditions are shown in Figure S4. Prepared powders had similar morphologies and exhibited a highly porous structure with fine BZS10 particles. To clarify the powder morphologies and particle sizes of composites in detail, high-magnification SEM images of BZS10–3 vol % Co3O4 and BZS10–5 vol % Co3O4 annealed at 630 °C for 6 h and 880 °C for 6 h were shown in Figure S5. Regardless of their compositions and annealing conditions, agglomerated fine BZS10 particles approximately 100 nm in diameter were confirmed. Although prepared composites indicated similar morphologies clarified from SEM images, the decrease in their BET-specific surface areas (Table S2) for composites subjugated to high temperature implies grain growth and coarsening. This is understood to be because the dispersed Co3O4 acts as a sintering aid42 to promote sintering.

In this study, the states of dispersed Co3O4, that is, dispersed as a secondary phase or dissolved into the BZS10 matrix, are crucial for understanding its role in surface exchange properties. Several observation techniques have been attempted to clarify the Co distributions. First, the distribution state of Co3O4 was investigated using EDS in the high-magnification mode. Figure 2 shows high-magnification EDS maps of BZS10-3 vol % Co3O4 and BZS10-5 vol % Co3O4 annealed at 630 °C for 6 h and 880 °C for 6 h. For 3 vol % 630 °C (Figure 2a), Co is localized in certain areas, and it corresponds to Co3O4 particles of about 680 nm with faceted structures by comparing the Co map and the SEM image. In contrast, for 3 vol % 880 °C (Figure 2b), Co is uniformly dispersed as well as Sc. Because such nanolevel dispersion is generally unachievable through ball-milling,49,50 it is reasonable to assume that dispersed Co3O4 undergoes certain changes in its chemical state. Furthermore, by combining these results with the trend line of the lattice constants, dispersed Co3O4 likely dissolved and formed a solid solution. For 5 vol % Co3O4, localization of Co was observed regardless of the annealing temperature (Figure 2c,d). Co3O4 particles with diameters of 320–340 nm were confirmed from the corresponding EDS maps and SEM images.

EDS maps of BZS10 with 3 vol % Co3O4 prepared at (a) 630 °C for 6 h and (b) 880 °C for 6 h, and BZS10 with 5 vol % Co3O4 prepared at (c) 630 °C for 6 h and (d) 880 °C for 6 h.

Since only XRD and EDS analyses could not provide consolidated insights regarding the chemical states of the composites prepared under different heat treatment conditions, another approach to precisely recognize these differences was taken. This study elucidated the state of Co by isothermal magnetization measurements at low temperatures and temperature-dependent magnetization measurements. Co3O4 exhibits antiferromagnetism derived from the superexchange interaction between Co2+ ions at the t2g level and has a Néel temperature (TN) of approximately 30–40 K.43−45 Thus, the presence of TN is a good indicator of whether Co3O4 remains in the composites or is decomposed and transformed into other chemical forms, such as solid solutions. First, the magnetic susceptibility χ of the composites without heat treatment, with heat treatment at 630 °C, and with heat treatment at 880 °C was computed from the isothermal magnetization curves at 10 K (Figure S6) and summarized in Figure 3a. For samples without annealing, χ increased linearly as a function of the Co3O4 content because only antiferromagnetic Co3O4 affected the total magnetization of the composites. The magnetic susceptibility of the samples annealed at 630 °C was exactly on this linear trend, which implies that the composites did not undergo any compositional changes from the original powders. Powders annealed at 880 °C, on the contrary, indicated that the magnetic susceptibility was significantly higher by approximately an order of magnitude than that of the mixed powders without annealing and those annealed at 630 °C, suggesting a change to other magnetic phases. Note that this phase change of cobalt cannot be clearly detected by XPS (Figure S7), suggesting no notable change in the valence state due to this phase change.

(a) Magnetic susceptibility obtained from the isothermal magnetization curve of composites annealed at 630 and 880 °C and without an annealing process as a function of Co3O4 content. Temperature dependence of magnetization curves of composites with 1, 3, 5, and 10 vol % Co3O4 annealed at (b) 630 °C and (c) 880 °C.

To investigate the magnetic properties of the composites in more detail, temperature-dependent magnetization measurements were conducted for all annealed powder samples (Figure 3b,c). For the composites annealed at 630 °C, profiles associated with antiferromagnetism, which exhibited a sharp increase in magnetization at low temperatures and a maximum of approximately 40 K corresponding to TN, were obtained. However, the composites annealed at 880 °C exhibited different profiles, depending on the Co3O4 content. Below x = 3, a temperature dependence typical for paramagnetic materials46,47 was observed, despite the initial dispersion of antiferromagnetic Co3O4, whereas a small cusp of magnetization emerged at approximately 40 K above x = 5.

Based on these results, a schematic describing the chemical states of the composites at each composition and annealing temperature is shown in Figure 4. For the samples prepared at 630 °C, dispersed Co3O4 remained in BZS10 as a secondary phase, regardless of its composition. For the composites prepared at 880 °C, the change in their magnetic phases from antiferromagnetic to paramagnetic below x = 3 implies a change in the chemical state of dispersed Co3O4. By combining the results of XRD, EDS, and VSM analyses, it seems reasonable to say that annealing composites at a high temperature causes the decomposition of Co3O4 and the dissolution of Co into BZS10. The formation of a solid solution at higher temperatures was substantiated by the study of Co and Sc codoped barium zirconate performed by Uehara et al. and the study of interface reactions using PCFC cells by Mikami et al.40,48 According to the phase analysis of BZS10 with various Co dopant concentrations, the trend line of the lattice constant followed Vegard’s law with [Co] less than 0.2, suggesting the formation of a Co-containing solid solution. Because the lattice constant in this study showed a similar trend for composites annealed at high temperatures, the formation of a solid solution is also implied. According to the cross-sectional electron probe microanalyzer measurement of PCFC cells comprising the LSCF cathode, BZYb electrolyte, and NiO-BZYb anode, the presence of Co in the whole electrolyte region was confirmed, suggesting the diffusion and dissolution of Co from the decomposed LSCF cathode to the BZYb electrolyte.48 In this study, the decomposition of Co3O4 was also confirmed by the change in its magnetic phase from antiferromagnetic to paramagnetic, and the presence of Co in the region of the entire composite implied the formation of a Co-containing solid solution. The dissolution of Co into BZS10 generated holes owing to the charge compensation. Moreover, a proton defect was introduced by consuming oxygen vacancies during the hydration reaction. Considering these, composites annealed at 880 °C are expected to show both proton and hole conductance; however, the amount of these defects is not clear. Therefore, this study refers to Co-containing solid solution mixed conductors (MCs) in a broad sense with the notation BZS10Cδ, where δ is the molar ratio of the Co cation.40,48 Above x = 5, the composites formed the solid solution, yet some of Co3O4 remained as a secondary phase because the composites already reached their solubility limit.

A schematic describing the chemical states of composites prepared under different conditions.

The surface exchange properties of the composites with different chemical states and various compositions of Co3O4 were evaluated by PIE under a 10% oxygen atmosphere. To ensure the reproducibility of the data, measurements were performed multiple times for each composition. As examples, the temperature-dependent isotope fraction curves of BZS10-1 vol % Co3O4 prepared at intermediate and high temperatures are plotted in Figure 5a,b, respectively. To avoid the difficulty of comparing isotope fractions for each sample owing to the change in the initial isotope fraction at every attempt, the fraction of each isotope gas species was normalized to that of the initial isotope oxygen atom 18O, denoted as Fi = fi/f18in (i = 36, 34, and 18). As shown in Figure 5a, no changes in isotope fractions were detected below 300 °C, suggesting that the reaction rate was lower than the detection limit of the evaluation system. Above 300 °C, the amount of 36O decreased, similar to a sigmoid curve, whereas that of 34O behaved like a Gaussian curve, reaching the maximum value at a certain temperature. The decrease in the amount of the overall isotope oxygen 18O started above 350 °C, corresponding to the temperature at which the oxygen incorporation reaction started. The isotope fraction finally saturated at approximately 600 °C and reached the upper detection limit. Similar trends were observed in Figure 5b, except for the increase in the peak top of 34O and the shift of the temperature indicating the maximum 34O to the lower side, implying the enhancement of the dissociative adsorption reaction by the presence of solid solutions. The isotope fractions of pure BZS10 and the composites with other compositions and chemical states are summarized in Figures S8 and S9, respectively.

Temperature dependence of the normalized isotope fraction of composites with 1 vol % Co3O4 prepared at (a) 630 °C and (b) 880 °C. (c) Maximum F34ex and (d) temperature showing a maximum F34ex as a function of Co3O4 content.

As shown above, the peak top of F34ex and the temperature showing the maximum F34ex are good indicators of the enhanced dissociative adsorption reaction. Therefore, these computed from the Gaussian fitting were plotted as a function of Co3O4 content to clarify the relationship between the activity of the dissociative adsorption reaction, chemical states, and compositions of the composites (Figure 5c,d). The data points represent the mean values of multiple measurements, and the values of each measurement are summarized in Table S3. For the overall trend, while the value of the maximum F34ex increased monotonically from approximately 0.6 to 1.2 for composites without solid solutions, it indicated an abrupt enhancement to approximately 1.4 at x = 1 and did not indicate further changes regardless of the Co3O4 content for composites containing solid solutions. A similar trend was observed for the temperature at which the maximum F34ex was observed. These results imply that Co-containing MCs served as more active dissociative adsorption catalysts than single-phase Co3O4, even for a dissolved Co concentration equivalent to only 1 vol % Co3O4. In contrast, no significant changes in max F34ex and T (max F34ex) above x = 5 imply that the presence of Co3O4 did not largely contribute to the enhanced dissociative adsorption in the composition range in which both solid solutions and single-phase Co3O4 coexist.

The total surface exchange reaction rate R0 was derived using eq 1 within the temperature range where a decrease in f18ex was observed. n, τr, and S denote the reaction amount in the entire reactor, the average residence time of the reactor, and the surface area of the powder samples computed by nitrogen adsorption.36 An Arrhenius-type plot of R0 of composites without and with Co-containing solid solutions is indicated in Figure 6a,b. Note that only the data ranges where R0 followed a linear trend were extracted.

An Arrhenius-type plot of the total surface exchange reaction rate R0 of composites with x vol % Co3O4 prepared at (a) 630 °C for 6 h and (b) 880 °C for 6 h. (c) R0 of the composites at 500 °C as a function of Co3O4 volume fraction.

The surface exchange activity of pure BZS10 was relatively low, 3.6 × 10–6 mol–O·m–2·s–1 at 500 °C, which was comparable to R0 of typical ionic conductors.36,39 With an increase in the volume fraction of Co3O4, an enhancement of R0 compared to that of pure BZS10 was observed for all composites, regardless of their chemical states; however, the enhancement rate differed. In the systems without solid solutions (Figure 6a), R0 monotonically increased with increasing Co3O4 loading and reached 2.3 × 10–5 mol–O·m–2·s–1 at 500 °C for BZS10 with 10 vol % Co3O4, which was approximately 6.4 times higher than that of pure BZS10. In the systems with solid solutions (Figure 6b), R0 reached a maximum value of 2.2 × 10–5 mol–O·m–2·s–1 at x = 1, which was approximately 6.1 times higher than that of pure BZS10, and abruptly declined above x = 3 despite an increase in Co3O4 content. These trends are also easily recognized in Figure 6c, which shows a plot of the surface exchange reaction rates at 500 °C as a function of Co3O4 content.

From the relationship between the surface exchange properties, chemical states of the composites, and Co3O4 content, a conceptual model of the surface exchange reaction, extended from the model established by Schaube et al., is presented in Figure 7 to elucidate the activation mechanism of the surface exchange reaction.39 Under the assumption that pure BZS10 behaves as a typical ionic conductor, such as doped ceria, the surface exchange reaction proceeds via the following elemental steps: O2– adsorption on the surface of BZS10 and the incorporation of O2– into oxygen vacancy VÖ, dissociation of O2– within VÖ, and the incorporation of atomic oxygen into another VÖ. Among these steps, dissociation of O2– becomes a rate-limiting step because breaking off a bond of adsorbed oxygen requires high temperature without a catalyst, resulting in a lack of dissociated oxygen on the surface, as shown in Figure 7a. Consequently, the oxygen vacancies were not efficiently utilized for oxygen incorporation, making the total surface exchange reaction less active. For the composites of BZS10 and Co3O4 (Figure 7b), since oxygen adsorption and dissociation selectively occurred at the surface of Co3O451 without O2– being specially incorporated into VÖ, a significant amount of dissociated oxygen was formed and efficiently incorporated, leading to the promotion of R0. The enhancement became more remarkable with an increase in the Co3O4 content, caused by the formation of more dissociated oxygen, as shown in Figure 5c.

Conceptual surface exchange reaction model of (a) BZS10 and (b) BZS10 with Co3O4 as a secondary phase, (c) BZS10 with dissolved Co, and (d) BZS10 with dissolved Co and also with Co3O4, extended from the model established by Schaube et al.39

In contrast, the composites of BZS10Cδ and Co3O4 solid solutions exhibited different activation mechanisms. At x = 1, where all Co3O4 dissolved in BZS10 to form solid solutions, all BZS10Cδ surfaces functioned as active sites for oxygen dissociation, as shown in Figure 7c, resulting in a stronger dissociation activity than that of BZS10 with a higher concentration of Co3O4 (10 vol %), as shown in Figure 5c,d. The dissociated oxygen was then effectively incorporated into the oxygen vacancy; therefore, R0 was sufficiently enhanced despite the low Co3O4 loading of 1 vol %. At x = 3, R0 was reduced despite the increase in the dissolution amount of Co. Based on the f34ex analysis at x = 1 and x = 3 presented in Figure S9d–f, the peak top of f34ex remained constant, yet the overall amount of 34O increased with an increase in the width of the Gaussian fitting curve, implying comparable oxygen dissociation activity and deactivation of the incorporation reaction. This was likely due to the decrease in the oxygen vacancies associated with the hole compensation preferred by Co dissolution, which is expressed by the defect equilibrium in eq 2.52

Above x = 5, both BZS10Cδ and Co3O4 are expected to function as catalysts to dissociate adsorbed oxygen (Figure 7d). However, the trend of the constant maximum F34ex and the temperature taking the maximum F34ex in Figure 5c,d imply that Co3O4 did not contribute to the additional enhancement of the oxygen dissociation activity. Moreover, the reduction in R0 observed from x = 1 to x = 3 was also evident from x = 3 to x = 5. This was also likely due to the decrease in oxygen vacancies associated with the hole compensation preferred by Co dissolution, as supported by temperature dependent magnetization measurements (Figure 3c). Under the assumption that the Co solubility at 880 °C is the concentration equal to 3 vol %, the magnetization of the remaining 2 vol % Co3O4 should be reflected. However, because the observed magnetization of Co3O4 was much smaller, it is reasonable to consider that the Co solubility is between 3 vol % and 5 vol %. Therefore, the mechanism of R0 decrease due to the deactivation of oxygen incorporation is also applicable. In contrast, after reaching the solubility limit above x = 5, the decrease in the amount of oxygen vacancies stopped; therefore, R0 reduction no longer occurred.

The results of this study indicated that different chemical states of the composites derived from different preparation conditions lead to completely different activation behaviors of the surface exchange reaction. Because many studies are unfortunately less likely to focus on the effect of the chemical states of composites on their cathode properties, there is ambiguity regarding the factors that contribute to the activation of surface exchange properties. By precisely evaluating the relationship between their chemical states and surface exchange properties, this study clarified that Co-containing solid solutions formed by high-temperature heat treatment are the determining factors that activate their surface exchange properties. Another significant point is that increasing the Co3O4 content is not always effective for improving the reaction rate R0 of barium zirconate-based protonic conductors, which require high-temperature heat treatment in the fabrication processes, although it has been widely acknowledged as an effective dissociative adsorption catalyst for enhancing the surface exchange properties of various oxides. In addition, a small amount of Co3O4 (approximately 1 vol %) was sufficient to achieve superior surface exchange properties, and an additional dispersion above 1 vol % reduced the surface exchange reaction rate. Therefore, the amount of dissociative adsorption catalyst is a significant factor in the design of efficient PCFC cathodes. If the above findings are applied to the design of barium zirconate-based cathodes, optimal cathodes should consist of doped Ba–Zr oxides, a small amount of Co3O4 (approximately 1 vol %), and an electroconductive aid such as Ag. However, it should be noted that the practical application of Co-containing cathodes in PCFCs is their high thermal expansion coefficient and side reactions with electrolyte materials.53,54 The impact of dispersed Co3O4 and Co dissolution into BZS10 on such aspects needs to be clarified in future work. Furthermore, to mitigate following these issues, the preparation of composites at intermediate temperatures by processes including hydrothermal method, cold sintering, and spark-plasma sintering is one of the effective approaches, yet versatile preparation processes applicable to a wide range of oxides are still in progress.55−57

Conclusions

This study focused on the surface exchange properties of composite air electrodes consisting of a protonic conductor BZS10 and a dissociative adsorption catalyst Co3O4 using the PIE technique and elucidated their relationship with changes in chemical states and compositions. The composites treated at high temperature led to the formation of a Co-containing triple conductor BZS10Cδ due to the dissolution of dispersed Co3O4, while no changes in the chemical states were observed for composites annealed at intermediate temperature, as clarified by magnetization measurements. These differences in chemical states were reflected in the results of the isotope exchange reaction. Based on the analysis of the temperature-dependent isotope fraction, the presence of BZS10Cδ resulted in a significant enhancement of the dissociative oxygen adsorption activity; however, its activity could not be further improved by increasing the loading of Co3O4. In contrast, the composites without BZS10Cδ exhibited a monotonic enhancement of the oxygen dissociation activity in proportion to the concentration of Co3O4. Based on the evaluation of the total surface exchange reaction, the abrupt R0 enhancement at x = 1 for the composites containing BZS10Cδ corresponds to the efficient incorporation of a significant amount of dissociated oxygen. R0 reduction above x = 3, despite an increase in Co3O4 loading, was realized by the decrease in oxygen incorporation due to the decrease in oxygen vacancies associated with hole compensation by Co dissolution. On the other hand, the R0 of the composites without BZS10Cδ exhibited monotonic growth as a function of the Co3O4 content, similar to the trend of the oxygen dissociation activity. Based on the results, different chemical states of composites derived from different preparation conditions lead to completely different activation behavior of the surface exchange reaction.
