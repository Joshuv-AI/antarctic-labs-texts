Ambient biothermal stress, preconceptional thyrotropin abnormalities, and the risk of preterm birth: a nationwide Chinese cohort study
Authors: Xinghou He, Mengyao Li, Hongbing Xu, Bin Zhang, Xinyi Lv, Long Wang, Chuanyu Zhao, Xuyang Shan, Yuan He, Yan Fang, Yuanyuan Wang, Huiying Xu, Jihong Xu, Xiaoming Song, Ya Zhang, Hongguang Zhang, Ying Yang, Xu Ma, Wei Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Epidemiologic evidence on thermal stress and preterm birth (PTB) is based on ambient temperature rather than a biothermal metric. Thyrotropin abnormalities have been associated with PTB and may increase thermal vulnerability. However, it remains unknown whether thermal stress and thyrotropin abnormalities synergistically contribute to increased risk of PTB. Here we conducted a nationwide cohort study among 6,218,131 singleton live births in China. Biothermal stress was measured using the Universal Thermal Climate Index. We found that both heat stress and cold stress during each trimester were associated with increased risks of PTB, with hazard ratios ranging from 1.06 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.05, 1.07) to 2.33 (95% CI: 2.30, 2.35) and from 1.08 (95% CI: 1.07, 1.09) to 1.68 (95% CI:1.67, 1.70), respectively. Participants with subnormal (<0.37 mIU/L) or supranormal thyrotropin levels (≥4.88 mIU/L) and biothermal stress had higher risks of PTB, compared with those with normal thyrotropin levels and non-thermal stress. Additive interactions were also identified between biothermal stress and thyrotropin abnormalities. We estimated that up to 13.52% of PTB were attributable to biothermal stress and thyrotropin abnormalities. Our study showed a synergistic effect of biothermal stress and preconceptional thyrotropin abnormalities, highlighting the importance of climate adaption measures and thyroid management toward pregnant women under climate change scenario.

Introduction

Preterm birth (PTB, <37 completed gestational weeks), the leading cause of perinatal infant morbidity and mortality worldwide, is associated with a variety of lifelong health concerns such as neurodevelopmental impairments, gastrointestinal disorders, and respiratory and cardiovascular diseases1–3. Globally, PTB affects ~4%–16% of pregnancies and accounts for an estimated 740,000 infant deaths in 2021, which contributes to considerable disease burden for healthcare systems4. Reducing preterm-related neonatal and child mortality is crucial for achieving the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal on ensuring healthy lives and promoting well-being for all at all ages. Despite major advances in health care, PTB incidences in most countries have increased significantly over the past decade5, suggesting that ubiquitous or exacerbating risk factors may play a role.

In the context of climate change, extreme temperature events have been increasing in both intensity and frequency over the past decades, which presents major health threats, especially for vulnerable populations including pregnant women and developing fetuses6. Previous studies suggested that heat stress may disturb hormone homeostasis, cause dehydration, and induce oxidative stress and inflammation, all of which may lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes7,8. Better understanding on the association of extreme heat or cold with maternal and fetal health is important for quantifying the overall burden of climate related conditions, especially as PTB contributes many person years lost to these estimates. In the past few years, maternal heat or cold exposure during pregnancy has been associated with incident PTB, based on a large body of evidence from ecological studies, cross-sectional surveys, case-crossover studies, prospective cohorts, systematic reviews and meta-analyses6,9–11. However, the existing literature is limited to estimating heat or cold exposure based on air temperature, with or without relative humidity. It is well known that human exposure to multiple meteorological factors in the real world, while conventional single meteorological index such as air temperature cannot account for the net heat load of human exposure and the heat balance between total thermal environment and human physiological or behavioral responses12,13. The Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI), a comprehensive biothermal metric that reflects the external thermal environment and thermophysiological response of the human body, has been recommended and applied in heat-event warning and weather forecasting systems14–16. Recently, few epidemiological studies have used UTCI to evaluate the impact of heat stress on pregnancy outcomes14,17, with only one study focusing on PTB18.

Maternal thyrotropin (thyroid-stimulating hormone, TSH) serves as a key regulator of thyroid physiology, which plays a vital role in fetal development and wellness during pregnancy19–21. An increasing number of epidemiological studies have reported that alterations of maternal TSH level during preconception and pregnancy were associated with elevated risk of multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes such as PTB22–27. Our previous study among reproductive-aged women based on China National Free Prepregnancy Checkups Project (NFPCP) also found that the fluctuations of preconceptional TSH, even within the reference range, can significantly elevate the risk of PTB19. Furthermore, the thyroid gland plays a crucial role in regulating metabolism, oxygen consumption, and heat production to maintain core body temperature28. These metabolic and thermoregulatory processes are sensitive to ambient temperature variations and thyroid function status even during resting conditions. Epidemiological and mechanistic studies indicate that extreme thermal exposure can influence circulating TSH levels in humans by disrupting the synthesis of thyrotropin-releasing hormone by hypothalamic neurosecretory cells28–32. In addition, TSH abnormalities impair the precise coordination of central and peripheral thyroid hormone signaling and disrupt the balance of thermogenesis and energy partitioning, thereby enhancing physiological susceptibility to thermal extremes33,34. Though current evidence suggests a link between temperature, TSH, and PTB, it remains unclear whether heat or cold stress may combine with preconceptional TSH abnormalities to trigger PTB incidents, which is of great health implication.

In this nationwide prospective cohort study, we examine the effects of heat and cold stress during pregnancy and preconceptional TSH abnormalities on risk of PTB. We further assess whether biothermal stress and TSH abnormalities jointly contribute to incident PTB. We show that heat and cold stress during the first, second, and third trimesters are associated with increased risks of preterm birth. More importantly, synergistic interactions are observed between biothermal stress and preconceptional TSH abnormalities on preterm birth. We estimate that up to 13.52 % of preterm births are attributable to biothermal stress and preconceptional TSH abnormalities.

Results

Study participants and environmental exposures

A total of 6,218,131 birth records were included in this study, spanning 31 provinces in China (Fig. 1). Of these, 598,660 (9.63%) were defined as PTB, including 448,205 (7.21%) moderate PTB, 91,315 (1.47%) very PTB, and 59,140 (0.95%) extremely PTB (Supplementary Table 1). Compared with term birth group, PTB group was more likely to have subnormal TSH levels (4.4%) or supranormal TSH levels (2.8%). We observed significant differences between term birth and PTB groups in all variables. The mean (standard deviation [SD]) gestational ages were 33.3 (3.7) weeks in the PTB group and 39.8 (1.4) weeks in the term birth group. Compared with term births, PTB deliveries were observed more frequently among mothers who were younger than 25 years (39.7%), who were underweight before pregnancy (14.2%), who had primary school or below education (57.1%), who were multiparas (45.7%), and who were farmers (66.6%). Participants who had irregular or no folic acid supplementation (58.4%), underwent cesarean delivery (29.0%), delivered a boy (54.1%), and conceived in spring (30.2%) or winter (27.7%) were more likely to deliver preterm. The distribution of levels of meteorological factors during each trimester and season is shown in Supplementary Table 2. The mean (SD) UTCI during the first, second, and third trimesters was 16.02 (10.37) °C, 16.49 (10.60) °C, and 14.35 (10.59) °C for all participants, respectively. The mean UTCI (SD) during the spring, summer, autumn, and winter was 15.47 (6.24) °C, 27.56 (3.54) °C, 15.99 (6.79) °C, and 1.82 (7.42) °C, respectively. The Spearman’s correlation coefficients among UTCI during each trimester are shown in Supplementary Table 3. The characteristics of individual-experienced heat or cold are shown in Supplementary Table 4. Associations between TSH abnormalities and PTB

The associations of preconceptional TSH levels with PTB and its subtypes are shown in Supplementary Table 6. We observed that both subnormal (HR: 1.27 [95% CI:1.25, 1.28]) and supranormal preconception TSH levels (HR: 1.07 [95% CI:1.05, 1.09]) were associated with higher risks of PTB, compared with the reference group. As for different subtypes of PTB, statistically significant risks for very PTB and extremely PTB were observed among participants with subnormal TSH levels, but not among those with supranormal TSH levels.

Interactions between biothermal stress and TSH abnormalities

The interactive effects of biothermal stress and preconceptional TSH levels on the risk of PTB and its subtypes are shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1–2. Participants with both biothermal stress and TSH abnormalities had excess disease risk as compared with those who had either factor alone. In specific, under heat stress during the third trimester, the HRs of PTB were 3.12 (95%CI: 3.02, 3.22) for subnormal TSH group, 2.40 (95%CI: 2.30, 2.50) for supranormal TSH group, and 2.32 (95%CI: 2.30, 2.34) for normal TSH group. In the additive scale, we observed significant interaction between subnormal TSH levels and heat stress during the first and third trimesters on the risk of PTB (Table 2). Specifically, for heat stress during the third trimester and subnormal TSH group, the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP) were 0.54 (95% CI: 0.43, 0.64), and 0.17 (95% CI: 0.14, 0.20), respectively, indicating significant synergistic effects of subnormal TSH levels and heat stress on PTB risk. Similar significant synergistic effects were also observed on the risks of moderate PTB and very PTB, but not on the risk of extremely PTB. As for supranormal TSH levels, significant synergistic effects were observed with heat stress during the first trimester, contributing 5% (95% CI: 0.3%, 10%) and 7% (95% CI: 1%, 12%) to the risk of PTB and moderate PTB, respectively. Significant synergistic effects were also observed for exposure to cold stress and TSH abnormalities. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses largely supported the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Tables 9-11). The similar results were observed when we further adjusted for PM2.5 and O3 exposure in the main model. Additional adjustment for environmental exposures of pesticide and radiation in our main model did not change our results. The analysis adjusting for mode of delivery showed that exposure to heat and cold stress was associated with higher risk of PTB, consistent with the main results. When we used ambient temperature to define heat and cold exposures, the results showed that heat and cold exposures were still significantly associated with increased risks of PTB, although the effect estimates statistically differed from the main results. In addition, the associations remained robust in analyses that excluded participants with missing values of covariates, with gestational week ≥42 weeks, and with exposure to indoor renovation. Similar association estimates were observed in analyses using the imputed datasets. Biothermal stress exposure defined by different thresholds was also associated with increased risk of PTB and its subtypes. The results of TSH abnormalities defined by using an official reference range (subnormal level: <0.75 mIU/L; supranormal level: >5.60 mIU/L) were not significantly different from our results. The Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models yielded effect estimates for biothermal stress-PTB and TSH-PTB associations that were similar to those obtained from Cox regression analyses. Consistent with the main results, the analysis of distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) incorporating Cox model showed that heat stress exposure during weeks 1-37 of gestation and cold stress exposure during weeks of 1-7 and 28-37 of gestation were associated with increased risks of PTB and its subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 3). The results of restricted cubic spline regression showed a J-shaped dose–response relationship between preconceptional TSH or multiples of the median (MoM) of TSH levels and PTB (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this nationwide cohort study among more than 6.2 million births, we have shown that heat and cold stress during each trimester of pregnancy, especially during the third trimester, was associated with increased risks of PTB and its subtypes. In specific, we found positive interactive associations between heat or cold stress and preconceptional TSH abnormalities. The greatest risks of PTB were observed among participants with heat stress exposure and subnormal TSH levels. This study provides novel evidence that TSH abnormalities may synergistically interact with heat or cold stress to further increase the risk of preterm birth, highlighting the importance of implementing adaptation measures and managing preconception thyroid function to prevent preterm birth in the context of global climate change.

A growing number of studies have explored the effects of heat or cold exposure during pregnancy on PTB based on air temperature metrics6,9,35,36. A recent cohort study among 1,225,722 births in Sydney, Australia found that extreme heat in the third trimester of pregnancy was strongly associated with increased risk of PTB, with an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 1.61 (95% CI: 1.55,1.67)9. Evidence from Changsha, China indicated that both high and low temperature during the first and third trimesters were associated with PTB risks37. A multicenter study among 223,375 singleton deliveries in the United States showed that heat and cold exposures during early pregnancy were associated with 11% (95% CI: 1%, 21%) and 20% (95% CI: 11%, 33%) increases in the risk of early PTB, respectively36. A study among 5,347 live singleton births in France reported positive associations between PTB and cold exposure at weeks 7-9 (relative risk [RR]: 1.3, 95% CI: 1.0-1.6) and heat exposure at weeks 1-5 (RR: 2.0, 95% CI: 1.05-3.8) and 20-26 (RR: 2.9, 95% CI: 1.2-6.8) after conception35. The heterogeneity of these findings might be attributed, at least in part, to population characteristics, geographical differences, climates zones, and socioeconomic status. A recent case-crossover analysis among 15,576 births in Western Australia investigated the association between UTCI and spontaneous PTB and found that acute heat stress was associated with an increased risk of spontaneous PTB (RR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.04-1.06)18. In the present study, we observed significant associations between heat and cold stress with PTB during each trimester of pregnancy, and the associations became stronger during the third trimester (heat: HR, 2.33; 95% CI, 2.30-2.35; cold: HR, 1.68; 95% CI, 1.67-1.70). Increased risks of PTB subtypes due to heat or cold stress were also observed, especially for extremely PTB, which is consistent with previous findings36. Previous evidence suggests that fetal growth restriction predominantly manifests in late gestation, following observed placental insufficiency and progressive depletion of fetal glucose and oxygen reserves during the third trimester38. This pathophysiological cascade establishes the third trimester as a critical risk window for PTB. Additionally, due to the varied exposure windows for pregnant women during the third trimester, the high risks of PTB could be attributed to both acute and chronic effects of biothermal stress. Considering time-varying exposures, we used the time-dependent Cox proportional hazard model for data analyses which could effectively take into account the varied exposure windows and outcomes39. Furthermore, the Cox proportional hazard model could also eliminate potential confounding due to the increasing probability of birth with higher gestational age and seasonal variation in the gestational age distribution of pregnancies at risk (<37 weeks)40,41. We found that the confidence intervals of the effect estimates in this study are narrower than those in previous studies. This phenomenon may be partly due to the large sample size and the wide and heterogeneous climate zones of this nationwide study, which is vital for generating robust findings.

The adverse effects of thyroid dysfunction during pregnancy have been affirmed in previous epidemiological studies23,42,43. Even subclinical hypothyroidism (defined as increased TSH with normal thyroid hormone) has been found to significantly increase the risk of multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes including PTB23,42. Medical guidelines are in agreement that suspected thyroid dysfunction should be confirmed and managed as early as possible in pregnant women. However, thyroid dysfunction before pregnancy should not be ignored. Several recent studies have reported that maternal TSH abnormalities before pregnancy were associated with elevated risks of PTB25–27,44. A study in China showed that low (<0.40 mIU/L) and high (>4.00 mIU/L) maternal preconceptional TSH level were associated with 27.4% (95% CI: 12.2, 44.6) and 34.4% (95% CI: 12.6, 47.3) higher risks of PTB, respectively25. In our previous study in 2022, we also provided convincing evidence of a significant association between preconception TSH levels and the elevated risk of PTB, even when TSH levels were greater than 2.50 mIU/L but within the clinical reference range19. These findings were further confirmed in this study, in which we observed that subnormal (HR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.22-1.25) and supranormal TSH levels (HR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.06-1.09) before conception were associated with increased risks of PTB and its subtypes. The observed higher risk of PTB associated with subnormal TSH levels compared to that associated with supranormal TSH levels in our study is consistent with previous studies19,45. However, other studies reported contrary findings and there was no plausible explanation for the underlying mechanisms25,44. This variation in findings may be due to differences in participant characteristics, variations in baseline TSH levels, and heterogeneous reference ranges across studies.

Although previous studies have explored the independent effects of heat stress, cold stress, and TSH abnormalities on incident PTB, no study investigated the interaction between heat or cold stress and preconceptional TSH abnormalities. In this nationwide cohort study, we found that TSH abnormalities and heat or cold stress synergistically increased the risk of PTB. Furthermore, 4%-27% of PTB risk could be attributed to the additive interactions, suggesting that the interactive effect of TSH abnormalities and biothermal stress exceeds the sum of their individual effects. Stronger interactive effects were observed with co-exposure to heat stress and subnormal TSH levels, with the strongest effect observed during the third trimester. This could be attributed to the stronger independent effects of heat stress and subnormal TSH levels on PTB, compared to the effects of cold stress and supranormal TSH levels. We found that the risk of PTB was higher among individuals co-exposed to biothermal stress and supranormal TSH levels, compared to those with normal TSH levels and non-thermal stress. However, no significant synergistic interactions were observed among the supranormal TSH group exposed to heat stress during each trimester, whereas such interactions were observed among those exposed to cold stress. This may be partially explained by the increased vulnerability of individuals with supranormal TSH levels to cold exposure due to low heat production rates33. The observed additive interactions between biothermal stress and TSH abnormalities in this study contribute to the identification of high-risk population, which has a potential guiding importance for the primary prevention of PTB. Specifically, pregnant women with thyroid dysfunction should pay more attention to extreme heat and cold. This novel insight has significant implications for clinical management and public health intervention. More population-based studies are warranted to confirm our findings and to explore the causal relationship.

Previous studies have revealed the pathways and potential mechanisms in adverse pregnancy outcome related to the independent effects of maternal thyroid dysfunction or biothermal stress. It is plausible that thyroid dysfunction may disrupt the thermoregulatory progress including altering metabolic rate, vasodilatation, and sweating, thereby making the body more susceptible to extreme temperature33,34. The common potential pathways of biothermal stress and TSH abnormalities also make their synergistic effects biologically plausible. Heat stress and alterations of thyroid function could impair placental function, affecting fetal growth and development, which may lead to an early delivery46,47. Heat stress and thyroid hormone abnormalities also could contribute to PTB via inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways48,49. Additionally, heat stress and TSH abnormalities may increase the secretion of oxytocin, triggering the parturition mechanism and stimulating uterine contractions, potentially resulting in PTB50–52. Cold stress exposure and thyroid dysfunction also share common physiological processes that may associate with PTB. For example, both cold exposure and subclinical hypothyroidism may lead to vascular increased blood viscosity, vascular resistance, and hypertension53,54, which might limit uteroplacental perfusion, thereby adversely affecting the development of fetus and increasing the risk of PTB. However, the exact mechanisms underlying the interactive effects of biothermal stress and TSH abnormalities on incident PTB need to be further elucidated in future studies.

Findings from this study can be of important public health implications for promoting maternal and fetal well-being. The United Nations has included pregnant women among at-risk populations to climate change and extreme temperature55, highlighting specific physiological changes during pregnancy and social vulnerabilities to changing climate6. Traditionally, thyroid status, a key factor for fetal development, has often been ignored in previous research assessing the risk of temperature-related PTB, which may lead to an underestimated impact on disease burden. Our study provides evidence that maternal thyroid dysfunction, even before pregnancy, may actually increase the risk of temperature-associated PTB. Further, we estimated that biothermal stress and preconception thyroid dysfunction were responsible for up to 13.52% of PTB. Our findings emphasize the importance of ongoing education for health professionals and the public and the necessity for increased awareness about the adverse effects of extreme temperature and preconceptional TSH abnormalities in pregnant women. From a clinical standpoint, obstetricians and endocrinologists should take note of the emerging evidence and adopt a more active role in communicating climate risks to childbearing women or patients and integrating climate factors into clinical management. From a public health perspective, targeted interventions, such as extreme-temperature surveillance and warning systems, need to be developed. Ideally, these systems should timely deliver personalized alerts tailored to risk at individual level based on the local climate conditions, mitigating the risk of prematurity and other temperature-related diseases. Although extreme-temperature warning systems have been established in cities in the United States, the UK, Canada, and other countries56,57, few rural districts implemented such systems. Particular efforts must be taken to develop warning systems informed by local vulnerabilities for rural populations. Additionally, governments should consider implementing universal preconception thyroid screening or selective screening for individuals with high personal or familial risks, especially in the region with extreme climate conditions. Given the increasing intensity and frequency of extreme temperature as climate crisis progresses, the relatively high prevalence of maternal thyroid dysfunction worldwide, and the considerable individual and societal burdens of PTB, policymakers should prioritize actions to improve population adaptability and reduce current and future health risks of extreme temperature.

This study has several strengths. First, employing a prospective study design and incorporating a large sample size from 2,433 counties across 31 provinces of China in this study greatly enhances the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Our participants were sourced from diverse geographic regions and climate zones, with a wide range of observed UTCI levels. This diversity is advantageous for investigating the association between biothermal stress and PTB, particularly in regions with extreme ambient temperatures. Second, we used UTCI as a more objective biothermal metric to reflect human thermal comfort and how human body reacts to the total thermal environment, making our findings more physiologically relevant. Third, investigating the interactive effects between preconceptional TSH abnormalities and biothermal stress added a novel and important dimension to this study, highlighting the significance and potential benefits of preconception healthcare. However, we acknowledge that this study is subject to several limitations. First, we estimated UTCI based on residence address, and lacked detailed information of indoor exposure and personal-level uses of air conditioners or fans in all microenvironments, which may lead to inevitable exposure misclassification. Second, despite having accounted for indoor renovation, we failed to gather data on other home environmental factors. These factors, including mold or damp stains and window pane condensation, have been found in associations with PTB58. Third, the thyroid function status was evaluated only based on TSH levels, and information about thyroid hormones such as free thyroxine and triiodothyronine was missing. However, TSH has served as the gold standard of the first-line thyroid function test for most clinical situations, including pregnancy59. Fourth, maternal thyroid physiology and TSH levels altered during pregnancy, but TSH levels were measured only once before pregnancy. Therefore, classifying solely based on preconceptional TSH levels may have been inadequate to accurately reflect thyroid status throughout the entire pregnancy. Additionally, the inclusion of participants with thyroid dysfunction who may receive treatment during gestation may underestimate the associations. However, based on the significantly higher risk of PTB in participants with preconception TSH abnormalities, we speculate that thyroid status was not well managed in this cohort. Previous studies have also reported that 72.8%-96% of general adults with TSH abnormalities persisted in abnormal TSH levels during follow-up38,60,61. Fifth, competing risks between PTB subtypes may lead to potential downward bias or even inverse associations between biothermal stress or thyroid dysfunction and late PTB. Thus, the observed associations between biothermal stress exposure and TSH abnormalities with MPTB or VPTB in our study may have been underestimated. To compensate for this limitation, we fitted Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models that accounted for competing risks between PTB subtypes, and the results were consistent with our main findings.

In conclusion, we found that biothermal stress during pregnancy was associated with increased risks of PTB and its subtypes. More importantly, we observed synergistic effects between biothermal stress and preconceptional TSH abnormalities on PTB. This study provides novel evidence that heat and cold stress may synergistically interact with TSH abnormalities to further increase the risk of PTB, highlighting the importance of implementing adaptation measures and managing preconception thyroid function to prevent PTB in the context of climate change.

Methods

Design and study population

The Institutional Research Review Board at the National Health Commission approved this study (IRB-201001). This nationwide cohort study was conducted based on the NFPCP, with details of the design (sampling, data acquisition, and data quality assessment) reported previously19. Briefly, NFPCP is a nationwide preconceptional health care service launched in 2010, covering 2,753 counties in 31 provinces of China, with the aims to provide free preconceptional health examinations, counseling, as well as early gestation and postnatal follow-ups for reproductive-aged couples. We collected NFPCP database data regarding preconceptional examination, early gestation follow-up, and postnatal follow-up for 6,542,908 Chinese women who participated in the NFPCP and successfully conceived after the preconception examination between January 1, 2014, and April 21, 2020. Finally, a total of 6,218,131 participants from 2433 counties in 31 provinces were included in this study (Supplementary Fig. 5) and the analyses were conducted between December 1, 2023, and June 1, 2024. Written informed consents were obtained from all NFPCP participants. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Exposure assessment

UTCI is a physiological equivalent air temperature (°C) that assesses human thermophysiological responses to complex meteorological conditions by integrating heat transfer within and across body segments, as well as heat exchanges with the thermal environment. By converting multidimensional environmental data, including air temperature, mean radiant temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed into a single scalar value, UTCI quantifies equivalent thermal stress under standardized reference conditions. The detailed calculation procedure of UTCI has been described in prior studies16,62. Briefly, UTCI is derived from the Fiala’s multi-node model of human thermal balance under the reference condition (wind speed of 0.5 m/s at 10 m above the ground, mean radiant temperature equal to air temperature, relative humidity of 50% or constant vapor pressure of 12 hPa, walking speed of 4 km/h, and metabolic heat production rate of 2.3 metabolic equivalents of task)63. The Fiala’s multi-node model consists of four primary subsystems: body construction, intra-body heat transfer, environmental heat exchange, and the thermoregulatory system with perceptual responses64. The body construction model comprises 12 spherical or cylindrical body compartments65, representing an average human body with a total surface area of 1.85 m2, weight of 73.4 kg, and 14% body fat. It estimates basal skin wettedness at 6%, mean skin temperature at 34.4 °C, and core temperatures of 37.0 °C in the hypothalamus and 36.9 °C in the rectum. The intra-body heat transfer model simulates dynamic heat and mass transport using Pennes’ bio-heat transfer equation and incorporates metabolic heat generation and blood circulation dynamics. The model of environmental heat exchange is established to calculate local net heat fluxes between the body surface and the environment, incorporating both natural and forced convection through combined-convection coefficients. The thermoregulatory system predicts four essential thermoregulatory responses of the central nervous system: vasoconstriction (reducing cutaneous blood flow), vasodilation (increasing cutaneous blood flow), shivering, and sweating (moisture excretion)66. The model of perceptual responses connects physiological outputs to subjective thermal sensation and comfort levels. Additionally, the Fiala’s multi-node model integrates an adaptive clothing model that dynamically adjusts clothing insulation and vapor resistance across a wide range of climatic conditions67.

Each woman’s address information was collected during the preconception examination, early gestation follow-up, and postpartum follow-up records. Three addresses were compared, and those women who moved during pregnancy were excluded. We calculated daily average UTCI by averaging the 24-hour data, then assigned the daily average UTCI data for each participant according to the longitude and latitude of their residential addresses. Average levels of UTCI for each trimester, including 1st trimester (1-13 weeks), 2nd trimester (14-26 weeks), and 3rd trimester (27 weeks of gestation to delivery) were estimated for each participant from 2014 to 2020. As the study covered 31 provinces across China, biothermal stress was categorized according to the local UTCI distribution of study participants in each trimester to reflect regional adaptability (Supplementary Table 12). Heat stress was defined as the average UTCI above the 90th percentiles of local and trimester-specific UTCI distribution. Cold stress was defined as the average UTCI less than the 10th percentiles of local and trimester-specific UTCI distribution. Daily concentrations of PM2.5 and 8-h maximum O3 at the residential address of participants throughout the study period were derived from the Nested Air Quality Prediction Modeling System68,69. Cross-validation analysis revealed that predictions from NAQPMS were good, with a coefficient of 0.94 for correlation between predicted values and ground monitoring measurements in mainland China70.

Serum TSH measurement

Fasting blood samples were collected from all participants and immediately sent to accredited laboratories affiliated with local authorized maternal and child health service centers. Serum samples were centrifuged and stored at -30°C before analysis. TSH concentrations were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits or electrochemiluminescence immunoassay according to the National Guide to Clinical Laboratory Procedures. Because NFPCP is a national project that includes a huge number of participants from 31 provinces across China, it was hard to apply a uniform TSH detection kit or method for all participants. But it could be confirmed that samples in the same county were measured with a single kind of TSH assay. In our previous study, a population-specific TSH reference range has been established19. Preconception TSH levels were classified into subnormal (<0.37 mIU/L), normal (0.37-4.87 mIU/L), and supranormal levels (≥4.88 mIU/L) according to the reference range. In addition, we calculated TSH MoM levels by dividing each participant’s thyrotropin value by the median value of thyrotropin in the reference population of the county in which they participated in the NFPCP. A TSH MoM value was regarded as an alternative indicator to absolute thyrotropin measurement that can mitigate the variability and systematic differences among various assays and laboratories71,72.

Outcome definition

The gestational age was estimated based on the time since the first day of the last menstrual period. PTB was defined as a gestational age from 20 through 36 completed weeks according to the criteria recommended by the World Health Organization73. We further classified PTB into three subtypes: moderate PTB (a gestational age from 32 through 36 completed weeks), very PTB (a gestational age from 28 through 31 completed weeks), and extremely PTB (20 through 27 completed weeks).

Covariates

In light of the prior knowledge and existing literature19,74,75, we initially considered the following covariates in our analysis: (1) maternal demographic characteristics: age at conception (16–24, 25–29, and 30-49 year), preconceptional body mass index (BMI) (<18.5, 18.5–23.9, and ≥24 kg/m2), ethnicity (Han, minority), education level (junior high school or below, senior high school, and college or above), residence (urban, rural), occupation (farmer, worker, and others including housewife, individual business, or other professions not specified above), alcohol drinking (yes, no), smoking (yes, no), passive smoking (yes, no), psychological pressure (yes, no), season of conception (spring, summer, autumn, winter), supplementation of folic acid (standardized use, no use or irregular use), geographical region (eastern, northeast, northern, northwest, southeast, southern, and central); (2) fetal variables: parity (primiparas or multiparas), infant sex (male, female), and year of birth (2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, and 2020); Since all variables had missing data rates below 7.0%, these missing values were included for all categorical adjustment variables76.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for participants’ characteristics and outcome measures. Spearman correlation analysis was used to examine the correlation between UTCI during different trimesters. The chi-square test, Mann-Whitney test, or Student’s t-test was used to compare the distributions of baseline characteristics between different groups.

We applied time-dependent Cox proportional hazard model to analyze the effect of biothermal stress during each trimester on PTB and its subtypes. Gestational age was fitted as the underlying timescale, and the event was defined as PTB, moderate PTB, very PTB, or extremely PTB. Exposures from adjacent time windows would act as unmeasured confounders, which leads to the bias in the effect estimates in separate models75. Therefore, biothermal stress during the first, the second, and the third trimesters were simultaneously included in a single model to estimate the effects of heat and cold stress. Directed acyclic graph was employed to identify a minimal sufficient adjustment set in the model: maternal age, drinking, active smoking, preconceptional BMI, education, occupation, geographic region, delivery year, and season of conception (Supplementary Fig. 6). Effect estimates are presented as HRs and corresponding 95% CIs.
