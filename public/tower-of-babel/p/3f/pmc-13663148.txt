Global profiling of the protein lactylome in porcine liver
Authors: Shuhao Fan, Shunyao Lu, Jinyu Guan, Yu Li, Wei Li, Yueyun Ding, Xianrui Zheng, Zongjun Yin, Xiaodong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Given the significant physiological and metabolic similarities between pig liver and human liver, along with the importance of pigs as excellent clinical models (successful xenotransplantation and survival in humans), this study presents for the first time a comprehensive lactylation map of the pig liver, revealing that protein lactylation (Kla) is a widespread and functionally important modification in this metabolically active organ. Through high-resolution mass spectrometry, we conducted a global analysis of lactylated proteins and sites in healthy pig liver, identifying 4,110 Kla sites on 1,486 proteins. Bioinformatics feature analysis indicated that these proteins are mainly located in the cytoplasm, mitochondria, and nucleus, and are significantly enriched in core metabolic pathways including glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and the pentose phosphate pathway. Motif analysis identified specific sequence patterns around lactoylated lysines. Comparative analysis showed that specific histone lactylation sites (such as H3K23, H2AK96, H2BK5) are conserved in pigs, mice, and humans, while also revealing tissue-specific characteristics compared to pig ovarian cells. These findings emphasize the close connection between liver lactylation and cellular energy metabolism, and provide a valuable comparative framework for studying the role of lactylation in human liver physiology and disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13200-0.

Introduction

Protein post-translational modifications (PTMs) represent a fundamental regulatory mechanism that rapidly expands the functional diversity of the proteome, enabling cells to dynamically respond to metabolic and environmental cues [1]. Common PTMs include phosphorylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, and oxidative modifications. These involve the addition of charged chemical groups to amino acid side chains, thereby influencing protein function [2]. PTMs are established as key regulators of processes such as calcium homeostasis, muscle development, and signal transduction [3]. Among the myriad of PTMs, acylation modifications—such as the well-characterized acetylation—have garnered significant interest [4]. The recent discovery of a series of novel, metabolically derived acylations, including succinylation, crotonylation, and β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb), has unveiled a direct molecular link between cellular metabolic status and functional outputs, particularly in epigenetic regulation and enzyme activity [5–7].

Lactylation (Kla), a novel PTM first identified on histone lysine residues in 2019, has emerged as a critical sensor linking glycolytic flux to gene expression [8]. Given the ubiquitous presence of lactate under physiological conditions, lactylation has become a focal point in epigenetics research, attracting widespread attention in processes with aberrantly high glycolysis, such as cancer, hypoxia, and inflammation [9]. Similar to acetylation, intracellular lactate is catalyzed by ACSS2 and GTPSCS to form lactyl-CoA. Subsequently, lactyltransferases facilitate a condensation reaction, transferring the lactyl group to the ε-NH2 side chain of lysine residues on proteins [10, 11]. Studies have identified several lysine acetyltransferases (KATs) that also possess lactyltransferase activity, including P300/CBP, GCN5, HBO1, KAT8, and TIP60, which exhibit cell type-specific catalytic activities [12]. Additionally, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase family members AARS1 and AARS2 can sense lactate levels and catalyze protein lactylation, playing crucial roles in p53 function regulation and the cGAS-STING innate immune pathway [13–15]. Histone deacetylases (HDACs) and sirtuin deacetylase family members have been confirmed to possess delactylase activity. HDAC1-3 and SIRT1-3 were among the first reported erasers of lactylation, with HDAC1 and HDAC3 broadly regulating histone lactylation in cells, while SIRT1/3 show selectivity for specific non-histone substrates [16–18]. Currently identified readers of lactylation modifications include BRG1 (containing a bromodomain), DPF2 (containing dual PHD finger domains), and TRIM33 (containing tandem Tudor-PHD domains) [19–21]. The coordinated action of these enzymes provides a molecular basis for cells to rapidly respond to metabolic program switching and regulate cellular activities.

Mounting evidence implicates histone lactylation in diverse biological processes, including macrophage polarization, neural function, and tumorigenesis [22, 23]. Beyond histones, non-histone protein lactylation is rapidly gaining recognition for its broader regulatory potential in cellular signaling and metabolism [24, 25]. Lactylation plays a significant regulatory role in human liver diseases. Histone lactylation can promote the expression of key genes, thereby inducing liver fibrosis and hepatocellular carcinoma proliferation [26, 27]. Non-histone lactylation has been reported to occur extensively on hepatic metabolic enzymes, participating in processes such as hepatic lipogenesis, tumor proliferation, and metabolic regulation [28–30]. Although the functions of lactylation in human and mouse livers have been partially elucidated, its role in the porcine liver remains unknown. Recent studies have reported the presence of lactylation in porcine granulosa cells, where it is involved in maintaining iron homeostasis [31, 32]. Given the liver’s high glycolytic activity and the established role of lactate as a signaling molecule, protein lactylation may constitutes a pervasive and functionally significant regulatory layer in porcine hepatic biology. In the context of liver physiology and pathology, aberrant lactylation has been implicated in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma and liver fibrosis, suggesting its role as a key metabolic regulator in this central metabolic organ [33].

The pig is one of the most important livestock animals and has long been domesticated by humans for meat. Simultaneously, due to striking anatomical, physiological, and metabolic similarities to humans, the pig is a premier preclinical model for human biomedical research [34, 35]. This relevance has been profoundly demonstrated by recent groundbreaking achievements in xenotransplantation [36]. The successful transplantation of a genetically engineered pig liver into a human patient, which extended life for 171 days, marks a milestone and underscores the critical importance of deep phenotyping of the porcine liver at the molecular level [37]. Based on the dual identity of pigs as both a vital agricultural animal and a medical model, understanding the impact of protein lactylation on porcine liver function in the context of liver physiology and pathology holds significant importance from both animal husbandry and medical perspectives.

Here, we present for the first time a comprehensive view of the lactylated proteome in porcine liver tissue through high-resolution mass spectrometry. This study aims to comprehensively identify lactylated proteins and their specific modification sites in porcine liver, characterize the biological pathways and molecular functions associated with these lactylated proteins through bioinformatics methods, with a focus on their involvement in metabolic processes. We also compare the data obtained in this study with the lactylated proteome data we previously obtained from porcine ovarian cells to elucidate tissue-specific and modification-specific features. Our work will provide valuable resources for understanding metabolic signaling in pigs and has potential significance for both agricultural science and human hepatology, bridging the gap from the farm to the clinic.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Our previous study detailed the sampling process of porcine liver tissue [38]. Briefly, a healthy three-way crossbred boar one healthy male three-way hybrid piglets [(Duroc × Landrace) × Large 88 White] (5 weeks of age) purchased from Lu ‘an Shitun Husbandry and raised at the Animal Experiment Centre of Anhui Agricultural University was carefully selected. It was humanely slaughtered by euthanasia through intravenous injection of 10 g pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg). Finally, through standardized surgical procedures, the corresponding liver tissues were collected under aseptic conditions, rinsed with PBS, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for 30 minutes, and then stored at ultra-low temperatures for subsequent lactylome analysis.

Protein Extraction

The untreated liver sample used for protein extraction was ground into a powder with liquid nitrogen and transferred to a 5 mL centrifuge tube. Four volumes of lysis buffer (1% SDS and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail) were added to the liver powder, followed by sonication for 3 min on ice using a high-intensity ultrasonic processor (Scientz). For PTM experiments, inhibitors were also added to the lysis buffer, e.g., 3 µM TSA and 50 mM NAM for acetylation, and 1% phosphatase inhibitor for phosphorylation. The remaining debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min. Finally, the supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit (Beyotime P0011) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The western blot (WB) method was consistent with that described in a previous study [38].

Trypsin digestion

The protein sample extracted from porcine liver was added to 1 volume of pre-cooled acetone, vortexed to mix, then added to 4 volumes of pre-cooled acetone, and precipitated at 20 °C for 2 h. The precipitate was washed 2–3 times with pre-cooled acetone. The protein samples were dissolved in 200 mM TEAB and ultrasonically dispersed. In the first digestion step, trypsin was added overnight at a 1:50 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio for the first digestion step. The sample was reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol for 30 min at 56 °C and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Finally, the peptides were desalted on a Strata X solid phase extraction (SPE) column.

Affinity enrichment of Kla peptides

To enrich the modified peptides, tryptic peptides dissolved in NETN buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0) were incubated with prewashed antibody beads (PTM Bio) at 4 °C overnight with gentle shaking. The beads were then washed 4 times with NETN buffer and twice with H2O. Bound peptides were eluted from the beads using 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. Finally, the eluted fractions were combined and vacuum dried. For liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis, the resulting peptides were desalted using C18 ZipTips (Millipore), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

LC-MS/MS analysis

The tryptic peptides were dissolved in solvent A and directly loaded onto a homemade reversed-phase analytical column (15 cm length, 100 μm i.d.). The mobile phase comprised solvents A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile/water). Peptides were separated with the following gradient: 0–0.5 min, 4% B; 0.5–0.6 min, 4–8% B; 0.6–13.6 min, 8–22.5% B; 13.6–20.5 min, 22.5–35% B; 20.5–20.9 min, 35–55% B; 20.9–21.4 min, 55–99% B; and 21.4–22.6 min, 99% B, at a constant flow rate of 400 nL/min on a Vanquish Neo UPLC system (ThermoFisher Scientific, United States). The separated peptides were analyzed using an Orbitrap Astral instrument equipped with a nano electrospray ion source. The applied electrospray voltage was 1900 V, the precursors were analyzed using an Orbitrap detector, and fragments were analyzed using an Astral detector. The full MS scan resolution was set to 240,000 for a scan range of 380–980 m/z. The MS/MS scan was fixed at a mass of 150.0 m/z and a resolution of 80,000. The HCD fragmentation was performed at a normalized collision energy of 25%. The automatic gain control target was set to 500%, with a maximum injection time of 3 ms.

Database search

The resulting MS/MS data were processed using SpectronAut (v.18). Tandem mass spectra were obtained for Sus_scrofa_9823_PR_20240407 in Fasta format (46176 entries) and concatenated with the reverse-decoy database. Trypsin/P was used as the cleavage enzyme, allowing up to four missing cleavages. The mass tolerance for the precursor ions was set to 20 ppm in the first search and to 5 ppm in the main search. The mass tolerance of the fragment ions was set to 0.02 Da. Carbamidomethylation of Cys was specified as a fixed modification, whereas oxidation of methionine, lactylation of lysine, and acetylation of the protein N-terminus were specified as variable modifications. The FDR was adjusted to < 1%. To obtain high-quality results, the results of database analysis were further filtered to identify proteins containing at least one peptide segment.

Functional annotation

We identified the modified proteins and used the EggNOG-mapper software to extract the GO ID for each protein based on the EggNOG database. The proteins were functionally annotated by categorizing them based on their cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes. We annotated protein pathways based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database and used the BLAST (blastp, e-value ≤ 1e − 4) tool to compare the identified modified proteins. From the BLAST comparison results of each sequence, we selected the highest-scoring match for annotation. WolFPsort software was used to annotate the subcellular localization of the proteins. The MoMo analysis tool, based on the Motif-X algorithm, was used to analyze the motif features of the modified residues [20].

Results

Global profiling of protein lactylome in porcine liver

To construct a comprehensive lactylome of porcine liver tissue, lactylated peptides enriched via immunoprecipitation were analyzed using high-resolution LC-MS/MS. A total of 6,868 peptides were detected, with lengths predominantly distributed between 7 and 20 amino acids, consistent with typical tryptic digestion patterns and indicating high data quality (Fig. 1A). Based on these peptides, we identified 1,486 lactylated proteins harboring 4,110 lactylated lysine sites (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table 1). Among these, 707 proteins contained a single lactylation site, 296 proteins contained two sites, and 63 proteins contained more than 10 lactylation sites (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table 2). The top three proteins with the highest number of modified sites were CPS1, ECHA (gene HADHA), and EHHADH, with 32, 26, and 20 lactylated lysines, respectively. Notably, all three are mitochondrial enzymes involved in energy metabolism. Subcellular localization analysis using WoLF PSORT revealed that the majority of lactylated proteins were localized to cytoplasm (508 proteins), mitochondria (422 proteins), and nucleus (292 proteins) (Fig. 1D).

Motif analysis

The distribution of amino acid residues surrounding modification sites, known as motifs, can influence enzyme substrate preference. To decipher the patterns of enzyme-substrate interaction, we analyzed the motifs of lactylation sites in the porcine liver. By examining the distribution of amino acids within a window of ± 10 residues around the modified lysine, we found significant enrichments (Fig. 2A, B). Glycine (G) was significantly enriched at positions − 1, + 1, and + 2. Alanine (A) was highly enriched at positions − 2, -1, and + 2 to + 4. Lysine (K) was significantly enriched at positions + 6 to + 10. Motif-X analysis identified six significantly enriched motifs. Among them, xxxxxxxxxxKla xGxxxxxxxx, xxxxxxxxxxKla xAxxxxxxxx, and xxxxxxxxxG Kla xxxxxxxxxx displayed the highest scores and are representative motifs for Kla in porcine liver (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 3). Comparative analysis with previous studies revealed that the xxxxxxxxxxxKla xAxxxxxxxx motif shows similarity to the KxAxxxxxxxxK motif reported in mice [39], but differs from patterns observed in porcine granulosa cells. This suggests that lactylation may exhibit both cross-species conservation and tissue specificity. In summary, we successfully identified specific lysine lactylation motifs in the porcine liver, revealing patterns that are both conserved and unique compared to other species and tissues.

Histone lactylation analysis

The histone octamer and chromatin intertwine to form nucleosomes, which are central to transcriptional regulation [40]. Histone lactylation is primarily implicated in promoting gene expression or marking enhancers. We analyzed the lactylation sites in the porcine liver and identified six sites on histones: three on histone H3, one on H2A, and two on H2B (Fig. 3). A comparative analysis of histone lactylation across different species and tissues revealed conservation among these sites [32, 39, 41]. Among the six sites, H3K23, H2AK96, and H2BK5 were conserved across human, mouse, and porcine liver tissues. These were also the only three sites conserved across human, mouse, porcine liver, and porcine granulosa cells. Five sites (H3K23, H3K79, H3K122, H2BK5, and H2BK34) were conserved between porcine liver and granulosa cells, while H2AK96 was detected only in the liver. These results indicate that some lactylation sites exhibit cross-species conservation, with higher conservation across different tissues within pigs, yet also demonstrate a degree of tissue specificity.

Functional analysis of lactylated proteins

To gain insights into the biological functions of lactylated proteins in the liver, we performed functional annotation using multiple databases. The Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG)/EuKaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG) database classification revealed that these proteins are primarily involved in post-translational modification, signal transduction, nuclear structure, and various metabolic pathways (Fig. 4A). KEGG secondary pathway analysis showed significant enrichment in pathways related to transport and catabolism, signal transduction, and carbon metabolism, consistent with the subcellular localization results showing predominant localization to mitochondria, nucleus, and cytoplasm (Fig. 4B).

Further functional investigations via GO and KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted. The top 20 enriched GO Biological Process (GO_BP) terms were all metabolism-related, with the most significant being monocarboxylic acid metabolic/catabolic process, nucleotide metabolism, and energy metabolism (Fig. 5A). The top four GO Cellular Component (GO_CC) terms were all mitochondria-related: mitochondrion, mitochondrial matrix, mitochondrial envelope, and mitochondrial membrane (Fig. 5B). GO Molecular Function (GO_MF) results indicated that oxidoreductase activity, catalytic activity, and ligase activity are the primary molecular functions of hepatic lactylated proteins (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Domain analysis revealed that lactylation sites are predominantly located within critical functional domains, including the biotin-requiring enzyme activity domain, acyl-CoA dehydrogenase C-terminal domain, thiolase C-terminal domain, and ATP-binding domain (Supplementary Fig. 1B). The KEGG results were also mainly enriched in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, fatty acid degradation, and multiple amino acid metabolic pathways (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that lactylated proteins in the liver are involved in regulating a wide array of biological functions.

Involvement of lactylation in energy metabolism

The primary energy-producing pathways in cells are the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), and glycolysis (Fig. 5C). Our KEGG enrichment results placed all three of these major energy metabolism pathways within the top 10 significantly enriched terms, indicating that protein lactylation may influence cellular energy metabolism. We examined the enzymes within these three pathways and found widespread involvement of lactylation (Fig. 6). In glycolysis, enzymes from glucose to phosphoenolpyruvate synthesis were found to be lactylated. In the TCA cycle, synthetic enzymes from citrate to succinate to oxaloacetate were also detected with lactylation. In the PPP, four enzymes (RGN, PGD, TKT, and TALDO) were lactylated. These findings suggest that lactylation may participate in the regulation of energy metabolism.

Discussion

Given the increasingly recognized role of lactylation in liver homeostasis, our study employed high-resolution mass spectrometry to draw the first global lactylation map of porcine liver. MS-based modification proteomics is a widely adopted strategy in lactylation research. For instance, several large-scale lactylome studies in humans have revealed the role of lactylation in tumor progression and metabolic disorders within the liver and digestive system [30, 42]. Our global lactylomic analysis identified 4,110 lactylation sites in the porcine liver, filling a gap in the understanding of hepatic lactylation in this species. We found that three histone lactylation sites are conserved among pigs, humans, and mice. Functionally, lactylated proteins in the porcine liver were highly enriched in metabolic pathways such as glycolysis and the TCA cycle, showing considerable similarity to findings in human liver studies.

As medical research advances towards greater precision, the limitations of mice as a biomedical model are increasingly constraining progress [43]. Fundamental differences between mice and humans in anatomical structure, physiological metabolism, and organ size limit the direct translation of murine research findings to the clinic [44]. Concurrently, the advantages of pigs as an excellent animal model are becoming more apparent. Compared to mice, pigs as large animals share closer genetic backgrounds, metabolic patterns, immune responses, and anatomical structures with humans [44–46]. This makes it feasible to generate pig models for various diseases using gene editing and somatic cell cloning technologies. Reliable and effective mechanistic studies for multiple diseases have been successfully conducted using pigs as model animals. In burn wound models, porcine skin, due to its similar thickness (30–140 μm vs. 50–120 μm) and structure to human skin, can firmly adhere to human wounds [47]. Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and intellectual disability (ID) are two common childhood psychiatric disorders regulated by miR-137 [48]. Knockdown of miR-137 in neuronal models derived from human, pig, and mouse induced pluripotent stem cells revealed that the pig model showed gene expression changes in key neurons more closely aligned with humans [44]. In glaucoma models, pigs also exhibited similarities to humans in gene expression patterns across multiple corresponding cell types [49]. These studies substantiate the superior characteristics of pigs as an animal model.

The liver is a structurally and functionally complex organ vital for maintaining normal life activities. According to the Global Burden of Disease Project, in 2010, over 2 million deaths worldwide were attributed to major liver diseases such as acute hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer, accounting for approximately 4% of global mortality [50]. In China, over one-fifth of the population suffers from some form of liver disease, particularly hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections, cirrhosis, liver cancer, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), alcoholic liver disease (ALD), and drug-induced liver injury (DILI), making liver disease a leading cause of morbidity and mortality [50]. Liver transplantation is one of the most effective treatments for end-stage liver disease, with 1-year survival rates reaching 80–85% for ALD [51]. The scarcity of matched donor livers is a major limiting factor. However, the emergence of multi-gene-edited pigs has significantly enhanced the feasibility of using pig livers as a source for human organs [52]. Advances in gene-editing technology can now address interspecies incompatibility, immune rejection, and coagulation dysfunction [45]. Building on a series of technological advancements in non-human primate xenotransplantation models, several successful cases of xenogeneic liver transplantation from genetically engineered pigs have recently been completed, bolstering confidence in this approach [53, 54]. One xenogeneic liver transplantation surgery from a Chinese research team in Anhui Province used a liver from a 10-gene edited pig transplanted into a patient with large hepatocellular carcinoma in the right lobe, resulting in 171 days of survival [37]. Although the patient ultimately died from recurrent upper gastrointestinal bleeding, no hyperacute or acute rejection, infection, or significant complications were observed during the transplantation period, and the patient’s liver and kidney functions remained stable [37]. This is not an isolated case. Doctors from Xijing Hospital also successfully transplanted a liver from a six-gene edited pig into a brain-dead patient, with results showing normal liver function and blood flow, no immune rejection, and normal bile production [55]. The maturation of pig liver xenotransplantation technology for clinical application could effectively address the scarcity of donor organs, offering better treatment and extended lifespans for patients with acute liver failure or end-stage chronic liver disease.

Glycolysis is known to be aberrantly active in various liver diseases, leading to excessive accumulation of its end product, lactate. Lactate regulates protein function via lactylation, playing a significant role in liver disease progression [33]. Histone H3K18la, the first identified lactylation site, widely regulates the progression of liver diseases. Studies have found that the enzymes HK2 and LDHA promote H3K18la by enhancing glycolysis, thereby participate in hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation [26, 56]. Inhibiting HK2 or LDHA to reduce lactate production can decrease H3K18la and the transcription of fibrosis-related genes, thereby alleviating fibrosis symptoms. Pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase 1 (PYCR1) is considered an important gene promoting hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression. Knockout of PYCR1 downregulates H3K18 lactylation, reducing IRS1 enhancer activity, thereby inhibiting HCC proliferation [57]. Beyond H3K18la, lactate can also promote HCC progression by enhancing lactylation at two other histone sites, H3K9 and H3K56 [27, 58]. The m6A-binding protein IGF2BP2 shows increased expression in liver fibrosis and activated HSCs, correlating with poor prognosis. IGF2BP2 promotes liver fibrosis progression by increasing aldolase A (ALDOA) expression and histone lactylation levels [59]. Non-histone lactylation also plays a crucial regulatory role in liver disease progression. Knockout of the MPC1 gene affects lactate levels in hepatocytes, influencing lactylation at the K673 site of fatty acid synthase (FASN), leading to reduced FASN activity and lipid accumulation [28]. Lactylation at K230/322 of ALDOA enhances the stemness of liver cancer stem cells (LCSCs) via the DEAD-box helicase 17 (DDX17) [60]. P300-catalyzed lactylation of ABCF1 promotes HCC cell proliferation via the HIF1α pathway [61]. Given the extensive regulatory role of lactylation in liver disease and physiology, understanding the comprehensive lactylome landscape of the porcine liver is beneficial for a deeper understanding of lactylation regulatory mechanisms in this organ.

In conclusion, our study provides a database of lactylated proteins in the porcine liver, identifying 4,110 lactylation sites on 1,486 proteins, enriching the understanding of the cross-species existence of lactylation. These lactylated proteins are primarily localized in the cytoplasm, mitochondria, and nucleus, and are extensively involved in energy metabolism-related pathways. The proteins with the highest number of modification sites are predominantly localized to mitochondria, suggesting a potential regulatory role in energy metabolism homeostasis. Porcine histone lactylation shares several conserved sites with humans, and motif analysis revealed highly enriched motifs shared with mice, possibly indicating conservation of protein lactylation across species. Our research provides a broad foundation for further in-depth investigation into the role of lactylation in porcine liver physiological functions and metabolic homeostasis.
