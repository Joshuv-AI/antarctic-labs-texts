Financial development, public health financing, and health outcomes in Sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Abubakar Musah, Godfred Aawaar, Godwin Musah, Abdallah Abdul-Mumuni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis paper examines the effect of multi-dimensional financial development on health outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). The study decomposes financial development into access, depth, and efficiency and tests their separate effects on health outcomes. The paper also examines the interaction effect of public health spending and financial development on health outcomes.MethodsThis paper is based on panel data for forty-five countries in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) collected from the World Development Indicators (WDI) of the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF) over the period 2000–2021. This study adopts the 2-step system Generalised Method of Moments (GMM) as the estimation technique to examine the relationships among the variables. This robust approach is chosen to effectively address potential issues of endogeneity, reverse causality, and unobserved country-specific heterogeneity inherent in the relationship between financial development and health outcomes.ResultsThe findings show that improvement in financial development measures improves health outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). The findings further show that, in SSA, higher out-of-pocket expenses lead to worsening health outcomes. The relationship between financial development and health outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa is strongly moderated by public health financing and out-of-pocket medical expenses, suggesting important synergistic and mitigating effects.ConclusionsThe findings imply that financial development, public health financing, and out-of-pocket health expenditure are critical when it comes to improving health outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa. To lower infant and maternal mortality rates and raise life expectancy, policymakers must implement targeted actions to strengthen the financial system and lower household out-of-pocket medical expenses whilst increasing public health financing. These findings guide health policy formulation for the improvement of health outcomes in SSA.

Introduction

The importance of health cannot be stressed enough given the fact that it precedes all human activities. The socioeconomic development of a nation is highly dependent on the well-being of its citizens. Investment in health is therefore non-negotiable as evidenced in the findings of Chireshe and Ocran [1]. It is therefore not surprising that health features prominently in multilateral development programmes such as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG3) and earlier, the Millennium Development Goals (MDG4, MDG5, MDG6).

The quality of health care in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has improved marginally since the adoption of the Millennium Development Goals [2]. However, progress is still substantially below the expectations of the people. Despite the numerous efforts to improve the health of the people in the region, health outcomes remain poor compared to the rest of the world. Maternal and child health is still a significant public health concern in SSA. In 2017 for instance, the region recorded the highest maternal mortality ratio in the world, with about 533 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births [3]. In 2020 alone, the SSA region’s 202,000 maternal deaths represented 70% of all maternal deaths globally [4]. The under-five mortality rate of 76 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2019 in SSA was the highest in the world [3]. World Health Organisation - WHO [5] also reports that an estimated 1 out of 13 children in the SSA sub-region die before their fifth birthday compared to 1 out of 199 in high-income countries. WHO [5] further reports that the region has the highest neonatal mortality rate globally, with 27 deaths per 1000 live births, compared to 3 deaths per 1000 live births in high-income countries.

The poor state of health in SSA is due mainly to the low levels of investment in the sector. Access to healthcare is limited in the region, with only 16% of the population having access to basic healthcare services in 2019 [6]. The COVID-19 pandemic has also exacerbated existing health challenges in the region, with limited resources and health systems overwhelmed by the pandemic [7]. According to WHO [8], in 2020, the sub-Saharan Africa region accounted for 25% of the global disease burden but only had 3% of the world’s health workforce. SSA’s low level of investment in the health sector stems from inadequate resources to fund investments in the health sector. WHO reports [6] show that, in 2021, the average healthcare expenditure in SSA is a meagre $186 per capita, compared to $3,840 in high-income countries. The Joint Learning Network for Universal Health Coverage (JLN) reports show that health funding is heavily dependent on external aid in many SSA countries. Aid, however, can be unpredictable and depends heavily on changes in donor priorities [9]. This reliance on external funding can lead to instability and a lack of sustainable investment in health systems. It also limits the ability of the sub-region to provide sufficient healthcare services to address the growing disease burden. Another challenge in health financing in SSA is the application of funds. The allocation of health funding is often skewed towards curative services rather than preventative services, which can have long-term benefits for population health.. Only about 2% of total health spending is allocated to prevention and public health programs [6]. This lack of investment in preventative measures can lead to the escalation of preventable diseases and higher healthcare costs in the long term. There is the need, therefore, to explore avenues through which domestic revenue mobilisation can be enhanced to provide adequate and sustainable funding to healthcare in SSA.

Financial development could be an avenue through which investment in healthcare could improve as it broadens the scope of opportunities available for individuals, businesses, and governments to raise funds for healthcare investments [1, 10]. This can lead to better access to healthcare services, which can in turn reduce morbidity and mortality rates and improve overall health outcomes [10]. Additionally, developed financial markets promote risk pooling, thereby reducing the burden of the cost of healthcare on households and individuals [11]. Health insurance and social health protection programs can provide a safety net for individuals and households facing high healthcare costs [11].

Although some efforts have been made, including the pioneering work of Claessens and Feijin [12, 48] to empirically analyse the nexus between financial development and health outcomes, some gaps remain. In the sub–Saharan African context, for instance, evidence on the nexus between financial development and health outcomes is scanty and limited to the work of Chireshe and Ocran [1, 49]. Furthermore, empirical research on the nexus between financial development and health outcomes mostly relies on financial depth and in a few instances financial access using single-dimensional measures. However, the use of unidimensional measures of financial development comes with limitations [13]. Researchers have voiced legitimate concerns about the use of single variable proxies for financial development given that it is a multifaceted concept [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Svirydzenka [13] noted that access and efficiency are equally important in assessing the quality of financial systems. If financial institutions and markets are inefficient, financial development’s contribution to economic progress would be restricted even if a financial system is sufficiently large and can provide broad access to businesses and individuals [13]. Evidence on the effects of the decomposed dimensions of financial development on health outcomes is however missing.

Via several routes, including income, education, credit access, infrastructure, and risk management, financial development improves health outcomes [12, 22]. Effective private and public mobilization of funds to finance healthcare is enhanced in a more developed and competitive financial system. It is argued that the benefits of financial development are translated into improvement in socioeconomic well-being through public spending [1, 23, 24]. Exploring how public health investment can amplify or be amplified by a developed financial system is, therefore, essential to health policy formulation. However, the literature fails to examine the interactive effects and potential complementarities of public health financing and financial development on health outcomes. Additionally, the disastrous impact of out-of-pocket health spending by households is mitigated or minimised by the improved financial sector that facilitates insurance uptake and accessible credit for health-related emergencies. Investigating whether financial development can alleviate the adverse consequences of high out-of-pocket spending is necessary but missing in the literature

Literature review

This section discusses the empirical findings on the nexus between financial development and health outcomes. Also presented here is the theoretical framework on which the study hinges. The theoretical literature and framework are presented in Sect. "Theoretical literature" while the empirical literature is presented in Sect. "Empirical literature".

Theoretical literature

The human capital theory and the health production function

Our investigation into the interplay between financial development, public health financing, and health outcomes in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is grounded in a multifaceted theoretical framework. We draw upon human capital theory to establish the fundamental value of health and the health production function to delineate the mechanisms through which health is generated and influenced by various inputs, including the financial system.

According to the theory of human capital, expenditures on services such as education, healthcare, and on-the-job training constitute critical investments in people that yield long-term benefits for both individuals and society as a whole [25, 26, 27]. Investments by governments, firms, and households in healthcare are expected to significantly improve health outcomes such as reduced mortality rates, reduced morbidity rates, and improve overall well-being [1, 28]. However, the ability of these economic agents to spend is contingent on their ability to raise funds for such investments.

The health production function (HPF), pioneered by Grossman [30], is a core concept in health economics that models the process by which various inputs are transformed into health outcomes. This framework is essential for understanding the multiple determinants of health and for guiding policy decisions aimed at improving well-being [30, 56]. Inputs refer to the diverse set of factors that contribute to the generation of health. They typically include medical care inputs such as expenditures on healthcare services, access to facilities, medical personnel, and pharmaceuticals. The model also emphasizes non-medical inputs, encompassing a broad array of socioeconomic determinants like income, education, and lifestyle choices (e.g., diet, exercise, hygiene). Environmental factors like access to clean water, sanitation, and air quality also significantly contribute to health production.

A well-functioning financial sector can significantly influence health outcomes by impacting both socioeconomic determinants and, indirectly, medical care inputs. One strategy for increasing people’s opportunities is improving financial sector development, which gives them competitive options for acquiring health insurance as well as opportunities to earn income [29]. The government receives more tax money as a result of increased income for both individuals and corporations due to financial sector development. Bhatta [29] presents the theoretical linkages between health outcomes and financial development in line with the health production function of Grossman [30] who argues that health outcomes are a function of individual inputs as discussed earlier. Bhatta [29] identified financial sector development, together with health infrastructure and government efforts, as the input variables in the determination of medical care supply. The financial system influences the availability of medical care by allocating funds for research and development, funding health education, building the infrastructure for health care, and lowering the cost of health insurance so that a larger segment of the population can afford it [29]. Bhatta [29] goes on to say that the price of medical care, people’s health status, and the interest rate on loans are the three input variables for demand for medical care.

According to Claessens and Feijin [12], households may now use credit and insurance products to help subsidize their investment in healthcare as a result of financial sector development. Economic growth creates opportunities that are indirectly created by financial development. As a result, more money is made, and individuals are better able to spend money on their health. When the economy expands, the government also receives more revenue from taxes, which allows it to spend more on public infrastructure for health care and give subsidies to residents. Besides taxes, financial development also enhances the ability of governments to mobilize resources in the domestic capital markets, which can be directed into health systems [43]. Public health systems also reduce vulnerability to health-related financial shocks, encouraging long-term financial planning and participation in formal financial systems [44]. Public health investment often drives the creation of complementary financial products (e.g., health insurance, savings for healthcare), deepening financial inclusion [45].

To summarise, the human capital theory establishes that expenditure on health leads to improvement in health outcomes such as life expectancy at birth, maternal mortality rate, disease prevalence, and child mortality rate. Financial development improves the incomes of both individuals and the government through economic growth, making health spending possible. Financial development also makes borrowing easy for households, makes insurance accessible to households, and facilitates access to healthcare. Theoretically, financial development influences health outcomes both directly (by making insurance more accessible and by relaxing borrowing requirements) and indirectly (by increasing household and governmental earnings as a result of economic growth).

Conceptual framework

Human Capital Theory, as advanced by Schultz [26] and Becker [27] and the health production function pioneered by Grossman [30], conceptualizes health as a vital component of human capital that enhances individual productivity, earnings, and overall economic growth. Within this framework, any investment that improves population health, whether through public provision, private spending, or enabling institutions, can be seen as an investment in human capital with long-term returns [30].

Based on the health production function, public health financing (a proxy for government investment in human capital) falls under medical care inputs. Out-of-pocket health expenditure, which reflects private investment by households in maintaining or improving health, also falls under medical care inputs, despite its potentially regressive effects in low-income settings [46]. Financial development enhances individuals’ and governments’ capacity to mobilize resources for health, smooth consumption, and access to health insurance thereby facilitating more effective health investments [43]. Financial development fits into the health production function as a socioeconomic determinant of health. Education and GDP per capita are also included as socioeconomic determinants of health based on Grossman’s Health Production Function [30]. The presence and quality of general infrastructure (communication networks, access to clean water and sanitation facilities) are critical environmental factors that influence health outcomes, especially in the context of SSA [57].

The multi-dimensional decomposition of financial development allows for a more precise theoretical exposition of its influence on the HPF. Financial Access directly impacts the medical care inputs by enabling households to afford preventative care, emergency treatments, and specialized services. It also allows for health providers to access funding for operational purposes. Financial access also influences non-medical inputs by allowing individuals to invest in better nutrition or education. Financial Depth enhances the availability of capital for large-scale medical care inputs like building hospitals, financing medical research, or developing robust public health programs. For governments, greater financial depth means an increased capacity to mobilize domestic resources for health sector investments. Financial Efficiency improves the productivity of both medical and non-medical health inputs. For instance, efficient financial systems reduce the costs of health insurance, making it more affordable, and ensuring that funds allocated for health infrastructure are utilized optimally.

To explore the complementarities between these channels, two interaction terms are included in the model: (Financial development × public health financing) and (Financial development × out-of-pocket health expenditure).

The interaction between financial development and public health financing is theoretically plausible. A more developed financial system can significantly amplify the impact of public health investments. For example, efficient financial markets can facilitate easier and more cost-effective government borrowing for health initiatives, improve the management and disbursement of public health funds, and enable public-private partnerships in healthcare infrastructural development. This means that for every dollar of public health financing, the health outcome return is greater in a more financially developed environment.

The mitigating interaction of financial development and out-of-pocket health expenditure interaction is also theoretically plausible. while high out-of-pocket costs can be detrimental to health, a developed financial system can help alleviate this burden. Improved financial access and efficiency enable greater uptake of affordable health insurance, wider availability of health-specific credit products, and better mechanisms for households to save for health emergencies.

These financial tools act as a buffer in preventing health shocks from leading to catastrophic financial consequences that force households to forgo necessary medical care. Therefore, in line with the human capital theory, the health production function, and the Claessens and Feijin [12] model, this paper analyses the relationships among the study variables using the framework presented in Fig. 1.

Empirical literature

Empirical literature on the nexus between financial development and health outcomes is scanty. Researchers have paid much more attention to the effect of financial development on economic growth and the effect of healthcareexpenditures on health outcomes [31]. Nonetheless, a few studies have been done on the topic. In Sub-Saharan Africa, the work of Chireshe and Ocran [1] remains prominent. They however failed to examine the effect of financial efficiency on health outcomes. Using information from 54 economies, Claessens and Feijen [12] conducted a ground-breaking analysis of the effect of financial development on health outcomes using descriptive statistics and cross-country comparisons. They discovered a significant positive relationship between life expectancy and private credit to GDP. Also, they discovered a link between private credit to GDP and the mortality rate for children under the age of five. The study, however, only relied on descriptive statistics and failed to establish causality. In a different study, Claessens and Feijen [12] used data from a sample of 49 countries to examine the effect of financial development on malnutrition. They employed panel regression estimation techniques, cross-country OLS, and instrumental variables. The findings showed that improving the financial sector lowers the rate of malnutrition. Both studies, however, made use of single-dimensional measures of financial development and did not also focus on the SSA context. Claessens and Feijen [12] also fail to incorporate the separate effects of financial access, financial depth, and financial efficiency in their analyses.

Bhatta [29] looks at the relationship between financial development and health outcomes in the setting of low-income Asian and high-income OECD nations. According to the study’s findings, financial development helps to improve health outcomes including longer life expectancies and decreased infant mortality. The financial development factors have larger correlations with health outcomes for developing nations than for developed nations, indicating that financial development is more important in producing better health outcomes in developing nations than in developed nations. However, Bhatta’s analysis does not examine the routes via which financial growth might affect health outcomes. Just like Claesens and Feijin, their study fails to examine the SSA context, the region with the worst statistics in terms of health outcomes.

In South Africa, the direction of causation between financial development and human capital was also explored by Hakeem and Oluitan [22]. The analysis discovered that there was only a marginal correlation between financial development and all proxies for human capital, except for life expectancy and secondary school enrolment. Similar to the majority of research in the literature, the impacts of the financial development’s parts broken down have not been discussed. However, analysing the separate effects of financial development components will provide the opportunity to formulate specific policies that work best.

By examining the relationship between financial development and health outcomes using micro and macro data sets, Rewilak [32] added to the nascent research on the topic. The study demonstrated a negative correlation between infant and child mortality rates and deep financial systems, as determined by bank deposits as a percentage of GDP.

Alam et al. [24] use yearly data spanning the years 1972–2013 to examine the contribution of financial development to the sharp increase in life expectancy in Bangladesh. They demonstrate that there is a long-run link between financial development and life expectancy in the context of globalization, income inequality, and economic growth by using cointegration and the ARDL bounds testing approach. The long-run elasticities show that globalization and financial development have a favourable impact on Bangladesh’s life expectancy. Their study only used one financial development indicator and did not examine the effect of financial development’s components on health outcomes. The study also differs from the current study in the sense that the findings are limited to Bangladesh and fail to capture cross-country dynamics.

In sub-Saharan African (SSA) nations, Chireshe and Ocran [1] investigated the impact of financial development on child health outcomes and life expectancy. They discovered that financial depth and access have a beneficial impact on health outcomes in SSA using the single proxies of financial access (number of ATMs and commercial bank branches per 100 000 persons) and financial depth (money supply and bank loans to the private sector to GDP ratios). These conclusions were true regardless of the models used (random effect, fixed effect, and Two-Stage-Least Squares (2SLS). However, their study failed to analysethe effects of financial efficiency and the overall development of the financial sector on health outcomes. They depended on unidimensional measurements of financial access and depth. Additionally, their study did not examine the transmission mechanism of financial development through public health financing on health outcomes. Healthcare spending, according to Chireshe et al. [1] and Rana et al. [23], is a crucial channel via which financial development affects health outcomes. The government can efficiently mobilise revenue in a better financial system. On the other hand, public spending enhances the financial sector’s performance and makes the financial system more liquid. An interaction effect capturing this transmission is therefore necessary, particularly in the most deprived setting (Sub-Saharan Africa).

Methodology and data

This section presents the methodology of the study, which is in four phases. Firstly, this section presents the description of the main variables used. Secondly, we present the data, and the sources of the data used in this study. In the final stage, we specify the empirical model and the estimation technique which helps us to estimate the linkages between the various measures of health outcomes and the various measures of financial development.

Variable descriptions and measurements

Dependent variables

Health outcomes are the dependent (outcome) variables of this study. Three (3) measures of health outcomes are used in this study, maternal mortality, child mortality, and life expectancy at birth. We compute an index for child mortality using under-five mortality rate (per 1,000 live births), neonatal mortality rate (per 1,000 live births) and infant mortality rate (per 1,000 live births) using principal component analysis (PCA) in Stata. PCA was conducted using the raw mortality indicators (under-five, infant, and neonatal mortality rates) without standardization since they all have the same scale of measurement. Thus, the analysis was based on the covariance matrix, and variables contributed to the index proportionally to their variance. The first principal component was used to construct the child mortality index, with weights determined by the eigenvector loadings. Higher scores reflect higher child mortality. We use maternal mortality and life expectancy at birth (years) as stand-alone additional measures of health outcomes. These variables are commonly used as measures of health outcomes in the literature.

Independent variables

Financial development

Financial development is the main independent variable of this study. Different measures have been used by different researchers. Financial development is defined as the set of elements, regulations, and institutions that contribute to successful financial intermediation and markets, as well as deep and broad access to funds and financial services [33]. Researchers have often focused on single-dimension measures of financial development such as the ratio of private credit to GDP, money supply, market capitalisation, and commercial bank branches [34, 35, 36]. Using such measures, however, does not prove sufficient as various aspects of finance capture different aspects of financial performance [14]. A financial development index most appropriately captures various aspects of financial development [38]. This study employs the IMF’s broad-based measure of financial development first proposed by Sahay et al. [16] and computed by Svirydzenka [13]. These measures of financial development capture various aspects of financial development from financial markets to financial institutions across depth, access, and efficiency. In line with Musah and Aawaar [21], this study extends the IMF data by computing broad-based indices for financial depth, financial access, and financial efficiency using the computed variables of access, depth, and efficiency for both financial markets and financial institutions from the IMF database. The study constructs an index for financial depth using financial institutions’ depth and financial markets’ depth, financial access using access to financial institutions and access to financial markets, and financial efficiency using financial institutions’ efficiency and financial markets’ efficiency.

Public health expenditure

Different researchers use different measures for public health financing (PHF) in the literature. In this study, public health financing is measured as government expenditure on health expressed as a percentage of GDP. This measure more appropriately captures total health financing by the government.

Out-of-pocket health expenditure

This represents household healthcare spending. Households directly pay for healthcare through out-of-pocket costs. We utilize the WDI of the World Bank’s out-of-pocket health spending per capita in international dollars at purchasing power parity.

Control variables

This paper includes basic infrastructure measured as the proportion of the population who have access to basic water services, real GDP per capita, and education level measured as gross enrolment ratio in primary education as control variables. These control variables were selected based on the literature.

Data and sources

This study collects data for the period 2000 to 2020 for forty-five (45) sub-Saharan African countries. The choice of this period is based purely on data availability on the study variables, particularly on the health outcomes for the sub-Saharan African context. Data on health outcomes (maternal mortality rate, under-five mortality, neonatal mortality, life expectancy at birth, and public health financing – measured as general government health expenditure expressed as a percentage of GDP) are downloaded from Health Statistics (World Development Indicators, WDI) of the World Bank. Financial development data is collected from the International Monetary Fund (IMF). The IMF broad-based financial development data computed by Svirydzenka [13] first developed by Sahay et al. [16] is used. Data on the control variables are also collected from the World Development Indicators of the World Bank.

To address possible endogeneity issues due to the dynamic nature of the dependent variables, reverse causality, and unobserved country-specific heterogeneity, this paper estimates Eq. (3) using Blundell and Bond’s [40] Two-Step System Generalized Method of Moment Estimator (two-step system GMM). The paper employs the two-step estimator in conjunction with Windmeijer’s [41] robust corrected standard errors. There are various reasons why the two-step SGMM was adopted. Firstly, the system GMM methodology enables us to treat the dependent variable as a dynamic process, accounting for the dependent variable’s previous level and the possibility of the effect of past health outcomes on present health outcomes. Second, the usage of the SGMM is more suitable since there are more countries (N = 45) than there are periods (T = 21). Finally, because the panel is dynamic, the GMM method enables us to adjust for endogeneity and potential nickel bias. While health outcomes studies are prone to endogeneity problems [24] and it might be challenging to obtain reliable external instruments, depending on a strategy that utilizes internal instruments can help allay endogeneity worries. We use the Arellano and Bond test for second-order serial correlation in the error term and the Sargan/Hansen test of overidentification limits to assess the consistency of estimates. Roodman [42] claims that the Sargan and Hansen tests evaluate sample analogues of the estimation’s moment conditions in order to assess the validity of the instruments. Due to the unreliability of study results from very proliferated instruments, the number of instruments employed should not exceed the number of groups. Last but not least, the error term could be serially correlated in the first order but not in the second order since that might indicate that the model has been mis-specified.

It also reveals that while GDP per capita is moderately correlated with other independent variables (e.g., out-of-pocket expenditure and infrastructure), its VIF values, though slightly high, remain below critical thresholds.

Financial development and child mortality

Table 5 shows that financial development index and financial efficiency record insignificant coefficients suggesting that overall financial sector development and financial efficiency do not significantly affect child mortality in SSA. The positive sign, however, is unexpected and could be because financial development is not inclusive enough to affect households most vulnerable to child mortality in SSA [47]. Financial depth and financial access record significant negative coefficients suggesting that improvement in financial depth and financial access reduces child mortality in SSA. Consistent with existing literature [24, 48], the results in Table 5 further show that public health financing has a significant and negative effect on child mortality in SSA in models 2 to 4 of Table 5. This implies that increased public spending on health reduces child mortality. However, it must be noted that in model 1, public health financing exhibits a positive coefficient, suggesting that increases in public health financing cause increases in child mortality. The coefficient is, however, insignificant. Out-of-pocket health expenditure by households however shows a significant positive effect on child mortality in models 1 and 2 showing that increases in out-of-pocket expenditure leads to increases in child mortality. The coefficient is however negative and insignificant in models 3 and 4.

On the transmission channels, the results show that public health financing moderates the nexus between financial access and child mortality since the coefficient of the interaction between financial access and public health financing is negative and significant. This shows that financial access is more effective in reducing child mortality in countries where the government spends more on healthcare. The interaction of public health financing with financial efficiency is counterintuitively positive and weakly significant at 10% as shown in Table 5. This appears to suggest that increasing public funding of health in an efficient financial market worsens child mortality. This could be because improved financial efficiency may encourage the reallocation of funds away from basic child health toward non-child health sectors, especially if not paired with good health policy design [58]. The interactions of public health financing with overall financial development and with financial depth are not significant in explaining child mortality.

The coefficients of the interaction terms of out-of-pocket health expenditure by households with financial development index and financial depth are negative and significant at the 5% level. This suggests that overall financial development and financial depth are more effective in explaining child mortality when out-of-pocket expenditure is high. The interaction of out-of-pocket expenditure with financial access is positive although not significant. The positive interaction suggests that increased financial access amplifies the adverse effects of high out-of-pocket health spending on child mortality, possibly by encouraging healthcare use that imposes unsustainable financial burdens on households. This could imply that even when people can access financial services, relying heavily on out-of-pocket payments can worsen child health outcomes if it leads to financial hardships. The interaction of financial efficiency with out-of-pocket expenditure also exhibits a counterintuitively positive sign although not significant. This is explained by the fact that increased financial efficiency may create fiscal space, but if governments reduce public subsidies for healthcare, households face higher out-of-pocket costs that can discourage care-seeking behaviour, leading to higher child mortality [59].

We also observed from the results in Table 5 that education shows counterintuitive significant positive coefficients across all models. A positive coefficient of education on child mortality in SSA may reflect the limitations of using gross primary enrolment as a proxy for meaningful education. This measure often includes over-aged or repeating pupils and does not capture educational quality or completion. As a result, high enrolment may not translate into improved health knowledge or behaviours that reduce child mortality. Gross enrolment ratios can be misleading and may not reflect actual learning outcomes [60]. This is consistent with existing literature that more schooling does not always mean better child health knowledge or practices [51, 61]. The coefficient of infrastructure is negative in models 1 and 3 but turns positive in models 2 and 4. However, all of them are not significant. Unexpectedly, the coefficient of GDP per capita is positive and statistically significant across all four models. This unexpected sign could be attributable to income distributional issues in SSA. When growth is captured mainly by elites, average GDP per capita rises but poor households (where most child deaths occur) see little benefit.

All estimated models in Table 5 pass the post-estimation diagnostics of GMM. The number of groups is greater than the number of instruments in all four models. The Arellano-Bond tests for serial correlation in the first-differenced residuals were performed. The first-order autocorrelation [AR (1)] test rejected the null hypothesis of no serial correlation as expected due to differencing. In all models, the AR (2) test failed to reject the null hypothesis of no second-order serial correlation (p-value > 0.05), indicating that the moment conditions are valid. The absence of second-order autocorrelation [AR (2)] is a necessary condition for the validity of the instruments.

Financial development and life expectancy at birth

Source: authors’ computations, corrected standard errors are in parentheses, ***p < .01, ** p< .05, *p < .1

In Table 7, the results show that public health financing has a positive and significant effect on life expectancy at birth across all four models, suggesting that enhanced public spending on health will improve access to healthcare at subsidised costs and improve the overall well-being of citizens thereby increasing life expectancy. The results further show that out-of-pocket health expenditure by households has a significant negative effect on life expectancy in all four models, showing that when the out-of-pocket expenditure is high, life expectancy reduces since households will struggle to pay for medical care. Financial development index records a positive and significant effect on life expectancy suggesting that improvement in the overall financial sector will contribute to the wellbeing of people and hence enhance life expectancy. The components of financial development (financial depth, financial access, and financial efficiency) however, do not show direct significant effects in explaining changes in life expectancy.

On the transmission channels, the results in Table 7 show that public health financing moderates the nexus between financial development index and life expectancy but does not significantly moderate the nexus between life expectancy and the components of financial development (depth, access, and efficiency). Public health spending therefore transmits the benefits of financial sector development into improvement of overall well-being reflected in longer life expectancy. This aligns with the health production function, which posits that systemic inputs like public spending are essential for health improvement [30]. The coefficient of the interaction between out-of-pocket expenditure and financial access is positive and significant at 1%. On the contrary, the coefficients of the interactions of out-of-pocket expenditure with all the other three measures of financial development are not significant. This positive interaction between out-of-pocket health expenditure and financial access indicates that in high out-of-pocket health expenditure contexts, access to financial services helps households better manage health expenses, thereby supporting health outcomes [62] and hence, improving life expectancy. The insignificance of the other interaction terms suggests that financial access is the most critical dimension of financial development for mitigating the health burden of out-of-pocket health payments.

GDP per capita has a significant positive effect on life expectancy in three of the models (1, 2 and 4) but has an insignificant positive effect in model 3. Infrastructure unexpectedly has a significant negative effect on life expectancy in all four models of Table 7. The unexpected negative effect of infrastructure (basic water access) on life expectancy likely reflects contextual factors rather than the harmful effect of water services. Access to basic water is our proxy for infrastructure in this study. Basic access to water does not guarantee the safety of the water. Hence, access to water could be going up but if the water quality is poor, it may hike waterborne diseases and hence depress life expectancy. The negative coefficient shown by infrastructure could be exposing the state of water quality in the SSA region. Education, however, has no significant effect on life expectancy across all four models.

All estimated models in Table 7 also pass the post-estimation diagnostics of GMM. The number of groups is greater than the number of instruments in all four models. The Arellano-Bond tests for serial correlation in the first-differenced residuals were performed. The first-order autocorrelation [AR (1)] test rejected the null hypothesis of no serial correlation, as expected due to differencing. In all models, the AR (2) test failed to reject the null hypothesis of no second-order serial correlation (p-value > 0.05), indicating that the moment conditions are valid. The absence of second-order autocorrelation [AR (2)] is a necessary condition for the validity of the instruments.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the study's findings, sensitivity analyses was conducted by testing the impact of major crisis periods on the relationship between financial development and health outcomes in Sub-Saharan Africa. Specifically, we examined the effects of the 2008–2009 Global Financial Crisis and the 2020–2021 COVID-19 pandemic. These years were excluded from the dataset, and the models were re-estimated accordingly.

Table 8 reports the robustness results from models re-estimated after excluding the crisis years of 2008, 2009, and 2020 to account for the potential for such periods to distort the relationships among the variables. The findings remain largely consistent with those from the full sample, confirming the robustness of the estimated relationships. Coefficients for key variables such as public health finanicning, out-of-pocket health expenditure (OOP), and financial development components retain their signs and levels of significance. Public health financing (PHF) continues to exert a significant negative effect on child and maternal mortality and a positive effect on life expectancy, reinforcing its key role in improving health outcomes. Out-of-pocket health expenditure is associated with higher child mortality and lower life expectancy, suggesting that increased private health spending may worsen health outcomes in the absence of adequate financial protection mechanisms. The direct effect of financial development (Findex) and its components (depth, access, efficiency) varies across models, with access and depth showing stronger and more consistent associations with reductions in child and maternal mortality. Moreover, some interaction effects between financial development and public health financing remain significant, particularly the negative and significant coefficient of financial access and public health financing on child mortality. This implies that financial inclusion enhances the effectiveness of public health spending.

Conclusions and policy implications

This study examined the effects of four financial development measures on three measures of health outcomes in SSA. The study further analysed the moderation effect of public education financing and out-of-pocket expenditure by households on the nexus between financial development and health outcomes in SSA. The two-step system GMM estimation technique was adopted in analysing the relationships among the dependent and independent variables.

Key findings of the study

The findings show that, to a greater extent, financial development plays a significant role in improving health outcomes in SSA. This is consistent with the evidence from Rwanda, where the expansion of community-based health insurance schemes, facilitated by improved financial infrastructure, contributed to significant reductions in maternal and child mortality [52]. The findings further show that the components of financial development (financial access, financial depth, and financial efficiency) largely show favourbale effects on the measures of health outcomes in SSA.

The study further finds that enhanced public financing of health will reduce child mortality and maternal mortality and also improve life expectancy in SSA. Evidence from Ghana shows that targeted government health expenditures, especially in child and maternal health programs, can lead to improvements in health indicators [53].

This paper also finds that increases in out-of-pocket expenditure worsen life expectancy in SSA. Consistent with this finding is the introduction of Universal Health Coverage in Thailand (launched in the year 2002) which significantly reduced out-of-pocket payments and improved access to healthcare [54]. The community-based health insurance (CBHI) program of Rwanda is another case that lends credence to the importance of reducing the out-of-pocket health spending. The CBHI significantly reduced user fees for a range of healthcare services for its members, leading to better health outcomes in Rwanda. Research shows that there is higher patronage of health services among CBHI users [52].

The interaction of public health financing with measures of financial development largely improves child mortality, maternal mortality and life expectancy in SSA. Finally, the study finds that the benefits of financial development are more pronounced when out-of-pocket expenditure on health is high. Sensitivity to crises period analyses revealed that the findings are robust and are not distorted by short-term shocks.

Policy recommendations

We therefore recommend implementing policies that are geared towards improving financial sector development to enhance health outcomes in SSA in line with the African Union’s Agenda 2063 strategy on continental financial institutions such as the African Investment Bank, Pan-African Stock Exchange, African Monetary Fund, and African Central Bank [63]. Improving financial access is crucial to enhancing health outcomes in the SSA region. To improve financial access, policymakers must adopt strategies to improve digital payment platforms, mobile banking, and microfinance services, especially in rural and underserved areas. Specifically, subsidies and tax incentives should be granted to mobile network operators and fintech companies to establish new agent points in areas with low to no financial services penetration. The example of Kenya’s M-Pesa model should serve as a reference point for low-income SSA countries to improve access to financial services. Policymakers should improve financial depth by enhancing domestic and external capital mobilisation for health infratructual development. Forging public-private partnerships for health care investment as well as promoting diversification of financial instruments could deepen the financial sector. For example, governments in the SSA region could promote the development of health bonds aimed at rasing capital for investments in the health sector. Competition is a key driver of service quality and affordability leading to efficiency in the financial sector. Policymakers should embark upon financial literacy programs to educate citizens on their health insurance options. Such campaigns can help them manage medical expenses effectively and hold healthcare providers accountable. Financial efficiency will ultimately result in judicious allocation of health resources.

Sub-Saharan African governments must also enhance public health financing in order to fully exploit the benefits of financial sector development. Governments must increase budgetary allocation to the health sector in tandem with the Abuja Declaration target of at least 15% [55] and the African Union’s Agenda 2063 [63]. Human capital development is one of the main priority areas of the African Union’s Agenda 2063. This remains critical to achieving sustainable health outcomes in the sub-region. Funding is a major challenge for the SSA region. However, governments can raise resources by fighting corruption, and tracking down on tax evasion, and illicit financial flows. These activities drain significant resources that could otherwise be invested in public services like healthcare.

Governments must also work towards lowering out-of-pocket health expenditures by households in order to improve the general well-being of citizens and save lives. SSA countries should expand risk-pooling mechanisms such as national health insurance schemes. Subsidies for vulnerable groups, removal of user fees for maternal and child health services, and social safety nets can protect households from catastrophic out-of-pocket health expenditures. Public health insurance schemes such as the National Health Insurance Scheme in Ghana should be implemented to take off the burden of out-of-pocket health expenditure on households.

Limitations and future reseach direction

This study, like many studies, is not free from limitations. A limitation of this study is the exclusive focus on out-of-pocket expenditures within private health financing, thereby not comprehensively capturing the full range of private sector contributions, such as private health insurance or corporate health programs. The exclusion of health infrastructure as an independent variable may limit the comprehensive understanding of its influence on health outcomes. Future research could benefit from incorporating these additional dimensions to provide a more holistic analysis. Sub-Saharan African countries are highly heterogeneous in terms of socio-economic, institutional, and regional characteristics [64]. Future studies could address this by conducting comparative analyses across different sub-regions (West, East, and South) to test the consistency of the relationships examined in the current study. Future work could also investigate gender-disaggregated health outcomes to determine whether financial development affects males and females differently. Variations in income levels may influence how different segments of the population access and benefit from financial services,. Future studies should, therefore, conduct heterogeneity analyses based on income levels to better understand the differential impact of financial development on health outcomes in Sub-Saharan Africa. Similarly, governance quality, such as the effectiveness of institutions, control of corruption, and regulatory quality, can shape how financial resources are allocated and utilised within the health sector. It will also impact public health financing on health outcomes in the SSA region. Exploring these dimensions can help support the development of context-specific financial development strategies aimed at improving health outcomes across SSA.
