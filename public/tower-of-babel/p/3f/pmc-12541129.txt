A Case of Drug‐Induced Lung Injury With Systemic Features Resembling DRESS Caused by Saiko‐Ka‐Ryukotsu‐Borei‐To and Goreisan
Authors: Tomoyuki Ogata, Susumu Takahashi, Kentaro Nakamura, Tomohiro Moriya, Takahiro Ishikawa, Takaaki Yamashita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 52‐year‐old man developed fever 3 days after starting Saiko‐ka‐ryukotsu‐borei‐to and Goreisan and was admitted with respiratory failure 16 days later. He showed trunk rash, blood eosinophilia, atypical lymphocytes and elevated liver enzymes. Chest computed tomography demonstrated interlobular septal thickening with patchy ground‐glass opacities. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid showed lymphocytosis with mild eosinophilia. Discontinuation of both drugs and corticosteroid therapy improved his clinical and radiological findings. This case represents drug‐induced lung injury with systemic features resembling drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), highlighting the importance of considering herbal medicines as potential causes.

Introduction

Drug‐induced lung injury (DLI) can result from a wide range of agents, including antibiotics, antineoplastic drugs and immunomodulators, and its diagnosis is often challenging because of the diversity of clinical presentations and radiological findings [1]. DLI due to herbal medicines has also been reported, and among them, an association with Scutellaria baicalensis (Ogon) has been suggested [2]. In addition, it has been reported that DLI is observed as an accompanying manifestation of drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), which is characterised by fever, rash, haematological abnormalities and organ involvement, in approximately 5% of cases [3]. A systematic review by Taweesedt et al. further highlighted that a wide spectrum of pulmonary manifestations, including interstitial infiltrates and acute respiratory distress syndrome, may occur in DRESS and can even present as initial signs [4]. In recent years, this discrepancy in definitions has prompted renewed discussion regarding the validity of the current disease concept. In this context, we report the first case of DLI with systemic features resembling DRESS caused by herbal medicines.

Case Report

The patient was a 52‐year‐old man with no major past medical or allergy history, and he was not taking any other medications. After initiation of Saiko‐ka‐ryukotsu‐borei‐to (TJ‐12; Tsumura & Co., Japan, 7.5 g/day in three divided doses) and Goreisan (TJ‐17; Tsumura & Co., Japan, 7.5 g/day in three divided doses), he developed fever on Day 3, a skin eruption on Day 15 and was admitted with respiratory failure on Day 16. Multiple edematous erythematous lesions measuring 0.5–3 cm in diameter with indurated central areas appeared on the trunk (Figure 1), and his SpO2 was 88% on room air. Chest computed tomography showed thickening of the interlobular septa and patchy ground‐glass opacities in both lungs (Figure 2). Laboratory tests revealed eosinophilia, atypical lymphocytes and elevated liver enzymes. There were no clinical findings suggestive of connective tissue disease, and the antinuclear antibody was negative. Drug lymphocyte stimulation tests (DLSTs) for both agents and human herpesvirus 6 (HHV‐6) DNA on day 16 after administration were negative. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) obtained from the right B5 segment demonstrated a total cell count of 1.13 × 106/mL, with 63% lymphocytes, 14% eosinophils and a CD4/8 ratio of 0.5. Cultures for bacteria, fungi and mycobacteria were all negative (Table 1). DLI caused by both agents was suspected and discontinuation of the drugs along with steroid pulse therapy (methylprednisolone 500 mg for 3 days) was initiated, followed by oral prednisolone (30 mg/day). Within 2 days of treatment initiation, the fever resolved and oxygenation improved and his dyspnoea also improved in parallel with oxygenation, while the skin eruption had disappeared by Day 14. Radiological findings also showed gradual improvement consistent with the clinical course. Because a DRESS‐like condition was suspected, corticosteroids were gradually tapered over 6 weeks and then discontinued. No recurrence was observed after cessation of steroids.

Discussion

This case involved DLI accompanied by systemic findings resembling DRESS during concomitant use of the herbal medicines Saiko‐ka‐ryukotsu‐borei‐to and Goreisan. In Japanese clinical practice, Saiko‐ka‐ryukotsu‐borei‐to is prescribed for anxiety, insomnia and palpitations and contains 2.5 g of Scutellaria root (per 7.5 g/day of the extract granules, crude ‑drug equivalent). In contrast, Goreisan is used for oedema, diarrhoea and heat exhaustion and does not contain Scutellaria. Previous reports have shown that most cases of herbal medicine‐related DLI were associated with preparations containing Scutellaria [1] and an analysis of adverse event databases also demonstrated that Scutellaria carries the highest risk [2]. In the present case, it is possible that Scutellaria contained in Saiko‐ka‐ryukotsu‐borei‐to was the causative component; however, involvement of Goreisan could not be completely excluded. Because both agents were started and discontinued on the same day and the DLST results for both were negative, the specific causative drug could not be identified. Moreover, the usefulness of DLST in herbal medicine‐related cases is limited and regardless of whether the result is positive or negative, causality must be interpreted with caution. Although rechallenge is the most reliable method to identify the causative drug, it is not ethically acceptable in routine clinical practice. This limitation applies to our case as well.

On chest computed tomography, the lung injury in this patient was characterised by interlobular septal thickening and scattered ground‐glass opacities. Similar findings have also been reported in lung injury caused by herbal medicines containing Scutellaria, although their frequency and underlying mechanisms are not consistent [1]. BALF analysis showed lymphocyte‐predominant infiltration with mild eosinophilia, suggesting that an allergic mechanism was involved at both the airway and alveolar levels. The pathogenesis of DLI involves both allergic and direct cytotoxic mechanisms, but allergic mechanisms are thought to predominate in herbal medicine‐related cases [1]. In particular, Type IV hypersensitivity (delayed‐type hypersensitivity) is considered important and the lymphocyte‐predominant BALF findings in this case support this mechanism.

In addition to lung injury, this patient presented with edematous erythema, liver dysfunction, fever and fatigue. Peripheral blood tests showed eosinophilia and atypical lymphocytes. According to the RegiSCAR scoring system for DRESS [5], the total score was 5, corresponding to ‘probable’ (Table 2). Points were assigned for eosinophilia, atypical lymphocytes and involvement of the lung and liver, whereas the skin rash was judged to be limited and not typical, and, therefore, scored 0. However, considering the morphology and timing of the skin eruption, we judged that the most appropriate diagnosis was DLI with systemic manifestations resembling, but not typical of, DRESS. DRESS is a delayed‐type hypersensitivity reaction that typically develops 2–8 weeks after drug initiation, characterised by fever, rash, eosinophilia, atypical lymphocytes and liver dysfunction. In Japan, a similar disease concept, drug‐induced hypersensitivity syndrome (DIHS), has been proposed [6]. Although these two conditions are often used interchangeably, unlike DIHS, DRESS does not require HHV‐6 reactivation as a diagnostic criterion and is not restricted to specific causative drugs, resulting in the inclusion of diverse cases. Recently, international consensus statements on the diagnosis and management of DRESS have been published, reflecting an increasing need to refine this disease concept [7]. Pulmonary involvement, including interstitial infiltrates, has previously been reported in association with DRESS and is considered part of its established clinical spectrum [4]. However, to our knowledge, no previous reports have described DRESS‐like systemic manifestations with pulmonary injury triggered by herbal medicines, underscoring the novelty and clinical significance of the present case. Atypical cases are easily overlooked in clinical practice and even if recognised, they may remain unreported. Systematic accumulation of similar cases and investigation of their relationship with DRESS will contribute to clarifying the disease concept and improving early recognition and appropriate management in daily practice.
