An innovative hierarchical porous oxide surface with enhanced surface area for promoting early-stage bone regeneration potential
Authors: Chia-Ling Shen, Kuo-Sheng Hung, Hsieh-Tsung Shen, Yu-Sin Jennifer Ou, Jungshan Chang, Chi-Hsun Tsai, Takashi Saito, Yi-Ren Pao, Keng-Liang Ou, Chih-Ming Tsai, Xiaoxia Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, an innovative hierarchical porous oxide surface (HPOS) with an increased surface area was developed on biomedical 316L stainless steel (SS) implants to promote early bone and implant integration. The surface features and in vivo performance of the HPOS implant were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy, nanoindenter, roughness measurement instrument, contact angle goniometer, micro-computed tomography, and histological analysis. Results showed that the HPOS consisted of a hybrid micro-nano-pores structure and had a relatively low elastic modulus (150.6 ± 7.8 GPa, *p < 0.05), high roughness (8.6 ± 1.2 μm, **p < 0.01), and a low contact angle (15.6 ± 1.8°, ***p < 0.001). Additionally, rapid new bone formation was observed on the HPOS of the 316L SS implant modified at 5 V for 5 min, producing pore sizes from approximately 300 nm to 13.5 μm, with bone contact interfaces exceeding 64% at 12 weeks. The HPOS maintained mechanical interlocking ability at the microscale, which positively influenced osseointegration. Moreover, the difference in new bone formation thickness between the unmodified control group (0.45 ± 0.11 mm) and the modified 316L SS implant with HPOS (0.66 ± 0.13 mm) was statistically significant at 12 weeks post-implantation (*p < 0.05). These findings suggest that forming an innovative HPOS on a 316L SS implant could offer a potential solution to enhance early-stage osseointegration in clinical applications.

Introduction

The incorporation of bone and metal implants describes osseointegration as fundamentally important, facilitating osteoblast formation through spontaneous adhesion to the implant surface for extracellular matrix mineralization [1, 2]. Since then, the degree of bone fixation for metal implants has been shown to shorten therapeutic periods and enhance bone quality for procedural treatments [3]. Most reported findings indicate that bone plays a vital role in producing blood cells necessary for the self-repair of bone defects. However, loosening and suboptimal integration of host bone with metal implants, such as biomedical 316L stainless steel (SS), remain ongoing issues [1, 4]. Failure to achieve integration between the bone and implant surface has been estimated to account for a high rate of implant failures over the years [5]. Therefore, the surface must possess bioactive characteristics to ensure that an implant remains well integrated with the bone for an extended period [2].

Despite the success of various surface treatments for 316L SS implants used in orthopedic and dental applications, complications persist, and these treatments rarely provide full physiological functionality [6, 7]. The 316L SS implant, while possessing adequate mechanical integrity, faces complications such as insufficient bone-tissue integration, inflammatory issues, and other localized reactions at the implant surface [6, 8, 9]. Recently, the fabrication of anodized 316L SS topography has focused on the growth of nanoscale structures, allowing for better osseointegration capabilities and drug delivery in shortened therapeutic times [2, 10, 11]. According to our previous study [12], anodizing 316L SS with an innovative hierarchical porous oxide surface (HPOS), referred to as a hybrid micro-nano-pores (HMNPs) structure, has been found to enhance osteoblast adhesion and improve osseointegration response potential. The diverse anodizing techniques that create an oxide layer for implant surface treatment, producing HMNPs topography, have inspired this current treatment approach. The dual porous structures, comprised of micro-pores containing γ-phase nano-Cr2O3 oxides and nano-pores including oxide layers, play critical roles in promoting cell viability [12, 13]. Other findings indicate that HMNPs structures with rough surfaces increase osteocyte differentiation and stimulate growth factor production, suggesting their potential to enhance implant integration [14, 15].

Although HMNPs structures can regulate cell behavior such as spreading, geometry site, and alignment through mechanical stimulation, the impact of nanoporous surfaces on osteoblast response has also gained attention. Early interactions between osteoblasts and modified nano-pores can significantly boost cell adhesion, differentiation, and proliferation [16, 17]. However, some studies have highlighted the difficulty in isolating the effects of nano-pore topography from those of other surface features, including micro-pore structures [16, 18, 19]. Broadly, research indicates that the biological synergy of micro or nano-pore topography on cellular behaviors can be enhanced when the porous surfaces obtain a combination of micro- and nano-pores without sacrificing the established advantages of micro-structures; nano-pores embedded within micro-pores appear to be most effective for creating a bioactive environment conducive to bone formation [20–22]. As stated above, this study focused on the HPOS composed of an HMNPs structure to promote early-stage osseointegration capability through an investigation using a rabbit model. Due to their size differences, this topic has become a subject of interest.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of the HPOS implant

The machined biomedical 316L SS implants (a diameter of 2 mm and a length of 10 mm) and discs (Φ10 × 1 mm) were adopted as substrates for analysis. In the beginning, the substrates received an ultrasonic cleaning process in acetone and ethanol, then rinsed in deionized water. After that, 4% nitric acid solution and 2% hydrofluoric acid were added to etch the substrates at 25 °C within 30 s. The substrates were ultrasonically cleaned using deionized water and then modified at 5 V for 5 min through an optimal anodization method into a 1 M sulfuric acid (98% H2SO4) and 1 M nitric acid (85% HNO3) solution under 25 °C constant temperature. The platinum counter electrode was adopted during the treatment process at a 40 mm distance between the platinum electrode and substrate, as shown in Fig. 1. Surface characterization

Surface morphology and chemical composition (semi-quantitative) of the investigated implants were examined using JSM-6500F field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, JEOL, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS; INCA, Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK) for average scales and inter-irregularities space on the surface area. The surface elastic modulus of the investigated samples was measured using nanoindentation with a diamond Berkovich indenter (Asmec-UNAT-M, Dresden, Germany) under a 10 mN load force with 5 s of dwell time. The study conducted an average of five tests (n = 5) per sample. Subsequently, the DPN 5000 dip-pen nanolithography equipped with an atomic force microscope system (NanoInk Inc., Skokie, IL, USA) was utilized to scan the nano-indented topography with a silicon probe under a scanning rate of 1 Hz and 512 scans in the tapping mode. In addition, the Surfcorder ET4000 roughness measurement instrument with a natural diamond-made scanning tip (R = 0.5 μm) (Kosaka Laboratory Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was applied to analyze the surface roughness (represented by root mean square, n = 5) at a scanning speed of 1 mm/s, load of 50 μN, and temperature of 25 °C. For the surface wettability evaluation, the model FTA-125 contact angle goniometer equipped with an optical subsystem (First Ten Angstroms Inc., Newark, CA, USA) was adopted to measure the droplet profile contact angle (deionized water drops of ~5 μl in volume, n = 5) on the investigated specimens.

Implantation procedures

All protocols involving animals followed the guidelines of the advisory committee for the laboratory animal center of Taipei Medical University (project identification code: LAC-2013-0180). This study was conducted on twelve male New Zealand white rabbits with an average weight of 3.5 ± 0.5 kg from the Livestock Research Institute located in Tainan City, Taiwan. Before the experiment, all rabbits were kept under maintained environmental conditions at 22 ± 2 °C, 75 ± 5% for relative humidity, with the controlled lighting cycle for each 12 h (dark and bright mode). The animals were randomly separated into two groups (six in each) for implantation evaluation at short-time observation (4 weeks) and long-time observation (12 weeks) (Fig. 2). General anesthesia was injected into the rabbits during the implantation process, which was initiated by ketamine hydrochloride with specific doses is 10 mg/kg ketamin 10% (CP-Pharma Partner, Burgdorf, Germany) and 0.125 mg/kg medetomidine (Pfizer Pharma, Berlin, Germany) then control by isoflurane with a concentrate of 2.5–3.5 vol.% oxygen, 0.5–1.0 L/min (Isoba, Essex Pharma, Munich, Germany). Following disinfection surgical used povidone iodine, then local anesthesia was injected with 2% lidocaine (1:100,000 epinephrine). The incision procedure started with a longitudinal method to dissect and expose the left and right femurs. Subsequently, the control and MNPs implants were randomly inserted into the bone defect area at a torque of 35 N-cm manually through a precise adjustable implant torque wrench (Hung Chun BIO-S Co., Ltd., Kaohsiung, Taiwan). Dressing the wound at the end of the procedure and continuing medication therapies, such as 40 mg/kg cephalotin administered 30 min post-operation through the intramuscular method, were provided. Finally, the rabbits were sacrificed at weeks 4 and 12 post-implantation.Fig. 2The evaluation of the investigated implants in the rabbit femoral: a anasthetized model, b bone defect area, c implants placed into the bone defect area, and d suturing post-implantation

Bone tissue regeneration measurement

The investigated specimens, including bone defect sites, were analyzed using high-resolution X-ray micro-computed tomography (μ-CT) images. The samples were scanned using Skyscan 2211 (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA) at 12-μm voxel resolution with a 360° scan. The voltage was 100 kV, and the current was 95 µA at 2.3 W micro-focus output with a 0.5 mm Ti filter. The three-dimensional images were performed using the reconstruction software GPUNRecon in the μ-CT system. Hereafter, CTAn software was applied to analyze the region of interest for on-growth bone, defined as 300 µm surrounding the investigated implants. The osteogenesis indices, including bone-to-implant contact (BIC), trabecular number (Tb.N), and bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV) ratio, were quantitatively analyzed for the peripheral bone tissue.

Histological examination

For histological assessment, euthanized rabbits were administered 100 mg/kg pentothal sodium via intravenous injection for a period of observation at 4 and 12 weeks post-implantation. The implant specimens were removed, and the femur was dissected free of any soft tissue. Continue to rinse the specimens in distilled water and immediately fix with 10% formalin for 2 weeks. The fixed specimens were decalcified with Plank Rychlo decalcifier (Muto Pure Chemicals Co., LTD, Tokyo, Japan) for 2 weeks then washed into distilled water, dehydrated, embedded, polished, stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), and analyzed using a digital scanner (Aperio CS model, Leica Biosystems, Bualo Grove, IL, USA) under different magnifications. In the analysis of each histological section, a crucial index of enhanced bone regeneration ability was also measured through new bone formation thickness for quantitative analysis. The thickness of new bone formation is defined as the length of new bone tissue extending outward from the bone defect area.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The one-way analysis of variance was applied to measure the significant differences between the two groups. Confidence grade was accepted at *p < 0.05 as the final findings for the statistical.

The observations over 12 weeks showed that the HPOS implant groups induced substantial bone regeneration compared to the control groups, as shown in Fig. 5a. Moreover, the findings supported by μ-CT analysis were used to evaluate bone regeneration. Generally, both groups exhibited new bone growth into the machined-implant area, with spongy bone detected as an initial regeneration stage. In the early 4 weeks post-implantation (Fig. 5b), the μ-CT images indicated that the HPOS implant groups promoted rapid bone growth. Bone regeneration responses

Osseointegration was quantified through histomorphometry after 4 and 12 weeks, as shown in Fig. 6. Overall, all investigated implants appeared well anchored into the bone-implant contact surfaces. The HPOS implant group seemed more tightly anchored than the control group, owing to their larger surface area. This difference was influenced by anodization treatment, which induced the formation of HMNPs on the implant surface. Notably, after 12 weeks, significant differences in the BIC ratio were observed between the control and HPOS groups (*p < 0.05) (Fig. 6a). Further analysis of trabecular bone number (Fig. 6b) demonstrated that the HPOS implant group outperformed the control in the long term, with a 51% increase in TB.N. Osseointegration characteristics

Evaluation of new bone formation through H&E staining over 4 weeks post-implantation (Fig. 7) revealed that bone healing occurred around the intervention site, with osteoclasts present at the implant interfaces (Fig. 7b, d). This represents the early stage of bone regeneration for each group. The HPOS implant groups (Fig. 7c, d) also showed initial osteoblast activity within 4 weeks. After 12 weeks (Fig. 8), the intervention site was fully healed, with newly formed secondary bone tissue and mature bone containing osteocytes in lacunae and osteoblasts on the trabecular surfaces. The tissue specimens displayed an indented surface surrounding the machined-implant interface, suggesting bone tissue proliferation into the HPOS, which indicates efficient osseointegration (Fig. 8c, d). Additionally, in both the control and HPOS groups, trabecular and cortical bone formed within 12 weeks (Fig. 9). Notably, new bone tissue extended more significantly from the bone defect area in the HPOS implant group (0.66 ± 0.13 mm) compared to the control group (0.45 ± 0.11 mm), with a statistical difference of *p < 0.05 (Fig. 9a). This feature suggests ongoing bone hyperplasia. Resting osteoblasts in the control group were smaller, flattened, and embedded in the calcified bone matrix at the edge of the implant surface (Fig. 9b). Discussion

This study used a rabbit model to evaluate a biomedical 316L SS implant with optimal anodization, which generated high levels of HMNPs that promoted surface-induced osseointegration. It is well-known that micro-pores facilitate osteoblast adhesion, while nanostructures enhance osteoblast proliferation and differentiation. Notably, within 300 nm nanoscales, significant promotion of osteoblast differentiation was observed on the dual MNPs structures, suggesting a synergistic effect due to mimicking the layered complexity of bone [23, 24]. Among these, micro-nanoscale structures with 300 nm pores showed the most pronounced biological effects, providing optimal inter-structure space and likely a rough surface area more efficient for osteoblast attachment [25, 26]. Moreover, smaller pore sizes with lower roughness in hierarchical HMNPs structures benefited faster cell propagation. The elastic modulus of the surface also differed significantly from the control, with HPOS exhibiting a lower modulus that could disperse stress concentrations, reducing stress-shielding effects [27]. Stress shielding can lead to bone tissue loss around the implant and poor osseointegration [28–30]. Therefore, the HPOS implant is believed to reduce stress shielding compared to the control. Additionally, the HPOS implant demonstrated excellent hydrophilicity, with a contact angle of 15.6 ± 1.8°, attributable to its well-aligned HMNPs structure, creating a larger surface free energy. This behavior can be attributed to the surface with a well-aligned HMNPs structure generating a larger surface free energy formation energy [31–34]. Surface free energy correlates with wettability, so increased surface area enhances hydrophilicity [31, 35]. Improved wettability impacts biological fluid, protein, and cell absorption on the implant surface, influencing cell morphology, adhesion, and proliferation [36–38].

The primary three-dimensional μ-CT results showed that the HPOS group had BIC, TB.N, BV/TV, and new bone formation thickness values of 46%, 0.6 mm, 51%, and 0.66 mm at 12 weeks, respectively. These results surpass those of the unmodified control group under the same conditions, indicating that anodized treatment to modify HMNPs structures plays a critical role in osseointegration [4, 25, 39]. The in vivo study further demonstrated that HMNPs structures facilitate superior osteogenesis, with better BIC and osteocyte accumulation than the unmodified surface. Moreover, the rabbit femoral implant increased BIC and bone volume around the HPOS by nearly 25 and 30%, respectively, compared to the control, by inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and thus activating implant-bone integration in machined models [39, 40].

Beyond histological findings, the bone area around the implant at 4 and 12 weeks post-implantation showed a second stage of bone regeneration, with high osteocyte density on the HPOS surface, which is critical for promoting rapid integration [41, 42]. Osteocytes act as mechanical sensors, regulating bone formation through osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and mineralization via dendrites, adapting to support loads long after initial osseointegration and maintaining the implant interface. They also sense mechanical loading through interactions with HMNPs structures, supporting ongoing stability [42, 43]. Notably, more osteocytes were present around the HPOS implant after 12 weeks, indicating a higher level of osseointegration. As indicated by previous studies, the anodized 316L SS surface produces various HMNPs structures that influence new bone formation activities. Moving forward, combining dual surface structure approaches and conducting comprehensive physical, chemical, and biological testing are of great practical and scientific importance. Such efforts could improve implant functions, induce greater bone formation, and prevent adverse reactions at the implant site [11, 14, 25]. Hence, fabricating HPOS with HMNPs structures is a promising strategy for enhancing bone-implant interactions during early-stage implantation. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to fully evaluate the effects of HPOS with HMNPs to closely mimic clinical conditions.

Conclusions

The fabrication of an innovative HPOS on the biomedical 316L SS implant using an optimal anodization process significantly enhanced early-stage osseointegration. The HPOS implant, which contains HMNPs, exhibited a relatively low elastic modulus, high roughness, and a low contact angle compared to the control sample. Bone regeneration in the rabbit femur model increased rapidly with the HMNPs structure compared to implants without surface modification. Therefore, these results suggest that developing an innovative HPOS can boost osteoinduction and provide a biomimetic approach for the hybrid porous hierarchical model, enabling further research into biomaterials with HMNPs characteristics.
