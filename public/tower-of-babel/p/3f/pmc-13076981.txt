Ultrasound Characteristics of Budd-Chiari Syndrome: A Multicenter Analysis
Authors: Jimmy Daza, Isabella Wiest, Xin Wu Cui, Bo Zhang, Yunhao Chen, Yi Dong, Chiara De Molo, Carla Serra, Nitin Chaubal, Ehsan Safai Zadeh, Christian Görg, Theresa Tscheu, Matthias P. Ebert, Andreas Teufel, Christoph F. Dietrich

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBudd-Chiari syndrome (BCS) is a rare liver disease defined as an obstruction of the hepatic venous outflow, potentially leading to severe complications like liver failure. BCS can be classified as primary (due to endoluminal venous lesions such as thrombosis) or secondary (due to external compression or invasion of the hepatic veins or inferior vena cava; IVC). While numerous case reports illustrate imaging findings in individual patients, systematic data processing across multiple regions is lacking and establishing consistent ultrasound diagnostic criteria could significantly improve early detection and patient outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study (2010–2020) included 118 patients from China (n = 59), Germany (n = 26), India (n = 18), and Italy (n = 15). The study used standardized ultrasound protocols, including Doppler and contrast-enhanced ultrasound, to assess hepatic venous outflow and other key findings, such as the presence of hepatic-vein thrombosis, caudate lobe hypertrophy, and collateral circulation.ResultsThe cohort comprised 118 patients (65 men, 53 women; mean age 46 years). Most cases had a chronic disease course (74%), and primary BCS (n = 96, 81%), related to thrombotic alterations, predominated over secondary BCS, related to compressive masses (n = 7, 6%). Key sonographic features observed included portal vein thrombosis (14%), hepatic-vein thrombosis (70%), caudate lobe enlargement (30%), and collateral venous circulation (83%). Among the geographical differences in symptoms, thrombosis of the IVC was common in Chinese patients (33%), while the confluence area and hepatic veins were more frequently involved in European patients.ConclusionsThis study provides the first multicenter analysis of ultrasound characteristics in BCS patients from multiple regions. The findings support ultrasound as an essential tool in the standardized diagnostic process for BCS, particularly for identifying key sonographic features such as hepatic-vein thrombosis and caudate lobe enlargement. These results underscore the importance of standardized ultrasound protocols in BCS to facilitate early diagnosis and improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

Budd-Chiari syndrome (BCS) is a rare liver disease. It is defined as partial or complete obstruction of the hepatic venous outflow (Fig. 1), due to varied mechanisms of blockage, excluding those due to heart disease, pericardial disease, sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, or veno-occlusive disease [1–4]. BCS usually involves the occlusion of the hepatic veins or the inferior vena cava (IVC) and can be differentiated by cause, location, type of vascular obstruction, and clinical manifestations [1, 3, 5]. BCS can be classified as primary, resulting from intrinsic venous thrombosis or endoluminal lesions of the hepatic veins or IVC, such as webs (Fig. 2), or secondary, caused by external compression or invasion by other masses. The distinction between primary and secondary BCS is crucial for diagnosis, as treatment approaches vary depending on the etiology.

Etiologies of primary BCS are often thrombotic in nature and include existing risk factors for hypercoagulable states that contribute to the occlusion of the venous outflow. Among them, myeloproliferative disorders, particularly essential thrombocythemia, and polycythemia Vera are the most frequent, but protein C and S deficiencies and antiphospholipid syndrome must also be considered [6]. Secondary BCS is less common and typically arises from external compression, such as space-occupying tumors, either benign or malignant, which can contribute to its development [7].

Clinically, patients with BCS present a varied range of clinical courses. While acute BCS can be suspected with gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., abdominal pain, ascites, occasional gastrointestinal bleeding) or liver-specific symptoms (e.g., jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy, or, in fulminant cases, acute liver failure), the chronic disease is usually asymptomatic [6]. Thus, diagnosis is usually performed with any of the three conventional imaging modalities (i.e., ultrasound (US, Fig. 3), computed tomography [CT], and magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) [8–10].

Case of a patient with an acute presentation of BCS. Contrast-enhanced US showed occluded hepatic veins, intrahepatic collaterals and dilated portal vein and hepatic artery.

Among them, US has several advantages when evaluating BCS, as it is widely available and less costly than the alternatives [11], being its main limitation an operator-dependent image quality [12]. However, US allows visualization and assessment of structures involved for a BCS diagnosis (i.e., the size and surface of the liver, the echo structure of the liver parenchyma, and blood vessels). Color Doppler US is the image of choice due to its essential information regarding the vessels (i.e., the patency of the hepatic veins and IVC, as well as the direction of blood flow and its velocity), and spectral Doppler US allows the quantification of blood flow in all the three hepatic vessels [5, 11, 13]. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) has been shown to improve the detection and characterization of liver neoplasia [14, 15] and can be used to characterize vascular alterations typical of BCS. However, in BCS altered perfusion patterns can limit the specificity of CEUS for diagnosing focal lesions, requiring biopsies when atypical enhancement (hyperplasia vs. tumors) is detected [16, 17].

Consequently, US, together with magnetic resonance imaging, has become the first-line imaging modality for BCS [18–20]. Still, while numerous case reports illustrate imaging findings in individual patients, and US, CT, and MRI show high diagnostic accuracy in different meta-analyses [13, 21–24], no multicentric studies with a significant number of patients with US diagnosis of BCS exists [25]. Therefore, this study aimed to identify clinical and US criteria for BCS diagnosis by comparing the baseline clinical characteristics, etiologic aspects, therapeutic approaches, and imaging features in a large multicenter cohort including 118 patients from China, India, Italy, and Germany.

Methods

Data Collection

This was a retrospective, multicenter cohort study conducted from 2010 to 2020 in four different countries across the world. Patients were enrolled from six tertiary care centers: Bologna (Italy), Mumbai (India), Frankfurt and Marburg (Germany), and Shanghai and Wuhan (China). All patients included had a diagnosis of BCS based on clinical criteria and imaging evidence of hepatic venous outflow obstruction. Trained physicians in each center performed the US evaluation following a common protocol (in terms of required views and use of Doppler/CEUS); however, because data were collected retrospectively, some measures (e.g., organ size descriptors) were recorded qualitatively by local examiners rather than quantitatively, leading to variability. Patients’ information was pseudonymized, and demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, date of birth, examination date) were assessed. The inclusion criteria were patients treated in the institutions with a confirmed diagnosis of BCS based on clinical and imaging findings, including US evidence of hepatic venous outflow obstruction due to thrombus, stenosis (primary BCS), or neoplasia (secondary BCS). Exclusion criteria included other causes of hepatic venous obstruction, such as heart disease or veno-occlusive disease.

Data Analysis

Because this was a retrospective study, not all variables were available for all patients; missing data were not imputed, and percentages are reported out of the total number of patients with data for each variable (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000549007). For nominally scaled data, frequencies were calculated. Means with standard deviations were calculated for rationally scaled characteristics. To compare correlations between variables of different nominally scaled variables, χ2 test was performed. A t test was performed to determine differences between groups of rationally scaled variables. For both tests, a significance level of 0.05 was defined. All statistical tests were performed with Excel (Microsoft).

Results

Patient Characteristics and Symptoms

A total of 118 patients with confirmed BCS were included: Germany (n = 26), Italy (n = 15), India (n = 18), and China (n = 59). The cohort was 55% male (65/118) and 45% female (53/118), with a mean age of 46 years (SD ± 11.5). Most of the patients were between their third and fifth decade of life. The youngest patient was 17, and the oldest 73 years old. Mean ages by gender and country are detailed in Table 1.

Cirrhosis was reported in 53 (45%) patients, mainly in chronic BCS. Most patients presented with nonspecific symptoms. Fatigue or general malaise were the most commonly reported, affecting 72% (n = 85) of the cohort. Abdominal pain was reported in 8% (n = 9) of cases, while ascites was present in 6% (n = 7). In 3 cases, ascites was the only symptom observed, and only 3 patients presented with two or more symptoms from the classical BCS triad (ascites, abdominal pain, and jaundice). Finally, a small subset of patients (4%, n = 5) were completely asymptomatic, highlighting the variability in clinical presentation across the cohort.

After US evaluation, ascites was detected in 46 (39%) patients, of which 23 patients required specific treatment. Etiology and Classification of BCS

Primary BCS (n = 100, 85%) due to prothrombotic causes such as coagulation disorders (i.e., essential thrombocythemia, polycythemia vera, and JAK2 mutations-related myeloproliferative disorders) and anatomical abnormalities (i.e., presence of IVC membranes, hypoplasia, and stenosis of the IVC), were the majority. Secondary BCS due to external mass-effect compression (n = 7, 5%), included hepatocellular carcinoma (n = 4), colorectal cancer (n = 1), primary liver sarcoma (n = 1), and renal cell carcinoma (n = 1). Finally, the etiology of 11 (10%) cases could not be determined.

BCS was classified as chronic in 87 (74%) patients, acute in 18 (15%), and acute-on-chronic in 3 (3%), with 10 patients unclassified due to insufficient clinical records. For more details, see Figure 5.

Specialized coagulation disorder testing was conducted in 51 of the 118 patients, revealing various abnormalities. A total of 28 patients had prothrombotic factors, such as elevated Factor VIII levels (n = 6), JAK2 mutations (n = 4), among others. In cases where testing was available, some patients showed elevated D-Dimer levels (n = 5), prolonged partial thromboplastin time (PTT) (n = 8), or prolonged thrombin time (n = 3). Idiopathic prothrombotic abnormalities, classified as having no identifiable cause despite workup, were present in 23 patients. Notably, specific coagulation testing data were lacking for patients from India and China, though some Chinese patients were reported as having abnormal coagulation parameters without further clarification.

BCS Characteristics

Key B-Mode and Doppler Findings

Thrombus formation was visible on B-mode US in 110 out of 118 (93%) patients, with hepatic-vein thrombosis present in 83 (70%) patients. The right hepatic vein was affected in 37 (34%) patients, the left hepatic vein in 33 (30%) patients, and the middle hepatic vein in 28 (25%) patients. The IVC was compromised in 50 (45%) patients. Combined thrombosis of multiple hepatic veins and/or the IVC was less frequent, but notable: Triple hepatic-vein thrombosis occurred in only 14 (12%) patients. Therefore, obstruction of all three hepatic veins is not a requirement for BCS diagnosis, and 3 patients showed combined thrombosis involving the IVC and two hepatic veins, as seen in Figure 6. Notably, 12 German patients (60%) and 9 Indian patients (50%) exhibited thrombosis in the confluence region, which was less common in the Italian cohort (1 case) and absent in China. Complete vessel occlusion by thrombus was reported in 48 (41%) patients, while partial occlusion was seen in 53 (45%) cases. Data on the extent of thrombosis were unavailable for 17 patients.

Although CEUS was performed in some patients, our retrospective data did not systematically capture CEUS-specific findings. Our lack of detailed CEUS results is a limitation, reflecting that CEUS was used qualitatively but not quantified across centers.

Collateral Circulation

Collateral venous circulation, a compensatory mechanism in chronic BCS, was observed in 98 (83%) patients. Collaterals were detected intrahepatically in 46% of cases (n = 54), extrahepatically in 19% (n = 20), and both intra- and extrahepatically in 27% (n = 32). Intrahepatic collaterals were predominant in the Chinese cohort. Collaterals were suspected but not confirmed in 8 patients of the Indian center. The absence of collaterals was documented in 20 cases, and in 7 instances, collaterals were not reported or discarded during the US evaluation.

Liver Parenchyma and Caudate Lobe

A heterogeneous liver parenchyma was identified in 110 of 118 (93%) patients. In 55 (47%) patients, the liver parenchyma appeared hyperechogenic, while an isoechogenic pattern was noted in 55 (47%) patients. Caudate lobe hypertrophy, a typical feature of BCS due to the lobe’s preserved drainage into the IVC, was documented in 35 (30%) patients, as seen in Figure 7. This feature was more common in patients with chronic BCS, particularly in advanced disease stages, where compensatory hypertrophy of the caudate lobe occurs due to venous obstruction.

Liver Size

Data regarding the liver size was provided for all 118 patients. In comparison to a normal liver at US examination, with a longitudinal diameter of the right liver at the midclavicular line inferior to 14 cm, almost half of the patients showed an enlarged liver (48%, n = 56), including most patients from Europe (95%, n = 39). Asian centers reported liver size as “small,” “medium,” or “large” without measurement: 46 presented a “medium” (or normally sized) liver, while 17 patients were classified as “large” (hepatomegaly) and 14 patients were classified as having a “small” liver.

Ascites and Splenomegaly

Ascites was present in 46 (39%) patients, with 23 of these cases classified as severe, requiring therapeutic intervention. Ascites was more frequently observed in patients with chronic BCS, especially those in the European cohort. Splenomegaly, which often accompanies portal hypertension, was found in 50% (59 patients) of the total cohort. Six (5%) patients exhibited splenic infarction, an indicator of advanced portal hypertension and venous obstruction. Splenomegaly was more frequent in patients with extensive IVC involvement and long-standing disease.

Portal Venous Flow and Perihepatic Lymphadenopathy

Portal venous flow abnormalities were observed in several patients. PVT was diagnosed in 16 (14%) patients, most of whom were in the chronic phase of BCS. PVT further exacerbated portal hypertension, contributing to complications such as ascites and splenomegaly. Perihepatic lymphadenopathy was noted in 4 (3%) patients, all of whom had advanced-stage disease, reflecting chronic liver congestion, and inflammation.

Therapy

Anticoagulation initiated in 38 (32%) patients, of which 31 received heparin, and 7 were treated with warfarin. Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPSS) was performed in 5 patients. Balloon angioplasty was conducted in 12 patients targeting the IVC and in 4 patients targeting the hepatic veins. Additionally, 6 patients received IVC stent implantation to restore patency and relieve venous obstruction. Liver transplantation (orthotopic liver transplantation) was carried out in 4 patients, and 1 patient underwent both TIPSS and liver transplantation. Angioplasty was performed alone in 1 patient. Supportive therapies were employed in patients with severe or refractory ascites. A total of 23 patients required repeated paracentesis for ascites management, and therapy data were not reported for 43 patients.

Discussion

BCS is a rare disorder characterized by hepatic venous outflow obstruction that must be considered in cases of sudden onset of symptoms in patients with predisposing procoagulant factors, and for which epidemiological data is limited. This multinational, multicenter study is the first to directly compare BCS across diverse regions, providing a unique window into how presentation, US findings, and etiologies may vary by geography. By encompassing a diverse patient population, our study offers a unique opportunity to explore the potential variations in disease presentation, etiology, and management strategies within different geographic and demographic contexts. Our findings confirm several key diagnostic features of BCS on US, highlight regional etiological and anatomic patterns, and underscore the need for standardized US criteria. However, observed differences should be interpreted cautiously, and further large-scale, prospective studies are needed to better understand these variations.

Clinical Presentation and Epidemiology

Across all cohorts, BCS rarely manifested with the classical acute triad (in fact, only ∼3% of patients presented with the combination of abdominal pain, ascites, and hepatomegaly). Instead, most patients had vague or subacute symptoms, reinforcing the need for a high index of suspicion and prompt imaging evaluation for diagnosis. Our patient demographics were comparable worldwide, with a mean age in the mid-40s, and slight male predominance overall. Notably, the Italian cohort differed, showing a female majority and a higher proportion of acute-on-chronic presentations. This aligns with prior observations that Western BCS patients are often female with acute symptomatic disease, whereas Eastern patients tend to be male and have more indolent, chronic disease courses [26, 27].

Despite these gender and presentation differences, the age of onset was similar across regions, reflecting BCS’s tendency to occur in young to middle adulthood worldwide – between 30s and 50s – although older age at diagnosis has been linked to worse outcomes [28]. The rarity of BCS globally and variability in national reporting make precise epidemiological comparisons difficult. For instance, one meta-analysis observed that Asia reports a lower annual incidence of BCS than Europe but a higher overall prevalence [29] – a paradox likely arising from differences in case detection and survival. Our multicenter data nevertheless suggest relatively comparable clinical profiles, with any regional variations requiring cautious interpretation.

Regional Etiological Patterns

The vast majority of cases in all cohorts (≈85%) were primary BCS caused by thrombosis of the hepatic veins and/or IVC, usually in the context of an identifiable prothrombotic condition. Only a small fraction (∼5%) was secondary BCS due to external tumor compression, confirming that malignancy-related BCS is uncommon but clinically important. Underlying hypercoagulable disorders were frequently documented – especially in European patients, where JAK2-positive MPNs and antiphospholipid syndrome were common findings. For example, our Italian center reported JAK2-mutated MPN in about 30% of cases, in line with prior studies where roughly one-third of BCS patients carry JAK2 mutations [30–32]. Overall, our findings support the literature noting that ∼80% of BCS patients have an underlying thrombophilic disorder, with about half involving myeloproliferative syndromes [6, 33].

We also found that nearly half of patients had established cirrhosis by the time of diagnosis, almost exclusively among those with chronic BCS. This likely resulted from prolonged hepatic congestion rather than being a cause of BCS, but it complicates interpretation of coagulation profiles: for example, a low protein C level could reflect an inherited deficiency or simply reduced synthetic function in cirrhosis [34]. Such interplay between liver dysfunction and thrombophilia must be kept in mind when evaluating BCS etiologies.

In contrast to the West, some Asian series have described higher rates of idiopathic or anatomically driven BCS. Consistent with that, we observed that IVC involvement dominated in the Asian cohorts, whereas hepatic-vein thrombosis predominated in the Western cohorts. Specifically, 42% of Chinese patients had thrombosis extending into the IVC, compared to much lower IVC involvement in Germany and Italy, where isolated hepatic-vein occlusions were more common. Indian patients showed an intermediate pattern, with thrombi often located at the hepato venous confluence.

This geographic divergence in clot location aligns with reports that IVC obstruction (sometimes due to membranous webs) is more frequent in Asia [1, 6]. Indeed, we documented one case of an IVC web in our Indian cohort, and suspect that undetected webs could underlie some of the Chinese cases with predominant IVC thrombosis, as such membranous IVC webs have been reported in up to 80% of BCS patients in certain Asian and South African populations [35].

Meanwhile, European patients more often had the classical pattern of hepatic-vein thromboses associated with myeloproliferative or autoimmune conditions (e.g., antiphospholipid syndrome). These patterns hint at distinct pathophysiological profiles – for example, chronic IVC disease in Asia versus acute hepatic-vein clots in the West – though further study is needed to confirm genetic or environmental predispositions. Notably, PVT was observed in 16 of our patients (mostly those with chronic BCS), reflecting advanced portal hypertension and a hypercoagulable milieu; its presence signals a more extensive thrombotic process and may portend additional complications [36]. Some experts have even proposed that cases with primary IVC obliteration represent a distinct entity (“obliterative hepato cavopathy”) rather than classic BCS [1], underscoring how markedly the disease spectrum can differ by thrombus location.

US Diagnostic Features

This study reaffirms that Doppler US is an indispensable first-line tool for BCS diagnosis across varied settings. Direct visualization of a venous thrombus on US is widely recognized as a cornerstone of BCS diagnosis [37, 38], and in our series, it was achieved in 93% of cases (110/118). This underscores Doppler’s high sensitivity for detecting hepatic outflow obstruction. Crucially, US identifies the characteristic site of occlusion in each region – whether in the hepatic veins or the IVC – illustrating that BCS-specific venous obstruction can be reliably visualized in real time across different populations.

It is relevant to note that the appearance of the thrombi evolves with disease chronicity: in acute BCS, clots appeared as hypoechoic, heterogeneous material within the vessel lumen, whereas chronic thromboses became more organized and highly echogenic, sometimes contracting and blending with surrounding liver parenchyma. This progression can make chronic occlusions harder to distinguish on gray-scale imaging [13, 39], reinforcing the value of Doppler flow assessment (and potentially CEUS) in long-standing cases.

In addition to the direct clot, several indirect US signs emerged as valuable indicators of BCS. The formation of intrahepatic collateral veins and enlargement of the caudate lobe were especially prominent features in our data. Collateral circulation – often seen as tortuous, comma-shaped vascular channels on Doppler – was present in the majority of patients, reflecting the liver’s attempt to decompress obstructed veins. Importantly, detection of venovenous collaterals had approximately 90% sensitivity in our cohort, making it a practical “rule-in” sign for chronic BCS, consistent with prior reports of ∼90% collateral prevalence [40, 41].

Likewise, caudate lobe hypertrophy, a classic hallmark of BCS, was frequently observed in patients with acute or subacute presentations. All Italian cases, for example, showed a prominent caudate lobe, echoing Western series where caudate enlargement occurs in roughly 70–80% of BCS cases [42, 43]. Investigators have found that the combination of caudate hypertrophy with absent or thrombosed hepatic veins is one of the most reliable diagnostic criteria for BCS [43]. This hypertrophy results from the caudate lobe’s unique venous drainage directly into the IVC, allowing it to enlarge when other hepatic veins are occluded. However, caudate changes were reported far less often in the chronic Chinese cases (where ∼90% were noted as “not prominent”), possibly because long-standing collateral pathways had alleviated congestion or simply due to inconsistent sonographic criteria. This discrepancy underlines the need for standardized definitions (e.g., what constitutes a “prominent” caudate lobe) to ensure such findings are comparable across centers.

Other indirect signs of portal hypertension were also common [11]: for instance, ascites was present in about 39% of patients overall – often massive enough to require therapeutic paracentesis – and splenomegaly (due to congestive portal hypertension and splenic sequestration of blood cells) was noted in nearly two-thirds of those assessed [44]. As expected, these signs correlated with disease acuity: chronic BCS tends to have an insidious or asymptomatic course [45], and indeed nearly all patients without ascites in our study were those with a chronic, well-compensated form, including every case in the Chinese cohort. In contrast, large-volume ascites was typically seen in acute or acute-on-chronic presentations.

The relative absence of ascites in long-standing BCS reflects the gradual development of extensive collaterals, which help alleviate portal pressure and prevent fluid accumulation. We also observed that acute BCS cases often had enlarged, congested livers with heterogeneous echotexture due to hemorrhagic infarctions – changes much less evident in slower onset disease [46]. Notably, all patients in this study underwent cross-sectional imaging (CT and/or MRI) as well, which aided in confirming thrombus extent, visualizing collateral pathways or regenerative nodules, and guiding interventional planning. However, these modalities primarily played a complementary role. US – especially with Doppler and contrast enhancement – remained the cornerstone of diagnosis in our cohorts, given its real-time capability, lack of invasiveness, and greater accessibility in varied clinical settings.

Implications for Management and Research

The regional differences in BCS pathology have practical implications for treatment strategies. In Western practice, hepatic-vein thrombosis is often managed with anticoagulation and, if needed, interventions like TIPSS or ultimately the liver transplantation [47, 48]. In contrast, the predominance of IVC lesions in Asia means that angioplasty and stenting of the IVC can be highly effective first-line therapies. Indeed, 19 of the Chinese patients in our series underwent IVC balloon angioplasty, whereas none of the German or Italian patients did. This disparity reflects how anatomy influences therapy: one large series reported a 95% success rate for angioplasty in Budd-Chiari cases with IVC webs [49] – a scenario more common in Asian patients.

Regardless of region, management should also include addressing the underlying prothrombotic condition (e.g., cytoreductive therapy for MPNs or immunosuppression for antiphospholipid syndrome) and careful use of anticoagulation (balanced against bleeding risks from portal hypertension). Current guidelines advocate a stepwise treatment approach, prioritizing minimally invasive interventions when possible [47, 48]. For example, the feasibility of angioplasty or shunt placement is evaluated first; if those are not possible or effective, medical management is optimized, and only then is TIPS or transplantation considered as a last resort.

From a research perspective, our experience highlights both the promise and challenges of studying this rare disease on an international scale. As a strength, this is the first study to compile US-based diagnostic data on BCS across multiple continents, providing an unprecedented comparative view that reveals both universal findings and intriguing regional patterns. Such an international perspective is especially valuable for a rare disease in which single-center experience is inherently limited.

However, the retrospective nature of our data and heterogeneity in US reporting between centers (e.g., differing terminologies and non-standardized grading of findings) limit the direct comparability of some results. We have attempted to mitigate this by including an online supplementary Table to quantify data completeness, and we strongly recommend adoption of standardized sonographic descriptors and protocols in future studies. Despite these limitations, our collaborative dataset offers novel insights into the frequency of useful US signs in different populations, which may help streamline the diagnostic workup of BCS globally. Future prospective multicenter studies, using uniform imaging criteria and larger patient numbers, are warranted to validate the regional differences observed and to further refine diagnostic algorithms. Ultimately, a globally collaborative approach – with shared data and harmonized protocols – will be key to improving outcomes in this rare but serious condition. In conclusion, our multinational study demonstrates that US reliably visualizes the venous outflow obstruction characteristic of BCS across diverse populations.

We confirm that Asian patients more often exhibit IVC involvement while European patients tend to have isolated hepatic-vein thromboses. Despite such differences, classic indirect features like collateral veins and caudate lobe hypertrophy remain highly useful “rule-in” signs of BCS everywhere. Practically, this means that clinicians should tailor their diagnostic focus (for instance, scrutinizing the IVC for webs in Asian patients versus carefully evaluating all hepatic veins in European cases) while still applying uniform criteria. These findings underscore the importance of tailored yet standardized diagnostic approaches and pave the way for better global understanding and management of this challenging syndrome.

Imaging Comparisons and Modalities

Although CT and MRI were used in all patients, US provided the primary diagnostic tool, with direct and indirect findings in >90% of cases. CT/MRI helped confirm thrombosis and map collaterals, but these are often less available in low-resource settings. Therefore, US remains the first-line modality, particularly when combined with Doppler and CEUS, but a multimodal approach remains essential when nodules or equivocal findings are present.

Limitations

Our data were retrospectively collected from six different centers. Due to the nature of retrospective data collection, response categories were not defined beforehand, such as an uniform cutoff for caudate hypertrophy, leading to heterogeneity in the reported US data that must be considered when evaluating the inferences. Also, terminology may differ between centers. For example, since no precise measures were given for liver and caudate lobe size, it remains unclear how comparable the data is, as it was reported as “small,” “medium,” or “large,” and “prominent,” or “not-prominent,” examiners may have applied different examination techniques, which may lead to differences. We now include a online supplementary Table quantifying missingness and recommend adoption of standardized sonographic descriptors in future studies.

Conclusions

This multicenter study demonstrates that US – particularly Doppler US – is an effective and accessible first-line imaging modality for diagnosing BCS across diverse geographic regions. Characteristic findings such as hepatic vein or IVC thrombosis, collateral venous pathways, and caudate lobe hypertrophy were reliably visualized in the majority of patients. Importantly, we confirm that BCS does not require thrombosis of all three hepatic veins for diagnosis; partial venous obstruction, especially when accompanied by secondary signs (e.g., collateral circulation), is sufficient to warrant the diagnosis. Furthermore, the findings underscore regional differences in BCS presentation, such as a predominance of IVC thrombosis in Asia and hepatic-vein thrombosis in Europe, which may reflect variations in pathophysiology and diagnostic practices.

What is the clinical and geographic context?

As shown in both the literature and our study, there are some differences in the presentation and diagnosis of BCS between Eastern and Western populations. In the Chinese population, BCS is more commonly associated with thrombosis of the IVC, while in Western populations, it is more commonly associated with coagulation disorders such as polycythemia vera and essential thrombocythemia. Eastern populations also tend to have a higher proportion of chronic cases in male patients, while acute cases of BCS in females are more common in Western populations.Are the US signs of BCS present?

Are the US signs of BCS present?

Are the US signs of BCS present?

When evaluating a suspected case, it is important to consider the most indicative common signs of BCS in US: hepatic-vein obstruction, collaterals, and caudate hypertrophy. The first step is to evaluate the blood flow in the hepatic vein and the presence of intrahepatic veno-venous collaterals with Doppler US, as the sensitivity of these findings is very high for BCS diagnosis; then verify if there is an enlargement of the caudate lobe, as seen in 80–91% of cases [11]. Additionally, consider any indirect findings such as the presence of signs of portal hypertension (i.e., hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and ascites).

Our results strongly support the call for standardized US protocols and reporting criteria in the evaluation of BCS. This should include: (1) objective liver and caudate lobe measurements (e.g., caudate: right-lobe ratio), (2) systematic documentation of vessel patency and collateral circulation, and (3) complete etiological workups including prothrombotic and malignant causes.

By implementing a standardized, US-based diagnostic workflow, clinicians can achieve earlier diagnosis, initiate timely therapy (e.g., anticoagulation, angioplasty, TIPS), and reduce progression to irreversible liver damage. Moreover, training and systematic use of Doppler US – with attention to key vascular features and patient context – represents a powerful tool in correct diagnosis and management. This study offers a foundation for future prospective efforts to validate diagnostic criteria and optimize care pathways for this rare but serious vascular liver disorder.
