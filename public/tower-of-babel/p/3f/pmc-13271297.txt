CAR-iNKT cells for cancer therapy: a comprehensive review of engineering, mechanisms, and clinical progress
Authors: Ying Yang, Jiaqi Gu, Yujie Ding, Yuqin Li, Zhida Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-modified T cells, as a revolutionary cancer immunotherapy, have achieved remarkable success in hematologic malignancies. However, the therapy is hindered by severe toxicity, complex manufacturing, and suboptimal efficacy in solid tumors. Invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells combine features of T cells and NK cells, bridging innate and adaptive immunity. Beyond direct cytotoxic functions, iNKT cells exert indirect antitumor effects through interactions with other immune cells while also modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME). Their exceptional TME infiltration capacity offers hope for solid tumor treatment. Leveraging their inherent major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-independent cytotoxicity, iNKT cells offer a viable strategy for developing “off-the-shelf” CAR cell products. Consequently, CAR-iNKT cell therapies developed by introducing CAR technology into iNKT cells demonstrate extraordinary potential as a next generation tumor immunotherapy. Despite being established and garnering attention, CAR-iNKT cell therapy remains nascent with numerous obstacles. This review comprehensively discusses CAR-iNKT cell therapy, from the fundamental biology of iNKT cells to the latest advances in engineering and clinical translation, offering new perspectives for its research, development, and application. Introduction

The cancer situation remains severe and stands as a formidable global health challenge, resulting in millions of deaths worldwide every year [1,2]. Traditional interventions such as surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy have limited long-term efficacy. It is imperative to develop innovative therapeutic approaches to enhance treatment efficacy and reduce adverse effects. The past decade has witnessed remarkable advances in immunotherapy, such as adoptive cell therapy (ACT), immune checkpoint therapy, and cancer vaccines [3]. As research continues to advance, cancer immunotherapy is increasingly being applied in clinical settings, paving the way for a bright future. Among these, ACT represents a paradigm shift in oncology, harnessing the power of the immune system to deliver highly targeted and potent cancer therapies [4]. ACT utilizing chimeric antigen receptors (CAR) technology epitomizes one of the most effective approaches for genetically engineering immune cells to combat tumors. Notably, CAR-T cells have shown exceptional efficacy in clinical studies, particularly for hematological malignancies [5]. Up to now, six CAR-T cell-based drugs have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [6]. However, the widespread application of CAR-T cells still faces significant bottlenecks. Due to the highly immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment (TME), its efficacy against solid tumors remains limited [7,8]. Additionally, high production costs constrain its large-scale implementation [9,10]. More concerning is the safety of CAR-T cells, with life-threatening toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) having drawn massive attention [11,12].

Consequently, scientists are exploring alternative cell options, among which invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells are an attractive candidate [13,14]. iNKT cells represent a specific subset of T cells and bridge the innate and adaptive immune systems, which exhibit shared morphological and functional properties with T and natural killer (NK) cells [15]. In contrast to the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) restricted activation of T cells, iNKT cell activation is dependent upon the recognition of lipid and glycolipid antigens presented by CD1d molecules on the surface of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [16,17]. This distinct MHC-independent recognition mechanism enables iNKT cells to bypass traditional human leukocyte antigen (HLA) restriction, which constitutes a viable strategy for developing “off-the-shelf” allogeneic CAR cell products [18,19]. Furthermore, iNKT cells not only directly kill tumor cells but also exert indirect antitumor effects through interactions with various immune cells. Additionally, they possess immunomodulatory functions, capable of regulating the TME and exhibiting significant TME infiltration capabilities, which provides a great potential for the treatment of solid tumors [14,20]. In summary, the combination of the inherent advantages of iNKT cells with advanced gene-editing technologies designed to augment their effector functions provides a hopeful strategy to develop highly potent CAR-iNKT cell therapeutics [21].

In this review, we provide a comprehensive discussion of CAR-iNKT cell therapy, beginning with the foundational biology of iNKT cells and extending to recent advances in engineering and clinical translation. We have outlined the advantages of CAR-iNKT cells in tumor immunotherapy, summarized the latest preclinical and clinical research findings, and identified current challenges along with corresponding strategies for resolution. Ultimately, this work provides a comprehensive assessment of the transformative impact of CAR-iNKT cell therapy on the cancer treatment landscape.

iNKT cells

Characterization of iNKT cells

iNKT cells, a specialized subset of T lymphocytes, have the characteristics of both T cells and NK cells, and play a crucial role in coordinating innate and adaptive immune responses against cancer, infectious diseases, and inflammatory disorders. The core characteristic of iNKT cells is the expression of semi-invariant CD1d-restricted αβ T cell receptors (TCRs) [22]. In humans, the TCR is Vα24-Jα18 paired with Vβ11; in mice, it is Vα14-Jα18 paired with Vβ8/Vβ7/Vβ2 [23]. Unlike classical MHC proteins, CD1d is a family of monomorphic, β2-microglobulin-associated glycoproteins expressed on most professional APCs, and it specializes in presenting lipid and glycolipid antigens rather than peptides [24]. Similar to classical T cells, iNKT cells migrate to peripheral tissues such as the liver, bone marrow, spleen, lungs, and peripheral blood after maturing in the thymus, the distribution frequency and functions of iNKT cells vary across different tissues [25]. Based on the expression of CD4 and CD8 cell markers, human iNKT cells are primarily classified into three subpopulations: CD4+, CD8α+, and CD4− CD8− double-negative (DN) cells [26]. Although all subpopulations can exert fundamental effector functions by recognizing CD1d molecules, they exhibit distinct functional polarization characteristics [27]. For example, CD8α+ iNKT cells exhibit the strongest Th1 polarization and cytotoxic activity, they highly express NK-related receptors and demonstrate the most potent killing capacity against CD1d+ tumor cell lines. Concurrently, they efficiently co-activate conventional CD8+ T cells, serving as excellent initiators of cell-mediated immunity [27]. DN iNKT cells occupy an intermediate position, exhibiting both potent cytotoxicity and Th1 bias, and are often classified alongside the CD8α+ subset as highly effective antitumor effector populations [27,28]. In contrast, CD4+ iNKT cells predominantly mediate Th2 responses and display relatively weaker cytolytic activity. Given differences in cytokine production and cytotoxic activation among these subsets, their relative proportions may influence susceptibility or resistance to immune-mediated diseases [29,30]. This functional divergence holds significant pathological implications within the TME. Studies reveal a progressive increase in CD4+ iNKT cell proportions from peripheral blood to liver tissue and ultimately to tumor sites in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma and other malignancies [31]. Given this subset’s predominant Th2 response and low cytotoxic activity, its accumulation at tumor sites not only impairs effective tumor cell clearance but also potentially promotes tumor growth and recurrence through the secretion of Th2 cytokines that suppress the expansion of tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells. In contrast, CD8α+ and DN iNKT cells, with their potent cytotoxic activity and ability to induce Th1 immune responses, are considered excellent inducers of antitumor immunity. Based on these findings, future iNKT cell-based tumor immunotherapy strategies may focus on: selectively enriching or adoptively transferring CD8α+ and DN iNKT cell subsets, restoring the proportion of CD4− iNKT cells within the TME, and inducing all subsets to generate Th1 responses, thereby maximizing the antitumor potential of iNKT cells.

The classical activation pathway of iNKT cells is mediated through TCR recognition of α-galactosylceramide (α-GalCer), a glycerolipid presented by CD1d, this synthetic glycerolipid was originally isolated from the marine sponge Agelas mauritianus as a potent antitumor agent [32]. TCR also recognizes certain microbial lipid antigens and the self-antigen isoglobotrihexosylceramide(iGb3) [33,34]. In addition to TCR recognition, iNKT cells can also be activated by cytokines, such as IL-18, IL-12 and IL-33 [35–37]. Following activation, iNKT cells elicit antitumor responses through direct or indirect mechanisms.

The antitumor mechanism of iNKT cells

A growing body of evidence supports the increasing interest in iNKT cells as candidates for cancer immunotherapy, particularly in the field of CAR therapy. Functional investigations have deepened our insight into the underlying mechanisms by which iNKT cells influence tumor immunity, which shown in Figure 1. First, iNKT cells possess direct tumor lysis effect. This effect depends on direct cell-to-cell contact and primarily occurs in cells expressing surface markers recognized by iNKT cell surface receptors. iNKT cells exert their direct cytotoxic effects through either cytotoxic granule exocytosis or extrinsic apoptosis mediated by death receptors [38]. Perforin and granzyme are the primary cytotoxic granules, stored in the cytoplasm or released upon activation. Following the establishment of contact, the granules undergo polarization toward the immunological synapse. Through exocytosis, cytotoxic molecules including granzyme and perforin are secreted into the target cell. Subsequently, perforin mediates the formation of pores in the target membrane, which facilitates the delivery of granzyme and ultimately induces tumor cell lysis [39–41]. A lesser-known cytotoxic granule, granulysin, whose cytotoxic activity primarily relies on its ability to form pores in pathogen membranes. This capability alters cellular membrane permeability, thereby inducing cleavage [42,43]. In contrast, the death receptor pathway requires Fas ligand (FasL) expressed on iNKT cells to bind to Fas receptor (FasR) on target cells. This ligand-receptor interaction induces a conformational change, triggering the assembly of the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC), which activates executioner caspases and amplifies the death signals leading to apoptosis [44].

This elevated IFN-γ further activates DCs, creating a positive feedback loop. The IFN-γ and IL-12 produced by iNKT cells and DCs powerfully activate multiple cell types–including NK cells, CD8+ T cells, and M1 macrophages–to drive antitumor responses. c. Regulatory function: iNKT cells can kill or suppress the function of immunosuppressive cells such as TAMs, MDSCs, and Treg T cells, thereby remodeling the TME, and following interaction with B cells, iNKT cells enable B cells to exert immunoregulatory functions. Second, as among the earliest immune responders, iNKT cells can quickly secrete a wide array of Th1 and Th2 cytokines, which can activate innate and adaptive immune cells and enhance the antitumor response [45]. Through the secretion of cytokines, iNKT cells can activate other immune cells, such as NK cells, dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages. Activated iNKT cells can upregulate IL-12 receptor and CD40 Ligand (CD40L) expression, inducing DC maturation and IL-12 production through crosstalk [46–48]. Subsequently, DC-released IL-12 significantly enhances iNKT cell IFNγ secretion, establishing a positive feedback loop that drives Th1-type immune responses [49]. DC maturation induces NK cells to produce increased IFNγ while upregulating MHC class I and II antigen presentation to T cells [50]. Additionally, cytokines such as IL-2, IL-12, and IFN-γ released by iNKT cells activate NK cells and expand them into lymphokine-activated killer (LAK) cells. These activated LAK cells upregulate effector molecules and adhesion molecules to exert antitumor effects [51,52].

Finally, it is worth noting that iNKT cells can also play an antitumor role by regulating the TME. In neuroblastoma models, iNKT cells kill CD1d+ tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [53]. TAMs are typical immunosuppressive cells that reside within the TME, promoting angiogenesis, enhancing tumor cell migration and invasion, and suppressing antitumor immunity [54]. Meanwhile, iNKT cells can impair myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)—key immunosuppressive cells—through CD1d- and CD40- dependent mechanisms, this indirectly releases suppressed immune effectors such as T cells, thereby restoring antitumor immune responses [55]. In summary, iNKT cells create a more favorable microenvironment for effector immune responses by modulating the TME. This combination of direct tumor lysis, indirect cytotoxicity, and TME remodeling makes iNKT cells highly attractive for next-generation engineered cellular immunotherapies.

Clinical studies: iNKT cells in cancer

Compelling evidence from preclinic studies demonstrates the innate ability of iNKT cells to fight tumors, primarily through methods involving endogenous activation or adoptive transfer of iNKT cells [56–61]. Following promising results in preclinical models, numerous human clinical trials have been initiated. Since the discovery of aGalCer as an effective activator of human and mouse iNKT cells in the 1990s [62], Treatment with aGalCer for activating endogenous iNKT cells has been evaluated in early-phase clinical trials for several types of cancer. In a phase I clinical trial of aGalCer (KRN7000) therapy for solid tumors to activate endogenous iNKT cells, increased cytokine production (tumor necrosis factor alpha and granulocyte macrophage colony-stimulating factor) was observed in some patients, with no dose-limiting toxicities noted [63]. Several other studies assessed DCs or APCs pulsed with aGalCer in vitro in patients with various tumors, such as non-small-cell lung cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and other advanced metastatic malignancies [64–68]. A recent randomized phase II study demonstrated that administering the glycolipid ligand aGalCer, loaded with CD1d-expressing human peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) derived APCs (APC/Gal) enhances the expression of granzyme B in NK cells following surgery in patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer. Functional analysis of iNKT and NK cells revealed that the immune response induced by APC/Gal treatment resulted from immunotherapy, but there was no significant difference in clinical outcomes between the treated group and the nontreated groups [69]. All these findings indicate that the best clinical outcome achieved with this approach is disease stabilization, despite some patients showing signs of immune activation. In vivo activation resulted in limited expansion of endogenous iNKT cells in patients, prompting researchers to pursue adoptive transfer of exogenous iNKT cells as a therapeutic approach. For example, a phase I clinical trial in patients with stage IIIB-IV melanoma demonstrated that infusion of autologous in vitro expanded iNKT cells resulted in transient peaks of circulating iNKT cells and alterations in the number and activation status of various immune cells in peripheral blood, including a general increase in activated monocytes [70]. And a clinical trial involving patients with advanced or recurrent non-small cell lung cancer also showed similar results [71]. At the same time, a combination therapy involving ex vivo expanded iNKT cells and APCs pulsed with aGalCer has also been employed in clinical studies [72]. Collectively, increasing evidence highlights the transformative promise of iNKT cell-based cancer immunotherapy, and meanwhile facilitates the ex vivo engineering of iNKT cells, which was essential for the emergence of CAR-engineered iNKT cell therapy.

CAR-iNKT cells

Structure of CAR-iNKT cells

iNKT cell immunotherapy still faces challenges such as low frequency of iNKT cells, insufficient infiltration in tumor cells, and the inability to recognize tumor cells due to the negative or downregulated expression of CD1d molecules [70,73–75]. The concept of CAR technology was first proposed in 1989 by the team of Israeli scientist Professor Zelig Eshhar [76]. It is a young but rapidly advancing immunotherapy. To date, CARs have evolved into different structures to modify various immune cells and target distinct tumors. The addition of CAR to iNKT cells combines their innate advantages, these engineered cells hold great promise as a novel and powerful therapeutic strategy, potentially leading to improved outcomes for cancer patients.

The CAR consists of four main components: an antigen-binding domain, a hinge, a transmembrane domain and an intracellular signaling domain. Each component plays a unique role, and optimizing CAR molecular design involves strategically altering its protein domains. The antigen-binding domain is the extracellular portion of the CAR and is responsible for recognizing the target antigen through a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) structure. ScFv consists of the variable heavy (VH) and variable light (VL) chains of a monoclonal antibody connected by a flexible linker, exhibiting high specificity and affinity. However, Excessive affinity in CARs has been linked to various drawbacks in some studies, including activation-induced cell death, T-cell exhaustion, and CRS-related toxicity [77,78]. Therefore, balancing affinity is crucial for achieving both effective immune responses and safety. Certain characteristics of the scFv, such as scFv linker and epitope location, are critical factors that govern CAR performance [79,80]. A recent study indicates that the length and compactness of the linker influence the functionality of the generated bispecific CARs [81]. The ScFv is connected to the transmembrane domain via a hinge region, with the most commonly used hinge domains derived from membrane-bound receptors (such as CD8 or CD28) or immunoglobulins (such as IgG1 or IgG4). Hinge regions and transmembrane domains have been commoditized and are the least studied components. However, different hinge molecules and features also affect CAR function [82]. For example, Hudecek et al. discovered that compared to CARs containing a long “hinge-CH2-CH3” spacer region, ROR1-CARs with a short “hinge-only” extracellular spacer region demonstrated superior efficacy in lysing ROR1+ tumor cells and inducing T cell effector functions [83]. The transmembrane domains anchor CAR to the cell membrane of effector cells, typically derived from CD3ζ, CD4, and CD28, and the transmembrane domain used influences the stability and function of the CAR [84]. The intracellular signaling domain generally contains a T cell activation domain derived from the CD3ζ chain of the TCR as well as one or more co-stimulatory domains, such as CD28 or 4–1BB. CD3ζ is a key signal component of CAR, providing signal 1 for activation into cells, which can initiate downstream signaling cascades and cell activation. Costimulatory domain provides signal 2 of activation enhancing internal activation and promoting persistence.

The classification of CARs is primarily based on the structural and functional evolution of their intracellular signaling domains. They are currently categorized into five generations，as shown in Figure 2. The first three generations of CARs are defined by the number of intracellular domains. The initial first-generation CAR comprised only the CD3ζ primary activation domain without any co-stimulatory elements. Second-generation incorporated one co-stimulatory domain, while third-generation included two co-stimulatory domains. The fourth generation of CAR, which have been termed T-cells redirected toward universal cytokine killing (TRUCKs), are genetically engineered from second-generation CARs by incorporating DNA sequences that induce the secretion of cytokines (such as IL-12, IL-15). They overcome the immunosuppressive TME and are primarily used to address the challenges posed by solid tumors [85]. The latest fifth-generation CAR is constructed by incorporating a truncated IL-2 receptor β-chain domain, which contains a STAT3 binding site, and enables antigen recognition to simultaneously trigger the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, demonstrating superior in vivo persistence and antitumor effects in tumor models [86].

CAR-iNKT cell manufacturing

The manufacturing process for CAR-iNKT cells is similar to that of CAR-T cells and CAR-NK cells. The process involves cell collection, activation, transduction (introduction of the CAR gene into cells), expansion and reinfusion of CAR-iNKT cells into the patient via intravenous or intratumoral administration, as shown in Figure 3. The first step is to obtain cells, which can be collected from both cancer patients and healthy individuals as autologous and allogeneic iNKT cells. However, iNKT cells are a rare type of lymphocyte in human peripheral blood, constituting only a very small fraction of the total monocyte population (typically between 0.01% and 0.1%) [29,87]. Therefore, effective isolation and expansion techniques are crucial. Various methods can be used to expand iNKT cells. Conventional method is to isolate iNKT cells from PBMCs by leveraging their characteristically elevated levels of α-GalCer and IL-2 agonists. A prospective next generation approach involves generating iNKT cells from hematopoietic stem and progenitor (HSP) cells and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). This innovative strategy offers the potential for an unlimited, standardized, and readily available “off-the-shelf” cell therapy product, overcoming the limitations of donor variability and lengthy manufacturing processes associated with conventional methods [88,89]. For example, Li et al. introduced an ex vivo feeder-free culture method to differentiate gene-engineered HSP cells into allogeneic iNKT (AlloiNKT) cells, and these cells exhibit potent antitumor efficacy [90]. After activation, the efficient transduction of CAR genes into iNKT cells is primarily facilitated by viral vectors (lentivirus or retrovirus) and enhanced by tropic factors (like IL-2 and IL-15) that promote cell division and viability. Tian et al. demonstrated that CD62L marks a distinct iNKT cell subset with high proliferative capacity, and showed that CD19-specific CAR-transduced CD62L+ cells mediate sustained tumor regression in a model of B cell lymphoma [91]. In addition, IL-21 plays a critical role in preserving the central memory-like CD62L+ iNKT cell subset during expansion by suppressing BIM-mediated apoptosis and enhancing antitumor functionality. The combined use of IL-2 and IL-21 significantly improved the expansion, cytotoxicity, and in vivo efficacy of CAR-iNKT cells against lymphoma [92].

Advantages of CAR-iNKT cells in tumor immunology

As noted earlier, unmodified iNKT cells offer considerable promise for immunotherapy, yet several obstacles persist. Therefore, genetically modifying these cells to express CAR proteins may maximize their antitumor potential [93]. Furthermore, existing research indicates that CAR-iNKT cells possess several advantages over CAR-T and CAR-NK cells in tumor therapy, potentially enhancing their clinical application prospects. On the one hand, CAR-iNKT cells outperform traditional CAR-T therapies by simultaneously leveraging a dual-target recognition mechanism mediated by CAR and innate receptors such as TCR and NK cell-associated receptors, as shown in Figure 4. Their TCRs can identify tumor antigens presented by CD1d, providing a extra cytotoxic pathway independent of the CAR. The synergistic effect of this dual targeting system has the potential to yield more potent antitumor activity [94].

Another advantage of CAR-iNKT lies in its high safety. Since CAR-iNKT cells MHC-independent recognition mechanism, there is low risk of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) [21,95]. A notable aspect of safety advantage is the reduced risk of CRS. CRS is a systemic inflammatory response, clinically manifested by fever, hypotension, and hypoxia,which arises from a chain reaction involving tumor lysis, macrophage activation, and subsequent hyperinflammation [96]. CAR-iNKT cell therapy exhibits lower CRS compared to conventional CAR-T cell therapy. Direct evidence shows detectable, significant increases in CRS-associated cytokines like IL-6, IFNγ, and TNFα after co-culture with CAR-T cells. In contrast, under conditions of equivalent tumor-killing efficacy, CAR-iNKT cells trigger substantially lower levels of these cytokine releases (intermediate for CAR-iNKT, nearly undetectable for CAR-NK), directly demonstrating their inherent resistance to triggering cytokine storms at the source [97]. The other aspect is the difference in cytokine secretion patterns. For instance, CAR-T cells primarily secrete CRS-associated cytokines like IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α. In contrast, the cytokine profiles secreted by activated CAR-NK and CAR-iNKT cells differ from those of CAR-T cells, contributing to their lower CRS risk [98]. The TME, rich in MDSCs and TAMs, along with interactions between CAR-T cells and myeloid components—particularly between macrophages and monocytes—can exacerbate CRS. The TME-modulating function of CAR-iNKT cells, coupled with their ability to selectively eliminate pro-inflammatory myeloid populations through CD1d-restricted recognition, contributes to their reduced CRS risk [99].

Recent studies on prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA) CAR-T and CAR-iNKT cell therapies for metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) revealed comparable antitumor effects in humanized mice. However, mice treated with CAR-T cells exhibited lethal GvHD, splenomegaly, and CRS, in contrast, CAR-iNKT represents a safer therapeutic option [100]. To overcome GvHD in CAR-T therapies and develop allogeneic CAR-T treatments, genetic modifications have been proposed to overcome HLA barriers, these include eliminating endogenous TCRs for GvHD and HLA molecules for host-versus-graft reactions [101,102]. However, such modifications pose significant manufacturing challenges. In contrast, the inherent non-allogeneic reactivity of iNKT cells offers a valuable advantage for developing allogeneic therapies. Long-term follow-up studies of CD19 CAR T-cell therapy for acute lymphoblastic leukemia have also reported high incidence rates of neurotoxicity and CRS [103]. It is noteworthy that adverse reactions associated with CAR-NK cell therapy are rarely reported. However, in a case report, patients with non-small cell lung cancer developed CRS following CAR-NK cell therapy [104]. In summary, most research studies report no significant side effects with CAR-iNKT cells, suggest that CAR-iNKT cell therapy will be safe, and more investigations are needed. Additionally, the combination of the inherent advantages of iNKT cells with CAR demonstrates potent antitumor effects. CAR-iNKT cells, as the first cells to migrate to the tumor site, may exert earlier and more effective effects on tumor cells by secreting cytokines, regulating other immune cells, and reshaping the immune microenvironment. The other crucial excellence of CAR-iNKT cells is their potential to eliminate solid tumors. While CAR-T cell therapy has demonstrated benefits in patients with hematologic malignancies, these cell-based therapies have yet to achieve satisfactory response rates in solid tumors [105]. The complex architecture of solid tumors presents formidable barriers to various treatments, yet outcomes for CAR-iNKT cells hold promise. In addition, CAR-iNKT cells promote epitope spreading and activation of endogenous T cell responses against tumor-associated neoantigens, enhance the expansion of T cell memory, thereby establishing durable anti-tumor immunity [106,107].

Moreover, the final cost of CAR-iNKT cell therapy in clinics is estimated to be significantly lower than CAR-T cell therapy. First, CAR-T therapy involves high costs for producing a single product using the patient’s own T cells, and lymphodepleting chemotherapy is typically required before CAR-T cell infusion, imposing a heavy economic burden on both patients and healthcare systems [108,109]. Second, the total production cycle takes approximately two weeks, a particularly critical period for patients [110,111]. Those with rapidly deteriorating conditions in resource-limited settings require heightened vigilance. Data from 2020 indicates that in academic hospital inpatient settings, the total cost of a single CAR-T cell treatment for hematologic malignancies (including CAR-T cell acquisition and infusion, lymphodepletion, and acute adverse event management) was approximately $454,611 [112]. However, CAR-iNKT cells can be produced using allogeneic cells for “off-the-shelf” manufacturing and do not require repeated doses, which significantly reduces treatment costs. Although research on CAR-iNKT cells remains limited, the existing findings demonstrate their promising efficacy and a favorable safety profile in cancer treatment.

Preclinical advances of CAR-iNKT cells in cancer

CAR-iNKT cell therapy is a field with great promise but is still in its infancy, with a growing body of preclinical studies are evaluating its antitumor efficacy across various cancer types. Since 2014, when Haczey et al. pioneered research on tumor immunotherapy using CAR-iNKT cells. They successfully engineered human iNKT cells to express a CAR targeting GD2, an antigen highly expressed in neuroblastoma (NB). The study found that CARs containing the 4–1BB costimulatory domain induced a striking Th1-like polarization in iNKT cells, while constructs incorporating both CD28 and 4–1BB significantly enhanced the cells’ in vivo persistence. These CAR-GD2 iNKT cells effectively localize with potent antitumor activity, and repeated injections substantially improved long-term survival in mice with metastatic neuroblastoma without inducing GvHD [113]. Subsequent studies demonstrated that co-expression of IL-15 enhanced the expansion capacity and antitumor function of CAR-iNKT cells targeting GD2 [114]. Xu et al. reported that while co-expression of IL-15 with either CD28 or 4–1BB increased CAR-iNKT absolute numbers, however, constructs containing 4–1BB unexpectedly induced excessive activation-induced cell death, thereby reducing numeric expansion compared to CD28-based counterparts [114]. This finding underscores a critical contradiction: the choice of costimulatory domain not only influences effector function but also profoundly impacts cell survival and expansion dynamics, which may be context-dependent. Several preclinical studies on CAR-iNKT cell therapy for B-cell lymphoma have yielded positive results [91,115,116]. A study found that CD62L+ cells transduced with CD19-specific CAR achieved sustained tumor regression in a B-cell lymphoma model. The highest iNKT expansion rates and CD62L expression levels were induced when CD62L was combined with co-stimulatory molecules CD86, 4–1BB, and OX40L. The expanded CAR-iNKT cells demonstrated persistent in vivo persistence and superior therapeutic activity in both lymphoma and neuroblastoma models [91]. However, the extent to which findings from immunodeficient mouse models can be translated to clinical research remains an open question. Addressing these issues through well-designed comparative studies and the development of more sophisticated models with intact immune function is crucial for guiding the clinical translation of CAR-iNKT therapy. A study reports the modification of iNKT cells to express CARs targeting CD38 and B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA), enabling them to effectively eliminate multiple myeloma cells while retaining their innate TCR-mediated killing capacity. Crucially, these CAR-iNKT cells exhibit favorable safety profiles and display a Th1-like cytokine signature, highlighting their significant therapeutic potential [117]. In another preclinical study reveals that AlloCAR70-iNKT cells exhibit potent cytotoxicity against primary and metastatic renal cell carcinoma (RCC) through CAR and NK receptor-mediated mechanisms, while selectively targeting the immunosuppressive TME via TCR recognition. Furthermore, these cells eliminate CD70+ host alloreactive T cells, promoting sustained therapeutic efficacy [118]. Recently, an increasing number of preclinical studies on CAR-iNKT therapies have been reported, demonstrating the efficacy and safety of CAR-iNKT cells in tumor immunotherapy [119–124].

Clinical progress of CAR-iNKT cells in cancer

Despite promising preclinical results, the clinical development of CAR-iNKT cell therapy is still in its infancy, with only a handful of early phase trials reporting initial findings, summarized in Table 1. An analysis of these preliminary clinical data revealed both encouraging signs and points that warrant careful consideration. For example, CD19 CAR-iNKT cell therapy is being tested in patients with relapsed or refractory B-cell malignancies (NCT05487651), including B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) and acute/chronic lymphocytic leukemia (ALL/CLL) [126]. Regarding safety, in a phase I dose-escalation trial, no cell-related early adverse events were observed except for one case of Grade 1 CRS. As for efficacy, among nine patients (seven NHL and two ALL), three NHL patients initially achieved partial remission (PR), and two progressed to complete remission (CR) [126]. One ALL patient attained CR at week 4 with incomplete hematologic recovery and no evidence of leukemia. Interestingly, CAR-iNKT cells exhibited dose-dependent expansion in vivo. Although circulating CAR-iNKT cells were undetectable beyond 3 hours in the lowest-dose cohort, cells were detected in post-treatment tumor biopsies, confirming their ability to infiltrate target tissues even at low doses [126]. A key limitation of this study is the small sample size and the heterogeneous patient population, which precludes drawing definitive conclusions regarding efficacy in specific subtypes. Furthermore, although CAR-iNKT cells exhibited dose-dependent expansion in vivo, their survival time was notably short. In the lowest-dose group, these cells were undetectable in peripheral blood after three hours, raising an unresolved question: Can the limited survival of allogeneic CAR-iNKT cells in vivo be reconciled with the need to achieve durable remission? Encouragingly, even at low doses, these cells were detected in post-treatment tumor biopsy samples, confirming their ability to migrate to the tumor; however, the functional status of these tumor-infiltrating cells remains unclear. Two other clinical trials targeting hematologic malignancies expressing CD19 are currently underway but have not yet reported results (NCT03774654 and NCT04814004), leaving the field in a state of anticipation.

CAR-iNKT cells in the clinical trial.

In the field of solid tumors, clinical translation efforts are also underway. Heczey et al. conducted an exploratory study evaluating the efficacy of autologous CAR-GD2 IL-15-expressing iNKT cells in three pediatric patients with refractory/relapsed neuroblastoma (NCT03294954) [127–129]. Results demonstrated that CAR-iNKT cells significantly expanded in vivo and preferentially accumulated within the TME, confirming that the innate tumor-homing properties of iNKT cells are translatable to humans [127–129]. While the authors reported tumor clearance in some lesions, the small sample size (n = 3) and the fact that the patients had undergone extensive prior treatment make it difficult to attribute the clinical benefit entirely to CAR-iNKT cells. Additionally, according to results published on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06182735), CD70-targeted CAR-NKT cells (CGC729) demonstrated encouraging efficacy in patients with relapsed and refractory metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) [125]. In terms of safety, no dose-limiting toxicity was observed in all subjects. The most common adverse events were associated with lymphocyte depletion, such as neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and leukopenia. One patient developed grade 2 CRS and grade 2 ICANS, but they were eventually improved. In terms of efficacy, the overall remission rate (ORR) of 4 eligible patients reached 50%. Among the five patients who met the conditions for pharmacokinetic analysis, significant amplification of CGC729 was observed in all patients regardless of the level of CD70 expression. CGC729 reached the peak amplification on days 14 to 28 and persisted in the blood for up to 20 weeks with persistent activity. Notably, clinical responses were observed in two patients with CD70-expressing RCC exhibiting low antigen expression levels [125]. This suggests CAR-iNKT cells can effectively circumvent the challenge of low antigen density, a frequent cause of antigen escape and treatment failure with conventional CAR-T cells in solid tumors. Two additional clinical studies of CGC729 for solid tumors are currently ongoing, with no results reported at this time. In summary, early findings demonstrate the versatility, safety, and promising efficacy of CAR-iNKT cell therapy across a range of cancers, highlighting its potential to transform current treatment paradigms. Nevertheless, several crucial unresolved issues remain in this field. All reported trials have been small-scale, early-stage studies and require validation in larger, dose-optimized cohorts. Given the variability in antigen expression and the heterogeneity of the TME, identifying patients most likely to benefit will be critical for future clinical trial design. Furthermore, it remains to be seen whether the positive signals observed in tumors with low antigen expression can be validated in large-scale, biomarker-stratified clinical trials. Additional research and further clinical trials are urgently needed to comprehensively validate the efficacy of CAR-iNKT cell therapy across various hematologic malignancies and solid tumors.

Challenges in CAR-iNKT cell therapy

Although CAR-iNKT cell immunotherapy demonstrates excellent antitumor potential, it faces several challenges that limit its functions and widespread use in clinical settings.First, large-scale production faces technical bottlenecks, iNKT cells are inherently scarce in the peripheral blood of healthy individuals, and studies have shown their numbers decline sharply in patients with malignant tumors [86,87,130]. Their isolation, activation, and in vitro expansion require highly optimized culture systems and complex processes. Establishing a stable, efficient, reproducible, and cost-controlled standardized production platform remains a practical challenge limiting their widespread application. Additionally, The superior TME-remodeling capacity of CAR-iNKT cells over CAR-T and CAR-NK cells is well recognized; however, multiple immune suppression mechanisms within solid tumors continue to impede CAR-iNKT cell infiltration, survival, and functional efficacy. iNKT cell function is regulated by multiple TME factors, including APC types, antigen variants, cytokines and metabolites [131,132]. Glucose restriction and lactate accumulation in the TME, resulting from tumor cell glycolytic activity, interfere with iNKT cell function [133–135]. Glucose restriction disrupts TCR signaling by impairing glycolysis-dependent TCR vesicle recycling, thereby suppressing IFN-γ production while preserving IL-4 production. This promotes Th2 polarization of iNKT cells and tumor progression [134]. Compounding this effect, concurrent lactic acid accumulation inhibits mTORC1 activation, downregulating PPARγ and subsequently reducing SREBP1-mediated cholesterol synthesis, further compromising IFN-γ production [133]. Together, these two metabolic stresses create a multilayered barrier to iNKT cell antitumor function. In hepatocellular carcinoma, the accumulation of long-chain acylcarnitine (LCAC)suppresses iNKT cell expansion and induces premature senescence [136]. Additionally, reduced cell division control protein 42 homolog (Cdc42) expression in tumor-infiltrating iNKT cells impairs their migration and immunological synapse formation with DCs, disrupting Th1 responses [137]. Conversely, certain αGalCer variants, such as AH10-7 and α-C-GalCer, induce iNKT cells to skew toward Th1-type immune responses, offering a potential therapeutic avenue [138,139]. Finally, safety concerns cannot be overlooked. Although existing clinical data suggest that CAR-iNKT therapies exhibit milder adverse effects than traditional CAR-T therapies, their long-term safety still requires validation through large-scale clinical trials. Potential off-target toxicity, cytokine-related risks, and the possibility of mutations arising from gene integration all necessitate careful evaluation alongside efforts to advance clinical translation. 、, collectively determining the complexity and difficulty of advancing CAR-iNKT therapy from the laboratory to clinical application.

Innovative strategies and future prospects in CAR-iNKT development

Development of off-the-shelf products

Extensive research has been conducted on isolation, selective expansion, and CAR-iNKT cell manufacturing to address the deficiency of iNKT cells. And, several effective extension methods have been developed. One approach involves generating autologous α-galactoside-pulsed APCs in vitro and injecting them into patients to activate endogenous iNKT cells. However, it is limited by the number and availability of endogenous iNKT cells [140,141]. Researchers have undertaken a series of explorations to overcome this challenge，including the isolation of universal cells and the utilization of HSP cells [88–90,142]. For example, Jiang et al. identified an universal NKT (UNKT) cell line that has superior killing effect on a variety of tumor cells [123]. Li YR’s team successfully engineered universal CAR-engineered NKT (UCAR-NKT) cells by integrating NKT TCR engineering with HLA gene editing technology in HSCs, combined with an in vitro feeder-free HSC differentiation culture system. These cells exhibit high yield, high purity, and strong stability, demonstrating excellent safety and anti-tumor performance [119]. However, there remains no unified standard for the manufacturing of CAR-iNKT cells at present. Encouragingly, iPSCs are gaining widespread attention due to their ability to proliferate indefinitely, their potential to differentiate into multiple cell types, and their suitability for gene editing [143]. These cells are obtained by reprogramming human somatic cells into pluripotent stem cells possessing the fundamental characteristics of embryonic stem cells (ESCs).These properties offer hope for achieving large-scale production and uniform quality control of CAR-NKT cells, laying a solid foundation for delivering “off-the-shelf” immunotherapies.

CAR-iNKT design optimization and therapeutic potential

The current CAR construction for CAR-NKT cells largely follows the design principles of traditional CAR-T cells. For hematologic malignancies, CAR design prioritizes rapid clearance and safety; whereas for solid tumors, multidimensional functional integration is required to overcome physical barriers, tumor heterogeneity, and immunosuppressive microenvironments [119,144]. Simultaneously, the unique biological characteristics of iNKT cells necessitate shifting CAR design from simple transplantation to targeted optimization. Optimizing CAR design, particularly selecting costimulatory domains and integrating immune-enhancing genes is crucial for enhancing the efficacy and safety of CAR cell therapies. Among costimulatory domains, 4–1BB has been demonstrated to be more suitable for solid tumor treatment scenarios requiring long-term persistence due to its advantages in promoting mitochondrial biogenesis, enhancing metabolic adaptability, and facilitating memory formation [145]. Further research is needed to develop additional co-stimulatory domains and explore the combined use of multiple co-stimulatory domains. The cytokine armor strategy, as one of the most effective approaches currently available, has been introduced separately. Single antigen targeting may induce immune escape, while simultaneously targeting multiple antigens holds promise for addressing this issue. Research demonstrates that combining the GRP78-specific peptide antigen-binding domain with the CD123-specific scFv generates a bispecific antigen-binding domain. This dual-antigen targeting successfully identifies and kills tumor cells expressing either GRP78 or CD123, and exhibits stronger antitumor activity than monospecific T cells when both antigens are expressed [81]. Interestingly, the interdisciplinary integration of immunology and computational biology demonstrates formidable power in tackling cutting-edge biotechnology challenges. For instance, in recent research, researchers successfully resolved the difficulty of surface expression for a novel dual-target CAR-T cell (IL-13 Rα2-B7-H3 tandem CAR) through systematic construct optimization and computational biology approaches. Not only was its expression restored, but its tumor clearance efficacy was also significantly enhanced both in vivo and in vitro [146]. Utilizing synthetic biology approaches to construct logic-gated circuits, such as synthetic Notch(SynNotch), has already been applied to CAR-T cells [147]. Future CAR-iNKT designs will become increasingly sophisticated, yielding more potent and durable “super” effector cells.

Cytokine engineering

Cytokine armoring enhances the survival, functional persistence, and antitumor activity of CAR-iNKT cells within immunosuppressive TME by genetically co-expressing specific cytokines within the CAR structure. Among these, IL-15 co-expression has been the most extensively studied. Numerous studies demonstrate that CAR-T and CAR-NK cells co-expressing IL-15 exhibit superior persistence and antitumor activity compared to cells without cytokine co-expression [148–151]. This observation also holds true for CAR-iNKT cells [114,127]. Additionally, other alternative cytokines are also being continuously researched. The expression of IL-12 in iNKT and CAR-NKT cells promotes extensive molecular reprogramming, thereby inducing Th1-polarized cells with memory characteristics, sustained activity, and antitumor capabilities [152]. Meanwhile, armoring with IL-21 activates STAT3 signaling in CAR-iNKT cells, leading to reduced exhaustion marker expression, prolonged in vivo persistence, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy against renal carcinoma, with no adverse effects detected [153]. Recent research reveals that co-expression of IL-18 in CAR-iNKT cells comprehensively reprograms iNKT cell metabolism. Compared to IL-15 expression alone, co-expression of IL-18 or IL-15/IL-18 in vitro enhances CAR-NKT cytotoxicity, proliferation, and cytokine secretion [154]. Preclinical and early clinical studies demonstrate that cytokine co-expressed CAR-iNKT cells retain their inherent low risk of CRS and neurotoxicity, exhibiting overall favorable safety profiles. This paves the way for developing a new generation of highly effective, durable, and safe “off-the-shelf” cytokine-armored CAR-iNKT cancer immunotherapies.

Combination therapy: the future paradigm

Monotherapy struggles to overcome complex tumors. In the future, CAR-iNKT therapy is highly likely to serve as the core or a crucial component of combination therapies. When integrated with gene editing technologies, immunomodulators, oncolytic viruses, and chemotherapeutic agents, it will form multidimensional comprehensive treatment strategies to achieve synergistic effects. A promising approach involves combining genetic modified CAR-iNKT cells with techniques such as genome editing, gene knockout, and gene knockdown to enhance their ability to resist immunosuppressive signals within the TME. For example, blocking immune checkpoints such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3), can mitigate the effects of immune suppression on CAR-iNKT cells [155]. This has also been validated in CAR-T cell therapy [156,157]. And CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing has been applied in phase I clinical trials in humans to delete the expression of the TCRα chain, TCRβ chain, and PD-1 in CAR-T cells to improve safety and effectiveness [158]. Another approach is to combine CAR-iNKT cells with immunomodulators to resist the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment [152,159]. A study combining CD19-targeted CAR-iNKT cells with PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibition demonstrated that nivolumab enhances cytokine release, cytotoxicity, and survival of CD19-CAR-iNKT cells, thereby amplifying their anti-lymphoma activity while preserving their ability to prevent GvHD [160]. Furthermore, combining CAR-iNKT therapy with conventional treatments such as radiotherapy or specific chemotherapeutic agents may enhance the infiltration of immune cells into the TME, thereby amplifying the efficacy of ongoing immunotherapy [155,161]. Finally, combined oncolytic viruses are being explored to enhance tumor infiltration and the persistence of immune cells, demonstrating potential for combination therapy in solid tumors [156,157].

Conclusion

CAR-iNKT cells have emerged as a highly promising new frontier in the fight against cancer. They represent a unique convergence of the potent, antigen-directed cytotoxicity of CAR technology with the inherent biological advantages of iNKT cells. Their inherent tissue-homing properties, particularly to immunosuppressive sites like the bone marrow and solid TME, position them as ideal vehicles for targeting hard-to-treat malignancies. Furthermore, their limited TCR diversity significantly reduces the risk of inducing GvHD, paving the way for successful allogeneic “off-the-shelf” therapies that can be manufactured at scale. However, the journey from a promising frontier to a mainstream therapeutic modality is not without its obstacles. Limited number of clinical trial studies and significant challenges remain. As discussed, innovative strategies are being explored to overcome these hurdles, including genetic engineering, metabolic reprogramming, rational combination therapies and the development of sophisticated “armored” CAR constructs. In conclusion, CAR-iNKT cells are not merely an alternative to CAR-T cells, but a distinct and advanced platform with the potential to overcome many of the limitations of current cell therapies. By continuing to innovate at the intersection of immunology, genetic engineering, and cell biology, CAR-iNKT cells are poised to redefine the landscape of cancer immunotherapy and bring us closer to curative treatments for a wider range of patients.
