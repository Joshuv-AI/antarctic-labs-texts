Popliteal Artery Compression Caused by Intraneural Ganglion Cyst of the Tibial Nerve
Authors: Sandra Feldler, Maximilian Zaussinger, Lisa Radacher, Kathrin Aufschnaiter-Hiessböck, Manfred Schmidt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Intraneural ganglion cysts are rare, benign mucinous cysts that arise within the epineurium of peripheral nerves. They most commonly involve the peroneal nerve, whereas involvement of the tibial nerve is exceptionally rare. Patients typically present with neurological deficits involving motor and sensory function of the affected nerve. We present a case of an intraneural ganglion cyst of the tibial nerve manifesting primarily with atypical, vascular compression–like symptoms of intermittent claudication. A 43-year-old patient was referred to our department with a 1-year history of exertional, cramp-like pain in the left calf. The pain occurred consistently within minutes of walking or running and subsided with rest, mimicking intermittent claudication. Duplex sonography and subsequent magnetic resonance imaging revealed an intraneural ganglion cyst of the tibial nerve originating from the superior tibiofibular joint, showing compression of the popliteal artery. On magnetic resonance imaging, compression of the popliteal artery to less than a third of its physiological diameter was demonstrated. Definitive surgical management involved decompression of the intraneural cyst and disconnection of the articular branch. Postoperatively, the patient’s intermittent claudication symptoms ceased. Intraneural ganglion cysts of the lower extremity represent a rare cause of neurological symptoms. This case report showed that they can also compress adjacent vascular structures, thereby mimicking intermittent claudication. These atypical symptoms can lead to an initial suspicion of a vascular etiology and hence pose a risk for misdiagnosis. Decompression of the ganglion and addressing the articular branch are essential steps to prevent recurrence and provide definitive symptom relief.

Intraneural ganglion cysts are benign mucinous cysts arising within the epineurium of peripheral nerves.1 They typically occur in the common peroneal nerve.2 Other nerves are affected far less often. A systematic review demonstrated involvement of the common peroneal nerve in 393 cases, with only 32 affecting the tibial nerve.2 The etiology of these cysts has long been debated.1,3 Increasing evidence has supported the articular theory, proposing that intraneural cysts are joint-derived.2 They arise from capsular defects of neighboring joints, enabling joint fluid to track along articular branches into the parent nerve.1 Tibial intraneural cysts typically arise from the superior tibiofibular joint (STFJ) or the tibiofemoral joint.3 Most affected patients present with neuropathic symptoms such as pain, numbness, or muscle weakness owing to fascicle compression.4 However, patients may also present with atypical symptoms that are not primarily neuropathic. Here, we report a rare case of an intraneural ganglion cyst of the tibial nerve arising from the STFJ, resulting in intermittent claudication due to compression of the popliteal artery.

CASE REPORT

A 43-year-old patient was referred to our department of plastic surgery with physical activity–induced left-sided calf pain. Other than alpha-gal syndrome, an allergy to the alpha-gal sugar found in mammalian meat and products, the patient was healthy and, by history, an active trail runner. He presented with a 1-year history of cramp-like calf pain, consistently triggered after a few minutes of walking or running and relieved by rest. This exertional, reversible pattern resembled intermittent claudication, raising suspicion of a vascular etiology. A few months after symptom onset, he experienced numbness of the left lateral foot edge. No motor impairment was observed. For further evaluation of the suspected vascular pathology, he was referred to sonography. Ultrasound revealed an intraneural ganglion of the left tibial nerve measuring 4.6 cm in length and 2 cm in width. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed that the left popliteal artery adjacent to the intraneural lesion was compressed to 1.45 mm (Figs. 1, 2). An articular branch from the distal aspect of the ganglion to the STFJ was identified as the potential cause. Before admission to our department, percutaneous aspiration had been performed elsewhere, resulting in temporary symptom relief. However, they recurred 3 months postintervention, and repeat MRI showed re-formation of the cyst, resembling the preinterventional measures. Hence, the patient was referred to our department. He underwent surgical exploration of the left tibial nerve in the popliteal fossa. The intraneural cyst was located deep between the 2 heads of the gastrocnemius muscle. An articular branch tracing from the distal aspect of the ganglion to the STFJ was identified (Fig. 3). The branch was carefully dissected, ligated with sutures, additionally clipped, and disconnected near the STFJ. Using intraoperative nerve mapping, a small longitudinal incision measuring 5–6 mm was made at the distal aspect of the ganglion in a stimulus-free interval to avoid injury to functional fascicles of the tibial nerve. The mucinous fluid was gently squeezed out, thereby decompressing the ganglion without postoperative neurological impairments. Ten weeks postoperatively, the patient reported cessation of symptoms. He had returned to sports and no longer experienced intermittent claudication. The sensory deficit of the lateral foot edge improved, with the patient reporting only mild hypesthesia in the affected area. Sonography demonstrated no residual popliteal artery compression, with a current diameter of 4.7 mm (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

Intraneural ganglion cysts are rare mucinous lesions within peripheral nerves.2 Tibial nerve involvement is uncommon, with only a few cases reported in the literature.2

This case was particularly interesting as the patient’s leading symptom was exertional calf pain resembling intermittent claudication. Typically, intraneural ganglion cysts present with neuropathic pain, with motor or sensory deficiencies due to compression of nerve fascicles.4,5 In our patient, the intraneural cyst expanded ventrally, causing compression of the popliteal artery against the posterior edge of the tibia within the rigid fascial compartment of the calf. This led to symptoms that were indistinguishable from peripheral arterial disease at initial clinical presentation. The atypical symptom pattern can obscure the underlying neural pathology and delay definitive diagnosis and treatment. Intraneural cysts of the tibial nerve are uncommon, and reports on vascular compromise in that context are exceedingly rare.2 In otherwise healthy, young individuals presenting with unilateral, intermittent claudication, differential diagnoses other than peripheral artery disease must be considered. Compression of the popliteal artery in the popliteal fossa or around the knee joint resulting in exertional pain is typically reported in the context of extraneural cysts. Cystic adventitial disease of the popliteal artery, Baker cysts, and intramuscular cysts are well-documented causes of unilateral intermittent claudication.6–8 Our case therefore adds to the spectrum of differential diagnoses of intermittent claudication.

Our case also underscores the relevance of addressing the underlying pathology to provide permanent symptom relief.9 A better understanding of the pathogenesis of intraneural cysts has led to increased awareness and improved radiological recognition of respecting lesions.9 Literature has proven that MRI has a higher sensitivity for identifying joint branches compared to ultrasonography.9 Identifying the pathoanatomy is paramount to adequately address the underlying cause surgically.9 As demonstrated in our patient, percutaneous aspiration can provide temporary relief, but recurrence rates are high if the articular connection remains unaddressed.2 Recurrence rates of up to 18.5% have been reported after surgical treatment of intraneural cysts arising from the STFJ, all occurring in cases where the articular branch is unaddressed.4 Definitive surgical management should therefore not only evacuate the cyst but also identify and disconnect the articular branch.2 As shown in our case, the patient was provided with symptom relief and was able to resume competitive sports.

CONCLUSIONS

This report presents a rare case of a tibial intraneural ganglion cyst causing intermittent claudication through popliteal artery compression. It highlights the potential for intraneural cysts to mimic peripheral arterial disease and underscores the importance of considering atypical etiologies in claudication-like presentations.
