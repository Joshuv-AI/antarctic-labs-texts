Cut point validation vs. real-world performance: the ultra-low cut point dilemma
Authors: Lindsay Denhoff, Cansu Dernek, Liam Heins, Chong Gu, Allie Renfro, Stephanie Pasas-Farmer, Robert Kernstock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCut point determination is a critical component in the development and validation of antidrug antibody (ADA) assays, directly influencing the assessment of immunogenic responses. Removal of analytical and biological outliers from employing Tukey’s outlier test may lead to reduced variability and consequently low or ultra-low cut points (ULCPs, defined here as signal-to-noise ratios ≤1.10). Such ULCPs raise concerns about assay reliability and the potential for elevated false-positive rates. To evaluate this, we analyzed ADA studies from two contract research organizations employing ULCPs. Given the heightened assay sensitivity from modern platforms and reagents, our findings indicate that ULCPs do not inherently compromise ADA assay performance or reliability. These results support the continued use of ULCPs when appropriately validated and statistically justified.KEYWORDS: Immunogenicity, cut points, low cut points, antidrug antibody (ADA) assays

Introduction

Undesirable immune responses in the form of antidrug antibodies (ADA) are an important consideration for the safety and efficacy of biotherapeutic drugs. The development of reliable and sensitive assays for identifying these immune reactions is therefore essential to the product development process for biopharmaceuticals. Determination of the cut point, which is the threshold used to differentiate between a positive and negative sample response, has been a focus of statistical standards and recommendations because its value can have a significant impact on the sensitivity and reliability of ADA assays. In this paper, we will focus on the calculation and impact of the screening cut point (SCP).

The need for general standards for cut point calculation has yielded several influential white papers recommending robust workflows for assay design and statistical evaluation of the results [1–4]. The US FDA and EU EMA have also issued several recommendations regarding best practices for ADA assay development and validation, most recently in 2019 by the US FDA [5]. Many of these approaches suggest removing outliers to reduce the influence of extreme values on the cut point calculation. Such approaches may result in a low SCP value (Signal to Noise, S/N, ≤1.20) or ultra-low (S/N ≤1.10) value, as described in further detail in the Method section below. With the development of more robust methods using sophisticated buffers to lower backgrounds and reduce variability, methods resulting in the removal of matrix components, improved assay technology platforms over the last few decades, and production of higher quality critical reagents, low cut points have become increasingly common as analytical background variation has decreased.

With the increased prevalence of low cut point values, concerns have been raised that such values may result in excessive false positive rates and diminished statistical power in classifying true positive ADA samples [6]. It is therefore important to examine the impact of these cut points on the reliability of ADA assays and on the assessment of ADA positivity. In this paper, we survey a variety of recent ADA studies from two contract research organizations (CROs), as well as a two-year survey of ADA studies performed with BioData Solutions’ Red Thread VergeⓇ software, a web application that implements an adapted workflow based on the Devanarayan et al. recommendations [1].

Our overall findings indicate that cut point values at or below 1.10 had little to no negative impact on sample assessment across the surveyed studies, as indicated by the following parameters and discussed in detail in proceeding sections of this paper: the need to establish a new cut point value during the study; high false positive rate during the study; or high failure rate of the low positive control (LPC) during validation and/or study. It is the recommendation of the authors that if a laboratory determines the cut point to be 1.20 or lower during validation, the scientists should evaluate the appropriateness of the cut point through examination of the individual population responses and their relationship to the negative control, the false positive rate during study analysis, and evaluation of the 1% failure rate low positive control (LPC) performance rather than assuming that the cut point is inappropriate prior to application.

Methods

A survey of 185 ADA assay screening cut points calculated using Red Thread Verge, an automated software tool, was conducted spanning a predetermined time frame from January 2022 to July 2024. The survey was conducted to assess the overall rate of low and ultra-low cut point determinations independent of factors such as drug modality, drug target, ADA assay format, or organization. Screening cut points were determined from normalized or log transformed data using Tukey’s outlier criteria followed by assessment of normality and parametric or nonparametric calculation of a plate-specific cut point (refer to Cut Point Approach Section). The cut points were categorized as low if equal to or below 1.20, and as ultra-low if equal to or below 1.10. The cut points were also identified as either clinical or pre-clinical. The low cut point limit of ≤1.20 was derived from the idea that many immunogenicity assays apply well-to-well coefficient of variation (%CV) limits of 20–25% in tandem with the PK assay acceptance criteria where the variance in measurements for the same sample can be up to 20%. These limits imply that only variances greater than 20% are significant. The ultra-low cut point limit was selected based on the argument that pipetting precision (typically 2–5%) and instrument analytical precision limits (often around 5%) may lead to the impression that these ULCP are unreliable.

Analytical metadata from immunogenicity studies were sampled from two contract research organizations (CROs) to investigate the long-term impact of ultra-low cut points. Studies were selected based on completeness of available metadata and the recovery of an ultra-low screening cut point during assay validation.

For each of the selected studies, the following properties were collected: the calculated screening cut point (SCP), confirmatory cut point (CCP), percentage of outliers removed, if a 1% failure rate low positive control (LPC) was statistically determined, the observed LPC pass rate, whether adjustment of the statistically determined LPC was necessary, the observed false positive rate using the calculated cut point when available, and whether an in-study cut point was later needed for the project.

Six (6) ultra-low cut point case studies were collected from ICON (CRO-A) and seven (7) were collected from Accurant (CRO-B), for a total of thirteen (13).

The observed False Positive Rate (FPR) was calculated by using the formula

FPR = B/(A+B) x 100

where A is the number of pre-dose/baseline samples that screen negative and B is the number of pre-dose/baseline samples that screen positive and confirm negative.

Cut point approach

Statistical analysis for cut point and LPC determination was performed using either Red Thread Verge or manually using JMP statistical software and Microsoft Excel. Both approaches implemented the statistical workflow recommended by Devanarayan et al., which outlines a series of steps into a general decision tree to standardize the calculation process and use appropriate statistical tests for determining end results [1]. The approach taken by Red Thread Verge is described here briefly and is shown in Figure 1.

Cut point calculation and workflow as employed by Red Thread Verge.

For screening cut point calculation, the geometric mean, arithmetic mean, or median of the negative control on each plate is used to determine signal-to-noise ratios for all screening sample data. Outliers are then identified using the Tukey Outlier test, followed by a normality test performed first on non-transformed data after outlier removal to determine whether to use a parametric or non-parametric calculation for the cut point. If either the p-value resulting from the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality is greater than 0.05, or the absolute skewness is less than 1, then the parametric approach is used for cut point calculation. If the p-value is less than 0.05 and the absolute skewness is greater than 1, then this process is repeated using the log transformation (natural log or log10) of the dataset. In this case, the log-transformed data is used to calculate the cut point if it passes the normality test as explained above; otherwise, a non-parametric calculation of the cut point is performed using the non-transformed data.

Where a 5% false positive rate is applied, a “parametric” approach refers to the standard formula of mean +1.645 × standard deviation, using the t-value from the standard normal distribution corresponding to the 95% upper confidence interval [1]. The “non-parametric” calculation instead uses the 95th percentile of the distribution of the individual sample results to determine the cut point.

Results

Cut point survey

Of the 185 screening cut points collected, 105 were from clinical studies and 80 were from preclinical studies. Of the 105 clinical studies, 59 (56%) were classified as low cut points (SCP ≤1.20) and 41 (39%) were classified as ultra-low cut points (SCP ≤1.10). Of the 80 preclinical studies, 46 (58%) were classified as low cut points and 24 (30%) were classified as ultra-low cut points. The overall rates recovered were 57% for low cut points and 35% for ultra-low cut points (Figure 2).

Case studies – CRO-A

The majority of biotherapeutics are monoclonal antibodies and this modality is represented in 5/6 case studies with the remaining case study being an Fc-fusion protein. Most assay formats were ECLIA bridging assays with acid dissociation, but one SPEAD (solid-phase extraction with acid dissociation) assay is represented in the table. There are also representative studies from both human (5/6 studies) and non-human primate. Excessive outlier removal is a potential contributor to the determination of an ULCP. In the authors’ opinion, removal of 10–15% of data points as outliers is acceptable. Greater than 15% of data points were removed in only one study, and in this case (A-5) an in-study cut point was needed, suggesting that excessive removal of data points may have resulted in a falsely low cut point. Case A-1 describes a disease population where >15% of data points were removed, however this was expected due to autoantibodies and/or preexisting antibodies present in the disease state which are not present in the healthy population. Confirmatory cut points are also included to describe differences between the screening and confirmatory assay tiers regarding variability among the individuals assessed for cut point determination. In most cases, the confirmatory cut point was below 20% (4/6 studies) and below 15% in half (3/6) of the cases suggesting very little variation among individuals tested.

The latest FDA guidance on immunogenicity validations suggests application of a statistically determined LPC concentration predicted to fail 1% of the time. Four of the six case studies used this 1% failure rate LPC for run acceptance and one of the other two case studies included the 1% failure rate LPC but didn’t apply acceptance criteria. When a ULCP is applied, robustness of the 1% failure rate LPC over the study duration is of potential concern. Overall, among the four case studies with 1% failure rate LPC concentrations included for run acceptance, only one study required adjustment of the LPC concentration due to excessive run failures that were attributable to the LPC. Of the remaining 3 case studies with 1% failure rate LPC concentrations included for run acceptance, the LPC accounted for 0–0.6% of run failures. Additionally, in the case of A-2, if acceptance criteria had been applied in the instance where a 1% failure rate LPC was calculated and included for informational purposes, no runs would have failed due to the statistically determined LPC. These data suggest that ULCPs do not result in any significant issues related to the performance of statistically determined LPC concentrations designed to fail 1% of the time.

Cut point appropriateness should be assessed in every clinical study, and it is generally recommended to ensure the false positive rate falls between 2% and 11% [3]. The potential for a high false positive rate is another concern associated with ULCPs. Four of six cases studies were within the 2–11% range, with one additional case study having an FPR just outside the target range at 11.4%. These data indicate that, in most cases, ULCPs are acceptable without adjustments for sample analysis.

Perhaps, the most significant concern with ULCPs is whether an abnormally high rate of immunogenicity will be observed in study. Overall, we found the rates of immunogenicity in the case studies to be in line with most other biotherapeutics. Only two studies had rates of immunogenicity >10%. One was a non-clinical study and the other was in an auto-immune indication, in both cases the rate of immunogenicity was expected to be higher than 10%.

Case study A-1

In the first case study, a bridging acid dissociation ECLIA (electrochemiluminescence immunoassay) assay format was used for a monoclonal antibody therapeutic, and a total of over 300 sample analysis runs were performed. In the validation, normal healthy and diseased donors were used to determine screening cut points of 1.05 and 1.08, respectively. A considerably greater proportion of cut point data (30%) were excluded as outliers in the disease population due to greater variability likely attributable to autoantibodies or preexisting antibodies. A statistically determined LPC concentration set to fail 1% of the time was initially applied, but an increased incidence of run failures attributable to the LPC justified an increase in the LPC concentration post-validation, suggesting that the ULCP may have impacted calculation of a 1% failure rate LPC from validation data. The Sponsor prospectively requested determination of an in-study cut point, which resulted in a screening cut point factor of 1.07 in the study population. This was very close to the disease state cut point of 1.08 determined during validation. Using the in-study cut points, the study false positive rate was determined to be 10%, which is within the expected range of 2–11%. The rate of confirmed ADA positive samples was relatively high in this study at 71.1%, but was expected for this therapeutic and indication. Therefore, we conclude that the ULCP did not result in an overly high rate of immunogenicity.

Case study A-2

In the second case study, a bridging acid dissociation ECLIA was used for a monoclonal antibody therapeutic. Fewer than 10% of datapoints were removed as outliers, suggesting that the cut point was calculated from a dataset without artificially reduced variability. A 1% failure rate LPC was calculated and included in the sample analysis runs but was not applied for run acceptance. The study's false positive rate was determined to be 11.4%, and the decision was made to proceed with the validation cut point. The rate of immunogenicity in samples was low at just over 5%, indicating that the ULCP did not result in a high rate of immunogenicity.

Case study A-3

This is the only example of an Fc-fusion protein therapeutic and also the only non-human assay. Similar to the other assays, it is an ECLIA bridging assay with acid dissociation. An acceptable percentage of outliers were removed when determining the cut point. A 1% failure rate LPC was not used in this study, therefore the frequency of run failures attributable to the LPC cannot be calculated. An in-study cut point was not needed, but there was a relatively high rate of immunogenicity which was expected since it was in a non-human population.

Case study A-4

This case study has the second largest sample analysis data set with over 100 runs. As with the other case studies, this is an ECLIA bridging assay with acid dissociation. During the cut point analysis, fewer than 10% of datapoints were removed as outliers. The 1% failure rate LPC did not require adjustment in the screening assay, but interestingly had to be increased in the confirmatory assay due to run failures. The study false positive rate was within the acceptable range (2–11%) and the rate of immunogenicity was quite low at <3%, indicating that the ULCP did not result in a high rate of immunogenicity.

Case study A-5

This is the case study employing an ECLIA bridging assay with acid dissociation. In this case, 25 commercial disease state individuals were evaluated three times during assay validation, and over 16% of cut point data were removed as outliers resulting in an ULCP. The ULCP was found not to be appropriate in the study population as the FPR was determined to be 23%. An in-study cut point from >50 pre-dose study samples was calculated to be 1.43, much higher than the cut point of 1.07 determined during validation. A 1% failure rate LPC was used during sample analysis, and the LPC was found to be robust. The rate of immunogenicity was very low using the in-study cut point.

Case study A-6

This case study describes an ECLIA using a SPEAD assay format. During the cut point analysis, 10% of data points were removed as outliers, and a 1% failure rate LPC was applied, which did not result in any run failures. The in-study FPR calculated using the validated cut point was within the acceptable range, and a low rate of immunogenicity was observed among the study samples. The ULCP did not negatively impact the performance of this assay.

CRO-B case studies

The case studies from CRO-B provide further examples of the impact of ULCPs on ADA studies. The metadata for these case studies are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Many of these studies used SPEAD assays which may result in “cleaner” distributions with less variation, potentially resulting in lower recovered cut points overall. These studies present an opportunity to sample the effects of this method and the resulting cut points on study performance.

For majority of the cases, an ULCP did not result in a negative impact on study performance, with a few notable exceptions.

Case study B-1

This study is a fully human monoclonal antibody therapeutic and was developed using a SPEAD assay. Even though it had an ultra-low cut point during the validation, the false positivity rate during sample analysis was 4.5% which is within the theoretically expected range of 2–11%. This validated cut point was used in multiple clinical studies with acceptable false positivity rates within the sample analysis as well as the acceptable range of 1% LPC run failure. The assay showed robustness and the ULCP did not negatively impact the performance of this assay.

Case study B-2

The case of study B-2, a preclinical validation assay, has the lowest cut point among all the provided cases. It involves a monoclonal antibody therapeutic using a SPEAD method that involves magnetic beads to detect the anti-drug antibody in non-human samples. It is an Electrochemiluminescence immunoassay that is conducted over two days. A parametric approach was applied, and normality was confirmed during the cut point analysis. There were no biological outliers and 4 analytical outliers were removed among 300 observations. In the assay, the negative control samples exhibited higher values than the naive individuals, resulting in low signal-to-noise ratios for the samples and therefore a very low cut point value of 0.99. This cut point unsurprisingly resulted in a highly elevated level of positivity and therefore an in-study cut point assessment during the preclinical sample analysis was conducted. The control animal samples were used to calculate the in-study cut point which still resulted in an ULCP of 1.04. The desired false-positivity rate of 2–11% determined from the control animals (those not receiving the test article) and predose samples was able to be achieved using the in-study cut point.

Case study B-3

This study is developed using an ACE method containing a pegylated, modified recombinant protein. A parametric approach was able to be applied, and normality was confirmed during the cut point analysis. Close to 10% of the data were removed as biological outliers or analytical outliers. The clinical sample analysis did not reach out to 100 runs; however, there were no observed LPC failures that exceeded the 1% rate. The false positivity rate of the samples was 5.4% which was acceptable and did not require an in-study cut point. The ULCP did not negatively impact the performance of this assay.

Case study B-4

This case study also involves an ACE assay performed over two days. Instead of a full validation, a method qualification was performed per the sponsor’s request and therefore the confirmatory assay was also omitted. During the screening cut point analysis, normality was confirmed and fewer than 10% of datapoints were removed as outliers, resulting with CP value of 1.08. After the sample analysis was initiated in the preclinical population, the false positivity was high at 45%, and the ULCP was found not to be appropriate in the study population. In-study cut point evaluation was conducted resulting in a cut point of 1.24, bringing the false-positivity rate of the samples to 5%.

Case study B-5

This case is another SPEAD method that involves magnetic beads to detect the anti-drug antibody in human samples. The therapeutic product is an ADC which led us to determine cut points for the whole drug, the antibody domain and the payload. Post validation, the cut point evaluations passed the normality and less than 3% outliers were detected using the parametric approach. The study has not reached out to sample analysis to evaluate the FPR.

Case study B-6

In this study, a bridging acid dissociation ECLIA (electrochemiluminescence immunoassay) assay format was used for a bi-specific protein therapeutic. Cut point was calculated for the screening as well as each domain of the confirmatory. While the screening evaluation failed on normality and used the non-parametric calculation, the confirmatories were calculated with parametric approach. A greater population of outliers were removed in this study compared to the other case studies discussed, which was 24.4%. The outliers removed could have been problematic; however, the program was terminated prior to sample analysis and the appropriateness of the cut point could not be ascertained as well as FPR. There was no LPC adjustment needed during the validation.

Case study B-7

This case involves a bi-specific fusion protein containing a monoclonal antibody. ACE method was used to detect positive controls against both the whole fusion protein and the monoclonal domain. During the validation cut point calculations, the human population failed the normality test leading to a non-parametric calculation of cut points for the screening and all relative domains. Once the study moved to clinical study sample analysis the LPC performed well, and the FPR remained within acceptable range of 2–11%. The ULCP did not negatively impact the performance of this assay during the sample analysis.

Most cases of ULCP for CRO-B posed no major problems in study performance. While several studies exhibited false positive rates within the standard acceptable range of 2–11%, none of these instances resulted in run failures due to the LPC or required adjustments to the LPC. This may reflect the lower variability inherent to the assay methodologies that were utilized for these studies, indicating that for modern and precise assay methods, low cut points can be appropriate.

Discussion and conclusion

As immunogenicity assays become more sensitive and low cut points are more commonly observed, it is important to carefully examine the consequences of these cut points on study performance. As observed in the survey of statistically determined screening cut points, a majority (57%) were classified as “low” (≤1.20) and a sizable minority (35%) were classified as “ultra-low” (≤1.10), indicating a high prevalence of these cut points for recent immunogenicity studies. Rates of low cut point recovery were observed to be similar between preclinical and clinical studies.

As demonstrated by the case studies summarized above, assays that generate ultra‑low cut points can display a wide range of performance characteristics; therefore, their suitability must be evaluated on a case‑by‑case basis. In some situations, an ULCP may be inappropriate, including but not limited to scenarios in which the screening cut point is artificially low because the negative controls are not representative of the individuals’ response used during cut point determination, or when excessive outlier removal leads to an ULCP that no longer preserves biological and analytical variability. However, the authors caution against assuming that all ULCPs are inherently unsuitable. The generation of a low or ultra‑low cut point alone should not be used to classify a cut point as inappropriate in the absence of additional supporting evidence. Overall, the authors find that most assays with ULCPs demonstrate acceptable performance characteristics and do not result in an overestimation of immunogenicity rates.

Bioanalytical scientists should examine several different statistical indicators to gauge the overall validity of proceeding with a cut point value. One factor to consider is the percentage of the data removed as outliers for the calculation of the cut point. Removing more than 15% of the observations as analytical or biological outliers may indicate that the resulting distribution is not representative of the true variation of the population in question, which may result in a problematic cut point value. In cases, such as this, increasing the interquartile range multiplier (i.e., from 1.5 to 3.0) to exclude fewer outliers or use of a more complex statistical model such as a mixed effects model may allow for more accurate consideration of the true population variability. Only one of the CRO-A case studies listed above evaluated the in-study performance of a cut point with more than 15% of the data removed as outliers (A-5). This study also recovered a 23% false positive rate and required an in-study cut point to be calculated.

Screening assay cut points target a false positive rate of 5%, and it is generally accepted that the observed value should fall within a range of 2–11%. As mentioned above, one case study for CRO-A (A-5) and one from CRO-B (B-4) showed highly elevated observed FPRs corresponding to the need to determine an in-study cut point. All other studies from CRO-A and CRO-B showed an observed FPR within or just beyond (A-2 at 11.4%) the acceptable range. While it is certainly possible that ultra-low cut points can recover a high FPR, this was not the case for the majority of sampled cases, and the causes and consequences of this situation will vary from study to study.

In conclusion, this survey demonstrates that low cut point values should be evaluated for experimental and statistical validity. As ADA assay platforms and approaches have advanced, cut point values of ≤1.20 S/N are becoming more frequent and should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis to determine if their use is appropriate. However, the authors do not consider low and ultra low cut points to be a cause for undue concern, as they can be functional in detecting ADA. While it is acknowledged that ULCPs with very high affinity-positive controls may result in ostensibly very sensitive assays (<5 ng/mL) and lead to the identification of presumably low level ADAs, discussion surrounding the clinical relevance of low level ADAs and whether they should be detected is beyond the scope of this paper. Proposed alternatives to the current three tier paradigm such as the consideration of S/N as a continuous variable and characterization of the impact of observed ADA to pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, efficacy, and safety offer methods of describing ADA beyond classification with a cut point [7–12]. As our approach to the assessment of immunogenicity continues to evolve, it is important to acknowledge that the transition will take time and an overlap in strategy is inevitable.

Future perspective

As platforms, workflows, and reagents for cut point determination have refined and advanced, systemic variability and interference leading to more prevalent biological variability have reduced. This trend is now becoming the norm rather than an exception, resulting in increasingly sensitive assays and lower variability in the naïve individuals evaluated during the cut point assessment, leading to much lower cut point values as shown in his paper.

As the occurrence of lower cut points increases, bioanalytical scientists need to become more comfortable with using ultra-low cut points. To successfully evaluate the propriety of ultra-low cut points (ULCPs), bioanalytical scientists need to be equipped with evolving knowledge and skills.

In addition to assessing the appropriateness of ULCP for a program, the bioanalytical community will need to reevaluate if the currently employed three-tiered approach is the correct approach to monitor ADA during clinical and preclinical trials. However, this question is outside of the scope of this paper.
