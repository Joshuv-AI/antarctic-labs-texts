HSF1 and YTHDC1 enhance DNA repair under heat stress by promoting repair-associated gene expression
Authors: Wenqing Ding, Qidong Deng, Tianyu Wang, Ruoqian Liu, Yunfan Xiang, Lulu Huang, Jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExternal stressors, particularly heat stress, induce DNA damage and genomic instability in cells. However, the mechanisms by which cells rapidly respond to such DNA damage remain largely unknown. In this study, we found that heat shock factor 1 (HSF1), the major transcription factor in the heat shock response, activated several non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway-associated genes, particularly NHEJ1, through puncta formation, thereby maintaining the stability of NHEJ pathway and alleviating the repair burden of DNA double-strand breaks caused by heat stress. Furthermore, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification and its reader YTH domain-containing protein 1 (YTHDC1) contributed to this process by promoting the splicing of NHEJ1. Knockdown of HSF1 or YTHDC1 increased nuclear intensity of phosphorylation of histone variant H2AX at Ser-139 (γH2AX) in heat-stressed cells, whereas NHEJ1 overexpression rescued this effect. Moreover, HSF1 or YTHDC1 overexpression increased NHEJ repair efficiency in heat-stressed cells, supporting the existence of an HSF1/YTHDC1–NHEJ1–DNA double-strand repair axis. Our findings reveal a mechanism by which cells repair DNA damage during the heat shock response, providing new insights into how cells maintain genomic stability under external stress conditions.

Introduction

The heat shock response is a specialized adaptive mechanism that cells have evolved to withstand extreme environmental stressors, such as hyperthermia or exposure to toxic chemicals. This response triggers the complex regulation of global gene expression and protein networks [1–3]. Investigating the regulatory mechanisms of the heat shock response helps us increase our understanding of cellular behaviors and biological processes, offering potential strategies to improve cellular resilience against hyperthermia and toxic conditions. A major consequence of heat stress is DNA damage [4–6]. Heat stress inhibits DNA topoisomerase 1 (TOP1) activity during the S phase, leading to DNA single-strand breaks (SSBs), which collide with reinitiated replication forks, further inducing DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) [7]. Moreover, heat stress induces DSBs in the G1 and G2 phases, which are marked by phosphorylation of histone variant H2AX at Ser-139 (γH2AX) [8]. Heat stress also disrupts DNA damage repair pathways. DNA polymerase β, a critical enzyme in the base excision repair (BER) pathway, is inhibited by hyperthermia, leading to a deficiency in BER under heat stress [9–13]. Furthermore, the increased resistance of tumor cells to cisplatin at 39°C–43°C suggests that heat stress may inhibit nucleotide excision repair (NER), which is responsible for repairing cisplatin-induced DNA interstrand crosslinks [14–20]. Although several studies suggest that heat shock proteins (HSPs) are involved in DNA repair [21–24], the precise mechanisms by which cells repair heat-induced DNA damage remain unknown. Understanding these mechanisms is important for determining how cells maintain genomic stability and regulate DNA repair processes during the heat shock response.

Within minutes to hours, after cells respond to external stressors, a series of rapid gene expression regulatory events occur to minimize cellular damage. Most genes are silenced, while a small subset, primarily those encoding HSPs, are highly upregulated, mainly through transcriptional activation by heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) [25, 26]. As the key transcription factor in the heat shock response, HSF1 undergoes phosphorylation and is transported into the nucleus upon sensing stress, activating the transcription of HSP genes [27, 28]. In addition, HSF1 also induces the transcription of the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) Satellite III (SATIII) gene loci, whose transcripts accumulate at their transcription sites, serving as scaffolds to form membrane-less organelles (nuclear stress bodies, nSBs) together with HSF1. These structures mediate transcriptional regulatory events, RNA splicing, and protein sequestration processes [29–35]. HSF1 is critical in regulating repair pathways for misfolded proteins caused by hyperthermia. However, its involvement in DNA damage repair remains unclear and requires further investigation.

In addition to HSF1, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression during heat stress [33, 35–37]. As the most abundant modification in mammalian messenger RNAs (mRNAs), m6A influences chromatin remodeling, RNA processing, protein translation, and other cellular processes [38–44]. m6A exerts its downstream effects through specific reader proteins, among which YTH domain-containing protein 1 (YTHDC1) is one of the most critical nuclear readers. YTHDC1 participates in RNA splicing, RNA nuclear export, and chromatin and transcriptional regulation [39–42, 44]. During heat stress, YTHDC1 is recruited to nSBs, leading to the upregulation of HSP genes and the retention of thousands of introns [33, 35]. However, whether this regulatory function is linked to DNA damage repair during heat stress remains to be determined.

Here, we demonstrate that heat shock induces the expression of a group of non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) DNA damage-related genes, particularly NHEJ1, promoting the stability of the NHEJ repair pathway to cope with the increased DNA damage caused by heat stress. Mechanistically, we found that HSF1 activates the transcription of these NHEJ-related genes. Knockdown of HSF1 caused a significant increase in nuclear γH2AX intensity, which was rescued by NHEJ1 overexpression. Furthermore, NHEJ1 mRNA transcripts colocalize with HSF1 puncta and also with H3K27ac, highlighting the important role of HSF1 puncta in regulating NHEJ1 gene expression. YTHDC1 was also present within these puncta, acting downstream of HSF1 to facilitate the upregulation of annotated genes, in part by promoting RNA splicing. Our findings reveal a new mechanism for regulating DNA damage-related pathways during heat stress, providing insight into how cells maintain genome stability in response to hyperthermia. Beyond its role in maintaining genomic stability, this mechanism may influence cancer treatment and cellular adaptation to stress. Since cancer cells heavily depend on stress response pathways, targeting the HSF1–YTHDC1–NHEJ1 axis could increase their susceptibility to therapeutic interventions. A deeper understanding of heat stress-induced DNA repair may improve hyperthermia-based cancer therapies and optimize biotechnological applications that require cellular resilience under extreme conditions.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

U2OS, A549, and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco, 11965) with 10% fetal bovine serum (GeminiBio, 900-108), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Meilunbio, MA0110), and 1% L-glutamine (Meilunbio, PWL031). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Transfection

Plasmid transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher, 11668019) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To overexpress NHEJ1, cells were transfected with the pcDNA3.0-NHEJ1 plasmid and harvested 24 h post-transfection.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection was carried out using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX ((Thermo Fisher, 13778075) as recommended. Cells were transfected with siRNA and harvested 48 h post-transfection. The siRNA sequences used in this study were:

Targeting YTHDC1:

sense: 5′ GGAGAAAGAUGGAGAACUUTT 3′,

antisense: 5′ AAGUUCUCCAUCUUUCUCCTT 3′,

Targeting SATIII:

sense: 5′ UGGAAUGGAAUGGAAUGGATT 3′,

antisense: 5′ UCCAUUCCAUUCCAUUCCATT 3′,

Targeting PAXX:

sense: 5′GGACUUCGAUGAGACCUGATT 3′,

antisense: 5′UCAGGUCUCAUCGAAGUCCTT3’.

Lentivirus packaging was used to generate stable cell lines by transfecting HEK293T cells with lentiviral packaging vectors psPAX2 and pMD2.G at a mass ratio of 1:0.75:0.25. Forty-eight hours after transfection, viral supernatants were collected and concentrated by adding 1:5 diluted Virus Concentration Buffer (0.25 g/ml PEG-8000, 750 mM NaCl), followed by rotation at 4°C overnight. The virus was then separated by centrifugation at 4°C, 4000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting pellets were resuspended in DMEM and used to infect cells in the presence of polybrene (4 μg/ml, Solarbio, H8761). The viral DMEM was replaced 6–8 h after infection. The cells were either harvested or subjected to selection by puromycin (2 μg/ml, Beyotime, ST551) 48 h after infection.

For U2OS cell lines with YTHDC1 knockdown coupled with Flag-YTHDC1 wild-type (WT) or Mut expression, pTRIPZ-Flag-YTHDC1 (WT or Mut)-shYTHDC1 vectors were used. To achieve comparable protein expression levels between WT and mutant constructs, cells were induced with doxycycline for 48 hours at 50 ng/ml and 1 μg/ml, respectively. For HSF1, NHEJ1 and IFFO1 knockdown, pLKO.1-shRNA vectors was used. The shRNA sequences used in this study were:

shYTHDC1: 5′

AGAGAAAGATGGAGAACTTAATTAGTGAAG CCACAGATGTAAATTAAGTTCTCCATCTTTCTCG 3′,

shHSF1: 5′

CCGGGCAGGTTGTTCATAGTCAGAACTCGA GTTCTGACTATGAACAACCTGCTTTTT 3′,

shNHEJ1: 5′

CCGGCCAACATTTGATTCGTCCTCTCTCGA GAGAGGACGAATCAAATGTTGGTTTTTTG 3′,

shIFFO1: 5′

CCGGCTCCAACATCAACGTGCTCAACTCGA GTTGAGCACGTTGATGTTGGAGTTTTTG 3′.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Cells were harvested, and total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), 500 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed using HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R323) to generate complementary DNA (cDNA). The cDNA was diluted 1:20 and mixed with specific primers and ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q712). RT-qPCR assays were performed in a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche), and the relative changes in gene expression were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal control.

RNA-seq

mRNA purification and RNA-seq library preparation were performed using the VAHTS Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, NR605) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Each sample was prepared from 1 µg of total RNA. Sequencing was conducted at Beijing Annoroad Medical Inspection Laboratory on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

RNA-seq data analysis

For RNA-seq analysis, raw sequencing reads from not heat-shocked (NHS) and heat-shocked (HS) samples were first assessed for quality using FastQC (v0.11.8). Low-quality bases and adapter sequences were trimmed using Trim Galore! (v0.6.7, https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/). Clean reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) [45] with default parameters. Mapping quality was evaluated using SAMtools (v1.17) [46]. Aligned reads were processed with featureCounts (v2.0.2) [47] to obtain raw read counts for annotated genes. Normalization and differential gene expression analysis were performed using DESeq2 (v1.36.0) [48] in R (v4.4.0). Genes with P-values <.05 and log2-fold change > log2(1.5) were considered to be differentially expressed. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were hierarchically clustered, grouped into three distinct expression patterns, and visualized using the ComplexHeatmap package [49]. Functional annotation of DEGs was performed using clusterProfiler package (v4.6.0) in R. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were conducted to identify enriched biological processes and pathways. Transcription factor enrichment analysis was performed using the Enrichr [50] web server with default settings, focusing on upregulated genes during heat shock.

HSF1 target gene analysis

Genes regulated by HSF1 were identified using the annotatePeaks.pl function in HOMER (v5.1) to annotate HSF1 ChIP-seq binding peaks during heat shock [51] software.

Nascent RNA analysis

Publicly available PRO-seq data from K562 cells under heat shock or control conditions were reanalyzed using deepTools to visualize HSF1-regulated damage-related genes. A 3 kb flanking region around the transcription start site (TSS) and transcription end site (TES) was included in the analysis.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) was performed according to a previously published protocol [39]. m6A was immunoprecipitated using the EpiMark N6-Methyladenosine Enrichment Kit (NEB, E1610S), and libraries were constructed using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 (Takara, 634413), both following the manufacturer’s protocols. Sequencing was conducted at Beijing Annoroad Medical Inspection Laboratory on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

MeRIP-seq analysis

For MeRIP-seq analysis, cleaned reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 [45] and converted into BAM format using SAMtools [46], followed by filtering for high-quality mapped reads. BAM files were then separated into forward and reverse strands using SAMtools. MACS2 [52] was used for peak calling on forward and reverse strands separately (threshold: -q 0.05) to identify m6A-enriched regions and quantify their peak intensities. Peaks from both strands were merged to generate the final peak set. For genes with multiple m6A peaks, methylation levels were defined as the sum of peak intensities from all identified peaks associated with that gene.

Immunofluorescence, fluorescence in situ hybridization, and confocal microscopy

Cells were seeded onto glass coverslips in 24-well plates. For immunofluorescence staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Permeabilization was performed by incubating cells in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min, followed by three additional washes in PBS. Samples were then blocked with PBS containing 1% BSA for 30 min and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-γH2AX (Abcam, ab26350), anti-H3K27ac (Abcam, ab4729), anti-HSF1 (Santa Cruz, sc-17757), anti-YTHDC1 (Abcam, ab122340), anti-Flag (Sigma–Aldrich, F1804), anti-METTL3 (Abcam, ab195352). All antibodies were used at the manufacturer’s recommended dilution ratios. After incubation, the samples were washed three times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies as follows: Goat anti-Mouse Alexa Fluor 594 (Thermo Fisher, A-11005) and Donkey anti-Rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher, A-21206) for confocal microscopy. For stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy, the secondary antibodies Abberior STAR RED (Abberior, STRED-1002) and STAR GREEN (Abberior, STGREEN-1001) were used. The samples were washed three more times in PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20, incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (1 μg/ml, Beyotime, C1002) for 5 min, and sealed.

For fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed three times with ice-cold PBS, and permeabilized with 70% ethanol at 4°C for 1 h. The samples were then washed with RNase-free hybridization buffer (10% formamide in 2× SSC) and incubated overnight at 37°C with the SATIII RNA antisense probe (5′ ATTCCATTCCATTCCATTCC 3′, 3′ biotin-labeled) diluted in hybridization buffer at a final concentration of 50 nM. The samples were then incubated with hybridization buffer at 37°C for 30 min, washed twice with 2× SSC at room temperature, blocked, incubated with antibodies, and stained with DAPI, following the immunofluorescence protocol. In addition, cells were incubated with streptavidin 488 (Invitrogen, S11223) simultaneously with secondary antibodies.

Images were acquired using a Leica STELLARIS 5 microscope or a Leica TCS SP8 STED 3X microscope for high-resolution STED imaging. Images were analyzed with ImageJ. For quantification, each dot in the statistical diagrams represented a single nucleus. The mean intensities of the indicated proteins were measured; the intensities of the control groups were normalized to 1. Other experimental groups were normalized relative to the control group.

Live cell imaging and FRAP assays

Cells were seeded onto glass-bottomed dishes and transiently transfected with pEGFP-GFP-YTHDC1 vectors. The cells were then imaged using a Nikon Live SR CSU W1 microscope 48 h post-transfection.

For fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assays, condensates within the cells were photobleached using a 488 nm laser at 100% intensity at the designated region. After the indicated recovery time, images were acquired and analyzed with ImageJ. Pre-bleach intensities were normalized to 1, with subsequent time points normalized relative to pre-bleach values.

NHEJ reporter assay and flow cytometry analysis

NHEJ reporter assays were conducted in EJ5-GFP-U2OS as previously described [53]. EJ5-GFP-U2OS cells were transfected with NHEJ1 overexpression vector for 48 hours, or knockdown shRNA or siRNA (HSF1, YTHDC1, NHEJ1, IFFO1 and PAXX) for 72 hours. Concurrently, cells were co-transfected with the pCMV-I-SceI-mCherry vector for 48 hours. Subsequently, cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and injected into a CytoFLEX LX (Beckman) for flow cytometry. For each analysis, 5 × 10³ mCherry-positive cells were collected. Each experiment was performed in three independent replicates. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software. The NHEJ efficiency was quantified as the ratio of GFP-positive/mCherry-positive double-positive cells to mCherry-positive single-positive cells.

Transcription factor enrichment profiling via enrichr

Transcription factor enrichment analysis was performed using the Enrichr [50] web server with default settings, focusing on upregulated genes during heat shock. Transcription factor binding enrichment analysis was performed using the Enrichr web server (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) [50]. Two gene lists were used as input: (i) genes significantly upregulated under heat shock [P-value <.05, log2FC > log2(1.5)], and (ii) a curated subset of DNA damage-related genes from the upregulated set. These gene lists were independently queried against the ENCODE TF ChIP-seq 2015 gene set library, which contains target gene sets derived from ChIP-seq experiments across diverse cell and tissue types. Enrichment significance was assessed using Fisher’s exact test, and Enrichr’s combined score (c-score), which integrates both P-value and z-score, was used to rank enriched terms. Results were considered statistically significant if the adjusted P-value was <.05.

Genome browser visualization

Genome browser visualization was performed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV, version 2.16.0). All genomic signal tracks, including RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, MeRIP-seq, were visualized on the human reference genome hg38. Normalized coverage tracks (bigWig format) were generated using bamCoverage from deepTools (v3.5.0) with the --normalizeUsing RPKM option.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 was used for data analysis. For all statistical analyses, an unpaired t-test was performed. Statistical significance was defined as: *P < .05, **P < .01, ***P < .001, and ****P < .0001. P-values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Upregulated DNA damage-related genes, particularly NHEJ1, promote DNA repair upon heat stress

To directly assess DNA damage during the heat shock response, we performed immunofluorescence staining on U2OS cells subjected or not subjected to heat shock, using γH2AX as a marker for DSBs. γH2AX intensity significantly increased following heat stress (Fig. 1A), indicating severe DNA damage and suggesting a requirement for DNA repair. Time-course analysis further revealed that γH2AX levels rose sharply within the first hour of heat shock, followed by a more gradual and sustained increase from 1 to 6 h (Supplementary Fig. S1A). To explore potential mechanisms involved in DNA damage repair during the heat shock response, we conducted RNA-seq analysis of U2OS cells with (HS) or without heat shock (NHS). DEGs were classified as upregulated (HS/NHS upregulated) or downregulated (HS/NHS downregulated) (Fig. 1B). The upregulated genes were highly associated with protein folding pathways (Fig. 1C), consistent with previous reports [54]. These genes were also remarkably enriched for DNA damage repair processes, particularly the NHEJ pathway (Fig. 1C). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed a significant enrichment of NHEJ pathway genes among HS/NHS upregulated genes (Fig. 1D). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis showed that multiple key scaffolding and accessory proteins in the NHEF pathway—including NHEJ1, PAXX, XRCC4, IFFO1, and APLF—were markedly upregulated upon HS (Fig. 1E). In addition, assays with chromosomally integrated NHEJ reporters [53] demonstrated an increase in NHEJ repair activity following heat stress (Fig. 1F). These findings indicate that U2OS cells likely activate the NHEJ pathway in response to heat stress-induced DNA damage.

Among the genes upregulated in response to heat stress, we specifically focused on NHEJ1, which has been reported to play a crucial role in the NHEJ pathway [55–58]. Western blot analysis confirmed an increase in NHEJ1/XLF protein levels (Fig. 1G). To evaluate the impact of NHEJ1 on DNA damage levels under heat stress, we knocked down NHEJ1 using shRNA and overexpressed it via transient plasmid transfection. As expected, NHEJ1 knockdown attenuates NHEJ efficiency (Fig. 1H), whereas its overexpression reversed this effect (Fig. 1I). Correspondingly, NHEJ1 overexpression significantly reduced γH2AX intensities (Fig. 1J), and a significant negative correlation was observed between NHEJ1/XLF protein levels and γH2AX intensity (Pearson’s r = −0.5816; Supplementary Fig. S1B), consistent with its role in promoting DNA damage repair during heat stress. Immunofluorescence localization assays confirmed that NHEJ1/XLF maintains strong colocalization with Lig4 under both NHS and HS conditions (Supplementary Fig. S1C), supporting its constitutive role in DNA repair complexes. In addition, we found that simultaneous knockdown of NHEJ1 with either IFFO1 or PAXX further impaired NHEJ repair efficiency and elevated γH2AX intensity (Fig. 1K and L), indicating synergistic roles among these accessory factors in the DNA damage response. Overall, our findings suggest that a cluster of NHEJ pathway-related genes, particularly NHEJ1, are upregulated during the heat shock response, for heat stress-induced severe DNA damage.

HSF1 regulates NHEJ1 expression during heat stress

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms underlying the upregulation of NHEJ pathway-related genes, we reanalyzed previously published PRO-seq data [59]. The nascent RNA levels of damage-related genes were higher in HS cells than in NHS cells (Supplementary Fig. S2A), suggesting that transcriptional activation may drive the upregulation of these HS/NHS upregulated damage-related genes. Since transcription factors typically mediate transcriptional activation, we applied the Enrichr method [50] to predict candidate transcription factors regulating these genes. Among all predicted transcription factors, HSF1 was predominantly enriched in the HS/NHS upregulated damage-related gene group and the total HS/NHS upregulated gene group (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S2B). Since previous studies showed that HSF1 activates the transcription of numerous genes during the heat shock response, particularly HSP genes [25, 26], we further reanalyzed published HSF1 chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled with high-throughput sequencing (ChIP-seq) data [59]. Changes in HSF1 binding intensity positively correlated with transcriptional alterations in response to heat shock (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Moreover, similar to HSP genes, a substantial number of DNA damage-related genes exhibited increased HSF1 binding under HS conditions (Fig. 2B). Genome browser (IGV) view visualization consistently revealed a significant increase in HSF1 binding signals at the NHEJ1 locus in HS cells compared with NHS cells (Fig. 2C). These results suggest that HSF1 is an upstream transcriptional regulator of DNA damage-related genes, including NHEJ1.

To verify the role of HSF1 in regulating NHEJ pathway genes, we knocked down HSF1 in U2OS cells (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Under heat stress, the mRNA levels of key accessory factors, including NHEJ1, IFFO1, and PAXX, were significantly decreased (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. S2E). Consistently, HSF1 knockdown markedly impaired NHEJ repair efficiency specifically under HS (Fig. 2E). In addition, HSF1 knockdown led to a sustained and pronounced increase in nuclear γH2AX intensity throughout the entire 6-h heat shock period, whereas NHEJ1 overexpression restored γH2AX levels to baseline (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. S2F), further supporting the role of HSF1 in promoting NHEJ1-mediated DNA damage repair.

In response to heat shock, HSF1 activates lncRNA SATIII transcription and forms regulatory nSBs [29]. Therefore, we hypothesized that HSF1 may regulate damage-related genes through nSBs. Immunofluorescence and FISH revealed strong colocalization of NHEJ1 mRNA with HSF1 under heat stress, displaying either a “fully colocalizing” or a “surrounding” pattern, with to the latter being statistically more prevalent (Fig. 2G). Moreover, HSF1 knockdown substantially reduces the formation of nuclear NHEJ1 RNA puncta and disrupts their colocalization under heat shock, indicating that this colocalization is functionally dependent on HSF1 (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, the active-transcription marker H3K27ac colocalized highly with HSF1 puncta (Fig. 2I) and NHEJ1 mRNA (Supplementary Fig. S2G), indicating transcriptional activation within these puncta. HSF1 puncta are spatially distinct from NHEJ1/XLF protein, suggesting that DNA damage repair is not directly executed within these nSBs (Supplementary Fig. S2H). Collectively, these results indicate that the HS/NHS upregulated damage-related genes, particularly NHEJ1, are transcriptionally regulated by HSF1 and exhibit a strong spatial relationship with HSF1 puncta during the heat shock response.

YTHDC1 localizes in the HSF1 puncta in an m6A-recognition dependent manner and contributes to DNA damage repair during heat stress

We next investigated whether additional components within HSF1 puncta participate in the transcriptional regulation of DNA damage-related genes. m6A RNA modification and its reader protein YTHDC1 localize to heat-induced nuclear puncta and are implicated in chromatin regulation and RNA processing. Therefore, we hypothesized that m6A modification may contribute to the transcriptional regulation of genes within HSF1 puncta. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that YTHDC1 and the major m6A methyltransferase Methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) colocalize with HSF1 puncta under heat stress (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S3A). In addition, FRAP assays suggested the liquid-like properties of YTHDC1 puncta (Supplementary Fig. S3B), consistent with previous reports that YTHDC1 can form phase-separated condensates [33, 60]. To determine whether YTHDC1 puncta formation depends on m6A-recognition, we knocked down YTHDC1 using siRNA in U2OS cells and rescued its expression with Flag-tagged WT or W377A mutant YTHDC1 [61]; the latter was defective in m6A binding (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Under heat stress, WT YTHDC1 fully restores its colocalization with HSF1 puncta under heat shock, whereas the YTHDC1 mutant failed to do so (Fig. 3B), indicating that m6A recognition is essential for recruiting YTHDC1 to HSF1 nuclear bodies during the heat shock response.

To further investigate the relationship between HSF1, YTHDC1, and METTL3 during the heat shock response, we examined their puncta formation at multiple time points during heat shock treatment. HSF1 rapidly formed puncta within 30 min of heat shock, which dissipated shortly thereafter (Fig. 3C). By contrast, YTHDC1 and METTL3 puncta began to emerge after one hour of heat shock. Subsequently, METTL3 puncta dissipated rapidly, while YTHDC1 puncta persisted for over 6 h, even following recovery (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S3D). This temporal difference suggests that HSF1, YTHDC1, and METTL3 occupy distinct positions along the heat shock response timeline. The prolonged retention of YTHDC1 in particular suggests it is more likely incorporated into durable RNA condensates, analogous to the reported RNA-mediated assemblies [62]. Interestingly, YTHDC1 and HSF1 puncta did not fully colocalize in a higher resolution. To resolve their spatial relationship, we used STED microscopy to obtain super-resolution images (Supplementary Fig. S3E). YTHDC1 and HSF1 were found to reside within the same condensate but their signals do not fully overlap, exhibiting “surrounding” or “adjacent” configurations with SATIII RNA overlapping with both proteins. HSF1 knockdown led to the loss of YTHDC1 puncta formation, whereas YTHDC1 knockdown had minimal effect on HSF1 puncta formation (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S3F), indicating that HSF1 acts upstream of YTHDC1 in puncta formation.

We next investigated whether m6A modification and its regulatory factors are involved in regulating the expression of DNA damage genes. Analysis of MeRIP-seq data from U2OS cells revealed that 81.9% of the HS/NHS upregulated genes contained m6A peaks, compared with only 57.5% of the HS/NHS downregulated genes (Fig. 3E). When we narrowed down the gene cluster to HS/NHS upregulated DNA damage-related genes, up to 94.6% of the genes were m6A-modified, including NHEJ1 (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, among all m6A-modified genes, the m6A peak intensity was significantly higher in HS/NHS upregulated genes than in HS/NHS downregulated genes (Fig. 3G). These results suggest that m6A modification may be pivotal in DNA damage repair during heat stress. To directly assess this, we knocked down YTHDC1 and observed a significant increase in γH2AX intensity under heat stress (Fig. 3H). Importantly, WT YTHDC1 effectively rescue the elevated γH2AX levels, whereas the YTHDC1 mutant failed to rescue this phenotype (Fig. 3I). These results confirmed that YTHDC1 contributes to heat shock-induced DNA damage repair in an m6A-dependent manner.

YTHDC1 regulates the splicing of NHEJ1 transcripts to upregulate its expression

HSF1 and YTHDC1 puncta did not colocalize with γH2AX foci (Supplementary Fig. S4A), indicating that these condensates were not directly involved in DNA damage repair in situ. Given the role of YTHDC1 in regulating gene expression [39, 40, 44], we performed RNA-seq in heat-shocked U2OS cells transfected with control or YTHDC1 siRNAs. GSEA revealed a significant decrease in the expression of HS/NHS upregulated genes upon YTHDC1 knockdown under heat stress (Fig. 4A). RT-qPCR further confirmed that YTHDC1 knockdown significantly reduced mRNA levels of NHEJ1, IFFO1, and PAXX (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S4B), suggesting that YTHDC1 regulates the upregulation of key NHEJ accessory factors during the heat shock response. Consistently, western blot analysis showed a marked reduction in NHEJ1/XLF protein levels upon YTHDC1 knockdown (Fig. 4C). Moreover, our immunofluorescence data showed that under heat stress, either YTHDC1 knockdown or treatment with METTL3 inhibitor STM2457 significantly increased γH2AX intensity, which could be effectively rescued by NHEJ1 overexpression (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. S4C and D). Together with the significant decrease in NHEJ levels under YTHDC1 knockdown (Fig. 4E), these findings collectively confirm a positive regulatory role of YTHDC1 in the NHEJ repair pathway.

Given the regulatory function of YTHDC1 in RNA splicing [44], we next examined whether YTHDC1 contributed to the splicing of HS/NHS upregulated genes. Intron levels of these genes significantly increased following YTHDC1 knockdown under heat stress (Fig. 4F), suggesting that YTHDC1 promotes their splicing. Intron-targeted RT-qPCR showed that expression of intron 2, 4, 5, and 7 of NHEJ1 significantly increased after YTHDC1 knockdown (Fig. 4G). To quantitatively assess the impact of YTHDC1 on NHEJ1 RNA splicing, we applied a refined splicing index [63], which confirmed that YTHDC1 knockdown significantly reduces NHEJ1 splicing efficiency (Fig. 4H). Treatment with STM2457 effectively reduced m6A modification on NHEJ1 mRNA and decreased NHEJ1 splicing efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S4E and F), indicating that recognition of m6A by YTHDC1 is essential for efficient NHEJ1 splicing during the heat shock response.

The HSF1/YTHDC1–NHEJ1 axis in heat shock response is a conserved mechanism

To extend our findings, we performed a parallel set of experiments in a distinct cellular model, A549 human lung carcinoma cells. In A549 cells, heat shock treatment robustly triggered the DNA damage response, as evidenced by a marked increase in γH2AX intensity (Fig. 5A). Concomitantly, we observed a significant upregulation of NHEJ1 at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5B), mirroring our initial findings in U2OS cells. to directly test whether NHEJ1 upregulation plays a functional role in the DNA damage response, we overexpressed NHEJ1 in A549 cells. Compared to control cells, NHEJ1 overexpression markedly reduced heat shock-induced γH2AX intensity (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, HSF1 knockdown attenuated NHEJ1 upregulation and exacerbated DNA damage upon heat shock (Fig. 5D and E), notably, this increase in DNA damage was rescued by NHEJ1 overexpression (Fig. 5E). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed the heat shock-induced nuclear colocalization of HSF1 and YTHDC1, forming distinct puncta that were largely absent under basal conditions (Fig. 5F). Consistently, knockdown of YTHDC1 in A549 cells markedly suppressed NHEJ1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels by impairing the splicing of NHEJ1 pre-mRNA (Fig. 5G and H). Accordingly, YTHDC1 knockdown led to increased DNA damage, a phenotype that was similarly rescued by NHEJ1 complementation (Fig. 5I). Taken together, the consistency of these results obtained from two distinct cell models strongly supports that the HSF1/YTHDC1–NHEJ1 axis represents a broadly conserved regulatory mechanism that coordinates the DNA damage response under heat stress.

Discussion

In response to stressors such as hyperthermia or toxic chemicals, cells initiate a series of stress responses. On one hand, a large group of genes are silenced, including the genes related to metabolism, cell cycle, and protein modification, etc. [64], possibly in purpose of protecting those genes from wrongly expressing. On the other hand, HSF1 is activated and induces the expression of HSPs, facilitating the refolding of misfolded proteins. In this study, we identified a previously unrecognized mechanism by which HSF1 regulates DNA damage repair during the heat shock response, providing new insights into how cells maintain genome stability under extreme stress conditions, as well as extending the functions of HSF1 beyond its canonical role as an activator of HSPs. Additionally, the rapid response of HSF1 puncta and the m6A responding pathway inside the puncta also suggest that such an HSF1–RNA–m6A axis might play roles in other rapid-response-needed conditions.

In parallel, we have highlighted the critical role of YTHDC1 in the heat shock response, where it facilitates the splicing of the NHEJ1 mRNA. YTHDC1 exerts diverse and sometimes context-dependent effects on gene regulation across different cell types and biological pathways. Since enhancer RNAs recruit YTHDC1 within BRD4 puncta to facilitate transcription [60], its role in the heat shock response may be similarly diverse. In addition, prior works have described an HSF1-SATIII RNA-YTHDC1 axis, in which HSF1 activates the transcription of SATIII, and the m6A-modified SATIII RNA further recruits YTHDC1 to function in splicing or transcription regulation [35]. Beyond this established axis, the potential for more extensive and complex interactions between HSF1 and YTHDC1 within the condensates remains an open question. In this study, as a major component inside the HSF1 puncta, YTHDC1 is yet found not completely colocalizing with HSF1 and SATIII RNA, but in some cases in a “surrounding or adjacent” pattern, which is evocative of the phenomenon of molecular self-demixing as described by Cardona [65]. The molecular basis of this distinctive spatial architecture and its potential functional implications remained important questions for future investigation. Since HSF1 has been reported to form distinct kinds of puncta, which exhibit varied impacts on transcription [66, 67], it is necessary to search whether YTHDC1 also function in diverse ways within these condensates when cells are facing external stressors. Furthermore, given that the EJ5-GFP reporter measures total end-joining events, it remains possible that HSF1 and YTHDC1 may also participate in regulating other DNA repair pathways under HS conditions, whose activities contribute to the observed signal.

Numerous research has established that hyperthermia suppresses homologous recombination (HR) and alternative NHEJ (alt-NHEJ) pathways [68–72]. While the roles of HR and alt-NHEJ under hyperthermia are relatively well-characterized, the function of classical NHEJ (c-NHEJ) has remained less explored. Our work reveals that the HSF1/YTHDC1 specifically activate several key scaffolding and accessory proteins of NHEJ pathway during heat shock and contributes to DNA damage repair. This finding aligns with reports of DNA-PKcs activation under HS [73] and supports a model in which c-NHEJ serves as a compensatory mechanism when other repair pathways are compromised. Given that several post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation and lactylation have been shown to regulate NHEJ1/XLF activity [74–76], it is plausible that similar regulatory modifications are involved in activating the c-NHEJ pathway during the cellular response to heat stress. The role of the c-NHEJ pathway in hyperthermia, and specifically how it is activated, warrants further attention and exploration.

Notably, local hyperthermia has been combined with radiotherapy or chemotherapy as a treatment for tumors for decades [77]. Heat stress is reported amplifying the therapeutic effect of the radiation or chemicals, possibly through leading to DNA damage or inhibiting several DNA repair pathways. Our study reveals a method for cells to promote NHEJ repair pathway to ensure genomic stability in toxic conditions. These results lead to a potential way for promoting the local hyperthermia therapy—targeting and inhibiting the formation of HSF1 or YTHDC1 puncta that form during heat shock response. Future studies validating these targets in vivo could pave the way for combinatorial regimens that maximize tumor sensitization to thermal therapy.
