Interpreting tissue stiffening with lung tumorigenesis by imaging architectural resembling of extracellular matrix components
Authors: Chuncheng Wang, Shuhao Qian, Wenyue Li, Lingxi Zhou, Lingmei Chen, Jia Meng, Rushan Jiang, Bo Niu, Ke Sun, Zhihua Ding, Xiaozhao Wang, Shuangmu Zhuo, Zhiyi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer leads to a series of physiological abnormalities. The remodeling of extracellular matrix (especially elastin and collagen fibers) has been drawing increasing attention as it is suggested to be a hallmark of tumorigenesis. However, the interaction between these crucial matrix components, together with their relationship to mechanical changes, remains poorly understood. Here, we develop a quantitative multiphoton microscopy system to elucidate the relationship between tissue stiffening and elastin-collagen interplay in lung cancer. Based on label-free images of both fibers, we establish a metric termed resemblance metric (RM) to characterize their interaction by quantifying the similarity of their morpho-structural distributions. Specifically, RM is found to increase with lung tumorigenesis, and exhibits superior sensitivity in identifying human lung cancer through ex vivo quantitative imaging. Nanoindentation results suggest a strong correlation between tissue stiffness and inter-channel interaction, notably greater than that between stiffness and any individual morpho-structural feature of either fiber type. Finally, the translational potential of RM-based imaging is demonstrated through tumor boundary identification via in vivo imaging within a mouse model harboring human lung cancer.

Introduction

Lung cancer has become the most frequently diagnosed cancer worldwide in recent years, persistently being the leading cause of cancer-related deaths1,2. There are mainly two histologic subtypes of lung cancer: small cell lung cancer (SCLC, ~15% of total cases) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC, ~85% of total cases), with the latter being less invasive and having a lower growth rate3–7. However, NSCLC patients still have a poor prognosis: over half of patients die within one year post diagnosis, and the 5-year survival rate is less than 20%; thus, accurate staging is vital to clinical therapies and scientific research8,9. While the surgical specimen has been the gold standard for NSCLC staging8, the classification system based on characteristics of pathological tumor, node and metastasis (TNM) is also widely accepted and used10. Meanwhile, tumor evolution is often accompanied by a significant macroscopic feature of extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffening, which is considered to be a major cause of poor tumor treatment outcomes. Accordingly, there have been emerging works focusing on improvements in the therapeutic strategies for tumors by reducing the hardness of ECM, including immune checkpoint blockade therapy11, adoptive cell therapy12, oncolytic virus therapy13 and therapeutic cancer vaccines14. Besides, softening the ECM through hyaluronic acid depletion could also improve perfusion of liver metastatic colorectal cancer and systemic chemotherapy response in mice15. However, current literature reporting mechanisms of ECM stiffening is limited, scattered, and often contradictory across contexts, due to complex patterns of genetic expression and intricate signaling pathways of ECM components. Therefore, an in-depth and precise understanding of the mechanism of matrix stiffening is still challenging, and related studies may inform innovative approaches for more effective tumor therapies.

Elastin and collagen fibers are two essential components of the ECM. Collagen fibers provide stiffness and strength for connective tissues, offering rigid support against tissue deformation, while elastin fibers contribute resilience, preserving tissue tenacity16,17. Current studies have explored the relationship between matrix stiffening and elastin or collagen during tumorigenesis. Nevertheless, most studies focused on the molecular level, such as signaling pathways18–20, with few studies from a sub-cellular perspective, especially from the morphology perspective. Moreover, no existing work currently explains the relationship between elastin-collagen interplay and matrix stiffening. Fortunately, advancements in optical technology, especially multiphoton imaging technology, enable us to obtain images of elastin and collagen fibers at sub-micrometer resolution under label-free conditions21. Combined with highly precise quantitative characterization, it is possible for us to study elastin-collagen interactions. Furthermore, the label-free advantage also paves the way for further in vivo imaging.

Structural remodeling of ECM components is considered a hallmark of tumorigenesis, playing an important role in application scenarios such as tumor identification, staging, grading, and prognosis22–24. Among all ECM components, elastin and collagen fibers are two major parts attracting rising attention as biomarkers of ECM abnormalities in tumorigenesis25,26. For collagen fibers, a widely recognized application for this hallmark is the establishment of tumor-associated collagen signatures (TACSs) in breast cancer models, which is closely related to tumor invasiveness and prognosis27,28. This application in the breast cancer model is a typical and representative study in the field of non-labeling biomarkers of tumorigenesis on ECM components, especially on the abundant and archetypal organization of collagen fibers in breast tissues. Besides, lysyl oxidase (LOX)-mediated collagen crosslinking stiffens breast ECM and promotes mammary tumor progression18. There are also ECM collagen fibers exhibiting different remodeling features along tumorigenesis in other cancer models. For example, in the ECM of pancreatic cancer, the collagen fibers were observed to be more oriented within the main tumor volume and were characterized as a dense capsule that surrounded the tumor mass, with the latter related to a higher elastic modulus in nanomechanical properties29. This collagenous capsule could serve as a biomarker to distinguish lesions of pancreatic tumors from normal pancreas, facilitating optimal margin identification in resection surgery30. In the prostate cancer model, collagen synthesis within the whole cancerous prostate was observed to be up-regulated, while collagen degradation at the cancerous foci was also noticed to be elevated31. Further, the alignment of collagen fibers for higher-malignancy-grade prostate cancer also increased32. However, collagen behavior induced by tumorigenesis might vary with cancer types. For instance, second harmonic generation (SHG) images revealed that collagen fibers within normal human tissues were observed to be more aligned than those within high-grade colonic dysplasia tissues33. In lung cancer model, collagen crosslinking mediated by LOX in lung cancer model promotes tumor cell proliferation and metastasis34. Apart from collagen variation, the pathological focal deposition of aberrant elastin fibers in abnormal amounts within the tissue (known as elastosis) indicated improved prognosis in low-stage tumors and was more commonly associated with well-differentiated tumors than moderately differentiated type within the adenocarcinoma model25. While in the surgical treatment of lung cancer, there is an urgent need for highly sensitive methods that can accurately identify tumor boundaries to define the scope of the surgery. However, current clinical methods for intraoperative guidance, including minimally invasive modalities such as video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS)35 and robot-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS)36, carry risks of potential intraoperative complications and involve high costs in both time and robotic consumables. Meanwhile, technologies for suspected lung cancer patients examining mediastinal lymph node metastasis such as endobronchial ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EBUS-FNA) and transesophageal endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) require training and have limited sensitivity37. Unfortunately, pinpointing the tumor boundary remains a challenge for existing technologies. Therefore, the label-free, highly-sensitive imaging of elastin and collagen based on multiphoton imaging technology, and corresponding structural remodeling information obtained thereby, are expected to offer promising possibilities for the accurate delineation of the boundaries of lung cancer for surgery.

In this study, we constructed a quantitative MPM imaging platform, selected optimal imaging channels based on the fluorescence spectral characteristics of elastin and collagen, and acquired images of these two ECM components simultaneously in human lung cancer samples in a label-free manner. Subsequently, voxel-wise morpho-structural features were extracted in a truly 3D context. We then developed a parameter to reflect the interplay between the two fibrous components along tumorigenesis. Our technique revealed that matrix stiffening correlated more significantly with this interplay than any individual morpho-structural feature of either elastin or collagen fiber in lung tumorigenesis, providing important implications for the exploration of signaling pathways and related therapeutic strategies. We then successfully discriminated between ex vivo normal and cancerous human lung tissues with this method. Finally, we demonstrated the platform’s efficacy in precisely identifying tumor margins via in vivo imaging in a murine model harboring human lung cancer, highlighting its promise for scientific research and clinical applications.

Results

MPM imaging of main components in lung ECM

To bridge micro and macro features of lung tissues during tumorigenesis, we performed quantitative MPM imaging to extract morpho-structural features of elastin and collagen fibers in lung ECM and nanoindentation testing to assess mechanical properties of decellularized lung tissue, as detailed in the workflow of this study (Fig. 1a). Notably, the time efficiency of MPM imaging was greatly improved through parallel image acquisition by collecting 525 ± 25 nm two-photon excited fluorescence (TPEF) signal from elastin fibers and 400 ± 10 nm SHG signal from collagen fibers by excitation at 800 nm, which also ensured minimum crosstalk according to their fluorescence spectra (Fig. 1a). Following biopsy, the fresh human lung specimen was cut into two pieces. One piece firstly underwent ex vivo MPM imaging to harvest signals from elastin and collagen fibers and subsequently went through mechanical characterization, and the other piece was stained by hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) to be assessed by pathologists for validation of the sample type (Supplementary Fig. 1). For mechanical characterization, the imaged piece was decellularized and cryo-sectioned for nanoindentation testing of matrix stiffness immediately after MPM imaging. As can be seen from reconstructed 3D images and 2D closed-ups of both components, elastin and collagen fibers in normal tissues showed loose and relatively more independent distributions (Fig. 1b), while those in cancer tissues appeared to be tangled or twisted with each other (Fig. 1c). Inter-channel resemblance metric (RM) characterization based on multi-parametric quantification

Lung ECM components included elastin fibers providing elasticity and collagen fibers offering tensile strength, constituting approximately over 24% and 60% of dry lung weight38, respectively. Although the relationship between tumorigenesis and ECM environment has been widely studied27,28, few studies have explored the tumor-induced elastin-collagen interaction and relative dysregulations in ECM. Inspired by the visible morpho-structural similarity (Fig. 1) of elastin and collagen fibers in the ECM within the human lung cancer model, we developed in this study a parameter termed resemblance metric (RM) aiming at resolving the interplay between these two components in a truly 3D context. The generation of RM was based on multi-parametric quantification, which offered comprehensive morpho-structural features of fiber-like structures, including local coverage, orientation, alignment, and waviness, achieving voxel-wise accuracy while maintaining high time efficiency. Specifically, we conducted pair-wise multicollinearity diagnostics for elastin and collagen fibers’ morphological parameters and acquired all Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results supported the lack of multicollinearity for all morphological parameters (i.e.,|r| < 0.7)39, indicating that there were no offending variables that needed to be removed from the establishment of RM.

The computational pipeline of RM was concisely illustrated with simulated elastin (magenta) and collagen (green) fibers (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3). The two types of fibers in separate channels firstly underwent multi-parametric morphological assessment to obtain parameter maps (Fig. 2a, merged inter-channel maps were for illustration purposes only), in which very few outlying singular waviness values at the edges and branches would not cause much deviation to the final RM map and subsequent statistical analysis. Secondly, the coordinates and minimum distance of non-background pixels (NBPs) from an NBP in one channel to the nearest NBP in the other channel were sought out to generate a minimum distance map, with a respective coordinate index map of minimum distance created for either fiber type. And the nearest NBP parameter maps were generated according to the nearest NBP coordinates and parameter maps for each channel (Fig. 2b). To be noted, in the waviness column of Fig. 2b, the pixel value did not correspond to the waviness feature of the fiber itself, but corresponded to the waviness of its closest fiber pixel from the other channel. That was why all “waviness” values in the first row (elastin mask) of Fig. 2b were low: the collagen fibers in the other channel were all straight. Thirdly, subtraction and absolute value conversion were applied to each channel between its multi-parametric maps and NBP parameter maps to produce difference maps, followed by a fusion operation across channels (Fig. 2c, detailed in “Methods”). Later, the fused difference maps of each parameter were scored with different scoring approaches according to characteristics of parameters, with indicative scoring function curves illustrated (Fig. 2d, detailed in “Methods”). Finally, the scored maps were performed with accumulative Hadamard product and pseudo-color coding to generate and visualize the final RM map (Fig. 2e), in which the inter-channel fibers with a high resemblance degree (close to 1 and red in RM map in the zoomed-in inset) were in great contrast with those from other less similar regions (close to 0 and blue in RM map). With this algorithm, the inter-fiber architectural resemblance can be intuitively perceived and easily adaptive to subsequent analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4). Mechanical properties correlating with elastin-collagen resembling in lung tumorigenesis

To examine the differences in mechanical properties between normal and cancerous lung tissues, as represented by Young’s modulus (with a higher value indicating higher stiffness), we performed nanoindentation measurements after tissue decellularization (Fig. 4a, see Supplementary Fig. 10 for details on the testing process and examples). Specifically, we utilized indentation loading to map the stiffness of 19 normal and 17 cancerous samples (double-blinded), with two typical color-coded 200 × 200 µm stiffness matrices shown in Fig. 4b. Compared with the nearly imperceptible variations in normal tissues with Young’s modulus of 0.99 ± 0.17 kPa (mean ± SD), the stiffness map of cancerous tissues presented a much higher Young’s modulus level with more heterogeneous distribution (17 ± 12 kPa). This intuitive observation revealed that the lung cancer tissue exhibited higher stiffness and heterogeneity compared with the normal tissue. Furthermore, the corresponding distribution histograms of these representative normal and cancer stiffness maps with overlaid Gaussian fitting curves and relative Gaussian fit coefficients are shown in Fig. 4c, which quantitatively characterize the stiffness distribution features. In detail, the normal tissue had an overall lower and more concentrated distribution, whereas the cancerous tissue had a notably higher and more scattered distribution (with 12-fold higher mean μ and 41-fold higher standard deviation σ) in stiffness, indicating that the normal and cancerous tissue samples differed significantly in both average stiffness and heterogeneity. Subsequently, mean and SD of Young’s modulus were obtained, which further corroborated the distinctions between these two groups (Fig. 4d). Moreover, we performed least squares linear fitting to analyze the correlation between the average Young’s modulus and RM in normal and cancer groups (Fig. 4e), which showed a strong linear relationship with R2 values of 0.67 and 0.82, respectively. RM applied to the identification of the tumor boundary via in vivo imaging

The application of RM-based MPM imaging was then demonstrated via in vivo imaging of a mouse model harboring human lung cancer. In total, 4 mice with induced human lung cancer and 4 normal mice as controls were involved in this study, with each mouse in the cancer group providing 3 cancer and 3 boundary image stacks, and each mouse in the control group yielding 3 normal image stacks. A total of 36 image stacks were eventually acquired (Fig. 5a, n = 12 image stacks for each tissue type). To highlight the translational potential of our method, we performed intravital imaging of mice under anesthesia, and efforts were made to minimize motion artifacts related to breathing (Fig. 5b, see Methods for more experimental details). Following image acquisition and RM-related 3D quantification (Fig. 5c), derived metrics including RM mean, RM SD and RM DDV were extracted for classifying different tissue types. These derived metrics were significantly different among the three groups, validating the sensitivity of RM-based assessments and revealing that the boundary region also underwent alterations in response to interaction between ECM components, similar to those observed in the tumor region but at a different level (Fig. 5d). By combining these metrics, distinct tissue types were successfully identified, as shown in the scatter plot with 70% coverage ellipsoids (Fig. 5e). Discussion

In this study, we developed the RM-enabled quantitative MPM imaging technique for label-free, high-resolution architectural mapping of elastin and collagen fibers in lung ECM. Using spectrum-based wavelength optimization, simultaneous submicron volumetric imaging of elastin and collagen fibers was achieved, which greatly improved imaging efficiency while minimizing signal crosstalk and avoiding exogenous agents. Using this platform, we analyzed the morpho-structural distribution of elastin and collagen fibers within the lung cancer model, and observed an overall higher resemblance level in the cancer group. This finding was successfully applied to the classification of ex vivo normal and cancerous tissues and the identification of the tumor boundary tissues from in vivo imaging. Additionally, our approach provided new insights into the mechanistic interpretation of matrix stiffening at the subcellular ECM component level during lung tumorigenesis. The high sensitivity and accuracy of our method supported its potential for clinical translation, particularly in offering precise guidance for tumor boundary identification during surgical excision.

We developed a parameter resemblance metric (RM) to quantify the overall morphological and organizational resemblance of two key fibrous structures within the ECM, inspired by a previous study investigating the interactions between distinct cellular cytoskeleton components40. However, this prior work merely considered limited features of fibrous structures, which led to a loss of morpho-structural information and a subsequent reduction in accuracy. To address this limitation, we optimized the algorithm by replenishing key features, including local coverage, directional variance and waviness to generate a more accurate, integrated and comprehensive map of elastin-collagen resemblance and extended the quantification algorithm to a 3D context. This optimized algorithm exhibits higher sensitivity, as evidenced by a stronger correlation with mechanical properties (Fig. 4e) than any single morpho-structural feature of either fiber type in normal and cancerous tissues (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Considering tumorigenesis, Young’s modulus, a vital indicator describing the mechanical properties of biological samples, is often used to distinguish tumors from normal tissues, especially in clinical breast palpation examinations due to abnormal in situ hardness in tumors41,42. This stiffening alteration in breast tissues was attributed to increased deposition and crosslinking of collagen in ECM during tumor formation43, with similar conclusions reported in studies concerning lung adenocarcinoma44 and prostate cancer45, sharing a common tendency to promote tumor proliferation and invasion46. In lung cancer models, cancer cells were generally softer than normal cells47,48, whereas the Young’s modulus of the ECM surrounding lung tumor cells was often higher than that in normal tissues49–51. In this context, we performed nanoindentation experiments on decellularized lung tissues to investigate the relationship between tissue stiffening and elastin-collagen resemblance. We found that RM exhibited a strong linear correlation with the mean Young’s modulus readouts in both normal and cancer groups, respectively, suggesting that high elastin-collagen resemblance might represent an additional key factor contributing to ECM stiffening during lung tumorigenesis, apart from the present well-established mechanism of collagen deposition and crosslinking. Conservatively, the observed morpho-structural convergence of elastin and collagen in tumor ECM might promote cancer progression and facilitate post-metastatic colonization. This might be driven by ECM-mediated catalysis of LOX- and lysyl oxidase-like proteins (LOXL), which possibly caused crosslinked elastin and collagen networks, thereby enhancing tensile strength and structural integrity52. Furthermore, in breast malignancy, abnormal LOX-mediated upregulation of fibrillar collagen led to increased ECM stiffness and further promoted fibrosis, thereby facilitating tumorigenesis18. Similarly, the enzymatic function of LOX was also implicated in ECM remodeling and prognosis in lung cancer53,54. However, direct evidence elucidating ECM stiffening in the lung cancer model at the subcellular level remained scarce. Our study offered direct, visual evidence of elastin-collagen resemblance by establishing a robust correlation between RM assessments and corresponding mechanical readouts. Future studies were advised to validate our method across a more abundant variety of disease models.

Moreover, the RM method could be further applied to boundary detection in the lung cancer model. The average computation time for RM was approximately 2 min for a 3D stack with a volume of 512 × 512 × 50 voxels (approximately 465 × 465 × 50 μm, see “Methods” for details), which could be further reduced via downsampling. Combined with the simultaneous elastin/collagen imaging taking 1–2 min per 3D stack, our quantitative MPM imaging system enabled rapid readout for inter-channel resemblance analysis, particularly when image acquisition and quantitative assessment were performed in a parallel way. For time-efficient identification of the tumor boundary, the strategy of discrete sampling around the tumor could be implemented. For example, 10 discrete samplings of 3D image stacks would take about 15 min. Notably, once the imaging at a given sampling site was completed, the RM analysis could be performed in parallel with imaging of the next sampling region. With this strategy and considering the case-dependent tumor size, sparse imaging combined with analysis of 8–12 sites along the tumor boundary could be roughly accomplished in about 25 min. Furthermore, the imaging parameters of the MPM system could be adjusted to further optimize the acquisition time in clinical scenarios, especially when extended to other disease models. When combined with intraoperative endoscopy and other similar clinical technologies, our platform could enable discrete yet accurate inspection of lesion regions along suspected tumor borders, thereby facilitating precise identification of tumor boundaries for biopsy guidance and even surgical resection. This approach would substantially improve current experience-based tumor boundary delineation and meet the demands of clinical translation.

In summary, we developed a quantitative MPM imaging platform (Fig. 1a) to characterize the morpho-structural convergence of elastin-collagen remodeling during lung tumorigenesis. Combined with mechanical testing (Figs. 1a and 4a), RM was shown to be positively correlated with lung tissue stiffness, providing direct evidence for an alternative mechanism underlying ECM stiffening at the subcellular level and acting as a robust metric for evaluating mechanical properties through label-free imaging. Furthermore, given that lung tumorigenesis was also accompanied by matrix stiffening, we performed additional in vivo imaging analysis with RM quantification, highlighting its promising potential for tumor boundary identification during intraoperative biopsy. These insights into matrix stiffening from the perspective of ECM component interactions might help guide the optimization of lung cancer and other disease treatments when combined with clinical imaging modalities such as elastography in the near future.

Methods

Parallel-acquisition multiphoton microscopy (MPM)

We acquired MPM images of lung tissues by a custom-built microscope with a 25× water dipping objective (NA 0.95, free working distance: 2.5 mm) and a tunable (680–1300 nm) Ti sapphire fs laser (InSight DeepSee; Spectra Physics; Mountain View, CA). Photomultiplier tube (PMT) detectors were utilized to collect emission photons, with the gain of the PMT kept fixed throughout this study. In detail, 800 nm was used for excitation for both TPEF signals of elastin fibers and SHG signals of collagen fibers, with TPEF signals isolated using the 525 (±25) nm dichroic mirror (DM) and bandpass filter (BF), and SHG signals isolated using the 400 (±10) nm DM and BF. These settings achieved time-efficient acquisition with minimal crosstalk for imaging, as instructed by the fluorescence spectra of these two fiber components at 800 nm excitation (Fig. 1a). Specifications of the main optical components were listed in Supplementary Table 1. TPEF and SHG images (512 × 512 pixels; 465 × 465 μm, with dwell time estimated to be 4 μs for both in vivo and ex vivo imaging) were obtained simultaneously for subsequent analysis. For each biological sample or imaging site, one 3D image stack was generated. The step size of 3D image stack along the depth direction was 1 μm, and the imaging depth typically ranged from 40 to 65 μm. These imaging settings were consistent for both ex vivo and in vivo imaging in our study, with imaging time estimated to be 1 to 2 min for each 3D image stack.

The laser power at the tissue side was approximately 35 mW on average. For safety considerations, considering that exposure to 60 mW NIR pulses (150 fs, 80 MHz) to biological tissues was estimated to be equivalent to a dose of 0.6 minimal erythema dose of UV exposure, 35 mW at the tissue side would not cause photodamage or irradiation-related effects for both ex vivo and in vivo imaging55. Besides, the image acquisition operation was monitored to ensure that no photodamage-induced distortion was observed.

Ex vivo human lung tissue preparation and imaging

We harvested lung tissue samples from patients with NSCLC of the adenocarcinoma subtype (LUAD), with detailed sample information shown in Table 1. A total of 63 Han Chinese patients from Fujian Provincial Hospital were involved in this study after obtaining informed consent from all patients and/or their legal guardians. In detail, the average size of each biopsy specimen was approximately 6 × 6 × 6 mm3. Each sample was cut into half, with one half processed for histology diagnosis by 3 authorized pathologists, and a consensus was reached to confirm the tissue type (normal vs. cancer, see Supplementary Fig. 1 for representative H&E-stained section examples). The other half was used for ex vivo imaging. In this way, we confirmed 122 lung specimens in total, including 59 normal and 63 cancerous tissues. Specifically, MPM imaging was immediately performed on samples after biopsy in Fujian Provincial Hospital. The size of the human lung cancer lesion was case-dependent, typically ranging from the millimeter to centimeter scale, and the width of the boundary region was estimated to be at submillimeter-scale based on H&E-stained sections. All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations and approved by the institutional review board at Zhejiang University (ZJU21410). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Murine model harboring human lung cancer and in vivo imaging

To evaluate the translational potential of this quantitative MPM platform, we induced a murine orthotopic model harboring human NSCLC through intrathoracic implantation of lung cancer cells for in vivo imaging56–58. Specifically, the H1299 cell line (CL-0165, 1 × 106 cells per T25 flask, Procell Life Science & Technology, Wuhan, China) was selected, which was suitable for inducing lung orthotopic tumors rapidly58. Firstly, the cell line was cultured in growth medium consisting of RPMI 1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator, which was authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination before injection. Secondly, H1299 cells were mixed with trypan blue and counted with a hemocytometer, and the mouse was anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of ketamine/xylazine mixture in advance. Next, a transverse incision around 1 cm was made about 0.5 cm below the inferior border of the left scapula along the midline long axis of the left lateral side of the mouse chest to expose the respiring lung as a pale pink structure after removal of soft tissue. Then, 50 μL cancer cell suspension containing 1 × 106 cells was injected to a depth of approximately 5 mm into the left lobe of the lung between the 6 th and 7 th rib, where the mouse lung was wider and thicker at this level. The above procedures were conducted with approval from the Animal Use and Care Committee at Zhejiang University (ZJU20190076). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Subsequently, in vivo imaging of mouse lung tissue was conducted around 2 weeks after cancer cell implantation, when primary tumors were established but prior to metastasis. Continuous anesthesia via isoflurane inhalation (3% for 2–3 min and 1.5% for the rest of the imaging time) was conducted throughout the in vivo imaging. Firstly, the skin, muscle and ribs were removed to expose the lung tissue, with care taken to avoid damage to surrounding tissues, such as nerves and blood vessels. Blunt forceps were used to gently retract the lungs to expose the tumor. Then, a glass coverslip was placed over the exposed region with a minimal quantity of cyanoacrylate-latex glue utilized to stabilize the interface of the mouse skin around the exposed area and the coverslip, keeping the tumor from contact with the glue. Next, in vivo imaging was performed with tape-based fixation for suppression of motion artifacts (Fig. 5b), which was also minimized by careful retraction of lung tissue and coverslip adhesion. The image quality on the screen was continuously observed so that the imaging could be restarted at a new point with optimal resolution and contrast once distorted. Additionally, a custom heated stage was used to maintain the mouse's body temperature. A total of 8 male BALB/c nude mice (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China), 10-week-old, were involved in this study, with half of them randomly assigned to be implanted with H1299 cells as the cancer group and the other half as the control group, using a computer-generated random number sequence. In addition, mice in both groups were housed in a standardized environment with cage locations balanced to minimize potential confounding factors. Notably, the imaged regions for normal controls were located at similar positions to those of tumors from the cancer group for reliable comparison. The MPM imaging setup (including the system, the objective, the wavelength settings, etc.) was identical to the one used for ex vivo human lung samples. Finally, three volumetric image stacks of tumor tissues and three stacks from tumor boundary regions were acquired from each cancer-type mouse, and three stacks of normal tissues were acquired from each healthy mouse (see Fig. 5 for more detailed location information). Compared to human lung tumors, mouse lung tumors developed much faster, with the boundary size also being case-dependent. Since our study focused on the early detection of lung tumor boundary, we monitored body weight for early signs of tumor development, resulting in tumor sizes typically around 10 mm and the boundary width was controlled to be far less than 1 mm. This tumor size was much smaller than the maximum tumor size permitted by the ethics committee (20 mm, referring to the ethical limit for subcutaneous tumor diameter). Meanwhile, as the ethics committee defined humane endpoints as body weight loss ≥ 20%, we performed in vivo imaging when the mice's body weight loss exceeded 10% but was less than 20% (about 2 weeks after tumor induction). Throughout the experiment, no animals exceeded the predefined humane endpoints. Besides, the maximum tumor size of 20 mm permitted by the animal ethics committee was not exceeded in any experiments. Corresponding source data for mouse experiments are provided in Supplementary Data 2. After imaging, mice were humanely euthanized, and tissues were collected and processed for histological staining. Pathologists blinded to the treatment groups classified the tissues as normal, cancerous, or transitional boundary tissues, and sample labels were coded by an independent researcher. After tissue type confirmation, pathologists further delineated a more precise transitional zone, and we defined imaging fields within this zone as boundary samples. Two representative H&E-stained sections were provided for both normal lung tissues and lung tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 13). Notably, tissue regions outside the tumor boundary in the sections were confirmed to be histologically normal.

Thickness algorithm

Thickness referred to the estimated voxel-wise diameter of the fiber structures64, and was applied to elastin fibers only, since the intertwining of collagen fibers made them hardly distinguishable on a single-fiber basis. Thickness quantification was also based on the segmented mask of fibrous structures. Briefly, the minimum distance search algorithm was performed to calculate the distance between each fibrous voxel and its nearest background voxel. Then, adaptive distance transmission was applied to unify voxels located perpendicular to the fiber, so that these voxels would have approximately the same estimated thickness value. Finally, a smooth correlation operator was used to approximate the true fiber thickness map.

Resemblance metric (RM)

The proposed optical parameter, resemblance metric (RM), quantified morpho-structural similarity between elastin and collagen fibers based on not only the nearest inter-channel fibrous voxel distances, but also all aforementioned 3D morphological and organizational features, including local coverage, orientation, directional variance and waviness. In general, a high elastin-collagen RM corresponded to a condition of their close spatial colocalization, similar density distributions, consistent orientations, identical alignment and analogous waviness. Before the establishment of RM, we ensured the lack of offending variables (i.e., the abovementioned morphological and organizational 3D features) by implementing multivariate analysis to evaluate multicollinearity between parameter pairs using the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient (r). The multicollinearity diagnostic results (Supplementary Fig. 2) revealed that all pair-wise Pearson correlation coefficients satisfied |r| < 0.739, revealing that there were no offending variables of either fiber that needed to be eliminated and that all parameters were mutually independent.

Nanoindentation experiment for Young’s modulus measurement

The nanoindenter, as the nanoindentation setup, was used to measure the Young’s modulus (stiffness) of both normal and cancer lung tissues. After MPM imaging, ex vivo samples were frozen and then transported to the nanoindentation laboratory in polystyrene foam containers with dry ice. After arrival, 19 normal and 17 cancer samples were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 30 min and decellularized with an orbital shaker. In detail, three 12-h washes with 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) solution were performed, interspersed with washes by distilled water (dH2O, 5 min) and PBS (25 min). Afterward, the residual detergent was rinsed off from the treated samples by two rounds of washing with dH2O (5 min) and PBS (25 min). Subsequent polishing with an angle grinder and cryo-sectioning into ~2 mm thick sections was conducted after rapid freezing at −80 °C. Thus, we acquired a flat-surfaced decellularized sample with a size of approximately 5 × 5 × 2 mm3. The treated sample was then glued to the glass slide, surrounded by a circle drawn by a hydrophobic pen and immersed in distilled water (Fig. 1a). Later, the Young’s modulus of cryo-sectioned lung tissues was tested by a nanoindenter (PIUMA, Optics 11, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Specifically, a soft cantilever with a 27.5 μm radius ball tip and a stiffness of 0.480 N/m was used. A nanoindentation testing matrix comprising 10 × 10 indents with 20 μm steps was performed on tissue sections. Additionally, a random scattering test with adjacent locations at intervals of at least 200 μm on average was also applied on the same group set to increase the datum volume. The effective Young’s modulus was obtained from the slope of the loading curve using the Hertz contact model. Examples reflecting more detailed testing processes were plotted, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 10.

Spectra measurement of elastin and collagen fibers

Spectral measurement of elastin and collagen was based on an established protocol55. Specifically, elastin powder (E7152, Sigma-Aldrich) derived from human lung through non-degradative extraction was utilized as the elastin sample. The elastin powder was rehydrated with one drop of water and then mounted between a microscope slide and a coverslip for further analysis. Collagen fibrillar gels were fabricated using Type I collagen isolated from rat tail tendons, serving as the collagen sample. The spectra of these samples were measured using the same custom system as that used for imaging, with excitation at a central wavelength of 800 nm (the same wavelength used for tissue imaging). TPEF signals were obtained and assessed, with the spectrum normalized to the maximum intensity for comparison.

Statistics and reproducibility

Using JMP 16.2.0, two-sided Student’s t-tests (95% confidence interval) were used to determine statistically significant differences in Figs. 3f and 4d, and one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test was used for the comparisons in Fig. 5d. Using MATLAB (R2023a), the data in Fig. 4e were fitted to a linear regression model to calculate R2 values. Experimental reproducibility details (e.g., sample sizes) were provided in the corresponding figure legends. Furthermore, a one-versus-all linear SVM model was applied to evaluate the performance of RM on the classification of three categories in Fig. 5f. The related classification accuracy was calculated using MATLAB (R2023a) and reported in the table in Fig. 5g. Notably, this three-class classification (normal, boundary and cancer) using in vivo images was performed using leave-one-out cross-validation approach, and corresponding ROC curves were generated using a one-versus-all strategy (Fig. 5f). Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients (r) were also calculated for multicollinearity diagnostic analysis using JMP 16.2.0.
