15-Year Experience of Patch Testing during Pregnancy: Retrospective Case-Control Study from a Tertiary Israeli Center
Authors: Daniel Hilewitz, Akiva Trattner, Yehonatan Noyman, Igal Igor Radinsky, Nuriel Ben Simon, Eran Galili, Shany Sherman, Daniel Mimouni, Igor Snast
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough patch testing (PT) is not contraindicated during pregnancy, most dermatologists defer testing due to lack of data and possibility that immunological changes might influence outcomes. In our center, we routinely perform PT during pregnancy as it is not known to be teratogenic and may lead to significant improvements in quality of life. This study aimed to assess whether pregnancy alters the outcome of PT.MethodsRetrospective, single-center case-control study was conducted at a tertiary Israeli dermatology clinic (2009–2023). Women who gave birth within 40 weeks of PT were identified, and for each, three age-, year-, and atopic dermatitis-matched controls were selected.ResultsOf 3,351 women tested, 54 (1.6%) were pregnant at the time of PT. Most were tested during the first trimester (63%). No significant differences were found in the frequency of positive patch test reactions between pregnant women and matched controls. The most common allergens in pregnant patients were nickel sulfate (61.1%), 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (13.64%), and fragrance mix II (5.5%).ConclusionPregnancy does not appear to affect patch test outcomes in early and mid-gestation. Due to limited third-trimester representation, further studies are needed to determine whether reactivity remains consistent throughout all stages of pregnancy.

Introduction

Current guidelines indicate that while there is no known risk associated with patch testing during pregnancy, many dermatologists choose to postpone testing as a precaution [1]. This hesitation is often due to theoretical concerns, including the possibility that hypercortisolemic states and the immunologic shift from a Th1- to Th2-dominant response during pregnancy could lead to false-negative or false-positive results [2]. Additionally, medicolegal considerations may contribute to the decision to delay testing.

To date, only a single report by White and Malik [3] has described patch testing performed during the third trimester of pregnancy. In that case, the patient exhibited positive reactions to nickel.

Our current study was conducted at Rabin Medical Center, a tertiary hospital affiliated with Clalit Health Services – the largest health maintenance organization in Israel. Our center follows a different approach: patch testing is not routinely postponed during pregnancy. This policy is based on the understanding that patch testing is not known to be teratogenic or harmful and may, in fact, lead to significant improvements in quality of life for individuals suffering from allergic contact dermatitis. In this first-to-date, single-center, case-control study, we present our 15 years of experience with patch testing during pregnancy.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective study included all patients referred to the Contact Dermatitis Clinic at Rabin Medical Center, a tertiary care hospital affiliated with Tel Aviv University, between April 2009 and December 2023. Medical referrals – almost exclusively from dermatologists or allergists – were screened by the clinic head. Only patients with a clinical suspicion of contact dermatitis underwent patch testing.

Patch Testing Procedure

Patch testing was performed using the European Baseline Series (Chemotechnique Diagnostics, Malmö, Sweden) with Finn Chambers on Scanpor tape (Hermal, Reinbek, Germany). A complete list of allergens included in the European Baseline Series used at our center is provided in online supplementary Table S1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551449). During the 15-year study period (2009–2023), the baseline series remained largely unchanged. The only modification relevant to this analysis was the introduction of 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA) into the European Baseline Series in 2020, in accordance with ESCD recommendations. All other allergens were used consistently throughout the study period. Allergens were applied to unaffected skin on the upper back and remained in place for 48 h. Test readings were conducted on days 2 and 3 in accordance with the International Contact Dermatitis Research Group (ICDRG) guidelines and were graded as weak (+), strong (++), or very strong/extreme (+++).

Patch application and readings were performed consistently by four well-trained nurses throughout the study period. Patients were excluded if they had applied topical corticosteroids to the test area or received systemic corticosteroids within 2 weeks before testing, or had undergone phototherapy within 1 month of testing.

Data Sources and Case-Control Design

The objective of this study was to assess whether pregnancy affects patch test results. The data were extracted from CHS using Clalit’s data sharing platform powered by MDClone from a national database that collects medical records from all Israeli hospitals. Women who delivered within 40 weeks following patch testing were identified. Delivery date and gestational age were recorded.

We employed a case-control study design. For each pregnant woman (case), three nonpregnant controls were selected from the female patch-tested cohort, matched by age, year of patch testing, and history of atopic dermatitis. Sex was not a matching variable because the entire study population consisted exclusively of women. Atopic dermatitis diagnoses were extracted from Clalit Health Services (CHS), the largest health maintenance organization in Israel (52 of 54 pregnant patients were CHS members).

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Rabin Medical Center and registered with the Israeli Ministry of Health. Identification data of patients were confidential. As data regarding relevance are not regularly coded in the patients’ files but rather needs to be extracted manually, data regarding relevance were unavailable.

Statistical Analysis

Frequencies of positive patch test reactions were compared between pregnant women and matched controls using the chi-square test. A two-tailed p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4.

Obstetric Data

In a post hoc manner, we manually identified 22 patients that gave birth in our center. Manual review of their medical records revealed no obstetric complications associated with patch testing.

Discussion

In this first-to-date, retrospective case-control study, we examined 54 pregnant women who underwent patch testing over a 15-year period at a tertiary Israeli contact dermatitis clinic. Controlling for age, year of testing, and a previous diagnosis of atopic dermatitis, we found no statistically significant differences, neither in the response to specific allergens nor in the overall rate of positive patch test reactions, between pregnant women and matched nonpregnant controls. These results suggest that pregnancy does not have a clinically meaningful effect on patch test reactivity. It is important to note that most pregnant patients in our cohort were tested during the first trimester, whereas only four women (7%) were tested in the third trimester, a period characterized by the highest physiologic cortisol levels and the most pronounced Th2 shift. This distribution may partially explain the absence of significant differences between cases and controls as early pregnancy is associated with a more modest degree of immune modulation compared with late pregnancy. Nevertheless, even when restricting the analysis to women in the second and third trimesters, no trend toward reduced reactivity was observed. Taken together, these findings suggest that if pregnancy-related immunologic changes influence patch test outcomes, the magnitude of this effect is likely small.

The European Society of Contact Dermatitis (ESCD) guidelines state that, while there is no known risk associated with patch testing during pregnancy, most dermatologists still opt to postpone testing during this period [1]. This cautious approach may be attributed to several factors. First: physiological changes: pregnancy involves hormonal and immunologic shifts, including a transition toward a Th2-dominant immune profile, which might theoretically influence patch test outcomes. A Th2-dominant immune shift is a well-recognized feature of normal pregnancy and is theoretically relevant to patch testing, which relies on a delayed-type (type IV) hypersensitivity response mediated primarily by Th1 cells. A reduction in Th1 activity during pregnancy could, in principle, lead to weaker reactions or false-negative results. However, in our cohort, we did not observe lower rates of positive reactions among pregnant women including those in the second and third trimesters, where Th2 polarization is more pronounced. While our sample size, particularly in late pregnancy, limits firm conclusions, the consistency of reactivity across groups suggests that any suppressive effect of the Th2 shift on patch test performance may be modest in clinical practice. Second: lack of data: as highlighted in a 2024 literature review by Pan et al. [3], only a single case report on patch testing in pregnancy was identified, underscoring the paucity of published evidence on this topic [4]. Third: medicolegal concerns: due to the limited data, physicians may be wary of performing patch tests during pregnancy, fearing that any subsequent obstetric complication, regardless of causality, might be attributed to the test. While patch testing is not contraindicated in pregnancy, clinicians should ensure that testing is performed only when clearly indicated and after informed consent. Given the general recommendation to avoid unnecessary procedures during pregnancy, documentation of the risk-benefit discussion is advisable to address potential medico-legal concerns. Importantly, it should be noted that there is no report in the literature regrading adverse effects in women who underwent patch testing during the first trimester, prior to recognizing they were pregnant. Moreover, none of the allergen patch tested is known to be teratogenic and concentration of allergens is similar to real-life use. For instance, para-phenylenediamine is tested routinely at 1% yet in hair dyes is present at approximately 3% or more. In contrast to other centers, Dr. Akiva Trattner, head of the Contact Dermatitis Clinic from 1995 to 2022 and a leading contact dermatitis specialist in Israel, upheld a longstanding policy of not postponing patch testing during pregnancy. This policy is grounded in the understanding that allergens used in standard patch test concentrations are not known to be teratogenic and may, in fact, lead to significant improvements in quality of life for individuals suffering from allergic contact dermatitis.

Importantly, given the low rate of major obstetrical complications in Israel (<5%) and the limited statistical power of our study, we made a pre-emptive decision not to evaluate obstetrical outcomes. Nonetheless, in a post hoc manner manual review of the medical records of 22 patients that gave birth in our facility revealed no obstetric complications associated with patch testing.

The prevalence of nickel sensitivity in our study was notably high [5–7]. In a recently published paper from our group, we reported that out of 5,234 consecutive patients, 2,158 (41%) were sensitive to nickel [8]. Sensitivity was significantly associated with female sex and age between 18 and 40 years. In over half of the cases, reactions to nickel were clinically relevant, with jewelry being by far the most common source of exposure. Several factors may explain the high prevalence observed in our cohort. In Israel, there is no legislation regarding nickel restriction, and there is an increased import of consumer products from China over the past two decades – particularly inexpensive jewelry and electronics. Additional reason is our center’s policy of screening referrals and avoiding low-yield testing. Consistent with our findings, the North American Contact Dermatitis Group reported a rise in nickel sensitivity from 14.3% in 1994 to 24.9% in 2021–2022, attributed to increased exposure to nickel-releasing items such as jewelry and electronic devices [6, 9].

Reactivity to 2-HEMA was also high compared to other studies. In a separate, recently published paper from our group, we reported the highest documented frequency of HEMA sensitivity to date, 8.1% at our center, with the highest prevalence (16.7%) seen in young women [10]. In 95% of cases, nail cosmetics were identified as the causative source, reflecting both the popularity of nail cosmetics in Israel and the global trend of increasing sensitivity to (meth)acrylates. As previously discussed, our pre-test selectivity likely contributed to the high rate of HEMA reactivity. Prevalence rates of other allergens in our study were generally in line with those reported in prior research. These high rates are largely attributed to widespread exposure to acrylate-containing nail cosmetics in Israel, which we identified as the causative source in 95% of cases. Given this epidemiologic context, the elevated HEMA positivity among pregnant women likely reflects true underlying sensitization rather than pregnancy-related immunologic modulation. Importantly, the similar prevalence in pregnant and nonpregnant controls further supports that pregnancy itself does not appear to alter HEMA reactivity.

In our cohort, the majority of women (63%) were tested during their first trimester, with fewer than 10% tested in the third trimester. This likely reflects both unrecognized early pregnancies at the time of testing and a tendency of atopic eruption of pregnancy to present or worsen in the first trimester, leading to referrals for patch testing to identify possible allergens contributing to symptom exacerbation. The main strengths of our study include a consistent 15-year policy of not deferring patch testing during pregnancy and the use of matched controls based on sex, age, and year of testing – key factors known to influence patch test outcomes.

However, the study has several limitations. First, while a within-subject comparison before and after pregnancy would best assess immunologic modulation, this design was impractical in a retrospective context. Second, we did not perform late stage reading, which might have affected results. Third, although our cohort included 54 pregnant women, the number of participants tested in the third trimester was particularly small (n = 4). This limits the ability to detect potential trimester-specific effects, especially given that late pregnancy is associated with the most pronounced immunologic changes. More broadly, the sample size also reduces our statistical power to identify differences in reactivity to allergens with lower prevalence. Matching by key determinants of patch test reactivity (age, year of testing, and atopic dermatitis) provided a robust alternative to mitigate confounding. Second, the sample size of pregnant women lacked sufficient statistical power to detect differences in reactivity to less common allergens. For example, the prevalence of positive reactions to fragrance mix II was 8.6% in the control group. Assuming a 30% reduction in reactivity during pregnancy, detecting a statistically significant difference would require approximately 1,400 pregnant participants. Nevertheless, the almost identical and high prevalence of nickel reactivity between pregnant and control groups supports the conclusion that pregnancy does not significantly impact patch test reactivity.

Third, although we attempted to match for a history of atopic dermatitis, it may have been more common in the pregnant group as atopic eruption of pregnancy often occurs as a new onset. Given the conflicting evidence on the relationship between atopic dermatitis and allergic contact dermatitis, the potential for confounding is unclear.

Fourth, as a tertiary referral center, our clinic may be subject to selection bias, with more severe cases being referred for testing during pregnancy. However, this bias may have been partially mitigated by our policy of screening referrals and avoiding patch testing in cases where the diagnostic yield was expected to be low. In addition, late readings (e.g., day 5 and later reading) were not routinely performed in our study; however, this practice was consistent across all patients.

In conclusion, this first-to-date study suggests that pregnancy, particularly in the first and second trimesters, does not significantly alter patch test reactivity. Given the potential for considerable improvement in quality of life, we support the continued use of patch testing during pregnancy when clinically indicated. Further large-scale prospective and retrospective studies with greater representation of late-stage pregnancy are warranted to confirm these findings.
