Long-term benefits of autologous stem cell transplantation versus intensive chemotherapy consolidation for acute myeloid leukemia patients: A propensity score matching analysis from the PETHEMA AML registry
Authors: Ana Alfonso-Pierola, David Martinez-Cuadrón, Rebeca Rodríguez-Veiga, Cristina Gil, Pilar Martínez-Sánchez, Teresa Bernal, Celina Benavente, Mónica A Romero-Riquelme, Josefina Serrano-Lopez, Juan M Bergua, Raimundo García-Boyero, Mar Tormo, Pilar Herrera, Claudia L Sossa-Melo, José A Pérez-Simon, Carlos Rodríguez-Medina, María F Bass-Maturana, José L López-Lorenzo, Lorenzo Algarra-Algarra, Belén Vidriales-Vicente, Manuel Pérez-Encinas, Manuel Barrios-García, Susana Vives, María J Sayas-Lloris, Marisa Capurro, Sebastián Hidalgo, Mayte Olave, Diana Cuervo-Lozada, Esperanza Lavilla-Rubira, Felipe Casado, Armando Mena-Durán, Marta Valero-Nuñez, Soledad Casado-Calderón, Amaia Balerdi, Vivianne Torres, Rosa Fernández, Víctor Noriega, Mariana Stevenazzi, Jorge Labrador, Pilar León-Maldonado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile allogeneic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT) is the preferred consolidation for high and most intermediate-risk acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients in first remission, the role of autologous SCT (auto-SCT) vs. chemotherapy (CT) when allo-SCT is not feasible or indicated, remains controversial. We conducted a real-world, retrospective cohort study using the PETHEMA AML registry to compare auto-SCT and CT. Multivariate Cox regression and propensity score matching (PS-matching) were used to adjust for confounding factors. A total of 1272 patients in first remission and who received 2 consolidation courses were included (615 receiving additional CT cycles and 657 undergoing auto-SCT). Overall, 78.08% of auto-SCT patients were diagnosed before 2017, compared to 38.11% in the CT cohort (p < 0.001). In the overall cohort, auto-SCT was associated with significantly prolonged overall survival (OS) (HR: 0.73, p < 0.001) and relapse-free survival (RFS) (HR: 0.73, p < 0.001). This benefit was particularly evident in patients ≤65 years, those with normal karyotype, and FLT3-ITD negativity. In the PS-matched cohort, the RFS advantage persisted (HR: 0.80, p = 0.092), but OS differences were not statistically significant (HR: 0.91, p = 0.563). The role of auto-SCT in the genomic and targeted agent era should not be discarded.Subject terms: Acute myeloid leukaemia, Stem-cell therapies

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a clonal hematologic malignancy characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of myeloid precursors, leading to impaired hematopoiesis [1]. Despite advances in induction therapy and improved complete remission (CR) rates, AML remains associated with a high risk of relapse. Allogeneic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT) is the preferred post-remission strategy in high- and most intermediate-risk AML [2]. However, a substantial proportion of patients may lack a suitable donor, have contraindications, or not receive allo-SCT for other reasons. For these patients, post-remission consolidation options include high dose intensive chemotherapy (CT) courses, which may be followed by autologous stem cell transplantation (auto-SCT) intensification [2, 3]. The Spanish Programa Español para Tratamientos en Hematología (PETHEMA) group has recommended auto-SCT in first CR, especially for low- or intermediate-risk patients or when allo-SCT is not feasible [3]. Nonetheless, its benefit remains uncertain, and use of auto-SCT is declining [2, 4, 5]. According to the European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EBMT), only 169 auto-SCT procedures were performed for AML in first CR at EBMT centers in 2022 [6]. I

Recent meta-analyses suggest that auto-SCT may provide improved relapse-free survival (RFS) versus CT, with similar overall survival (OS) [6]. However, the evidence is limited by selection bias and the lack of high-quality randomized trials.

This study analyzes real-world outcomes of auto-SCT vs. CT as consolidation in AML patients not receiving allo-SCT, using data from the PETHEMA registry. To avoid time-dependent selection bias, all patients were in first CR after 1 or 2 cycles of induction, which was followed by 2 consolidation CT courses, and then auto-SCT vs.1 or 2 courses of CT.

Methods

PETHEMA registry

The PETHEMA AML registry (NCT02607059) collects retrospective and prospective data on adults with AML, including demographics, disease characteristics, treatment, response, relapse, and survival outcomes. The PETHEMA AML registry (NCT02607059) is approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital Universitari i Politècnic La Fe (Valencia, Spain) and complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, unless deceased at the time of retrospective inclusion.

Eligibility

Patients aged 18 to 70 years old diagnosed with AML from August 1991 up to May 2024 who achieved first complete remission (CR) or CR with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi) after 1–2 induction cycles, followed by 2 consolidation CT courses, and then either auto-SCT or 1–2 additional CT courses, without subsequent allo-SCT. Patients with acute promyelocytic leukemia were excluded. To reduce time-dependent bias, all patients received at least 2 consolidation cycles. Patients receiving maintenance were not excluded. Induction consisted of anthracycline plus cytarabine, and consolidation was cytarabine-based. Auto-SCT conditioning regimens were myeloablative. All patients were treated according to standard clinical practice and analyzed regardless of PETHEMA guidelines adherence.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoints included OS and RFS, analyzed based on stratification by treatment approach (CT only vs. CT plus auto-SCT). Secondary endpoints assessed the influence of age, sex, baseline cytogenetics and molecular characteristics, and post-induction measurable residual disease (MRD) on OS and RFS across treatment strategy (CT only vs. CT plus auto-SCT). OS was defined from induction start to death; RFS from induction start to relapse or death. Patients alive and relapse-free were censored at last follow-up. CR/CRi and relapse definitions followed standard guidelines [7].

Cytogenetic were stratified according to the Medical Research Council (MRC) classification [8]. Gene mutations were obtained by standard PCR or next generation sequencing per routine practice. Measurable residual disease (MRD) was routinely assessed locally using flow cytometry. MRD status was determined based on post-induction 0.1% threshold in accordance with previous PETHEMA reports and the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2021 guidelines [9, 10].

Statistical analysis

Baseline patient characteristics were summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and absolute and relative frequencies for categorical variables. Associations between patient subgroups were analyzed using appropriate statistical tests: the Chi-square (χ²) test with Yates’s correction for categorical variables, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Student’s t-test, or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, depending on data distribution. A propensity score matching was conducted using key covariates, including age, sex, cytogenetic risk, FLT3-ITD mutation status, and need of a second induction treatment to achieve first CR/CRi. A logistic regression model with a caliper of 0.01 and no replacement was used to estimate propensity scores, adjusting for the average treatment effect. Time-to-event analyses were conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method, with subgroup differences assessed via the log-rank test. Follow-up data were updated as of January 2025. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to evaluate the association between treatment type (CT only vs. CT plus auto-SCT) and survival outcomes. Adjusted hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for covariates included in the final model. Additionally, interaction analyses were conducted to assess whether specific patient subgroups derived a particular benefit from auto-SCT. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (version 15.1), with statistical significance set at a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

In the overall study population, the median age was 53.2 years (range: 18.06–70.81) in the CT arm and 50.37 years (range: 18.22–70.87) in the auto-SCT arm (p < 0.001). In the CT arm, 34.98% of patients were diagnosed before 2017, compared to 70.42% in the auto-SCT arm (p < 0.001). There was a trend towards more female gender in the CT vs. the auto-SCT cohort (51.06% vs. 46.27%, respectively, p = 0.088). Cytogenetic risk was available among 453 patients in the CT and 567 in the auto-SCT group, and 34.88% vs. 19.93%, respectively, were classified as low risk (p < 0.001). FLT3-ITD mutation status was available for 493 patients in the CT and 450 in the auto-SCT arm, and 24.35% vs. 14.89% were positive (p < 0.001). NPM1 mutation status was available for 468 patients in the CT and 424 in the auto-SCT cohort, and 40.60% vs.51.41% were positive (p = 0.001). A second induction cycle was administered in 4.88% of patients in the CT and 2.13% in the auto-SCT arm (p = 0.007). Regarding response status, 1248 patients (98.2%) achieved CR and 23 patients (1.8%) achieved CRi. CRi was more frequent in the CT group (2.8%) than in the auto-SCT group (0.9%), a difference that was statistically significant (p = 0.013). Post-induction MRD was positive in 63.49% of patients in the CT and 67.26% in the auto-SCT arm (p = 0.313).

The PS-matched cohort (n = 237 in the CT arm and n = 240 in the auto-SCT arm) showed no significant differences in median age (51.1 vs. 50.9 years, p = 0.909) or sex distribution (48.52% vs. 52.08%, p = 0.437). Cytogenetic risk were also comparable between groups (p = 0.727). Additionally, FLT3-ITD mutations were present in 16.03% of the CT arm and 18.33% of the auto-SCT arm (p = 0.506), FLT3-TKD mutations in 10.13% and 6.67%, respectively (p = 0.456), and NPM1 mutations in 40.08% and 39.17%, respectively (p = 0.891). Regarding response status, 469 patients (98.3%) achieved CR and 8 patients (1.7%) achieved CRi. CRi was more frequent in the CT arm (3.0%) compared to the auto-SCT arm (0.4%), with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.031). MRD was positive in 24.64% of the CT arm and 35.94% of the auto-SCT arm (p = 0.045), and the treatment period also differed between CT and auto-SCT groups (after 2017 66.67% vs. 25%, respectively, p < 0.001).

Among patients treated with chemotherapy, mobilization failure was documented in 61 of 523 evaluable cases (11.66%), potentially contributing to the decision to forego autologous transplantation. Among patients undergoing auto-SCT, data on CD34+ cell dose were available in 433 cases. The median infused dose was 2.95 ×10⁶ CD34+ cells/kg (IQR 2.13–4.14). Conditioning regimen data were available for 552 patients in the auto-SCT group. The most frequently used protocols were FLUBU combinations (fludarabine plus busulfan), representing 39.5% of cases, followed by BEA regimens (busulfan, etoposide, and cytarabine) in 36.5%, and BUCY ± MEL regimens (busulfan, cyclophosphamide with or without melphalan) in 20.5%. Less commonly used regimens TBI + CY (total body irradiation plus cyclophosphamide; 0.7%), and other or unclassified regimens (2.5%). In the auto-SCT group, transplant-related mortality was low, with 8 deaths (1.22%) by day +30 and 10 deaths (1.52%) by day +100 post-transplant.

OS and RFS in the overall and PS-matched cohorts

The median follow-up was 29.37 months (IQR 14.49–68.99) for the entire cohort. The median OS was 150.62 months in the auto-SCT group vs. 75.85 months in the CT group (HR of 0.73, 95% CI: 0.61–0.87, p < 0.001). The median RFS was 55.06 months in the auto-SCT group and 26.12 months in the CT group (HR 0.73; 95% CI: 0.63–0.86, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). In the PS-matched cohort, median RFS was 62.78 months for the auto-SCT group compared to 34.86 months for the CT group (HR 0.80, 95% CI: 0.62–1.04, p = 0.092). The median OS in the PS-matched cohort was 144.97 months for the auto-SCT group versus 104.96 months for the CT group (HR 0.91, 95% CI: 0.66–1.25, p = 0.563) (Fig. 2).

To assess outcomes in a more contemporary cohort, a subanalysis was performed in patients diagnosed from 2017 onward (n = 524). Auto-SCT was associated with longer relapse-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) in the overall cohort. Median RFS was 63.57 months for auto-SCT versus 28.55 months for CT (HR 0.60, 95% CI: 0.44–0.82; p = 0.001), and median OS was not reached for auto-SCT compared to 75.85 months for CT (HR 0.52, 95% CI: 0.34–0.80; p = 0.003). In the PS-matched cohort, a similar trend favoring auto-SCT was observed, although differences did not reach statistical significance: median RFS was 62.78 vs. 34.86 months (HR 0.66, p = 0.150), and median OS was not reached vs. 75.85 months (HR 0.57, p = 0.125). OS and RFS according to age

Among patients older than 65 years, the median OS was 27.79 months for the auto-SCT group versus 35.68 months for the CT group (HR: 1.08, 95% CI: 0.67–1.75, p = 0.744); and the median RFS was 30.75 months in the auto-SCT group compared to 35.68 months in the CT group (HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.58–1.75, p = 0.981) (Fig. 4). Among patients aged ≤65 years, the median OS was 152.99 months for the auto-SCT group compared to 91.69 months for the CT group (HR: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.62–0.92, p = 0.005); and the median RFS was 62.78 months in the auto-SCT group versus 28.25 months in the CT group (HR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.62–0.87, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

These findings were consistent in the PS-matched population, although statistical significance was not reached. The median RFS in patients ≤65 years was 72.60 months for the auto-SCT group compared to 34.86 months for the CT group (HR 0.77, 95% CI: 0.58–1.02, p = 0.070) (Fig. 5), and the median OS was 144.97 months in the auto-SCT group versus 104.96 months in the CT group (HR 0.97, 95% CI: 0.68–1.38, p = 0.860). Among patients older than 65 years, the median RFS was 18.63 months for the auto-SCT group compared to 70.30 months for the CT group (HR 1.25, 95% CI: 0.61–2.58, p = 0.545), and the median OS was 39.03 months in the auto-SCT group versus 80.55 months in the CT group (HR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.43–2.25, p = 0.973) (Fig. 5). OS and RFS according to cytogenetic risk

Among patients with normal karyotype, auto-SCT was associated with significantly improved RFS (55.06 vs. 25.07 months; HR 0.68, 95% CI: 0.54–0.86; p = 0.001) and OS (129.27 vs. 57.62 months; HR 0.63, 95% CI: 0.48–0.82; p = 0.001) compared to CT. In favorable-risk patients, auto-SCT significantly prolonged RFS (not reached vs. 118.76 months; HR 0.59, 95% CI: 0.39–0.90; p = 0.013), with a non-significant trend toward improved OS (p = 0.061). In intermediate-risk AML, no differences in RFS (20.99 vs. 28.15 months; p = 0.847) or OS (50.23 months vs. not reached; p = 0.170) were observed. In adverse-risk patients, auto-SCT yielded numerically better RFS (15.50 vs. 10.74 months; p = 0.413) and OS (36.0 vs. 22.1 months; p = 0.322), but differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 4).

In the PS-matched cohort, the benefit of auto-SCT persisted in normal karyotype patients (RFS: 46.19 vs. 25.07 months; HR 0.80, p = 0.188) and CBF AML (HR 0.50, p = 0.043), while no significant benefit was seen in intermediate- or high-risk groups (Fig. 5). These subgroup differences are graphically represented in Fig. S1.

OS and RFS according to FLT3 and NPM1 mutations

Among FLT3-ITD negative patients, the median RFS was 63.57 months in the auto-SCT group versus 26.48 months in the CT group (p = 0.005, HR: 0.74 [95% CI: 0.60–0.91]), and the median OS was 152.83 months in the auto-SCT group compared to 97.83 months in the CT group (p = 0.023, HR: 0.74 [95% CI: 0.57–0.96]) (Fig. 4). In the PS-matched cohort, auto-SCT was associated with improved RFS (74.15 months vs. 48.75 months, p = 0.081, HR: 0.77 [95% CI: 0.57–1.03]) (Fig. 5) and OS (152.83 months vs. 104.96 months, p = 0.255, HR: 0.81 [95% CI: 0.56–1.17]) (Fig. 5).

For FLT3-TKD negative patients, the median RFS was 112.88 months in the auto-SCT group compared to 23.49 months in the CT group (p < 0.001, HR: 0.55 [95% CI: 0.39–0.77]), and the median OS was not reached in the auto-SCT group versus 85.94 months in the CT group (p = 0.009, HR: 0.57 [95% CI: 0.37–0.88]) (Fig. 4). In the PS-matched cohort, auto-SCT was associated with a prolonged RFS (62.78 months vs. 31.77 months, p = 0.004, HR: 0.46 [95% CI: 0.27–0.80]) and OS (not reached vs. 75.85 months, p = 0.040, HR: 0.49 [95% CI: 0.24–1.00]) (Fig. 5).

In NPM1 negative patients, the median RFS was 44.68 months in the auto-SCT group versus 21.48 months in the CT group (p = 0.029, HR: 0.76 [95% CI: 0.59–0.97]), and the median OS was 144.97 months in the auto-SCT group versus 86.20 months in the CT group (p = 0.119, HR: 0.79 [95% CI: 0.58–1.06]) (Fig. 4). In the PS-matched cohort, auto-SCT was associated with a trend towards improved RFS (47.99 months vs. 22.04 months, p = 0.138, HR: 0.77 [95% CI: 0.54–1.09]) and OS (122.96 months vs. 86.20 months, p = 0.720, HR: 0.93 [95% CI: 0.61–1.41]) (Fig. 5). These subgroup differences are also illustrated in Fig. S1.

MRD status and its impact on outcomes

Among MRD-positive patients, the auto-SCT group showed an improvement in RFS, with a median of 56.93 months compared to 21.98 months in the CT group (p = 0.022, HR: 0.65 [95% CI: 0.45–0.94]). In MRD-negative patients, a benefit was also observed with auto-SCT, with a median of 72.33 months versus 44.68 months in the CT group (p = 0.030, HR: 0.73 [95% CI: 0.56–0.97]). Regarding OS, MRD-positive patients treated with auto-SCT had a significant advantage, with a median not reached, whereas in the CT group, it was 38.89 months (p = 0.002, HR: 0.52 [95% CI: 0.34–0.79]). Among MRD-negative patients, the difference in OS was not statistically significant, with a median of 144.97 months in the auto-SCT group and 104.96 months in the CT group (p = 0.233, HR: 0.81 [95% CI: 0.59–1.14]) (Fig. 4).

In the PS-matched cohort, similar trends were observed, but only in MRD-negative patients, auto-SCT showed a clear benefit, with a median of 100.36 months compared to 22.33 months in the chemotherapy group (p = 0.010, HR: 0.57 [95% CI: 0.37–0.87]) (Fig. 5), and the median OS was 157.12 months in the auto-SCT group vs. 86.20 months in the CT group (p = 0.047, HR: 0.59 [95% CI: 0.35–0.99]) (Fig. 5). These MRD subgroup analyses are further illustrated in Fig. S1.

Multivariable analysis of survival predictors

In the multivariate Cox regression analysis performed on the overall cohort, auto-SCT was associated with an improved RFS and OS compared to CT (HR of 0.46, 95% CI: 0.27–0.78, p = 0.004; and HR 0.43, 95% CI: 0.22–0.83, p = 0.013, respectively) (Table 2).

In the propensity score (PS)-matched cohort, the protective effect of auto-SCT on RFS persisted, albeit with a reduced significance (HR: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.21–1.07, p = 0.073). For OS, the benefit of auto-SCT was no longer statistically significant (HR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.27–1.94, p = 0.524), indicating that patient selection biases might contribute to the observed survival advantage.

Regarding interaction analyses, no statistically significant interactions were identified between post-remission strategy (CT only or auto-SCT) and cytogenetic risk, FLT3-ITD, NPM1, MRD, age, or sex for either RFS or OS. However, among FLT3-ITD-negative patients, auto-SCT was associated with a significantly lower risk of relapse (HR = 0.41, P = 0.018) and improved OS (HR = 0.45, P = 0.025), although the interaction term itself did not reach significance. Similarly, NPM1 negativity was associated with poorer survival in both treatment groups (HR = 3.99, P < 0.001 for RFS; HR = 7.76, P < 0.001 for OS), but no differential effect by treatment was observed. The PS-matched cohort showed similar trends, though statistical power was reduced. The strongest association remained with FLT3-ITD-negative status, which correlated with improved RFS (HR = 0.19, P = 0.001) and OS (HR = 0.32, P = 0.064) across both treatment strategies (Table 2).

Allogeneic transplantation after relapse

Of the 1272 patients in the cohort, 163 (12.8%) received allo-SCT after first relapse and 10 (0.8%) after second relapse. In first relapse, median OS was 17.4 months (IQR, 6.9–NR) for patients initially treated with auto-SCT, and 10.2 months (IQR, 4.7–18.8) for those treated with chemotherapy (HR 0.69, 95% CI: 0.36–1.31; p = 0.241). In second relapse, OS was 6.8 months (IQR, 4.9–21.5) in the auto-SCT group; the median was not estimable in the chemotherapy group due to the low number of events (HR 0.13, 95% CI: 0.01–2.14; p = 0.155) (Figure S2).

Discussion

Our study represents the largest real-world cohort to date comparing auto-SCT to CT as consolidation therapy in AML patients who do not undergo allo-SCT [11–24]. By leveraging a comprehensive dataset from the PETHEMA AML registry, we provide a robust real-world analysis, accounting for potential selection biases through inclusion criteria (i.e, all patients had received 2 consolidation courses), PS-matching and multivariate adjustments. Previous randomized clinical trials evaluating auto-SCT versus chemotherapy have often been limited by methodological challenges, including substantial crossover between treatment arms and time-to-event analyses initiated from the achievement of first remission, rather than from the completion of consolidation [6]. Our study mitigates these biases by selecting patients who had completed at least two consolidation cycles prior to treatment assignment, thereby providing a more balanced comparison and minimizing early attrition due to relapse or treatment-related complications in the auto-SCT arms.

We show that auto-SCT provides a significant OS and RFS in the overall cohort, but after reducing the sample size to a PS-matched population, only a trend towards improved RFS remains. The lack of statistical significance for OS in the PS-matched cohort is likely multifactorial. Reduced sample size limits statistical power, particularly for OS, which requires longer observation periods. Moreover, the CT group included a higher proportion of patients diagnosed after 2017, likely benefiting from more effective salvage options—including targeted therapies and improved supportive care—which may have attenuated OS differences. Together with shorter follow-up in this group, these factors could contribute to the absence of statistical significance for OS. We could identify subsets that may particularly benefit from auto-SCT, such as those aged ≤65 years old, CBF leukemia, normal karyotype, FLT3 negative, and NPM1 negative.

Several randomized clinical trials, and further metanalyses, have assessed the role of auto-SCT vs. CT in first CR AML. The largest randomized study by Vellenga et al. included 258 and 259 patients in first CR assigned to auto-SCT and CT. Although we did not perform a randomized comparison, we included a larger number of patients, and we avoided time-dependent selection bias between CT and auto-SCT by including only those with at least 2 consolidation courses, which was followed by an auto-SCT or by more CT cycles. Our study derives from an epidemiologic registry, where several factors could influence the decision on performing auto-SCT or not: (1) auto-SCT cohort was younger, reflecting that older subjects could be less fit for this procedure; (2) less auto-SCT subjects had a favorable risk cytogenetic, maybe due to increasing use of gentuzumab ozogamycine in this setting [25]; (3) less FLT3 mutations among auto-SCT cohort, also reflecting use of induction followed by high dose cytarabine in combination with midostaurin [26]; (4) auto-SCT patients were mostly diagnosed before 2017, in line with trends reported by EBMT [27], and (5) mobilization failure may have contributed to the choice of chemotherapy in some cases, as 11.66% of evaluable patients in the CT group had documented mobilization failure. We could mitigate risk factors between auto-SCT and CT groups using a PS-matching strategy, although the transplant cohort continued to belong to an older period than the chemotherapy cohort. To further address this temporal imbalance, we performed a subanalysis restricted to patients diagnosed from 2017 onward. In this more contemporary subgroup, the benefit of auto-SCT in terms of RFS and OS was preserved, supporting the consistency and relevance of our findings in the context of modern AML management.

In line with several randomized studies and metanalyses, our multivariate and PS-matching analyses we support that auto-SCT can improve RFS (HR 0.46 and 0.47 in the overall and PS-matching cohorts, respectively). As in previous studies and metanalyses [6, 11, 13, 28], OS was not significantly impacted by auto-SCT performance (HR 0.43 and 0.73 in the overall and PS-matching cohorts, respectively), probably because of more opportunities for salvage with second-line therapy and allo-SCT in patients relapsing on the CT cohort. Importantly, early transplant-related mortality was low in the auto-SCT group, with 1.22% of patients dying by day +30 and 1.52% by day +100, supporting the safety of this approach in selected patients.

We identified several subgroups were auto-SCT could be especially recommended: (1) younger patients ( ≤ 65 years) may tolerate intensive consolidation therapy better and derive a greater advantage from a prolonged remission phase. Interestingly, these findings contrast with those reported by Heini et al., where older patients ( ≥ 65 years) undergoing auto-SCT in first remission demonstrated survival outcomes comparable to younger patients [29]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in patient selection criteria, as their study included only individuals deemed fit enough for auto-SCT; (2) patients with CBF or normal karyotype, in line with prior studies suggesting that low and intermediate-risk AML derives the most benefit from this consolidation strategy [6, 30]; (3) patients without FLT3 mutations, probably because they have no standard of care maintenance, like midostaurin for those harboring mutations. Also, FLT3 inhibitors were approved in Spain in 2017, meaning that a substantial proportion of patients with FLT3-mutated AML in this study did not receive FLT3 inhibitors, especially among the auto-SCT cohort. The meta-analysis published by Li et al. emphasized the importance of molecular profiling in selecting candidates for auto-SCT, particularly in intermediate-risk patients [31]. However, while that study suggested that molecular markers influence post-transplant outcomes, it did not specifically identify FLT3-ITD negativity as a determinant of superior benefit from auto-SCT.

On the other hand, NPM1 negativity was associated with worse outcomes, independent of the treatment approach. The interaction analysis did not reveal a statistically significant modifying effect between FLT3-ITD and NPM1 status, indicating a pattern that aligns with the subgroup outcomes illustrated in Fig. S1. These findings align with prior reports highlighting the protective prognostic impact of FLT3-ITD negativity, independent of NPM1 status, and underscore the importance of incorporating molecular profiling into treatment decision-making for auto-SCT candidates [32].

Regarding post-induction MRD status, we found that in the overall cohort both MRD negative and MRD positive patients had improved RFS when an auto-SCT was performed, but this was only significant for MRD negative patients in the PS-matching analyses (Fig. S1). To our knowledge this is the largest real-world study evaluating the impact of auto-SCT in the context of MRD status, suggesting that this procedure could benefit even MRD positive patients.

Our findings also reinforce the role of auto-SCT as a viable consolidation strategy not only for low- and intermediate-risk populations, but as a potential alternative for high-risk patients in settings where allo-SCT is not feasible. Also, haploidentical donor SCT could also be an option for AML patients in first CR without sibling or full matched unrelated donor availability. In this regard; the retrospective study by Chen J, et al. showed that auto-SCT offers similar OS as compared to haplo-SCT in intermediate-risk patients with MRD negativity, especially for those FLT3-ITD negative [33]. In our cohort, 12.8% of patients ultimately underwent allo-SCT in first relapse. Notably, those previously consolidated with auto-SCT showed a numerically longer median OS after allo-SCT compared to those treated with chemotherapy, suggesting that initial use of auto-SCT does not compromise the potential for successful salvage.

Several limitations should be acknowledged, like the retrospective design, and the lack of complete molecular data for all patients, limiting our ability to fully stratify outcomes according to contemporary risk classifications, such as the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2022 criteria. Furthermore, the inclusion of patients treated over multiple decades introduces potential variability in treatment protocols and supportive care measures, which could influence the observed outcomes. Due to the small number of patients with high-risk cytogenetics, results in this subgroup should be interpreted with caution and are not conclusive. However, despite the fact that patients undergoing auto-SCT were treated in earlier periods, the survival advantage of auto-SCT over CT remained consistent, reinforcing the robustness of our findings. Another limitation of our study is the inability to assess the long-term impact of different treatment strategies on the development of secondary malignancies or other late-onset comorbidities, which are important considerations when evaluating consolidation therapy options. These limitations underscore the need for prospective, randomized controlled trials to validate our findings and refine the role of auto-SCT in AML management. Finally, future research should focus on optimizing conditioning regimens, define appropriate cellular infused dose, and exploring maintenance strategies after auto-SCT.

In conclusion, this study provides the most extensive real-world evidence to date on the efficacy of auto-SCT compared to CT as consolidation therapy for AML patients who do not undergo allo-SCT. Our findings confirm a significant RFS benefit associated with auto-SCT, particularly in younger patients, those with favorable risk or normal cytogenetic, and those with FLT3 negativity. The role of auto-SCT in the MRD-guided and targeted agent era should not be discarded.
