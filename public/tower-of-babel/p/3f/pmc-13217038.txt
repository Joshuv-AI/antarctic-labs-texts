MGRN1 is linked to recessive heart and laterality defects: the first genotype-phenotype report in humans
Authors: Laura Kasak, Kristiina Rull, Anu Valkna, Maris Laan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRecurrent idiopathic severe fetal structural anomalies present major challenges for reproductive decision-making and genetic counselling. A non-consanguineous healthy Estonian couple had experienced two electively terminated pregnancies at 12–13 weeks’ gestation due to unexplained major fetal malformations and one early miscarriage. Their three pregnancies had resulted in unaffected newborns. Exome sequencing of fetal tissues from both terminated pregnancies identified a homozygous rare missense variant in MGRN1 (mahogunin ring finger 1), c.881G>A, p.(Arg294His) (Genome Aggregation Database V.4.1.0 allele frequency<8×10⁻⁵; no homozygotes reported in any population database). MGRN1 functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase critical for protein homeostasis and developmental signalling. The detected substitution compromises a conserved residue within the RING (Really Interesting New Gene) finger domain. Cascade testing revealed that the parents and one unaffected child were heterozygous carriers, while two healthy siblings were homozygous for the reference allele. Both affected fetuses displayed congenital malformations closely mirroring prenatal phenotypes reported in Mgrn1 homozygous mutant mice, including cardiac malformations (ventricular septal defect, outflow tract malposition, pulmonary artery hypoplasia) and abnormal left-right axis patterning. These findings provide the first evidence implicating MGRN1 in human congenital disease and support its role in early embryonic patterning.Keywords: Exome Sequencing; Female Urogenital Diseases and Pregnancy Complications; Heart Defects, Congenital; Prenatal Diagnosis; Genetic Variation

Introduction

Recurrent, severe, unexplained fetal anomalies pose difficult challenges for families and highlight the urgent need for molecular diagnosis. Congenital heart defects (CHDs) are the most prevalent developmental malformations, affecting up to 2.2% of live-born children worldwide,1 and represent a leading cause of both medically induced pregnancy termination and early fetal demise.2 Laterality defects, congenital anomalies of left-right axis development that affect the positioning of internal organs, are rare but often associated with complex CHDs.3

Genetic factors are considered the main drivers of CHDs and laterality defects, reflecting disruption of conserved developmental pathways during early embryogenesis. Despite this, establishing confident phenotype-genotype correlations remains challenging in many cases due to marked phenotypic heterogeneity linked to the same gene or even variant.4 Genome or exome sequencing (ES)—recently termed ‘genomic autopsy’ in the prenatal setting—has become central to uncovering the genetic basis of unexplained fetal anomalies.5 6 However, many families remain undiagnosed, particularly when the causal gene has not previously been implicated in human developmental disorders.

We aimed to solve the genetic cause of an idiopathic family case from Estonia presenting severe developmental anomalies in two fetuses and an additional early miscarriage with an empty gestational sac. Through fetal genomic autopsy, involving comparative analysis of ES data from the affected fetuses and their parents, we identified MGRN1, encoding Mahogunin Ring Finger 1, as a potential novel candidate gene for autosomal recessive CHDs and laterality defects in humans.

Methods

Detailed materials and methods are described in the online supplemental information.

Patients and clinical data

We investigated a non-consanguineous Estonian family with unexplained recurrent severe fetal anomalies. Of six pregnancies, two were terminated at 12–13 weeks due to complex CHDs and marked laterality abnormalities, and one resulted in an early miscarriage with an empty gestational sac (no biological material available) (figure 1a, table 1). Clinical information for the terminated pregnancies was obtained from medical records; prenatal imaging and standard fetal autopsy data were collected during routine procedures at the Women’s Clinic of East-Tallinn Central Hospital. Both parents and three living children were healthy, and evaluation by a clinical geneticist revealed no family history of a hereditary disorder.

Genetic testing in routine clinical diagnostics

Autopsy tissue material of both affected fetuses was investigated by standard genetic tests at the Genetics and Personalized Medicine Clinic, Tartu University Hospital. Karyotype of cultured fetal skin fibroblasts was normal (II-3: 46,XX; II-5: 46,XY) (figure 1A). Chromosomal microarray analysis detected no major copy number abnormalities, and clinical ES yielded no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in known developmental genes (TruSight One Expanded panel for II-3; phenotype-driven trio ES for II-5).

Exome sequencing and bioinformatic processing in the research setting

Genomic DNA from both affected fetuses and parental blood underwent ES at the NGS service laboratory of the Institute for Molecular Medicine Finland. Libraries were prepared with the Twist Human Core Exome EF kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq system (2×101 bp reads). Reads were aligned to GRCh38 and variants called using the DRAGEN Bio-IT pipeline. On-target median coverage exceeded 100×, with >95% of bases covered at ≥20×.

Variant filtering and prioritisation

Variants with depth of coverage >10 and genotype quality >20 were annotated using Ensembl VEP v111 (flags and plugins listed in online supplemental table 1). The ES data analysis pipeline followed the approach used in previous studies of family-based cases.7,9 We considered rare (minor allele frequency <1% in Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) V.2.1.1) protein-truncating, missense or canonical splice variants that were detected in both affected fetuses (online supplemental table 2). Variants with CADD V.1.6 PHRED score ≤10, reported as benign/likely benign in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) or predicted benign/tolerated by both SIFT (Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant) and PolyPhen algorithms were filtered out. X-linked recessive inheritance was not considered due to different fetal sexes. Autosomal or X-linked dominant models were considered unlikely given the severe phenotype in multiple offspring and unaffected parents. Biallelic variants, possibly linked to an autosomal recessive condition, were prioritised. Parental relatedness was assessed using the SNPRelate R package,10 confirming the absence of consanguinity (kinship=0).

Variant interpretation and validation

Interpretation followed the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and ClinGen (https://clinicalgenome.org/tools/clingen-variant-classification-guidance), considering also data available in literature and genetics databases, including the gnomAD, All of Us, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, the NCBI ClinVar, DECIPHER and Genomics England PanelApp and PanelApp Australia (online supplemental table 3). Candidate variants were visually inspected with the Integrative Genomics Viewer (https://igv.org). Validation and cascade testing of the MGRN1 c.881G>A variant were performed by Sanger sequencing. Segregation analysis was performed using the genotypes of seven family members.

Results

A family with recurrent fetal developmental anomalies

A non-consanguineous couple had a history of three pregnancies with abnormal fetal development and three newborns with no congenital anomalies (figure 1a). Maternal age at the first and the last pregnancy was 28 years and 35 years, respectively.

In fetus II-3 (female), prenatal ultrasonography at 12 weeks’ gestation revealed fetal hydrops, markedly increased nuchal translucency (7 mm) and cardiac valve regurgitation. Fetal autopsy of the medically terminated pregnancy detected laterality defects and severe cardiac malformations, including levocardia with right aortic arch and mirror-image branching, ventricular septal defect (VSD), hypoplastic pulmonary trunk and ductus arteriosus (table 1). In fetus II-5 (male), prenatal imaging at 13 weeks’ gestation showed a complex CHD. Fetal autopsy after medically terminated pregnancy revealed isolated cardiac anomalies—dextroposition of the aorta, pulmonary artery hypoplasia and stenosis and a VSD—with normal visceral arrangement (table 1). No genetic abnormalities were detected with the standard molecular diagnostics pipeline in the clinical setting.

In addition, between the two medically terminated pregnancies, an early spontaneous pregnancy loss had occurred at 4+6 weeks’ gestation (II-4, figure 1a). Ultrasonography at that time revealed an empty gestational sac measuring 11 mm, with a small adjacent haematoma.

Identification of a homozygous MGRN1 p.Arg294His variant in both affected fetuses

ES data from fetal autopsy material of cases II-3 and II-5 were compared with parental exomes (peripheral blood cells) to identify rare genotypes shared by the fetuses but absent in the parents. After data filtering, 44 shared variants were retained, including 43 heterozygous and one homozygous variant (online supplemental table 4). Given the family history, biallelic variants were prioritised. A single plausible candidate variant was retained from the analysis—MGRN1 (mahogunin ring finger 1) c.881G>A, p.(Arg294His) (NC_000016.10:g.4673583G>A, hg38). Both fetuses with congenital anomalies were homozygous for the alternative allele (A/A), whereas the parents were heterozygous (G/A) (figure 1a,b). The variant is rare in population databases, including gnomAD V.4.1.0 (rs201033314; allele frequency<8×10⁻⁵; no homozygotes among 806 798 subjects) and All of Us (allele frequency 1.3×10⁻⁵; no homozygotes among 4 14 802 subjects). Cascade testing in the family revealed that two unaffected offspring were homozygous for the reference (G) allele, and the third was heterozygous for the alternative (A) allele (figure 1a,b). All three heterozygous carriers (parents, one offspring) had displayed normal growth, prenatal and postnatal development, and no clinical features suggestive of congenital disease.

MGRN1 (alias RNF156) functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase critical for protein homeostasis and developmental signalling.11 12 The affected residue, arginine 294, is positioned within the highly conserved RING (Really Interesting New Gene) Finger domain, a catalytic region essential for ubiquitin transfer by E3 ligases and a hotspot for variants predicted to exhibit a considerable effect on protein function13 (figure 1c,d). In silico prediction scores support a deleterious effect for the p.(Arg294His) substitution (CADD 34, REVEL 0.856).

No other potential disease-causing genotypes (compound heterozygous variants in biallelic or de novo variants in monoallelic disease genes) were identified in the affected fetuses.

Phenotypic overlap between human cases and Mgrn1 mouse models

Mice carrying Mgrn1 mutations display abnormal early embryonic patterning, left-right axis defects and prenatal lethality due to complex congenital heart malformations with variable penetrance.14 The reported mouse models harbour recessive variants that result in markedly reduced or absent Mgrn1 transcript expression, with the described phenotypes observed exclusively in homozygous animals. These findings are consistent with the developmental anomalies reported in affected fetuses of the investigated family (table 1, figure 1e).

Discussion

We identified a homozygous MGRN1 c.881G>A, p.(Arg294His) variant segregating in a non-consanguineous family with complex developmental anomalies—CHDs in two fetuses and additional laterality defects in one case. Previously, MGRN1 has not been linked to any human congenital disorders in the scientific literature or in major curated databases of human genome variation and its phenotypic consequences. Given the low carrier frequency of the MGRN1 c.881G>A variant in the Estonian population (~0.12%; allele frequency 6.2×10⁻⁴; gnomAD V.2.1.1), the probability that two unrelated subjects (ie, parents) would both be carriers solely by chance is extremely low (~1.5×10⁻⁶). Thus, the observed segregation of the homozygous AA genotype with recurrent fetal anomalies in this family is unlikely to be coincidental.

MGRN1 is dynamically expressed during early human heart development, with the highest levels observed at 4–5 weeks and 8–13 weeks post conception, followed by fluctuations later in pregnancy and a gradual decline postnatally.15 In mice, Mgrn1 is expressed from early embryogenesis, including the node and developing heart,14 and interacts with Megf8 (multiple EGF-like-domains 8) in regulating Hedgehog signalling, a pathway critical for cardiac morphogenesis and laterality.16 17 Consistent with these functions, Mgrn1-deficient murine models show abnormal embryonic patterning and complex cardiac malformations14 (figure 1e). Intermediate phenotypes in transgenic Mgrn1 mice18 parallel the overlapping but distinct cardiac and laterality anomalies observed in our two affected fetuses, supporting variable expressivity of MGRN1 variants.

The segregation pattern, extreme population rarity and recapitulation of key phenotypes in Mgrn1 mutant mice, along with the combination of thorough autopsy-based phenotyping and cascade genetic testing within the family, provide strong evidence supporting MGRN1 as a candidate gene for human autosomal recessive CHDs with or without laterality defects. Rare autosomal recessive genotypes are estimated to contribute to a small but clinically relevant proportion of cases with CHDs (~2%) and laterality defects (>5%),19 highlighting that even in non-consanguineous couples, this scenario should be considered.

However, limitations of this study must be acknowledged. The evidence for the genotype-phenotype links is based on two affected fetal cases from a single family, which restricts generalisability. Prenatal phenotyping was constrained by gestational age (12 weeks vs 13 weeks), with variability in cardiac and laterality findings, illustrating the challenges of establishing definitive genotype-phenotype correlations in the prenatal setting. The effect of the p.(Arg294His) substitution at the protein level was not experimentally assessed. While functional assays can be informative, they may fail to fully capture the complexities of in vivo conditions.20 This must be considered for pleiotropic genetic defects that affect developmental signalling over a prolonged period and across multiple tissues and organs. Finally, although the genetic and phenotypic data support a monogenic aetiology in this family, we cannot formally exclude more complex models, such as oligogenic contributions or pathogenic variants outside the protein-coding regions not captured by ES. Additional evidence supporting MGRN1 as a CHD gene would include the identification of unrelated individuals with biallelic MGRN1 variants and overlapping phenotypes, as well as segregation across multiple families. Under current ACMG guidelines, the MGRN1 c.881G>A, p.(Arg294His) variant fulfils the criteria PM2 (extreme rarity), PM3 (biallelic occurrence consistent with recessive inheritance) and PP3 (multiple computational predictions supporting a deleterious effect). However, in the absence of independent confirmation of the gene-disease relationship, the variant remains formally classified as a variant of uncertain significance.

In summary, we provide the first evidence for MGRN1 as a novel recessive gene implicated in human congenital heart malformations and laterality defects. Genomic autopsy is not yet routine, leaving a large proportion of congenital abnormality-related deaths unexplained.5 6 Our family case emphasises the value of combining detailed prenatal phenotyping, fetal autopsy, cascade testing and cross-species functional evidence to move variants beyond uncertain significance.21 A recent study of pregnancy loss and perinatal death showed that up to 26% of findings pointed to potentially novel candidate genes,6 underscoring the importance of detailed phenotyping at autopsy and follow-up investigations. As prenatal sequencing becomes increasingly routine, systematic reporting of such cases will be essential to reduce diagnostic uncertainty, facilitate accurate recurrence risk estimation and counsel the families experiencing severe fetal anomalies about reproductive options towards a healthy newborn.
