Split hamate fractures as part of complex ulnar carpometacarpal injuries: radiographic and functional outcomes
Authors: Julia Glaser, Evelin Schmidt, Martin Aman, Berthold Bickert, Leila Harhaus-Wähner, Tomke Cordts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSplit hamate fractures, which most often are a complicating part of fracture dislocations of the 5th or 5th and 4th carpometacarpal joints, are rare and challenging injuries that can severely affect hand function. The injury usually requires surgical treatment. However, both short- and long-term data on the effectiveness of these interventions are limited. The objective of this study is to advance understanding of this rare hand surgical condition by investigating its epidemiology, etiopathogenesis, subsequent complications, and functional outcomes.MethodsBetween 2010 and 2024, a total of 29 patients with a split hamate fracture were treated at a level-1 trauma center, of which 12 patients are included in this study. Using patient surveys (DASH, SF-36, PRWE), along with clinical and radiological examinations, the hand function of a total of 12 patients with split hamate fractures is evaluated.ResultsTwelve male patients with a split hamate fracture were included, with a mean age of 28.1 years at the time of surgery. In 10 patients (83%) the dominant right hand was affected, most involving, the injuries resulted from direct trauma or a fall; surgery was performed on average of 12 days after injury. Functional outcomes were favourable, with a mean DASH score of 14.1 and PRWE score of 14.8, indicating minimal residual reduction of function in most cases. Radiological follow-up showed complete fracture consolidation in 92% of patients, while degenerative changes and implant-related findings were observed in 58% and 42% of cases, respectively.ConclusionThis case series demonstrates that split hamate fracture can achieve excellent functional and radiological outcomes with surgical treatment, while also highlighting specific challenges in surgical approach and osteosynthesis.

Introduction

The hamate accounts for about 5% of all carpal bone fractures [1–3]. In this entity, split hamate fractures (SHF), which involve a complete coronal fracture through the body of the hamate, are a rare subtype; these fractures often result from high-energy trauma, such as a fall on the outstretched hand or a direct blow to the wrist, but can also occur due to axial loading of the hamate during forceful fist closure [4–6]. They are most often combined with a carpometacarpal (CMC) dislocation of the 5th or the 5th and 4th CMC joint- and complicate the overall clinical presentation [7, 8]. The hamate bone itself is a crucial component of the distal carpal row, articulating with the fourth and fifth metacarpals distally, as well as the triquetrum and capitate bones proximally, making it essential for proper hand function.

Fractures of the hamate have historically been classified based on their location, with early classifications distinguishing between fractures of the hamulus (Type 1) and fractures of the body (Type 2). In 2005, Hirano and Inoue expanded this system by dividing the rarer Type 2 fractures into coronal (Type 2 A) and transverse (Type 2B) subtypes [9]. Despite this classification, the morphology and extent of these fractures can vary significantly even within these subgroups. Most Type 2 A fractures involve only minor avulsions of the dorsal aspect of the hamate body (Fig. 1A). In contrast, split hamate fractures represent the most severe form of Type 2 A fractures, where the entire body of the hamate is split along its length, from the CMC joint to the articulation with the triquetrum (Fig. 1B).

Accurate diagnosis and treatment planning require a detailed assessment of fracture morphology and fragmentation [7, 10]. Standard imaging including posterior-anterior and lateral X-rays views are essential to show the CMC dislocation, but computed tomography (CT) is mandatory to assess the additional carpal fractures and the full extent of the injury. CMC dislocations associated with these fractures must be reduced and stabilized usually with wire transfixation. The treatment of hamate fractures depends on the size and extent of the fracture fragments. Minor avulsion fractures can often be managed conservatively, while larger fractures require either closed or open reduction with wire or screw fixation. In split hamate fractures, the significant disruption of carpal alignment necessitates surgical intervention in all cases to restore carpal stability, CMC joint surfaces and carpal height.

In treatment, the CMC dislocations are addressed first by reduction to a perfect alignment of the longitudinal axis of the metacarpals, and by stabilisation using K-wire fixation. The treatment of the hamate fracture depends on the size and extent of the fracture fragments. Whereas minor avulsion fragments can be managed without additional fixation, larger fractures require either closed or open reduction with K-wire or screw fixation. In split hamate fractures, the significant disruption of carpal alignment necessitates open reduction and screw fixation in many cases.

Currently, there is no isolated case series specifically addressing split hamate fractures. As a subtype of coronal hamate body fractures, these injuries are exceedingly rare and are usually seen only in larger hand surgery centers. The literature contains only a few large case series on hamate body fractures in general, with split hamate fractures appearing infrequently. For example, Ebraheim et al. reported 11 cases of hamate body fractures between 1992 and 1995 [11], but only one of these was a split hamate fracture. Hirano and Inoue’s 2005 study described 29 hamate fractures, of which five were classified as split hamate fractures [9]. Other studies, such as Wharton et al. in 2010, found 14 coronal hamate body fractures, though they did not further differentiate between subtypes [12]. Eder et al. documented 19 coronal hamate body fractures over five years at the Charité University Hospital in Berlin, but again did not distinguish between avulsion injuries and full splits [13].

Therefore, we aimed to analyze patients with a split hamate fractures as a distinct entity to further add literature to this rare topic.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a pseudonymized, retrospective analysis involving a clinical internal query within the controlling department for the period from 2010 to 2024. For analysis, we included all potentially relevant ICD codes for a split hamate fracture (S62.17, S62.10, S62.19, S62.8) within the framework of the G-DRG (German Diagnosis Related Groups) reimbursement system. This analysis identified 29 patients with a major hamate fracture, of which 25 were confirmed to have a true split hamate fracture with a fracture plane extending from the base of the fifth metacarpal to the triquetrum (Table 1). Following the approval of the Ethics Committee of the State Medical Association of Rhineland-Palatinate (Ethics approval number 2023–16957), these patients were contacted, and we received responses from 12 patients who consented to participate in the study.

Patients were invited to a follow-up examination in our institution. The examination was conducted at least three months postoperatively. In addition to current X-ray diagnostics of the affected wrist in two planes, we performed a CT scan using thin-section technique of the respective carpal region at the time of follow-up to assess the fracture’s healing process.

Patients were explicitly invited for a clinical follow-up examination after submission of the expert assessment, which included standardized measurements using finger and upper extremity assessment sheets according to the Neutral-0 method. In addition, an assessment was conducted using standardized questionnaires, including the DASH (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand) questionnaire, the PRWE-G (Patient-Rated Wrist Evaluation - German version), and the SF-36 (Short Form 36 Health Survey). The clinical examination was carried out according to a standard assessment protocol. This involved compiling data on fracture-related symptoms and functional limitations and analyzing them in conjunction with the other collected data to reach a comprehensive assessment.

Operative technique

All patients included in this study had undergone operative treatment of a split hamate fractures either by open or by closed surgical techniques, using screw or K-wire fixation. The choice of surgical approach was based on fracture morphology, joint involvement, and intraoperative stability assessment. Operations were performed by experienced hand surgeons.

Outcome parameter

The specific endpoints of this study were to evaluate the post-traumatic functional outcomes of the patients, with particular focus on their recovery and return to daily activities. Furthermore, the study aimed to investigate the relationship between radiological findings and clinical outcomes, including patient-reported outcomes such as pain and functional limitations. Special attention was given to assessing fracture healing and joint alignment, especially at the level of the carpometacarpal (CMC) joints. Finally, the positioning, integration, and overall condition of the implanted osteosynthesis materials were systematically evaluated to determine their impact on clinical recovery.

Results

Epidemiology

A total of 12 patients with split hamate fractures aged 18 to 41 (mean 28) years were included in this study. All patients were male (n = 12), and all but one were right-handed. The fracture dislocation occurred mostly on the right side (11 right vs. 1 left). In 10 out of 12 cases (83.3%), the fracture affected the dominant hand. All hamate fractures were split-hamate injuries. Concomitant CMC 4/5 fracture-dislocations or subluxations occurred in 7/12 cases, metacarpal base fractures in 6/12, comminuted fracture patterns in 4/12, and an associated capitate fracture in 1/12 patient. The injury mechanisms ranged from direct trauma (e.g., punching) to falls and motor vehicle accidents. The average time interval between the traumatic event and the surgical intervention was 12 days. At the time of follow-up, patients had a mean age of 31.6 years. The follow-up examinations were conducted after an average of 44.5 (± 24,95) months (3.7 years) postoperatively (Table 2).

Operative approach

Among the twelve cases, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) using cortical screws was performed in five patients, allowing direct visualization and anatomical reconstruction of the hamate fracture. In four cases, closed reduction followed by percutaneous Kirschner wire (K-wire) fixation was applied to stabilize the affected carpometacarpal (CMC) joints. Two patients received combined procedures addressing concurrent metacarpal base injuries, with closed reduction and temporary transfixation using K-wires. One patient underwent a complex combined approach with screw fixation and multiple K-wire stabilizations targeting both hamate and adjacent metacarpal fractures.

Clinical examination

At follow-up, patients reported mild residual pain with a mean Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score of 1.3 out of 10. Postoperative complications were documented in 1 out of 12 patients (8.3%), consisting of delayed wound healing requiring extended dressing changes, which did not affect the functional outcome. Five patients (42%) presented with persistent soft tissue swelling at the time of clinical follow-up. Grip strength on the affected side averaged 43.9 kg, which corresponds to 93.0% of the strength on the unaffected side (47.2 kg). The 2-point pinch strength showed a mean of 11.4 kg on the affected side and 10.8 kg on the unaffected side, representing 105.6%. On average, wrist extension reached 56.7° on the affected side compared to 67.9° on the unaffected side. Wrist flexion averaged 69.2° (affected) and 71.2° (unaffected). No clinically relevant deficits were observed in shoulder or elbow range of motion.

DASH and PRWE questionnaires

Functional outcome was assessed using the DASH (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand) and PRWE (Patient-Rated Wrist Evaluation) questionnaires. Both scores were calculated for each patient individually. The DASH scores ranged from 0.0 to 45.8 points, with a mean of 14.1 points. Most patients reported only minimal or mild upper limb disability, with eight patients scoring below 15 points. SF-36 questionnaires

Patients rated their general health status on average as “very good” to “good” (mean score: 2.1 on a scale from 1 = excellent to 5 = poor), including those with minor complications such as swelling and delayed wound healing, who also reported a good status. Functional limitations due to physical health were minimal, as most items in the physical functioning section averaged between 2.5 and 3.0 indicating little to no restrictions in daily activities.

Pain intensity and pain-related interference were also low, with scores averaging 2.0 to 2.2, suggesting mild or no significant chronic pain at follow-up. Similarly, questions addressing limitations due to emotional problems and social functioning showed favorable results, with most items scoring in the range of 1.5 to 2.0, reflecting low psychological burden and good mental health.

Radiological assessment

Radiological CT follow-up was performed in all 12 patients and systematically evaluated regarding fracture consolidation, joint congruity, degenerative changes, and implant-related findings. Fracture healing was complete in 11 cases (91.7%), while one patient (SH12) showed incomplete osseous consolidation with persistent fragment separation.

An additional case (SH1) exhibited signs of pseudarthrosis despite surgical screw fixation. However, the patient remained asymptomatic, and therefore no surgical revision was performed. At a follow-up of 39 months, the patient demonstrated a low level of disability with an individual DASH score of 4.61.

Joint alignment was classified as intact in 8 patients (66.7%), whereas 4 patients (33.3%) demonstrated joint surface irregularities, including small to pronounced step-offs or slight malpositioning, particularly in the carpometacarpal joints. Post-traumatic osteoarthritic changes were identified in 8 patients (66.7%) and ranged from mild subchondral sclerosis and pseudo-cystic changes to more advanced joint degeneration. Implant-related abnormalities were noted in 5 cases (41.7%), including screw protrusion beyond the cortical bone and radiolucent zones suggestive of loosening.

No cases of implant failure or infection were observed, all K-wires had been removed, and in two patients the screws had been removed, too, at the time of follow-up. Overall, the radiological findings reflect good consolidation in most cases, though frequently accompanied by mild to moderate degenerative changes and occasional implant-associated irregularities (Fig. 2). Discussion

This study presents the largest known case series of operatively treated split hamate fractures to date and offers a structured analysis of both clinical function and radiological healing outcomes after a mean follow-up of 3.7 years. The included patients were all young and male, with trauma predominantly affecting the dominant right hand. Functional results were generally favourable: the mean DASH score was 14.1 points, with eight of twelve patients scoring below 15, indicating only mild impairment in activities of daily living. These low values indicate that daily and occupational tasks were largely unaffected in most of the cohort. Similarly, the average PRWE score of 14.8 points suggested minimal wrist-related symptoms. These low values indicate that daily and occupational tasks were largely unaffected in most of the cohort. Grip strength on the injured side averaged 93% of the contralateral hand, and wrist mobility was only slightly reduced. Pain levels were low (VAS 1.3), and only one complication (delayed wound healing) was documented. Two patients demonstrated comparatively high DASH scores of 36.76 (SH4) and 45.83 (SH12). In patient SH4, no clear clinical correlate was identified despite very good functional performance; however, minor joint irregularities, including a step-off of approximately 2 mm at the base of the fourth metacarpal bone after fracture-dislocation, may potentially account for the reported symptoms. Patient SH12 had the shortest follow-up period (3 months); a longer follow-up might therefore reveal an improvement in the DASH score.

The surgical techniques applied varied depending on the fracture type and joint stability: five patients underwent open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) with screws, while others were treated with K-wire stabilization via closed or combined approaches. In addition, plate osteosynthesis represents a viable alternative, particularly in complex fracture patterns with carpometacarpal joint involvement, where dorsal buttress plating can restore joint congruity and provide stable fixation [14]. Importantly, all methods led to high rates of consolidation, with no evidence of implant failure or secondary dislocation, suggesting that both open and percutaneous techniques can be effective in restoring mechanical stability in these injuries.

In contrast, radiological evaluation revealed more frequent structural alterations than expected. Although fracture consolidation was achieved in 11 out of 12 patients (92%), 33% of patients showed joint surface incongruities, and two-thirds (8 out of 12) exhibited CT signs of post-traumatic degenerative changes—such as subchondral sclerosis, pseudocysts, osteophyte formation, or narrowing of the joint space. Implant-related abnormalities were also present in nearly half the cohort. None of these conditions, however, affected the functional outcome, and all patients remained almost pain-free as described in the SF-36 outcome. Even patients with radiographic evidence of arthrosis or minor joint malalignment did not necessarily report functional limitations or relevant pain. This mismatch between radiological pathology and subjective impairment highlights the complexity of interpreting long-term sequelae after hamate fractures and suggests that structural changes may not always translate into clinically meaningful deficits. It must also be acknowledged that it is not possible to clearly distinguish whether postoperative pain, described in the patient-related outcomes, originated from the split-hamate fracture itself or from associated CMC dislocations or fracture-dislocations, when present.

These findings are in line with the limited existing literature, which generally reports good to excellent outcomes following hamate fracture fixation, yet rarely includes detailed imaging or long-term follow-up. Our study extends this knowledge by emphasizing that, despite the presence of radiological abnormalities in a significant proportion of cases, functional recovery can be satisfactory. The importance of patient-reported outcomes and individualized follow-up assessments is underscored, as they may provide a more accurate picture of treatment success than imaging alone.

While our case study provides valuable insights into the immediate outcomes of hamate fractures, further research is essential to fully understand long-term recovery patterns and refine treatment protocols. Long-term studies are needed to track the progression of recovery and assess how different factors influence functional outcomes and radiological healing over time. Evidence from other carpal fractures, such as capitate fractures, highlights the importance of extended follow-up periods, as meaningful assessment of clinical and radiological outcomes—including the detection of late complications such as osteoarthritis—may only become apparent after many years [15].

One critical area of concern is the potential for post-fracture complications, such as delayed fracture consolidation or malunion. Nevertheless, the relatively long mean follow-up of 3.7 years provides robust mid-term outcome data. While this duration is sufficient to capture most clinically relevant complications, it remains possible that arthrotic changes may become symptomatic only beyond the current follow-up period. It is hypothesized that these issues, combined with the development of post-traumatic arthritis in the carpometacarpal joints, particularly CMC4 and CMC5, can significantly contribute to ongoing functional limitations and pain. Such complications underscore the importance of early and effective intervention to prevent adverse outcomes [16].

Further exploration into the factors influencing long-term recovery is crucial for improving outcomes in patients with hamate fractures. Although our study highlights good functional and radiological results in the short term, the persistence of symptoms or complications such as arthritis in the CMC joints could affect the long-term quality of life. Investigating the relationship between fracture healing and the development of arthritis could provide deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying these persistent issues.

Additionally, although our findings suggest effective initial treatment protocols, the variability in patient outcomes cannot be attributed to specific treatment strategies within the present study, as correlations between fracture type and osteosynthesis method were not performed. This highlights the need for more detailed, stratified analysis in future investigations.

This study is limited by its retrospective design and the sample size, may not fully capture the heterogeneity of split hamate fractures. A conservative treatment approach is generally not feasible, as these injuries are typically associated with concomitant carpometacarpal dislocations, necessitating surgical stabilization. Furthermore, although a standardized clinical and radiological follow-up protocol was applied, some variability in the timing and completeness of data collection may have influenced individual results. The cohort was also demographically homogenous, consisting entirely of young, male patients, which is consistent with the age distribution described in the literature but restricts the generalizability of the findings to broader populations.

Conclusion

The case study of 12 patients provides valuable insights into the fracture characteristics, surgical management and clinical outcomes of split hamate fractures. This focused approach allowed for a detailed examination of fracture healing, functional recovery, and the impact of various treatment strategies. Operative stabilization—whether via open reduction and screw fixation or closed reduction with K-wire techniques—resulted in reliable consolidation and favourable mid-term outcomes in this young population. Despite radiological signs of degenerative changes or joint incongruities in a subset of patients, clinical outcomes were largely positive, with low pain scores, preserved grip strength, and excellent functional performance reflected in DASH and PRWE results. These findings underscore the importance of tailored operative strategies and highlight that structural abnormalities on imaging do not always correlate with clinical impairment. Moreover, the study emphasizes the role of patient-reported outcomes and individualized follow-up in treatment evaluation. Addressing remaining challenges related to osteosynthesis and implant integration, future research should aim to optimize long-term recovery pathways and incorporate technological advancements (e.g. individual patient solution implants) to further refine treatment protocols for this rare but functionally relevant injury.
