Association of HDL-cholesterol related inflammatory ratios with reproductive outcomes in women with polycystic ovary syndrome undergoing frozen-thawed embryo transfer
Authors: Xiaoyu Zhang, Jun’an Yu, Qunyan Zhu, Ze Ren, Siqi Wen, Xuefeng Huang, Zhiyang Zhou, Sisi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine disorder and a major cause of anovulatory infertility, often requiring assisted reproductive technology (ART). The freeze-all strategy followed by frozen-thawed embryo transfer (FET) can reduce the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) and improve endometrial receptivity. However, women with PCOS still experience suboptimal reproductive outcomes. Increasing evidence suggests that chronic low-grade inflammation may contribute to these impairments. Conventional inflammatory markers, such as circulating cytokines, are not routinely assessed in clinical practice, underscoring the need for simple and accessible indicators. Recently, several hematologic and lipid-based inflammatory ratios—lymphocyte-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (LHR), neutrophil-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (NHR), platelet-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (PHR) and monocyte-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (MHR)—have been proposed as accessible indicators of systemic inflammation. Nevertheless, their predictive value for ART outcomes in women with PCOS remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 1445 women with PCOS undergoing their first frozen embryo transfer cycle at the reproductive medicine center of a tertiary hospital between 2010 and 2023. The associations of reproductive outcomes with NHR, LHR, PHR and MHR were assessed using logistic regression models with or without adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsHigher quartiles of NHR, LHR, and PHR were associated with increased miscarriage rates and decreased live birth rates, whereas no consistent pattern was observed for MHR. No significant differences were found across quartiles in biochemical pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, preterm delivery and low birth weight. Compared with women in the lowest quartile, those in the highest quartile had significantly higher odds of miscarriage for NHR (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 2.51, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.39–4.63), LHR (2.32, 1.23–4.49) and PHR (2.47, 1.34–4.64). In addition, LHR and PHR were inversely associated with live birth after full adjustment.ConclusionElevated NHR, LHR, and PHR were associated with adverse reproductive outcomes in women with PCOS undergoing FET. These findings suggest that HDL-C related inflammatory ratios may have potential value as accessible markers of systemic inflammation in this population. Background

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent reproductive and endocrine disorders, affecting approximately 4% to 21% of women of reproductive age worldwide [1]. It is characterized by ovulatory dysfunction, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology, and is frequently accompanied by metabolic disturbances such as insulin resistance, obesity, and dyslipidemia [2]. Beyond infertility, women with PCOS face an increased risk of long-term complications including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and pregnancy-related morbidity, making it a major health concern [3]. An increasing proportion of women with PCOS seek assisted reproductive technology (ART), such as in vitro fertilization (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), to achieve pregnancy due to impaired oocyte maturation or other infertility factors [4, 5].

Although women with PCOS demonstrate a favorable ovarian reserve during controlled ovarian stimulation (COS), they usually experienced an increased risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) during fresh embryo transfer cycles[6, 7]. Furthermore, supraphysiological hormonal exposure during fresh transfers may impair endometrial receptivity, contributing to suboptimal reproductive outcomes [8]. To address these challenges, the “freeze-all” strategy followed by frozen-thawed embryo transfer (FET) has been widely adopted in this population [9, 10]. This approach substantially reduces OHSS risk and may enhance endometrial synchronization. Nevertheless, even in FET cycles, women with PCOS remain at increased risk of adverse reproductive outcomes, including miscarriage, preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and preterm birth [11–13]. To date, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly elucidated.

Emerging evidence suggests that chronic low-grade inflammation is a key feature in the pathogenesis of PCOS and may play a pivotal role in its reproductive and metabolic dysfunction [14]. Elevated levels of systemic inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), complement component 3 (C3), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-18 (IL-18), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), have been consistently reported in PCOS women [15, 16]. Such systemic inflammatory activation has been linked to impaired oocyte quality, disrupted embryo development, and reduced endometrial receptivity, thereby compromising ART outcomes [17, 18]. However, the measurement of these markers requires specialized assays and laboratory resources, limiting their applicability in routine clinical practice. This underscores the need for cost-effective and readily accessible biomarkers that can capture systemic inflammation and predict reproductive prognosis in PCOS patients.

Recently, several hematological indices derived from routine blood counts have been proposed as simple, inexpensive, and reproducible markers of systemic inflammation. Ratios such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have been reported to be significantly elevated in PCOS compared with healthy controls in meta-analyses [19, 20]. More recently, novel composite indices that integrate immune cell counts with lipid metabolism—namely, the monocyte-to-high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio (MHR), lymphocyte-to-HDL-C ratio (LHR), neutrophil-to-HDL-C ratio (NHR), and platelet-to-HDL-C ratio (PHR)—have attracted increasing attention. These ratios are thought to reflect both inflammation status and HDL-C’s anti-inflammatory, antioxidant capacity, which are often impaired in women with PCOS [21]. Indeed, Usta et al. reported that MHR was significantly elevated in PCOS women and remained an independent predictor of the syndrome after adjustment for BMI, insulin resistance, and high-sensitivity CRP [22]. Similarly, Herkiloglu et al. demonstrated that MHR level was particularly increased in obese PCOS women, with a cutoff of 10.1 showing good sensitivity and moderate specificity for diagnosing PCOS [23]. Despite these insights, the prognostic value of these HDL-C related inflammatory ratios for reproductive outcomes in PCOS, particularly in the context of FET cycles, has not yet been systematically examined.

Therefore, the objective of the current study was to investigate the associations between HDL-C related inflammation ratios, including the LHR, NHR, PHR and MHR, and reproductive outcomes in women with PCOS undergoing FET. By integrating systemic inflammatory and lipid-related parameters, this study aimed to identify accessible biomarkers that may support individualized reproductive management in this high-risk population.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study included a total of 5121 infertile women with PCOS at the Reproductive Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University from 2010 to 2023. The diagnosis of PCOS was based on the Rotterdam consensus criteria [24], requiring at least two out of three features to be present: (1) oligo- or anovulation; (2) clinical or biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism; and (3) polycystic ovaries on ultrasound. The exclusion criteria included: (1) females aged over 40 years; (2) couples undergoing in vitro maturation, or preimplantation genetic testing; (3) severe male factor (e.g. sperm biopsy); (4) female factors affecting clinical outcomes, including hydrosalpinx, endometriosis or adenomyosis, endocrine dysfunction, thyroid dysfunction, abnormal renal and liver function, chromosome abnormality, uterine malformation, recurrent miscarriage; (5) missing or ongoing pregnancy outcomes; and (6) missing monocyte, lymphocyte, neutrophil, platelets count or HDL-C test values.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (No: KY2024-R117). Written content was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study and all data were deidentified.

ART procedure

Controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) was performed according to standardized clinical protocols. Oocyte retrieval was conducted 34–36 h after hCG trigger and the oocytes were fertilized through conventional in vitro fertilization (IVF), intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), or IVF+ICSI based on semen condition. Day 3 embryos were graded (A-D) using the ASEBIR criteria [25], with top-quality embryos (grades A/B) prioritized for fresh transfer or cryopreservation, while remaining embryos cultured to the blastocyst stage. Endometrial preparation for FET followed the routine protocols of our center, and luteal phase support was administered post-transfer until confirmation of pregnancy.

Laboratory testing

As part of the routine baseline evaluation before initiation of IVF/ICSI treatment, blood samples were collected after an overnight fast of at least 8 h. Baseline peripheral monocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes and platelets count, along with serum total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), HDL-C and fasting blood glucose (FBG), were quantified by an automatic blood analyser. Sex hormones were measured with an ultrasensitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. HDL-C related inflammation index (NHR, LHR, PHR and MHR) were defined respectively as neutrophil, lymphocyte, platelet and monocyte count (×109/L) divided by HDL-C concentration (mmol/L). Since these inflammation index exhibited skewed distributions, log2 transformation was applied to approximate normality when used as continuous variables.

Outcome definition

Our primary outcome was live birth, referring to the delivery of at least one live-born infant. Secondary outcomes were (1) biochemical pregnancy, confirmed by a serum β-hCG level > 10 mIU/mL at 12–15 days post-fertilization; (2) clinical pregnancy, defined as the presence of intrauterine gestational sac with fetal heartbeat at 6–7 weeks; (3) miscarriage, termination of a pregnancy before 22 weeks of gestation; (4) preterm birth, delivery after 22 but before 37 completed weeks of gestation; and (5) low birth weight, defined as birth weight < 2500 g for singletons or at least one neonate < 2500 g in twin deliveries.

Laboratory and embryological outcomes were evaluated by the number of oocytes retrieved, oocyte maturity rate (proportion of MII oocytes among all oocytes retrieved), fertilization rate (proportion of normally fertilized 2PN zygotes among mature oocytes), cleavage rate (percentage of cleavage-stage embryos obtained from normally fertilized 2PN zygotes), optimal cleavage embryo rate (proportion of grade A/B embryos among all cleavage-stage embryos), blastocyst formation rate (proportion of embryos reaching blastocyst stage among those cultured beyond day 3), and optimal blastocyst rate (proportion of blastocysts with morphology graded ≥3BB according to the Gardner classification, among all blastocysts).

Statistical analysis

Outliers were identified from the distribution of each covariate, with values above the 99th percentile treated as missing. Baseline characteristics were compared between women with missing and available exposure data in the original pre-imputation dataset to assess potential selection bias. Overall, the two groups were broadly comparable, although some differences were observed (Supplemental Table 1). Missing covariate data were then imputed using 5-fold multiple imputation based on the Markov Chain Monte Carlo approach, whereas variables with more than 25% missingness (e.g., Anti-mullerian hormone, AMH) were excluded from further analysis. Characteristics were similar between complete-case and imputed datasets, and the missing rates ranged from 0.00% to 6.44% (Supplemental Table 2). Continuous variables were presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)] due to non-normal distribution, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages). We used the four HDL-C related inflammation indexes, NHR, LHR, PHR and MHR, as independent variables and stratified the study population according to their quartiles. Baseline and clinical characteristics across groups were compared using Kruskal-Wallis H test for continuous variables and Pearson Chi-square test for categorical variables. In addition, embryonic and reproductive outcomes of the groups were compared using the same statistical methods.

Feature selection is an important step in model construction. Age and weight status were included a priori because of their established clinical relevance. Additional covariates for multivariable adjustment were selected using group lasso logistic regression with live birth as the outcome (Supplemental Figure 1). This approach was used to reduce overfitting, address potential multicollinearity among candidate predictors and handle categorical variables appropriately. Candidate predictors comprised the baseline and clinical treatment characteristics presented in Table 1, with the exception of AMH, which was excluded because of substantial missingness. Using 10-fold cross-validation, the group lasso procedure identified following covariates as additional covariates for adjustment at the optimal λ selected by the minimum binomial deviance criterion: follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), FBG, TG, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and fertilization method (IVF; ICSI; IVF/ICSI).

Logistic regression models were used to examine the associations between reproductive outcomes and each HDL-C related inflammation index, with odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) estimated for each. Model 1 represents a univariate analysis. Model 2 was adjusted for age and weight status (underweight, normal weight; overweight; vs. obesity; according to Chinese body mass index classification) [26]. Model 3 was further adjusted for covariates as indicated by the group lasso selection. Nonlinear associations of HDL-C related inflammation indexes (log2 transformed) with reproductive outcomes were explored using the restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression model with three nodes set, adjusting for covariates in Model 3.

To assess the potential influence of temporal changes in ART practice over the study period, we performed two sensitivity analyses by additionally adjusting Model 3 for calendar year and by stratifying the analysis of live birth according to calendar period (2010–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2023). In addition, since AMH is an important marker of ovarian reserve but had substantial missingness in our study, we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to women with available AMH data to assess whether further adjustment for AMH altered the observed associations.

All analyses and plotting were performed using R statistical Software (version 4.4.1), with P values < 0.05 considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Of 5121 infertile women with PCOS treated at our center between 2010 and 2023, 1445 women undergoing their first FET cycles were included in the final analysis after applying the predefined exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Their baseline and clinical treatment characteristics, stratified by HDL-C related inflammatory indexes, are shown in Table 1. The participants had a median age of 29 years and a median infertility duration of 3 years. The characteristics showed approximately the same trend across four indexes, with patients in the higher-quartile group tending to be obese, having higher values for SBP, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), basal testosterone, FBG, TG, and LDL-C but lower values of AMH and basal FSH, compared to those in the lower-quartile group. In addition, use of a GnRH-antagonist protocol during controlled ovarian stimulation was more frequent in higher-quartile groups. Fertilization method, the number of embryos transferred, and transfer stage were generally comparable across quartiles, with the exception of higher proportion of single-embryo transfer in the upper NHR and PHR quartiles and higher proportion of cleavage-stage transfer in the upper MHR quartiles.

For MHR, only Q2 showed 90% higher odds of miscarriage versus Q1, with no does-response pattern (P for trend = 0.212). In addition, women in the upper quartiles of LHR (Q3: 0.66, 0.48–0.90; Q4: 0.69, 0.48–0.98) and PHR (Q3: 0.71, 0.51–0.98; Q4 0.58, 0.41–0.82) had lower odds of live birth than those in Q1 after fully adjustment. The initially observed inverse association between NHR and live birth in both the unadjusted (model 1) and partially adjusted (model 2) models was attenuated and no longer significant after fully adjustment (model 3). For clinical pregnancy, an inverse association was observed only for PHR Q4 in model 1 and 2, and this association was not retained in model 3.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we observed that several HDL-C related inflammatory indices were associated with reproductive outcomes in women with PCOS undergoing FET. Higher levels of NHR, LHR, and PHR were consistently associated with an increased risk of miscarriage, while LHR and PHR were additionally linked to reduced live-birth rates. In contrast, MHR showed a weaker and no dose-dependent association. Embryological parameters, including fertilization and cleavage rates, were comparable across quartiles. Notably, these associations were observed for pregnancy outcomes but not for early embryological parameters, suggesting that these indices may reflect systemic conditions relevant to implantation and early pregnancy maintenance rather than oocyte or embryo competence.

Chronic low-grade inflammation is a well-recognized feature of PCOS and has been implicated in multiple aspects of reproductive dysfunction [15, 27]. Elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and CRP, have been reported and are closely associated with metabolic disturbances and subfertility in this population [16, 28]. In the context of assisted reproduction, systemic inflammation has been linked to impaired oocyte quality, reduced embryo viability, and compromised implantation [29, 30]. Our findings are broadly consistent with previous reports linking composite inflammatory indices to adverse IVF outcomes, and extend this concept to HDL-C related inflammatory indices in FET cycles.

Beyond systemic cytokines, HDL particles are increasingly recognized as active regulators of inflammation and oxidative stress. Accumulating evidence indicates that HDL exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, contributing to the maintenance of endothelial function [31–33]. However, several studies indicates that HDL-C function is impaired in women with PCOS. For example, obese PCOS patients exhibited reduced paraoxonase 1 (PON1) activity and altered HDL-C subclass distribution, reflecting diminished antioxidative capacity [34]. Similarly, elevated HDL-C inflammatory index values and oxidative stress markers have been linked to decreased sex hormone-binding globulin levels, highlighting the interplay between HDL-C dysfunction, oxidative stress, and endocrine imbalance [35]. Even in non-obese PCOS women, proteomic profiling has revealed lower ApoM and increased complement C3 in HDL-C associated proteins, suggesting intrinsic abnormalities in HDL-C composition and function [36]. These alterations may weaken HDL-C’s capacity to counteract immune cell-mediated inflammation, thereby contributing to impaired reproductive function.

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to comprehensively evaluate four HDL-C related inflammatory ratios (LHR, NHR, PHR, and MHR) in relation to reproductive outcomes in PCOS women undergoing FET. The observed association between NHR and miscarriage may reflect neutrophil-driven inflammation, as activated neutrophils release reactive oxygen species and proteolytic enzymes that can disrupt both follicular and endometrial environments [37, 38]. Similarly, an elevated PHR may indicate platelet activation, which promotes pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic pathways and has been implicated in adverse pregnancy outcomes [39]. In contrast, LHR integrates lymphocyte counts with HDL-C and may capture impaired immune tolerance at the maternal-fetal interface [40, 41]. Reduced lymphocyte activity has been associated with inadequate T-cell-mediated regulation and failure to establish maternal tolerance during implantation [40–42]. Consistent with these observations, our findings revealed that higher PHR and LHR were associated with both increased miscarriage and reduced live-birth rates, suggesting their potential involvement in processes related to pregnancy maintenance.

Although MHR is frequently elevated in PCOS and correlates with insulin resistance [22, 23], our study revealed only a weak and no dose-dependent association between MHR and reproductive outcomes in PCOS women undergoing FET. This finding suggests that its predictive value in this context may be limited and should be interpreted with caution. One possible explanation is that MHR primarily reflects metabolically driven monocyte activation, which may not directly influence peri-implantation immune processes once metabolic confounders are considered [43]. In addition, HDL-C, used as the denominator of MHR, reflects circulating cholesterol levels rather than HDL functional capacity. Given that HDL dysfunction is well documented in PCOS, HDL-C levels alone may not adequately capture the lipid-immune interactions relevant to successful implantation [44]. These considerations may partly explain the limited predictive performance of MHR in our analysis.

From a clinical perspective, these HDL-C related inflammatory indices offer several advantages. They can be easily calculated from routine complete blood counts and lipid profiles, making them inexpensive, reproducible, and feasible for clinical implementation [45, 46]. Their value is particularly relevant in the context of FET cycles, which have become the preferred strategy for PCOS patients owing to the elevated risk of OHSS in fresh embryo transfer [6, 9]. In the setting of FET, simple inflammatory ratios may provide additional information on systemic inflammatory status. While these indices show potential for identifying women at higher risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, their role in guiding personalized management strategies remains to be established. At present, their use should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

In this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and single-center setting may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. The long study period also raises the possibility of heterogeneity related to evolving clinical protocols, although adjustment for calendar time did not materially alter the results. Second, inflammatory indices were measured at a single pre-treatment time point, and temporal changes during ovarian stimulation and early pregnancy were not captured. Third, although we adjusted for several covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. In addition, the lack of detailed cytokine or immune profiling limits mechanistic interpretation. Finally, clinically relevant cut-off values for these indices have not been established in reproductive populations, which restricts their immediate translational value.

Future studies should prioritize prospective validation in multicenter cohorts and the establishment of clinically meaningful thresholds for risk stratification. Integration of these indices with established predictors into multivariable models may improve risk assessment. In parallel, mechanistic studies incorporating systemic inflammatory markers and endometrial immune profiling are needed to clarify underlying pathways. Whether interventions targeting systemic inflammation can modify these indices and improve ART outcomes in women with PCOS also warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, elevated NHR, LHR, and PHR were independently associated with increased miscarriage risk, while higher LHR and PHR were also related to reduced live-birth rates in women with PCOS undergoing FET. These findings highlight HDL-C related inflammatory indices as promising, cost-effective biomarkers for reproductive prognosis in this high-risk population. However, prospective validation and mechanistic studies are essential before their incorporation into routine clinical practice.
