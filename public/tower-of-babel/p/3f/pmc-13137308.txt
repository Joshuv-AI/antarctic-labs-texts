Dual task performance in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus: A systematic review with meta‐analysis
Authors: Cristián Barros‐Osorio, Eduardo Sandoval‐Obando, Cintya Odar‐Rojas, Iván Cuyul‐Vásquez, David Martínez‐Pernía, Walter Sepúlveda‐Loyola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDaily activities require the simultaneous execution of cognitive and motor tasks. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is associated with functional impairments that may compromise dual‐task performance, leading to increased dual‐task interference.ObjectiveTo compare dual‐task performance and dual‐task interference between individuals with T2DM and healthy control subjects.MethodsThis study followed the reporting recommendations of the PRISMA statement. The literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, WoS, LILACS, and PEDro databases. We included studies involving individuals diagnosed with T2DM aged over 18 years, assessed using any dual‐task evaluation protocol and compared to healthy control subjects. Methodological quality was independently assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist and meta‐analyses were performed using JAMOVI software.ResultsOf the 1,864 articles initially identified, 6 studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising a total of 468 participants. Individuals with T2DM exhibited poorer motor performance during dual‐task evaluations, including reduced gait speed (SMD = −0.74 [95%CI: −1.22 to −0.27], P = 0.002) and longer stride times (SMD = 0.34 [95%CI: 0.03 to 0.65], P = 0.039). Additionally, they demonstrated greater dual‐task interference in both motor (SMD = −0.67 [95%CI: −1.16 to −0.19], P = 0.006) and cognitive tasks (SMD = −0.33 [95%CI: −0.66 to −0.03], P = 0.033) compared to healthy control subjects.ConclusionIndividuals with T2DM exhibit poorer dual‐task performance and greater dual‐task interference. These findings highlight the importance of including DT assessments in the clinical evaluation of individuals with T2DM. However, due to the limited number of studies, further research is warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic, preventable, and treatable metabolic disease characterized by impaired insulin function and signaling, leading to increased glucose levels and insulin resistance in peripheral tissues 1 . T2DM has been associated with decreased muscle strength, reduced skeletal muscle mass, diminished aerobic capacity, postural balance disorders, and an increased risk of falls 2 , 3 . These physical impairments in individuals with T2DM negatively affect their ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs) and their quality of life 4 . Furthermore, these patients have demonstrated cognitive impairments, including deficits in attention, memory, and verbal fluency, among others 5 . The interaction between the physical and cognitive consequences of T2DM can create challenges in performing dual tasks 6 , thereby reducing their ability to engage in daily activities and maintain an independent lifestyle 7 .

Dual‐task (DT) performance is defined as the simultaneous execution of cognitive and motor tasks 8 . DT performance is essential for evaluating functionality in individuals with T2DM, as most ADLs require concurrent cognitive and motor tasks 9 . DT performance can be assessed through motor activities such as gait, static and dynamic balance, or muscular strength exercises, combined with cognitive tests such as verbal fluency, mathematical problem‐solving, visuospatial working memory, or visuospatial attention 10 , 11 . Assessing DT performance can reveal dual‐task interference (DTI) 12 , which occurs when the simultaneous execution of two tasks (motor‐cognitive) impairs the performance of one or both tasks due to increased competition for processing and attentional resources 13 .

Studies have highlighted the relevance of assessing DT since they have reported that increased DTI is associated with reduced postural control, balance, gait speed, muscle strength, and executive functions in different populations 14 . Thus, evaluating DT performance and calculating the DTI in individuals with T2DM is crucial to understanding its impact on motor and cognitive parameters 15 . While previous research has shown that individuals with T2DM tend to exhibit poorer performance on both motor and cognitive tests, the degree to which simultaneous cognitive‐motor task interference occurs remains unclear 2 , 3 , 5 . DTI holds high clinical relevance, as the ability to perform dual tasks is necessary for the execution of most activities of daily living 8 , 9 . However, to date, no systematic reviews have compared DTI between individuals with T2DM and healthy control subjects (HCS). Therefore, the aim of this systematic review was to compare the performance during DT and DTI between individuals with T2DM and HCS, aiming to provide key insights to inform the design of strategies for comprehensive clinical management in this population.

METHODS

This study is a systematic literature review with meta‐analysis conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement 16 . The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024619313).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (i) Population: Adults >18 years diagnosed with T2DM; (ii) Exposure: Individuals assessed using dual‐task tests, defined as the simultaneous execution of a motor and a cognitive task; (iii) Comparator: T2DM individuals compared to age‐ and sex‐matched HCS; and (iv) Outcomes: the performance on DT (motor and/or cognitive interference) and protocols for dual‐task assessment. Observational cohort or cross‐sectional studies, clinical trials, and randomized trials including structured cognitive, motor, or dual‐task assessments were included.

Studies were excluded if they: (i) did not describe a dual‐task protocol or (ii) included individuals with T2DM‐related complications such as amputations, neuropathy, or diabetic retinopathy.

Information sources and search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in five electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, WoS, LILACS, and PEDro) using the terms “type 2 diabetes mellitus,” “diabetes mellitus,” “non‐insulin dependent diabetes mellitus,” “adult‐onset diabetes mellitus,” “dual‐task,” “motor task,” “cognitive task,” and “multitask task” (see Appendix A). The search was performed up to May 2025.

Selection process and data extraction

Articles were screened for eligibility based on titles and abstracts by two independent reviewers (I.C‐V. and C.O‐R.), who classified the citations as “include,” “exclude,” or “maybe.” Articles classified as “include” or “maybe” were reviewed in full text. Disagreements were resolved by a third independent reviewer (W.S.L.). Database search results were managed using Zotero Reference Manager and Rayyan software, and duplicates were removed. Data was extracted by two team members (I.C‐V. and C.O‐R.). The following information was extracted: Characteristics of the population (sample size, age, gender); DT assessments (motor and cognitive); and main results.

Study risk of bias assessment

The quality of studies was assessed using validated tools adapted to the study type, specifically the JBI Checklist for observational, transversal, and quasi‐experimental studies to evaluate the risk of bias 17 .

Synthesis methods

A quantitative synthesis was performed if there were at least three studies with comparable data. A quantitative synthesis was performed for the gait speed, stride time, motor DTI, and cognitive DTI. Motor DTI was measured as the reduction in motor performance when a motor task was performed concurrently with a cognitive task. Cognitive DTI was measured as the decline in cognitive performance when a cognitive task was performed simultaneously with a motor task. A meta‐analysis was conducted to calculate pooled estimates using the standardized mean difference (SMD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the overall outcomes across studies 18 . The weighted sample size method was used to summarize effect sizes from multiple independent studies. Fixed‐effects models with the Mantel–Haenszel method or random‐effects models with the DerSimonian‐Laird method were used depending on the degree of heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was evaluated using visual inspection (overlapping CIs) and the I2 statistic, with thresholds of 0%–40% for non‐important, 30%–60% for moderate, 50%–90% for substantial, and 75%–100% for considerable heterogeneity 19 . Meta‐analysis was performed using JAMOVI software 5.4. Publication bias was assessed visually through funnel plots and using the method proposed by Sterne, Egger, and Smith (see Appendix B, C, D, E (Figures A1, A2, A3, A4)) 20 . A descriptive analysis (narrative synthesis) of the qualitative findings from selected studies was also presented. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, meta‐regression analyses were conducted for each outcome using continuous moderators. The dependent variable was the study‐specific effect size (standardized mean difference), and the independent variable was the moderator of interest. The standard error (SE) of each effect size was used to compute the sampling variance, and inverse‐variance weighting was applied in all regression models. Meta‐regressions were performed using a weighted least squares approach in Python (version 3.11), and results were reported as regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and P‐values. For moderators with fewer than three studies providing complete data, meta‐regression was not conducted. Separate analyses were performed for each outcome included in the meta‐analysis.

Certainty assessment

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to assess the overall certainty of evidence 21 , evaluating study limitations, consistency of effects, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Evidence quality was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low. The GRADE profiler (GRADEpro) was used to create a “Summary of Findings” table.

DT evaluation protocols

To assess motor performance, gait analysis and lower limb muscle strength evaluations were conducted in combination with various cognitive tasks 23 , 24 , 26 . Gait analysis was performed using a 4.88 m automated walkway (GAITRite, CIR Systems Inc., New York, NY, USA) equipped with integrated pressure sensors 22 , Shimmer® 3 inertial sensors to measure parameters such as stride time, stance, swing, heel strike, and toe‐off during gait evaluation 23 , and the GaitMat II system (E.Q., Inc., Chalfont, PA), which consists of a 4 m pressure‐sensitive walkway 24 . For lower limb muscle strength evaluation, the “30‐s sit‐to‐stand” test was used, and the time required to complete the test was measured using the Chronojump‐Bosco System™ software 26 . Regarding the static balance assessment, a computerized posturography system (NeuroCom) was used to measure stability in a still position and during double weight tasks 27 and a measurement system based on a sway meter manufactured by the company CDR System in order to evaluate the movement of the center of pressure, reflecting postural stability 25 .

To assess the performance in cognitive tests, calculation, memory, and verbal fluency were employed. For calculation tests, T2DM and HCS were asked to subtract 7 consecutively starting from 96 22 , while in another test, participants were instructed to count backward by 3 s starting from 100 26 . Other tests included counting in series of 3 and 7 27 . Regarding memory tests, participants were asked to recall the longest possible sequence of numbers and recite it while performing the gait activity 24 and repeat words heard through headphones at different levels of difficulty, both in single‐task and dual‐task conditions (combined with balance) 25 . For verbal fluency, participants were asked to alternate recitation of alphabet letters (A‐C‐E) 23 (Table 1). Finally, the formula used to calculate the DTI for both cognitive and motor performance is presented in Figure 2 23 .

Formula used to analyze dual‐task performance. Dual‐task interference (DTI) was calculated as the relative changes in performance between dual‐task and single‐task conditions using the following formula: DTI (%): (dual‐task – single‐task)/single‐task × 100.

Dual‐task performance

T2DM individuals exhibited poorer motor performance compared to HCS during DT assessments, as evidenced by alterations in gait parameters, including prolonged swing time, stance and single‐leg stance times, and reduced cadence. 22 , 23 , 24 , alterations in lower extremity muscle strength 26 and postural control, measured through static balance 25 , 27 . Individuals with T2DM when motor and cognitive tasks were performed simultaneously presented reduced gait speed (SMD = −0.74 [95% CI: −1.2198 to −0.2729], P = 0.0020) and longer stride time (SMD = 0.3389 [95% CI: 0.0294 to 0.6485], P = 0.039), compared to HCS (Figures 3 and 4 respectively). Publication bias for gait speed and stride time was evaluated using funnel plots (Appendices B, C). In addition, T2DM individuals also presented lower cognitive performance compared to HCS, characterized by reduced performance in visual processing, memory, global cognitive performance, and verbal fluency 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 . Risk of bias in studies

There was a very low quality of evidence according to the GRADE rating (Table 3). Fifty percent of the studies 22 , 23 , 27 reported low risk of bias (6 to 7 points) and 16.67% of the studies 25 had a medium risk of bias (5 points). In contrast, the other 33.33% 24 , 26 , were categorized as high risk of bias (1 to 2 points). Specifically, studies with low methodological quality exhibited the following main issues: (i) unclear definition of inclusion criteria, (ii) insufficient detailed description of study subjects and their context, (v) inadequate identification of confounding factors, and (vi) unreliable outcome measurement methods. Meta‐regression

Meta‐regression analyses were performed to examine the influence of age, body mass index, and proportion of men on effect sizes (Table 5). For Gait Speed, no significant associations were observed (Age: β = −0.039, 95% CI –0.261 to 0.184, P = 0.271; BMI: β = 0.406, 95% CI –5.797 to 6.610, P = 0.558; % men: β = 0.032, 95% CI –0.173 to 0.236, P = 0.298). For Stride time, the proportion of men was significantly associated with the effect size (β = −0.023, 95% CI –0.0436 to −0.0025, P = 0.045), while age and BMI were not. For Cognitive DTI, neither age nor proportion of men were significantly related to the effect size; BMI was excluded due to insufficient data (n = 2 studies). For Motor DTI, BMI showed a significant inverse association with the effect size (β = −0.433, 95% CI –0.736 to −0.130, P = 0.020), whereas age and proportion of men were not significant predictors. In addition, publication bias was assessed for sensitive analysis of motor DTI, excluding studies with a high risk of bias using a funnel plot (Appendix F (Figure A5)).

Sensitivity analysis

To examine the robustness of the pooled effect, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding studies rated as having a high risk of bias (Rucker et al. and Barrios‐Fernández et al.), which met only one or two methodological quality criteria 24 , 26 . After excluding the high‐risk‐of‐bias studies, the pooled estimate remained significant and even slightly larger (SMD = −0.94; 95% CI: −1.46 to −0.43; P < 0.001), while heterogeneity was markedly reduced (I2 = 75.7%) (Figure 8). This stability of the pooled effect after removal of lower‐quality studies indicates that the main findings are robust and not driven by studies at high risk of bias. For the remaining outcomes, sensitivity analyses could not be conducted due to the limited number of eligible studies (≤3), which precluded the generation of meaningful pooled estimates after exclusion.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review with meta‐analysis found that individuals with T2DM experience significant difficulties in simultaneously performing dual tasks, resulting in a high dual‐task interference characterized by reduced performance in both motor and cognitive parameters during DT execution compared to healthy controls. These difficulties may manifest as a significant reduction in gait speed, increased stride time, and decreased performance in certain cognitive functions such as rapid visual processing, delayed memory, and verbal fluency 22 , 23 , 24 , 26 .

Optimal motor and cognitive function are vital for daily activities requiring simultaneous interaction of multiple motor and cognitive tasks, such as walking while talking on the phone or conversing with someone while preparing food 28 . The observed DTI in individuals with T2DM may be associated with various interrelated factors 29 . Studies have shown that individuals with T2DM exhibit slower gait speed, increased step cadence, shorter step length, and longer stride time—factors exacerbated when a new cognitive task is performed simultaneously 30 , 31 . This may relate to the muscle weakness, impaired dynamic balance during walking, and increased risk of falls observed in individuals with T2DM 32 . These impairments during activities like walking become more pronounced when a cognitive task is added 33 . Additionally, reduced gait speed and increased stride time have been suggested as possible compensatory strategies for maintaining balance due to the complexity of the task being performed 34 . However, this strategy may, over time, lead to an increased risk of falls, fear of movement, and disability, ultimately affecting the performance of activities of daily living in individuals with T2DM 35 . Reduced physical performance during walking may be related to increased cognitive‐motor interference, as other studies have reported that adding a cognitive task to a motor activity increases cognitive demand in individuals with T2DM 36 , 37 . On the other hand, other studies observed that subjects with T2DM presented alterations in static balance, showing greater postural oscillation in dual‐task situations such as subtracting numbers in series of tree, which could represent another risk factor for falls, especially in everyday situations that demand greater attention capacity 25 , 27 . In the present meta‐regression analyses, a higher proportion of men was significantly associated with smaller effect sizes for stride time, and higher BMI was significantly associated with smaller effect sizes for motor dual‐task interference, whereas no significant associations were observed for gait speed or cognitive DTI. These results indicate that sex distribution and body composition may contribute to between‐study variability and should be considered when interpreting motor‐cognitive performance outcomes in individuals with T2DM.

Another factor associated with DTI in individuals with T2DM is cognitive impairment 38 , which may be linked to various metabolic, inflammatory, and oxidative stress factors 39 . Poor control of lipid profiles, insulin levels, and glycemia has been associated with reduced total brain volume and increased white matter lesions in individuals with T2DM 40 . These dysregulations have been linked to processes of neuroinflammation, impaired cerebral perfusion, oxidative stress, and disruption of the blood–brain barrier, as well as reduced activation of glucose transporter proteins GLUT‐3 and GLUT‐4, β‐amyloid plaque accumulation, and tau protein hyperphosphorylation 41 . Collectively, these mechanisms are implicated in neurodegeneration, including white matter tract degeneration, which may disrupt functional brain connectivity 42 and lead to progressive declines in cognitive domains such as verbal fluency, processing speed, procedural memory, and numerical reasoning 43 . The findings regarding increased DTI provide relevant evidence for understanding functional decline in adults with T2DM through brain functional alterations and motor pathway involvement 11 . Insulin resistance at the brain level compromises insulin receptor‐mediated signaling in neurons, reducing glucose uptake and altering the modulation of neurotransmitters such as GABA and glutamate, impairing neuronal metabolism, which decreases the connectivity of the default mode network (DMN) and the activation of key brain regions during motor and cognitive execution 44 . Furthermore, other factors associated with T2DM, such as endothelial dysfunction and microangiopathy, decrease cerebral vascular reactivity, reducing regional blood flow around cognitive demands 45 . In adults with T2DM, motor dysfunction involves alterations at multiple levels of the nervous system, both central and peripheral 46 . Muramatsu et al. (2020) described that T2DM can affect the primary motor cortex, the corticospinal tract and at the peripheral level the alpha and gamma motoneurons, which can be related to the decrease in motor nerve conduction and the deterioration of neuromuscular excitation‐coupling 47 . These alterations produce clinical symptoms such as the reduction of muscular strength and resistance, especially in the lower extremities, affectation of proprioception and modulation of the stretch reflex, which contributes to the compromised postural control 48 . This convergence of deficits compromises the ability to manage simultaneous tasks, accelerating functional decline and reducing independence in the performance of activities of daily living, so the DTI may emerge as a sensitive marker of the multisystemic impact of T2DM and a possible objective to consider for the realization of multimodal interventions in clinical practice 49 .

This systematic review demonstrates that the impact of diabetes extends beyond isolated motor or cognitive domains. In individuals with T2DM, performing simultaneous cognitive and motor tasks generates measurable interference in both domains—referred to as motor and cognitive DTI. The findings highlight the importance of considering both cognitive and motor function when assessing the functional capacity of individuals with T2DM. DT assessments are highly sensitive for detecting motor and cognitive impairments that may not appear during the isolated evaluation of a single motor or cognitive component 50 , 51 . Therefore, DT assessments may be useful for identifying cognitive and motor alterations associated with functional impairments, such as fall risk or cognitive decline, which are common in the diabetic population 14 . Similarly, DT assessments are crucial and highly feasible in clinical practice 52 , 53 . The protocols used in studies to evaluate DT performance in individuals with T2DM are applicable in clinical settings due to the time efficiency of administering these tests 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 . It is recommended to calculate DTI using mathematical formulas for analyzing DT performance (Figure 2), although normative values are not yet available in the literature 54 .

Paradoxically, despite growing interest in studying DT performance in individuals with T2DM, only six studies have been published to date, and DT assessments are still rarely implemented in clinical practice 55 . This highlights the need for further research in this area, as cognitive‐motor performance may be compromised in individuals with T2DM 6 , 8 . Moreover, exercise interventions that incorporate DT components—combining motor tasks (e.g., walking or balance training) with cognitive activities and stimuli (such as serial subtraction, verbal fluency tasks, or audiovisual stimuli) 56 , 57 should be adapted and implemented in individuals with T2DM population to improve integrated motor‐cognitive function. These exercise programs could be delivered in home‐based or community group settings, thereby also promoting social engagement and participation 58 , 59 . To monitor dual‐task interventions programs, specific outcome measures should be prioritized in clinical practice. For motor performance, the 4‐meter walk test with a cognitive task yields key parameters such as gait speed and stride time, closely link to a fall risk 22 , 23 , 24 . Other test who evaluated the motor performance is the 30´s sit to stand test, provided practical indicators of lower limb strength 26 , and postural stability (eyes open/closed), which evaluates static balance 25 , 27 . Cognitive assessment can be efficiently performed with serial subtraction (by threes or seven) and alternating letter recitation (A, C, E, G), capturing language, working memory, and cognitive flexibility 22 , 25 , 26 , 27 . DT testing requires only low‐cost equipment and can be integrated into functional training circuits combining walking, obstacle negotiation, and balance tasks with simultaneous cognitive challenges, thereby enhancing ecological validity and alignment with daily life motor–cognitive demands. Consistent with this rationale, findings from the analyzed studies indicate that adults with T2DM demonstrate poorer motor performance—particularly in gait speed, stride time, and static balance—compared with healthy controls 22 , 23 , 24 , 26 , 27 . In parallel, cognitive performance deficits were evident, with lower scores observed in tasks assessing working memory and verbal fluency 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 . These combined impairments underscore the importance of selecting DT outcome measures that capture both motor and cognitive domains, as such an approach may provide a more comprehensive understanding of functional limitations in this population.

Based on the findings of this review, a brief clinical performance assessment battery for DT can be proposed that integrates motor and cognitive tests. This battery includes: (i) the 4‐meter walk test combined with serial subtraction tasks to obtain parameters of gait speed, stride time, and cognitive‐motor interference 22 , 23 , 24 ; (ii) the 30‐s sit‐to‐stand test to obtain an indicator of global lower limb strength, both in single and dual tasks 26 ; and (iii) a 30‐s static balance test with eyes open and then closed with cognitive tests such as alternating letter reciting that can assess postural stability, working memory, language, and cognitive flexibility 22 , 25 , 26 , 27 . These combinations of tests can be applied in less than 10 min, requiring minimal equipment and allowing to provide a comprehensive view of the interaction between motor control and cognitive performance in people with T2DM, whose adoption in clinical practice could facilitate the early detection of subjects with functional vulnerability and risk of falls, while its prospective validation will allow to establish a predictive value against possible cognitive deterioration and adverse events, defining a standardized framework for the evaluation of TD in this population 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 . Although this review highlights the potential clinical relevance of dual‐task assessments, formal validation of their predictive value requires prospective study designs. Future research should incorporate performance metrics such as receiver operating characteristic curves, area under the curve, optimal cutoff determination, and minimal clinically important differences to establish the accuracy of dual‐task batteries in predicting falls, fractures, cognitive decline, or other adverse events in individuals with T2DM.

Finally, first, substantial heterogeneity was observed in dual‐task evaluation protocols across studies. Second, the number of studies included in the quantitative synthesis was limited, and many presented a high risk of bias and low methodological quality, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Third, inconsistent reporting of glycemic control represents an important source of clinical heterogeneity. Several included studies did not report standardized metabolic markers such as glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), limiting the ability to account for differences in disease control across study samples. Given the well‐established relationship between glycemic dysregulation, peripheral neuropathy, vascular dysfunction, and cognitive impairment, inadequate metabolic characterization may contribute to effect dilution and increased heterogeneity in pooled estimates. Therefore, future studies should systematically report standardized glycemic control metrics to strengthen the interpretation of dual‐task outcomes in individuals with T2DM.

In addition, high‐quality research is needed to better characterize cognitive and motor impairments in individuals with diabetes and to standardize dual‐task assessment protocols capable of reliably detecting these deficits 6 , 58 . This may contribute to the identification of risk factors associated with cognitive‐motor frailty and the development of new intervention strategies for this population 59 , 60 . Likewise, future studies should adopt stricter reporting standards, including detailed information on diabetic peripheral neuropathy, disease duration, medication use, metabolic control, and vascular integrity. The use of validated screening tools is recommended, such as the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument for neuropathy assessment, glycated hemoglobin for metabolic control, and vascular function measures such as the ankle–brachial index 61 , 62 . Integrating these assessments would not only improve result standardization but also enable more robust subgroup analyses and meta‐regression approaches to clarify the differential impact of these factors on motor and cognitive decline. Furthermore, future trials should incorporate standardized neuropathy and vascular assessments, including vibration perception threshold testing, nerve conduction studies, DN4, or toe–brachial index, to enhance participant characterization and support more refined analytical models.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review highlights that adults with T2DM exhibit poorer performance in dual‐task activities compared to healthy control subjects, reflecting greater motor and cognitive dual‐task interference. This impairment is characterized by reduced motor patterns, such as slower gait speed and increased stride time, as well as lower cognitive performance, affecting functions such as rapid visual processing, delayed memory, and verbal fluency. Furthermore, the findings emphasize the need to incorporate DT assessment into the clinical evaluation of individuals with T2DM, enabling the early detection of functional impairments that might not be apparent in conventional evaluations. Future research is needed to standardize assessment protocols and establish normative DTI values for more precise and effective clinical application.
