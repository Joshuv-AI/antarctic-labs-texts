Reconsidering cytoreductive therapy in high-risk pediatric mastocytosis: From diffuse cutaneous to smoldering systemic disease
Authors: Mara Giavina-Bianchi, Marina França de Paula Santos, Alessandra Tellian Jacob, Stella Arruda Miranda, Jorge Kalil, Pedro Giavina-Bianchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric mastocytosis is usually benign and self-limiting, but diffuse cutaneous forms carry a higher risk of fatal anaphylaxis and/or underlying systemic disease. Cytoreductive treatment can modify the prognosis in these patients.Key words: Diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis, systemic mastocytosis, cytoreductive treatment, childhood, pediatrics, fatal anaphylaxis, midostaurin

Mastocytosis is a clonal disorder of mast cell progenitors that is generally caused by somatic gain-of-function mutations in the KIT gene, leading to pathologic accumulation and activation of mast cells. It is classified into cutaneous and systemic forms, with systemic mastocytosis (SM) further subdivided into bone marrow, indolent, smoldering, and advanced variants. Cutaneous mastocytosis (CM) predominates in pediatric patients and usually follows a benign course with spontaneous resolution during adolescence. Conversely, SM typically affects adults and tends to persist lifelong.1,2

Pediatric mastocytosis usually manifests as maculopapular CM (MPCM), diffuse CM (DCM), or mastocytoma (≤3 lesions). Whereas MPCM is common and mostly benign, DCM is a rarer, more severe variant marked by extensive skin involvement and (often) systemic symptoms with elevated serum tryptase levels, raising concern about more aggressive disease phenotypes.3 Systemic forms, including smoldering and aggressive SM, are unusual but possible in children.1

A recent retrospective cohort study at Necker Children's Hospital (Paris, France) analyzed 33 cases of pediatric DCM over nearly 3 decades. The study highlighted the fact that patients with DCM had significantly higher baseline serum tryptase levels (mean 47.5 μg/L vs 7.4 μg/L in MPCM), greater anaphylaxis prevalence (12.1% vs 2.4%), and a stronger association with aggressive SM (12.1% vs 0.9%). KIT D816V mutation was consistently present in aggressive SM cases. Importantly, 21.2% of patients with DCM received early systemic therapy (imatinib, midostaurin, or sirolimus), which was generally effective and well tolerated. The study concluded that DCM differs significantly from MPCM, with a higher risk of severe anaphylaxis and systemic progression, suggesting targeted cytoreductive therapy in select cases.4 In this case report, we discuss the potential role of early cytoreductive therapy in high-risk pediatric patients, emphasizing pediatric cases representing 2 endotypes of mastocytosis. Both patients described in this report were first evaluated during infancy (before age 1 year), a period during which bone marrow biopsy is often avoided owing to procedural risks and limited feasibility, thus underscoring the clinical challenges in early risk stratification.

Case reports

Patient 1

A male infant developed hyperpigmented, bullous maculopapular lesions at age 2 months that was initially misdiagnosed as atopic dermatitis (Fig 1). The lesions rapidly progressed to widespread thickened plaques with intense pruritus and recurrent flushing. The patient’s initial serum tryptase level was 34 ng/mL (normal <11.4 ng/mL), rising to 86.9 ng/mL over 12 months. The patient was first evaluated at our center before age 1 year, when bone marrow biopsy was considered high-risk and therefore not performed. Apart from elevated tryptase levels, the patient exhibited no other clinical or laboratory findings suggestive of systemic disease. Despite symptomatic treatment with H1 and H2 antihistamines, the patient continued to experience systemic episodes. At age 1 year, he developed severe irritability, diarrhea, flushing, and lethargy. The anaphylactic reaction was ineffectively treated with 2 doses of epinephrine—one administered via autoinjector at home and the other administered in the emergency room. His condition progressed to fatal cardiopulmonary arrest.

Peripheral blood testing for the KIT mutation was requested; unfortunately, the patient died before molecular analysis could be performed. This case reflects features described in the Necker Children's Hospital cohort, namely, early infancy onset, bullous eruption in 27.2% of cases, a high baseline tryptase level, and severe systemic symptoms. In the reported cohort, systemic cytoreductive therapies were initiated in some patients.4 Our case raises critical questions as to whether early cytoreductive therapy could have altered the disease outcome.

Patient 2

A 2-year-old girl with diffuse erythematous brown lesions (Fig 2) since birth experienced recurrent flushing, diarrhea, and multiple anaphylaxis episodes. Her serum tryptase level was higher than 200 ng/mL, and she exhibited hepatosplenomegaly suggestive of systemic involvement. The patient was first evaluated at our center during infancy (before age 1 year), when bone marrow biopsy was deferred owing to age-related procedural risks. Biopsy samples of the skin and gastrointestinal tract obtained via endoscopy showed infiltration by abnormal mast cells expressing CD2 and CD25. Peripheral blood PCR detected the KIT D816V mutation. Bone marrow biopsy was not performed. Instead, the diagnosis was supported by extracutaneous mast cell infiltration documented on gastrointestinal biopsy, detection of the KIT D816V mutation in peripheral blood, marked hepatosplenomegaly, and persistently elevated serum tryptase levels exceeding 200 ng/mL, all of which are findings consistent with smoldering SM. Treatment with midostaurin, a multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor, resulted in improvement of the patient’s skin lesions (Fig 2), reduced systemic symptoms, and decreased tryptase levels (from >200 ng/mL to 88 ng/mL), consistent with the effective responses reported in the Necker Children's Hospital cohort.4 The patient has been followed for 2 years while undergoing treatment. Midostaurin was administered at a dose of 30 mg/m2 twice daily. No severe adverse events were observed, and treatment was accessed through a medical emergency request. This case exemplifies the potential benefits of cytoreductive therapy in pediatric patients with systemic involvement.

Discussion

Both of our cases illustrate the clinical spectrum of pediatric mastocytosis, in which diffuse cutaneous disease may be associated with markedly elevated serum tryptase levels, severe anaphylaxis, and evidence of systemic involvement. Importantly, whether systemic disease represents true progression over time or was already present but unrecognized at initial presentation often remains unclear. Diagnosing SM in children is particularly challenging, as reliable markers beyond organomegaly, laboratory abnormalities suggestive of systemic involvement, and elevated serum tryptase level are limited. Moreover, invasive procedures such as bone marrow biopsy are frequently deferred during infancy, thus increasing reliance on clinical severity and biochemical markers to guide risk assessment and management decisions.3

Our first case, which was characterized by markedly elevated serum tryptase level in the absence of confirmed systemic involvement, highlights a common clinical dilemma: in real-world settings—particularly in cases in which molecular diagnostics are delayed or unavailable—risk stratification often relies primarily on clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and serum tryptase levels. Our second case supports the use of cytoreductive therapy in select clinical scenarios, demonstrating significant clinical improvement during midostaurin therapy, including prevention of recurrent anaphylaxis and marked improvement of systemic symptoms. This case also illustrates how molecular testing and extracutaneous biopsies, beyond bone marrow evaluation, can play a pivotal role in establishing the diagnosis of systemic disease in children. In conclusion, pediatric DCM differs from MPCM by virtue of a more aggressive course and risk of systemic disease. The present cases underscore the need for heightened vigilance and reconsideration of treatment paradigms. Cytoreductive therapies, although traditionally reserved for advanced SM, may benefit select pediatric patients with high-risk DCM or smoldering SM. Further studies are urgently needed to establish safety, efficacy, and treatment guidelines in this vulnerable population.
