Association between repeat number polymorphisms of sex hormone-related genes and gender phenotype variations in university students
Authors: Mizuho Igarashi, Yuko Katoh-Fukui, Atsushi Hattori, Kyongsun Pak, Shoko Sasaki, Maki Fukami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough common repeat number polymorphisms in 3 hormone-related genes (AR, CYP19A1, and ESR2) have been implicated in the variations in several sexually dimorphic phenotypes, their contributions to the variations in gender identity (GI) and sexual orientation (SO) remain to be clarified.AimTo clarify the possible association between the repeat number polymorphisms and gender phenotype variations in the general population.MethodsWe used paper-based questionnaires to select 80 individuals with atypical gender phenotypes (the case group) and 114 control individuals (the control group) from 736 university students. The case group was further divided into two subgroups: one consisted of individuals with low GI scores (the atypical GI subgroup), and the other consisted of individuals with non-heterosexual orientation (the atypical SO subgroup). Repeat numbers of the longer and shorter alleles in each participant (Alleles 1 and 2) were examined through microsatellite analysis. Repeat numbers of AR in females were adjusted for the X chromosome inactivation status.OutcomesWe examined the statistical differences in the repeat numbers between the case and control groups, and between each subgroup and the control group.ResultsThe overall differences between the case and control groups and between each subgroup and the control group were small. However, the repeat numbers of AR in males of the case group were larger than those of the control group (P = 0.049), and the repeat numbers of ESR2 Allele 1 were larger in males of the atypical GI subgroup than in those of the control group (P = 0.046). In addition, females in the atypical SO subgroup had smaller repeat numbers of CYP19A1 Allele 2 than control females (P = 0.011).Clinical ImplicationsThese results indicate the complex genetic basis of gender phenotype variations.Strengths & LimitationsThis is the first study that addresses the possible association between common polymorphisms in hormone-related genes and gender phenotypes in the general population. Given the small number of our subjects and the modest differences in the repeat numbers between the case and control groups, our results await further validation.ConclusionThe results indicate that the contribution of repeat number polymorphisms of the 3 genes to gender phenotype variations in the general population is small, although the repeat numbers of AR and ESR2 are potentially associated with atypical gender phenotypes in males.

Introduction

Gender identity (GI) and sexual orientation (SO) are the major components of sexuality in humans.1 Recent studies have revealed significant variations in GI and SO in the general population.2,3 We investigated 736 university students using paper-based questionnaires and found that only ∼15% of birth-assigned males and ∼5% of birth-assigned females had the maximum GI score that corresponds to complete gender congruence, and only ∼80% of males and ∼60% of females had exclusive heterosexual orientation.4 Gender phenotypes are assumed to be determined by several genetic, hormonal, and psychosocial factors.5 Although twin studies suggested a significant role of genetic factors in the development of atypical gender phenotypes,6 such factors remain largely unknown. In our previous study, an exome-based genome-wide association test identified a few genes whose rare single nucleotide variants (SNVs) accumulated in individuals with atypical gender phenotypes.4 For example, rare SNVs of TDRP were more commonly identified in males and females with gender incongruence than in control individuals, and rare SNVs in SEC16B were frequently detected in females with non-heterosexual orientation. However, these SNVs explained only a small portion of gender variations in the cohort.

The androgen receptor gene (AR), the estrogen receptor β gene (ESR2), and the aromatase gene (CYP19A1) play major roles in sex hormone action.7–9 Genetic variants in these genes, particularly their common repeat polymorphisms, have been implicated in the variations in several sexually dimorphic phenotypes. For example, short tandem repeat polymorphisms in AR, ESR2, and CYP19A1 have been linked to the risk of premature adrenarche, osteoporosis, and breast cancer, respectively.10–12 However, previous studies yielded conflicting results as to whether these polymorphisms are associated with atypical gender phenotypes. Hare et al. and Fernandez et al. reported that large repeat numbers of AR and ESR2 were associated with male-to-female and female-to-male transsexualism, respectively,13,14 while Ujike et al. argued against these associations.15 Furthermore, no studies have examined the association between these repeat number polymorphisms and gender phenotype variations in the general population.

The present study aimed to answer the question: Do common repeat number polymorphisms of AR, ESR2, or CYP19A1 contribute to variations in gender phenotypes in people of the general population? We hypothesized that these polymorphisms affect GI or SO by modulating the action of androgens and estrogens. To test this hypothesis, we performed molecular analyses for university students recruited in our previous study.

Methods

Ethical issues

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board Committee at the National Center for Child Health and Development (Project ID, 1874). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

We performed molecular analyses for 80 individuals with atypical gender phenotypes (the case group) and 114 control individuals (the control group). These individuals were selected from 736 university students recruited in our previous study.4 In that study, we assessed GI and SO of each participant using paper-based questionnaires. The questionnaires have been utilized for gender assessment in multiple studies.16–18

Detailed methods of gender assessment were described in our previous paper.4 First, to calculate the GI score, each participant was requested to provide answers to 15 items. The answers to each item were selected from “very true (score 7)” to “not true at all (score 1)”. The GI score was defined as the sum of the scores of the 15 items and ranged from 15 (obvious gender incongruence) to 105 (complete gender congruence).4 SO of each participant was assessed using a questionnaire for 3 items (sexual attraction, sexual fantasies, and emotional preference). The answer options consisted of SO toward “females only” (score 1), “females mostly” (score 2), “females somewhat more than males” (score 3), “both sexes equally” (score 4), “males somewhat more than females” (score 5), “males mostly” (score 6), and “males only” (score 7), as well as “non-binary”, “not certain”, and “I have no experience”. Consequently, the SO score of each participant ranged from 3 (exclusively toward females) to 21 (exclusively toward males).4

We categorized 80 participants (27 birth-assigned males and 53 birth-assigned females) into the case group.4 These individuals were aged between 20 and 33 years (median 21 years). The case group was further divided into 2 subgroups (atypical GI and atypical SO subgroups). The atypical GI subgroup consisted of 18 birth-assigned males and 27 birth-assigned females who satisfied the following criteria: (1) individuals whose self-recognized gender was opposite to the assigned sex, (2) individuals who answered that his/her self-recognized gender is non-binary, and (3) individuals whose GI scores were below the 5th percentile of all participants of this cohort with the same assigned sex. The atypical SO subgroup consisted of 12 birth-assigned males and 40 birth-assigned females who satisfied the following criteria: (1) males whose SO scores were 5–7 or “non-binary” for at least one of the three items, (2) males who selected a SO score 4 for one or more of the three items, and did not select scores 1 or 2 for the remaining item(s), (3) females whose SO scores were 1–3 or “non-binary” for at least one of the three items, and (4) females who selected a SO score 4 for one or more of the three items, and did not select scores 6 or 7 for the remaining item (s). Three males and 14 females were included in both subgroups.

We selected participants with typical gender phenotypes as the control group. Of the 736 participants, 46 males and 72 females were assessed as having nearly complete gender congruence (male and female participants whose GI scores were ≥ 105 and ≥ 100, respectively). These cut-off values were determined to select a similar number of participants to that of the case group. Then, 2 females were excluded because they had low SO scores (1 or 2) for one or more items. The other participants had exclusive heterosexual orientation. Furthermore, we excluded 2 individuals whose DNA samples were insufficient for molecular analyses. Consequently, the control group consisted of 45 males and 69 females aged between 20 and 27 years (median 21 years).

Molecular analysis

Genomic DNA samples were extracted from saliva using the Oragene-Discover kit (DNAgenotek, Ottawa, Ontario, Canada). We analyzed the CAG repeat polymorphism in exon 1 of AR (NM_000044.6) of Xq12, the TTTA repeat polymorphism in intron 4 of CYP19A1 (NM_000103.4) on 15q21.2, and the CA repeat polymorphism in intron 5 of ESR2 (NM_001437.3) on 14q23.2. The genomic regions containing these polymorphisms were PCR-amplified using primers based on previously reported designs.19–21 PCR products were analyzed using 3130xl, 3500xL or SeqStudio Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). The size of the PCR products was determined by microsatellite analysis using Gene Mapper Software 3.7, 5.0, and 6.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Considering that AR is subjected to X chromosome inactivation (XCI),22 the phenotypic consequences of repeat number polymorphism in this gene may be affected by the XCI status of the 2 alleles. Thus, we performed HpaII-mediated DNA methylation analyses of AR4 and calculated the XCI-weighted mean of the 2 alleles in each female.10

Karyotyping was not performed in this study. However, the results of molecular analyses of the previous4 and present studies, including exome sequencing and AR microsatellite analysis, suggested that all tested individuals in this cohort had birth-assigned sex-compatible karyotypes.

Statistical analysis

Comparison between the case and control groups

We examined the repeat numbers of AR, CYP19A1, and ESR2. The normality of the distribution of variables was visually assessed. The mean, SD, and range were calculated for continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, and the median, interquartile range, and range for variables that showed non-normal distribution. Statistical differences in the repeat numbers between the case and control groups of each sex were analyzed using R (version 4.4.1; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Welch’s two-sample t-test and Mann–Whitney U test were applied. For AR, we analyzed the repeat number on the single allele of each male, and the XCI-weighted mean of the 2 alleles in each female. For the other 2 genes, we examined the repeat numbers of the relatively longer and shorter alleles in each individual (designated as Alleles 1 and 2, respectively) and the average repeat number of the 2 alleles. The two-sided alpha for each test was set at 0.050, and multiplicity was not considered since this study was exploratory.

Next, we genotyped participants for each gene and classified them into 3 categories: (1) individuals who had 2 above-median length alleles (alleles containing more repeat numbers than the control group median, namely, more than 18, 7, and 23 repeats for AR, CYP19A1, and ESR2, respectively) (genotype 1), (2) individuals who had one above-median length allele and one median or below-median length allele (genotype 2), and (3) individuals who had 2 median or below-median length alleles (genotype 3). The differences in the genotype frequency between the case and control groups were analyzed by Fisher’s exact test.

Comparison between each subgroup and the control group

We analyzed the difference in the repeat numbers of AR, CYP19A1, and ESR2 between each subgroup and the control group. The methods were the same as those mentioned above.

Results

Comparison between the case and control groups

The results are summarized in Figure 1 and Table 1. The mean repeat numbers of AR in males of the case group were significantly larger than those of the control group. In females, there was no difference in XCI-weighted means of the 2 AR alleles between the case and control groups; the median repeat numbers of Alleles 1 and 2 were similar between the 2 groups. Furthermore, there were no significant differences in the repeat numbers of CYP19A1 or ESR2 between the case and control groups of both sexes; the repeat numbers of Alleles 1 and 2 and the average repeat numbers of the 2 alleles of CYP19A1 were comparable. Likewise, the repeat numbers of Alleles 1 and 2 and the average repeat numbers of the 2 alleles of ESR2 were also comparable between the case and control groups of both sexes. In addition, we observed no differences in the genotype frequencies of the 3 genes between the case and control groups (Table 2).

Comparison between each subgroup and the control group

The repeat numbers of the 3 genes were almost comparable between the atypical GI or atypical SO subgroups and the control group of both sexes (Tables 3 and 4). Specifically, the mean repeat numbers of AR in males of the 2 subgroups tended to be larger than those of control males; however, there were no significant differences. Yet, we observed significant differences in the 2 items. First, Allele 2 of CYP19A1 in females of the atypical SO subgroup tended to contain fewer repeats than that of the control group, although the median values were the same. Second, Allele 1 of ESR2 of the males of the atypical GI subgroup contained more repeats than that of the control group. On the other hand, the genotype frequencies of the 3 genes were similar between each subgroup and the control group (Table 2).

Discussion

This study investigated the possible association between repeat number polymorphisms in AR, CYP19A1, and ESR2 and gender phenotype variations in the general population. The results showed that male individuals with atypical gender phenotypes tended to have more repeats of AR than control individuals (mean values, 19.4 vs. 18.1). Previous studies have suggested that the repeat numbers are negatively correlated with the receptor activity of the AR protein.23 In vitro functional assays revealed that an AR protein with 31 glutamine residues in the polyglutamine stretch exhibited ~64% of the transactivation activity of a protein with 15 residues.24 Our data can be explained by assuming that a relatively low AR activity due to large repeat numbers is associated with the susceptibility to atypical gender phenotypes in birth-assigned males. Consistent with this, Hare et al. reported that male-to-female transsexual people tended to carry a large repeat number of AR.13 Although we found no significant differences in AR repeat numbers between each subgroup and the control group, these results can be ascribed to the limited number of participants in each subgroup. However, the effects of this polymorphism on gender diversity appear to be small, because the distribution of the AR repeat numbers in males of the case and control groups largely overlapped. Moreover, we found no association between the repeat numbers and gender phenotype variations in females.

The median repeat numbers of CYP19A1 were comparable between the case and control groups and between each subgroup and control groups. In addition, there was no difference in the genotype frequencies between the case and control groups. These results are consistent with those of previous studies which showed no association between this polymorphism and transsexualism in both sexes.15 Still, we found that females in the atypical SO subgroup had smaller repeat numbers of Allele 2 (the shorter allele in each individual) than control females. Since in vitro assays showed that CYP19A1 repeat numbers parallel with aromatase activity,25 relatively low aromatase activity due to small numbers of these repeats may affect SO in females. However, the difference in the repeat number between the atypical SO subgroup and the control group was small. Furthermore, atypical gender phenotypes have not been reported in patients with aromatase deficiency due to CYP19A1 loss-of-function mutations.26 Thus, the association between the CYP19A1 repeat number polymorphism and SO remains questionable.

The repeat numbers of ESR2 were similar between the case and control groups, and the distribution of ESR2 repeat numbers was comparable between each subgroup and the control group. However, we found that the median repeat number of ESR2 Allele 1 (the longer allele in each individual) was even larger in males of the atypical GI subgroup than in control males. These results are consistent with those of the previous report by Henningsson et al. which linked long alleles of ESR2 to male-to-female transsexualism.27 Kudwa et al. reported that changes in the action of ERβ affected sexual behavior in mice of both sexes.28 Of note, large repeat numbers of ESR2 were linked to relatively high receptor activities.29 Thus, although this repeat polymorphism is located in an intron, increased numbers of these repeats may enhance the action of estrogens due to the high ESR2 expression to affect the GI of males. Nevertheless, since the difference between the atypical GI subgroup and the control group was small, this polymorphism likely has only minor effects on GI. Indeed, we observed no difference in the genotype frequencies between the case and control groups, and between each subgroup and the control group. Further studies are necessary to examine the significance of the ESR2 polymorphism in gender phenotype variations.

This is the first study that examined the possible association between common repeat number polymorphisms in AR, CYP19A1, and ESR2 and gender phenotype variations in the general population. Our results indicate that the overall contribution of these polymorphisms is small, while the repeat numbers of AR and ESR2 may exert some effects on gender phenotypes in males. Since we observed no or only slight differences between individuals with the most atypical gender phenotypes and individuals with the most typical phenotypes, these repeat numbers are unlikely to play a major role in the gender variations in all 736 students. However, this study has several limitations. First, the number of participants in each subgroup was small. Second, we did not examine the environmental factors of the participants, although psychosocial factors are known to affect gender phenotypes. Lastly, there were only modest differences in the repeat numbers between the case and control groups. Thus, the results of this study await further validation.

Conclusion

This study revealed a limited role of repeat number polymorphisms of the 3 sex hormone-related genes in gender variations of the general population. Our data showed a possible association between the repeat numbers of AR and ESR2 and atypical gender phenotypes in birth-assigned males, which needs to be validated in future studies.
