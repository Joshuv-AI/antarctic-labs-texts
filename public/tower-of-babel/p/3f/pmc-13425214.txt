Ferroptosis, foiled: the ENO1-SCD1 axis in AML
Authors: Daniel R Moskop, Eric M Pietras
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Wu et al1 identify a metabolic escape route in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in which the metabolic enzyme α-enolase 1 (ENO1) regulates lipid metabolism through promotion of stearoyl–coenzyme A desaturase 1 (SCD1) transcription to enhance resistance to ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of cell death.

Key challenges in the development of improved therapies for AML include defining how the metabolic features of leukemia stem cells (LSC) that initiate and sustain the disease diverge from normal hematopoietic cells and identifying actionable strategies to target these metabolic dependencies. LSC rely heavily on oxidative phosphorylation and can adapt their use of amino acid catabolism or fatty acid oxidation to support energetic needs under therapeutic stress, contributing to drug resistance.2, 3, 4, 5 Against this backdrop, Wu et al took a broad approach to find enriched genes in AML that might influence metabolic plasticity and push cells toward drug resistance. Using a combination of publicly available AML gene expression and CRISPR-dependency data sets, the authors identified a set of genes that both are highly expressed in AML cell lines and essential for their survival. Given previous work linking ENO1 to LSC function and chemoresistance,6 the authors selected this candidate for additional follow-up. Importantly, the authors extended their bioinformatic analyses to 2 patient-centered observations: ENO1 expression is higher in patients with AML than in healthy controls, and its expression correlates with worse event-free survival in patients with AML. These observations supported ENO1 as a potential biomarker for worse clinical outcomes and provided the authors with clear rationale for mechanistic studies that define how ENO1 affects AML cell fate and survival. To corroborate previously published data and better define ENO1’s functional role in AML, the authors genetically knocked down ENO1 in MV4-11 and Kasumi-1 AML cells and found suppressed growth and colony-forming potential. Critically, the authors corroborated these data with experiments in primary AML patient cells and found comparable results. Additionally, they found that ENO1 knockdown impaired engraftment potential of MV4-11 cells into immunodeficient NSG mice, whereas ENO1 overexpression in HEL cells enhanced engraftment and leukemic cell infiltration into other organs.

These studies begged the question as to how ENO1 supports LSC function. Using multiple complementary assays, the authors determined that ENO1 loss triggers increased lipid peroxidation, elevated intracellular iron levels, and an increase in glutathione oxidation, all hallmarks of ferroptosis. Mechanistically, transcriptomic studies performed by the authors revealed that ENO1 knockdown reduces RNA levels of the enzyme SCD1, a critical catalyst in the conversion of saturated fatty acids to monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs). MUFAs themselves serve as potent inhibitors of ferroptosis by a variety of mechanisms, including reducing accumulation of polyunsaturated fatty acids, which are vulnerable to peroxidation, into membrane phospholipids.7 Along these lines, complementary metabolomics analyses performed by the authors revealed decreased abundance of monounsaturated fatty acids in lipid membrane species that are integral components of cellular phospholipid membranes. These data suggest that ENO1 regulation of SCD1 levels directly affects the lipid composition of cell membranes, establishing a ferroptosis-resistant cell state (See figure). These findings provide key mechanistic context to the authors’ previous observation that ENO1 knockdown makes AML cells more susceptible to ferroptosis.

Outside of its enzymatic activity in converting fatty acids, ENO1 also has known roles in both RNA and DNA binding.8,9 Given this, the authors next sought to determine whether ENO1 directly regulates SCD1 expression through either modulating its transcription or mRNA stability. Although the authors found no change in SCD1 mRNA stability after ENO1 knockdown, they discovered that ENO1 influences SCD1 transcription by directly binding to its promoter, and this DNA binding function is not entirely dependent on ENO1’s catalytic activity. To confirm SCD1’s functional effects in AML cell survival and lipid remodeling, Wu et al rescued SCD1 expression in their ENO1 knockdown cells and found that SCD1 reintroduction effectively reduced ferroptosis in vitro and restored tumor progression in xenograft models in vivo. Moreover, the authors found similar results after supplementing ENO1 knockdown cells with oleic acid, a monounsaturated fatty acid and major catalytic product of SCD1. To then round out the clinical significance of their story, the authors asked whether ENO1 mediates resistance to the anthracycline daunorubicin, a major component of induction chemotherapy regiments in AML and a known inducer of ferroptosis. The authors determined that ENO1 knockdown sensitizes AML cell lines and primary AML cells to daunorubicin treatment, whereas ENO1 overexpression increases resistance to the drug. Moreover, the authors used the SCD1 inhibitor SSI-4 in combination with daunorubicin and found that SSI-4 resensitizes cells with high levels of ENO1 to daunorubicin both in vitro and in a xenograft model. Altogether, these investigations provide evidence that targeting SCD1 can circumvent ENO1-driven resistance to daunorubicin, thereby establishing a metabolic checkmate that resensitizes AML cells to therapy.

Taken together, Wu et al establish previously unappreciated links between ENO1 and regulatory pathways that modulate lipid metabolism and ferroptosis, while identifying potential approaches for circumventing ENO1-driven therapy resistance. Importantly, the SCD1 inhibitor SSI-4, used here by the authors, is under active investigation in a clinical trial for solid tumors,10 underscoring the clinical relevance of their approach. Extending these results to patient-derived xenografts with primary AML cells represents a key next step to strengthen the translational relevance of this study. Second, although the ENO1-SCD1 axis modulates daunorubicin sensitivity in bulk leukemia models, testing this link directly in empirically defined LSC populations will clarify whether the mechanism operates in cells most responsible for therapy resistance and relapse. Addressing these points will help determine how broadly ENO1-SCD1 lipid remodeling contributes to chemotherapy resistance and whether this axis represents a therapeutic liability in LSC. Further investigations can also establish whether ENO1 expression influences responses to other common therapeutic regimens, such as venetoclax/azacitidine, in which upfront resistance and relapse rates remain a challenge. Lastly, the extent to which ENO1 expression and/or activity is involved in earlier stages of myeloid malignancy remains an open question. Altogether, this work offers strong rationale for further evaluation of SCD1-directed treatment combinations in AML and other myeloid diseases.
