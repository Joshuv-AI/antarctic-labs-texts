Neutrophil extracellular traps drive local tumor progression and metastasis following thermal ablation in liver cancer via TLR9-mediated inflammatory feedback and immunosuppression
Authors: Zhou Tian, Jinchao Zhao, Yueyang Wang, Shaomin Zou, Junkang Ding, Tangye Zeng, Yizhao Zhou, Bo Zhang, Honghe Zhang, Zhe Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLocal tumor progression and metastasis substantially compromise the therapeutic efficacy of thermal ablation in liver cancer. Although the inflammatory microenvironment contributes, the specific role of neutrophils after ablation remains unclear. This study aimed to clarify the roles of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) after thermal ablation and evaluate the translational potential of NETs-targeted strategies.MethodsThe adverse prognostic impact of thermal ablation-induced neutrophil and inflammatory changes in liver cancer was retrospectively evaluated. Circulating NETs were quantified by ELISA, and their association with local tumor progression was analyzed. An orthotopic liver cancer mouse model and patient-derived neutrophils elucidated the spatiotemporal dynamics and mechanisms of ablation-induced NETs, and pharmacologic inhibition was used to investigate NET formation, function, and translational potential.ResultsThermal ablation rapidly activates neutrophils, elicits a sustained local inflammatory response and NET formation, which is closely associated with poor prognosis in patients with liver cancer. Depletion of neutrophils, inhibition of NET formation, or degradation of thermal ablation-induced NETs markedly suppressed post-ablation tumor progression. Mechanistically, tumor cell-intrinsic reactive oxygen species generated under heat stress contributed to nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) activation, promoting p65 binding to theC-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1) promoter and transcriptional upregulation of CXCL1. This process drove CXCL1-C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) signaling and led to the formation of a characteristic band-like NETs-enriched zone at the peri-ablational margin. In turn, NETs activated toll-like receptor (TLR)9-dependent NF-κB,mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling in heat-stressed cancer cells, reinforcing a pro-inflammatory feedback loop. Concurrently, NETs impaired CD8+ T-cell responses and fostered an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Together, this coordinated pro-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effect promotes liver cancer progression following thermal ablation, which can be effectively reversed by dual blockade of NF-κB and CXCR2.ConclusionsOur research uncovers a novel mechanism underlying tumor-promoting inflammation after thermal ablation, highlighting the critical role of NETs as mediators through TLR9 activation of downstream inflammatory pathways and the significant potential of NETs-targeted strategies in combination with thermal ablation.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Our research reveals a previously unrecognized mechanism underlying tumor-promoting inflammation after thermal ablation: NETs exacerbate post-ablation tumor-promoting inflammation through toll-like receptor (TLR)9-dependent inflammatory feedback. Specifically, we identified that thermal ablation activates neutrophils and induces nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) driven NET formation, which promotes TLR9-dependent NF-κB,mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling, reinforcing a pro-inflammatory feedback loop and fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment that drives liver cancer progression after thermal ablation therapy.

Introduction

Thermal ablation therapy including radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and microwave ablation, is a minimally invasive and highly effective non-surgical local treatment for liver cancer.13 However, the high rate of local tumor progression and metastasis following treatment remains a major challenge to achieving better clinical outcomes.4 5 During thermal ablation, hyperthermic injury to tumor tissues leads to the release of immunogenic intracellular substrates, which trigger the activation of inflammatory signaling pathways and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, accompanied by infiltration of various inflammatory cells.68 Clinically, patients undergoing thermal ablation often exhibit a significant inflammatory state such as fever, elevated peripheral blood C-reactive protein, increased inflammatory cytokines, and neutrophilia.912 Crucially, elevated inflammatory markers after thermal ablation have been associated with poorer prognosis, reflecting shorter recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) in patients with liver cancer.1315 Neutrophils are the most abundant and rapidly responding leukocytes during inflammation and tissue injury, but their roles in local tumor progression and metastasis of liver cancer after thermal ablation are not well understood.

As the key effector mechanism of neutrophils activation, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are web-like structures composed of decondensed chromatin fibers decorated with histones and cytotoxic enzymes.16 17 NETs contribute to cancer progression through complex and context-dependent mechanisms, facilitating tumor growth, immune evasion, promoting angiogenesis and cancer-related thrombosis.18 Conversely, NETs may also exert antitumor effects by inducing tumor cell apoptosis via cathepsin G or suppressing cancer cell proliferation.19 20 Despite the dual roles in tumor progression, it remains unclear whether thermal ablation could induce NET formation or NETs could affect local tumor progression and metastasis following ablation. Here, we demonstrate that thermal ablation stimulates neutrophil activation and NET formation, which promotes local tumor progression and metastasis after the treatment. These findings offer new insights into the biological mechanism of local tumor progression and metastasis following thermal ablation therapy.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from patients with liver cancer scheduled to undergo thermal ablation therapy. Specifically, blood was collected 1 day before and on the first day after the procedure. The samples were collected in EDTA-coated tubes and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C to separate plasma and blood cells. The cellular fractions and isolated plasma were subsequently used for the analysis of NETosis, as well as citrullinated histone H3 (Cit-H3), MPO-DNA (myeloid peroxidase-DNA) complexes and plasma C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1).

Orthotopic liver cancer model and RFA treatment

An orthotopic liver cancer mouse model was established by intrahepatic injection. 1 week later, tumor-bearing mice were subjected to RFA when tumors reached a diameter of approximately 6–8 mm, ensuring comparable baseline tumor burden across groups. Tumor size was assessed by direct measurement during laparotomy. RFA was then performed using a monopolar electrode, while sham-operated controls underwent identical surgical procedures without RFA treatment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism V.9.3, unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean±SEM. Statistical details, including sample sizes (n) and tests used, are provided in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical significance was defined as *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ****p<0.0001; ns indicates no significance.

Detailed materials and methods are described in the online supplemental materials.

Results

Neutrophil activation and NET formation in liver cancer following thermal ablation therapy

To investigate the neutrophils and inflammatory response following thermal ablation, we performed a paired analysis of whole blood cells from patients with liver cancer before and after thermal ablation. The results showed that neutrophils increased from 62.51% to 77.89% (figure 1A), accounting for the overall leukocyte elevation after thermal ablation (figure 1B). Additionally, imaging in patients with liver cancer revealed a peri-ablational hyperenhancing zone that persisted for several weeks after thermal ablation therapy, indicating a sustained local inflammatory response (figure 1C). Meanwhile, Gene Ontology-biological process analysis of integrated transcriptomic (GSE212604) and proteomic (PXD036767) data from post-thermal ablation hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissues revealed significant enrichment of neutrophil-related processes and inflammatory responses,21 including neutrophil degranulation and chemotaxis, leukocyte migration, and activation of inflammatory signaling pathways (figure 1D). These findings further indicate pronounced neutrophil mobilization and activation following thermal ablation. Given that the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is a classical inflammatory marker, a retrospective analysis of published studies was performed to assess the clinical significance of inflammation and elevated neutrophils for long-term outcomes after thermal ablation (online supplemental figure S1A and online supplemental table S1). This analysis revealed that elevated NLR was significantly associated with increased risks of recurrence and mortality, with shorter RFS (HR=1.54, 95% CI 1.30 to 1.82) (figure 1E) and OS (HR=1.56, 95% CI 1.29 to 1.88) (online supplemental figure S1B).

NETs represent a functional manifestation of activated neutrophils and play a critical role in tumor progression.17 To investigate whether thermal ablation induces NET formation (figure 1F and online supplemental table S2), we performed flow cytometry to analyze the proportion of NETosis in patients with liver cancer after thermal ablation. The results showed a significant increase in CD15+CD16+ neutrophils following thermal ablation, accompanied by reduced expression of CD15 and CD16, suggesting neutrophil recruitment and activation (figure 1G and H). Importantly, a marked increase in the proportion of neutrophils undergoing NETosis was detected in peripheral blood after thermal ablation (figure 1I and J). In patients with liver cancer’s plasma, the NETs component Cit-H3 increased 1.8-fold (figure 1K and L), and the MPO-DNA complex increased 1.5-fold (figure 1M) compared with preoperation levels. Moreover, our results revealed that patients with local tumor progression showed a greater increase in plasma MPO-DNA complexes than those without local progression (figure 1N and O). Altogether, these findings indicate that neutrophil activation and NET formation occur after thermal ablation, which is associated with local tumor progression in patients with liver cancer.

NETs around thermal ablation lesions promoted post-treatment local tumor progression and metastasis

To evaluate the spatial distribution and time course of NETs induced by thermal ablation, we established an orthotopic liver cancer mouse model and performed RFA treatment. Neutrophil infiltration and NETs in the ablated tissue were then analyzed at multiple time points post-treatment (figure 2A). As shown in figure 2B, within the first 5 days after RFA treatment, an approximately 800–1,000 μm wide band-like NETs region, as indicated by MPO/Cit-H3/DNA staining, was observed around the ablation site. In contrast, in the sham RFA group, NETs were sparsely distributed throughout the tumor tissue. To further validate NET formation, immunofluorescence staining for neutrophil elastase (NE) and DNA was performed 1 day after RFA (online supplemental figure S2A). Consistently, a prominent band-like distribution of NE-positive signals was detected surrounding the ablation zone, whereas NE signals in the sham RFA group remained scattered, corroborating the findings obtained from MPO/Cit-H3/DNA staining. At 7 days after RFA, the NET-enriched zone at the tumor margin was markedly diminished (figure 2C and D). In contrast, increased but dispersed NETs were observed at the expanding tumor margins in the sham RFA group. Collectively, these results indicate that thermal ablation induces a spatially organized accumulation of NETs at the margin of the ablation zone, which persists for several days following treatment.

To investigate the role of NETs in liver cancer progression following thermal ablation, we depleted neutrophils using an anti-Ly6G neutralizing antibody prior to RFA (figure 2E). Neutrophil depletion markedly reduced local tumor progression, as evidenced by decreased liver weight and tumor volume (figure 2F), and significantly inhibited spontaneous lung metastasis (figure 2G). Consistently, both inhibition of NET formation using a PAD4 inhibitor (figure 2H) and degradation of NETs by DNase I (online supplemental figure S2B) significantly suppressed local tumor progression, reduced liver tumor burden (liver weight and tumor volume) (figure 2I, online supplemental figure S2C and D), and inhibited lung metastasis following RFA (figure 2J and online supplemental figure S2E). Altogether, these findings demonstrate that targeting neutrophils (the cellular source of NETs), NET formation (PAD4-dependent), or NETs degradation (DNase I-mediated) consistently attenuates tumor progression, indicating that NETs induced by thermal ablation play a critical role in promoting liver cancer progression.

Thermal ablation induces NET formation via CXCL1-CXCR2 axis activation

We next sought to investigate the underlying mechanisms driving NET formation following thermal ablation. Hepa1-6 and Huh7 liver cancer cells were subjected to heat treatment and the culture supernatant was collected to prepare conditioned medium (CM). The CM was subsequently applied to mouse bone marrow-derived neutrophils (BMDNs) and human peripheral blood neutrophils (PBNs) to assess whether the heat-induced tumor-derived mediators could trigger NET formation (figure 3A and online supplemental figure S3A). After co-culture with CM, both mouse BMDNs and human PBNs exhibited notable NET formation, characterized by the colocalization of MPO, Cit-H3 and DNA (figure 3B). Additionally, flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in the proportion of NETosis among CM-treated mouse BMDNs and the expression of CD11b and Ly6G were markedly elevated (figure 3C), indicating that CM stimulation promotes neutrophil maturation and activation, and suggesting the presence of a NETs-inducing factor secreted by cancer cells after heat stress.

To identify the potential NETs-inducing factor, we used a Luminex assay to measure 31 cytokines and chemokines in CM from Hepa1-6 cancer cells. The results showed that heat stress increased the secretion of multiple cytokines and chemokines by cancer cells (online supplemental figure S3B). Among them, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), CXCL1, CC chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), CCL7, and CCL20 were significantly elevated, with levels more than three times higher than those in the non-heated control group (figure 3D). We then tested recombinant forms of these cytokines and chemokines at different concentrations to evaluate their ability to induce NET formation. The results revealed that CXCL1 significantly stimulated NET formation (figure 3E and online supplemental figure S3C), aligning with previous reports that tumor-derived C-X-C chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) agonists act as potent triggers for NETosis.22 Consistent with this finding, eliminating CXCL1 from the heat-treated CM using a neutralizing antibody before treating mouse BMDNs and human PBNs, or pre-blocking CXCR2 on neutrophils with a CXCR2 antagonist, both significantly inhibited CM-induced NET formation (figure 3F). Moreover, in the thermal ablation models in vivo, either CXCL1 neutralizing antibody or CXCR2 antagonist could also obviously reduce neutrophils accumulation and NET formation (figure 3G). Taken together, these data indicate that cancer cells secrete CXCL1 under heat stress, which interacts with CXCR2 on neutrophils to induce NET formation.

NF-κB-dependent transcriptional activation of CXCL1 drives NET formation under heat stress

To investigate how heat stress increases CXCL1 expression and induces NET formation, we analyzed RNA-sequencing data from tumor tissues of mice treated by RFA. As expected, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to innate immune response, neutrophil chemotaxis and activation, and chemokine activity in the RFA-treated group (online supplemental figure S4A). In the chemokine activity pathway of the GSEA analysis, Cxcl1, as one of the top-ranked core enrichment genes, was significantly upregulated after RFA treatment (online supplemental figure S4B). Interestingly, only Cxcl1 messenger RNA (mRNA) expression was significantly increased among those five differentially secreted factors in heat-treated CM by Luminex assay (figure 4A). In line with this, we examined CXCL1 expression and secretion by liver cancer cells under heat stress, and significant increases in CXCL1 mRNA expression and secretion were detected under sublethal heat treatment between 40°C and 44°C (figure 4B and C, online supplemental figure S4C and D). Moreover, CXCL1 in the peripheral blood of patients with liver cancer was significantly elevated after thermal ablation therapy (figure 4C). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that CXCL1 expression was markedly increased in tumor tissues following RFA treatment (online supplemental figure S4E and F). Notably, CXCL1 was predominantly localized to viable tumor cells at the peri-ablational zone, surrounding the necrotic core, consistent with the sublethal temperature range of thermal ablation therapy (approximately 40–44°C). Temporally, CXCL1 levels were elevated during the first 5 days after RFA and diminished by day 7, closely paralleling the spatial and temporal dynamics of neutrophil infiltration and NET formation.

Among the pathways associated with CXCL1 regulation,23 our GSEA results revealed that the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling pathway exhibited the highest normalized enrichment score with the most significant statistical relevance (figure 4D and online supplemental figure S4G). The previous integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analysis of patients with HCC tissues post-thermal ablation also revealed significant activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway (figure 1D), further suggesting its critical role in the inflammatory response to thermal ablation. As such, we evaluated the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in liver cancer cells under different heat stress conditions. Consistent with the changes of CXCL1 expression and secretion, sublethal heat treatment significantly increased the levels of p-IKKα/β, p-IκBα and p-p65, accompanied by nuclear translocation of p65 (figure 4E and online supplemental figure S4H), compared with untreated control cells, indicating robust activation of the NF-κB pathway. To determine whether heat stress-induced CXCL1 upregulation depends on NF-κB signaling, we performed p65 knockdown followed by heat stress (online supplemental figure S5A). The results showed that p65 knockdown significantly attenuated CXCL1 mRNA expression and protein secretion (figure 4F and online supplemental figure S5B), and also reduced heat-induced CXCL1 promoter activity (figure 4G). Moreover, mutation of the predicted p65-binding site within the CXCL1 promoter markedly impaired heat-induced promoter activation (online supplemental figure S5C), further supporting the functional importance of p65-mediated transcriptional regulation of CXCL1 under heat stress conditions. In addition, chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative PCR analysis showed that heat stress markedly enhanced p65 binding to the canonical NF-κB motif within the CXCL1 promoter (figure 4H). Together, these results indicate that heat stress promotes CXCL1 expression through NF-κB-dependent transcriptional activation.

Given the well-established role of oxidative stress in NF-κB activation,24 25 we herein explored whether heat stress induces NF-κB activation through reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation. Our results showed that intracellular ROS levels were significantly increased under heat stress across 40–46°C conditions (online supplemental figure S5D). Furthermore, treatment with N-acetylcysteine (NAC) effectively scavenged heat stress-induced ROS accumulation (online supplemental figure S5E). Both heat stress and hydrogen peroxide treatment activated the NF-κB pathway, whereas NAC treatment markedly attenuated heat-induced NF-κB activation and suppressed p65 nuclear translocation (online supplemental figure S5F and G). These results suggest that ROS-mediated oxidative stress contributes to heat-induced NF-κB activation, although additional upstream mechanisms, such as cytokine-mediated or other stress-related signaling pathways may also be involved.

NF-κB signaling was further inhibited using the NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11–7082 to validate its role in heat stress-induced CXCL1 expression and its translational potential, which significantly suppressed CXCL1 expression and secretion in liver cancer cells under heat stress (figure 4I and online supplemental figure S5H). Furthermore, in vivo NF-κB inhibition markedly reduced thermal ablation-induced CXCL1 expression, neutrophil infiltration, and NET formation (figure 4J and K). Collectively, these findings suggest that heat stress-induced ROS contributes to NF-κB activation, which in turn transcriptionally regulates CXCL1 expression and promotes NET formation after thermal ablation therapy (figure 4L).

Thermal ablation-induced NETs promote tumor progression via TLR9-mediated inflammatory pathways

NETs contribute to tumor initiation and progression by modulating proliferation, invasion, migration, angiogenesis, and the immune microenvironment.16 Our results showed that NETs elimination significantly reduced Ki-67 expression (online supplemental figure S6A and B), whereas CD31, E-cadherin, and N-cadherin levels remained unchanged, suggesting that NETs primarily promote post-ablation tumor progression by impacting tumor growth rather than affecting angiogenesis or epithelial-mesenchymal transition. We next treated Hepa1-6 cells with NETs purified from mouse BMDNs and Huh7 cells with NETs purified from human PBNs to investigate how NETs promote tumor progression following heat stress (figure 5A). Liver cancer cells were treated at 42°C for 15 min and subsequently co-cultured with either purified NETs or DNase I-treated NETs; the results showed that NETs significantly enhanced the proliferation (figure 5B), migration and invasion (figure 5C and D, online supplemental figure S6C and D) of heat-stressed cancer cells, whereas DNase I treatment effectively abrogated these tumor-promoting effects of NETs. Notably, DNase I alone did not affect the growth or motility of heat-stressed Hepa1-6 and Huh7 cells (online supplemental figure S6E–G), indicating that DNase I does not directly alter tumor cell behavior under these conditions.

Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) play the pivotal roles in malignant progression by regulating inflammation and immune evasion.72628 Moreover, the previous Gene Ontology-Biological Process (GO-BP) analysis showed that activation of toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling was enriched in HCC tissues following thermal ablation (figure 1D). However, it remains unclear whether TLRs could be changed by NETs and promote post-thermal ablation liver cancer progression. Our results demonstrated that NETs treatment selectively upregulated TLR9 expression in both murine and human liver cancer cells (figure 5E and online supplemental figure S6H). In vivo, TLR9 expression was significantly increased at the tumor margin following RFA treatment, showing a spatial pattern highly consistent with that of NETs (figure 5F). Subsequent analysis of TLR9-dependent inflammatory signaling pathways revealed that NETs treatment increased the phosphorylation of STAT3, p65, JNK and p38, further enhancing CXCL1 expression (figure 5G, online supplemental figure S6I and online supplemental figure S7A). Furthermore, the TLR9 antagonist effectively abrogated the activation of these inflammatory pathways (figure 5G and online supplemental figure S7A), suppressed the proliferation (online supplemental figure S7B), migration and invasion (online supplemental figure S7C and D) of heat-stressed cancer cells exposed to NETs.

To further validate the tumor cell-intrinsic role of TLR9 in NETs mediated tumor progression, TLR9-knockdown Hepa1-6 and Huh7 cells were established (online supplemental figure S7E). Transwell assays showed that TLR9 knockdown significantly attenuated NETs-induced tumor cell migration and invasion in vitro (figure 5H and online supplemental figure S7F). To extend these findings in vivo, orthotopic liver tumor models were established using shNC or shTLR9 tumor cells followed by RFA treatment (figure 5I), and efficient TLR9 knockdown in tumor tissues was confirmed by immunohistochemical staining (online supplemental figure S7G). Under sham conditions, no significant difference in tumor growth was observed between the shNC and shTLR9 groups (figure 5J and K). However, following RFA treatment, the progression of residual tumors was significantly reduced in the shTLR9 group compared with the shNC group (figure 5J and K), and histological analysis of liver tissues showed smaller areas of viable tumor in the shTLR9 group (figure 5J). Notably, despite the significant difference in local tumor progression, no significant changes were observed in overall tumor burden, including liver weight and liver/body weight ratio (figure 5K). Similarly, a trend toward reduced metastatic burden was observed in the shTLR9+RFA group, but no statistically significant differences were detected among groups (online supplemental figure S7H).

Collectively, these results indicate that NETs promote the malignancy of heat-stressed cancer cells via TLR9-dependent inflammatory signaling, contributing to local tumor progression following thermal ablation. However, the limited impact on overall tumor burden and metastasis suggests that targeting tumor cell-intrinsic TLR9 alone may be insufficient to fully suppress post-ablation tumor progression, implicating additional mechanisms such as NETs-mediated remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment.

NETs barrier formation around the ablation region restricts CD8+ T-cell infiltration and activation, driving CD8+ T-cell exhaustion

The impact of NETs on the post-thermal ablation local immune microenvironment remains unclear. Our results revealed that DNase I-mediated clearance of NETs significantly increased the number of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells following RFA treatment (figure 6A). Flow cytometry analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells showed that NETs clearance significantly increased the number of total T cells and CD8+ T cells (figure 6B, online supplemental figure S8A and B). Moreover, the proportions of granzyme B (GZMB)+ CD8+ T cells and interferon (IFN)-γ+ CD8+ T cells were also significantly increased (figure 6C and online supplemental figure S8C), while the proportions of other immune cell types remained unchanged, indicating a primary effect of thermal ablation-induced NETs on CD8+ T cells within the local microenvironment. To further directly evaluate the inhibitory effects of NETs on T-cell function, we treated mouse spleen-derived CD8+ T cells with NETs derived from mouse BMDNs, the results showed that DNase I-mediated degradation of NETs effectively reversed NETs-induced suppression of CD8+ T-cell proliferation and cytotoxicity, as indicated by a significant increase in the proportion of Ki-67+, GZMB+, and IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells in the DNase I-treated group (figure 6D and E). Given that nuclear translocation of p65 is a key mechanism of T-cell activation,29 we further investigated NETs effect on T-cell activation. The granulocyte-like cells were successfully differentiated from HL-60 promyelocytic leukemia cells to generate NETs (figure 6F and online supplemental figure S8D), which significantly inhibited p65 nuclear translocation in Jurkat cells (a human T lymphocyte cell line) (figure 6G), these findings support the suppressive role of NETs in CD8+ T-cell infiltration and activation after thermal ablation therapy.

Combined inhibition of NF-κB and CXCR2 suppresses liver cancer progression post-thermal ablation through reshaping tumor microenvironment

Given the pivotal roles of NF-κB in driving CXCL1 transcription and the CXCL1-CXCR2 axis in mediating neutrophil recruitment and NET formation, we investigated whether combined inhibition of NF-κB and CXCR2 could more effectively suppress post-thermal ablation tumor progression by providing a dual blockade of upstream inflammatory activation and downstream chemokine signaling (figure 7A). As shown in figure 7B, combined treatment with the NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11–7082 (BAY11) and the CXCR2 inhibitor SB225002 (SB225) markedly reduced the local tumor progression and the spontaneous lung metastasis after thermal ablation (figure 7C and D). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that combined inhibitor treatment markedly increased total immune cell infiltration (figure 7E), decreased the proportions of CD11b+ myeloid cells and granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and increased the proportions of dendritic cells, CD8+ T cells, and natural killer (NK) cells (figure 7F and G and online supplemental figure S9A). Moreover, both combination and monotherapy promoted a shift toward M1 macrophage polarization (online supplemental figure S9B), BAY11 or SB225 alone modestly increased IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells (figure 7H) but did not affect IFN-γ+ NK cells (online supplemental figure S9C). Notably, the combination therapy further elevated the proportions of GZMB+ CD8+ T cells and GZMB+ NK cells (figure 7I and J), creating a more immunologically “hot” tumor microenvironment. Finally, Ki-67 analysis demonstrated that the combined strategy effectively suppressed the post-thermal ablation growth of residual tumor (figure 7K). Taken together, these results show that NF-κB and CXCR2 inhibition curbs post-thermal ablation local tumor progression and metastasis by reshaping tumor microenvironment.

Discussion

Local tumor progression and metastasis following thermal ablation represent major challenges that compromise long-term outcomes in patients with liver cancer.4 In this study, we show that thermal ablation triggers a strong local inflammatory response and drives NET formation, which is closely associated with poor prognosis of patients with liver cancer. We demonstrate that NF-κB dependent CXCL1 transcription drives NET formation under heat stress, contributing to a band-like NETs-enriched zone around ablation lesions. Through stimulation of TLR9-dependent inflammatory signaling, NETs sustain inflammation and form a positive feedback loop that promotes tumor progression, while also fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates immune evasion.

Neutrophil elevation is widely recognized as a hallmark of inflammation.30 In the present study, we demonstrated that a high NLR (a key immune-inflammatory marker) during the peri-ablation period was associated with shorter RFS and OS in patients with liver cancer. Despite growing evidence of neutrophil-mediated effects, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Recent studies have shown that various therapies, including cisplatin or adriamycin/cyclophosphamide chemotherapy31 and radiotherapy,32 33 can induce NET formation and thereby influence post-treatment outcomes. Notably, we found that NETs induced by thermal ablation exhibit distinct spatial characteristics compared with those triggered by systemic therapies. While chemotherapy-induced NETs are typically diffusely distributed,31 34 thermal ablation induces NETs that are highly concentrated at the peri-ablational margin. Using orthotopic mouse models, we showed that NETs form a band-like structure at the tumor margin, rapidly emerging after ablation and gradually diminishing within approximately 1 week. Crucially, this NETs-enriched zone spatially overlaps with residual tumor proliferation, reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and functional exhaustion. These findings suggest that NETs establish a “physical and functional barrier” at the ablation margin. Dense NETs structure may limit effector T-cell infiltration, while NETs-associated components promote T-cell dysfunction and directly enhance tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Such microenvironmental remodeling contributed to local tumor progression and metastasis after thermal ablation. However, due to the minimally invasive nature of thermal ablation, comprehensive analysis of NETs distribution and persistence in patient tissues remains challenging. Further studies using clinical samples would be helpful to validate these findings and assess their translational relevance.

NETs-associated components can serve as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to activate PRRs and exert diverse biological effects.3537 Although the role of PRRs in thermal ablation remains incompletely defined, our findings indicate a TLR9-mediated reciprocal inflammatory circuit between NET formation and tumor cell signaling. Specifically, heat stress-induced NF-κB activation promotes CXCL1 expression, which contributes to NET formation at the peri-ablational margin. In turn, NETs further enhance heat-stressed liver cancer cell malignancy by upregulating TLR9 expression and activating TLR9-dependent inflammatory signaling, leading to downstream activation of NF-κB, STAT3, p38 MAPK, and JNK pathways, as well as further induction of CXCL1. As such, this establishes a self-amplifying inflammatory loop in which NETs act as an “amplifier” of thermal ablation-induced inflammation, sustaining a protumorigenic microenvironment at the ablation margin. However, it is also vital to recognize that NETs-independent inflammatory pathways may also contribute to the peri-ablational microenvironment. Thermal ablation-induced sterile injury can release DAMPs, such as HMGB1 and extracellular ATP, which activate innate immune signaling and promote inflammatory amplification, thereby influencing myeloid recruitment and tumor cell behavior.7 Meanwhile, NET formation represents a terminal effector program of neutrophils, accompanied by the release of neutrophil-derived factors during activation or cell death.16 These NETs-independent components, including reactive oxygen species and inflammatory cytokines, may also contribute to local inflammatory remodeling and tumor progression after thermal ablation. Taken together, NETs likely represent only one component of a broader neutrophil-driven inflammatory network, and NETs-independent mechanisms may also contribute to post-ablation tumor progression.

NF-κB is a central regulator of inflammatory signaling, orchestrating cytokine expression and shaping the tumor microenvironment.23 38 In the context of thermal ablation, which induces acute sterile inflammation, our findings suggest that NF-κB functions as a key hub linking heat stress to neutrophil recruitment and NET formation, thereby coordinating the initiation and amplification of inflammatory responses at the peri-ablational margin. Heat stress is known to induce global cellular stress responses, among which oxidative stress represents a major upstream trigger of NF-κB activation.24 In this study, our data support a role for tumor cell-intrinsic ROS in mediating heat-induced NF-κB activation. However, this mechanism is unlikely to be exclusive. Cytokines released from surrounding cells under heat stress may further activate NF-κB through paracrine signaling. In addition, our findings indicate that NETs can in turn reinforce NF-κB activation in tumor cells, suggesting the presence of a feedback mechanism, and reflecting that NF-κB activation following thermal ablation is driven by multiple interacting pathways. Consistent with the complexity of NF-κB regulation under heat stress conditions, we observed increased IκBα phosphorylation accompanied by NF-κB activation, whereas total IκBα protein abundance remained relatively stable. Previous studies have suggested that NF-κB activation under cellular stress conditions does not always strictly follow the classical “IκBα phosphorylation–degradation” paradigm. ROS-mediated NF-κB activation has been reported to occur with or without obvious IκBα degradation,24 and the dynamic negative feedback regulation of NF-κB signaling may further contribute to maintaining relatively stable steady-state IκBα abundance despite increased phosphorylation.39 These findings may partially explain our observation that heat stress induced elevated p-IκBα while total IκBα protein levels remained relatively unchanged.

Given this central role, targeting NF-κB represents a rational therapeutic strategy; however, the CXCL1-CXCR2 axis is subject to compensatory regulation and may be sustained by multiple cellular sources,40 suggesting that inhibition of NF-κB alone may be insufficient. In this context, combined targeting of NF-κB and CXCR2 provides a vertical blockade by simultaneously limiting chemokine production and receptor-mediated signaling, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while potentially allowing lower doses of NF-κB inhibitors. Notably, previous studies have often employed relatively high doses of NF-κB inhibitors (eg, about 10 mg/kg BAY 11–7082 or higher),41 42 which may be associated with systemic toxicity and broad immunosuppressive effects. In contrast, we adopted a lower-dose strategy (2.5 mg/kg BAY 11–7082) to preferentially suppress NF-κB activation in tumor cells, particularly under heat stress conditions where NF-κB signaling is markedly enhanced. This consideration is supported by the context-dependent roles of NF-κB, which promotes tumor progression in tumor and myeloid cells, but is required for activation of T cells and NK cells.43 Therefore, partial inhibition of NF-κB may selectively attenuate tumor-promoting inflammation while minimizing impairment of antitumor immunity, providing a more balanced and potentially translatable therapeutic approach. Although direct NF-κB inhibitors are not currently established as standard anticancer therapies, alternative approaches targeting the NF-κB pathway, such as clinically approved drugs proteasome inhibitors (eg, bortezomib) and experimental IKK inhibitors, may provide more clinically feasible strategies. Further investigation into combining these NF-κB targeting approaches with thermal ablation may further improve their translational relevance in this setting.

In conclusion, our study confirmed the tumor-promoting role of thermal ablation-induced NETs in liver cancer and revealed the interplay between NETs and ablation-induced inflammation. These findings highlight neutrophils and NETs as key mediators of ablation-induced inflammation and potential targets to optimize liver cancer thermal ablation strategies.
