Physician experiences with clinical uncertainty in the trauma setting: making clinical guidance accessible to those in need
Authors: Anika Srinath, Emma Lee, Gianni Aragon, Giuliano Borda-Luque, Gabriela Zavala Wong, Ana Lucia Carranza, Eduardo Huamán, Andrea Olabarrera, Manuel J Rodríguez, Kirsten Senturia, Lucas Távara Wiess, Lacey LaGrone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGlobally, trauma patients suffer from high rates of preventable deaths, in part driven by low rates of access to and application of resource-relevant evidence-informed clinical guidance. This mixed-methods study assessed the accessibility (barriers to attempting to use guidance), clarity (ease of comprehension), utility (based on resources and time), and implementation of trauma guidance for Peruvian injury providers.MethodsSemistructured qualitative interviews were conducted in Spanish at three hospitals in Lima, Peru. Interviews were analyzed in Dedoose V.9.1.12 using an iteratively developed codebook; quantitative self-administered surveys were then developed and distributed at the Pan American Trauma Society Conference. Surveys were analyzed using descriptive statistics and frequencies.Results38 interviews and 83 surveys were conducted with surgical attendings, emergency physicians, and surgical and emergency residents across all years of training. Access barriers included paywalls, language barriers, low user-friendliness, and technology limits (poor internet, few computers). A mobile app and portable physical guidance are preferred potential solutions. Utility barriers included low equipment maintenance and high patient volumes. Resource-based guidance, conciseness (eg, flowcharts), and standardization are perceived to potentially increase utility. Implementation barriers included limited training time, reliance on more experienced colleagues rather than guidance, low enforcement, and low funding. Potential facilitators are increased administrative support and staff willingness to use guidance improving patient mortality, clinical errors, and length of stay. No barriers to clarity were reported.ConclusionBarriers to using clinical guidance included low user-friendliness, low local applicability, and an unsupportive culture. Mobile apps, resource stratification, and administrative involvement are priorities to address these needs. Trauma guidance adapted to local realities may reduce preventable deaths in acute care. Further work is needed to identify how to create and distribute updated clinical guidance to better serve trauma providers worldwide.Level of evidenceEconomic and Value-based Evaluations Level 2.

Introduction

Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) account for 90% of injury deaths worldwide, an estimated third of which are preventable with improved quality of care.1 Trauma care providers in LMICs often contend with poor hospital infrastructure, lack of nationalized emergency services, poor protocol standardization, and limited resources, which contribute to increased trauma-related mortality.2 A qualitative study with rural Australian trauma providers revealed such barriers were not unique to LMICs; most interviewees in rural areas of this high-income country (HIC) noted significant resource limitations and a fragmented healthcare system as major barriers to providing high-quality care.3

Clinical guidance (eg, protocols, algorithms, and best practice guidelines) improves quality of care and decreases preventable errors by compiling evidence and presenting recommendations to clinicians on optimal care.4 However, clinical guidance is underused.5 At a level 1 trauma center in an HIC, compliance across recommended trauma-specific processes of care averaged 58%, concurring with a study at a level 1 trauma center in a different HIC in which compliance with resuscitation protocols was 42% for “minor” traumas and 53% for “major” traumas.6 7 Additionally, the WHO found that providers from 18 LMICs fulfilled on average 47% of recommended care for any given procedure across almost 100,000 consultations.5 HICs display comparable rates: a retrospective analysis of ovarian cancer care in California found 37% of women received standard treatment under national guidelines, with a 30% decreased risk of 5-year mortality when compared with women who did not receive standard treatment.8

A scoping review of empirical studies conducted in LMICs found several factors contributed to uneven guidance compliance, including poor infrastructure preventing direct guidance application, lack of equipment and medications limiting guidance utility, and heavy workload restricting time to review guidance. In contrast, staff awareness of clinical guidance facilitated use, and providers preferred guidance perceived as clear, adaptable, and user-friendly.9 Furthermore, a qualitative study of the WHO Trauma Care Checklist found revolving staff, resistance from providers, and dismissive attitudes hindered use of guidance, and endorsement by front-line providers and institutional leadership facilitated use.10 Although clinical guidance does not directly resolve these issues, it may empower providers to request resources from governmental agencies recommended for specific clinical scenarios and populations.

Lima, the capital of Peru, contains varied resource levels, providing an opportunity to assess how existing trauma guidance meets diverse healthcare practitioner needs in an upper middle-income country.11 The study participants had worked in private clinics where necessary resources were readily available, and public hospitals where adaptations to significant limitations were essential. The urban hospitals frequently applied reputable international guidance within the context of severe resource constraints. Our aim was to assess: (1) accessibility, (2) clarity, and (3) utility of trauma clinical guidance among a cohort of providers at three urban public hospitals in Peru. Our findings provide a contextual assessment of the current needs in trauma guidance for resource-variable settings and can inform future studies on interventions to improve the quality of trauma care.

Methods

Study design and research personnel

We conducted an exploratory sequential mixed-methods study using semistructured interviews and surveys with trauma providers in Peru. Clinicians were interviewed at three public urban hospitals in Lima (June 2024) and surveys were distributed during the Pan-American Trauma Society Conference (Lima, Peru; August 2024). This was part of a larger study to understand the clinical guidance needs of acute care providers in variable resource settings. All participants provided verbal consent for the interviews and completed consent forms for the surveys. This report follows the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research.12

The accessibility, clarity, utility, and implementation of clinical guidance were assessed through interviews and surveys. Accessibility was defined as perceived burden of paywall, language, and physical barriers in using clinical guidance; clarity as ease of comprehension in terms of vocabulary and writing structure; utility as usefulness based on providers’ resources, time, and needs based on the clinical diagnosis; and implementation as ease of integration of updated guidance into clinical workflow.

The research team included five medical students (AS, EL, ALC, AO, LTW), an American trauma surgeon and researcher (LL), a medical anthropologist (KS), and five Peruvian research physicians with context expertise (GZW, GA, GB-L, EH, MJR). Data were primarily collected by the medical student (AS) and analyzed by the medical student (AS) and research physician (GZW).

Data tools

We developed the interview and survey for this study informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) given its utility in evaluating clinical settings, providers, and intervention characteristics.13

The 21-question interview guide was created to capture (1) demographics, (2) hospital practice setting, (3) clinicians’ responses to clinical uncertainty, (4) potential guidance improvements, and (5) the process of implementing new guidance (online supplemental appendix A). The questions on clinical uncertainty determined where clinicians with doubts turn for help and how their preferred clinical guidance compares to international guidance. Questions evaluating guidance ascertained how to improve guidance, ideal guidance design, factors influencing guidance implementation, and measurements of guidance impact. Three Peruvian physicians pilot tested the interview guide and adapted the content to increase clarity. The survey was developed based on themes identified during analysis of the interview responses and included demographic questions and 16 questions relevant to trauma guidance. In the survey, trauma guidance was defined as recommendations to diagnose and treat traumas based on current medical knowledge and consideration of any relevant benefits and harms.

Sampling frame

Interviews

Interviewees were recruited by the data collector (AS) using snowball sampling during interviewees’ shifts at times not interfering with their clinical duties. Emergency physicians, general surgeons, 1st-year and 2nd-year emergency residents, and 1st-year to 3rd-year surgical residents who worked in trauma were included. “Emergency physicians” refer to physicians exclusively working in the emergency room, and “surgeons” are general surgeons performing operations in acute settings. We used a sampling matrix to ensure relevant characteristics were included across our sample, such as gender, professional specialty, and level of training. Formal training in trauma among the general surgeons was not assessed. One interview was completed in each demographic category except for male emergency residents; data sufficiency was achieved at 38 interviews.14

Surveys

Surgeons, surgical residents, and emergency physicians were recruited using convenience sampling to complete the surveys at the XXXVI Panamerican Congress of Trauma, Critical Care, and Emergency Surgery 2024 in Lima, Peru; incentives included a small giveaway of notebooks and metal pins. 83 Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) surveys were fully completed digitally by Peruvian physicians.

Data collection

Each interviewee completed a 15–20 minute private, in-person, audio-recorded interview in the hospital in Spanish. Each survey respondent completed a 10 min, self-administered REDCap survey in Spanish on an iPad or personal smartphone at the Panamerican Congress.

Data analysis

Interview audio files were transcribed and translated by members of the research team using Notta transcription service. Personally identifiable information was redacted for privacy. Interview quotes were identified by specialty (ie, “EM” =emergency medicine, “GS” =general surgery) and level of training (“R1” =intern, “R2” =2nd year resident, “R3” =3rd year resident, “A” =attending). Data were uploaded into Dedoose V.9.1.12 for coding and analysis as described by Braun and Clarke.15 16 Steps for codebook development were: initial codes created based on the interview guide, related CFIR constructs, study aims; codes expanded after review of three transcripts; codes tested on six additional transcripts by two coders (AS and GZW); codebook edited until a comprehensive codebook was created. Nine transcripts were coded independently by both coders. Differences in coding were resolved through discussion until agreement was reached, and AS coded the remaining 29 transcripts. During data synthesis, coded excerpts were organized into themes and subthemes with relevant quotes. Demographic data were tabulated (table 1).

Results

Demographics

38 Peruvian physicians were interviewed, mostly male (63%) surgeons (61%). 83 Peruvian physicians completed the survey, again mostly male (77%) surgeons (96%) (table 1).

Practice setting

The hospital setting was reported to inform the relevance of clinical guidance to clinicians given high patient volumes and limited infrastructure. All three hospitals treated emergencies and traumas under the Ministry of Health, known as “MINSA,” and delivered care to east, north, and south Lima. Some interviewees reported patients without emergencies inappropriately used emergency services, restricting time to use guidance and supplies needed for proper care.

The large population with the limited space we have doesn’t allow us to follow the guidelines as we would like. We sometimes have to compromise. (GS R2_4)

Additionally, providers mentioned relying on limited resources, with less funding than hospitals within other insurance systems. There was little knowledge of options to repair broken machinery, and interviewees reported that despite being high-complexity MINSA hospitals (the largest and most well-equipped), none of the hospitals had MRI, interventional technology (such as interventional catheterization), nor imaging equipment for each wing of the hospital. These limitations prevented clinicians from following guidance that required the use of unavailable equipment. As several providers mentioned:

The infrastructure is not…adequate, the space is not adequate, there are many unforeseen events with electricity, also equipment that breaks down, lacks maintenance… (EM A)

Interviewees indicated exceptions to these limitations: all hospitals performed laparoscopic surgeries, multiple providers felt their workplaces had higher technological capacity than previous workplaces, and one hospital had an alarm to signal trauma arrivals for emergency services coordination.

Barriers to clinical guidance implementation

Access barriers

Over 60% of survey respondents struggled to access guidance when needed due to paywalls, technology barriers, and lack of time to search, aligning with interview responses. Paywalls reportedly decreased access to clinical orientation resources, and access to the ATLS guidelines required payment for the course and manual. Physicians also mentioned subscription fee barriers to articles or updated guidance. As one described,

…new guidelines are not…available to everyone…if you’re enrolled in a cardiology society or…internal medicine society, they can give you access. But not everyone can…access…the updated guideline…when it comes out. (EM A9)

Another access barrier was language, including low English proficiency combined with no translation services, a dearth of guidance in Spanish, and as one physician noted, no direct translation available from English to Spanish for certain medical terms.

…the English translation is not always clear. It’s like your colleague [fellow U.S. medical student] when she did her flowcharts, she couldn’t translate them into Spanish because there are words that don’t even have an exact correlation in Spanish. (EM A4)

Interviewees referenced numerous barriers to accessing both hard-copy and online guidance: one hospital’s specific guidance was on paper in an office, but several physicians were unaware it existed; not all international guidance was readily found on the internet (which was frequently unstable); hospitals lacked computers in every wing; and institutional policies hindered access through domain restrictions on websites. A provider described,

There are some pages…restricted by the institution…we can’t even access YouTube to review a procedure that could greatly help us when we’re in a tough spot. (EM A2)

Utility barriers

Multiple interviewees felt patient volume prevented them from adequately following clinical guidance due to a lack of beds, limited time per patient, and insufficient medical equipment. Furthermore, poorly maintained equipment reportedly hindered physicians from following protocols requiring CT imaging or certain interventional treatments.

…if we are doing a CT scan to decide whether…to operate on a patient…we can’t always do a CT scan in the emergency area here… that’s the difference, the accessibility we have to paraclinical resources. (GS A9)

Many hospitals also lacked the medications recommended by standard clinical guidance, as several physicians noted.

Several times…during my residency training, I’ve had to consult with attendings and tell them look this guide gives me this information, but we don’t have this medication. (EM A7)

Adoption barriers

Interviews with healthcare practitioners revealed several barriers hindering adaptation of guidance to better fit their clinical settings.

First, most Peruvian physicians are dual practicing, with limited time for training in new guidance. Physicians also expressed concern about spending time adapting guidance and educating peers without compensation.

Time is a barrier, because the way of life we lead here in our country forces…98% of doctors to work in two or three places at the same time… there is little time left for them. (GS A3)

Second, interviewees described a culture of resistance to change. Physicians who had adjusted their practice for years to fit preexisting guidance indicated hesitation to apply adapted guidance. Several interviewees also relied on senior expert opinion rather than clinical guidance. Furthermore, interviewees worried updated guidance differing from previously used evidence-based guidance, even in the delivery of the same content, may increase the risk of medical mistakes.

…everyone already has an academic background and a way of acting…it would be complicated, it’s not like people will adapt overnight…To make someone who is 60…who already has their way act differently, it’s not going to happen because they base their way of acting on their experience. (GS R1)

Third, physicians perceived hospital administrations as more reactive than proactive, hindering initiatives to improve quality of care. Interviewees thought leadership was disorganized with frequent turnovers, administrations would not provide compensation to update guidance, and that low enforcement of standardized guidance inhibited efforts toward change. Emergency physicians also mentioned low cohesion and funding in the specialty, obstructing efforts to create adapted guidance for urgent care in Peru.

Finally, lack of clear roles on patient arrival reportedly further complicated guidance implementation. Physicians suggested guidance define who is responsible for what interventions, depending on the specific care required:

Maybe cards: what I do, what you do- you go to the airway, you go to venous access, you go to resuscitation, you go to the crash cart… (EM A4)

Facilitators of clinical guidance implementation

Access facilitators

Almost 70% of survey respondents indicated a digital application would be most usable for delivering clinical guidance, aligning with interviews, followed by 14% preferring guidance on websites. Only 5% of respondents favored clinical guidance delivery through medical journals, the status quo. Interviewees suggested placing clinical guidance on an app, a folder downloaded onto hospital computers, or a website for quick access. Physicians thought the guidance platform should be easy to use and well known by staff. For hospitals with fewer computers or limited internet, physicians suggested portable physical guidance with common pathological findings and placing copies in acute trauma spaces.

Utility facilitators

Survey respondents felt cost-effectiveness was the least important factor analyzing usability of clinical guidance, and ease of navigation at the bedside and being up-to-date were the most important factors. In interviews, physicians agreed updated guidance was important for utility and adapted protocols based on available resources would be more helpful:

It [adapted guide] is very useful considering that we are in a developing country where the ATLS will have to guide us at our level, not using higher technology resources, but what we have here. (GS R1_2)

Furthermore, interviewees said paying for UpToDate was necessary due to its convenient search function, compilation of many guides, and brevity, facilitating ease of use. One provider explained:

Of course, if it is a guideline that carries a… modification that makes our lives easier, and that helps us in the management of patients, we all adapt it to our reality. (GS R2)

Another resident described how guidance could reduce the number of patients in Trauma Shock (resuscitation area in Peruvian hospitals):

What…we lack a lot…in triage, is…defining what deserves to enter Trauma Shock and what doesn’t. Maybe be more specific…because sometimes patients who don’t really require Trauma Shock are sent there. (GS R2_3)

Surgeons preferred guidance that helped them decide the best treatment plans depending on the type and severity of trauma. Several physicians suggested implementing a system to regularly update hospital guidance to contain the latest treatment techniques for different traumas. One surgeon said,

…the new practices aren’t in the guide…it needs updating… Where do I search?…Not in the guide. The guide is very old…what is lacking is updating the new information that has arrived… (GS A10)

On the other hand, emergency physicians prioritized guidance discussing rapid treatment of trauma at the point of entry, including techniques for using resources efficiently and how to treat common complications or errors.

Interviewees suggested more usable guidance could be presented as algorithms and flowcharts visualizing what steps to follow and where to send the patient. One resident described how the current guidance design can be exhausting:

There are some guidelines that only come in text, and…as an R1 [first-year resident], with the fatigue from the entire shift, it’s a bit tedious. So, it would be great if there were more graphics. (GS R1_6)

However, some physicians mentioned flowcharts may sacrifice clarity of the clinical guidance due to excessive brevity, and attached mental maps with in-depth explanations of each step can balance this perceived issue.

Finally, interviewees suggested including various trauma protocols based on available resources and medications, perhaps even accounting for the hospital’s insurance coverage. For instance, several hospitals only have ultrasound for diagnoses, and more ultrasound-based protocols would help physicians optimize their equipment. Additionally, physicians supported potentially modifying international guidance to fit the physical characteristics and cultural beliefs of the average Peruvian patient.

There are American, European guidelines…but they have a different reality…The type of patient, the physique, the constitution, the shock a patient in South America could withstand versus an American patient who has a broad build…is variable. (EM A11)

Adoption facilitators

Almost all survey respondents declared they would be more likely to use trauma guidance standardized across their hospital network and the nation, and over 70% of respondents claimed they would use guidance endorsed by local leadership or colleagues. Interviews confirmed standardized guidance created by local physicians, colleagues, or familiar institutions was preferred by study participants; several physicians suggested hospital staff or national experts could adapt guidance for pathological findings commonly seen in their field.

Additionally, 90% of survey respondents were more likely to use trauma guidance with multidisciplinary authorship and from a variety of trauma care settings. Interviewees supporting this opinion proposed more frequent trauma congresses to promote physician involvement, consider local needs, and ensure consistent access to updated guidance.

Finally, per interviews, hospital staff frequently adapt to foreign guidance and recognize medicine as an evolving field. One surgeon emphasized any increase in lives saved would be a strong motivator to implement updated guidance in the clinical setting:

I can say…a year has passed, and the mortality rate for abdominal trauma has decreased by 0.5% already. That is a triumph. 0.5% may be a little bit, but it’s a parent, it’s a mom, someone who has kids, so it’s worth the effort. (GS A3)

Interviewees recommended hospital administration organize trainings to facilitate the dissemination and adoption of updated guidance and mandate the use of updated clinical guidance to maximize potential benefits in patient outcomes:

There should be some type of penalties…for those who don’t follow the guidelines…The doctor should say, I did this because it’s supported here, because the clinical practice guideline of this hospital says this is what is done. (GS A7)

Discussion

This mixed-methods study confirmed challenges faced by trauma physicians in applying clinical practice guidance and barriers and facilitators to implementing changes. Barriers to access were subscription fees, lack of guidance in Spanish, and poor centralization of written guidance resources, whereas potential solutions included a mobile app or portable physical guidance. Barriers to utility were excess patient volumes and limited functional medical equipment, potentially alleviated by brevity, easy navigation, and guidance accounting for locally available equipment. Barriers to implementation were limited training time, resistance to change, and unsupportive administration, with possible solutions being familiarity with the clinical guidance source, interdisciplinary authorship, motivation to improve patient well-being, and standardization of protocols. There were no barriers found in guidance clarity. Finally, preferred methods to evaluate the adapted guidance’s success at improving clinical workflow were morbidity and mortality, efficiency of patient care, and error rates.

These findings are heavily supported in the literature. Subscription fees, inadequate search time, and low awareness pose access barriers per a qualitative study and meta-analysis.17 18 Recent research has acknowledged the dominance of the English language in scientific literature, excluding readers who do not consider English their first language. For example, a systematic review found English-predominant biology journals had higher impact factors and typically rejected articles in other languages, equating scientific excellence with publishing in English. These journals often obligate authors to not publish elsewhere, severely limiting access.19 The Lancet found similar restrictions in medical literature, urging medical journals to publish abstracts in the origin country’s language.20 Although there are few studies regarding why journals prefer publishing in English, given that language barriers may compromise quality of care, global organizations should be aware of this accessibility limitation if publishing guidance exclusively in English. Improved guidance utility through adjustments for existing resources and easier navigability has been noted in a meta-analysis and qualitative study.2123 When clinical guidance deviates from hospital resources, it negatively impacts the physician’s perception of their ability to use the guidance.24 Physicians also preferred well-organized, succinct guidance that still included necessary details.18 Interviewee suggestions to design guidance as flowcharts with mental maps could meet this preference. Implementation facilitators such as locally developed guidance, supportive hospital culture, and standardization have been noted in several other qualitative studies.2527 The ATLS was the most used trauma guide among interviewees, and adapting the ATLS to local realities would optimize a guidance tool which is quick to apply and widely used.

Finally, the preferred methods to evaluate the impact of updated guidance mentioned in interviews are reflected in the 31 novel indicators used to standardize evaluation of trauma care.28 These indicators provide a comprehensive assessment of clinical guidance based on quality of care and include measures Peruvian physicians thought would be most important.

Limitations

Given the specific geographic and institutional context, these results are not generalizable to all LMIC settings. Most interviews were unscheduled, with physicians working in the hospital; constricted time availability may have influenced participant responses. This discrepancy is noted in how attending interviews tended to be longer than resident interviews. All participants worked at urban public hospitals that may not be comparable with their rural counterparts in terms of available resources. Participants included general surgeons who work in acute care, and the number of surgeons who received specialized training in trauma was not assessed, limiting the comparability of this sample to surgeons with known formal trauma training.

Conclusion

Our study provides insight into barriers and facilitators influencing the use of clinical guidance in low-resource trauma settings. Barriers included low user-friendliness, low local applicability, and an unsupportive culture. Mobile apps, resource stratification, and administrative involvement are priorities to address these needs. Best practice must use evidence-based guidance, but without alternatives accounting for available resources, physicians are left without tools to treat their patients, potentially negatively impacting clinical outcomes. Further research is needed to investigate how changes in clinical guidance delivery impact acute care workflow and outcomes, and how to create and distribute updated clinical guidance to better serve trauma providers worldwide.
