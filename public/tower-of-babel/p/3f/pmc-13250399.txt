Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic Model to Predict Drug–Drug Interactions With the Antibody–Drug Conjugate Trastuzumab Deruxtecan
Authors: Akiko Watanabe, Katherine L Gill, Kuan‐Fu Chen, Hannah M Jones, Mailys De Sousa Mendes, Iain Gardner, Masakatsu Kotsuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe topoisomerase I inhibitor payload (DXd) released from the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2‐targeted antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) trastuzumab deruxtecan (T‐DXd) is eliminated by hepatic uptake via OATP1B1/3, metabolism by CYP3A, biliary excretion via P‐glycoprotein and breast cancer resistance protein, and urinary excretion. In a clinical drug–drug interaction (DDI) study between T‐DXd and ritonavir (a strong cytochrome P450 [CYP] 3A/organic anion transporting polypeptide 1B inhibitor) or itraconazole (a strong CYP3A inhibitor), a 1.2‐fold increase in DXd exposure was observed. In this study, a physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model of T‐DXd was constructed to quantitatively describe the pharmacokinetics (PK) of DXd and the DDI of DXd released from T‐DXd. The minimal ADC PBPK model was developed to describe both intact ADC and payload PK using in vitro data of DXd and clinical PK data after T‐DXd administration. The developed model reasonably described the PK of T‐DXd and DXd after intravenous administration of T‐DXd. The main elimination pathway of DXd was estimated to be biliary excretion, followed by urinary excretion, with metabolism contributing to a lesser extent. DDIs with ritonavir and itraconazole simulated by the PBPK model reproduced the clinical DDI study results, indicating that the impact of concomitant medications was not clinically meaningful. These findings suggest that this model may potentially be used for DDI evaluation of future DXd‐containing ADCs, aiding in the prediction of DDIs under different scenarios and in specific populations.Keywords: antibody–drug conjugate, deruxtecan, drug–drug interaction, physiologically based pharmacokinetic model

Introduction

Trastuzumab deruxtecan (T‐DXd) is a human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)‐targeted antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) composed of a humanized monoclonal antibody specifically targeting HER2 (trastuzumab), a cleavable tetrapeptide‐based linker, and DXd, a potent topoisomerase I inhibitor payload molecule [1, 2]. T‐DXd is under development for the treatment of solid tumors, including, but not limited to, breast cancer, gastric cancer, and non‐small cell lung cancer. The approved dose of T‐DXd is 5.4 mg/kg every 3 weeks for breast cancer and 6.4 mg/kg every 3 weeks for gastric cancer.

Patients with cancer are often elderly; they may be receiving concomitant medications for other diseases and treatments for infections, which raises concerns about potential drug–drug interactions (DDIs). ADCs consist of an antibody component linked to a payload, necessitating an evaluation of DDIs involving both the antibody and the payload [3]. However, reports on DDIs related to the antibody component are generally limited. In contrast, the payload is a small molecule that can function as a substrate or inhibitor/inducer of metabolic enzymes and transporters, requiring a similar assessment of DDI risks similar to those of conventional small molecules. Regarding the perpetrator DDI risk of ADC payloads in general, while in vitro evaluations have shown inhibitory or inductive effects on metabolic enzymes and transporters, the actual risk of DDIs may be limited because of the very low plasma concentrations of the payload. Regarding victim DDIs, because the payload is generally cytotoxic and its plasma concentration often affects safety, concerns may arise if the payload serves as a substrate for metabolic enzymes or transporters, as co‐administration with inhibitors of these elimination pathways could lead to increased plasma concentrations.

In vitro studies have identified DXd, the payload of T‐DXd, as a substrate of cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A, organic anion transporting polypeptide (OATP) 1B1/1B3, P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) [4]. Furthermore, excretion studies following intravenous administration of [14C]T‐DXd in monkeys demonstrated that the radiolabeled compound was primarily excreted in feces, with some payload excreted in urine. The main component detected in both urine and feces was unchanged DXd. In bile‐duct cannulated rats, administration of [14C]DXd showed that most of the radioactivity was excreted in bile, with unchanged DXd being the main component detected [4]. These findings suggest that DXd is predominantly excreted unchanged via bile, with partial metabolism by CYP3A. Therefore, concomitant use of CYP3A inhibitors or transporter inhibitors involved in biliary excretion may lead to increased plasma concentrations of DXd.

In clinical settings, although the plasma concentration of DXd after T‐DXd administration is much lower than that of intact T‐DXd, it has been shown to be associated with hematologic toxicity [5]. Thus, it is important to evaluate the impact of concomitant medications on the pharmacokinetics (PK) of DXd. A clinical DDI study was conducted with T‐DXd, assessing the effects of ritonavir, which has inhibitory effects on both CYP3A and OATP1B, and itraconazole, a strong CYP3A inhibitor [6]. When T‐DXd was administered intravenously at 5.4 mg/kg in combination with either ritonavir or itraconazole, the serum concentration of DXd increased by approximately 1.2‐fold in both cases. Moreover, combination treatment did not result in clinically significant safety changes compared with T‐DXd monotherapy. It was concluded that the increase in DXd exposure resulting from the co‐administration of ritonavir or itraconazole was not clinically significant.

Currently, multiple ADCs using the same DXd payload are under development. The use of physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling for the assessment of DDI risk of ADCs with the same payload has been proposed [3]. PBPK modeling has been used for DDI risk assessments of a series of ADCs containing monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) as a payload. MMAE, the payload of brentuximab vedotin, polatuzumab vedotin, and enfortumab vedotin, is a substrate of CYP3A and P‐gp that acts as a CYP3A inhibitor [7]. Clinical DDI studies have been conducted for brentuximab vedotin to evaluate the DDI risks both as a perpetrator and as a victim for its payload, MMAE. Subsequently, PBPK modeling was employed for DDI evaluations of polatuzumab vedotin and enfortumab vedotin [8, 9, 10]. The PBPK model construction incorporated in vitro studies of MMAE and ADCs containing MMAE, in vivo absorption, dissolution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) study results in animals, and clinical PK profile data. Furthermore, the PBPK model was validated using clinical DDI data of brentuximab vedotin. These PBPK models were used to predict DDIs with concomitant medications following administration of ADCs containing MMAE, with the results reflected in the product labeling. Thus, when multiple ADCs with the same payload are under development, existing DDI study data from earlier ADCs can be very useful in assessing DDI risks for subsequent ADCs using PBPK models.

In this study, we incorporated the existing PK properties of DXd into a PBPK model to gain a deeper understanding of the PK characteristics of DXd following T‐DXd administration and to assist in the DDI risk assessment for subsequent DXd‐ADCs.

Methods

Clinical Data

Clinical data from studies DS8201‐A‐J101 and DS8201‐A‐A104 were used in this modeling exercise [6, 11]. In Clinical Study DS8201‐A‐J101, T‐DXd was administered at 5.4 and 6.4 mg/kg every 21 days in the dose‐expansion cohort [11]. Data from the dose‐expansion cohorts (Parts 2a and 2c) were used for the PBPK modeling. In Clinical Study DS8201‐A‐A104, T‐DXd was administered at 5.4 mg/kg every 21 days [6]. The DDI assessment was conducted during Cycles 2 and 3. Inhibitors were administered from Day 17 of Cycle 2 until the end of Cycle 3. Ritonavir was administered at a dose of 200 mg twice daily (BID), and itraconazole at 200 mg BID on Day 17 of Cycle 2 and then 200 mg once daily on subsequent days. The effect of the inhibitors on payload kinetics was assessed by comparing the area under the curve (AUC)17day and maximum plasma concentration (C max) in Cycle 2 (no inhibitor) and Cycle 3 (with inhibitor).

PBPK Model

The Simcyp Population‐Based Simulator Version 21 (Certara UK Limited, Sheffield, UK) was used for all simulations. Serum and plasma concentrations were assumed to be equivalent.

T‐DXd Model

The minimal ADC model within the Simcyp Simulator was used to describe the PK of T‐DXd. In the model, extravasation of the protein from the vascular space into the tissue, endosomal uptake, binding to neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), and subsequent recycling to the vascular space or transcytosis to the interstitial space are accounted for [10, 12]. The equilibrium dissociation constant for 1:1 drug–FcRn complex (K D1) for T‐DXd was calibrated using the measured data for trastuzumab (K D = 1.64 μM) (data on file) and published data for trastuzumab (K D = 0.773 μM [15]), which came from the same publication as the endogenous immunoglobulin (Ig) G K D used in the Simcyp Simulator [12]. A 1:2 stoichiometry for the binding of ADC and FcRn was modeled as this is more physiologically realistic than the use of a 1:1 binding stoichiometry [16].

The endosomal uptake rate via fluid phase endocytosis (K up) and fraction of FcRn‐bound monoclonal antibody recycled (FR) values were estimated using the parameter estimation module within the Simcyp Simulator by fitting to the observed plasma concentrations following 5.4 mg/kg (Clinical Study DS8201‐A‐A104) [6, 17]. The catabolic clearance (0.0175 L/h) and additional clearance (0.0017 L/h) values were retained at the values used to describe endogenous IgG kinetics in the Simcyp Simulator.

Within the ADC model, the payload can be liberated from the ADC by sequential deconjugation or, if an ADC molecule is cleared (i.e., via catabolic clearance or degradation of the ADC), all of the payload still conjugated to the ADC is liberated. The default sequential deconjugation rate constant in Simcyp V21 (K dec = 0.004167 h−1) was used for T‐DXd. Deconjugation had a minimal contribution to the release of DXd in the simulations. Complete degradation of the ADC in the endosome was the main driver for DXd appearance in plasma in the PBPK model. Following ADC degradation, all payload molecules can be fully released into plasma or partially released, with only a fraction of the payload molecules entering the circulation. The fraction of payload released from the site of catabolism (F rel) in the PBPK model was set to 0.5, as this resulted in concentrations of DXd that were in line with the observed data. DXd Model

The PBPK model for DXd was developed using a bottom‐up approach and available in vitro and in vivo non‐clinical data. The steady‐state volume of distribution of DXd was predicted to be 0.2 L/kg using Method 3 [22] within the Simcyp Simulator. This is similar to the allometrically scaled value from monkey data (0.15 L/kg) [4], after accounting for interspecies differences in unbound fraction. The clearance of DXd is dependent on metabolism, hepatic uptake, hepatic efflux, and renal clearance processes. To accommodate all of these processes, a permeability‐limited model of the liver (PerL) was nested in the full PBPK model for DXd [13].

In the PerL model, the rate of metabolism of DXd was calculated from the unbound intrinsic metabolic clearance (CLint) scaled from human liver microsomes (HLM). The HLM CLint was divided between CYP3A4 clearance and other metabolic pathways based on an in vitro HLM ketoconazole experiment.

The passive diffusion (CLPD) and the overall active hepatic uptake of DXd were calculated based on the results of in vitro human hepatocyte experiments. Data from HEK293 cells expressing OATP transporters were used to estimate the ratio of the OATP1B1/OATP1B3 contribution to DXd hepatic uptake, assuming the same expression levels of OATP1B1 and OATP1B3 in transfected HEK cells. The rate of biliary efflux was calculated from the scaled unbound hepatic P‐gp and BCRP intrinsic clearances from human colon adenocarcinoma (Caco‐2) cells and the relative expression factor for P‐gp and BCRP between Caco‐2 cells and hepatocytes. A detailed description of the in vitro modeling of P‐gp, BCRP, OATP1B1, and OATP1B3 and derivation of their transport kinetic parameters is available in Methods S1 and Table S1.

It was assumed that any DXd effluxed into bile in the PerL model entered the intestine via gall bladder emptying, where it was excreted unchanged into the feces rather than re‐absorbed. This assumption is consistent with findings in non‐clinical ADME studies, where the compound is largely excreted unchanged in feces [4]. This is assumed to be due to the action of intestinal efflux transporters (BCRP and P‐gp).

The renal clearance was predicted to be 2.82 L/h based on allometric scaling using monkey renal clearance data [4], after accounting for interspecies differences in unbound fraction. The final input parameters for the DXd PBPK model are summarized in Table 1.

Model Validation

The PK of T‐DXd and DXd and the DDIs with ritonavir and itraconazole were simulated and compared with observed clinical data from Clinical Study DS8201‐A‐A104 [6]. The default PBPK models for ritonavir and itraconazole (and OH‐itraconazole) within Simcyp V21 were used in DDI simulations. In this study, the reproducibility of the inhibitor plasma exposures (ritonavir and itraconazole) could not be confirmed because the inhibitor plasma concentrations were not measured. However, the predictive accuracy of these models has been confirmed using clinical data in Simcyp. The default ritonavir model does not contain inhibition constant (Ki) values for OATP1B1 and OATP1B3; hence, values of 0.042857 μM and 0.051429 μM were added into the ritonavir PBPK model (further details are given in the Methods S2, Tables S2 and S3). PK prediction performance was assessed by comparing predicted and observed PK parameters. A predicted‐to‐observed ratio between 0.5 and twofold was considered acceptable based on published criteria [23].

The DDI simulations were conducted with the Sim‐Japanese and Sim‐Cancer populations. Simulations were conducted using 10 trials per study subject and were aligned with the study design. Where possible, the demographics of the simulated population were matched to those of the subjects in the clinical trials. For the ritonavir study, the simulations were performed with 10 trials of 12 Japanese subjects aged 48–70 years or cancer patients aged 48–80 years (67% were female), based on Clinical Study DS8201‐A‐A104 [6]. For the itraconazole cohort, there were 14 Asian subjects aged 31–69 years (28.6% were female).

DXd Pathway Mass Balance

To verify whether the DXd model—constructed using a bottom‐up approach based on in vitro and preclinical in vivo data—accurately describes the mass balance of DXd, pathway mass balance was assessed following a single intravenous (IV) dose of DXd (0.1 mg administered over 0.5 h). The simulations were performed with 10 trials of 14 cancer patients aged 31–69 years (28.6% were female), based on the design of Clinical Study DS8201‐A‐A104 [6].

Accounting for Risk of Uncertainty in DXd DDI Predictions

Owing to the lack of clinical data following direct administration of DXd to verify the DXd model parameters, sensitivity analyses were performed to assess parameter identifiability and evaluate potential risks to the conclusions drawn from the DDI study predictions. Sensitivity analysis was performed by increasing or decreasing the contribution of CYP3A4 metabolism, uptake (OATP1B1 and OATP1B3), efflux (P‐gp and BCRP), or renal clearance by up to 10‐fold. The impact on the predicted DDI between unconjugated DXd and itraconazole or ritonavir following administration of T‐DXd 5.4 mg/kg every 21 days was assessed.

Results

Model Development

The PBPK model accuracy reproduced the observed PK parameters of T‐DXd (5.4 mg/kg), with AUCtau and C max within 1.5‐fold of the observed values (Table 2). Two different T‐DXd F rel values were tested. When 100% of DXd was released back to the blood (F rel = 100%), the DXd AUCtau was overpredicted (Table 2). When only 50% of DXd was released back to the blood (F rel = 50%), the recovery of the DXd PK profile was improved, and AUCtau was predicted within 1.25‐fold of the observed values (Table 2 and Figure 2). F rel = 50% was used in subsequent simulations.

Model Validation

The T‐DXd and DXd profiles following administration of 6.4 mg/kg were well recovered by the model, with PK parameters predicted within 1.5‐fold of the observed values (Table 2 and Figure 2). The simulated ritonavir and itraconazole DDI effects on DXd AUC were consistent with the observed data, with geometric mean ratios (GMRs) within 1.1‐fold of the observed values (Table 3). The GMRs of C max of DXd with ritonavir and itraconazole were slightly overpredicted, with GMRs within 1.5‐fold of the observed values. The simulated results were comparable between F rel = 100% and F rel = 50% (Table S4). PK profiles showing the observed and predicted DXd concentrations in the presence of itraconazole and ritonavir are shown in Figure S1.

DXd Pathway Mass Balance

The contribution of each elimination pathway in humans was evaluated following the simulation of a single dose of DXd directly (0.1 mg over a 0.5 h IV infusion) in the unconjugated DXd model (Table S5). Biliary clearance was the main route of elimination for DXd, accounting for 62.3% of the total elimination (Table S5). Renal elimination was also a notable pathway, contributing to 32.6% of the total elimination of DXd. Metabolism by CYP3A4 and other HLM pathways contributed minimally to the total elimination of DXd (4.8% by CYP3A4, 0.3% by others).

Accounting for Risk of Uncertainty in DXd DDI Predictions

Sensitivity analyses of DXd clearance pathways with GMRs for DXd from DDI simulations following administration of T‐DXd 5.4 mg/kg every 21 days in the presence and absence of ritonavir or itraconazole are shown in Figure 3, Table S6 (ritonavir), and Table S7 (itraconazole). A 10‐fold increase or decrease in DXd CYP3A4 metabolism had a negligible impact on the predicted DDIs between T‐DXd and ritonavir or itraconazole. A weak interaction (1.25‐fold ≤ C max or AUC GMR < 2‐fold) was predicted with ritonavir even when DXd uptake clearance was increased up to 10‐fold, renal clearance was decreased up to 10‐fold, or efflux clearance (into bile) was increased or decreased by up to 10‐fold. No interaction was predicted with ritonavir when unconjugated DXd uptake clearance was reduced ≤ 10‐fold or renal clearance was increased ≤ 10‐fold. Similar to the baseline model, no interaction was predicted with itraconazole even when DXd uptake clearance was reduced ≤ 10‐fold, efflux clearance was increased ≤ 10‐fold, or renal clearance was increased ≤ 10‐fold. A weak interaction (1.25‐fold≤ C max or AUC GMR < 2‐fold) was predicted with itraconazole when DXd uptake clearance was increased ≤ 10‐fold, efflux clearance was decreased ≤ 10‐fold, or renal clearance was decreased by 10‐fold. The baseline model for DXd generally gave the most accurate predictions of the observed DDI with T‐DXd and ritonavir or itraconazole.

Discussion

T‐DXd is a HER2‐targeted ADC composed of a humanized monoclonal antibody specifically targeting HER2, a cleavable tetrapeptide‐based linker, and a potent topoisomerase I inhibitor payload (DXd). Non‐clinical findings suggested that DXd is predominantly excreted as the unchanged form in the bile via OATP1B1/3‐mediated hepatic uptake and excretion from the liver by P‐gp and BCRP, with some metabolism by CYP3A and other CYPs. A clinical DDI study of T‐DXd was conducted to evaluate the impact of inhibitors for CYP3A and OATP1B1/1B3 on the PK of DXd after T‐DXd administration. This study revealed that the increase in DXd exposure was minimal (approximately 1.2‐fold with ritonavir or itraconazole). In this study, a PBPK model of T‐DXd was developed and reported for the first time to account for the impact of these inhibitors on the PK of DXd and assist in the DDI risk assessment for subsequent DXd‐containing ADCs.

The minimal ADC PBPK model was able to recover plasma concentration–time profiles and exposure levels of T‐DXd and unconjugated DXd following administration of T‐DXd 5.4 and 6.4 mg/kg with acceptable accuracy (Table 2). T‐DXd C max and AUCtau predictions differed from the observed data by approximately 20%. The T‐DXd F rel was adjusted from 1 to 0.5 to reproduce the observed DXd AUCtau. F rel is a parameter that defines the proportion of DXd released into the bloodstream relative to the total amount of DXd bound to the administered ADC, and it affects the absolute exposure of DXd (AUC and C max). The reduction of F rel indicates that within the PBPK model, not all DXd molecules liberated from the ADCs are released into the systemic circulation. This assumption seems reasonable, since it is likely that DXd released from T‐DXd taken up by the liver is directly excreted into bile. In fact, the plasma exposure of DXd following administration of T‐DXd to monkeys was approximately half that observed with direct administration of DXd (data on file).

The T‐DXd PBPK model also reproduced the observed changes in the AUC of DXd in DDI studies with ritonavir and itraconazole. Both clinical data and PBPK simulations showed minimal increases in the DXd AUCtau (Table 3). The PBPK model slightly overpredicted the DXd C max GMRs compared with clinical data, in which no increases in C max were observed. This discrepancy may be partly explained by clinical findings showing that the DXd C max after multiple doses of T‐DXd decreased by 24% compared with Cycle 1, likely due to reduced release from decreased numbers of HER2‐expressing tumor cells after multiple doses of T‐DXd [24]. The DDI study between T‐DXd and ritonavir or itraconazole was designed as a single‐sequence crossover study to compare Cycle 2 (ADC alone) versus Cycle 3 (ADC with inhibitor). When the inhibitor is present, the C max in Cycle 3 may increase; however, this increase may not be apparent relative to the C max value observed in Cycle 2 without the inhibitor, owing to the decreased number of HER2‐expressing tumor cells. In the T‐DXd PBPK model, the phenomenon leading to the decreased payload release was not considered, which may have led to the overprediction of the C max GMR.

The impact of F rel (1 vs. 0.5) on the prediction of DDI with ritonavir and itraconazole was also investigated (Table S4). The predicted GMRs of the DXd C max and AUC values were the same regardless of the F rel values. Because DDI primarily evaluates the effect on DXd released into the bloodstream, if F rel remains the same in the presence and absence of an inhibitor, and if the DXd blood concentration is sufficiently low and within the linear range, it will not affect the DDI results.

A recognized limitation of the PBPK model for DXd is the absence of human PK data to verify the performance of the model when DXd is administered directly. Mass balance following different elimination pathways in humans was evaluated following administration of a single dose of DXd directly (0.1 mg administered over 0.5 h via IV infusion) in the unconjugated DXd model. The renal clearance, liver metabolism, and biliary clearance in humans were predicted to be 32.6%, 5.1% (4.8% by CYP3A4, 0.3% by others), and 62.3%, respectively (Table S5). The pre‐clinical data indicated that at 24 h after IV administration of 1 mg/kg [14C]DXd to bile duct‐cannulated rats, 19.8%, 71.5%, and 1.0% of the radioactivity were excreted in urine, bile, and feces. respectively. At 336 h after IV administration of 6.4 mg/kg [14C]T‐DXd to monkeys, 86.0% of the radioactivity was recovered, with 18.7% in urine and 67.3% in feces [4]. No metabolite peaks were detected in the excreta of both species. The most abundant radioactive component was unchanged DXd. The predicted mass balance in humans based on the DXd model was consistent with the mass balance data from the pre‐clinical species (rat and monkey).

To evaluate the impact of the estimated contribution of DXd clearance pathways on predicted DDIs with ritonavir and itraconazole, sensitivity analyses of each clearance parameter were conducted (Figure 3, Tables S6 and S7). In particular, changes in uptake clearance and renal clearance had some impact on the results of the DDI prediction. An increase or decrease in uptake clearance values resulted in an increase or decrease in the C max and AUC ratios, while changes in renal clearance had the opposite effect. However, the magnitude of these changes was limited, and the classification as a weak interaction remained unchanged. In contrast, the mass balance was markedly altered when individual elimination clearances were varied (data not shown). Considering both the plausibility of the mass balance based on animal data and the reproducibility of the clinical DDI study results, the reliability of the current model can be considered reasonably high.

Another limitation of the PBPK model is that time‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD) was not considered within the PBPK model, as the doses of interest were high enough that TMDD was saturated. The dose‐escalation phase in Clinical Study DS8201‐A‐J101 established that area under the curve from 0 to last (AUClast) increased proportionally with doses above 3.2 mg/kg [11]. Hence, extrapolation of the PBPK model to lower doses or ADCs with the same linker and payload but different antibody, where TMDD may be important (i.e., not saturated), is not appropriate. However, this PBPK model structure is considered sufficient for predicting DDI at doses where TMDD is saturated.

In conclusion, the PBPK model of T‐DXd, constructed using nonclinical and clinical results obtained to date, appears to appropriately describe the PK characteristics of DXd and to reasonably reproduce the results of clinical DDI studies. This PBPK model is expected to be useful for DDI evaluation of subsequent DXd ADCs with the same payload and linker. The DXd model can be directly applied when evaluating new DXd‐containing ADCs. To construct the ADC model, the minimum in vitro and preclinical data needed for a reliable prediction would include the K D value and drug‐to‐antibody ratio distribution for FcRn. If the PK profiles of the ADC and DXd differ from those of T‐DXd, the parameters of the ADC model (e.g., K up, FR, and F rel) must be adjusted to accurately represent these observed PK profiles. F rel is thought to depend primarily on the extent of ADC uptake into target‐specific and non‐target tissues. Even for subsequent ADCs with the same payload and linker, F rel may differ if tissue distribution differs; therefore, optimization of F rel using clinical PK data from subsequent ADCs is necessary. In fact, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration review of datopotamab deruxtecan [25], a subsequent DXd‐based ADC following T‐DXd, noted that this PBPK analysis is applicable to evaluating DDI potential for DXd‐based ADCs that share the same linker and payload as T‐DXd. Moreover, this PBPK model will be widely useful for DDI prediction under different scenarios (such as different doses or inhibitors) and PK prediction in specific populations (such as in pediatric populations and patients with organ impairment).
