Characteristics of JIA-associated uveitis at the age of transition
Authors: Anna Raymond, Alice Combier, Sophie Hecquet, Dominique Monnet, Fatam El Fakih, Julien Wipff, Gertrude Touanga, Pierre Quartier, Yannick Allanore, Marion Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJuvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the most common rheumatic disease in children. Uveitis is the most frequent extra-articular manifestation of JIA and a major cause of visual morbidity. Despite advances in immunomodulatory therapy, many patients reach adulthood with active ocular inflammation or vision-threatening complications. The transition from pediatric to adult care represents a vulnerable period. The primary objective of our study is to describe ophthalmologic and rheumatologic disease characteristics at the time of transition from pediatric to adult care.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with JIA and past or present uveitis who transitioned to adult rheumatology at Cochin Hospital between 2016 and 2024. Clinical, ophthalmologic, and therapeutic data were collected from electronic medical records. Descriptive statistics were performed. Comparative analyses were exploratory and intended to describe differences between subgroups rather than to test predefined hypotheses.ResultsA total of 46 patients were included. Median age at JIA diagnosis was 7.5 years [IQR 2.0–16.0] and median age at first uveitis was 6.0 years [IQR 3.0–12.2]. Median follow-up after transition was 2.44 years [IQR 1.17–3.94]. Most patients were female (80%, n = 37) and had oligoarticular JIA (59%, n = 27). Chronic uveitis predominated (83%, n = 38). Ocular complications occurred in 46% (n = 17), including cataract (24%, n = 11), glaucoma (20%, n = 9), and keratitis (7%, n = 3). Over half (57%, n = 13/23) experienced ≥ 5 flares since diagnosis. Biologic DMARDs were prescribed in 53% (n = 23/43), predominantly anti-TNF agents.ConclusionThis study highlights the substantial burden of JIA-associated uveitis at the time of transition to adult care, characterized by frequent complications, persistent disease activity, and a high need for biologic therapy. Our findings emphasize the necessity of structured and continuous ophthalmologic follow-up across all JIA subtypes, alongside close multidisciplinary collaboration, to prevent long-term ocular damage and preserve visual function.

Introduction

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the most common rheumatic disease in children, classified according to the International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) criteria [1]. Uveitis is the most frequent extra-articular manifestation of JIA occurring in approximately 8–30% of patients [2, 3]. In the oligoarticular subtype, which predominates in younger children aged 0–6 years, uveitis typically presents as a chronic, anterior, bilateral, and asymptomatic inflammation [4, 5]. In contrast, acute anterior uveitis—often associated with HLA-B27 and spondyloarthritis—is usually episodic, unilateral, and manifests with ocular pain, redness, and photophobia [6]. Chronic anterior uveitis (CAU) remains the most common form of uveitis in the pediatric population. Its insidious and asymptomatic course often delays diagnosis, highlighting the need for regular ophthalmological screening to detect inflammation before irreversible complications occur.

In terms of treatment, topical corticosteroids remain the first-line therapy for active uveitis in children with JIA, with the goal of rapidly suppressing intraocular inflammation [2]. However, both American and French guidelines now emphasize that prolonged dependence on topical corticosteroids is no longer acceptable due to the risk of cataract and glaucoma, and because the advent of biologic therapies has revolutionized the management of severe or refractory JIA-associated uveitis [2, 4].

Although the advent of biologic therapy has significantly improved long-term morbidity and mortality, a significant number of patients still reach adulthood with active joint or ocular disease. In a cohort of 80 patients from a Canadian team, 26% had clinically detected joint damage, 31% had joint damage identified through imaging, and 14% experienced uveitis during the transitional period [7]. The transition period is the structured and intentional shift during which adolescents and young adults with chronic health conditions transfer from pediatric services to adult healthcare systems, with the aim of maintaining continuity of care and supporting their evolving medical and developmental needs [8]. Case series suggest that inadequate transition from pediatric to adult care can exacerbate ophthalmological complications. In one report, two out of nine patients developed severe vision loss or blindness because of interrupted follow-up and treatment during this critical phase [7]. Thus, the transition period represents a crucial window for maintaining disease control and preventing irreversible complications, particularly in JIA-associated uveitis, where treatment interruption and loss to follow-up are frequent. However, standardized recommendations for uveitis monitoring during transition are lacking, and the role of ophthalmologists in this process remains poorly defined. Therefore, this study focused on disease activity and complications occurring during the transition from pediatric to adult care.

The primary objective is to describe ophthalmologic and rheumatologic disease characteristics at the time of transition from pediatric to adult care. The secondary objectives were to compare clinical presentation and treatment burden according to the presence or absence of uveitis at transition, and to compare patients with chronic versus acute uveitis.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study included all consecutive JIA-associated uveitis patients transitioning from pediatric to adult care at Cochin Hospital (Paris) between 2016 and 2024.

Data sources and population

Data were extracted from the electronic medical records of JIA patients who were part of a pre-existing longitudinal cohort transitioned to adult care in the Rheumatology Department of Cochin Hospital.

To be included in the study, patients had to meet the following criteria: a diagnosis of JIA according to ILAR criteria, at least one episode of uveitis, and have attended at least one transition consultation at Cochin Hospital. Exclusion criteria included patients with a lack of follow-up prior to transition.

Monitoring during transition

Effective transition was defined as the first consultation in the adult rheumatology department, scheduled at the request of the pediatrician, usually around 16 years of age, and conducted according to a standardized protocol with a dedicated medical team identified within the adult department. Thereafter, patients were followed exclusively in the adult rheumatology department, with visits scheduled every 3 to 6 months according to disease activity and treatment. Ophthalmologic follow-up included one consultation at the time of transition, with subsequent evaluations performed according to clinical need and assessed in the present study. Transition was considered complete once the patient had attended at least one follow-up visit in adult rheumatology care without further involvement of the pediatric team.

Variables

We collected demographic data such as age at diagnosis of JIA, age at transition visit, date of last follow-up and duration of follow-up.

Characteristics of JIA were collected: subgroup, severity reflected by articular destruction defined based on descriptions in medical records, including radiographic hip erosion recorded at transition. Positivity of diagnostic biological markers was reported: rheumatoid factor (RF) > 15 IU/mL, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) > 20 IU/mL, Antinuclear Antibodies (ANA) > 1:80, presence of HLA-B27.

Uveitis characteristics were collected either from the electronic ophthalmological record, when available, or from the rheumatological record. Type of uveitis was categorized as chronic or acute anterior uveitis. The presence of posterior segment involvement and bilateral disease were also recorded. To assess the severity of uveitis, the occurrence of ocular complications was documented: Glaucoma was defined as optic nerve damage with corresponding visual field loss, and elevated intraocular pressure (IOP ≥ 21 mmHg). IOP was measured using Goldmann applanation tonometry, and visual field defects were assessed by standard automated perimetry. Synechiae were identified as adhesions between the iris and adjacent structures on slit-lamp exam, cataract was diagnosed by lens opacification on slit-lamp examination, and vision loss was defined as a decrease in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) on the Snellen chart, confirmed on at least two consecutive visits. Patients with multiple uveitis flares were identified as those who experienced 5 or more flares during the entire follow-up period. A flare was defined as an episode of active uveitis following a symptom-free interval of at least 3 months between relapses. According to the literature, the risk of ocular complications or poor visual outcomes has been associated with as few as two flares per yearr even just one episode during follow-up [9, 10]. Given the median duration of 13 years from diagnosis to transition in our cohort, and the lack of robust data regarding this parameter, we chose to use a threshold of five flares across the follow-up period. We also collected data on the frequency and setting of ophthalmologic follow-up, categorized as hospital-based or private practice care. Finally, persistent uveitis at transition was defined as uveitis that was either documented during the transition consultation or newly diagnosed shortly thereafter.

Treatments administered during follow-up were recorded, including those initiated specifically for uveitis, such as biologic and targeted synthetic DMARDs (bDMARDs / tsDMARDs) and their type, conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs), or intravenous corticosteroids. Data were also collected on the specific treatment subtypes used in the overall management of the inflammatory disease, including anti-TNF alpha agents, anti-IL17 agents, anti CTLA-4 therapy, JAK inhibitors, and azathioprine. Among bDMARDs and tsDMARDs, individual molecules were recorded to determine the number of bDMARDs to which patients had been exposed from diagnosis to transition. Each anti-TNF agent was considered a distinct molecule; therefore, switching between different anti-TNF therapies was counted as exposure to separate bDMARDs.

Statistical analysis

We performed descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on the distribution. Categorical variables were presented as percentages. Comparative analyses were exploratory and intended to describe differences between subgroups rather than to test predefined hypotheses. Continuous variables were compared using Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study. All data were anonymized to protect patient identities, with access limited to the research team. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. According to the Health Research Authority guidelines, Ethics Committee approval was not required, as the study was descriptive in nature and all information was collected anonymously.

Results

Cohort description and overall follow-up

We identified 46 patients with JIA and past uveitis who had undergone transitional care and follow-up at Cochin Hospital. Descriptive characteristics are presented in Table 1. Our population was followed for a median duration of 2.44 years [IQR 1.17–3.94] after transition. The median age at JIA diagnosis was 7.5 years [IQR 2.0–16.0], and 80% (n = 37) of patients were female. The median age at first uveitis onset was 6.0 years [IQR 3.0-12.2]. Regarding JIA subtypes, 59% of patients (n = 27) had oligoarticular JIA (oligo-JIA), 11% (n = 5) had polyarticular JIA (poly-JIA), 17% (n = 8) had spondyloarthritis (SpA), 9% (n = 4) had psoriatic arthritis, and 4% of patients (n = 2) had undifferentiated JIA. Among the cohort, 83% (n = 38) had chronic uveitis, while 17% (n = 8) had acute anterior uveitis.

Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) were positive in 58% (n = 26/45) of patients, and HLA-B27 was present in 10% (n = 5) of patients, four of whom had SpA. Data about ANA status was missing for one patient. Overall, 83% of patients had chronic uveitis (n = 38) and 17% acute anterior uveitis (n = 8).

Regarding treatments, bDMARDs were prescribed in 53% of patients (n = 23/43) specifically for uveitis. Biologic exposure varied by JIA subtype: 63% (n = 27) of those with oligo-JIA, 50% (n = 5) with poly-JIA, 62% (n = 8) with SpA, 0% with psoriatic arthritis, and 100% (n = 2) of patients with undifferentiated JIA received a bDMARD. Data on biologic treatment were available for 43 patients.

All patients treated with bDMARDs received an anti-TNF. The mean number of bDMARDs to which patients had been exposed from diagnosis to transition was 1.89 in oligo-JIA and 2.4 in SpA. Overall, all patients received topical corticosteroids, 52% (n = 21/40) received systemic corticosteroids and 41% (n = 18/44) were treated with conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs). Data on systemic corticosteroids and csDMARDs were available respectively for 40 patients and 44 patients.

Disease characteristics during the transition period

At the time of transition, 50% of patients (n = 22/44) still had active uveitis. Among those, acute anterior uveitis accounted for 27% of cases.

Ocular complications were present in 46% of patients with available data at transition (n = 17/37), including glaucoma (n = 9/17), cataract (n = 11/17), and keratitis (n = 3/17).

Follow-up data were available for 33 patients. However, data on follow-up frequency were only available for 24 patients; among them, 54% (n = 13/24) had ophthalmologic visits at intervals longer than 6 months. Hospital-based follow-up was reported in 58% of patients with available data (n = 19/33).

Regarding treatment at transition, 53% of patients with available data (n = 23/43) received bDMARDs at transition; all were treated with anti-TNF agents. Additional therapies introduced at transition included abatacept (n = 2/43; 5%), anti-IL-17 agents (n = 5/43; 12%), anti-IL-6 (n = 1/43; 2%), azathioprine (n = 1/43; 2%), and JAK inhibitors (n = 2/43; 5%).

The median age at first uveitis was 4 years [IQR 2.0–9.0] in the chronic group versus 17.0 years [IQR 14.5–18.5] in the acute group (p < 0.001).

Chronic uveitis occurred predominantly in girls (n = 31/38; 82%) and was mainly associated with oligoarticular JIA (n = 26/38; 68%). In contrast, acute anterior uveitis was more frequently observed in patients with SpA (n = 3/8; 43%) or polyarticular JIA (n = 2/8; 25%), and less often in oligoarticular JIA (n = 1/8; 12.5%) (p = 0.015). ANA positivity was more common in the chronic group (n = 25/37; 68%; p < 0.01), with one missing data point.

Bilateral involvement was observed in 79% of patients with chronic uveitis and in none of those with acute uveitis. Data on the timing of uveitis onset were available for a limited number of patients; among them, uveitis as the first manifestation of JIA occurred only in the chronic group (n = 10/28; 36%).

Ophthalmological complications were more frequent in chronic uveitis (n = 16/31; 52%) than in acute uveitis (n = 1/6; 17%) (p = 0.19). Similarly, more than five flares during follow-up were reported in 50% of chronic cases (n = 15/30) versus 17% of acute cases (n = 1/6).

Among patients with ongoing uveitis at transition, oligoarticular JIA was the most frequent subtype (59%; n = 13), followed by SpA (18%; n = 4), while other subtypes were less represented. The subtype distribution did not differ markedly from that observed in patients whose uveitis occurred only before transition.

A majority of patients with uveitis at transition (68%; n = 13/19) had experienced more than five flares since diagnosis. Their follow-up appeared suboptimal, with only 23% having ophthalmologic visits at intervals < 6 months and 31% lacking systematic follow-up.

In terms of treatment, patients with uveitis at transition received biologic or immunosuppressive therapies in 76% of cases (n = 16/21; p < 0.01), with a median of 2.0 [IQR 2.0-4.5] different treatments. They received anti-TNF but also JAK inhibitors (n = 1/21; 4.5%), azathioprine (n = 2/21; 9%) and anti-IL17 agents (n = 3/21; 14%). Additionally, systemic corticosteroids were prescribed in 68% (n = 13/20) and csDMARDs in 52% (n = 11/22). In these patients, biologic therapies were specifically initiated for uveitis control.

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into the characteristics and management of patients with JIA-associated uveitis at the time of transition to adult care. The primary objective of our study was not to identify new risk factors, but to provide a real-life snapshot of disease activity, treatment burden, and follow-up practices at the time of transition from pediatric to adult care.

Our findings are consistent with previous reports. The majority of patients with JIA-related uveitis are female, present with an oligoarticular form, are diagnosed before seven years of age, and test positive for antinuclear antibodies (ANA) [11]. Uveitis was predominantly chronic, anterior, and bilateral. At transition, uveitis remained active in approximately half of the patients and over 40% of patients experienced ocular complications and recurrent flares. Ultimately, more than 50% of patients required biologic therapy (bDMARDs) for uveitis management.

Although chronic uveitis was mainly associated with oligo-JIA, a subset of eight patients developed chronic uveitis in the context of spondyloarthritis (SpA) or psoriatic arthritis. These cases were often complicated and occurred beyond the transition period. This observation is in line with published literature, suggesting that uveitis in JIA is intrinsically linked to the disease itself, rather than confined to a specific subtype. These findings underscore the importance of close ophthalmologic follow-up and patient education about warning signs across all JIA subtypes [12].

Notably, 50% of our cohort (n = 22) continued to experience uveitis flares during transition to adult care, highlighting the challenges of disease management during this vulnerable period. Patients with uveitis at transition exhibited more complications, more frequent flares, and greater need for biologic therapy (bDMARDs). This points out the importance of early identification of high-risk patients to adapt therapeutic strategies accordingly. While risk factors for uveitis onset in JIA are well established, those associated with severe or complicated forms remain less clearly defined [11, 13]. Inadequate management of uveitis after transition may result in long-term visual impairment, reinforcing the need for a structured and coordinated transition program [14]. Despite European consensus guidelines recommending ophthalmologic evaluations every three months for at least three years in JIA-associated uveitis, only 35% of our patients were monitored more frequently than every six months [2]. Our findings emphasize the necessity to educate patients and families about the risk of uveitis flare recurrence after transition, and the importance of maintaining regular ophthalmologic follow-up.

Regarding treatment, biologic DMARDs were prescribed for uveitis in 53% of patients. In the literature, more than 50% of patients require biologic therapy due to insufficient control with methotrexate alone [15]. Although several biologic agents are approved for arthritis, adalimumab remains the only licensed therapy for uveitis. However, as in the literature, patients in our study received various types of treatment, many of which could help control the disease [16]. This underlines the urgent need for additional therapeutic options to prevent vision loss in children with JIA, as adalimumab does not always sustain remission [17]. Secondary loss of response to anti-TNF therapy has been described, often related to anti-drug antibody formation, which necessitates treatment switching. Expanding the range of available therapies could also reduce dependence on topical corticosteroids, which are linked to the most common complications observed in our study, namely glaucoma and cataract [18].

Our results also draw attention to the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration between rheumatologists and ophthalmologists. Managing JIA-associated uveitis requires coordinated, multidisciplinary care to ensure optimal disease control and continuity through adulthood. In our study, all patients received disease-modifying therapy initiated primarily for ophthalmologic indications, underscoring the direct influence of ophthalmologic follow-up on systemic treatment decisions. Developing standardized protocols for shared decision-making and regular follow-up may improve treatment adherence and clinical outcomes, thereby reducing the risk of vision-threatening complications. Disability related to joint destruction or complicated uveitis has been reported in up to 20% of JIA patients [5].

To our knowledge, this study is among the few to specifically characterize the clinical course and management of JIA-associated uveitis during the transition from pediatric to adult care. While previous studies have focused primarily on uveitis onset and pediatric outcomes, data addressing disease activity, treatment burden, and ophthalmologic follow-up at the time of transition remain scarce. Our findings demonstrate that a substantial proportion of patients continue to have active uveitis at transition, with frequent flares and ongoing need for biologic therapy. In addition, we highlight a significant gap between recommended ophthalmologic monitoring and real-life clinical practice during this period. By focusing on this vulnerable phase of care, our study contributes novel real-world data that may support the development of structured, multidisciplinary transition programs [7].

The strengths of this study include the relatively large sample size, which provides a meaningful dataset to identify trends and outcomes in JIA-associated uveitis, and the detailed ophthalmologic characterization, which enhances understanding of disease progression and management strategies. Moreover, this study focuses on a critical transitional period that is rarely explored, providing novel insights into the continuum of care from pediatric to adult settings. However, limitations include incomplete documentation in medical records and reduced statistical power due to the single-center design. Missing data were handled by using available-case analysis. No imputation was performed due to the descriptive nature of the study. We acknowledge that missing data may affect the precision of estimates and subgroup comparisons.

Although most children with chronic anterior uveitis avoid severe visual loss, the disease still imposes a substantial burden on quality of life for both patients and families, who must cope with the emotional, social, and logistical burden of chronic illness [19]. Pediatric care typically involves the family unit, whereas adult care emphasizes patient autonomy and self-management. Transition planning should therefore extend beyond medical concerns to include psychological well-being and health-related quality of life, as JIA patients have an increased prevalence of mental health issues including depressive disorders [20]. Similarly, children with uveitis often face academic, interpersonal, and developmental difficulties, comparable to those seen in other chronic diseases. These challenges may compromise follow-up and worsen outcomes. Implementing a structured transition plan initiated during early adolescence, and integrating medical, social, psychological, and educational support from a specialized multidisciplinary team, is therefore essential.

Our findings support the need for structured, multidisciplinary transition programs, informed by real-life data, to ensure continuity of care and reduce the risk of persistent or complicated uveitis in adulthood. This includes regular ophthalmologic follow-up — every four months for at least four years, in line with current guidelines — as well as psychological, social, educational, and vocational support to ensure a successful transition and long-term disease control [3].

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study underscores the critical importance of structured and continuous ophthalmologic follow-up across all JIA subtypes during the transition to adult care. Sustained monitoring is essential to reduce the burden of chronic uveitis, prevent long-term ocular complications, and preserve visual function and quality of life. Further research is needed to evaluate long-term outcomes of patients with JIA-associated uveitis after transition to assess the efficacy of tailored treatment strategies in mitigating the risks associated with this condition.
