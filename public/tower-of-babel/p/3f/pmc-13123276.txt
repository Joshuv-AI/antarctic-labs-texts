Game, Set, and Match: A Scoping Review of Matching Characteristics for Control and Intervention Groups in Adaptive Behavioral Interventions for Physical Activity or Healthy Eating Designs for Populations with Overweight and Obesity
Authors: Jamie Whitney, Victoria A Suhy, Abigail M Pauley, Danying Shao, Owais Khan, Amy M Moore, Sy-Miin Chow, Daniel E Rivera, Danielle Symons Downs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBehavioral interventions targeting physical activity and/or healthy eating are often used for weight regulation, and adaptive intervention designs that personalize the dosage based on individuals’ responses to the intervention components may optimize impact. However, little research has examined how best to design/optimize non-pharmacological adaptive weight regulation trials, and decisions regarding selection/use of control groups in these trials are less clear. This scoping review aimed to identify and describe study and participant characteristics reported and/or used to match intervention and control groups within non-pharmacological, adaptive behavioral weight regulation interventions. Systematic search of adaptive interventions targeting physical activity/healthy eating for weight regulation was conducted following PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Study and participant characteristics reported and/or matched between groups were independently extracted and coded. Of 1,431 articles screened, 4.4% met the inclusion criteria. Study designs were protocol/rationale/design papers (43%), randomized control trials (40%), and pilot/feasibility trials (17%). Most (73%) had a control/comparison condition; 100% matched measurement protocols and dosage exposure. Many (51%) reported participant characteristics (e.g., age, body mass index [BMI]), but only 35% of studies used these as matching factors. Some studies (35%) matched groups on content. These findings illustrate that most adaptive weight control interventions matched the groups based on study characteristics (e.g., measures) but few used participant characteristics (e.g., BMI) despite potential moderating influences on weight regulation. This is an important gap in the literature, and future research is needed to explore the extent to which individual participant characteristics in non-pharmacological adaptive weight control interventions impact dosage efficacy/effectiveness.Keywords: Adaptive behavioral intervention, healthy eating, matching control, physical activity, scoping review

Introduction

Obesity is a complex chronic disease in which an excessive accumulation of adipose tissue impairs health, increasing risks for hypertension, heart disease, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, and several cancers.1,2 In the past two decades, rates of obesity in adults in the United States (US) have risen from approximately 32% in 2003 to 42% in 2023, and in turn, adult obesity-related healthcare costs have increased in excess of $172 billion.3,4 Despite the rapid increase in the use of glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP1s) for weight regulation, there are nevertheless many individuals who may not be able to take these medications such as people with comorbid conditions and medication interactions or allergies, and some special populations such as pregnant women and older adults with gastrointestinal conditions.5,6 As such, there is still great interest in developing effective non-pharmacological behavioral interventions to reduce obesity rates and related adverse health outcomes and healthcare costs. As obesity is primarily driven by an imbalance between energy expenditure and energy intake, physical activity (PA) and healthy eating (HE) are key components of weight regulation (WR) interventions. These components also improve health and well-being, and reduce risks of many non-communicable chronic diseases.7,8 For example, Wing et al. evaluated two weight management approaches, one targeting small daily changes (e.g., reducing energy intake by approximately 100 kilocalories per day) and the other targeting one large change (e.g., reducing weight by 5–10 pounds initially) followed by weight maintenance.9 Both approaches resulted in significant weight loss over 3 years (ranging from 0.56 kg to 2.37 kg) and lower incidence of obesity at all assessment timepoints with differing rates of obesity between groups with 9% in the large change group, 8% in the small change group, and 17% in the control group. Also, the approach utilizing the one large change significantly reduced the proportion of participants gaining more than one pound at the three-year follow-up (24% compared to 33% or 41%, respectively).9 Thus, demonstrating that although there is not currently a ‘gold standard’, multiple intervention approaches can have beneficial effects on weight status.

A common design used in weight regulation interventions is the randomized controlled trial (RCT) with the traditional two comparison groups: one group of participants that receives the intervention treatment and the other control or comparison group that does not receive the treatment.10 Recent methodological advancements in trial design, however, have resulted in the rapid increase of alternative approaches that utilize unique strategies to adjust proposed treatment based on an individual’s needs with aims to improve impact and efficiency of the intervention.11 For example, the Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST) framework introduced by Collins and colleagues aims to improve efficiency and effectiveness of behavioral interventions to maximize outcomes.12 This strategy uses three phases: screening, refining, and confirming to optimize an intervention. There are a wide range of intervention designs that can be tested within the MOST framework. Just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAI) use individual data in real-time to personalize dosages.13,14 Sequential multiple assignment randomized trials (SMART) use individual participant data to inform re-randomization at specified points in the intervention, typically assessing intervention response rate.12,13 Multilevel adaptive implementation strategies (MAISY) are optimized interventions where multiple levels are targeted (i.e. system, unit, or practitioner) with multiple randomization time points.15 Intervention trials in screening and refining phases may not need a control or comparison condition whereas a control group is recommended during the confirming phase.12

Further, the capability to utilize individual participant data (e.g., social-demographic information, health status, continuous measurements of target behaviors) can provide critical information to understand how individuals are responding to the intervention in real time, which in turn, can influence future decisions about when and how to adapt dosages.13,16 This allows researchers to maximize efficiency of intervention delivery and efficacy or effectiveness of its impact on targeted health outcomes. As such, adaptive interventions that change based on an individual’s PA or HE behaviors may be particularly useful to personalize dosage based on how each participant responds to intervention components over time rather than the “one size fits all” approach. For example, the Healthy Mom Zone Study adapted the PA and HE intervention dosages based on each pregnant woman’s needs and responsiveness to the intervention components and the extent to which she regulated her weight gain within the Institute of Medicine (2009) guidelines.17,18 However, decisions regarding selection and use of control groups in many these trials are less clear. That is, when making decisions regarding control conditions in RCTs there is often a tradeoff between internal validity and power to detect an effect. Some researchers have categorized types of control conditions on a hierarchy or continuum from least stringent or rigorous to most stringent. For example, Mohr and colleagues outlined the most common control group conditions, ranked by the level of rigor/stringency with more rigorous requiring larger sample sizes and less rigorous groups requiring smaller sample sizes.19 Freedland and colleagues and Goldberg and colleagues also organized control conditions similarly.20,21 Table 1 depicts a combined hierarchy based on these three articles.19–21

Freedland and colleagues evaluated usual care control groups in behavioral intervention RCTs and the role of non-study related healthcare participants may receive on design and implementation of behavioral interventions.20 The authors reviewed previous behavioral intervention methodology texts to inform a hierarchy ranking the different types of control conditions based on their stringency, or the expected effect the control condition will have on the target outcomes.19,22–24 Freedland and colleagues also noted some limitations to this hierarchy, namely that it does not take into account differing intervention goals.20 For example, for early phase RCTs, like feasibility trials, less rigorous control groups may be more useful based on the primary aims of the trial, while later phase RCTs, like effectiveness trials, may need more rigorous control group designs to determine effectiveness.19,20

Expanding on this previous research, Goldberg and colleagues proposed a typology and decision tree to guide the selection of potential control conditions based on eleven types of mobile health (mHealth) interventions (e.g., no treatment, active placebo, mHealth comparative efficacy, etc.).21 The tree has up to four different decisions [therapeutic vs non-therapeutic, mHealth vs non-mHealth, more vs less stringent, specific group decision] used to determine ideal control condition for a mHealth intervention and groups are ranked from lowest comparison strength to highest.21 For example, if a control condition is not intended to be therapeutic, the choice is between no treatment, minimal placebo, or active placebo control groups based on comparison strength necessary.21

However, a noted gap in these studies is the lack of attention given to addressing whether the control group should be matched to the intervention group or the extent to which specific characteristics should be considered when matching these groups. For example, groups may be matched on the target sample’s inclusion criteria or social-demographic information, measurement protocols, amount of content that is delivered, and/or the intensity and frequency of the dosage. Reporting these details can provide contextual information to understanding potential moderating factors impacting the intervention as well as overall magnitude of intervention effects. The determination of characteristics to be matched may closely follow the stringency of the control condition with more stringent control conditions requiring more characteristics to be matched between groups.19–21

Given the lack of research examining adaptive interventions targeting PA/HE for weight/obesity related outcomes and the lack of clear guidelines for control groups within these interventions, there is an important need for research that examines how intervention and control groups are matched on study and participant characteristics to inform future research exploring the impact of intervention design characteristics on weight and obesity-related outcomes. Thus, the primary purpose of this scoping review was to identify and narratively describe the type of study and participant characteristics reported and the extent to which the studies used these characteristics to match control groups to intervention groups within adaptive weight regulation interventions targeting PA and/or HE among populations with overweight and obesity. We hypothesized that most studies would report matching on study characteristics such as the measures/devices used and dosage exposure of the intervention followed by participant characteristics such as age, BMI, or gender.

Methods

Data sources

A collaborative process utilizing systematic search criteria with a trained university librarian was used to develop the search strategy. This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines, utilizing both the checklist and flow diagram.25 The protocol for this review was registered through Open Science Framework (registration identifier: KSYPX) after the manuscript was submitted.26 The search was conducted in the following databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and PsychInfo and PsychArticles via Proquest. Key words included adaptive behavioral interventions, PA, HE, and weight regulation. Table 2 describes the type of search conducted in each database along with search terms. The timeframe for this search was set from 1998 to present to account for the introduction of adaptive PA, HE, and weight regulation interventions into the behavioral intervention literature.12,27–29 Specific search criteria and characteristics were compiled, and search was completed from March 2024 to December 2025. A grey literature search was conducted in PsycINFO and PsycArticles via ProQuest. Articles were uploaded to Zotero citation management software and duplicates were removed before screening.30 Review articles were excluded however, their references were searched for additional applicable articles, and an additional eight articles were included.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included in this review if they met the following criteria: (a) described the design and methodology of a non-pharmacological, adaptive behavioral intervention that had an intervention group and/or control group (protocol papers, pilot or feasibility trials, and/or fully-powered RCTs were included), (b) targeted weight regulation (includes weight loss or regulation of weight gain) via behaviors like PA and/or HE, (c) target population: adolescents with overweight or obesity or adult participants ages 18 and older with overweight or obesity including pregnant and postpartum women, (d) study was conducted in any geographical area, and (e) study was available in the English-language. Exclusion criteria were not meeting the inclusion criteria.

Data extraction and synthesis

The data extraction process included several steps developed between the researchers and the librarian. The two first coauthors reviewed titles and abstracts for applicability, followed by full texts of remaining articles. Data extraction and coding were completed by the first author with independent coding by the second author. Coding was compared and all discrepancies were discussed and, when necessary, the last author provided final oversight to resolve any remaining discrepancies until 100% were resolved for interrater reliability. Study characteristics focusing on the study design (e.g., pilot/feasibility trials, RCTs, etc.), intervention versus control group designs (e.g., interventions targeting PA or HE, control conditions with no treatment or those getting TAU), and target outcomes of the intervention (e.g., increasing PA, weight loss, improving diet) were extracted. Additional study characteristics included measures and devices used, intervention content delivery, and dosage exposure (e.g., delivery method, length of the intervention, number and frequency of assessment timepoints, etc.). Participant characteristics extracted included populations’ socio-demographic information such as age, BMI, activity level, and pregnancy status (e.g., pregnant or postpartum; gestation status e.g., singleton/multiple).

A narrative synthesis of the data was completed for study and participant characteristics and a comparison was made between characteristics matched between intervention and control groups and those that were only reported. Tables were constructed to summarize extracted data including article citations, study design, intervention details, control condition details, outcomes, and characteristics used to match control and intervention conditions. Descriptive statistics were used to examine the frequency and percentage of studies that matched the participant and study characteristics.

Results

The PRISMA flow diagram for this review is shown in Figure 1. The initial search resulted in n = 2,598 articles: Scopus: 939, Web of Science core collection: 749, PubMed: 573, PsycINFO and PsycArticles via ProQuest: 245, and CINAHL: 92. After removing duplicates, n = 1,431 articles remained for the title and abstract review; 86.16% (n = 1,233) were removed for not describing an adaptive weigh regulation intervention with either PA or HE components and 10.27% (n = 147) were removed for only including abstracts, review article, book, or not describing an adaptive weight regulation intervention. An additional n = 10 studies were added from the reference lists of review articles and those that cited included studies and n = 2 articles from a grey literature search utilizing the Dissertations and Theses database via ProQuest. Thus, a total of n = 63 articles were included in the full review.

Study characteristics

Of the included n = 63 articles, 74.60% (n = 47) were located in the US, 25.39% (n = 16) were located outside of the US. Of the n = 63 articles, 42.86% (n = 27) were protocol, rationale, or design methodology of an adaptive intervention, 39.68% (n = 25) were fully-powered adaptive interventions, and 17.46% (n = 11) were pilot, feasibility, or proof of concept trials, see Table 3. Please see the Supplemental Materials for the reference list of the reviewed studies. Intervention designs were: adaptive, tailored, or personalized interventions (49.21%; n = 31), SMARTs (25.40%; n = 16), and JITAIs (25.40%; n = 16). The majority of interventions targeted PA only (50.79%; n = 32), HE only (20.63%; n = 13), PA and HE combined (22.22%; n = 14), or weight regulation, but did not specify whether they targeted HE or PA (6.35%; n = 4). Weight was a primary outcome in n = 22 studies (34.92%) and of these, studies either targeted weight loss (63.64%; n = 14) or weight regulation (36.36%; n = 8). Of the 63 interventions, 73.02% (n = 46) indicated the use of a control condition and some studies utilized multiple conditions (i.e., an active treatment control and a no treatment control), so the following percentages will add to slightly over 100%. Most (56.52%; n = 26) used active treatment controls (typically a non-adaptive version of the intervention), other designs were usual care or treatment-as-usual (21.74%; n = 10), no treatment (19.57%; n = 9), attention control (6.52%; n = 3), minimal contact (2.17%; n = 1), or did not provide details (2.17%; n = 1).

Study design overview.

All studies with control groups (100%; n = 46) matched intervention and control conditions with the same measurement protocol for self-report measures such as validated questionnaires evaluating physical or psychological symptoms (80.43%; n = 37) or technological devices (e.g., Fitbit, 26.09%; n = 12 or accelerometer, 21.74%; n = 10). Most studies (93.48%; n = 43) matched groups with the same measures and/or devices, of which matching both measures and devices was the most common (65.22%; n = 30) followed by only matching surveys and questionnaires (23.91%; n = 11) or only matching the devices (4.35%; n = 2). Some studies (34.78%; n = 16) matched intervention content delivered between intervention and control groups, including behavior change content (e.g., messaging prompts, adaptive goal setting, or education content). Of these 16 studies, 68.75% (n = 11) provided a non-adaptive form of intervention targeting the same outcomes as the experimental intervention (e.g., non-personalized messaging prompts or non-personalized goals), and the remaining 31.25% (n = 5) provided usual care or standard content to both groups. All studies with a control condition (100%; n = 46) matched the intervention and control groups based on dosage exposure. Total length of the intervention and number/frequency of assessment time points were the primary measures of dosage exposure. Some studies also matched time spent interacting with study staff and materials (43.48%; n = 20), and mode of delivery of the intervention (41.30%; n = 19). For example, some (68.42%; n = 13/19) utilized a mobile application (e.g., an app on a phone) to virtually deliver the intervention.

Nearly all (97.82%; n = 45) studies randomized participants to intervention or comparison groups. The one study that did not randomize recruited their control participants from a larger study and intervention participants via flyers. One study reported randomizing participants but did not give any details regarding randomization strategies. Of the 45 studies randomizing participants, only 28.89% (n = 13) stratified participants using the same participant characteristics they matched between groups. The remaining n = 32 studies (71.11%) randomized participants but did not stratify or match participants based on specific participant characteristics. Most studies (66.67%; n = 30) reported compensation, of which 90.00% (n = 27) matched the amount of compensation provided and timing of delivery, 3.33% (n = 1) provided the same amount of compensation, but differed in the timing of delivery, 3.33% (n = 1) randomized compensation between groups (e.g., financial, charity donation), and 3.33% (n = 1) study provided the intervention group with higher compensation than the control group.

Participant characteristics

Of the 46 articles including a control condition, 100% included participant characteristics as inclusion/exclusion criteria, but only 67.39% (n = 31) reported sample-specific participant characteristics. Of these, 84.78% (n = 39) were conducted in adults aged 18 years or older and 10.89% (n = 5) in adolescents. Only 51.61% (n = 16/31) matched at least one participant characteristic between intervention and control groups. Participant characteristics that were matched between groups were sex/gender (56.25%; n = 9), BMI (50%; n = 8), age (31.25%; n = 5), gestational age or weeks postpartum (25.00%; n = 4), parity (12.50%; n = 2), gestation status (12.50%; n = 2), Transtheoretical Model (TTM) motivational stage of change (12.50%; n = 2), PA level (6.25%; n = 1), HbA1c (6.25%; n = 1), diabetic condition (6.25%; n = 1), race/ethnicity (6.25%; n = 1), and miscellaneous comorbidities (i.e., asthma 6.25%; n = 1).

Discussion

The primary purpose of this scoping review was to identify and narratively describe the type of study and participant characteristics reported and the extent to which studies used these characteristics to match control and intervention groups within adaptive behavioral weight regulation interventions targeting PA and/or HE components among populations with overweight and obesity. In summary, all the studies in this review matched the control and intervention groups with the same study measurement protocols (i.e., measures and devices). However, other study characteristics such as dosage exposure (e.g., length of intervention) and program content as well as participant characteristics (e.g., age, BMI, sex/gender) were used less frequently to match groups. The few studies using participant characteristics to match intervention and control groups highlights an important gap in the current literature as these characteristics have the potential to moderate the influence of PA/HE on weight regulation. Future research is needed to examine if matching characteristics in non-pharmacological adaptive designs impacts intervention efficacy/effectiveness on weight-related outcomes.

As hypothesized, all studies matched measures, devices, and the intervention dosage exposure, between intervention and control groups. Studies typically administered all primary and secondary outcome measures to both the intervention and control conditions with the same frequency. Studies that matched the groups with the devices only were interventions that did not administer any questionnaires or surveys outside of collecting data on demographic information. Studies that matched only the measures did not provide any devices to participants but rather matched the groups on the use of questionnaires and clinical measures (e.g., blood pressure or HbA1c measures). All studies matched the total length of the intervention between the intervention and comparison group(s) and often matched the assessment time points for all measures. By matching the measures and devices used and the length of the intervention, researchers can maximize the internal validity of their study by controlling for a vast number of threats to internal validity, like maturation and instrumentation bias.31 Based on the findings of this review, when matching control groups to adaptive interventions, researchers should continue to match measurement protocols including measures, devices used, and the number of assessment timepoints to ensure that the measurement protocol is not influencing the target outcomes of the study, see Table 4.

Recommendations for future interventions.

Less than half the studies matched groups based on content delivered, which focused on behavior change, educational materials, or messaging prompts. Most studies providing content to both groups provided either usual care or standard education to all participants or a non-adaptive form of the intervention as the comparison condition. This finding is consistent with those of Forman and colleagues who utilized the Weight Watchers™ application for both groups to examine whether an adaptive intervention, in addition to the app, was more effective for weight loss than the app alone.32 Researchers should consider if matching content between the intervention and control groups would support the effects of the intervention or if the content is a primary component of the intervention, matching may not be warranted. Other important characteristics which can impact intervention outcomes and compliance are the strategies for participant compensation and randomization. Regarding matching groups on participant compensation, all but one of the studies providing compensation used the same amount of compensation for the intervention and control groups. For example, Cadmus-Bertram and colleagues matched groups with the same amount of compensation for the same activities such as completing study measures and sessions.33 In addition, Kramer and colleagues used different types of compensation as part of the intervention, highlighting the potential effect of compensation on intervention outcomes.34 As such, it is also recommended that future researchers consider matching compensation between the intervention and control groups in order to limit any compensatory responses to the intervention based on expected compensation. Also, with respect to randomization, nearly all (n = 45/46) of the studies in this review randomized participants to groups. However, only a handful of studies stratified the participants within the groups based on participant characteristics (i.e., age, BMI, sex/gender). Stratifying participants to groups based on matched participant characteristics may allow researchers to better ensure that the groups are not systematically different based on important demographic variables such as age, BMI, or gestational age in pregnancy.

Although over half of the studies reported sample-specific participant characteristics, only a third used these characteristics to match the intervention and control conditions. Those that matched the target population tended to match on characteristics like sex/gender, BMI, and age, which are all factors that can greatly influence weight regulation. By matching these characteristics, researchers can be more confident that any significant differences in their target outcomes are due to the intervention and not due to systematic differences between participants. Additionally, some studies matched participants on the presence of any comorbid conditions or the level of severity of their condition. For example, the study by Vetrovsky and colleagues stratified by those with prediabetes versus diabetes to ensure both groups had an even mix of both to evaluate the extent to which the intervention impacted each condition.35 Further, there were four studies that included pregnant women (Choi et al., Downs et al., Germeroth et al., and Sridhar et al.) and one study that included postpartum women (Fjeldsoe et al.), all of which matched gestational age at study start and half of which matched participants based on pre-pregnancy BMI.17,36–39 By matching these characteristics between groups, researchers can better discern adaptive interventions efficacy and effectiveness in regulating weight and thus ideally reducing obesity rates in different populations.

The reviewed studies did not report on the methods of matching (e.g., propensity score matching, synthetic control methods, machine learning approaches).40–43 These are important gaps to highlight as these variables and methods can have influences on overall weight and weight retention, so matching one or more of these characteristics, as well as fully understanding the how and why these variables are matched could be beneficial.18,35,44 Understanding and applying additional methods for matching may be helpful to reduce biases and control for characteristics that are unable to be matched.40–43 This review highlighted that regardless of population being studied, adaptive interventions should continue to match measures/devices used and dosage exposure of the intervention as well as matching influential participant characteristics (e.g., age, presence of comorbid conditions, BMI/pre-pregnancy BMI) to improve overall efficacy and effectiveness of the intervention on outcomes.

There are several strengths of this review. First, study procedures adhered to the PRISMA-ScR guidelines to ensure the best review practices. Second, was the inclusion of multiple study designs and stages of research including publications on protocols, pilot trials, and feasibility trials in addition to fully powered interventions. Third, the inclusion of multiple populations with overweight or obesity, including pregnant and postpartum women, allows for further generalizability of the findings across multiple populations. Finally, this is one of the first studies to examine matching characteristics in adaptive interventions and it may provide initial evidence about the importance of designing control groups within future adaptive behavioral interventions. However, there were some limitations including the possibility of overlooking relevant studies or mis-identifying applicable studies even though we followed PRISMA-ScR guidelines. In addition, this review only included sources written in English, which may exclude some potentially applicable studies. Lastly, it was beyond the scope of this review to conduct a statistical review of effect size differences in intervention outcomes.

Conclusions

This scoping review highlighted that, regardless of population being studied, measurement protocols and dosage exposure of the intervention were matched, but they did not often match participant characteristics or content delivered. This gap in matching participant characteristics is particularly important given that individual factors may moderate the impact of PA/HE on weight and obesity-related outcomes in both the general population and prenatal/postnatal participants. Therefore, by matching these characteristics between groups in adaptive interventions researchers can be more confident that the effects on weight and obesity related outcomes are true intervention effects and not due to systematic differences between groups.

STATEMENT OF PUBLIC HEALTH SIGNIFICANCE

Given the impact of individual variability in weight regulation, this scoping review identified the importance of matching non-pharmacological, adaptive behavioral intervention and control groups on both study characteristics (e.g., measurement protocols) and participant characteristics (e.g., BMI) to optimize intervention impact on weight regulation outcomes.
