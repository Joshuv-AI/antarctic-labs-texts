Triglyceride-Glucose Index and Mortality Risk in the General Population: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Prospective Studies
Authors: D’Elia Lanfranco, Saverio Stranges, Ersilia La Fata, Francesca Fortunato, Raffaele Palladino, Domenico Rendina, Ferruccio Galletti

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Among the modifiable cardio-metabolic risk factors contributing to CVD, insulin resistance (IR) is a major determinant [2, 3]. Robust experimental evidence has demonstrated the deleterious effects of IR on CVD risk, through mechanisms including sympathetic nervous system activation [4], dysregulation of the nitric oxide pathway [5], and impaired renal sodium handling [3, 6]. Consequently, the early identification of IR is pivotal for detecting individuals at elevated CVD risk.

A wide range of markers has been evaluated for IR detection; however, most require insulin measurement in biological samples, which limits their applicability in both clinical practice and large-scale observational or experimental studies [7]. The triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index has emerged as a promising alternative biomarker, as it is simple, inexpensive, and does not rely on insulin assays [8, 9]. Notably, the TyG index reflects both hepatic and peripheral IR [8].

Although it represents a relatively novel biomarker, the TyG index has drawing increasing attention in the scientific literature. A previous meta-analysis including only three studies reported inconsistent associations with CVD and all-cause mortality in the general population [10], but its interpretation was hampered by important limitations, including a male-only cohort, non-comparable TyG scaling, and possible collinearity among covariates [11]. More recent prospective observational studies generally indicate a positive association between TyG and CVD risk, though heterogeneity remains, partly due to differences in study populations and analytic methods [8, 12–23].

A recent meta-analysis reported that the TyG index was associated with CVD events and mortality, with results stratified by diabetes status [24]. However, that study did not evaluate the predictive value of TyG in general populations and lacked key methodological components, such as dose-response evaluation, exploration of the association shape, identification of potential thresholds, and additional analyses to account for heterogeneity.

Given the heterogeneity of available evidence on the predictive role of TyG, the limitations of the previous meta-analyses [10, 24], and the lack of a universally accepted cut-point for risk stratification, together with the established role of IR in CVD risk and the critical importance of early detection, we undertook a meta-analysis to address these gaps. Specifically, we aimed to evaluate the predictive value of the TyG index for all-cause and CVD mortality in general populations, to delineate the shape and magnitude of the dose-response relationship, and to identify the most appropriate TyG threshold for mortality risk prediction.

Methods

The present systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) were carried out according to the statement Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [25] (Supplemental Table 1) and Meta-analyses Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) [26] (Supplemental Table 2). The study protocol was registered (CRD42024591479).

A systematic search of the available publications was performed using the Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus, and MEDLINE/PubMed up to 15 August 2024, which was later updated to March 19, 2025. The search strategy, without restrictions, included the expressions “triglyceride glucose index" OR "triglyceride-glucose index” OR "TyG" AND "mortality" OR "death", or combinations thereof, either in medical subject headings or in the title/abstract (Supplemental Table 3). Further information was retrieved by manually searching references from recent reviews and relevant published original studies.

The data selection and extraction were independently conducted and reported following the PRISMA guidelines [25] by two researchers (ELF and FF). The titles and abstracts of the studies retrieved in the searches were screened to identify the studies that met the predefined inclusion criteria. The full texts of the potentially eligible studies were then retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Discrepancies regarding the inclusion of studies and the interpretation of data were resolved in conference with a third researcher (LD).

To be included in the meta-analysis a published study had to meet the following inclusion criteria (PICO framework, Supplemental Table 4): (a) peer-reviewed original articles, (b) studies involving the adult population, (c) studies involving the assessment of TyG as the baseline exposure, (d) studies in which the predictive role of TyG on all-cause or CVD mortality was prospectively determined, (e) studies in which there is an indication of the number of participants exposed and the rate or number of deaths in the different categories of TyG, (f) studies in which there is an assessment of risk estimation (hazard ratio—HR, relative risk—RR, odds ratio—OR) for specified TyG categories, (g) studies with a follow-up of at least 2 years (mean or median) to minimize the risk of reverse causation.

The risk of bias in each study included in the SRMA was evaluated according to established criteria by the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies—of Exposures (ROBINS-E) tool [27] (Supplemental Table 5).

The quality of the body of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology [28].

Statistical analysis

Adjusted HRs were extracted from the selected publications, and standard errors (SEs) were derived from the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Both linear and non-linear associations between TyG and mortality risk were evaluated, using estimates adjusted for the number of potential confounders reported. To assess potential non-linearity, TyG was modeled with restricted cubic splines incorporating four knots at fixed percentiles (5, 35, 65, and 95%) of the TyG distribution.

Departure from linearity was tested by examining the null hypothesis that the coefficient of the second spline was equal to zero. A one-stage, dose–response, random-effects meta-analysis was then performed [29], accounting for the correlation between estimates across TyG categories. For each category, the corresponding median value was assigned; when median or mean values were not reported, the midpoint of the lower and upper boundaries was used. If the lowest category was open-ended, its lower boundary was set to zero; if the highest category lacked an upper boundary, its amplitude was assumed equal to that of the preceding category. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Q-test (p < 0.20, indicating heterogeneity).

Next, comparisons of mortality risk between the highest and lowest TyG categories were conducted.

Pooled HRs with 95% CIs were calculated using a random-effects model (restricted maximum–likelihood). Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding one cohort at a time to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the overall estimates. Heterogeneity was further quantified with the Cochrane Q statistic and the I2 metric. Publication bias was explored visually with funnel plots and formally with Egger’s and Begg’s tests; if significant asymmetry was detected, pooled estimates were recalculated using the “trim-and-fill” method.

Subgroup analyses and meta-regression were conducted to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, including study- or patient-level characteristics.

TyG values were reported according to both scales commonly used in the literature, standardized through a conversion formula [30]. In two cohorts [21], where HRs were originally presented using the second quartile as the reference category, HRs were recalculated using the first quartile as the reference.

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA software, version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

From a total of 1,164 publications retrieved, 30 prospective studies met the predefined inclusion criteria (Fig. 1) [12-23, Supplemental Table 6]. Fourteen of these studies referred to the same cohort—National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) [19, Supplemental Table 6], and two to another cohort—National Health Insurance Database (NHID) [17, Supplemental Table 6]. To avoid duplication, we retained only the study within each dataset that provided the largest number of participants and events and the longest follow-up duration [17, 19]. Three additional studies [Supplemental Table 6] were excluded because they lacked sufficient data to perform dose–response analyses, while one study [11] was excluded due to the use of a TyG scale that was not comparable with those of the other studies. Eventually, 12 studies providing data on 14 independent cohorts were included in the meta-analysis.

The main characteristics of the included studies are summarised in Table 1. Five cohorts originated from East Asia (China and Korea), three from Europe (England, Italy, and Sweden), two from the United States and the Middle East (Iran), and one was an international multicentre study. All investigations enrolled both men and women. All but one study [20] reported data on all-cause mortality, and all but two [13, 16] provided CVD mortality outcomes. Across the included cohorts, the number of participants ranged from 4259 to 6,667,138. The mean baseline TyG index varied between 7.87 and 9.13 units, with a median follow-up ranging from 4.6 to 25 years and a mean participant age between 24.7 and 57.9 years. The prevalence of diabetes ranged from 1.1 to 16.6%, and hypertension prevalence from 7.2 to 70.0%. Six studies excluded individuals with previous CVD, two included a small proportion of such participants (3.1% and 6.7%), and five did not report this information. Eight studies explicitly evaluated whether the association between TyG and mortality followed a linear or non-linear pattern.

TyG and All-cause Mortality

A total of 11 studies (13 cohorts) contributed to the analysis of all-cause mortality, comprising 10,738,680 participants and 184,696 deaths [12–19, 21–23] (Table 1). The test for departure from linearity did not indicate a significant deviation (p for non-linearity = 0.82), supporting a linear dose–response association between baseline TyG and all-cause mortality. The dose–response model showed that the risk became evident for TyG values above approximately 6.9 units (or 3.79 in the alternative scale). Each one-unit increase in TyG was associated with a 14% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.04–1.24, p = 0.003; heterogeneity: p < 0.001; R2 = 0.31) (Fig. 2A).

When comparing the highest versus the lowest TyG quantile, a significant positive association was confirmed (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.10–1.36, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 = 98%, p< 0.001). Visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested a slight asymmetry (Supplemental Fig. 1); however, Egger’s (p = 0.07) and Begg’s (p = 0.95) tests indicated no significant publication bias. The “trim-and-fill” method did not identify any potentially missing studies, confirming the robustness of the findings. A direct association between TyG and all-cause mortality was observed in most cohorts (n = 11), reaching statistical significance in nine, while two studies showed a non-significant inverse trend (Fig. 2B). Sequential exclusion of individual cohorts yielded HRs ranging from 1.19 to 1.26, indicating no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimate (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Additional analyses (Supplemental Tables 7 and 8). Meta-regression identified mean age, average TyG, and the lowest TyG category as significant contributors to heterogeneity (p < 0.05). Subgroup analyses confirmed that both mean TyG and the lowest TyG values significantly modified the association (p for interaction = 0.04), whereas the difference by age (<47.3 vs. ≥47.3 years) did not reach statistical significance (p for interaction = 0.11), as well as the multivariate meta-regression result did not confirm the trend (p > 0.05). Stratification by country of origin revealed that in non-Asian populations, TyG was linearly (p for non-linearity = 0.16) and positively associated with all-cause mortality (HR per 1-unit increase = 1.16, 95% CI 1.02–1.32, p = 0.03; heterogeneity: p = 0.01; R2 = 0.73). In contrast, the association in Asian cohorts was weaker and not statistically significant (HR 1.17, 95% CI 0.99–1.37, p = 0.06; p for non-linearity = 0.43). However, when comparing the highest and lowest TyG categories, the magnitude of risk was comparable between Asian and non-Asian cohorts (HR 1.21 vs. 1.27, p for interaction = 0.72). Moreover, the results stratified according to the risk-of-bias assessment indicated a difference between low-risk cohorts and the others (HR: 1.30 vs. 1.13), but this difference was not statistically significant (p for interaction = 0.15). None of the other study or participant characteristics contributed significantly to heterogeneity, including sex, duration of follow-up, sample size, body mass index, prevalence of hypertension and diabetes, or overall risk of bias (p > 0.05).

Quality of body of evidence. Based on the GRADE methodology, the certainty of evidence for the TyG and all-cause mortality association was rated as low, reflecting the observational design of the included studies (starting level: low), a one-level downgrade for statistical inconsistency due to substantial heterogeneity (with consistent direction of association and stable sensitivity analyses), and a one-level upgrade for a clear linear dose–response gradient (Table 2).

TyG and Cardiovascular Mortality

Ten studies (12 cohorts) provided data for CVD mortality, encompassing 10,810,680 participants and 58,919 deaths [12, 14, 15, 17–23]. The relationship between baseline TyG and CVD mortality was also linear (p for non-linearity = 0.77). The dose–response analysis indicated that risk increased for TyG values above approximately 6.9 units (or 3.79 in the alternative scale). Each one-unit increase in TyG was associated with a 16% higher risk of CVD mortality (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.04–1.28, p = 0.006; heterogeneity: p < 0.001; R2 = 0.59) (Fig. 3A).

When comparing the highest and lowest TyG categories, CVD mortality risk remained significantly elevated (HR: 1.28, 95%CI: 1.12–1.45, p< 0.001) (Fig. 3B), but with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 96%, p < 0.001). Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Supplemental Fig. 3) showed an approximately symmetrical distribution of studies around the pooled effect estimate, and both Egger’s (p = 0.55) and Begg’s (p = 0.63) tests excluded significant publication bias. The “trim-and-fill” adjustment did not suggest missing studies. Across individual cohorts, a direct association between TyG and CVD mortality was observed in ten, reaching significance in seven, while only one study showed a non-significant inverse association and another reported no clear relationship (Fig. 3B). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings, with pooled HRs ranging between 1.22 and 1.32 when individual studies were sequentially omitted (Supplemental Fig. 4).

Additional analyses (Supplemental Tables 9 and 10). Among the study-level characteristics examined, meta-regression identified age as a significant determinant of between-study heterogeneity (coefficient: −0.018, 95% CI −0.033 to −0.004, p = 0.01). Despite the difference was not significant, the stratified analysis by the median value confirmed a stronger association in studies with a younger mean age (<51 years: HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.17–1.68) than in those with older participants (≥51 years: HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.02–1.28; p for interaction = 0.06).

Regional stratification, excluding the international PURE cohort [20], again showed that in non-Asian populations the association was linear (p for non-linearity = 0.38) and significant (each one-unit increase in TyG, HR: 1.08, 95%CI: 1.02–1.14, p = 0.004), whereas no significant association was detected among Asian cohorts (HR: 1.24, 95%CI: 0.94–1.63, p = 0.12; p for non-linearity = 0.64). A similar pattern was observed when comparing extreme TyG categories (non-Asian HR: 1.25, 95%CI: 1.09–1.42; Asian HR: 1.34, 95%CI: 0.95–1.73; p for interaction = 0.68). Furthermore, stratification by risk-of-bias level revealed a difference between low-risk and other cohorts (HR: 1.36 vs 1.19), although the difference did not achieve statistical significance (p for interaction = 0.25).

No significant heterogeneity was associated with other study or participant characteristics, including sex, duration of follow-up, cohort size, body mass index, prevalence of hypertension or diabetes, and overall risk of bias (p > 0.05).

Quality of body of evidence. According to the GRADE criteria, the certainty of evidence for the TyG and CVD mortality association was rated as low, reflecting the observational design of the included studies (starting level: low) and a one-level downgrade for statistical inconsistency due to substantial heterogeneity (with consistent direction of association and stable sensitivity analyses), together with a one-level upgrade for a clear linear dose–response relationship (Table 2).

Discussion

This meta-analysis provides compelling evidence that the TyG index is significantly and linearly associated with both all-cause and CVD mortality in the general population. The dose–response analysis demonstrated a clear linear relationship, whereby each one-unit increase in TyG corresponded to approximately a 14–16% higher risk of death starting from 6.9 units (or 3.79 in the alternative scale). This association was further confirmed in categorical comparisons, with individuals in the highest TyG categories showing a consistently greater mortality risk than those in the lowest categories. These findings underscore the clinical relevance of TyG as a simple, reproducible, and continous metric and an independent predictor of adverse outcomes.

In subgroup analysis, regional differences were evident. In the dose–response analyses, the association between TyG and both all-cause and CVD mortality reached statistical significance only in non-Asian populations, whereas in Asian cohorts the relationship did not remain significant. In contrast, in the high-versus-low analysis, TyG was associated with all-cause mortality in both Asian and non-Asian groups, while the excess CVD mortality was driven predominantly by non-Asian cohorts. This pattern suggests that ethnic and metabolic background may modulate the strength of the association.

Moreover, the shape of association varied systematically with geography and baseline metabolic profile. While several individual cohorts showed non-linear or threshold-type relationships, other studies exhibited linear trends across the TyG continuum (see Table 1). Notably, these study-level variations contrast with the overall linear dose–response relationship observed in the meta-analysis, suggesting that population-specific non-linearities tend to converge toward a linear pattern when data are aggregated. This implies that, despite local threshold effects, the underlying biological relationship between TyG and mortality may be fundamentally linear on a global scale.

Overall, these findings consolidate and expand current knowledge, integrating evidence from large, ethnically diverse cohorts and emphasising that both the absolute TyG distribution and the functional form of the association shape mortality risk, thereby explaining much of the heterogeneity observed across studies while reinforcing the robustness of the pooled linear trend.

A distinct sex-related pattern also emerged in the association between the TyG index and mortality outcomes. Higher TyG values were more strongly and linearly associated with all-cause and CVD mortality in women than in men, as shown in the Korean young adult cohort [17]. These findings suggest that elevated TyG levels in young women may represent a more adverse metabolic phenotype, potentially driven by lower muscle mass, chronic low-grade inflammation, or hormonal influences on lipid and glucose metabolism [31, 32]. By contrast, in men, higher TyG levels may partly reflect greater adiposity or nutritional reserve, attenuating their prognostic impact. Nevertheless, although these pathophysiological mechanisms are consistent with the evidence reported in the Korean population, the meta-regression analysis did not identify sex as a significant modifier of the TyG–mortality association, indicating that the observed sex-related differences do not independently account for between-study heterogeneity.

Beyond sex differences, the included studies were heterogeneous in both characteristics and findings.

Marked heterogeneity emerged across cohorts in both baseline TyG levels and the shape of the association with mortality. Mean TyG values ranged from 7.87 in the CARDIA study [13] to 9.13 in the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study [22], reflecting wide metabolic diversity across populations.

Meta-regression analyses identified baseline TyG levels, particularly the mean and lowest quantile values, as key contributors to inter-study variability. An inverse trend was observed: cohorts with higher baseline or lowest TyG values tended to show weaker associations with mortality, whereas populations with lower TyG exposure exhibited steeper risk gradients. This suggests that in metabolically healthier populations, even small increases in TyG may confer proportionally greater mortality risk.

Age distribution also contributed to heterogeneity: younger cohorts (<47 years for all-cause and <51 years for CVD mortality) showed steeper risk gradients, consistent with the hypothesis that early metabolic impairment confers greater long-term vulnerability. Despite these variations, the direction of the association remained consistent across all analyses, reinforcing the biological plausibility of TyG as a universal indicator of IR-related mortality risk, while emphasising the importance of demographic and metabolic profiles in predictive modelling.

In addition to demographic differences such as age and sex, heterogeneity in study quality and design may also have influenced the findings. The stratified analysis by risk-of-bias level indicated a higher hazard ratio among low-risk cohorts compared with those with potentially higher risk, although the differences were not statistically significant. Such variation should be interpreted in light of the methodological differences across the included studies. For instance, one study, deemed to be at high risk of bias, employed verbal autopsy to ascertain outcomes and included only participants aged over 35 years [15], thereby introducing a potential for misclassification bias. Another study may have been subject to selection bias owing to the exclusion of individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus [20], which consequently undermines its external validity. Moreover, several studies did not assess whether the association between the TyG index and mortality outcomes followed a linear or non-linear pattern [12, 18, 20, 22], which may have obscured relevant dose–response relationships. These methodological inconsistencies likely contributed to the observed heterogeneity and underscore the need for cautious interpretation of the pooled findings.

Although the observed associations were robust and directionally consistent across analyses, the overall certainty of evidence was rated as low for both all-cause and CVD mortality. Within the GRADE framework, the evidence started at low (observational design), was downgraded by one level for statistical inconsistency due to substantial heterogeneity, and upgraded by one level for a clear linear dose–response gradient. Accordingly, heterogeneity tempered certainty despite consistent findings across sensitivity and subgroup analyses. The low rating mainly reflects the observational nature of the evidence base and between-study variability. Nonetheless, effect estimates were broadly comparable in magnitude, the dose–response pattern was clearly linear, and there was no evidence of publication bias. Taken together, these elements support confidence in the observed associations, while acknowledging that causal inference remains limited by the underlying study designs.

The observed associations are biologically plausible, given the established role of IR in CVD pathophysiology. The TyG index, which integrates fasting triglyceride and glucose concentrations, reflects both hepatic and peripheral IR [8]. Elevated TyG values indicate impaired glucose uptake, enhanced hepatic gluconeogenesis, and increased production of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, all of which contribute to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and low-grade systemic inflammation [3]. IR further disrupts nitric oxide signalling, augments sympathetic nervous system activity and renal sodium reabsorption, and promotes a pro-atherogenic lipid profile through the accumulation of diacylglycerols and free fatty acids [3]. Collectively, these mechanisms drive arterial stiffening and vascular remodelling [3], ultimately leading to increased CVD mortality [2].

Consistent with recent evidence, the TyG index has also been associated with hypertension [33, 34], diabetes [35, 36], metabolic syndrome [37, 38], and subclinical atherosclerosis [39, 40], thereby reinforcing its role as an integrative biomarker of metabolic and vascular injury. The presence of major cardiometabolic risk factors may plausibly influence clinical outcomes, particularly conditions such as diabetes [41] and hypertension [42], which are closely linked to metabolic disturbances. Nonetheless, the meta-regression analysis did not reveal any significant impact of these risk factors on the predictive performance of the TyG index, suggesting that its prognostic value may be relatively independent of such underlying conditions.

Of note, the findings of our SRMA are consistent with those of a recent meta-analysis that evaluated the prognostic role of the TyG index in relation to CVD events and mortality and reported results separately for individuals with and without diabetes [24]. Nevertheless, that meta-analysis was based on a smaller population and lacked several key analytical components, including an assessment of the dose–response relationship, exploration of the shape of the association, identification of potential mortality thresholds, and meta-regression analyses to account for sources of heterogeneity. In contrast, our SRMA addressed these methodological limitations, thereby providing a more comprehensive and nuanced assessment of the prognostic value of the TyG index. Furthermore, the absence of a dose–response analysis in the previous meta-analysis negatively influenced the certainty of evidence as judged by the GRADE framework.

In terms of clinical implications, the TyG index emerges as an accessible, low-cost biomarker for the early identification of individuals at elevated cardio-metabolic risk. It can be readily derived from routine fasting lipid and glucose measurements, making it particularly suitable for primary care and population-level screening, including in resource-limited settings. However, several challenges limit its immediate implementation. The absence of standardised cut-off values, the coexistence of two calculation methods, and variability in baseline metabolic profiles across populations hamper the consistent interpretation and comparability of results across studies. Moreover, pharmacological and lifestyle factors that modify triglyceride or glucose concentrations may modulate its predictive value.

Future research should aim to standardise TyG calculation methods and establish population-specific reference ranges and clinical thresholds. Additional longitudinal studies and randomized trials are needed to evaluate whether reductions in TyG through lifestyle or pharmacological interventions translate into improved survival. Given its biological rationale, reproducibility, and cost-effectiveness, the TyG index could serve as both a surrogate endpoint in prevention trials and a complementary parameter within established CVD risk prediction models.

Strengths and limitations

The present SRMA has several strengths. First, the use of stringent inclusion criteria ensured the selection of high-quality prospective studies, thereby reducing the risk of bias and enhancing internal validity. Second, the analysis encompassed an exceptionally large sample, over ten million participants drawn from multiple continents, providing unmatched statistical power and improving the generalizability of the findings across diverse populations. Third, the study simultaneously evaluated both the shape and strength of the dose–response relationship and the contrast between the highest and lowest TyG categories, offering a more comprehensive assessment of the risk gradient. Fourth, it allowed the identification of potential TyG thresholds for all-cause and CVD mortality, refining its clinical interpretability. Fifth, the overall quality of evidence was systematically graded using the GRADE framework, ensuring transparency and methodological robustness. Finally, an extensive exploration of heterogeneity sources, including demographic, metabolic, and methodological factors, provided valuable insight into context-specific differences. Taken together, these features make this work the most comprehensive synthesis to date of prospective evidence on the relationship between TyG and mortality. The inclusion of diverse populations enhances external validity, and the dose–response modelling approach enables precise risk estimation across the TyG continuum. Moreover, sensitivity analyses and publication bias assessments confirmed the stability and reliability of the results, further strengthening the robustness of the conclusions.

Although this meta-analysis provides robust and internally consistent evidence, some limitations should be acknowledged. The substantial heterogeneity among the included studies represents a major consideration, reflecting differences in study design, population characteristics, baseline TyG distributions, and outcome definitions. While extensive subgroup and meta-regression analyses were conducted to identify potential sources of variability, unmeasured factors may still contribute to residual heterogeneity. Furthermore, the observational design of all included studies precludes causal inference, and residual confounding by unmeasured lifestyle, dietary, or genetic variables cannot be excluded. Most cohorts relied on a single baseline TyG measurement, limiting the ability to capture longitudinal variability or dynamic changes in IR over time. Data also remain scarce for African and Latin-American populations, restricting the global representativeness of the findings. Finally, modest inter-laboratory differences in triglyceride and glucose assays may have influenced comparability and contributed to residual measurement bias.

Conclusions

This systematic review and dose–response meta-analysis of prospective cohorts from general populations demonstrates that the TyG index is independently and linearly associated with both all-cause and CVD mortality. The analysis revealed that each 1-unit increase in TyG corresponds to a 14% higher risk of all-cause mortality and a 16% higher risk of CVD mortality, with these risks becoming evident from approximately TyG 6.9 (or 3.79 on the alternative scale). These findings underscore the potential of TyG as a continuous biomarker for early detection of elevated CVD mortality risk in population-based settings, offering a simple, cost-effective tool for broad implementation in clinical practice.

Future research should focus on standardizing TyG calculation methods, establishing population-specific reference ranges, and identifying clinical thresholds. Additionally, randomized trials and longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate whether lifestyle or pharmacological interventions that lower TyG levels can effectively reduce CVD risk and improve long-term health outcomes.
