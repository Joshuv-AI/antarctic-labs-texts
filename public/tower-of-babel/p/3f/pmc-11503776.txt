Occurrence, Fate, and Reduction Measures of Phthalates in the Cooking Process: A Review
Authors: Jiaying Wang, Xueyu Weng, Shuang Liu, He Zhang, Qingqing Zhu, Qiuguo Fu, Thanh Wang, Chunyang Liao, Guibin Jiang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhthalate esters (PAEs), which are commonly used as plasticizers in food processing and packaging, are common pollutants. Dietary intake is the primary route to human exposure to PAEs. Data show that retail packaged and cooked foods are most likely to be contaminated with PAEs, indicating that the food preparation and cooking processes may contribute to the migration and transformation of PAEs in food. In this review, we collected the latest investigations on the occurrence of PAEs in different food categories, discussed the mechanisms of the fate of PAEs during cooking, and summarized the reduction measures of PAEs in food. Foods such as water and beverages, meat, condiments, and baby foods are linked to PAE exposure. Many cooking techniques and procedures, including steaming, boiling, microwave heating, toasting, frying, and packaging, are responsible for the occurrence, migration, and transformation of PAEs in foods. The occurrence, transformation, and migration of PAEs in food are influenced by a variety of factors, including food categories, the cooking process, packaging materials, and physicochemical properties of PAEs. There are several ways to prevent or reduce PAEs in food, including the selection of safe packaging materials, proper food categories, cooking methods, and replacement of PAEs.Keywords: Food, Cooking process, Phthalates, Transformation, Migration, Reduction measures

Introduction

Phthalates, also referred to as phthalic acid esters (PAEs), are phthalic acid derivatives that are esters of 1,2-benzenedicarboxylic acid with the acid groups at the ortho-position.1,2 PAEs are synthetic organic chemicals that emerged in the 1920s3 and are synthesized by the reaction of benzo dicarboxylic anhydride and alcohols,4 which change from small molecules, such as methanol and ethanol, to dodecanal containing straight or branched chains and are synthesized as a large number of different types of PAEs.5 Based on their side chain structures, PAEs can be divided into low- and high-molecular-weight groups.6 Although high-molecular-weight PAEs, such as bis(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), have more than six carbon side chains and are primarily used as plasticizers, low-molecular-weight PAEs, such as diethyl phthalate (DEP), di-n-butyl ortho-phthalate (DBP or DnBP), and benzyl butyl phthalate (BBP), have less than six carbon side chains and are primarily used in cosmetics and personal care products (Table S1).2,7−11 PAEs are plasticizers that are frequently used as modifying agents in the manufacturing of thermoplastics.12,13 In particular, PAEs are used in infant toys, personal care products, and cosmetics.14−18 As typical endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), PAEs may enter the human body through ingestion, inhalation, and skin contact resulting from the widespread use of these products, causing considerable harm to human health.19−27 The ingestion and inhalation of PAEs are mainly through drinking water, food, and outdoor air,28 of which the proportion of air inhalation is only 1%,29 whereas ingestion of contaminated water and food accounts for approximately 90%.30 Therefore, dietary intake is the primary source of human exposure.3,20,31

Packaged, cooked, and ready-to-eat foods are most likely to be contaminated with PAEs, and the content of PAEs is higher than that in raw foods, suggesting that food preparation and cooking processes, such as packaging and manufacturing, steaming and boiling, microwave heating, frying, and roasting, may contribute to the migration and transformation of PAEs in food.32,33 In general, the concentrations of PAEs can change during cooking processes, mainly because of migration, including the evaporation of volatile compounds with high vapor pressures, such as dimethyl ortho-phthalate (DMP) and DEP.34,35 In addition, PAEs can be degraded to phthalic acid, an intermediate of phthalic acid monoesters, during cooking. In addition, studies have indicated that phthalate plasticizers contained in plastic food packaging materials can migrate and leach into food, posing risks to human health. However, there have been no relevant reviews on the fate of PAEs during cooking. Summarizing the migration and transformation of PAEs during food preparation and cooking processes is crucial for understanding the health risks of phthalates, given the exposure and potential toxicity of foodborne PAEs to humans.

With “phthalate”, “cooking or thermal process or heating”, and “food or foodstuff” as keywords searched on the Web of Science, 352 pieces of literature was identified. Literature on the occurrence of PAEs in different food group categories, the transformation and migration of PAEs in different cooking or thermal processes, the determining factors of PAEs’ fate during the cooking process, and measures for reducing human exposure to PAEs from food was selected as references. Based on the aforementioned approach, this study focuses on the occurrence of PAEs in various food groups, the mechanisms for the migration and transformation of PAEs, the related influencing factors, and the measures necessary to limit the presence of PAEs in food.

Reported PAE Concentrations in Different Food Groups

PAEs have also been detected in water and other beverages. The average concentration of DEHP in Canadian juices was 183 μg/kg, which was relatively high.36 This trend could be explained considering that PAEs are used to adjust the color, taste, and texture of beverages37 and many businesses illegally use PAEs as a substitute for emulsifier cloud-specific foods38 to improve turbidity and appearance.39 PAEs were also detected in beef, poultry, pork, and aquatic products, among which levels of PAEs in aquatic products were higher than in other foods, especially shrimp and fish, with 0.06 and 0.26 mg/kg, respectively.40 The mean values of DEHP in beef, poultry, and pork were greater than 300 μg/kg, where the DEHP levels in beef varied considerably from region to region, with U.S. samples reaching only the detection limit, whereas Canadian samples exhibited levels of up to 1100 μg/kg.41 In addition, DEHP was found in three sea salt samples because sea salts can be contaminated with PAE-containing microplastics.39

Researchers have detected widely used PAEs in children’s products such as infant formulas and nutritional supplements; thus, children face a greater risk of PAE exposure.57 PAEs detected in infant formula could range from 80 to 3000 μg/kg.41 A study detected no DEHP in the extracts of milk bottle cap gaskets and other infant formula composite containers at the detection limit of 0.05 μg/mL, suggesting that the PAEs found in infant formula are not packaging-related.56 In addition, nutritional supplements such as probiotics and vitamin supplements are a major source of PAE contamination and are often used as excipients to stabilize pharmaceuticals.59 A study showed that the levels of DEHP were higher in two popular probiotics among all foods tested,60 which may be due to flavoring powders added to dietary supplements during the manufacturing process.39 Taiwan’s Food and Drug Administration prohibited the addition of DEHP to probiotics and vitamin supplements.57

PAEs in food can be derived from environmental sources and/or via food handling processes and packaging, such as waterborne or airborne chemical industrial waste, through inattentiveness in transporting raw products, and from unsuitable packaging materials.57 PAEs are accumulated in edible parts of fruits and vegetables as well as animal products from the natural environment (e.g., from water, soil, and sludge), especially water, as ubiquitous occurrences of such chemicals in the environment.61 For instance, PAEs were detected at very high levels in freshwater fish in which no packaging materials were used, indicating the contamination of lakes and rivers by PAEs owing to the widespread use of consumer products containing PAEs.61 The migration of PAEs from packaging materials to food could be another source. Studies have detected the migration of PAEs from cans to vegetables and fruits.40 In addition, various food-handling processes can also lead to an increase in PAEs in foods owing to the use of plastics in these processes.61 Considerably higher PAE levels were found in foods in contact with materials such as plasticized polyvinyl chloride (PVC) used in various studies, for example, tubes, conveyor belts, or disposable gloves. Thus, food-contact materials may represent an important source of contamination, especially for foods with a higher fat content.61,62 In addition, there is considerable variability in the degree of PAE contamination of foods, depending on packaging and processing practices and lipid content.63

Fate of PAEs in Different Cooking Processes

PAEs in food and packaging materials can transform and migrate during cooking, contributing to changes in their composition and content (Figure 1). In general, the concentrations of PAEs can change during cooking processes, mainly because of migration, including the evaporation of volatile compounds with high vapor pressures, such as DMP and DEP.34,35 In addition, PAEs can be degraded to phthalic acid, an intermediate of phthalic acid monoesters, during cooking. In addition, several studies have indicated that PAEs residing in plastic food packaging materials can migrate and leach into food, thereby causing contamination.

Fate of PAEs during the cooking process.

Steaming and Boiling

Studies have shown that steaming and boiling can decrease the PAE content in food in most cases, probably because of the increased water content and decreased fat content. For example, the concentration of PAEs in starchy foods decreases after boiling, especially BBP and DEHP, mainly due to the absorption of water causing the increase in weight64; and the reduction of DEHP in eggs, chicken liver, and chicken breast after steaming was detected up to 11.6–33.1%.65 However, boiling radishes can lead to a large migration of DEHP into food, probably because of the large amount of oil broth used in the process of cooking.66 Notably, PAEs have chemical structural stability and heat resistance; therefore, the loss of PAEs with high vapor pressures and small molecular weights is not due to degradation but due to evaporation and is associated with a long processing time.45

Microwave Heating

Microwaves are among the most common cooking and reheating methods because they are easy to use and require only a specific container that can withstand the heating cycle. In most cases, these containers are composed of glass, ceramic, plastic (polypropylene), or polycarbonate. In theory, the containers used for microwave heating are inert, and studies have shown that migration of various chemicals may occur during microwave heating.67 However, the effect of microwave heating on the migration of PAEs is not significant in commonly prepared household foods. The mean, minimum, and maximum concentrations of most compounds were remarkably similar before and after heating, suggesting that microwave heating has a minimal effect on the migration of plasticizers and additives from containers to foods and that the cooking process or the initial exposure of the food in plastic containers may be responsible for the detected contamination levels. This could be explained by the fact that microwave heating occurs at the core of the food, such that temperatures favorable for contaminant migration from the plastic container do not reach the food contact surface.48 Notably, plastic bags and poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC) films are often used to cover food during microwave heating, and the PAEs in plastic bags and films can be easily released into food in high-temperature environments. The results of covering food simulant with PVC film followed by microwave heating showed that, after 3 min, the level of DEHP in food increased significantly, with peak concentrations in the range of 0.267–4.264 μg/g. The tolerable daily intake (TDI) of DEHP ingested through food increased from 5.78% unheated to 63.16–92.20%, followed by DBP, indicating that PVC film covering during microwave heating is not safe.68

When leaving the containers and films aside, the level of PAEs in the food increased during microwave heating. This may be because microwave radiation and heating change the structure of the packaging material, further enhancing the interaction between the packaging material and the food ingredients, intensifying the molecular motion, and facilitating the migration into foods.69,70 However, the concentration of certain volatile PAEs also decreases because of the increase in temperature.71 In addition, the volatilization of fats and oils during the heating process may leach PAEs from the packaging material and into food.72

Frying

In most cases, frying can reduce the PAE content in food. Studies have shown that frying potatoes in vegetable oil reduces most PAE concentrations compared to those of raw potatoes. The reduction in DEHP content in eggs, chicken liver, and chicken breasts after deep-frying can range from 10.6 to 43.5%.65 Notably, margarine plays a significant role in the frying process. When onions, peppers, and margarine were fried together, the DEHP content of the peppers decreased from 71.77 to 22.06 lg/kg. When margarine is added during frying, the levels of DiBP and DEHP in meat and fish were lower than those in the absence of margarine.64 In general, frying reduces the levels of PAEs in food, probably because PAEs are lipophilic compounds that readily leach into cooking oil during frying. However, deep-fried cooked foods may also have increased PAE concentrations. A study found that deep-frying cooked potatoes in a stainless-steel pan with margarine increased the concentrations of DiBP, DnBP, BBP, and DEHP.64 This may be explained by the small amount of PAEs in the cooking oil, which may have migrated into the cooked food during frying.

Roasting

In normal cases, roasting can reduce the PAE content in food. A study found that the reduction in DEHP content in eggs, chicken liver, and chicken breast after roasting could be up to 26.1–52.5%.65 In addition, another study indicated that phthalic anhydride (PAA) produced by the thermal decomposition of PAEs can react with amino acids during coffee roasting to produce phthalimide in coffee beans,73 suggesting that the roasting process can induce the chemical conversion of PAEs to other substances.74 The PAE content decreased during roasting, which may have been due to the degradation of PAEs. As illustrated in Figure 2, in the absence of a trace amount of water, the decomposition pathway proceeds via the formation of a monoester, an unstable compound in which the interactions of neighboring groups can lead to the formation of an anhydride compound. Furthermore, phthalate acid is an indicator of monoester hydrolysis and is generated in the presence of at least 10% water. However, roasting may also increase the levels of PAEs in food, which may be related to roasting methods, such as grilling65 and microwave ovens, due to microwave contamination.64

(A) Thermal decomposition of phthalic acid ester to phthalic anhydride occurred in the absence of a trace amount of water. (B) Thermal decomposition of phthalic acid ester to phthalic acid occurred in the presence of at least 10% water, while it is not formed with a lower amount of it.

Packaging

Food packaging migration has been shown to occur in many different packaging materials such as recycled cardboard packaging, milk bottles, canned food, and water bottles.75 Related studies include the migration of diisononyl orthophthalate (DINP) from paper packaging materials made of recycled carbonless copy paper to foods, the migration of DBP, BBP, and DEHP from aluminum foil laminates to butter, the transmission of PAEs from polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottles stored at high temperatures for 10 weeks to water, the migration of PAEs from plastic packaging to sea salts and curries, the migration of PAEs from cans to vegetables and fruits,40 and the migration of DEHP from plasticized caps or cap seals of glass packages to foods76 Moreover, numerous studies have shown higher concentrations of PAE metabolites in the urine of individuals who regularly consume plastic packaged foods.39,77,78 Consumers usually perceive paper and board as safe and healthy because of their natural wood origin. However, chemical hazards from potential migrants, such as labels and printing, must also be considered.79 When analyzing the extraction of PAEs from different cheese packages, levels of DEHP were found to be 2–4 times higher in areas with colored or printed labels than without these labels, suggesting that printing inks and adhesives are a possible source of PAEs.56 Children are exposed to packaging more frequently. For kindergarten and elementary school children, the intake of DEHP and DBP through school meals increased by an average of 12–18% and 30–50%, respectively. In a study on school-packed meals for children aged 3–10 years, the median concentrations of DEHP and DBP were higher after packaging, with average increases of 113% and 125%, respectively. In addition, high temperatures facilitate the release of PAEs from the packaging. The concentrations of DEHP and DBP increased significantly in freshly baked bread rolls as well as in the traditional Egyptian hot foods, Koushary and Foul Medamas, after rapid packaging.50,81 Although leaching from plastic containers is the main source of DEHP exposure,82 the migration rate in beverages is low (mean 0.065 g/g), probably because of the lower solubility caused by the polarity of water and the non-polarity of DEHP, as well as the fact that DEHP has low volatility and is not easily transferred to beverages through the gas phase because these containers are generally kept upright, with a lack of direct contact between the beverage and the cap seal or liner.43

Contamination by PAEs can also occur during the production of food products, which requires the use of plastics,83 such as grilling salmon with aluminum foil, frying potatoes in factories84 with conveyor belts,64 transporting hot soups and cooking oils with plastic food packaging,85 producing espresso using coffee machines with plastic components,86 and transporting milk and spirit with plasticized tubes.87 Notably, studies have shown that the instantaneous migration of high levels of PAEs occurs when gloves are exposed to high temperatures and fat-rich foods. Analysis of boxed lunches showed significantly higher levels of DEHP in packed lunch (0.80 ± 11.8 mg/kg) than in set lunch (0.012 ± 0.30 mg/kg), which may be due to the disposable PVC gloves used for food preparation, containing 22–41% DEHP. Moreover, it was found that the concentration of DEHP in almost all food products after packaging exceeded 8 mg/kg, except for potato salads (0.30 mg/kg), which could be explained by the fact that the plant workers did not wear PVC gloves during the production of potato salads. In addition, DEHP was not detected in the oil used for frying food at the plant before cooking, but the level of DEHP was 10.2 mg/kg after cooking, which may be related to the migration of DEHP from gloves during the handling of fried food.66

In summary, during contact between packaging materials and food, some process agents migrate into the food, causing serious food safety problems.79,88 Data have shown that cooked and retail packaged foods were commonly contaminated with PAEs,32,33,89 especially DEHP and DBP, which do not react with polymers, are not chemically bound to plastic substrates, and exist as free-moving and leachable phases,18 and thus readily migrate from plasticized PVC materials to food under thermal or mechanical stress,90 such as tubing commonly used in milking processes, lid liners, food packaging films, gloves used in food manufacturing, conveyor belts, cookware coatings, and baby bottles made of polypropylene.50,61,91 Notably, paperboard does not contain PAEs as plasticizers, but paints, adhesives, and printing inks may contain them as sources of PAEs.92,93

The Factors Determining the Fate of PAEs in Cooking Processes

PAEs can migrate from packaging to food and occasionally degrade into PAA during cooking. Several factors determine the fate of PAEs during cooking, including food categories, cooking processes, packaging materials, and physicochemical properties of PAEs. Figure 3 shows the factors affecting the PAEs’ migration and transformation during different cooking processes.

Factors affecting the fate of PAEs in the cooking process.

Food Categories

Different food groups have different fat contents. Numerous studies have suggested that the migration of PAEs is closely related to fat content, which can be explained by the fact that PAEs are lipophilic compounds that tend to concentrate in the lipid fraction of foods.94,95 A study found that the PAE content decreased after cooking in all other foods (starchy products, meat, and fish), except vegetables, which remained largely unchanged,64 probably because of the lower fat content of vegetables. Another study indicated that the residues of DEHP in eggs, chicken liver, and chicken meat after cooking ranged from 56.5 to 82.1%, 64.9 to 94.1%, and 47.5 to 78.9%, respectively, among which the highest DEHP residues were found in chicken livers because they are richer in fat and the DEHP dissolved in the liver is difficult to remove. In addition, starchy foods, such as pasta and rice, tend to absorb water and gain weight during the cooking process, causing a decrease in the PAE content. However, a study found that potatoes lose weight during cooking, thereby increasing the concentration of PAEs.64 In addition, the complexity and roughness of food can affect the PAE content. Pasta and rice with tomato sauce, beans, and vegetable broth have a more complex composition and can remain bitter (at temperatures above 60 °C) for a longer time than other dishes, which can further influence the release of plasticizers.50 When rice, fried balls, and cooked radishes were treated with PVC gloves containing 30% DEHP, only fried balls exhibited migration of DEHP, which was attributed to their rough surface.66

In summary, the fat content could affect the concentration of PAEs, which could be explained by the fact that the molecular structure of the PAEs is similar to that in fats and oils, making it easier to form hydrogen bonds. Moreover, due to the looser organization of fats, it is relatively easier for PAEs to diffuse into the fat content in food. Thus, the rate of migration of PAEs from the packaging to the food depends on the lipophilicity of the food base system. In addition, weight changes during the cooking process as well as the complexity and roughness of the food could also play a role in determining the concentration of PAEs in food.

Cooking Process

Heating Temperature

During cooking, the concentration of PAEs in food is sensitive to temperature. A study found that the mean value of DEHP after frying eggs at 180 °C for 5 min was significantly higher than the mean value after frying at 100 °C for 6 h.65 During coffee roasting, the DEP concentration is significantly dependent on higher temperatures, with API production observed only at 220 °C.74 Milling during green tea production had the least effect on PAE levels, which was attributed to the absence of high-temperature treatments or other blasting measures in this step.45 In addition, the storage temperature could affect the migration of PAEs from the packaging to the food. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the increase in storage temperature can promote the transfer of PAEs from plastic to food,95−98 such as high-temperature (above 25 °C) storage of drinking water in PET bottles,17 rapid packaging of freshly baked bread rolls,50 and heating food in paper bags and plastic packaging.75,99,100 In addition, a decrease in the storage temperature can prohibit the transfer of PAEs from plastics to food. A Canadian study reported high concentrations of DnBP and DEHP in nonfrozen packaged meat products, whereas no PAEs were detected in frozen foods.43 Studies have shown that the treatment of cooled fried meatballs with PVC gloves resulted in only a 0.34 mg/kg increase in DEHP concentration.66

In summary, for more volatile substances, where volatilization losses are greater than those of migration, heating reduces the concentration of PAEs in food. For example, the heating of frozen foods packed in cardboard in a microwave oven reduced the concentration of some migrated substances through volatilization.71 It was demonstrated that most of the DEP evaporated when heated up to 175 °C and completely evaporated at 298 °C.101 However, heating can increase the concentration of non-volatile substances, and when the migration of PAEs is greater than volatilization, the concentration of PAEs in food is higher after heating.71 It has been shown that the tendency of PAEs to migrate from polyethylene materials to food is linearly dependent on temperature, with migration increasing with temperature, and can be described by the Arrhenius relationship,102 increasing the risk of contamination.64 An increase in temperature leads to an increase in compound decomposition and polymer chain breakdown, which is released from the plastic surface,103 leading to an increase in the migration of PAEs.67,104

Cooking Style

Different cooking processes have different effects on the fates of PAEs. A study found that the levels of DiBP in food decreased after boiling and frying but did not change after steaming.64 Another study found that the content of DEHP was 0.08 mg/kg in raw chicken, 13.1 mg/kg after frying, and 16.9 mg/kg after packaging.66 Studying the leaching of PAEs from infant feeders that were treated differently (boiling, autoclaving, and microwave sterilization), boiling resulted in approximately 26–54% lower leaching of PAEs than the other two treatments and was safer for infant feeding.100

Researchers have indicated that the drying process can decrease the PAE content. One study found that, when chicken was cooked for up to 7 h after freeze-drying, the level of DEHP decreased, indicating that DEHP was lost along with the water. Freeze-drying reduces DEHP concentrations to a greater extent than grilling, both of which are drying treatments.65 Another study suggested that, in tea production, DEHP accumulates during spreading, fixing, and rolling but decreases during drying, which may be related to water evaporation, DEHP degradation, and co-distillation due to the relatively low vapor pressure and high thermal stability of DEHP.105,106

In addition, studies have found that the urinary levels of PAE metabolites are significantly higher in people who regularly consume microwaved foods.39 When investigating the migration of milk powder from plasticizers, it was found that the level of migration under microwave conditions was significantly higher than that under conventional heating conditions, which may be attributed to the different heating principles of the two heating methods. Microwaves cause molecules to vibrate at high speeds and are heated internally; therefore, the migration rate is high, and the same migration rate is achieved over a relatively shorter period. In conventional high-temperature heating through heat transfer, heat is transferred from the surface to the inside. The heating rate is slow and uneven, the migration rate is slow, and the time required is considerable,107 resulting in a difference in the heating efficiency. In addition, changes in the molecular interactions between packaging materials during microwave heating may also lead to enhanced migration.70

The fate of PAEs was affected by the addition of spices during cooking. After adding spices to chicken roasted in plastic bags, the concentrations of DIBP and DBP increased significantly, and higher concentrations of DIBP and DBP in spices were detected at 12.4–38.5 μg/kg, indicating that PAEs in meat may not be migrating from plastic bags but due to the addition of spices during cooking.83

pH Value

Studies have shown that the migration of PAEs in bottled beverages increases with decreasing pH values.97 When investigating the liquid placed in an acrylic container and heated for 0–5 min at four different pH values (3, 5, 7, and 9), the results showed that DEHP and DBP had the highest migration rate under strongly acidic conditions (pH = 3) at the highest cumulative concentrations of 159.8 and 104.9 μg/L, respectively. In addition, DEHP migration was detected only under strongly acidic conditions (pH = 3), whereas, under weakly acidic (pH = 5) and neutral (pH = 7) conditions, migration was minimal.108

Cooking Utensils

A study suggested that the migration of DBP during microwave heating is related to the volume and shape of the containers because of the different temperatures reached during heating. With the vessel with the smallest volume of 300 mL, significant temperature changes were observed in time intervals of 1–3 min during microwave heating, while vessels with large volumes of 1000 and 1200 mL had much lower temperatures at the same power and, therefore, exhibited minimal variation in the concentration of DBP.67 Another study simulated the migration of DBP from containers into food during microwave heating, with the concentration of DBP in new containers ranging from <limit of quantitation (LOQ) to 2.0 μg/L and in containers used over a protracted time period ranging from <LOQ to 7.5 μg/L, indicating that the release of PAEs in plastic containers increased with the continued use of the containers.75 In addition, studies have found that DEP, DiBP, DnBP, and DEHP may be present in the coatings of nonstick cookware products;109 these chemicals may migrate from coatings to meat and fish foods in the absence of margarine.64

Packaging Materials

Packaging Material Type

PAEs can migrate into food from different materials, such as plastic containers for soft drinks and mineral water, metal bottle caps and glass cap PVC gaskets for foods, and recycled cardboard packaging for baby food.57,96 When different packaging types were used for the same food, there were differences in the migration of PAEs. Figure 4 shows three different materials normally used in food packaging: impermeable, permeable, and porous materials. Impermeable materials include “hard” materials such as metals, glass, and ceramics. The material is an absolute barrier, and there is no migration from the interior but the migration is confined to a surface phenomenon only. Permeable materials are exemplified by plastic-based materials such as plastics, rubbers, and elastomers. The material offers limited resistance to migration, but this can occur not only from the surface but also from the interior of the material. Porous materials are exemplified by paper and board materials with a heterogeneous open network of fibers with large air spaces or channels. In particular, low-molecular-weight substances can migrate rapidly with minimal hindrance.

Depiction of the chemical migration from and through different materials.

Plastic materials, paper, and board materials have a higher PAE content compared to those of other materials. Studies have shown that the concentration of DMP is significantly higher (20 times) in PET water bottles than in glass bottles.110 In addition, one study found that transparent plastic food containers had particularly high levels of PAEs compared to white opaque plastic food containers, which could be explained by the fact that transparent plastic food containers are softer and stronger than opaque food containers, which require a higher use of plasticizers, and the foaming process induces more plasticity.111 When functioning as primary packaging, paper and boards are most often used in contact with dry solid foods. In this situation, the substances present on the paper and board can migrate into the food by direct contact (Figure 5a) or indirectly through the gas phase between the material and food surfaces (Figure 5b). Generally, the public perception is that paper bags are safer than plastic bags. However, the concentration of PAEs could be higher in heated paper bag samples than in plastic packaging99 because some packaging cardboards are made of recycled products, and PAEs, such as DiBP, are used in printing inks to improve surface adhesion, flexibility, and wrinkle resistance.89,112 Thus, DiBP and others can migrate from cardboard to food.64 In addition, the rate of migration from paper materials to food is much higher than that from plastics.113 Paper-based packaging materials have large voids that allow small molecules to migrate from the outside into food (Figure 5c). For example, when paper beverage cups are stacked, the inner and outer layers come into contact with each other, allowing contaminants (e.g., ink) to migrate into the food.109

Contact Time and Contact Area with the Packaging Materials

Numerous studies have indicated that a prolonged contact time could increase the migration of PAEs from packaging materials to foods,78,97,108 which in some cases could be up to 50 times or more.75 This is attributed to the increased mobility resulting from increasing contact time; however, there is no linear correlation between the migrated PAEs and the contact time in an aqueous solution.98 Thus, the longer the contact time with the packaging, the greater is the migration of PAEs from the packaging to the food.96 Notably, the migration of PAEs was easily detected during the early stages, when the transfer was relatively rapid. The migration then tends to decelerate until equilibrium is attained at a later stage.45,114 A larger volume for the same weight can increase the contact area with the package,50 thereby resulting in a larger migration rate. When investigating a variety of fresh meat, poultry, and fish, with samples placed on polystyrene trays covered with a plasticized film that touched only one surface of the product, the 220 μg/g level was found in a smoked salmon fillet, and the next highest level, at 14 and 9.5 μg/g, was found in a chicken breast and regular ground beef sample, respectively. The surface areas of these foods were 130, 72, and 27 μg/cm2, respectively, indicating that salmon had a larger contact area with the package, which resulted in a higher migration rate.43

Physicochemical Properties of PAEs

The literature suggests that the migration process could be influenced by properties such as the molecular structure, relative molecular mass, and polarity of the migrating species. Studies have found that DBP and DINP have the greatest migration potential in mineral water, DEHP and DBP have the greatest migration potential in edible oil, DBP and DEHP exhibit large transfer potentials in both edible oil and mineral water, and DMP, DINP, and diisodecyl phthalate (DIDP) exhibit a lower migration.96,98 In general, as the relative molecular mass of PAEs increases, the chain lengths gradually increase, structures gradually exhibit greater stability, solubility in water gradually decreases, polarities gradually decrease, and volatilization in food during the cooking process gradually decreases.115 Thus, the migration ability gradually decreases.116 However, a study found a high concentration of migrated DEHP, which could be explained by the fact that long-chain PAEs, such as DEHP, are weakly bound to packaging materials and thus migrate easily when heated.98

Others

In addition to the aforementioned determining factors, the sugar content and gas pressure in the bottle, spraying alcohol on gloves, and ultraviolet (UV) radiation can also affect the fate of PAEs during cooking. A study suggested that the concentration of PAEs in nonalcoholic malt beverages was negatively correlated with the sugar content and gas pressure in bottles.44 Another study found that spraying alcohol onto gloves promoted the migration of PAEs, even in cold, fat-free foods, because alcohol contains PAEs.66 In addition, in a study on processed salami, the migration of PAEs in a blank sample increased by 35.62% after UV irradiation, indicating that UV irradiation could play a role in the migration of PAEs in food.

Key Measures for Reducing Human Exposure to PAEs from Food

Choosing the Proper Food Categories

High levels of DEHP are found in dairy products (butter, heavy cream, cheese, and milk) and spices (coriander, chili, and cinnamon), the sources of which may be environmental transfers (air deposition, soil root uptake, and pesticide use) or food-contact materials (conveyors and packaging materials) used during production and storage.61 Another study found that flavoring powders added to dietary supplements during production may be responsible for DEHP exposure.39 Therefore, excessive dietary supplement intake by children is not recommended. Numerous studies have suggested that the consumption of organic fresh fruit and vegetarians is significantly and negatively correlated with the metabolites of PAEs in the human body.117−120 Therefore, high amounts of organic fresh fruit and vegetables instead of butter and heavy cream in the preparation of food should be recommended.40 Children, pregnant women, and the elderly, who are more vulnerable to exposure to PAEs, should consume more fresh fruits and vegetables while reducing the intake of packaged food and avoiding excessive intake of dietary supplements.

Selecting the Proper Cooking Ways

Several heating processes can release PAEs from food packaging materials into food. However, the migration of PAEs from packaging materials into food during cooking can be reduced in several ways, such as by allowing steam to escape from food during microwave heating72 and reducing transport time.50 When studying the leaching of PAEs from infant feeders that were treated in different ways (boiling, autoclaving, and microwave sterilization), researchers suggested that boiling resulted in about 26–54% lower leaching of PAEs compared to the other two methods of treatment and was safer for infant feeding. Notably, the sterilization of infant utensils is also a risk factor that allows physically bound PAEs to enter water and milk.100 As the release of PAEs from packaging materials to food is positively related to the heating temperature, reduction of the contact between food and packaging materials during the heating process is recommended.

Use of Proper Food Packaging Materials

Previous studies have demonstrated that the loss of tissue crystal structure, increase in amorphous regions, and shrinkage of micro-ordered regions during microwave heating greatly contribute to DEP migration,104 suggesting that PVC should not be heated in a microwave oven.72 Studies have also suggested that heating frozen food in cardboard packaging in a microwave oven can reduce the concentration of PAEs through volatilization; however, heating can also accelerate the migration and increase the concentration of non-volatile substances. Therefore, for cardboard inks used in food contact packaging, lower migration levels can be maintained if the content of low-molecular-weight volatiles and the transfer of high-molecular-weight components are controlled by offsetting.76 In addition, the release of various PAEs from the white opaque plastic containers was relatively low owing to their low solubility, indicating that they are relatively safe for use. However, higher food temperatures and high-fat food characteristics, as well as higher consumption frequency, will eventually exacerbate health risks.69,111 Therefore, children should avoid disposable plastic food containers. If eating takeout food is unavoidable, the frequency of consumption should be minimized, and lower-risk plastic food containers should be used for food.111

Several strategies have been developed to reducing the migration of PAEs from packaging materials, such as coating the surface of the material with a non-migrating substance, creating a network structure inside the material, and preventing PAEs from leaching from the polymer matrix system.121 In addition, adding nanoparticles, such as silica starch nanocrystals and cellulose nanocrystals, to films of packaging materials can also prevent the migration of PAEs.122 In addition, gamma plasma or UV irradiation of packaging films can produce highly compact structures and hinder migration.123,124 Attaching plasticizers to PVC chains is another method of preventing PAE migration.125 In addition, large-molecular-weight plasticizers with slow migration rates or ionic liquids can be used.126,127

Most studies on inhibiting plasticizer migration have focused on conventional plastic polymers, which cause serious environmental problems. Starch-based food packaging materials have attracted considerable attention because of their degradability, which can alleviate environmental crises.128 By controlling the degree of substitution of esterified starch films, stronger molecular interactions, more organized crystal structures, and more compact aggregation structures can be formed between the PAEs and starch ester molecules, which can affect the diffusion coefficient and change the migration rate of PAEs in the material,107 thereby inhibiting the migration of PAEs.101

Using Replacements for PAEs

The replacement of PAEs could be another strategy to reduce the exposure of the human bodies, including di-2-ethylhexyl terephthalate (DEHTP) and di(isononyl)cyclohexane-1,2-dicarboxylate (DINCH), which are currently used in various consumer applications.129,130 The replacement of PAEs could result in lower toxicity and thus pose fewer hazards to human health. In a study on DEHTP exposure in rats, adverse effects on the reproductive system, kidneys, or liver were not observed.131 Additionally, in a study on DEHP and DEHP replacements in a murine cell line, DEHP was found to be more cytotoxic than DINCH.132 DEHTP is now used instead of DEHP in flexible PVC products, children’s toys, medical devices, and food contact materials.130 Recent studies on human exposure have provided evidence for this trend, as several studies have demonstrated a decrease in urinary DEHP metabolite concentrations in recent years,133−136 whereas urinary DEHTP metabolites have increased.134,137 Similarly, DINCH is now used in the U.S. for a range of applications, including for children’s toys, food contact materials, vinyl flooring, and medical devices. Therefore, the application of replacements for PAEs is an effective method to reduce exposure to PAEs, and thus, it could weaken the negative impact of PAEs on human health.

Future Research Prospects

PAEs occur in a variety of foods, including water and beverages, alcohol, meat, aquatic products, dairy products, fats and oils, vegetables and fruits, cereal-based products, condiments, as well as infant foods. As these foods are not consumed raw, the fate of PAEs in food during cooking should be considered. PAEs can migrate from food packaging materials into food and are occasionally transformed into PAA in different cooking or thermal processes, such as steaming and boiling, microwave heating, frying, roasting, and packaging, and numerous factors can determine the fate of PAEs during the cooking process. Although many studies have investigated the effect of cooking on chemical concentrations in foods, such as polybrominated diphenyl ethers, polychlorinated biphenyls, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, furans, pesticides, and heavy metals, research on the effect of cooking on PAEs has been limited, and further investigations in this area should be strengthened.64 Notable perspectives for future research on PAEs in food are presented herein.

(1)It is challenging to analyze PAEs in food because they are ubiquitous in the environment; therefore, food products are easily contaminated with PAEs. Currently, the analytical methods for PAEs in food are usually time-consuming and require multiple measurements, such as preconcentration and cleanup procedures, limiting the number of food samples being measured. Therefore, appropriate analytical techniques for detecting foodborne PAEs should be developed.

Therefore, systematic errors should be considered in research on simulated food.
