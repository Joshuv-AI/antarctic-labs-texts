Leadless pacemaker tine fracture and dislocation: two case reports
Authors: Yiqing Shen, Haiwei Li, Weiping Sun, Yutong Liu, Yining Wang, Fei Hang, Yongquan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rate of leadless pacemaker implantation is increasing worldwide. To date, there have been few reports of leadless pacemaker dislocation and extraction. This article describes two cases of leadless pacemaker tine fracture and dislocation. One case was an elderly male, and the pacemaker was found to have fallen off to the right pulmonary artery one month after surgery, and a new leadless pacemaker was implanted; the other was an elderly female, and the leadless pacemaker fell off to the right ventricle 9 months after surgery. The leadless pacemaker was removed, and a dual-chamber permanent pacemaker was implanted. After leadless pacemaker implantation, regular programming should be performed to detect abnormalities early and perform clinical intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05233-2.

Introduction

Permanent cardiac pacemakers are an effective treatment for bradyarrhythmias [1]. With the advent of leadless transcatheter pacing systems (TPS), they have become a viable alternative to traditional pacemakers [2], Specifically, for patients with vascular occlusion, infection, renal dysfunction, superior vena cava malformation. However, the experience with leadless pacemaker (LP) and device dislodgement remains limited [3, 4].

There are 2 types of leadless pacemakers currently available in clinical practice. A recent study reveals that although Medtronic Micra and Abbott Aveir differ in their design, fixation mechanism (tine-based fixation for Micra, screw-in helix fixation for Aveir), and implantation technique; these 2 devices appear to have a similar short-term safety profile [5].

The Micra LP uses four Nitinol tines for myocardial fixation- a innovative and novel device design [6], and to date, there Only 7 cases tine fracture have been reported so far in the MAUDE (Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience) database [7].Device dislodgement of leadless pacemakers is rare, and the relationship between tine fracture and dislocation has not been well described. To our knowledge, cases involving migration of a Micra device with evidence of tine fracture are exceedingly uncommon. Here, we present two patients with Micra migration—one embolized into the pulmonary artery and another displaced in the right ventricle—with residual tine fragments identified. These cases highlight novel device-related complications and underscore the importance of risk–benefit assessment and long-term follow-up in patients receiving leadless pacing systems.

Case report 1

A 72-year-old male (height 173 cm, weight 60 kg, BMI 20.05 kg/m2) presented for routine follow-up one month after leadless pacemaker implantation. The patient had a long-standing history of "sick sinus syndrome," managed initially with dual-chamber pacemaker implantation 20 years earlier. He subsequently developed pocket infections (2 and 5 years post-implantation) and infective endocarditis with vegetations on the leads and tricuspid annulus. He underwent complete system extraction and tricuspid valve replacement with a bioprosthetic valve. Due to persistent atrial fibrillation, a single-chamber epicardial pacemaker system was implanted. Three years ago, battery depletion prompted generator replacement. However, two months prior to the current presentation, epicardial lead dysfunction (impedance > 3000 Ω) was detected, prompting implantation of a leadless pacemaker (Micra™ VR, Medtronic) after informed consent.

During the initial procedure, multiple sites in the right ventricle were tested before adequate fixation was confirmed by standard tug testing.

At the current visit, pulmonary computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a hyperdense nodule located in the right lower pulmonary artery near the anterior basal segment (Fig. 2). Management

The patient remained hemodynamically stable. After thorough discussion, the patient and his family declined retrieval of the embolized device. A new leadless pacemaker was successfully implanted at an alternative right ventricular septal site (Fig. 3E), with stable parameters confirmed at one-month follow-up.

Case report 2

The patient is a 68-year-old female who underwent leadless pacemaker (Micra™ AV, Medtronic) implantation at another hospital 9 months ago due to third-degree atrioventricular block. About one month post-operation, she began experiencing chest discomfort. Pacemaker interrogation showed good parameters, and symptomatic medication was prescribed. Two weeks before admission, a follow-up pacemaker interrogation revealed battery life < 2 months and a ventricular threshold > 5 V/0.24 ms. Transthoracic echocardiography and chest X-ray confirmed dislodgement of the leadless pacemaker.

Management

After routine disinfection and draping of the bilateral femoral areas, both femoral veins were punctured. A temporary pacemaker was implanted via the left femoral vein. A 23 F introducer sheath was inserted via the right femoral vein, through which a snare retrieval system (Gooseneck snare kit, GN2000 ev3) was advanced. The tine of the dislodged leadless pacemaker was successfully captured and removed (Fig. 4). Multiple position attempts were made to deploy the leadless pacemaker, but all tests showed bad pacing parameters with high thresholds. Therefore, leadless pacing was abandoned. After discussion with the patient’s family, it was decided to perform dual-chamber permanent pacemaker implantation.

Discussion

Dislodgement of leadless pacemakers is rare, typically occurring in the pulmonary artery or right ventricle, and usually happens either during the procedure or in the early postoperative period [3, 8, 9]. Known risk factors include insufficient device fixation and anatomical abnormalities of right ventricle. Micra LP guidelines recommend that at least two tines engage firmly with myocardial tissue to minimize dislodgement [10]; however, no prior reports have documented tine detachment from the Micra device itself.

Case1 has a complex history—including recurrent infections, multiple system extractions, and tricuspid valve replacement—likely contributed to altered right ventricular architecture. Case 2 was an elderly woman with a history of breast cancer. During the mid-term follow-up, it was found that the pacing threshold abnormally elevated, and it was eventually shown to be dislocated in the right ventricle. The cause of the dislocation is still unclear. Fortunately, the dislocated leadless pacemaker was successfully removed during the operation in case2. Residual tine embedded in the right ventricular septum in both patients, a finding not previously reported, suggests that the structural integrity of the Micra device may have been compromised.

The exact mechanism behind the tine fracture remains unclear. Possible contributing factors to tine fracture and damage may include direct damage to the tines during implantation, manufacturer defects, operator errors during device manipulation, or durability of the nitinol tines [7]. Hu and colleagues speculated that the cause for the tines fracture could be attributed to insufficient strength, with the squeezing force exerted on the tines due to the Micra's position at the junctional zone between the free wall and the septal wall [11].Whether specific device implantation locations, or the angle at which the device is implanted within the right ventricle contribute to undue stress on the nitinol tines cannot be definitively from the currently published literature.

The the MAUDE database of tine fracture showed that the tine fracture had no relationship with the patient's symptoms and changes in pacing parameters. The analysis may be related to the Micra tine-based fixation mechanism described that 2 engaged tines provide 15 times the holding force required to prevent dislodgement, accentuating the redundancy in the fixation mechanism. However, it is not clear whether it was caused by only one tine hooking the myocardium, causing dislocation after tine fracture, or by increased force on the other tine after one tine fracture, which led to dislocation. Development of the device required innovation, maybe including a novel fixation approach for an entirely leadless pulse generator.

To date, there is a lack of detailed evidence regarding the safety and feasibility of retrieving and reimplanting TPS devices. Funasako et al.demonstrate the safety and feasibility of TPS retrieval with a mid to long RV [12]. Besides, guidelines recommend prompt retrieval of embolized leadless devices [9, 13], conservative management was chosen in case1 due to the patient's stability and informed preference. Future risks such as thrombosis or infection will require close monitoring. Moreover, risk stratification tools have been explored in other device populations. For instance, the MADIT-ICD score was shown to predict long-term cardiovascular events in patients with conventional implantable cardioverter-defibrillators [14]. Although not directly validated for leadless pacemakers, such prognostic approaches highlight the importance of systematic long-term monitoring in patients with device-related complications.
