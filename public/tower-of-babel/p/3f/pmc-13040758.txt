Awareness and perceptions of AR/VR in dentistry: a cross-sectional study among Turkish dental students
Authors: Suay Yağmur Ünal, Ayşe Gül Öner Talmaç, Gaye Keser, Filiz Namdar Pekiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThis study aimed to assess the awareness, exposure, and perceptions of dental students toward augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) technologies, and to evaluate their readiness for curricular integration.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was administered to 320 clinical-phase dental students equally distributed between Marmara University and Kahramanmaraş Sütçü İmam University. This exploratory study employed a convenience sampling approach using an adapted survey instrument. The structured questionnaire included items on demographics, prior awareness, usage, participation in AR/VR-related activities, and willingness to engage in training and research. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and non-parametric tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsOnly 20.9% of students reported prior awareness of AR/VR in dentistry, and less than 1% had direct experience. Attendance at related workshops or seminars was rare (3.1%). Despite this limited exposure, 83.8% expressed interest in receiving training and 74.4% indicated willingness to participate in research. No significant differences were observed by gender or institution. However, final-year students reported significantly greater interest in both training and research compared with certain lower years (p < 0.01), suggesting that clinical maturity may influence perceptions.ConclusionsDental students demonstrated limited prior exposure but strong enthusiasm for AR/VR integration into education. A phased approach is recommended, starting with elective modules and pilot workshops, followed by gradual incorporation into core curricula. Faculty development and continuous student feedback should accompany this process to ensure effective adoption. Such integration has the potential to improve preparedness for complex clinical procedures and foster engagement with innovative learning modalities. Findings should be interpreted in light of the exploratory design and self-reported data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08900-w.

Introduction

Background

Over the past decade, dental education has undergone a substantial transformation driven by rapid advances in digital technologies. Contemporary educational models increasingly emphasize not only the transmission of theoretical knowledge but also the development of clinical competence through experiential and simulation-based learning [1]. In this context, immersive technologies such as augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) have attracted growing attention as innovative tools with the potential to reshape preclinical and clinical dental training [2].

AR and VR differ fundamentally in their modes of interaction and pedagogical application. Augmented reality enhances the real environment by superimposing digital content—such as three-dimensional (3D) anatomical structures, procedural guides, or diagnostic overlays—onto physical models or simulated clinical settings. This blended approach enables learners to contextualize virtual information within real-world scenarios, thereby supporting spatial reasoning and anatomical comprehension [3, 4]. Virtual reality, by contrast, places the learner in a fully computer-generated environment that allows complete immersion in simulated clinical situations. Within VR platforms, procedures can be practiced repeatedly under standardized conditions, independent of patient availability or clinical risk [5].

Beyond visualization, both AR and VR offer structured and adaptable learning environments in which task difficulty, anatomical variation, and procedural complexity can be systematically controlled. These features are considered particularly valuable for the acquisition of psychomotor skills and clinical decision-making. Previous studies have suggested that VR-based simulators, especially when combined with haptic feedback, may enhance technical performance in operative dentistry and related disciplines when compared with conventional preclinical training alone [3, 6]. Furthermore, early performance metrics obtained from VR simulation systems have been reported to correlate with subsequent clinical competence, indicating a possible role for these technologies in formative assessment [6].

However, while the theoretical advantages of AR and VR in dental education are well articulated, the empirical evidence supporting their widespread effectiveness remains variable. Reported outcomes differ considerably across studies, depending on educational context, technological infrastructure, and instructional design. As a result, AR and VR are currently regarded as promising adjuncts rather than established replacements for traditional teaching methods. From the learner’s perspective, technology-enhanced instruction is frequently perceived as engaging and motivating. Survey-based research has shown that dental students often express positive attitudes toward AR- and VR-supported learning, citing improved interactivity, clearer understanding of complex procedures, and greater confidence in skill acquisition. Nevertheless, positive perceptions do not necessarily translate into readiness for implementation or sustained curricular integration [5–8].

Despite increasing global interest, the adoption of AR and VR in undergraduate dental curricula remains inconsistent. Several barriers have been identified, including high initial costs, ongoing maintenance requirements, the need for advanced technical infrastructure, and limited faculty experience with immersive educational platforms [9]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in developing and emerging educational systems, where access to advanced simulation technologies may be limited. Recent surveys conducted in such contexts have demonstrated low levels of student familiarity with AR and VR, as well as minimal institutional availability, highlighting a significant gap between technological potential and practical application [10].

Although existing studies suggest that AR and VR could enrich dental education when used alongside conventional instructional methods, data regarding students’ awareness, perceptions, and readiness—especially in resource-limited settings—remain scarce [6–10]. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate dental students’ awareness and attitudes toward AR and VR technologies, their prior exposure to these tools, and their readiness for potential curricular integration. By surveying students from two universities, the study sought to provide empirical insights that may inform curriculum planning, guide institutional investment decisions, and contribute to bridging the gap between emerging digital innovations and current educational practices in dentistry.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

The study was designed as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating cross-sectional survey. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among undergraduate dental students enrolled in the clinical phase of their studies at two Turkish universities: Marmara University-Istanbul (MU) and Kahramanmaraş Sütçü İmam University-Kahramanmaraş (KSU). A total of 320 students participated, equally distributed between the two institutions (n = 160 per site). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines. The sample was stratified to ensure proportional representation from each class year (2nd to 5th year), with 80 students (25% of the total) from each year group. Recruitment followed a convenience sampling approach, whereby participants were approached during clinical training sessions or on campus. The inclusion of one metropolitan and one regional public university was intended to capture variability in educational context while maintaining curricular comparability. While this method facilitated data collection, it carries a risk of selection bias due to non-random recruitment; this limitation is addressed in the Discussion section. All participants provided informed consent before completing the survey.

Survey instrument

The data collection tool was a structured questionnaire, adapted and modified for this study based on previously published surveys assessing AR/VR awareness in dental education, particularly the work of Masood et al. (2024). The items were reviewed by a panel of experts to ensure suitability for undergraduate dental students. The English version of the final questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1 [8].

The structure of the questionnaire was informed by comparable cross-sectional survey designs published in the literature. To improve clarity, the survey items were carefully worded in plain Turkish and formatted with closed ended (mostly dichotomous) response options. The consistency of the responses was evaluated post hoc, and descriptive feedback from respondents indicated that the items were well understood.

The final questionnaire consisted of two sections: Sect. 1, demographic data (age, gender, grade, university). Section 2: AR/VR-related items, covering (a) prior awareness, (b) personal use during training, (c) participation in related workshops/seminars, (d) willingness to engage in AR/VR research, (e) interest in receiving formal training, and (f) awareness of specific AR/VR dental software. Most items used dichotomous Yes/No responses (e.g., “Before this survey, had you heard about the use of AR/VR in dentistry?”). The final questionnaire was administered in Turkish via both online and paper-based formats between April and May 2025. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Formal internal consistency analysis was not performed, as most items were dichotomous and designed to capture awareness and attitudes rather than latent constructs.

Data collection and analysis

Responses were coded and entered into SPSS (version 29.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables: categorical data as frequencies and percentages; continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median, and range. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and graphical inspection (histograms, Q-Q plots).

For bivariate analyses: Two-group comparisons of continuous variables (e.g., age) used the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data, reported with median differences and p-values. Categorical variable associations were tested with Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test as appropriate. For all χ² tests, the test statistic (χ²) and degrees of freedom (df) were calculated and reported alongside p-values. For ordinal variables such as grade, chi-square tests for trend were applied when relevant. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Distribution of demographic characteristics

Of the participants, 25.0% (n = 80) were in their 2nd year, 25.0% (n = 80) in their 3rd year, 25.0% (n = 80) in their 4th year, and 25.0% (n = 80) in their 5th grade. Half of the students (50.0%, n = 160) were enrolled at KSU, and the other half (50.0%, n = 160) were enrolled at MU (Table 1).

Regarding responses to AR/VR-related questions, 20.9% (n = 67) reported having heard about the use of AR/VR technology in dentistry prior to the survey. Only 0.9% (n = 3) had used VR technology in their dental education or practice, while 0.6% (n = 2) had used AR technology. Attendance at a workshop, seminar, or training session on VR/AR in dentistry was reported by 3.1% (n = 10). A total of 74.4% (n = 238) expressed willingness to participate in research studies or trials involving VR/AR, and 83.8% (n = 268) indicated interest in receiving training on the use of VR/AR in dentistry. Awareness of any VR or AR application designed for dental education or practice was reported by 10.3% (n = 33) of the participants (Table 2).

The distribution of responses to AR/VR-related questions did not differ significantly by grade overall (p > 0.05). However, a statistically significant difference was found in responses to the question, “Would you be willing to participate in research studies or trials related to VR and AR in dentistry?” (p = 0.007, p < 0.01). A statistically significant difference was also observed for the question, “Would you be interested in receiving training on the use of VR and AR in dentistry?” (p = 0.001, p < 0.01). Fifth-year students gave a “Yes” response significantly more often than second-, third-, and fourth-year students (p = 0.005, p = 0.015, and p = 0.001, respectively; p < 0.05). Additionally, third-year students were significantly more likely than fourth-year students to respond “Yes” (p = 0.035, p < 0.05) (Table 6).

Discussion

This study reveals a substantial gap between dental students’ exposure to AR/VR technologies and their interest in such tools. Awareness of AR/VR in dentistry was limited—only about one-fifth of participants had heard of these technologies being used in dental education, and almost none had personal experience. This indicates that while the current integration of AR/VR in dental curricula is minimal, students showed interest in such innovations.

Previous research supports these findings. Huang et al. reported that after a brief VR-based implant training session, dental students showed high interest and perceived VR as a valuable adjunct to traditional methods [9]. In Khalid et al.’s survey, 81% of respondents agreed that combining simulation, including AR/VR, with conventional teaching enhances learning outcomes [8]. Together, these results suggest that dental students are not resistant to technology but require structured opportunities to experience and evaluate its benefits.

Our findings regarding differences between class years provide additional insight. Final-year students exhibited the highest interest in AR/VR (96%). This may relate to curriculum design and clinical exposure. Senior students, having faced complex clinical cases and recognized limitations in traditional training, may better appreciate AR/VR’s potential to enhance skills,. Literature suggests that exposure to AR/VR earlier in training, followed by reinforcement in later years, can maintain engagement and improve skill acquisition [11, 12]. Therefore, integrating AR/VR from the preclinical phase, with consistent application in clinical years, may optimize both awareness and acceptance.

The present findings align with previous research indicating that dental students generally exhibit low prior exposure but a strong interest in adopting digital technologies in education. Similar to our results, Yılmaz et al. reported limited knowledge of advanced technologies among Turkish dental students yet highlighted their willingness to embrace innovation once introduced into training programs [13]. In the field of dental imaging, Hung et al. emphasized the growing relevance of personalized digital tools, including AR/VR, for improving diagnostic accuracy and educational outcomes [14]. Likewise, Hegde et al. found that both Australian dental students and practitioners perceived emerging technologies positively, though practical experience remained scarce [15].

Scoping reviews further confirm these trends Uribe et al. underscored the importance of integrating generative AI and immersive technologies into dental curricula to prepare students for future practice [16], while their global survey of educators revealed increasing attention to chatbots and AR/VR-based teaching tools [17]. On the clinical side, Eschert et al. documented the uptake of digital technologies in dentistry and maxillofacial surgery, reinforcing the need for student-level exposure to such applications [18]. Similarly, Aldakhil et al. concluded that awareness of advanced technologies in dentistry remains uneven and called for structured integration into teaching [19].

At the curricular level, Dantas and Dias da Silva showed how digital health concepts are being gradually incorporated into postgraduate dental programs, suggesting a pathway that could also be adopted at undergraduate levels [20]. Complementary to this, Schwendicke et al. proposed a core curriculum framework for AI in oral healthcare [21], and Thurzo et al. highlighted the transformative potential of digital technologies, including AR/VR, for updating dental education [22]. Collectively, these studies support the interpretation that although exposure is currently limited, structured integration strategies are feasible and widely endorsed across dental education systems.

Institutional and demographic factors appeared less influential. Gender did not significantly affect responses, aligning with previous research indicating equal adaptability among male and female students [2, 3]. Moreover, the similarity in responses between a large urban university (MU) and a smaller regional institution (KSU) suggests that the lack of AR/VR exposure is a widespread issue in Turkish dental education, not limited to specific institutional contexts.

From a curriculum development perspective, the observed “awareness–interest gap” underscores the need for structured integration strategies. A gradual approach appears most effective, beginning with elective workshops or short modules that introduce AR/VR tools, followed by the incorporation of successful applications into core courses. Nevertheless, before wide-scale curricular changes, pilot implementations and controlled evaluations are required to confirm the effectiveness of AR/VR training in improving clinical competencies. To ensure sustainability, faculty members should also receive dedicated training to develop the necessary skills for effective implementation and long-term use [23].

Addressing barriers identified in the literature is essential. Cost is a common concern, particularly for advanced haptic VR systems [24]. However, more affordable VR headsets and smartphone-based AR applications are emerging, reducing financial obstacles. Technical maintenance and lack of standardized frameworks remain challenges; thus, institutions should consider user-friendly systems, dedicated technical support, and collaboration with other universities to share resources and best practices [2, 6, 9, 25].

This study’s limitations include its scope—two universities within one country—which may restrict generalizability. Institutions already employing AR/VR might yield different results. The self-reported nature of the data introduces potential recall and social desirability biases. Additionally, the cross-sectional design captures perceptions at a single point in time, limiting causal inference. Future research should involve larger, more diverse samples, longitudinal designs, and experimental interventions to assess both learning outcomes and changes in perception. Qualitative studies, such as focus groups, could further clarify students’ motivations and concerns regarding AR/VR. Additionally, although the questionnaire was adapted from a previously validated survey, modifications made for the context of Turkish dental students have not undergone full psychometric validation. This may limit comparability across studies.

Despite these limitations, our findings indicate that dental students are ready to embrace educational technologies. Introducing AR/VR should be purpose-driven, targeting specific learning objectives. For example, AR can be highly effective in teaching spatial anatomical relationships, while VR with haptic feedback can improve manual dexterity in operative dentistry [3, 5]. Institutions could also engage students in AR/VR research and development, fostering both innovation and critical evaluation skills. By aligning implementation with curriculum needs and leveraging student enthusiasm, dental schools can bridge the current awareness–interest gap and enhance the overall quality of training.

Conclusions

This study showed that dental students from two Turkish universities had very limited prior exposure to AR/VR technologies yet demonstrated strong enthusiasm for training and research in this area. No differences were observed by gender or institution, while final-year students expressed greater interest, likely reflecting increased clinical awareness.

Based on these findings, a stepwise integration of AR/VR into dental curricula is recommended. Small-scale applications, such as AR-based anatomy modules or VR simulations in clinical skills training, could serve as entry points. Faculty development and structured feedback mechanisms should accompany these efforts to ensure effective adoption.

Such strategies have the potential to enhance interactive and safe learning opportunities; however, their effectiveness should be further tested through longitudinal and multi-institutional studies before widespread implementation.
