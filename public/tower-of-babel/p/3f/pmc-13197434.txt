Carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling unveil deep-sea microbial niches in the Atacama Trench
Authors: Miguel Arribas Tiemblo, Armando Azua-Bustos, Javier Sánchez-España, Daniel Carrizo, Antonio Molina, Isabel Herreros, Olga Prieto-Ballesteros, Miguel Ángel Lominchar, Óscar Ercilla Herrero, Victoria Baca-González, Iñaki Yusta, Andrey M. Ilin, Felipe Gómez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep-sea sediments host microbial communities essential to nitrogen, sulfur, and carbon cycling, yet those in the Southern Hemisphere remain understudied. In this work, we analyze sediments from an oceanic trench off northern Chile using metatranscriptomics, aiming to relate community composition with functional activity. Most assayed sites form part of a broadly uniform deep-sea ecosystem fueled by the degradation of marine snow through diverse aerobic and anaerobic pathways. Nitrogen cycling within them is driven by archaeal nitrification and bacterial dissimilatory nitrate reduction. One site, however, appears to be a sulfur-based cold seep inhabited by mats of thiotrophs and vesicomyid clams within darker sediments. In this site, ANME archaea are largely absent despite the presence of dolomite crystals, which are typically associated with anaerobic methane oxidation. Instead, abundant subsurface pyrite and active sulfate reduction point to substantial sulfur cycling. Across the region, heterotrophic communities dependent on marine snow transition to chemosynthetic ones supported by reduced sulfur in a sulfur-driven cold seep with minimal fluid flow.

Introduction

The Peru–Chile Trench is a 6000-kilometer-long oceanic trench located off the coast of Chile and Peru. It is formed by the subduction of the Nazca Plate beneath the South American Plate1. Its deepest section, off the Atacama Desert in northern Chile coast (the Atacama Trench), exceeds 8000 mbsl (Meters Below Sea Level). The whole South American Plate, including the Atacama Desert and its associated continental slope, have remained under stable latitudinal and hyper-arid conditions for the past 200 million years2. The potential ecological stability along the oceanic seafloor between the Chilean coast and the subduction zone, along with evidence of living brachiopods in the area3,4 motivated the Living Fossils of the Atacama Trench expedition, as part of Schmidt Ocean Institute’s (SOI) 2024 program to search for ancient deep-sea ecosystems in this region. One of the main objectives of the expedition is to characterize the microbial communities present in the deep-sea sediments studied throughout its duration.

Microbial communities within these ecosystems are composed almost entirely (>90%) of bacteria and archaea5. These are taxonomically diverse and mediate key biogeochemical cycles, including those of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur6. Their composition varies across marine environments, with seamounts typically hosting richer communities than trenches, and cold seeps falling in between in terms of diversity but featuring more specialized taxa6,7. Community structure often reflects resource availability, from organic detritus to porewater rich in reduced compounds such as sulfide and ammonia5. Hadal trenches (<6000 mbsl) are often distinct environments, owing to their relative isolation and higher pressures. Microbial communities within them are rich in endemic piezophiles, and variations between trenches are often caused by differentially abundant levels of Gammaproteobacteria8. These communities focus on the degradation of recalcitrant organic matter by piezophiles of well-studied taxa of classes like Marimicrobia and Aquibacter9.

Hadal trenches lie, however, outside of the range of the ROV used in the expedition (max. 4500 mbsl), and sampling was conducted in abyssal marine sediments between 2000 and 4000 mbsl. Archaeal communities in these sediments shift from the previously mentioned nitrate-reducing oxic communities to uncultured euryarchaeotal and crenarchaeotal lineages in the anaerobic and nitrate-limited depths of the sediments10. Bacterial communities also vary from aerobic and versatile ones mainly composed of Alpha-, Gamma- and Deltaproteobacteria, Gemmatimonadetes and Actinobacteria to uncultured members of Chloroflexi and Planctomycetes in the nutrient-poor and anoxic subsurface10,11. In typical deep sea sediments—where organic carbon (OC) and nitrogen are generally provided by detritus from primary producers12—the nitrogen cycle is primarily driven by nitrifying archaea of the phylum Nitrososphaerota, such as Nitrosopumilus13. These organisms oxidize ammonia to nitrite and nitrate in an oxygen-dependent process, restricting their activity to surface sediments. The resulting nitrate supports additional nitrogen-cycling pathways, including bacterial nitrite reduction14. Carbon consumption and production in these regions is mainly heterotrophic in origin, although chemoautotrophic production by archaea can reach up to 20% of all carbon production15.

A detailed morphological, mineralogical, and chemical study of marine snow in the Atacama Trench12 has shown that the composition of these settling suspended sediments can drastically change with depth, varying from organic-rich, diatom-dominated in the upper 100 m to siliciclastic-rich, detrital mineral-dominated in the rest of the water column. This sharp decline of the organic component of marine snow during the upper hundreds of meters is considered to be the result of intense heterotrophic consumption by many different microbial metabolisms (including sulfate-reducing bacteria) in the OMZ12. A collateral effect of this microbial consumption or organic matter across the water column is a very limited export of fresh organic carbon to the microbial communities inhabiting the deep seafloor8,16.

Although most of the seafloor is dominated by cycles that depend on this sedimented matter, specialized niches can arise in localized sites like cold seeps. These are deep-sea regions where H2S, CH4, and hydrocarbon-rich fluids seep from the seafloor, often fueling highly specialized chemolithotrophic communities17–23. Unlike hydrothermal vents, cold seeps are independent of geothermal activity24–26. They have been found at depths ranging from 50 to over 5000 mbsl7,22,23,27–29 and are notably long-lived ecosystems, capable of sustaining life for centuries30. Methane and hydrocarbons within these fluids are often consumed by an array of specialized microorganisms. In the upper oxic layer of the sediments, bacterial aerobic methano- and methylotrophs like Methyloprofundis tend to be abundant. Archaeal methanotrophs of the ANME-1, 2, and 3 groups are the only microorganisms capable of oxidizing methane in anoxic environments, and drive its oxidation in the subsurface of methane-rich cold seeps31,32. Seepage fluids often contain high concentrations of sulfide and reduced sulfur compounds, which can sustain large communities of filamentous, large sulfur-oxidizing bacteria of the Beggiatoaceae family, which form visible and diagnostic white mats. These microorganisms store NO3- within vacuoles, which is later used to oxidize HS- seeping from the sediment to elemental sulfur (S0). This is later stored in the form of globules and gives them their characteristic white hue. When in need of energy, these bacteria can oxidize their stored S0 to SO42- through NO3- or O233–35. These environments host a wide range of other specialized microorganisms, such as Asgard archaea, sulfate-reducing bacteria from the DSS group and SEEP-SRB1 clade, methanogens like Methanosarcina, and fermenters and reductive dehalogenators of the Chloroflexi phylum36–40. The discovery and characterization of undescribed cold seeps in the region was another of the main objectives of the expedition.

Here we report the results of seven dives conducted during the expedition, targeting three main sites located 30–40 km offshore from the port of Antofagasta, Chile (Fig. 1A). Sites 6 and 7 were each visited once, and averaged depths of 3900 mbsl and 4500 mbsl, respectively. Site 9 was visited on four separate occasions (subsites 9, 9.1, 9.2, and 9.3), with depths ranging from 2400 to 2900 mbsl. Most transects examined a relatively uniform deep-sea ecosystem. However, during the exploration of site 9.2, a bed of vesicomyid clams was discovered at a depth of 2836 mbsl41. These bivalves can form symbiotic relationships with bacterial thiotrophs in the southern Pacific coast of the Americas27. At the moment of sampling, the studied area of the Pacific Ocean showed a marked thermocline with surface water displaying a relatively warm temperature of around 18 °C which sharply declined with depth (e.g. 7 °C at 500 m depth, 4 °C at 1000 m, and a minimum temperature of 1.7 °C near the seafloor at around 4000 m depth; see Supplementary Fig. 1 in the Supporting Information). Redox conditions and oxygen availability also changed markedly through the water column; the dissolved oxygen profiles obtained in the area (see Supplementary Fig. 1 in the Supporting Information) showed a well oxygenated surface layer (e.g. 90-100% oxygen saturation in the upper 40 m), an Oxygen Minimum Zone (OMZ) with a thickness around 300 m (virtually anoxic waters with undetectable oxygen concentrations between 100 and 400 m depths) and a hypoxic water column with oxygen saturation between 20 and 40% in the 500–4000 m depth range (Supplementary Fig. 1). On the other hand, salinity showed very little variation and remained between 34.5‰ and 35‰ at all depths12. The chemical analyses of water samples taken at different depths (see Supplementary Table 1 in the Supporting Information) provided a composition that is in line with previous studies of the Pacific Ocean44,45. The ocean water chemistry was vertically homogeneous (very little differences in the concentrations of most major ions; Supplementary Table 1), and the only remarkable feature was the observation of increasing silicon contents with depth (e.g. 2188 µg/L Si at 20 m vs. 9100 µg/L at 3855 m; Supplementary Table 1), which has been ascribed to the effects of the upwelling Humboldt current12.

In this work, we analyze metatranscriptomic data from the surface and subsurface of the sediments of these sites. We perform an integrated taxonomic and functional analysis to identify active metabolic pathways and their associated microbial taxa. Additionally, we explore the biogeochemical dynamics of the anomalous site 9.2. By comparing this site to other sedimentary environments across the seafloor, we investigate the biogeochemical processes that sustain microbial life in this understudied region. This study describes a cold seep-like environment in Northern Chile, well over 2000 km from the nearest two regions with proven cold seeps, the Northern Peruvian Subduction Zone27 and the Chile Triple Junction (CTJ)46. We also provide what is, to date, one of the few -omics driven studies of deep-sea microbial communities on the Pacific Coast of South America, focusing on the taxonomical and functional characterization of a cold seep in this region.

Results

Mineralogical and geochemical composition of seafloor sediments

As unveiled by XRD, the mineralogical composition of seafloor sediments in the studied area was found to be dominated by detrital silicates, including quartz, feldspar, plagioclase (dominant and intermediate), mica and other clay minerals, amphibole (likely hornblende) and pyroxene (Fig. 2A). These minerals were abundant in all sediment samples irrespective of their location and depth within the sediment. Chlorite was also present in most sediments at a minor proportions. Another important component found in these sediments was the biological debris settled from the upper zone, mostly consisting of diatoms. The composition of the sediments is coincident with the dominantly siliceous composition of the detrital minerals and diatom-rich biological debris observed in the marine snow of the area12, which reaches the seafloor after a long journey from the ocean surface to the bathypelagic zone.Fig. 2Mineralogical characterization of sediments from Site 9.2.A X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis of sediment from Site 9.2. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of dolomite (B, C) and pyrite (D, E) crystals at different depths within a core from site 9.2.

Mineralogical characterization of sediments from Site 9.2.

A X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis of sediment from Site 9.2. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of dolomite (B, C) and pyrite (D, E) crystals at different depths within a core from site 9.2.

In addition to the biogenic and detrital sediments, which are common in the seafloor of the studied area, we observed abundant dolomite in the uppermost cm of the sediments of Site 9.2 (Push cores 7 and 9, Fig. 2A). This mineral was only found near the sediment/water interface as euhedral, rhombohedral crystals with edges of 8–10 µm long (Fig. 2B, C), and never found in the SPM fraction of the water column as detrital or authigenic mineral12, nor in other sediments outside this site, so that its presence in the uppermost sediments of Site 9.2 was surprising and deserves further studies. Many dolomite crystals showed textural features suggestive of partial dissolution (e.g., dissolution pits and hopper-like crystals; Fig. 2B, C), so that these authigenic carbonates seem to be metastable. However, it is worth noting that these sediments are situated at a depth slightly below 2800 m, which is theoretically well above the carbonate compensation depth (CCD), that in the Pacific Ocean has been proposed to be comprised between 4000 m and 5000 m47,48.

Pyrite was also found as an abundant authigenic mineral at depths greater than 3 cm. This mineral was observed mostly as large (25 µm in diameter), framboidal subspherical aggregates, which at higher depths resembled pseudocubic arrangement (Fig. 2D), though it was also occasionally observed as very fine-grained (sub-micron) clusters formed by nanometric (100–200 nm) framboids (Fig. 2E). These two contrasting textures and sizes observed in the pyrite crystals found in the sediments of Site 9.2 suggest different precipitation mechanisms or crystallization kinetics. In any case, it is worth noting that pyrite framboids were also found in other sediment samples at certain depths, while the large pyrite crystals were only found in Site 9.2. Additionally, deeper samples showed more prominent characteristic XRD peaks, suggesting the increase of abundancy and/or crystallinity of pyrite with depth12.

From a geochemical perspective, the sediments were rich in Si, Al, and Fe with lesser amounts of Ca, Na, K, and Mg, in good agreement with the mineralogical composition dominated by aluminosilicates (Supplementary Table 2). Site 9.2 sediments appeared to be enriched in iron with respect to the sediments of the surrounding areas (ca. 7-8%wt. vs. ca. 5 %wt., respectively) but depleted in Si and Al (Supplementary Table 2). This chemical feature may well reflect the abundance of pyrite in Site 9.2, though unfortunately, we lack data for total sulfide concentration in the sediments to demonstrate this possibility. Among the trace elements, B, Ba, Cr, I, P, Sr, V, and Zn were the most abundant, with concentrations in the order of a few hundred ppm in all cases and no apparent vertical trend with depth (Table S2). As, Cr, Pb (three elements commonly bound to iron oxy-hydroxides), and B were enriched in Site 9.2 with respect to the surrounding sediments, while Ba and Zr were depleted (Supplementary Table 2). Total organic carbon (TOC) and nitrogen (TN) were similar across all samples. In the first cm of the sediment TOC and TN reached 1% and 0.15% (% dw) respectively. In the 1–3 cm range, levels dropped to 0.4-0.7% and 0.05–0.09% (% dw)12. SEM imaging and EDX elemental analysis only found detectable levels of sulfur in the sediments in Site 9.2. Sulfur levels were slightly lower in the first 6 cm (0.21 ± 0.03 at%) than in the 6 to 12 cmbsf range (0.34 ± 0.05 at%) (Supplementary Table 3). Sulfur (S) data were often correlated with regions of abundant iron (Fe) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Nitrate and nitrite levels were below detection limits (<10 mg/L for NO3- and <0.5 mg/L for NO2-) in all samples analyzed through the two strip-based tests we employed. Analysis through ion chromatography allowed quantifying 4 abundant cations and 3 common anions (Supplementary Table 4). Nitrates and nitrites were, again, below the detection limits in all samples. Sodium (Na+) and chloride (Cl-) are key components of saltwater and were above the detection limits in all cases. Potassium (K+), magnesium (Mg2+), calcium (Ca2+), and Bromide (Br-) are common members of soluble salts and were present, regardless of sediment depth, at consistent and generally invariable concentrations in the 500–1500 mg/L range. Bromide (Br−) was rarer (60–70 mg/L) but behaved similarly otherwise. Sulfate (SO42−) was present in similar concentrations across most samples. In Site 9.2, however, its concentration slightly increases with depth (2920 → 3086 → 3279). Ammonium (NH4+) was only detected in the subsurface (6–18 cm) of Site 9.2 at relatively low concentrations (<150 mg/L). Its colocalization with Na+ in the chromatogram, however, hindered its detection, and it may be present at lower concentrations in other samples. The spatially restricted localization of ammonium in the subsurface of Site 9.2 points to it being a heavily reducing environment, which is coherent with the likely abundance of reduced sulfur compounds and the absence of oxygen. The pH of the porewater was constant in all samples, in the 7.2–7.4 range, regardless of site or depth.

The exception was the 6–12 cm layer in S9.2_Core6, where RNA of sufficient quality for Illumina sequencing could be recovered. Taxonomic analyses of these samples showed that the microbial communities they contain were largely homogeneous across all samples at higher taxonomic ranks (Fig. 3A, B). Bacterial sequences ranged from 75–90% of all taxonomically resolved reads, followed by archaeal reads at 5–10%. Viral and eukaryotic reads made up 1–5% of the total across all samples. Alpha diversity was assessed through the calculation of the Shannon, Simpson, and Chao1 indexes (Supplementary Fig. 3). Statistical comparisons (t-test) showed no significant differences (p = 0.3) in the alpha diversity of samples outside the seep, and those taken within the seep. Samples within the seep showed noticeable differences, however, although the absence of replicates didn’t allow for statistical comparisons. The diversity of sediment samples outside of the seep was similar (1.8–1.9 Shannon diversity), while that of sediments in the seep was lower, regardless of depth (1.7–1.8), and the microbial mats in the seep displayed a higher Shannon diversity (1.93). Taxonomical richness was estimated through the Chao1 index. At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota dominated all samples, followed by the ammonia-oxidizing archaeal phylum Nitrososphaerota, which was present throughout but sharply decreased below the surface. Other consistently represented bacterial phyla included Chloroflexota, Planctomycetota, Nitrospinota, and Acidobacteria. Thermodesulfobacteriota was largely absent in ocean sediment samples but comprised 2–3% of all RNA reads in Site 9.2 (Fig. 3C, A).

Archaeal diversity in surface samples was also generally uniform (Fig. 3D, B), with over 90% of reads assigned to Nitrososphaerota. Other phyla—including Bathyarchaeota, Euryarchaeota, and Thermoplasmatota—each represented less than 2% of archaeal RNA reads. A marked community shift was observed in S9.2_Core6 at 6–12 cm depth, where Nitrososphaerota accounted for less than 15% of archaeal reads. This layer was instead dominated by Bathyarchaeota (30%) and other lineages.

Differentially abundant microbial taxa

All 5293 bacterial and archaeal taxa identified using the GenBank nr database were analyzed for differential transcript abundance across four main sample categories: Ocean Sediment, Site 9.2 – Mat, Site 9.2 – Surface (0–6 cm), and Site 9.2 – Subsurface (6–12 cm). Log₂-fold change (LFC) was calculated as the log₂ of the ratio between the abundance of each taxon in each sample and its average abundance across all four ocean sediment samples, which served as the baseline reference. Microbial taxa with LFC > 5 or <−5 in any sample were selected and plotted (Fig. 5). Taxa were grouped based on the specific environments in which they were over- or underrepresented: outside Site 9.2, in Site 9.2, and in specific regions of Site 9.2, such as the microbial mats or the subsurface.Fig. 5Log₂ fold change (LFC) in gene expression abundance of selected taxonomic groups relative to the average abundance in standard ocean sediment samples.Each site is compared against this reference. Most, if not all, families present outside of Site 9.2 were also present within it. Some groups were, however, more abundant outside of Site 9.2. Ammonia-oxidizing archaea of the Nitrososphaerota phylum, including families Nitrosopumilaceae and Nitrososphaeraceae, were rarer in Site 9.2 (LFC of −5) and nearly absent below the surface (LFC of −10), reflecting their oxygen-dependent metabolism. Some bacterial families of mostly aerobic microorganisms involved in diverse processes followed similar trends, but again with minor differences. While most taxa present in sediments outside the seep were also found within it, many taxa were exclusive to Site 9.2 and were significantly overrepresented in either the sulfur-oxidizing bacterial mat or the 6–12 cm depth layer.

Several microorganisms were consistently abundant in all metatranscriptomes from Site 9.2, regardless of depth or location. These included archaeal methanogens from the Methanomicrobia phylum (LFC of +5), like Methanohalophilus, as well as members of other lineages, such as Prometheoarchaeota and other members of the Asgard kingdom, like Lokiarchaeia and other archaea typical of cold seeps (LFC of +7). Among bacteria, sulfur-oxidizing and reducing bacteria like Sulfurovum and Tepiditoga, and hydrothermal bacteria like Calditrichota were largely restricted to site 9.2

There were also substantial differences between the three metatranscriptomes obtained from Site 9.2. Taxa abundant in the surface (0–6 cm) of the seep were also systematically present in the two other subsamples. The subsample associated with white bacterial mats displayed, however, very high levels of members of the Beggiatoaceae family like Thioploca (LFC of +8), Thiomargarita (LFC of +9), Sulfuriflexus (LFC of +9), Thiogranum (LFC of +7), Marithioploca (LFC of +11), Parabeggiatoa (LFC of + 11), Halobeggiatoa (LFC of +8), and Beggiatoa (LFC of + 9), all chemolithotrophic sulfur-oxidizers and typical members of the previously mentioned white mats33–35. Specific sulfate reducers from the family Desulfobulbales (LFC of +6) and genus Thermodesulfovibrio (LFC of +9) and iron reducers like Deferrimicrobia (LFC of +4) were also somewhat overrepresented in the microbial mat.

By far the most distinct communities were those present in the subsurface (6–12 cm) of Site 9.2. These differed markedly from all other samples (Fig. 5), featuring a diverse array of archaeal taxa involved in methane cycling, including methanogens such as Verstraetearchaeota (LFC of +7)49, Methanosarcina (LFC of +8), Methanomassiliicoccus (LFC of +11), and Methanofervidicoccus (LFC of + 10). Notably, Alkanophaga (LFC of +10) and Methanophaga (ANME-1, too rare to compute LFC) were found exclusively in this layer. Alkanophaga was 30 times more abundant than Methanophaga (0.00156 vs 0.00006% of all sequences). The phylum Lokiarchaeota of the Asgard kingdom, typical inhabitants of cold seeps, were also highly abundant (around 20% of all archaeal sequences) and overrepresented (LFC of +11) in the subsurface of this site, alongside closely related phylae like Thorarchaeota and Helarchaeota.

Three thermophilic archaeal classes were also overrepresented at depth: Thermococci (LFC of +7), Thermoplasmata (LFC of +6), and Thermoprotei (LFC of +8). Although originally described in environments exceeding 60°C, these lineages have also been detected in marine sediments, where they engage in diverse metabolic roles50,51. Bathyarchaeota (LFC of +7), a generalist phylum prevalent in anoxic sediments38, dominated archaeal diversity in the subsurface of Site 9.2, with over 25% of all archaeal reads. Two archaeal orders involved in sulfur cycling—Desulfurococcales (LFC of +5) and Archaeoglobales (LFC of +10)—were also enriched. Both include the only known archaeal sulfate and thiosulfate reducers.

Among bacteria, Dehalococcoidia class (LFC of +4) stood out in the subsurface of Site 9.2. These obligate organohalide respirers consume hydrogen and likely relied on fermentation-derived hydrogen while reducing organohalide compounds in the sediment39. Other abundant subsurface bacteria were anaerobic, metabolically versatile sediment specialists, such as Abyssubacteria (LFC of +6, sulfate reducers/oxidizers), Sulfurimonas (LFC of +8, a hydrogenotroph common in sulfidic environments52), Aerophobetes (LFC of +7, putative fermenters), and Armatimonadota (LFC of +5), which can couple the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway with oxidative glycolysis53. Bacterial sulfate reducers like Desulfoluna (LFC of +7) and Desulfamplus (LFC of + 6) were also common in the subsurface of Site 9.2.

In cold seeps, carbon may also be sourced from the oxidation of seeping methane and hydrocarbons, which can be oxidized aerobically in the surface of the sediment, or anaerobically through specialized archaea of the ANME group. Three major carbon fixation pathways were detected across all samples. The Wood-Ljungdahl pathway (cooS), capable of fixing CO2 into Acetyl-CoA under anaerobic conditions, was upregulated at site 9.2 (>40 tpm vs <10 tpm outside of it). In the top 6 cm, it was mainly expressed by Planctomycetota (order Brocadiales), whereas in the 6–12 cm layer, Chloroflexota and Dehalococcoidia dominated. The Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) cycle is the main source of organic matter of oxygenic phototrophs. In the deep sea, it is employed by chemosynthetic bacteria to produce organic matter from CO2 and energy from the oxidation of chemical compounds. Its marker gene, rbcL, was highly upregulated in all three metatranscriptomes from site 9.2 (30–60 tpm vs <2 tpm outside the site). This pathway was largely restricted to Gammaproteobacteria within Pseudomonadota, with minor contributions from Zeta-, Beta-, and Alphaproteobacteria. The reverse TCA (rTCA) cycle produces carbon compounds using CO2 and reduced compounds through an inversion of the Krebbs cycle. It was the third major carbon fixation pathway found in the samples, though it was the least prevalent and not differentially expressed across them. It was mainly associated with Nitrospirota and Nitrospinota in surface samples, with smaller contributions from Campylobacteria at site 9.2. In S9.2_Core6_6–12, however, rTCA genes were more prevalent in archaeal sequences and in Campylobacteria and Bipolaricaulota, with no representation from Nitrospirota or Nitrospinota.

Nitrogen metabolism

The cycling of nitrogen is known to drive a significant part of the metabolism of microbial communities in the upper centimeters of deep-sea sediments. Nitrogen within recalcitrant sedimented organic matter is freely available for nitrifiers, which can derive energy from progressively oxidizing ammonia to nitrite, and then to nitrate. The abundant nitrate in these sediments can then be subjected to denitrification in specific anaerobic niches, which eventually leads to the release of N2 gas. This prevents the accumulation of nitrates in the sediments. All samples were grouped close together, except for the subsurface metatranscriptome of site 9.2, where processes involved in nitrogen cycling were less prevalent (Fig. 6D).

Genes linked to nitrogen metabolism were strongly downregulated in the subsurface layer (S9.2_Core6_6–12, Fig. 7B). Nitrification genes (hao, amoA-C), involved in the oxidation of ammonia to nitrite, were prevalent in surface sediments, which are aerobic (5–6 mg/L DO). nirK, involved in denitrification (70–450 tpm), was one of the most abundant genes across all surface samples. The ammonium transporter amt was also highly transcribed across all samples (250–1500 tpm). Dissimilatory nitrate reduction was not common in the oxygen-rich surface sediments and the deeper layers, with genes specific to it (nirB/D, narI) being scarce (<5 tpm). This suggests that anaerobic respiration occurs primarily via alternative pathways. Genes involved in glutamine (glnA) and glutamate (gltB/D) synthesis were expressed ubiquitously (>10 tpm). Assimilatory nitrate reduction genes (nirA, narB) were consistently expressed across samples at low levels (1–5 tpm) but did not exhibit differential regulation.

Genes involved in the oxidation and reduction of nitrite (narG/H) were very highly expressed (200–500 tpm). These genes are likely a combination of narGH and their homologs nxrAB. The former participates in denitrification together with nirK, while the latter are involved in the oxidation of nitrite to nitrate, and are characteristic of bacteria from the genus Nitrospira, which were abundant in all samples. Based on this, nitrogen metabolism in these samples appears driven by two steps of nitrification. Nitrifying archaea oxidize ammonium to nitrite through amoA-C, and Nitrospira bacteria then oxidize nitrite to nitrate through nxrAB. The abundant resulting nitrate is then likely used for denitrification through a cohort of bacteria and the nirK, narG, and narH genes.

Sulfur metabolism

Sulfur cycling is, compared to the two previous elements, a less prevalent process in most of the biosphere. Under oxic conditions, sulfur is mainly present in solution in the form of sulfates. These sulfates are often used as the end acceptors of electrons by sulfate reducing bacteria in anoxic environments. The main products of sulfate reduction are reduced sulfur compounds like H2S, S0 and thiosulfates, which hold substantial amounts of energy. This energy can be utilized by sulfur oxidizing chemolithotrophs. In environments rich in reduced sulfur compounds, such as cold seeps, whole ecosystems can be supported by the energy provided to sulfur oxidizers by reduced sulfur compounds within the seeping fluids. In the case of the expression of genes participating in sulfur metabolism, NMDS separated sediment samples into two distinct groups (P = 0.026) (Fig. 6C). The groups included the four sediment samples from outside Site 9.2 and all three samples in Site 9.2, suggesting that processes involved in the cycling of sulfur are starkly different inside and outside of Site 9.2. Dissimilatory sulfate reduction (DSR) is a process through which anaerobic organisms use SO42− as the end acceptor of electrons instead of O2. We used five genes as markers of this process: met3, dsrA, dsrB, aprA, and aprB. The sulfate adenyltransferase met3 converts sulfate to 5′-phosphosulfate (APS) and participates in sulfur assimilation. While met3 functions in DSR, it is also involved in assimilatory sulfate reduction (ASR) and other pathways. met3 expression was high across all samples (>20 tpm), especially in the 6–12 cm sediment layer at site 9.2 (135 tpm). Both the AprA/B (50–150 tpm) and DsrA/B (30–100 tpm) complexes were abundant in Site 9.2. DSR was a common process in the anoxic sediment layer at site 9.2 and prevalent in the communities inhabiting the sulfur-oxidizing mat.

Assimilatory sulfate reduction (ASR), on the other hand, involves the reduction of sulfate not as an end electron acceptor but as a means of incorporating it into organic compounds like cysteine and methionine. Genes related to ASR were uniformly expressed across surface samples (10–20 tpm) but downregulated in the 6–12 cm sediment layer at site 9.2 (<2 tpm). The one exception was cysC, which catalyzes the transformation of APS to PAPS (3′-Phosphoadenosine-5′-phosphosulfate) and was strongly upregulated in the two surface samples (Mat and Surface) from site 9.2.

Chemotaxis, motility, and growth

Active bacterial communities are often signaled by the presence of genes involved in motility, in interaction with the environment, and in replication. Unlike previous subsections, we found no significant differences (P = 0.315) among samples for genes related to motility, chemotaxis, and growth. All seven samples are clustered together, indicating functional similarity among communities in these traits. Flagellin (fliC), involved in the formation of the flagellum, showed very high (>400 tpm) levels across all samples (Fig. 8B). Flagellar assembly genes (fliN/M, motA) were consistently expressed across samples but at lower levels than fliC. Genes linked to chemotaxis (cheA/Y/V/W, mcp) showed similar abundance across samples (10–50 tpm), peaking in microbial mats near site 9.2. Genes linked to bacterial growth were categorized by function. Quiescence-associated genes (rpoS) were rare in all samples ( < 20 tpm). Genes related to active growth—including RNA polymerization (rpoA/B), exponential growth (rpoD), and cell division (ftsF), were also abundant (>100 tpm) in all samples. This pattern was most pronounced in the microbial mats at site 9.2, which exhibited the highest expression of growth and motility genes. These mats are likely complex communities rich with active microorganisms and niches rich in reduced sulfur compounds, which may promote chemotaxis and active growth.

Genes involved in general metabolic processes, protein translation, stress response, and cellular transport were consistently abundant and showed noticeable differences in expression (Fig. 9). The degradation of other organic compounds like glucomannan (gmuG) was also driven by members of the Planctomycetota phylum in the same region. The phage shock protein (Psp) regulon is responsible for maintaining the proton motive force and protecting the inner membrane of bacteria when the cytoplasmic membrane is under stress. It is regulated by interactions between pspA/pspB, and was inactivated in most bathyal sediments, which displayed significant pspA overexpression (300 tpm) and limited presence of pspB (<150 tpm). Although this trend was maintained in the subsurface of Site 9.2, the transcriptional activator of the operon (pspF) was substantially overexpressed (150 tpm). This gene was almost uniquely expressed by a member of the Scalindua genus, a group of marine anaerobic ammonium oxidizers that lack peptidoglycan in their outer wall, and may exhibit some degree of basal psp operon expression to account for that54,55. Toxin-induced stress, as modulated by dinJ, appeared rare regardless of site (<2 tpm). Levels of the cold shock protein cspA were high in all samples, with minimal differences between them. Genes involved in cell division like ftsA and ftsZ were present in all samples (<100 tpm) but expressed at significantly higher levels in the subsurface of site 9.2 (>200 tpm). Both proteins were predominantly expressed by Chloroflexota bacteria, which appear to be actively growing below the immediate surface of site 9.2. The initiation of translation, as indicated by infB, was consistent in most samples but noticeably lower at site 9.2, especially in its subsurface. RNA polymerization via rpoB was common (>200 tpm) and showed little variation between samples, as did the expression of ribosomal proteins RP-S1 and RP-L10. RP-L18e, however, was almost uniquely expressed in the microbial mat by an unclassified eukaryote.

The Sec pathway is involved in the secretion of newly formed proteins to the extracellular medium or to the cytoplasmic membrane. Its main component is secY, which forms the core channel protein. This gene was common in most samples (30–80 tpm) and very abundant in Site 9.2 (>200 tpm). There, this pathway was mainly driven by Nitrospinota, whereas outside this site, bacteria of the phylum Pseudomonadota were the primary drivers. The Type 2 Secretion System (T2SS) acts as a second stage of secretion of toxins and enzymes destined to the extracellular environment. Its activity was estimated via gspG56, which was consistent across samples (>100 tpm) except in the subsurface of site 9.2, where it was upregulated (>700 tpm) by several bacterial phyla. This overabundance of the T2SS in the subsurface may partially explain the overexpression of genes related to toxin and phage-induced stress.

Discussion

In general terms, the taxonomic composition of most communities described here is similar to previously reported deep-sea sediments, which are dominated by bacteria of the Pseudomonadota, Chloroflexota, and Planctomyceta phyla, and archaea of the Nitrososphaerota phylum13,40,57–59. Members of Nitrososphaerota were absent below the immediate surface of the sediments, likely due to their metabolic activity depending on the availability of nitrogenated organic matter, ammonia, and oxygen. The absence of ANME archaea was initially surprising, as it implied low bioavailability of methane in Site 9.2. However, similar, primarily sulfur-driven niches have been previously described elsewhere in the Pacific coast of the Americas60. Microbial abundance and RNA yields decreased with sediment depth, consistent with reduced availability of sedimented organic matter. Total Nitrogen (TN) and Total Organic Carbon (TOC) levels in the sediments were similar to those described in the bathyal seafloor in the literature, reaching a maximum of TN ~ 0.15% and TOC ~ 1% dw in the surface, and gradually diminishing with depth61,62.

The most anomalous communities were found in the cold seep-like environment of Site 9.2, where vesicomyid clams formed rich beds. These chemosynthetic mollusks specialize in colonizing sulfide-rich ecosystems63,64, and contain sulfur-oxidizing bacteria that are generally maternally inherited, and show high levels of connectivity to other clam colonies65,66. Vesicomyid clam habitats in cold seeps can also enable a higher degree of macrofaunal diversity than what could be expected purely from bacterial chemosynthetic activity67. The microbial mats in the seep were dominated by several sulfur-oxidizing chemolithotrophs of the Thiotrichaceae family, mostly similar to those described in similar mats elsewhere33,34,42,68. Some archaeal phylae typical of the anoxic regions of cold seeps, like Bathyarchaeota and Lokiarchaeota were similarly present in the subsurface of Site 9.2, as were several bacterial sulfate reducers, which also typically inhabit these locations36,43,49,57,60

ANME archaea are largely absent in the newly described cold seep

ANME archaea are also typical inhabitants of cold seeps, where they engage in the anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM) in consortia with sulfate reducing bacteria (SRBs)32,50,57,69. ANME archaea were scarce in all samples, except for ANME-2d, which were present in all sediment samples. This family, however, enables AOM through concomitant nitrate/nitrite reduction, and is not diagnostic of cold seeps37,70. It was more prevalent outside of Site 9.2, where the cycling of nitrogen appears more relevant. The only other ANME archaea found in all samples was a member of Candidatus Methanophagales (ANME-1), which was only present in the subsurface of Site 9.2 at low levels (<0.00006% of all sequences). It was also 30 times less abundant than its close relative Candidatus Alkanophagales, which selectively degrades multicarbon alkanes over methane71,72. All this is coherent with the inferred scarcity of methane in this site, due to the limited methanogen population and the apparent absence of methane seepage. The absence of ANME methanotrophs and methanotrophy-related genes like mcrA and met, the limited presence of hydrocarbon-degrading archaea implies there is little to no availability of methane in the area (Fig. 5/7A). Hydrocarbon and sulfur seeps that lack ANME representation have been recently described60, and limited seepage of multicarbon alkanes may partially explain the community composition found in Site 9.2.

Genes involved in element cycling point to a carbon-fixing and sulfur reliant community

The presence of several upregulated pathways of carbon fixation in Site 9.2 also implies that the community present within it is, to some degree self-sustainable. The ability to fix dissolved CO2 is essential in chemolithotrophic niches, and is widespread in hydrothermal vents and cold seeps43,73. The Wood-Ljungdahl pathway (cooS), which enables H₂-dependent CO₂ fixation74 and the Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) cycle, based on CO₂ fixation via rbcL using ATP and NADPH75 were both upregulated in all three samples from Site 9.2.

Nitrogen cycling was primarily represented by nitrification through Nitrososphaerota archaea (Fig. 3D). This is an aerobic process and, as such, cycling was mostly restricted to the upper centimeters of all sediment samples, where dissolved oxygen concentrations were in the 5–6 mg/L range. These archaea, along with other nitrifying bacteria, would be responsible for the consumption of sedimented nitrogenated organic matter, and the production of nitrate and nitrite14,76 through specific genes (amoA-C, nirK, hao). The high transcription levels of the ammonium transporter amt suggest there is bioavailable ammonia in these upper layers of the sediment. Nitrate produced through nitrification may then be used through dissimilatory nitrate reduction by bacteria or ANME-2d archaea (Fig. 7B).

Sulfur cycling was driven by two main metabolic processes: sulfur oxidation through the SOX operon and sulfate reduction. The former took place in the aerobic surface, and the latter in the anaerobic subsurface. Sulfur abundance can be estimated through tusE, a gene involved in the synthesis of 2-thiouridine77, a modification at the tRNA wobble position that facilitates codon-anticodon pairing. Though not directly involved in sulfur metabolism, tusE is often upregulated in sulfur-rich environments78, as was the case in all samples from Site 9.2 (Fig. 8A).

The Sox pathway enables sulfur-oxidizing bacteria to chemolithotrophically utilize thiosulfate. It is encoded by the SoxXYZABCD operon, which produces four periplasmic proteins: SoxYZ, SoxAX, SoxB, and SoxCD79,80. The complete operon fully oxidizes thiosulfate to sulfate, whereas an incomplete operon lacking SoxCD results in accumulation of zero-valent sulfur (ZVS), commonly observed in cold seeps81. SoxCD displayed low levels in Site 9.2 and comparatively higher ones outside of it. The opposite pattern took place with all other genes in the operon. This suggests that sulfur-oxidizing bacteria in Site 9.2 express the incomplete Sox pathway, enabling growth on abundant reduced sulfur and accumulating polysulfide in globules within cells or sediments. Outside site 9.2, SoxCD is overexpressed while other Sox complexes are less abundant, indicating sulfur oxidation is less prevalent due to lower sulfur concentrations.

Dissimilatory sulfate reduction (DSR) is a respiratory process where sulfate-reducing bacteria use sulfate (SO₄²⁻) instead of oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor, producing various reduced sulfur compounds82. DSR begins with aprA/B reducing APS to sulfite, followed by dsrA/B reducing sulfite to sulfide83. DSR is one of the main metabolic pathways present in Site 9.2, particularly in its subsurface, as it is an eminently anaerobic process. Significant sulfur cycling appears to be taking place in this site, where reduced sulfur is oxidized by thiotrophic organisms in the surface, through the SOX operon, and the resulting sulfates and thiosulfates are then reduced again through DSR in the subsurface68,79. However, the abundance of sulfur-related genes can't be explained exclusively through the cycling of sulfur compounds (Fig. 8A). Based on gene expression, community composition, and visual analysis, some limited amount of reduced sulfur appears to be locally seeping in Site 9.2, although the intensity of this seepage is unknown. Another possibility is that the seep itself might be extinct, and its microbial community is currently sustained by the cycling of previously assimilated reduced sulfur84,85.

Phage and toxin stress are common in the otherwise active and motile communities of the cold seep

Communities were generally highly motile (fliC) and actively growing (ftsF). This was particularly the case in the thiotrophic mats of Site 9.2 (Fig. 8B). The subsurface of deep sea sediments is generally anaerobic, and organic matter (OM) is consumed through oxygen-independent processes like fermentation and the respiration of nitrate, sulfate, and organohalides86,87. The catabolization of purines (yagS) and sugars (gmuG), and abundant fermentation through glycolysis (pyk, GAPDH) are common processes in the subsurface of our samples. Most genes involved in fermentation and in the catabolizing of OM in the subsurface of Site 9.2 were driven by unclassified Chloroflexota bacteria39,88. The abundance of genes involved in the maintenance of the redox state (msrB, qmoA) in the subsurface of site 9.2 also suggests that redox reactions play an important role in the metabolism of microorganisms within it.

Communities within Site 9.2 and its subsurface seem to be subjected to higher levels of phage and toxin-induced stress (Fig. 9). Although dinJ, an antitoxin gene involved in the general stress response89, was not abundant in any samples, genes involved in the degradation of antibiotics (pac) were present. There was also substantial secretion through the Sec pathway90 and the Type 2 Secretion System (T2SS, estimated via gspG)56. The abundance of secretion pathways in both the surface and subsurface of site 9.2 suggests that toxin secretion and antitoxin response are frequent processes in the microbial communities inhabiting this site. Cold seeps are known to harbor a high diversity of active viruses and prophages91 which can subject communities within them to substantial phage stress. This is further supported by the overexpression of the transcriptional activator of the psp operon (Phage Shock Protein) in the subsurface of Site 9.2.

Microorganisms in the sulfur oxidizing bacterial mat of Site 9.2 displayed moderately higher abundances of genes involved in motility, chemotaxis, and growth (Fig. 8B). Mats formed by Beggiatoa-like sulfur oxidizers are known to migrate long distances (in the 1–10 m range) over prolonged periods of time, and traverse varied microenvironments92. These microorganisms can store nitrate in vacuoles, which allows them to reduce sulfide in anoxic conditions for enough time to migrate to the oxic/anoxic interfaces where they thrive93. Motility and chemotaxis are then essential processes that enable sulfur oxidizing chemolithotrophs to carefully control their location and inhabit the optimal locations within the sulfide and oxygen gradients they inhabit. This strategy is very successful in these niches, and sulfur oxidizing communities can grow rapidly and continuously, which also explains the abundance of growth-related genes in the mat.

Pyrite and dolomite offer a glimpse into a more active, past seep

Dolomite precipitation in the modern ocean is rare and occurs only in very restricted microenvironments—this is known as the dolomite problem in the geological and geochemical literature94–97. One of the few environments where dolomite forms at the present-day seafloor is active cold seeps, where it appears as a cold seep carbonate (CSC), which is a byproduct of anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM) coupled to sulfate reduction (SR)69,94,98,99. Dolomite crystals were found only near the sediment/water interface at Site 9.2 (Fig. 2), supporting the idea that these carbonates precipitated in a reaction front where bicarbonate ions (HCO3−) produced through microbial metabolism reacted with Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions in seawater to form the dolomite crystals. Additionally, the presence of abundant pyrite crystals in subsurface layers (3–9 cm) alongside dolomite crystals just below the seafloor strongly supports the syngenetic precipitation of these minerals via carbon mineralization coupled to sulfate reduction. However, the absence of ANME archaea and genes involved in anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM) in Site 9.2 suggests methane is not a freely available energy source in these sediments, making the exact origin of the dolomite uncertain. Sulfate reduction itself can promote dolomite formation, however, through the local increase of alkalinity100,101. The apparent abundance of reduced sulfur—whether from seeping fluids or sulfate reduction—may also explain the presence of pyrite in the subsurface without invoking AOM-SR processes102. The sediments in Site 9.2 were the only ones to display detectable levels (~0.2 wt%) of sulfur when assayed through SEM-EDX. This sulfur was often associated with iron, likely in the form of iron sulfides like pyrite, and with barite (Supplementary Fig. 2). Iron sulfides are the easiest way for any seeping sulfur to remain in the sediments, as sulfates, thiosulfate, polysulfides and hydrogen sulfide are soluble in seawater and will not be detectable in the mineral phase of the sediments as easily103–105. Dolomite was found in a more advanced state of dissolution in PC7 in Site 9.2 than in PC9 in Site 9.2. PC7 was taken in a less active region, without a living clam bed, and little indication of any kind of seepage. PC9, on the other hand, was taken in the main site, next to the original clam bed. The presumed active seepage of reduced sulfur in the main clam bed may then support the active formation of dolomite through the previously discussed processes. The presence of partially dissolved dolomite in nearby sites could also suggest that the whole region, or at least specific parts of it, experienced more abundant seepage in the past.

Communities in the seep rapidly shift towards anoxic metabolisms as depth increases

Surface communities outside of the seep clustered together in all NMDS analyses and appear to remain relatively invariant over long distances and depths, as our samples were obtained over 30 km away from each other and 2000–4000 mbsl. The metabolism of microorganisms within these samples was driven by the degradation of recalcitrant nitrogenated organic matter and phytoplankton polysaccharides present in the marine snow that reached these depths, which is often heavily degraded16,106,107. The inflow of marine snow is also a constant in the surface of cold seeps, which explains why most taxa and functions present outside of the seep where also present within its surface. However, microorganisms and genes involved in the metabolism of sulfur, particularly on its oxidation, were very abundant in the immediate surface of the seep. The surface of the seep was then dominated by a combination of oxic metabolisms feeding off the abundant reduced sulfur present in the region and off the sedimented marine snow from the upper layers of the water column.

Small depth changes, however, led to drastic alterations in the communities and pathways present in the samples. Just 6–12 cm below the surface of the seep, processes dependent on oxygen are largely absent, suggesting that the sediment is primarily anoxic at these depths. The relative abundance of dissolved ammonium in the subsurface of the seep (Supplementary Table 4) further establishes it as a heavily reducing and anoxic environment. The absence of oxygen leads to the rise of a wide variety of oxygen-independent pathways, from the respiration of sulfate and nitrate to fermentation and methanogenesis60,99,108,109. The limited access to marine snow in the subsurface also led to a general downregulation of processes involved with the cycling of nitrogen and the heterotrophic fixation of CO2, which also enabled an abundance of lithotrophic carbon fixation pathways (CBB cycle, WL pathway, rTCA cycle) in the subsurface. Overall, communities shift to aerobic ones centered around the degradation of marine snow outside the seep, to mixed oxic communities feeding off marine snow and reduced sulfur in the surface of the seep, to anoxic ones driven by sulfate reduction, carbon fixation, and a wide variety of specialized pathways in the subsurface of the seep.

Intense sulfur cycling sustains specialized communities in a cold seep undergoing limited active seepage

The biological and geological features at Site 9.2 initially appeared compatible with a cold seep, as supported by the presence of symbiotic and chemosynthetic clams, darker sediment coloration, and widespread white bacterial mats. The detection of dolomite in upper sediment layers and pyrite in the subsurface first suggested abundant methane and sulfur in seeping fluids. However, the absence of ANME archaea and genes linked to anaerobic oxidation of methane, a hallmark of methane seeps, implies little to no active methane seepage. The presence of dolomite may alternatively be explained by the consistent, albeit low, abundance of methanogens in the subsurface, by alkalinization by sulfate reducers, or by limited methane seepage at some point in the past. While intense microbial sulfate-reduction in the region may partly explain the presence of thiotrophic mats and clam beds, it cannot fully account for the highly localized nature of the site. Based on the microbial communities and active metabolisms present, the site appears predominantly sulfur-driven, with little to no evidence of methane (Supplementary Fig. 4). The metabolic activity of much of the community within Site 9.2 may be sustained by microbial cycling of the abundant sulfur present, provided by past and present seepage in the region.

Communities in bathyal sediments of the continental slope near the Atacama Desert thus seem to depend on various sources of carbon and energy and are highly adapted to the niches they inhabit. These microbial communities experience significant shifts in composition and metabolic activity between sediments dependent on marine snow and those that inhabit what appear to be sulfur-based seeps at various stages of extinction. These seeps appear to be mainly driven by the cycling of sulfur and show little microbiological and mineralogical evidence of active methane seepage.

Methods

Sample collection

Sampling was conducted during Schmidt Ocean Institute’s expedition FK240524 aboard the R/V Falkor (too), using the Remotely Operated Vehicle (ROV) SuBastian. Samples were obtained under permit #E-2024-350 from the Subsecretaría de Pesca y Acuicultura of the Ministerio de Economía, Fomento y Turismo of Chile. Project name was PINV E-2024-153 “Caracterización de la biodiversidad bentónica”.

The sediment samples were collected from a cluster of sites located off the northern coast of Chile, spanning latitudes approximately 23.31°S to 23.83°S and longitudes 70.85°W to 70.95°W. Site 6 is located around 23.31°S, 70.95°W, at the deepest sampled waters, with sediment cores taken from 3882–3902 m depth. Site 9 and Site 9.1 are situated further north near 23.73–23.83°S, 70.85–70.93°W, with sampled depths ranging from 2387–2868 mbsl. Site 9.2 lies between the previous sites at approximately 23.60°S, 70.85°W, with sediment cores collected at 2840–2841 m depth. Overall, the sampling locations cover a latitudinal span of ~0.52° (~58 km) and a longitudinal span of ~0.10° (~11 km), across water depths ranging from roughly 2387 m to 3902 mbsl. A total of seven push cores were collected for metatranscriptomic analysis. Core sampling locations were chosen and coordinated through live video footage. Five cores (S9_Core14, S9.1_Core8, S6_Core3, S6_Core8, and S7_Core8) represented typical deep-sea sediments. Two additional cores were obtained from site 9.2 (S9.2_Core6 and S9.2_Core10), which was initially described as a potential cold seep based on visual analysis.

Cores (~20 cm in length) were recovered using a push-coring system operated by ROV SuBastian. They were extracted using a press and sectioned into 6 cm intervals. Each section was further divided into 10 g subsections and stored in 50 ml Falcon tubes. Sediments were either immediately frozen at −80 °C or resuspended in 20 ml of 99% ethanol or RNAlater before freezing at −80 °C and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. Select processed samples were transported on dry ice to the University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU) immediately after the expedition for geochemical analysis.

Mineralogical analysis

The mineralogy of the sediments was analyzed by X-ray Diffraction (XRD) on a PANalytical X’Pert Pro diffractometer (Malvern Panalytical, Almelo, Netherlands) and interpreted using High Score X’Pert software package at SGIker analytical services (UPV/EHU). The diffractograms were acquired in a continuous scan range of 5 to 70 °2θ with the step size of 0.01° and constant rotation. Working conditions included Cu Kα radiation generated at 40 kV, 40 mA, a programmable divergence slit, graphite monochromator, and a PIXcel detector. Subsequently, these were interpreted using HighScore X’Pert software package (Malvern Panalytical, Almelo, Netherlands) and ICDD database.

Trace elements in the sediments were measured by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) using a NexION 2000 equipment (PerkinElmer Inc., Shelton, Connecticut, USA) following a previous acid digestion at 220 °C in an Ethos Touch Control Screen microwave furnace. Samples were analyzed along with a digestion blank with forty-seven elements and a control standard to evaluate possible instrumental deviations. The analyses were always conducted under a KED mode (kinetic discrimination) to avoid polyatomic interference and to check the signal stability of all the elements during the validation of the working parameters.

Seafloor sediments were also characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at the Electronic Microscopy and Materials Microanalysis Service of the SGIker facilities of the University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU). These samples were previously washed with MilliQ water and then mounted on a 25 mm graphite stub, coated with a thin carbon layer, and analyzed with a JEOL JSM-7000F field emission scanning electron microscope (10 mm working distance, 20 kV, 1 nA) equipped with an ULTIMA MAX 65 SSD energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) system for semi-quantitative chemical analysis. The EDX was calibrated with the reference Cu foil. Raw X-ray intensity values were ZAF-corrected using the Aztec Live Nanoanalysis System software (Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK) with a set of standards for quantification.

SEM-EDX analysis was later carried out again at the Centro de Astrobiología to further describe the presence of sulfur species in samples from Site 9.2. Samples were lyophilized and homogenized in a mortar, then deposited onto conductive graphite stubs. Samples were then sputtered and coated with gold in a Bio-Rad SC 502 instrument to prevent charging under the electron beam and observed with a Scanning Electron Microscope JEOL JSM-5600 LV and an Electronic microscope XL30S FEG Philips, Netherlands (SEM-FI) with an acceleration voltage of 20 kV and a working distance of 20 mm. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) microanalysis using an INCAx-sight with a Si-Li detector (Oxford, UK) was used for qualitative elemental composition determination (detection limit of 10% of the main element). Lighter elements (C, O, and N) were detected by the INCAx-sight with a Si-Li detector of the instrument, and the quantitative numerical data of the spectra obtained were referenced as default to the higher peaks obtained in each spectrum (C in our case).

Porewater composition

Porewater was extracted from the sediment cores by adding set amounts (30 g) of sediment to 50 ml Falcon tubes and centrifuging for 10 min at 12,000 × g. This left 1–4 ml of clear and available porewater that was collected and frozen at −80 °C prior to further analysis. Nitrate and nitrite were initially quantified through MQuant® Nitrate Test strips (colorimetric, 10–500 mg/L (NO3−)) and REFLECTOQUANT® Nitrite Test strips (Reflectometric, 0.5–25.0 mg/L (NO2−)). Porewater was further analyzed through Ion Chromatography. Anions were quantified through an 861 Advanced Compact IC (Metrohm) chromatographer equipped with a Metrosep. A Supp 7–250/4.0 column, under a flow rate of 0.7 ml/min in 0.1 M Na2CO3. Cations were quantified through a 930 Compact IC Flex (Metrohm) chromatographer and a Metrosep C3 − 250/4.0 column, under a flow of 1 ml/min in 3.5 mM HNO3.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 5 g of sediment per sample using the Qiagen PowerSoil RNA Isolation Kit according to the manufacturer instructions. RNA of sufficient quality was recovered from the 0–6 cm sections of all cores. Due to a steep decline in microbial abundance with depth, RNA was not recovered below 6 cm in most samples. One core from site 9.2, where microbial abundance was higher, yielded RNA of acceptable quality in the 6–12 cm section.

Extracted quality RNA (A260/280 > 1.8, RIN > 7) was treated with Baseline-ZERO DNase (DB0711K, Epicentre). Libraries were prepared using the Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep, Ligation with Ribo-Zero Plus kit. Seven samples yielded high-quality data and were retained for downstream analysis. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq1000/2000 system using the P2 XLEAP-SBS 2 × 150 kit, generating an average of 100 million reads per sample. Read quality was assessed with fastp110, using a minimum length of 50 bp, a minimum base quality of 20, and a sliding window of 10 bp. Across all samples, 99% of reads passed quality control.

Metatranscriptome assembly and mapping

Functional and taxonomical analysis was performed using the SqueezeMeta v1.7.2 pipeline111 in co-assembly mode. Assembly was done using rnaSPAdes112 and short contigs (<200 bps) were removed using prinseq113. Following assembly, gene sequences were taxonomically classified with strict identity thresholds for species through superkingdom ranks. Contig statistics were obtained through prinseq, and RNAs were predicted using Barrnap113. 16S rRNA sequences were taxonomically classified using the RDP classifier114, and tRNA/tmRNA sequences were predicted using Aragorn115. ORFs were predicted through Prodigal116.

Taxonomical classification was done running Diamond against the GenBank nr (NCBI) database (Downloaded September 2024), a composite of GenBank, GenBank updates, and EMBL updates. The number of targets for DIAMOND global ranking during taxonomic assignment was 100. The minimum percentage of genes assigned to a taxon in order to assign it as the consensus taxonomy for that contig was 50%. Taxonomic assignment was performed against the GenBank NR database, which may overrepresent certain taxa. To mitigate potential database bias, we applied stringent alignment criteria and interpreted taxonomic results conservatively, emphasizing relative trends across treatments rather than absolute abundance estimates. Similarity searches for KEGG117 were done using Diamond118, and HMM homology searches were done by HMMER3119 for the Pfam database120. Read mapping against contigs was performed using Bowtie2121.

Pathway prediction for KEGG117 and MetaCyc122 databases was done using MinPath123. Taxon abundance was calculated based on the number of reads aligned to contigs associated with each specific taxon. The pipeline was run using 40 threads.

Statistical analysis

Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was calculated using the vegdist function from the R (v4.5.2) package vegan124 to quantify taxonomic and functional differences among samples. Statistically significant differences were assessed via PERMANOVA using the adonis2 function from the same package, with 999 permutations. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) plots were generated using the metaMDS function in vegan and visualized in RStudio. Shannon & Simpson Diversity and the Chao1 Index were calculated through the vegan package in RStudio.
