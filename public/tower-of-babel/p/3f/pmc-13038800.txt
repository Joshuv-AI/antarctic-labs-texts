Mechanoreceptor-mediated adaptations to physical exercise: from acute responses to long-term training effects
Authors: Emilie Maufroy, Nicolas Baeyens, Gael Deboeck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute exercise induces a marked increase in blood flow to active muscles, elevating mechanical forces on the vascular wall, most notably fluid shear stress and circumferential stretch. These stimuli are sensed by endothelial and vascular smooth muscle cells, triggering intracellular signaling cascades that, over time, drive vascular remodeling and long-term functional adaptation. Although growing evidence supports the role of hemodynamic forces in vascular remodeling, the precise mechanistic pathways and their relative contributions remain incompletely defined. This narrative review synthesizes current knowledge on peripheral vascular responses to exercise increased hemodynamic mechanical forces, with particular emphasis on cellular mechanotransduction. Further, it also describes long term vascular adaptations, which differ according to vessel type and function, thereby shaping overall vascular structure.

Introduction

Exercise is a critical hormetic stimulus that disrupts homeostasis and drives significant physiological adaptations through multiple regulatory mechanisms [1]. A key cardiovascular adaptation to exercise is the increase in cardiac output (CO), which ensures adequate oxygen delivery to meet the heightened metabolic demands of active muscles. This rise in cardiac output is closely coupled with a redistribution of blood flow, with skeletal muscles receiving up to 80–90% of total cardiac output during intense exercise, compared to only ~ 20% at rest [2, 3]. Beyond systemic adjustments, exercise imposes substantial hemodynamic and mechanical forces on the peripheral vasculature. The increase in muscle blood flow proportionally elevates vascular shear stress, the primary mechanical force sensed by endothelial cells, as well as circumferential stretch, contributing to vascular and microcirculatory adaptations [4]. These forces activate mechanoreceptors that regulate vascular tone and remodeling, playing a crucial role in both acute and long-term vascular health [5]. Acute responses to a single bout of exercise have been more frequently studied, particularly regarding the release of vasodilatory substances [6], vascular growth factors [7], and the mechanotransduction processes by which cells interpret mechanical stress [4]. However, long-term vascular adaptations are mainly reported through macroscopic changes such as improved tissue perfusion [8], increased capillary density [9], and larger vessel diameters [10]. In contrast, much less is known about the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying these structural adaptations. Despite these well-established physiological responses, the regulation of mechanoreceptors and their precise role in vascular adaptation remain poorly understood. Furthermore, few studies have investigated the chronic effects of exercise-induced hemodynamic stress on vascular remodelling.

Cardiac response to increase blood flow and adaptation

This section outlines the heart’s dynamic capacity to increase cardiac output in response to elevated metabolic demands during exercise, and the structural and functional adaptations induced by endurance training that enhance this capability. Although the primary focus of this review centers on vascular adaptation mechanisms, it is critical to first recognize that effective perfusion of peripheral tissues depends on the volume of blood ejected by the heart. During acute aerobic exercise, cardiac output increases in a near-linear fashion with rising exercise intensity, primarily driven by direct stimulation of the heart via the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) [11]. This neural stimulation triggers the systemic release of catecholamines, which accelerate heart rate and boost myocardial contractility [12]. Concurrently, venous return is markedly increased as a result of rhythmic skeletal muscle contractions, augmented respiratory activity, and a systemic decrease in vascular resistance [13]. The resulting increase in preload activates the Frank-Starling mechanism, thereby enhancing stroke volume and overall cardiac performance. Endurance training, characterized by repeated bouts of sustained aerobic activity, initiates a multifaceted cascade of mechanical, hemodynamic, and molecular stimuli that drive extensive cardiac and vascular remodeling. Persistent elevations in venous return and stroke volume impose a chronic volume load on the myocardium, leading to eccentric hypertrophy [14]. This physiological remodeling is characterized by longitudinal cardiomyocyte elongation and serial sarcomere addition, resulting in enhanced mechanical efficiency, preserved myocardial architecture, and the absence of pathological fibrosis [15]. Functionally, these adaptations enhance early diastolic filling by promoting myocardial relaxation and increasing preload, thereby improving cardiac efficiency and supporting elevated circulatory demands [16]. Meta-analytic data reveal significant increases in left ventricular mass (LVM) following endurance training, with detectable changes emerging as early as two weeks into a structured regimen [17]. In a landmark longitudinal study, Arbab-Zadeh et al. demonstrated that one year of progressive endurance training in previously sedentary individuals preparing for a marathon led to substantial LVM augmentation, approaching values observed in elite endurance athletes [18]. In addition to structural adaptations, regular aerobic training induces electrophysiological remodeling of the heart, characterized by increased heart rate variability (HRV) and a lower resting heart rate [19]. Elevated HRV, reflecting beat-to-beat fluctuations in cardiac cycle length, indicates enhanced autonomic regulation, whereas the decrease in resting heart rate is primarily attributable to an increased stroke volume [20]. At the molecular level, endurance training promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and modulates microRNA expression profiles associated with cardiac growth, metabolic regulation, and stress resilience, collectively reinforcing myocardial energetics and structural integrity [20, 21]. The transition from rest to exercise is accompanied by a profound redistribution of cardiac output, orchestrated through a complex interplay of central command, autonomic regulation, and local vascular phenomena [8]. SNS activation is pivotal in this process, promoting vasoconstriction within nonessential vascular territories such as the splanchnic and renal circulations, thereby channeling blood flow toward contracting skeletal muscle [11]. Despite the recognized importance of sympathetic drive, the precise mechanisms by which perfusion is selectively enhanced in active musculature remain a subject of ongoing debate. Two principal hypotheses have been advanced: one posits a contribution of sympathetic cholinergic vasodilation, particularly at the onset of exercise, while the other emphasizes regional sympathetic withdrawal as the dominant mechanism [2, 22]. Current evidence, however, increasingly favors the latter, highlighting the primacy of local metabolic control in overriding sympathetic vasoconstriction [23]. In exercising muscle, the accumulation of metabolites such as adenosine, nitric oxide (NO), prostaglandins (PG) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) elicits robust vasodilation [24]. Beyond their direct vascular effects, several of these mediators (adenosine and ATP) attenuate the constrictor influence of sympathetic outflow, a phenomenon termed functional sympatholysis [25, 26]. For a more extensive discussion of the mechanisms underlying exercise hyperaemia and the interplay between local metabolic control and sympathetic regulation, readers are referred to the comprehensive review by Joyner and Casey [2]. Importantly, the hemodynamic perturbations arising from blood flow redistribution during exercise are detected by the vascular wall, where they initiate complex signaling cascades that modulate vascular function. The capacity of the vasculature to sense and respond to these mechanical stimuli represents a critical determinant of both the acute adjustments observed during a single bout of exercise and the long-term adaptations elicited by repeated training. The mechanisms underlying this vascular mechanosensitivity will be discussed in detail in the subsequent section of this review.

Peripheral vascular response to exercise

During exercise, alterations in hemodynamic forces, including fluctuations in blood flow, pressure, stretch, strain, and compression, generate dynamic changes in the mechanical stress imposed on the vascular walls. Two primary forces generated from flowing blood are fluid shear stress (FSS), the frictional force exerted by blood flow on the vessel walls, and the circumferential stretch, which occurs as the walls are distended by the increased of blood pressure [27]. In this section, we will examine these two mechanical forces in detail, with particular emphasis on the processes of mechanotransduction and the vascular responses elicited by hemodynamic stimuli. FSS rises acutely during exercise and promotes greater expression and release of vasodilator mediators, including NO, PG, adenosine and ATP, via the activation of distinct signalling pathways [28, 29]. Due to its strategic localization along the luminal surface, the endothelial glycocalyx is optimally positioned to participate into processes of mechanosensation and mechanotransduction that govern blood flow-dependent shear forces [6]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that glycocalyx degradation provokes microvascular perfusion heterogeneity, thereby reducing capillary RBC flux and limiting oxygen extraction [30–32]. Elevated FSS deforms the glycocalyx and leads to the activation of cation channel Piezo1 channels and the complex composed of PECAM1, VEGFR2/3, and VE-cadherin and contributes to endothelial cell polarity by flow [5, 33]. Piezo1 activation promotes Ca2⁺ influx and the opening of non-selective cationic channels [34, 35]. The resulting Ca2⁺ entry initiates intracellular signalling cascades that stimulate NO release, which in turn relaxes vascular smooth muscle cells through the soluble guanylyl cyclase/cGMP/PKG pathway [36]. Rode and Beech reported that endothelial Piezo1 exerts vascular bed-specific effects, causing vasoconstriction in the mesenteric artery and vasodilation in the femoral [35] and saphenous arteries [37]. This heterogeneity may be partially attributable to the dominance of endothelium-dependent hyperpolarization (EDH) in mesenteric but not femoral arteries, linked to more efficient gap junction coupling between endothelial and smooth muscle cells in the mesenteric circulation [35]. More recently, Bartoli et al. [38] reported that endothelial-specific deletion of Piezo1 in mice impairs exercise capacity, accompanied by capillary rarefaction and a reduced pericyte-to-fiber ratio, indicative of compromised vessel stability. During exercise, Piezo1 acts as a central mechanosensor, regulating blood pressure, vascular tone, and blood flow redistribution [39]. Its deficiency results in impaired performance, skeletal muscle microvascular rarefaction, and reduced NO production, thereby compromising vasodilation and potentially promoting hypertension [40]. Beyond acute vasomotor regulation, Piezo1 activation by FSS is crucial for the induction of Kruppel-like factor 2 (KLF2) [27] and Kruppel-like factor 4 (KLF4) [41], transcription factors that orchestrate endothelial gene programs with anti-inflammatory, antithrombotic, and antioxidant functions. Deng et al. [42] demonstrated that FSS below the physiological threshold attenuates MEKK3/ERK5/KLF2 signalling, thereby relieving inhibition of Smad2/3, which translocate to the nucleus and initiate inward vascular remodelling. Consistently, Coon et al. [43] observed higher KLF2 expression in the thoracic aorta, characterized by laminar flow, compared to the aortic arch, where complex multidirectional flow predominates. Hemodynamic forces under unidirectional flow further promote expression of KLF2 and KLF4 activity, whereas this expression is suppressed by disturbed flow [41]. Although the precise mechanisms through which distinct flow patterns regulate KLF2 and KLF4 expression remain incompletely defined, current evidence underscores their pivotal role in endothelial protection against atherosclerosis. Collectively, Piezo1 emerges as a critical interface between vascular mechanics and exercise physiology. The endothelial junctional complex composed of PECAM1, VEGFR2/3, and VE-cadherin activation leads to the phosphorylation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and the subsequent production of NO. NO diffuses into vascular smooth muscle cells where it stimulates soluble guanylate cyclase, elevating cyclic GMP (cGMP) levels, and increased cGMP promotes smooth muscle relaxation, inducing vasodilation and enhancing blood flow to skeletal muscle [44]. Over the past decades, accumulating evidence has highlighted the role of these mechanotransducers: PECAM-1, VE-Cadherin, VEGFR2 [45], and VEGFR3 [46] in orchestrating vascular remodeling when chronically deviated from its defined threshold or “set point”. The”set point “ represents the physiological range of FSS within which endothelial cells maintain vascular integrity and remain in a quiescent state [47]. Deviations below this range trigger inward remodeling, whereas FSS exceeding the set point induces outward remodeling [48]. Recent work [46] demonstrated that VEGFR3 expression level modulate endothelial sensitivity to shear stress. Lymphatic endothelial cells, with higher VEGFR3 expression, exhibit a lower shear-stress set point than human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Manipulating VEGFR3 expression shifted this set point: reduction in lymphatic endothelial cells increased it, while overexpression in HUVECs decreased it. In vivo, reduction of VEGFR3 expression in zebrafish and adult mice led to arterial narrowing, underscoring its critical role in vascular adaptation. Complementary to this, the VE-PTP/Tie-2 axis acts as a mechanosensory system: venous endothelium, with low VE-PTP and high Tie-2, is more responsive to low FSS, while arterial endothelium, with high VE-PTP and low Tie-2, shows reduced sensitivity [49]. VE-PTP is thus proposed to represent another genuine regulator of the shear stress set point, alongside VEGFR3. The vascular set point is a critical determinant of remodeling initiation, with notable variation across vascular territories, suggesting vessel-specific regulatory mechanisms. Such heterogeneity implies that identical training stimuli may elicit divergent remodeling outcomes depending on the vascular bed involved, which presents a different set point. Furthermore, the set point threshold itself may be modulated by exercise, and differ between trained and untrained individuals (See Fig. 1). Therefore, comprehensive studies are required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and to establish the physiological relevance of this variability. FSS also modulates angiogenesis through vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), a central regulator of capillary formation. Elevated FSS enhances NO production, which subsequently upregulates VEGF expression [9, 50]. Galie et al. [51] further examined the role of FSS in angiogenesis using a microfluidic system that enabled precise control of blood flow around endothelial cells. Their study demonstrated that a FSS threshold exceeding 10 dyn/cm2 induces endothelial sprouting, independently of hydrostatic pressure or cell–cell junction integrity. This sprouting process requires activation of matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP-1), which is critical for extracellular matrix degradation and endothelial invasion. Notably, inhibition of VE-cadherin, which loosens intercellular junctions, does not alter the FSS threshold necessary for sprouting. Once initiated, sprouting must be sustained by continuous flow; otherwise, the newly formed vessels regress. Beyond its vascular responses, FSS also exerts significant effects on cellular metabolism. Yamamoto et al. [52] demonstrated that a 15-min elevation in FSS reduces membrane cholesterol via two complementary mechanisms: efflux, involving export to extracellular acceptors, and internalization, through endocytosis of cholesterol-rich domains. Collectively, these processes decrease membrane cholesterol content, enhance membrane fluidity, and stimulate mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, thereby increasing ATP production. These metabolic effects, observed in both venous (HUVECs) and arterial (HPAECs) endothelial cells, are reversible upon cessation of FSS. The second blood-related mechanical force is circumferential stretch, which can be augmented by both elevated intraluminal pressure and the relaxation of vascular smooth muscle cells [53]. Endothelial cells can sense this circumferential stretch, transducing the associated mechanical stress through diverse signalling pathways, including integrins, ion channels, G protein-coupled receptors, and receptor tyrosine kinases [28]. Activation of these pathways can induce increased expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), reactive oxygen species (ROS) and other peroxides [54], as well as adhesion molecules such as VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 [55]. The interpretation of the effects of circumferential stretch is inherently complex, as it simultaneously promotes the release of proatherogenic mediators (e.g., adhesion molecules and ROS) and antiatherogenic mediators (e.g., eNOS) [56]. Most experimental studies have examined the effects of sustained circumferential stretch, typically applied continuously over 24 h, and have consistently reported a predominance of proatherogenic signaling [4]. Such paradigms, however, largely mimic pathological conditions such as hypertension, where chronic mechanical overload drives maladaptive vascular remodeling. In contrast, physical exercise also imposes repeated circumferential stress, but in a temporally restricted manner, limited to a few hours. Within this specific physiological context, circumferential stretch appears to favor beneficial vascular remodeling, enhancing endothelial function and conferring protection against the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis [57]. Both FSS and circumferential stretch activates signaling pathways involving matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that modulate substrates relevant to angiogenesis (VEGF) [4]. VEGF, particularly its isoform VEGF-A, is a key proangiogenic factor produced during exercise. Multiple stimuli, including metabolic stress, increased capillary shear stress, NO production, and mechanical tension, contribute to the induction of VEGF-A expression in skeletal myocytes [4]. The production of VEGF-A is partially regulated by hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), which becomes activated under hypoxic conditions [58]. HIF-1α has been identified as a critical regulator of the “angiogenic switch,” orchestrating the balance between pro-angiogenic and anti-angiogenic molecules through its oxygen-sensitive transcriptional activity [59]. In addition, angiopoietins ANGPT1 and ANGPT2, ligands of the tyrosine kinase receptor Tie2, play central roles in regulating vascular stability and remodeling [60]. ANGPT1 reinforces endothelial junctions and pericyte interactions, thereby preserving vessel integrity and limiting permeability. By contrast, ANGPT2 antagonizes this effect, promoting vascular plasticity [7]. ANGPT2 is produced by endothelial cells, satellite cells, and myocytes, and its expression is induced by hypoxia, pro-inflammatory mediators and VEGF-A [7, 60]. Concurrently, exercise also triggers angiostatic mechanisms that counterbalance proangiogenic signals and prevent excessive vascular proliferation. VEGFR1, a competitive antagonist of VEGFR2 signaling, is upregulated following exercise, together with inhibitory factors such as endostatin and thrombospondin-1 (THBS1) [58, 7]. Tang et al. [61] demonstrated that insufficient VEGF dependent signaling-induces apoptosis in mouse skeletal muscle, underscoring VEGF as an essential survival factor for muscle capillarity. Several studies have further reported a peak in VEGF-A mRNA and protein expression up to six hours after exercise [45], accompanied by elevated levels of ANGPT2 and HIF-1α [7, 62]. While muscle adaptations to exercise are partly driven by mechanical stress, a range of biochemical and cellular mechanisms also play crucial roles [63]. In summary, current evidence identifies vascular mechanoreceptors as pivotal elements in detecting blood flow variations elicited by acute exercise. These hemodynamic disturbances initiate an immediate cascade of responses, including vasodilation, redistribution of blood flow, and release of angiogenic mediators. This short-term regulation not only ensures functional adaptation but also establishes the foundation for more durable transformations. The possibility that the shear stress set point evolves with training introduces the concept of progressive vascular plasticity, whereby the sensitivity of arteriolar, venous, capillary, and lymphatic networks adapts over time. Future investigations should determine whether vascular reactivity at a given workload differs between trained athletes and sedentary individuals, and whether the vascular system progressively recalibrates its threshold of perception and regulation (Fig. 1). Vascular remodeling to endurance training

In this section, we focus on training-induced adaptations, a central topic in integrative physiology. These adaptations illustrate the remarkable plasticity of biological systems under repeated mechanical and metabolic challenges. They manifest across multiple levels of organization, from intracellular molecular signaling to structural remodeling of vascular networks. Experimental and clinical studies have provided valuable molecular mechanisms insights underlying these responses and have highlighted the associated vascular adaptations, particularly the structural and functional remodeling of distinct vascular segments. However, despite these advances, important gaps remain regarding how long-term molecular changes ultimately drive and sustain vascular remodeling. The literature [8], notably the contributions of Thijssen and colleagues [4, 57], proposes a segmented approach that differentiates conduit arteries (diameter ≥ 1,000 µm), responsible for central blood flow transport; resistance arteries (300–1,000 µm), which regulate blood distribution and pressure; and the microcirculation, comprising arterioles (10–300 µm), capillaries (≈6 µm), and venules (10–300 µm), which serve as the primary site of gas, nutrient, and metabolic exchange. This classification provides a systematic framework for understanding the effects of training on vascular function, emphasizing the complementarity between early molecular adjustments and long-term hemodynamic and structural remodeling. Conduit arteries, including the popliteal, brachial, and femoral arteries, undergo both functional and structural adaptations in response to exercise and training stimuli. Tinken et al. [64] reported a biphasic vascular response in the brachial and popliteal arteries following eight weeks of lower limb exercise training. In this biphasic response, endothelial function, assessed by flow mediated dilation (FMD), exhibited a transient enhancement at two weeks, likely due to increased eNOS expression and NO availability [65], but regressed toward baseline by week eight. In contrast, arterial dilator capacity progressively increased throughout training, reflecting structural remodeling that restores shear stress homeostasis. In a subsequent investigation, Tinken et al. [66] demonstrated that brachial FMD acutely rose in response to thirty minutes of heating, handgrip exercise, and cycling, in direct proportion to antegrade shear rate. This effect was not present in the cuffed arm, thereby confirming FSS as the principal mechanistic stimulus. Supporting these observations, Birk et al. [67] showed that repeated elevations in antegrade shear during lower limb exercise augment brachial artery endothelial function even in non-exercising muscle beds, firmly establishing FSS as the predominant driver of conduit artery adaptation. Further evidence comes from handgrip training study [68] where acute bouts and eight weeks of bilateral training improved maximal voluntary contraction in both cuffed and non-cuffed arms. However, gains in FMD and peak blood flow were observed only in the non-cuffed arm, reinforcing the conclusion that vascular adaptations are mediated primarily by shear stress rather than muscle activation. These findings demonstrated that exercise induces temporally distinct vascular adaptations, beginning with an early enhancement of endothelial function and progressing toward structural remodeling of the vascular wall. These changes are not confined to the actively engaged musculature but propagate throughout the vascular system, underscoring the systemic nature of vascular plasticity. Longitudinal evidence further supports structural adaptation: aerobic training lasting 6 to 12 weeks enlarges femoral artery diameter [69], and trained individuals consistently display greater muscular conduit artery dimensions compared to sedentary controls [70]. Thijssen et al. [57] reported a dose dependent relationship between training volume and increased femoral artery FMD, accompanied by reductions in arterial wall thickness. Meta analyse [10] corroborate these findings, identifying a dose response relationship between aerobic exercise intensity and endothelial function, with clinically meaningful improvements (≈1% increase in FMD per 2 MET increment or 10% rise in relative intensity). Training modality also exerts a significant influence on vascular outcomes. Comparative studies indicate that high intensity interval training (HIIT, interval of low and high intensity) more effectively enhances conduit artery function, particularly brachial artery FMD, than continuous aerobic training (CT) [44]. A meta analysis by Ramos et al. [71] confirmed the superiority of HIIT over moderate intensity CT in improving macrovascular function, although variability exists depending on sample size and secondary endpoints (training modality). The “4 × 4” HIIT protocol, four bouts of four minutes at 85–95% HRmax, was particularly effective in augmenting endothelial function. In contrast, a 4 week HIIT program improved vascular function more than MICT without affecting arterial stiffness or blood pressure, yet after 12 months both interventions yielded comparable adaptations, indicating that HIIT’s induce early advantage [72]. Although the FITT paradigm (Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type) [73] provides a framework for tailoring exercise, the specific impact of intensity and duration on vascular remodeling driven by hemodynamic forces remains insufficiently explored and warrants further investigation. Resistance artery function is frequently evaluated using forearm strain gauge plethysmography, a technique that quantifies peak blood flow following the release of prolonged ischemia (≈15 min) or a combined ischemic and exercise stimulus [4]. Despite its widespread application, methodological variability across protocols poses challenges for direct inter-study comparisons [8]. Nonetheless, current evidence indicates that maximal post-ischemic blood flow in the lower limbs is markedly elevated in trained individuals relative to their untrained counterparts, reflecting structural vascular remodeling induced by exercise [8]. In rats, Laughlin et al. [74] demonstrated that endurance training enhances arteriolar density within skeletal muscle, with the extent of adaptation differing according to muscle fiber type. Subsequent transcriptomic analyses from the same research group revealed that both endurance and sprint training elicit distinct gene expression profiles across vascular territories, with divergent transcriptional responses observed between diaphragm arteries [75] and arterioles supplying locomotor muscles [76]. These molecular adaptations were accompanied by increased expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and elevated nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, collectively supporting enhanced vasodilatory capacity [75, 76]. These findings emphasize that vascular plasticity is not uniform but rather highly dependent on vessel identity, muscle phenotype, and the nature of the training stimulus. Translating such mechanistic insights to human physiology remains complex, owing to interspecies differences in muscle fiber composition and vascular architecture. Nevertheless, clinical data consistently associate physical inactivity with reduced capillary density and dysregulated vasoconstrictor signalling, including elevated levels of endothelin-1 (ET-1) and angiotensin II (ANGII) [57]. Resistance arteries are also key regulators of peripheral blood flow, primarily through the establishment of myogenic tone, the intrinsic ability of vessels to constrict or dilate in response to intraluminal pressure [77]. This property is largely determined by vascular smooth muscle cells, although the extent of endothelial involvement remains debated. Evidence from small femoral arteries indicates that endothelial removal does not alter myogenic responsiveness, pointing toward a structural basis for this regulation [78]. Conversely, age- and training-related differences in skeletal muscle arterioles appear to involve endothelial adaptations, with endurance exercise restoring myogenic responsiveness in aged rats and further enhancing it in young animals [79]. Additional work in a rat model of unilateral sciatic denervation revealed that aerobic exercise abolishes disparities between atrophic and contralateral limbs, underscoring the critical role of FSS and hemodynamic load in sustaining myogenic responsiveness [80]. However, as all evidence derives from rat models, extrapolation to human physiology must be approached with caution. Myogenic responsiveness has also been examined in porcine coronary arteries. Endurance training enhances myogenic tone [81], likely via protein kinase C (PKC)-dependent modulation of intracellular Ca2⁺ signaling and voltage-gated Ca2⁺ channels [82]. Subsequent investigations report increased responsiveness in trained swine, associated with higher L-type Ca2⁺ channel (LTCC) current density compared to sedentary controls [83]. These findings suggest that exercise-induced adaptations reinforce vascular myogenic mechanisms across species, with Ca2⁺ channel regulation emerging as a key determinant of vascular tone. In the human microcirculation, relatively few investigations have focused on arterioles and, in particular, venules, whereas the majority of studies have concentrated on capillary networks. Notably, the pioneering histological investigations by Hermansen and Wachtlova [84] first demonstrated that capillary density varies with training status, being lower in sedentary individuals than in trained subjects. Subsequently, Andersen provided evidence that endurance training effectively increases skeletal muscle capillary density [85]. Capillary angiogenesis, the formation of new capillaries from existing vessels, occurs via two mechanisms: sprouting and intussusception. Sprouting involves endothelial proliferation, basement membrane degradation, and tip cell migration, and has been observed during muscle contraction [7]. Furthermore, Hoier and Hellsten [45] proposed that sprouting is triggered by mechanical stretching of capillaries during muscle overload. In contrast, intussusception represents a flow-dependent angiogenic mechanism. It is characterized by the formation of intraluminal pillars composed of endothelial and perivascular cells, which progressively divide a single capillary into two distinct vessels. Unlike sprouting, intussusception requires minimal endothelial proliferation and does not involve basement membrane degradation. This mechanism contributes to increasing capillary number while preserving the hierarchical organization of the microvascular network [7]. Olfert and colleagues suggested that intussusception is induced by elevated FSS within the capillary lumen during hyperaemia. Morphologically, sprouting yields dense capillary loops, whereas intussusception produces long parallel vessels [86]. Aerobic exercise is believed to stimulate both these angiogenic pathways [7]. A recent meta-analysis by Liu et al. [87] examined skeletal muscle capillarization in healthy individuals. Across 57 trials from 38 studies (10% athletes, 90% sedentary participants), capillary-to-fiber ratio (C:F) was assessed in 391 subjects and capillary density (CD) in 428 subjects using histological cross-sections of muscle biopsies. Exercise training increased C:F by 33% and CD by 49.8 capillaries/mm2. The magnitude of adaptation was greater with high-intensity (54%) or moderate-intensity (21%) exercise compared to low-intensity training. Importantly, long-term training did not yield further increases in capillarization, suggesting a plateau effect. Jensen et al. [88] demonstrated in the vastus lateralis that HIIT induced endothelial cell proliferation and increased capillarization within four weeks, with no additional gains by week seven. Although endothelial proliferation returned to baseline thereafter, the capillary expansion was permanent and evenly distributed across type I and type II fibers. A recent study of ours [89] did not report a capillary angiogenic response; instead, it described vascular remodeling primarily driven by mechanical shear forces. Eight weeks of either CT or HIIT increased resting capillary diameter from pre- to post-intervention, yet only HIIT induced a pronounced increase in pericyte coverage around the capillaries. We interpreted this structural remodeling as a protective adaptation at the microvascular level, limiting excessive dilation during maximal exertion. This interpretation was further supported by a hemodynamic model. Lemieux and Birot [62] reviewed the effects of exercise under hypoxic conditions on skeletal muscle angiogenesis. They reported consistent increases in capillary density (CD) and capillary-to-fiber ratio (C/F), whereas VEGF expression was occasionally elevated above baseline but not consistently observed, and THBS1 responsiveness tended to normalize with repeated exercise. Altogether, these findings support the view that ambient hypoxia combined with exercise represents a stronger angiogenic stimulus than exercise alone. Data on hypobaric and normobaric hypoxia, predominantly derived from exercise studies, reveal significant changes in CD, C/F, and angiogenic factors. Notably, CD and C/F data are mostly derived from animal models, whereas serum/plasma analyses come from human studies. At the molecular level, acute exercise induces transient peaks in VEGF-A, ANGPT2, and HIF-1α [61, 90]. In human quadriceps, members of the MMP family display temporally distinct regulation: MMP9 expression rises after a single bout of exercise, whereas MMP2 and MMP14 are upregulated after 10 days of endurance training and remain elevated through 5 weeks, underscoring dynamic extracellular matrix remodeling during adaptation [91]. With prolonged training, however, the expression of MMPs, HIF-1α, VEGF-A, and THBS1 typically returns to baseline after 4–8 weeks, consistent with a long-term adaptive regulation of angiogenic signaling. In parallel, increased ANGPT1 levels in trained muscle [61], may reflect stabilization and maturation of the vascular network. Collectively, these findings indicate that exercise enhances CD and C/F, but angiogenic signaling evolves dynamically over time. While the acute responses of molecular signaling pathways to exercise have been extensively characterized, there remains a conspicuous paucity of studies examining how these pathways are modulated and reprogrammed during sustained training. Consequently, the temporal dynamics and adaptive remodeling of signaling cascades during prolonged exercise interventions remain largely unresolved. Moreover, comparisons between trained and untrained muscle suggest a physiological ceiling in vascular plasticity, highlighting potential limits to angiogenic adaptation in athletes. In summary, Exercise induces long-term systemic vascular adaptations, primarily mediated by shear stress and endothelial signaling; however, responses are vessel-segment specific, encompassing structural remodeling of conduit arteries, modulation of resistance artery tone, and capillary angiogenesis within the microcirculation. These segment-dependent adaptations are accompanied by distinct transcriptional profiles, indicating locally specialized regulatory mechanisms. The precise mechanistic link between acute exercise-induced signals and sustained vascular remodeling remains incompletely understood, and the preponderance of evidence derives from animal models, underscoring the need for mechanistic investigations in humans to elucidate the functional significance and molecular determinants of these heterogeneous vascular responses.

Perspective

Exercise induces systemic vascular plasticity through mechanosensitive signaling, with shear stress as a primary driver, leading to long-term structural and functional adaptations across the vascular tree. While acute responses highlight vessel-specific mechanoreceptor activation and rapid functional adjustments, chronic adaptations involve segment-dependent remodeling and angiogenesis. Furthermore, the timing of these long-term adaptations, as well as gene expression profiles, appears sensitive to FITT parameters, with different exercise modalities, intensities, and durations eliciting heterogeneous vascular responses. Key questions remain regarding how acute mechanosensitive signals translate into sustained structural change, how set points of vascular sensitivity evolve with training, and how these processes vary across vascular segments. Addressing these gaps in humans is critical to elucidate the molecular and functional determinants of vascular adaptation, optimize exercise interventions, and tailor training strategies to maximize cardiovascular and metabolic health benefits.
