Mutual interaction of the entomopathogenic and endophytic fungus Metarhizium anisopliae with zearalenone as a native component of crude Fusarium extract
Authors: Monika Nowak, Przemysław Bernat, Sylwia Różalska
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study revealed the consequences of the interaction of a widely used bioinsecticide and endophyte Metarhizium anisopliae with the hazardous mycotoxin zearalenone (ZEN) as a pure substance and with ZEN as a native component of a crude Fusarium extract. In the environment, microorganisms encounter a mixture of metabolites secreted by other organisms living in the same area, not single substances. The obtained results suggest that M. anisopliae, exposed to a variety of active substances produced by Fusarium graminearum, is able to eliminate ZEN. Within 14 days, M. anisopliae biotransformed 90.8% and 85.8% of ZEN as a pure substance and ZEN as a native component of the F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM), respectively, through reduction predominantly to α-epimers of zearalenols and zearalanols, considered more estrogenic than ZEN, which can raise concerns. Compared to pure ZEN, E-Fg-RM significantly affected the production of Metarhizium secondary metabolites by increasing the destruxins amount by approximately 20–25% and reducing the swainsonine content by 96.2%. All these findings provide a possible picture of the interaction of M. anisopliae with ZEN in the wild, mainly as a result of the use of crude extract from Fusarium, which contained a mixture of different metabolites.Keywords: Fusarium mycotoxins, Zearalenone, Metarhizium, Biotransformation, Secondary metabolitesSubject terms: Natural hazards, Fungi, Environmental biotechnology, Environmental impact

Introduction

Fusarium mycotoxins contaminate food and feed worldwide, posing a major threat to human and animal health and life due to their toxicity and high structural heterogeneity1,2. These fungi produce zearalenone (ZEN), trichothecenes such as deoxynivalenol, nivalenol, T-2 or HT-2, and fumonisins3–5. They also secrete polyketide pigments like naphthoquinones, which are frequently linked to Fusarium-infected crops6. Maize, wheat, and barley are most exposed to Fusarium mycotoxin contamination. The amount of mycotoxins in these plants can be up to a dozen or even tens of mg kg−11,5,7–10. Among Fusarium mycotoxins, ZEN is thought to be especially harmful. Consumption of ZEN-contaminated products can result in a number of disorders in animals and humans, including reproductive system disorders such as uterine enlargement, decreased fertility, increased embryo resorption, and changes in serum hormone levels, as well as immunotoxic, hepatotoxic, hematotoxic, and nephrotoxic effects11. ZEN can be removed by microbial biodegradation12,13, however, little is known about the ability of fungi coexisting with Fusarium to metabolize ZEN, and the possible consequences of this interaction are unexplored. Moreover, studies on the elimination of Fusarium mycotoxins by microorganisms mostly focus on single compounds14. To the best of our knowledge, there is no data on the removal of ZEN as a substance in its native form, naturally produced by Fusarium.

Entomopathogenic fungi belonging to the genus Metarhizium are capable of living in a variety of environmental conditions as well as in the presence of compounds harmful to other species due to their extremely flexible metabolism. They are soil-borne microorganisms involved in multitrophic interactions with pests and plants15. Due to their strong insecticidal properties, Metarhizium fungi are introduced into the environment as bioinsecticides16. These unique fungi can also form a mutually beneficial symbiotic relationship with the plant as endophytes. It has been proven that Metarhizium anisopliae can promote the growth of plants such as maize or wheat17,18. Since these fungi are widely distributed in the environment, they are exposed to the harmful effects of environmental pollutants. Metarhizium has been documented to deal with various contaminants, like xenobiotics, including 4-n-nonylphenol, or pesticides such as acetamiprid, ametryn, or atrazine through degradation, accumulation, and detoxification19–21,29. Therefore, we hypothesized that M. anisopliae, used in bioinsecticide formulations, would also metabolize zearalenone, with which it is in contact due to the colonization of a common niche with Fusarium species. Moreover, the question should have been asked whether there would be alterations in the secondary metabolism of M. anisopliae during such an interaction. Changes in the production of secondary metabolites by microorganisms used for the elimination of environmental pollutants are often overlooked, but they themselves can cause adverse effects. Like destruxins or swainsonine, which are products of the secondary metabolism of M. anisopliae, which are increasingly detected in plants, and the consequences of consuming them are not well understood22–24. Metarhizium secondary metabolites also play a role during the infectious process of insects, so any changes in their production may affect the ecological functions of these fungi25.

In this study, we analysed whether (1) M. anisopliae is able to metabolize ZEN, both during exposure to that naturally produced by Fusarium and as a pure substance; (2) M. anisopliae biotransforms zearalenone via reduction to zearalenols and zearalanols, which are metabolites with an altered estrogenic potential relative to zearalenone; (3) the extract of Fusarium comprising native ZEN and a pure substance affect the metabolic activity and permeability of cell membranes of M. anisopliae; and (4) a pure ZEN and the extract of Fusarium comprising native ZEN affect the secondary metabolism of the entomopathogenic fungus. Such a wide range of results allowed us to reveal the possible effects of the interaction of a widely used bioinsecticide and plant growth promoter with the toxic secondary metabolites of Fusarium contaminating food and feed globally.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Zearalenone, ɑ/β-zearalenol, ɑ/β-zearalanol, zearalanone and destruxin B were purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Destruxin A and swainsonine were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). All analytical standards were of a purity above 98%. Reagents and solvents for liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) and reagents not listed above were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Microorganisms and media

Metarhizium anisopliae ARSEF7487 (isolated from Schistocerca gregaria, Ethiopia) was obtained from the ARSEF collection (The Agricultural Research Service Collection of Entomopathogenic Fungal Cultures, USA). Fusarium culmorum DSM1094 (isolated from Triticum aestivum; Indiana, USA) and Fusarium graminearum DSM4527 (isolated from Zea mays, Germany) were purchased from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH.

Sabouraud Dextrose Broth (SDB) (BioMaxima, Poland) and Czapek Dox Broth (CDB) (BD-Difco, France) were used for the cultivation of M. anisopliae. Fusarium spp. were grown on self-prepared media. Two types of media were selected for Fusarium cultivation to verify whether there would be differences in the amounts of mycotoxins produced. The first one was a Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB), which was prepared according to the recipe described by Yokota et al.26 and the second was a Rice Medium (RM) according to Desjardins et al.27, both with slight modifications (Table S1).

Cultivation conditions of M. anisopliae ARSEF7487

After 14 days of cultivation on ZT agar slants (glucose (4 g L−1), Difco yeast extract (4 g L−1), malt extract (6° Blg) and agar (25 g L−1); pH 7), M. anisopliae was inoculated in SDB at a ratio of 1:9 and incubated on a rotary shaker (120 rpm) for 1 day at 28 °C in the dark. Then, 2 mL of the preculture was transferred to 18 mL CDB in 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks and the fungal cultures were incubated at the same conditions as a preculture.

This procedure was applied for the studies described in sections "Fungal biomass estimation of M. anisopliae", "Biotransformation of zearalenone by M. anisopliae and presence of mycotoxin in non-autoclaved and autoclaved mycelium", "Metarhizium anisopliae secondary metabolites (destruxins and swainsonine) estimation", and "Metarhizium anisopliae metabolic activity and permeability of cell membranes".

Cultivation conditions of F. culmorum DSM1094 and F. graminearum DSM4527

Potato Dextrose Broth

After 14 days of cultivation on Potato Dextrose Agar (BD-Difco, Le Pont-de-Claix, France) slants, the Fusarium cultures were inoculated in 20 mL of PDB. After 1 day of incubation, 10 mL of the precultures was transferred to 90 mL of PDB in 200 mL Erlenmeyer flasks and incubated on a rotary shaker (120 rpm) at 28 °C. After 21 days, the fungal cultures were filtrated through Whatman filter paper Number 1 with a pore size of 11 µm (Sigma-Aldrich). After filtration, 80 mL of the obtained culture medium was dispensed in equal volumes into four Falcon tubes, and the QuEChERS extraction from the culture medium was performed. Briefly, 20 mL of the culture medium with 10 mL of acetonitrile was vortexed at 3000 rpm for 1 min. Next, QuEChERS salts (4 g MgSO4; 1 g NaCl; 1 g C6H5 Na3O7∙2H2O; 0.5 g C6H6Na2O7∙1.5 H2O) were added to each sample, and the samples were again vortexed for 1 min. After centrifugation (10 min; 8000 rpm), the entire acetonitrile top layers were collected in one Falcon tube and evaporated to dryness under pressure of 5 hPa and at 40 °C. Next, the residues were dissolved in 1 mL of methanol, and the crude extracts were analysed with LC–MS/MS.

Rice Medium

After 14 days of cultivation on Potato Dextrose Agar, Fusarium spp. were inoculated in RM (1 slant with mycelium per 50 g of rice) in 200 mL Erlenmeyer flasks and then incubated at static conditions at 28 °C. After 21 days, 50 mL of methanol was added to each culture, followed by placing them on a rotary shaker (120 rpm). After 3 h, methanol was filtered through Whatman 1 paper with a pore size of 11 µm. Then, 50 mL of methanol was added again to each culture, and the procedure was repeated. From each culture, 80 mL of methanol extracts were obtained and evaporated to dryness under pressure of 5 hPa and at 40 °C. Next, the residues were dissolved in 1 mL of methanol, and the crude extracts were analysed with LC–MS/MS.

Preparation of zearalenone stock solution and Fusarium extracts for experiments with M. anisopliae

Mycotoxins solutions were prepared immediately before the cultivation of M. anisopliae. A stock solution of zearalenone (ZEN) was prepared in methanol at the concentration of 1 mg mL-1. The crude Fusarium extracts in methanol were first filtered through sterile PTFE syringe filters with a pore diameter of 0.2 µM (Sartorius, Germany) into a sterile tube for purification, particle removal, and sterilization. Then, the filtrates were diluted in methanol so that the concentration of ZEN in each of them was 1 mg mL−1. Finally, Fusarium extracts used in the experiments with M. anisopliae were standardized to the same amount of ZEN, i.e. 1 mg mL−1.

Fungal biomass estimation of M. anisopliae

Biotic controls of M. anisopliae (without ZEN or Fusarium extracts) and tested cultures of M. anisopliae (supplemented with pure ZEN, F. graminearum extract from RM (E-Fg-RM), F. graminearum extract from PDB (E-Fg-PDB), and F. culmorum extract from RM (E-Fc-RM) (all extracts contained ZEN as a native component); the final concentrations of ZEN in the cultures were 1, 0.5, 0.25, and 0.1 mg L−1 (doses detected in crop plants1) were cultivated for 24 h. For biomass estimation the fungus was separated by filtration and then dried at 105 °C to obtain a constant weight.

Biotransformation of zearalenone by M. anisopliae and presence of mycotoxin in non-autoclaved and autoclaved mycelium

For elimination and biotransformation experiments, abiotic controls of ZEN and F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) (without fungal biomass), biotic controls of M. anisopliae (without ZEN or Fusarium extract), and tested cultures of M. anisopliae (supplemented with pure ZEN or E-Fg-RM containing ZEN as a native component; the final concentration of ZEN in the cultures was 1 mg L−1) were cultivated at nine time points (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, and 14 day). The presence of ZEN in the non-autoclaved M. anisopliae biomass was determined after 1, 2, and 7 days of incubation.

For the sorption assay, after 4 days of incubation the biomass of M. anisopliae was separated from the culture medium by filtration through Whatman 1 paper with a pore size of 11 µm, rinsed three times with sterile water, and finally suspended in 20 mL of sterile water. The biomass prepared in this way was autoclaved three times at 120 °C for 20 min (2 bars). After returning to room temperature, ZEN as a pure substance and ZEN as a native component of E-Fg-RM were added to the cultures so that the final concentration of ZEN in the cultures was 1 mg L−1. The incubation times of the prepared cultures were 2 and 24 h.

The QuEChERS extraction procedure was performed for the whole fungal cultures (culture medium along with fungal biomass) in the elimination and biotransformation study, and separately for the biomass of M. anisopliae (washed three times in distilled water after filtration of the whole fungal culture through Whatman 1 paper with a pore size of 11 µm) and separately for the culture medium. Briefly, 20 mL of whole fungal culture or 20 mL of culture medium or fungal biomass suspended in 20 mL of water was added to 10 mL of acetonitrile and vortexed at 3000 rpm for 1 min. Next, QuEChERS salts (4 g MgSO4; 1 g NaCl; 1 g C6H5 Na3O7∙2H2O; 0.5 g C6H6Na2O7∙1.5 H2O) were added to each sample, and the samples were again vortexed for 1 min. After centrifugation (10 min; 8000 rpm), 1 mL of the top layer was collected for quantitative analyses of ZEN by LC–MS/MS. Additionally, 7 ml of the top layer was evaporated to dryness under pressure of 5 hPa and at 40 °C, and then the residue was dissolved in 1 mL of methanol for quantitative analyses of zearalenols and zearalanols by LC–MS/MS.

Metarhizium anisopliae secondary metabolites (destruxins and swainsonine) estimation

Biotic controls of M. anisopliae (without ZEN or Fusarium extract) and tested cultures of M. anisopliae (supplemented with pure ZEN and F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) containing ZEN as a native component; the final concentration of ZEN in the cultures was 1 mg L−1) were prepared. After 1, 2, and 7 days of incubation, the mycelium was separated from the culture medium by filtration through Whatman 1 paper with a pore size of 11 µm. The QuEChERS extraction procedure was performed separately for the fungal biomass and culture medium. After extraction, 8 mL of the top layer was evaporated to dryness under pressure of 5 hPa and at 40 °C, and then dissolved in 1 mL of acetonitrile for quantitative analyses by LC–MS/MS.

Metarhizium anisopliae metabolic activity and permeability of cell membranes

To assess overall metabolic activity and the permeability of fungal cell membranes during exposure to pure ZEN and ZEN as a native component of F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM), a metabolic activity test with fluorescein diacetate (FDA) according to the method of Różalska et al.28 and the permeability test with propidium iodide (PI) according to the procedure of Nowak et al.29 were carried out after 1, 2, and 7 days of incubation. The obtained results were defined as fluorescence intensity per g of dry weight. The final results were expressed in %, where the fluorescence intensity per g of dry weight for the biotic control was set at 100%. Then, it was calculated what percentage of fluorescence intensity per gram of dry weight was obtained for each tested sample in relation to the biotic control, representing 100%. Values below 100% indicated a decrease in the measured parameter, while values above 100% indicated an increase in the measured parameter.

Fusarium pigments assay

The total amount of naphthoquinone pigments was determined with the use of the method by Medentsev et al.30. The absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically at λ = 500 nm, and the value was converted by the coefficient of millimolar extinction 7.3.

LC–MS/MS methods

Quantitative analyses of zearalenone, α/β-zearalenol, α/β-zearalanol, and zearalanone

The analyses were conducted on a QTRAP 4500 mass spectrometer (Sciex, USA) coupled with the LC Agilent 1200 system (Agilent, USA) using the methods by Nowak et al.31. Details of the methods can be found in the Supplementary Data (Tables S2 and S3).

Quantitative analysis of destruxins A and B

Quantitative analyses of destruxin A (dtxA) and B (dtxB) were performed according to the procedure described by Nowak et al.29. Details of the method can be found in the Supplementary Data (Table S4).

Quantitative analysis of swainsonine

The analysis was conducted on a QTRAP 4500 mass spectrometer coupled with the LC Agilent 1200 system. Details of the method can be found in Supplementary Data (Table S5).

Statistical data

Fungal biomass estimation was conducted in four repetitions. Measurements of fungal metabolic activity and permeability of cell membranes were carried out in six repetitions. The other experiments were performed in triplicate. Variabilities of samples were given as standard deviations (± SD). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Tukey’s post hoc test were used for the assessment of statistical significance for normally distributed results. For results that were not normally distributed, the non-parametric equivalent of one-way ANOVA, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, and means were compared on multiple comparisons of the p-values. Statistical analyses were carried out using the Statistica 13.1 (StatSoft, Tulsa, OK, USA).

Results

Fusarium extracts composition

Fusarium extracts were analysed for the content of zearalenone (ZEN) and the total amount of naphthoquinone pigments. Fusarium species produced more ZEN in a Rice Medium than in a Potato Dextrose Broth (Table 1). Fusarium graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) was the most abundant in ZEN and naphthoquinone pigments. Five times less ZEN and almost 1.5 times less naphthoquinone pigments were detected in Fusarium culmorum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fc-RM) than in E-Fg-RM. After extracts standardization to ZEN at the concentration of 1 mg mL−1, the highest amount of dyes was detected for F. graminearum extract from Potato Dextrose Broth (E-Fg-PDB), then for E-Fc-RM and the lowest for E-Fg-RM (F. culmorum extract from Potato Dextrose Broth (E-Fc-PDB) was rejected due to the initial ZEN content below 1 mg mL−1) (Table 1).

Fungal biomass estimation of M. anisopliae

The growth of Metarhizium anisopliae after pure ZEN treatment was not inhibited (Fig. 1). The highest limitation of the strain growth was observed in the presence of F. graminearum extract from Potato Dextrose Broth (E-Fg-PDB) at all tested concentrations. At the concentration of 0.1 mg L−1, the growth was limited by 8.02 ± 2.27%, while at the 1 mg L−1, it was reduced by 59.04 ± 3.67%. F. culmorum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fc-RM) at the ZEN concentration of 1 mg L-1 inhibited the growth by 13.05 ± 2.61%, while at concentrations of 0.25 and 0.1 mg L−1, it enhanced the growth by 12.74 ± 2.85% and 18.40 ± 0.76%, respectively. Only F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) did not inhibit the growth of M. anisopliae. On the contrary, at concentrations of 0.5 and 0.25 mg L−1, it increased the growth by 8.75 ± 1.1% and 13.20 ± 0.79%, respectively.

Statistically significant differences within the type of treatment (ZEN, E-Fg-RM, E-Fg-PDB, E-Fc-RM) were determined between the untreated culture (biotic control) and the culture treated with pure ZEN or the obtained extract containing ZEN at concentrations of 1, 0.5, 0.25, and 0.1 mg L−1 and were marked as follows—* (p < 0.05).

Therefore, ZEN as a pure substance at the concentration of 1 mg L−1 and F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) with ZEN as a native component at the concentration of 1 mg L−1 were selected for further experiments.

Elimination of zearalenone in M. anisopliae and presence of mycotoxin in non-autoclaved and autoclaved mycelium

Metarhizium anisopliae exhibited potential in the ZEN elimination (1 mg L−1) regardless of the form of mycotoxin added to the growth medium (Fig. 2).Metarhizium anisopliae removed approximately 90.8% of ZEN from cultures treated with pure substance and approximately 85.8% of ZEN from cultures treated with F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) with native ZEN as a component during 14 days. The beginning of the ZEN elimination process was similar for cultures with pure ZEN and E-Fg-RM, and after 1 day of incubation, the highest loss of ZEN per day was observed at the first incubation time point (approximately 30%).

The elimination of zearalenone (ZEN) at the initial concentration of 1 mg L−1 by M. anisopliae in the cultures treated with pure ZEN (A) and crude extract of F. graminearum from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) (B). SSD within the time point between ZEN and E-Fg-RM samples—* (p < 0.05).

ZEN was absorbed by the non-autoclaved biomass of M. anisopliae (Table S6). Already after 1 day of incubation, 302.5 ± 18.8 and 281.6 ± 16 µg L−1 of ZEN remaining in culture were detected in fungal extracts from cultures treated with pure ZEN and E-Fg-RM, respectively. After 7 day of incubation, 106.0 ± 0.4 and 147.4 ± 1.1 µg L−1 of ZEN were detected in mycelial extracts from cultures treated with pure ZEN and E-Fg-RM, respectively, which, as a percentage of the total amount of ZEN remaining in cultures with pure ZEN and E-Fg-RM, accounted for 94.8 and 85.7%, respectively.

ZEN was also shown to be absorbed by the autoclaved mycelium of M. anisopliae (Table S7). After 2 h of incubation of autoclaved biomass with pure ZEN and E-Fg-RM with ZEN as a native component, 628.7 ± 4.0 and 602.6 ± 3.9 µg L−1 of ZEN (with the initial concentration of 1 mg L−1) were detected in biomass, respectively. After 24 h, the amount of ZEN in the autoclaved mycelium increased to 828.9 ± 21.0 and 760.9 ± 5.9 µg L−1 in cultures with pure ZEN and E-Fg-RM, respectively.

Biotransformation of zearalenone by M. anisopliae to zearalenols and zearalanols

Metarhizium anisopliae eliminated ZEN from the culture medium by reduction to zearalenols (ZELs) and zearalanols (ZALs) (Fig. 3). The reduction to ZELs was an intensive process occurring throughout the cultivation period (Fig. 3A,B). A further reduction to ZALs was also observed, but to a lesser extent (Fig. 3C,D). After 1 day of incubation, the percentages of individual hydroxyl derivatives in the amount of removed ZEN were as follows: 7.0% (α-zearalenol; α-ZEL), 4.6% (β -zearalenol; β-ZEL) and 0.003% (ZALs) for the pure ZEN-treated cultures, and 6.4% (α-ZEL), 6.0% (β-ZEL) and 0.008% (ZALs) for the F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM)-treated cultures. The most noticeable differences between the cultures with pure ZEN and E-Fg-RM were the lower amounts of α-ZEL, and the higher amounts of β-ZEL and ZALs. The level of ZALs was the highest in the cultures treated with E-Fg-RM throughout the incubation period (excluding the amount of α-ZEL and β-ZEL at 4 and 3 day of incubation, respectively). After 5 days of incubation, the content of ZELs in the M. anisopliae cultures with the addition of Fusarium extract was the highest compared to cultures with pure ZEN. The contents of α-ZEL and β-ZEL in the cultures treated with pure ZEN were not noted at the highest level at any incubation time point. At the end of the experiment, the hydroxylated derivatives constituted 2.7% and 4.1% of ZEN eliminated by M. anisopliae in the cultures treated with pure ZEN and those with ZEN as a native component of E-Fg-RM, respectively.

Effect of mycotoxins on the metabolic activity of M. anisopliae and permeability of cell membranes

The fungal metabolic activity increased in cultures supplemented with mycotoxins at each incubation time point (Fig. 4A). A significant increase in metabolic activity was noticed between 1 and 2 days of incubation by 18.7 ± 5.9% for the pure ZEN-treated cultures. The measurement of metabolic activity at 2 days of incubation in relation to the biotic control was 29.5 ± 3.9% higher. In cultures treated with F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM), no statistically significant differences in metabolic activity were noted between 1 and 2 days of incubation, while between 2 and 7 days, there was an increase in metabolic activity of approximately 26%. At 7 days of incubation, the metabolic activity of M. anisopliae in E-Fg-RM-treated cultures was higher by 42.9 ± 8.8% compared to the biotic control.

Statistically significant differences (SSD) were marked as follows: SSD for a type of sample (ZEN or E-Fg-RM) referring to the biotic control within the time point—* (p < 0.05), ** (p < 0.001); SSD within the time point between ZEN and E-Fg-RM samples—# (p < 0.05); SSD for a type of sample (ZEN or E-Fg-RM) between the time points: 1 d and 2 d–a; 1 d and 7 d–b; 2 d and 7 d–c (p < 0.05).

In the pure ZEN-treated cultures, no effect on the permeability of cell membranes of M. anisopliae at 1, 2 and 7 days of incubation was noted (Fig. 4B). A significant increase in cell membrane permeability was observed in M. anisopliae treated with E-Fg-RM at 2 day of incubation (by 40.0 ± 3.3% compared to the biotic control). At the last incubation time point, the situation stabilized and there were no statistically significant differences between the samples.

Production of M. anisopliae secondary metabolites upon exposure to Fusarium mycotoxins

Destruxins content

In the culture medium, statistically significant differences in the amounts of dtxs were observed at each incubation time point only for F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM)-treated cultures (Fig. 5D–F) (compared to the biotic control). Intriguingly, at 1 day of incubation, the contents of dtxA and dtxB in the cultures treated with E-Fg-RM decreased to 58.1 ± 10.1% and 52.0 ± 3.5% of the biotic control, respectively, but at subsequent time points, the trend changed towards enhancing dtxs production. The highest increase was recorded at 2 d, i.e. 3.1-fold for dtxA and 1.8-fold for dtxB (Fig. 5E).

Statistically significant differences (SSD) were marked as follows: SSD for a type of sample (ZEN or E-Fg-RM) referring to the biotic control within the time point—* (p < 0.05); SSD within the time point between ZEN and E-Fg-RM samples—a (p < 0.05).

More significant changes in the dtxs content were observed in the mycelium than in the culture medium (Fig. 5A–C). At 1 day of incubation, there was an increase in the concentration of dtxA and dtxB in the pure ZEN-treated cultures to 140.4 ± 6.1% and 126.9 ± 2.9% of the biotic control, respectively, and their reduction in the cultures with the addition of E-Fg-RM to 85.1 ± 5.1% and 43.0 ± 4.9% of the biotic control, respectively (Fig. 5A). At 2 d, the content of dtxA and dtxB significantly multiplied in the mycelium to 163 ± 20.9% (dtxA) and 176.4 ± 2.8% (dtxB) in the pure ZEN-treated cultures and to 381.6 ± 3.1% (dtxA) and 459.8 ± 56.8% (dtxB) of the biotic control in the cultures treated with E-Fg-RM (Fig. 5B).

At 7 day of incubation, there were no statistically significant differences in the content of dtxs between the biotic control and the cultures with pure ZEN in both the culture medium and mycelium, while in those with ZEN as a component of E-Fg-RM, there was still an increase in their amounts, i.e. 1.2-fold for dtxA and 1.4-fold for dtxB in the mycelium and 1.3-fold for dtxA and 1.2-fold for dtxB in the culture medium.

At 1 day of incubation, the amount of swainsonine was 1.3-fold and 1.2-fold higher in comparison to the biotic control in pure ZEN and E-Fg-RM-treated cultures, respectively. The addition of E-Fg-RM caused a 15-fold decrease in swainsonine concentration in the mycelium of M. anisopliae after 2 days of incubation. Discussion

While the addition of Fusarium extracts to the cultures of Metarhizium anisopliae with ZEN as a native component at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 1 mg L−1 significantly affected the growth of the entomopathogenic fungus, no significant differences were observed after the treatment with pure ZEN. The naphthoquinone pigments content was a factor that significantly differentiated the obtained extracts of Fusarium species standardized against amount of native ZEN at the concentration of 1 mg L−1. These pigments are known to exhibit phytotoxic, insecticidal, antibacterial, and fungicidal activities32. In Fusarium graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM), which did not inhibit the growth of M. anisopliae, the pigments content was 4 and 2.6 times lower than in F. graminearum extract from Potato Dextrose Broth (E-Fg-PDB) and Fusarium culmorum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fc-RM), respectively, which limited the growth of the fungus by almost 60% and 13%, respectively. Thus, the growth of M. anisopliae was not limited by ZEN but could have been influenced by substances additionally produced by Fusarium, including naphthoquinone pigments.

According to the results of our research, M. anisopliae has the capacity to eliminate zearalenone. Importantly, the fungus eliminated ZEN as effectively when exposed to pure substance as when treated with F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) with ZEN as a native component. The scientific research indicates the possibility of removing ZEN by fungi and bacteria14,31,33–37, but only in the case when the cultures are treated with pure substance. We proved, that ZEN was also removed by M. anisopliae after culture supplementation with F. graminearum crude extract with ZEN as a native component at the same concentration as the synthetic compound. In the environment, microorganisms are not exposed to the action of single substances, but to the mixture of metabolic products of other organisms living in the same area. ZEN accompanied substances in E-Fg-RM only slightly slowed the elimination of mycotoxin—by approximately 7 and 5% at 7 and 14 day of incubation, respectively, compared to pure ZEN-treated cultures. The obtained results suggest that M. anisopliae, exposed to the mixture of active substances produced by F. graminearum in the natural environment, may also be able to eliminate ZEN.

Metarhizium anisopliae converted ZEN by reduction to hydroxylated derivatives. α-zearalenol and β-zearalenol are formed via reduction of the C6’-ketonic carbonyl group of ZEN, followed by a reduction of zearalenols to α-zearalanol and β-zearalanol2. In the present study, M. anisopliae converted ZEN to a higher amount of α-ZEL than β-ZEL (Fig. 3). As it is commonly known, the estrogenicity of the α-epimers is higher than ZEN estrogenicity, in contrast to the β-epimers, which have a similar or lower estrogenic potential than the parent compound38,39. Such a ratio of α-ZEL to β-ZEL did not disturb the metabolic activity of M. anisopliae. Quite the opposite, at 1 and 2 days of incubation, where the content of hydroxylated derivatives was high, the fungus showed increased metabolic activity compared to the biotic control, and this effect was also maintained in a 7-day culture. This means that the presence of hydroxylated derivatives with a predominance of α-epimers did not slow down the overall metabolic activity of M. anisopliae. It is worth emphasizing that the high content of hydroxyl ZEN derivatives did not last throughout the entire cultivation period, undergoing further, undetermined changes. Considering the situation where the mycotoxin was added to the culture once in a certain amount, this effect could be thought of positive and would mean that M. anisopliae would not circulate a metabolite with greater estrogenic potential as it was further transformed. However, the question arises as to whether the constant contact of the biocontrol agent with zearalenone will not result in a permanent biotransformation of the mycotoxin to a metabolite that poses a greater risk to human and animal health and life. Undoubtedly, this issue requires further research.

It is also worth referring to the presence of ZEN in the fungal cells. ZEN and its metabolites accumulate in plant cells and tissues, and consequently also in human and animal cells, through ZEN-contaminated plant food product consumption40. ZEN was also detected in its unchanged form in the post-cultivation extract of non-autoclaved and autoclaved M. anisopliae biomass. By culturing ZEN with M. anisopliae autoclaved biomass, the strong binding of ZEN to the surface of fungal cells was demonstrated. For non-autoclaved biomass, it was not confirmed whether ZEN binds to the cell surface or accumulates inside the cells. Only after 2 days of incubation the permeability of M. anisopliae cell membranes in the presence of F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) increased by 40.3% compared to the biotic control, which may have allowed ZEN to penetrate into the mycelium.

The determination of the effects of Fusarium metabolites on the secondary metabolism of M. anisopliae is an emerging issue. Potentially efficient elimination of Fusarium mycotoxins by entomopathogenic fungi can cause interference with the production of secondary metabolites in M. anisopliae, whose toxic effects have also been demonstrated41.

Undoubtedly, the most numerous group of secondary metabolites of M. anisopliae are destruxins (dtxs), cyclohexadepsipeptides with α-hydroxy acid and five amino acid residues in their structure42. Apart from the insecticidal properties, in which dtxA, dtxB, and dtxE have the greatest contribution, these compounds exhibit a number of other properties, and their production does not take place only in direct contact with the insect41. What’s more, dtxs are increasingly detected in plants, and the effects of consuming them are not well understood22,24. According to Nowak et al.29, acetamiprid, which is one of the world’s leading chemical insecticides, significantly inhibits the production of dtxs in Metarhizium species29. Since destruxins are extracellular metabolites, their content in the culture medium is more relevant. In this study, the secretion of dtxs by M. anisopliae to the culture medium after exposure to pure ZEN was not disrupted, while the use of the F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) with ZEN as a native component influenced dtxs production. The initial inhibition of dtxs production in the cultures treated with E-Fg-RM was not dependent on ZEN but probably on the other components of the extract. However, referring to the results from 2 and 7 days of incubation, the situation was reversed, and higher contents of dtxs were determined in cultures treated with E-Fg-RM. It could be of a two-fold nature. From one perspective, increased production of dtxs may result in stronger insecticidal properties of M. anisopliae. However, on the other hand, more of these compounds may enter plants and thus the food chain, with unexplored consequences. The issue of dtxs production by Metarhizium during exposure to various environmental factors seems to be an interesting research thread that needs to be explored.

Swainsonine is an indolizidine alkaloid with α-mannosidase inhibitory activity43,44. It is associated with a plant-derived secondary metabolite, however, it is produced by endophytic fungal species, including M. anisopliae. Grazing animals can develop a chronic neurologic disease characterized by weight loss, depression, changed behavior, libido disorders, infertility, birth defects, and death by consuming swainsonine-contaminated plants45,46. On the other hand, swainsonine has antimetastatic, anticancer, or immunostimulant activity, but its insecticidal properties still remain unexplored47,48. In this research, pure ZEN had no effect on the production of swainsonine by M. anisopliae, while F. graminearum extract from Rice Medium (E-Fg-RM) led to an almost complete reduction of swainsonine production, as its content increased 3.5 times between 1 and 7 days of incubation, whereas there was a 107-fold increase in the biotic control. From the point of view of the losses in the inventory that may result from animal diseases triggered by eating plants rich in swainsonine secreted by M. anisopliae45, the inhibition of its production during exposure to Fusarium mycotoxins may have a positive effect. In the face of the role of swainsonine for the fungus itself, which is still an unexplored issue, the consequences of the inhibition of its production by Fusarium mycotoxins are also unknown, so this gap in knowledge needs to be addressed in future work.

Metarhizium anisopliae, which is used to control crop pests and as a growth promoter of crops exposed to contamination with Fusarium mycotoxins, can effectively remove zearalenone contaminating food and feed globally. Importantly, M. anisopliae eliminated ZEN both as a native component of Fusarium extract and as a pure substance. However, the conversion of ZEN to reduced α-epimers, which show increased estrogenic potential compared to the initial compound, may be of concern. Studies revealed that the secondary metabolism of M. anisopliae was impaired during exposure to Fusarium mycotoxins, the consequences of which require further clarification.
