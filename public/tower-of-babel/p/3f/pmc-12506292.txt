Distribution of Mukawa virus (Phlebovirus mukawaense) in Russia
Authors: Anna S Gladkikh, Alena V Dereventsova, Tatiana M Ianshina, Ekaterina O Klyuchnikova, Valeriya A Sbarzaglia, Alena A Sharova, Anastasia S Gritseva, Margarita R Popova, Tatiana V Arbuzova, Edward S Ramsay, Alexander S Klimentov, Ivan S Kholodilov, Galina G Karganova, Vladimir G Dedkov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhleboviruses, a diverse group within the Phenuiviridae family, include mosquito-, tick-, and sandfly-borne viruses, several of which are pathogenic to humans and animals. Among them, Phlebovirus mukawaense (formerly Mukawa virus, MKWV), a tick-associated member of the Phlebovirus genus, exhibits features, indicative of an emerging arbovirus with potential pathogenicity for humans and animals. This study investigated the distribution, genetic diversity, and host/vector interactions of MKWV across Russia. It identified two genotypes: the previously described Mukawa genotype and the newly characterized Gomselga genotype. This study presents complete genomic sequences of ten MKWV isolates from Russia and analyzes their phylogenetic relationship with other known isolates. The isolates share 88–89% nucleotide identity, and 97–98% RdRp amino acid identity, with the MKWV reference strain (MKW73). This places them within the same species under current ICTV classification criteria. Genetic distance correlated with geographic origin for the L and M segments. The S segment, particularly the NSs gene fragment, showed less regional variability. The findings suggest that previously described Gomselga viruses in Russia are part of the MKWV species and highlight the virus’s broader geographic distribution and genetic diversity across East Asia and Europe. Segment reassortments were observed both within the Gomselga genotype and within the Mukawa genotype. While the Mukawa genotype replicates in mammalian cells, the Gomselga genotype does not. This indicates possible differences in host tropism or pathogenic potential. Despite these differences, both genotypes have evolutionary features consistent with arboviruses, including reassortment and geographical clustering of genomic variants. Although vertical transmission in ticks remains unproven, the detection of MKWV in multiple tick species and vertebrate hosts supports the likelihood of vector-borne transmission. These findings suggest that MKWV is a probable arboviral pathogen with an extensive geographic range and potential public health relevance. Introduction

Phleboviruses are members of the large Phenuiviridae family (order Hareavirales, class Bunyaviricetes). The family includes over 159 species, grouped into 23 heterogeneous genera, some of which can cause serious disease in humans and animals [1]. Among the various phenuiviruses, illness in vertebrates and humans has primarily been associated with mosquito-borne phleboviruses. Such cases can range from mild influenza-like forms to severe meningoencephalitis, with responsible pathogens including: the Toscana, Sicilian and Naples phleboviruses; Rift Valley fever virus; etc. Among tick-borne, disease-causing phenuiviruses, several are noteworthy: Bandavirus dabieense (severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus, SFTSV); Bandavirus heartlandense (Heartland virus); and Bandavirus bhanjanagarense (Bhanja virus) [2, 3].

The genus Phlebovirus includes 67 species and a large number of unclassified viruses infecting mammals, including livestock and humans. They are transmitted by mosquitoes, phlebotomine sandflies, or ticks. Viruses classified in the genus Phlebovirus form a monophyletic cluster distinguished from other phenuiviruses [4]. According to the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), the species demarcating criteria in the genus is less than 95% identity by RdRp amino acid sequence [1].

The phlebovirus genome encompasses three single-stranded RNA segments encoding four structural proteins: RNA dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp); two external glycoproteins (Gn, Gc); and a nucleocapsid protein (N). It also encodes two non-structural proteins (NSs, NSm) which have been recognized as a significant virulence factor due to their ability to counteract host innate antiviral responses [5]. However, some phleboviruses do not encode NSm in the glycoprotein precursor [1].

Phleboviruses are viruses of blood-feeding arthropods such as ticks, mosquitoes, and sandflies. Some phleboviruses belong to the group known as arboviruses, which are transmitted between mammals through the bites of blood-feeding arthropods. Some, such as the mosquito-borne pathogen Phlebovirus riftense, are associated with human disease. Some phleboviruses appear to circulate in arthropod populations. High-throughput sequencing has revealed a significant number of tick phleboviruses that lack an identifiable M segment. These viruses have been classified into a separate group called the M segment-deficient group [6, 7]. Several aspects remain unclear: whether this characteristic is specific to tick viruses, implying a unique mode of existence for phleboviruses; whether the M segment is present in undetectable quantities; or if M segments of these viruses are so different from known viruses that it is impossible to match them with the identified S and L segments. It is also unclear whether these viruses are capable of replication in vertebrate cells.

A novel virus of the Phenuiviridae family, Phlebovirus mukawaense (formerly Mukawa virus, MKWV), was first isolated in 2018 from Ixodes persulcatus ticks in Mukawa (Hokkaido, Japan) using tick cell line SE6 [8]. By phylogenetic reconstruction, Mukawa virus clusters next to mosquito/sandfly-borne phleboviruses. Despite that, molecular footprints of viral proteins and biological characteristics have defined MKWV as more like a tick-borne phlebovirus [8]. MKWV features unique properties. On the one hand, it is a tick virus. On the other, its proximity to pathogenic mosquito viruses may also indicate pathogenicity.

Not much is known about the pathogenic potential of the newly discovered phlebovirus. The virus can infect Vero cells successfully, indicating the ability of MKWV to replicate in mammalian cells [9]. The first identified strain, MKW73, replicated in Huh7 cells up to titers of 107 50% tissue culture infectious doses (TCID50)/ml. Inoculation of 3-week-old C57BL/6J mice was followed by deaths of all infected animals, with postmortem detection of replication in brain and spleen. This indicates that MKWV can replicate in mice and systematically spread within the body [8]. In experiments with adult animals, no clinical manifestations of MKWV infection were shown, but viral RNA was detected in some tissues, but not in whole blood [10].

Mass surveillance of ticks in China [9] has revealed MKWV from I. persulcatus, Haemaphysalis concinna, and Dermacentor silvarum (GenBank IDs: ON408126 - ON408128, direct submission). MKWV sequences have also been obtained from I. persulcatus ticks in Russia (GenBank IDs: PP525061 - PP525081, direct submission). A serological survey in wildlife identified the presence of neutralizing antibodies against MKWV in both Yezo deer and raccoons captured at the initial location of discovery in Hokkaido [10]. There is a single strain isolated from human liver, without a case description (GenBank IDs: PP999500 - PP999502, direct submission). Several findings indicate that P. mukawaense is a pathogen with underestimated epidemic potential: an ability to replicate in mammalian cells and mice; routine detection of the virus in ticks; and presence of antiviral antibodies in wild animals.

So far, MKWV has been obtained from ticks in Japan [8], China [11], and Russia. Regarding MKWV genomes currently available in GenBank (as of 10/01/2025), there are only 17 complete (or near-complete) genomes and 19 partial genomes (with fragments of all three segments deposited). Considering the wide geographic distribution of other tick-borne phenuiviruses, MKWV may also be widely spread in other regions.

Previous reports have described several newly found phleboviruses in Russia. One of them, termed ‘Gomselga virus’, has been found in the Republic of Karelia, the Republic of Tuva, the Chelyabinsk region [12–14], and the Irkutsk region (GenBank IDs: PP554667 - PP554678, direct submission). The virus was identified based on L segment fragments. Based on 88% pairwise nucleotide sequence identity, it was historically considered a new species (Gomselga).

In this study, we determined the complete genomes of the previously described viruses and one new isolate from the Republic of Karelia found in the same location seven years later after the first finding. Based on phylogenetic analysis, we show that these viral sequences belong to a single P. mukawaense species with two genotypes. We also describe the spatial variability of the virus.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and tick identification

In this study, we used tick suspensions from specific regions containing previously described viruses for which an L segment fragment (~ 500–600 nt) had been previously sequenced [13, 14]. The associated areas were the Republic of Karelia, the Republic of Tuva, and the Chelyabinsk region. The viruses are described in Table 1; Fig. 1. In addition, seven I. persulcatus ticks collected in the Republic of Karelia were subjected to deep shotgun sequencing in 2021. Ticks were collected by flagging from vegetation (flag size: 0.6 m × 1.0 m), as described earlier [15]. The identity and sex of ticks were determined based on morphology following earlier recommendations [16]. Ticks were homogenized individually using a Bioprep-24 mechanical homogenizer (Allsheng, China) for 1 min in 400 µL of phosphate buffered saline (pH 7.0, Dako, Denmark) before extraction. Following centrifugation (13,000 g for 2 min), supernatants were used for nucleic acid extraction.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription

Total nucleic acids were extracted and purified using the RIBO-prep DNA/RNA extraction kit (AmpliSens®, Moscow, Russia) according to manufacturer recommendations. DNA/RNA was eluted with 50 µL of elution buffer (AmpliSens®, Moscow, Russia). Viral RNA was reverse transcribed by first-strand cDNA synthesis using the NadPrep Total RNA-To-DNA Module (Nanodigmbio (Nanjing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Samples of cDNA were stored at − 70 °C until amplification.

Genome enrichment, library preparation, and sequencing

An overlapping primer panel for P. mukawaense genome enrichment was designed with primerscheme tool v.1.4.1 [17]. PCR amplification of the entire genome constituted a mixture of S, M, and L segments; primers were multiplexed to make three amplification pools (10 pmol/mL). PCR conditions for amplification featured 35 cycles using a 60 °C annealing temperature. Reactions were performed in a C1000 Touch thermocycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). PCR amplification products (~ 500 bp) were analyzed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. Products were purified using AMPure beads (Beckman Coulter, UK) and prepared for high throughput sequencing.

Library preparation was performed according to the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA Kit protocol with the TruSeq DNA CD Indexes Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). For shotgun metagenomics, libraries were prepared with the KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit (Kapa Biosystems Pty (Ltd), Cape Town, South Africa). Quality assessment of final libraries was carried out on the QIAxcel Advanced capillary system (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Fragment sizes (amplicon insert plus sequencing adapters) were about 620–650 bp. The obtained libraries were quantified, normalized, pooled, and loaded onto an Illumina MiSeq platform with 300 bp paired-end reads. The Miseq reagents kit v3 (600 cycles) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Genome assembly and phylogenetic analysis

Quality assessment of raw sequencing reads was performed using FastQC v.0.12.1 [18]. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed with BBDuk v39.01 [19] to remove contaminants and improve read quality. De novo assembly was conducted using SPAdes genome assembler [20]. Contigs were screened for viral sequences using BLASTn against the NCBI nucleotide (nt) database [21], with matches retained if they exhibited an e-value < 1e−5 for taxonomic affiliation.

Cleaned reads from enriched genomes were aligned to the MKW73 reference genome, the first described Mukawa virus, detected in Hokkaido, Japan [22]. Bowtie2 v2.5.3 [23] with parameters optimized for high sensitivity was used. Variants were identified using FreeBayes v1.3.7 [24] and filtered to retain high-confidence variants with BCFtools v1.19 [25]. Consensus sequences for each segment were generated, masking regions of low coverage. Ambiguous positions were substituted using samtools consensus v1.20 [26]. Genomes were annotated using vigor v4.1.20200702 [27]. Sequences were deposited in the NCBI database under the numbers PQ204551-PQ204580.

Viral genomic segments were aligned using MAFFT v7.520 [28], and alignments were refined (removing poorly aligned regions and gaps) with trimAl v1.4. rev22 [29]. In addition, complete or partial M, L, and S segment sequences from 33 P. mukawaense isolates were extracted from the NCBI database for further analysis. Kuriyama virus, a tick-borne phlebovirus isolated in Hokkaido Island (Japan) near the location where the MKW73 strain was isolated [30], was taken as an outgroup for rooting phylogenetic trees as a most closely related neighbor to MKWV. After trimming, alignment lengths were: 914 bp for L segment; 2,879 bp for M segment; and 1,438 bp for S segment. Phylogenetic trees for each segment were constructed using IQ-TREE v2.3.4 with automatic model selection mode [31] and 1,000 bootstrap replications. Trees were visualized with the iTOL v6.9.1 [32] and R packages.

Pairwise sequence similarity was analyzed using the dist.dna function of the ape package in R. Standard parameters and the evolutionary model “K80” for genetic distance calculation, as well as simple counting (model = “raw”) for sequence similarity, were used. Matrices of pairwise distances from DNA sequences were visualized as a heatmap using the heatmap package in R.

Segment reassortment

Segment-wise phylogenetic comparison was carried out with a patristic-distance clustering workflow applied to the maximum-likelihood consensus trees that had been reconstructed separately for the S, M, and L MKWV coding regions. For each tree, patristic distances (the sum of branch lengths between every pair of tips) were extracted using BioPython 1.79 to build a full distance matrix. Clustering was performed with the TreeCluster (max_clade) algorithm. The optimal number of clusters for the L segment was determined by maximizing the Calinski–Harabasz index in the range 2 ≤ k ≤ 8. The resulting k was then imposed on S and M for consistency across segments. After pruning the outgroup (Kuriyama virus strain CZCT80Q), clusters with fewer than three isolates were merged into their nearest larger neighbor based on mean patristic distance, preserving monophyly throughout. Because no bootstrap filtering was applied, all resolved branches contributed to the distance matrix and fine-scale splits were preserved. Each pair (segment, cluster) was treated as a node. Every genome therefore generated three segment-to-segment links that record how its cluster assignment changes between the S, M, and L topologies. Nodes and weighted links were rendered with the Chord element of Holoviews v1.17 running on the Bokeh 3.1 backend.

Probable recombination events

A test for probable recombination events was performed with SimPlot v3.5.1.0 [33] and with the Recombination Detection Program (RDP) v4.101 [34] using eight methods provided by the software.

Amino acid sequence similarity

To calculate the number of substitutions in amino acid sequences, Python v.3.9 and the Seaborn library were used to generate plots. MKW73 isolate sequence was used as a reference group.

Infection of mammalian cell lines

Phleboviruses-positive tick suspensions were utilized for virus isolation. Homogenate derived from these ticks was filtered and subsequently used for inoculation of Vero and Huh-7.5 cells using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) Supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2mM L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (GIBCO). Cells were maintained under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. One hour post-inoculation, the medium was replaced, and cells were further cultured at 37 °C for six generations, each lasting 3 days. Analysis was conducted with subsequent validation using a real-time system [35].

Results

Genomic characterization of new phlebovirus isolates

In the isolates analyzed, the complete sequence lengths of L, M, and S segments were 6297, 3292, and 1870 nucleotides, respectively. ORFs found in segments correspond to: RdRp for L segment; pre-glycoprotein polyprotein GP complex (Gn, Gc) for M segment; and nucleocapsid protein (N) and non-structural protein (NSs) for S segment.

Complete L segment sequences had 88–89% similarity with Mukawa virus L segment sequences in GenBank. Translated amino acid sequences had 97–98% similarity with RdRp protein of known Mukawa viruses. Phleboviruses from the same tick suspensions were previously designated as ‘Gomselga viruses’ based on 500–600 bp RdRp fragments (Table 1). Current ICTV demarcation criteria within the genus Phlebovirus (95% RdRp aa identity) do not allow differentiation of the analyzed isolates into another species.

For phylogenetic analysis, we analyzed a 914 nucleotide L segment fragment due to a lack of complete segment sequences for the majority of submitted isolates from Russia. During analysis, some sequences were labeled as Gomselga virus, and some as Mukawa virus, according to the original names deposited in the NCBI database.

When analyzing nucleotide similarity in isolate genomes, a correlation was observed with the geographic distance from the sites of virus detection. L segment sequence fragments of samples from China, Primorye Territory, Trans-Baikal Territory, and one isolate from the Republic of Tuva share > 91% nt similarity based on pairwise similarity analysis of trimmed sequences with the reference Japanese isolate MKW73 (Fig. 1). Russian isolates, those from the Irkutsk region and one isolate from the Republic of Tuva share less than 90% L segment similarity with MKW73.

Like the L segment, M segment sequences featured high similarity. When comparing M segment nucleotide sequences with the reference isolate (MKW73), sequences from China, Primorye Territory, Trans-Baikal Territory, and isolate Tuva-3 (Republic of Tuva) have similarity above 90%. With the exception of isolate T28815, which has 89.5% M segment similarity with MKW73, the remaining available MKWV sequences from Russia feature 86–88% M segment similarity with MKW73.

By S segment, available MKWV sequences from Russia have similarity above 90% with MKW73; isolates achieved in this study have similarity 91–95% with MKW73. Thus, the correlation between sequence identity and geographic location for the S segment is much lower than for the L and M segments. By S segment fragment encoding nonstructural protein NSs, MKWV isolates from China, Primorye Territory, Trans-Baikal Territory share identity above 90% with MKW73. Sequences of NSs gene from the Republic of Karelia, Chelyabinsk region, and the Republic of Tuva have 88–92% similarity with MKW73.

Kuriyama virus is the most closely related to MKWV. Regarding identity with all MKWV strains/isolates, it has: 84–85% nucleotide sequence identity by L segment; 83–85% identity by M segment; and 78–80% identity by S segment.

Phylogenetic analysis of Mukawa virus sequences

Phylogenetic reconstruction by MKWV L segment indicated the presence of two distinct clusters. The first includes isolates previously named as ‘Mukawa viruses’ from Japan, China, Trans-Baikal Territory, Primorye Territory, and the Republic of Tuva. The second includes isolates from Russia previously named as ‘Gomselga viruses’ as well as newly sequenced isolates obtained in this study. The formation of two distinct clusters suggests the existence of two different genotypes. One of them (Gomselga genotype) has been identified only in Russia so far. The other (Mukawa genotype) circulates in Russia (Asian part), China, and Japan. In the Gomselga cluster, two subclusters can be derived (Fig. 2). One subcluster includes isolates from the Irkutsk region and the Republic of Tuva. The other subcluster includes isolates from the Chelyabinsk region, the Republic of Karelia, and one isolate from the Republic of Tuva. Isolates NE-ShL2 (from a D. silvarum tick) and 346–6443 (from a H. concinna tick) group together with isolates from I. persulcatus ticks. A newly deposited isolate from human liver, Nac-Org5, groups tightly with isolates from I. persulcatus ticks, but published data is not available about this case.

Within this cluster, one can notice a grouping of isolates: most isolates from Russia are grouped together to form a subgroup with two isolates from Heilongjiang province (China).

Phylogenetic analysis of MKWV by S segment again identified the same. Gomselga and Mukawa clusters (Fig. 4). The Irkutsk-4 Gomselga virus isolate is located in the Mukawa genotype subcluster next to isolates from the Republic of Tuva and Primorye Territory. Within the Gomselga cluster, sequences from the Republic of Tuva and Irkutsk region formed separated subclusters. Sequences from Chelyabinsk region and the Republic of Karelia grouped together. The incongruence of phylogenetic tree topology by each segment indicates multiple reassortment events for MKWV segments. The unique topologies revealed by segment (L, M, S) suggest reassortment by one or two segments in the majority of samples. Reassortments were observed both within the Gomselga genotype and within the Mukawa genotype. Clustering based on patristic distance matrix shows that the clusters formed for each of the segments are not congruent with those for other segments (Fig. 6).

In the Gomselga genotype, all isolates from the Irkutsk region and the Republic of Tuva are grouped together (with the exception of isolate T28607) by L segment. Isolate T28607 (Republic of Tuva) is grouped together with the strain from the Irkutsk region by M segment, while the other isolates from the Republic of Tuva form a separate group. By S segment, isolates from the Irkutsk region (except Irkutsk-23) clustered together, close to the cluster of isolates from the Republic of Tuva. By S segment analysis, the Irkutsk-4 isolate was localized within the Mukawa genotype. S segment reassortment between the Gomselga and Mukawa genotypes supports our statement that they are genotypes of the same species. Moreover, probable S segment reassortment events in strains/isolates belonging to the Mukawa (pathogenic) and Gomselga (unclear pathogenicity) genotypes may lead to the appearance of Gomselga variants with a high zoonotic potential.

In the Mukawa genotype, there were also multiple reassortments. Isolates from Primorye Territory, which grouped together by M segment, were divided into different groups by L and S segments. Reassortments were also noted among isolates from China: strains YKS1, NE-TH3, and 346–6446, which grouped together by L segment, did not group together by M or S segments. The position of the reference strain (MKW73) also changes depending on segment (Fig. 6).

The cluster map derived from patristic-distance partitioning (Fig. 7) departs repeatedly from the S, L, and M segment patterns. This pattern matches the branching order seen in the individual trees. The Chelyabinsk/Karelia Gomselga isolates form S4 and align cleanly with L2, while the Irkutsk/Tuva sequences appear as S2 and match L1. The largest Mukawa S3 cluster (12 isolates spanning Chita, Primorye, Heilongjiang) breaks down into 2 clusters (S3 ↔ L4 and L5). Kuriyama CZCT80Q retains an isolated position in all three segment trees and anchors the diagram as the self-connected S7–M7–L7 triad. Gomselga Irkutsk-4 from L1 appeared in the Mukawa M3 and S1 clusters (Table 2).

Analysis of protein-coding sequences

Amino acid sequence substitution patterns were analyzed for the ten isolates obtained in this study. The length of the L segment coding region encoding RdRp was 2098 aa, in which 80 positions had substitutions relative to the reference strain MKW73. The similarity of samples from different regions to the reference, and to each other, was higher than 99% only in the 1000–1400 aa region. For the L segment, higher variability was observed in the 100–1000 aa range. The maximum differences were observed for Gomselga genotype samples from the Republic of Karelia.

The length of the M segment coding region encoding the MKWV glycoprotein precursor was 1051 aa, with 81 positions having substitutions relative to the reference. For the M segment, variability was observed for the fragment encoding G1 protein. The G2 region was more conserved. Isolates from the Republic of Karelia and the Chelyabinsk region featured the lowest similarity values with the reference group. Russian isolates of MKWV do not encode NSm in the glycoprotein precursor, which is consistent with the data received earlier for MKWV isolates from Japan and China.

The length of the S segment coding region encoding nucleocapsid was 247 aa, in which 2 positions had substitution relative to the reference. The similarity of samples from different regions to the reference, and to each other, was close to 100%. Only two mutations in the protein sequence between different regions were identified.

The length of the S segment coding region encoding the nonstructural protein was 340 aa, and 66 positions had substitution relative to the reference. The similarity of samples from different regions to the reference drops sharply in the 180–220 aa interval. The lowest similarity for all isolates was at the end of the protein, with the lowest values for MKWV isolates from Karelia and the Chelyabinsk region (Fig. 8). Evaluation of replication in mammalian cell lines

Five virus-containing tick suspensions collected in Russia were used to assess viral replication in mammal-derived cell lines (samples IDs: T27359, GOM-Che-T22634, GOM-Che-T22645, T27077, T28815). The suspentions were used to inoculate Vero cells. Viral replication was monitored for a maximum of 21 days. During this period, supernatant samples were collected every three days (starting immediately after inoculation) to track potential viral replication. Huh-7.5 cells were used for inoculation with sample T27359; supernatant samples were collected three times every two days. No cytopathic effects (CPEs) were observed in the inoculated cultures. The collected supernatants were subsequently tested using real-time PCR to detect the presence of phlebovirus-specific RNA. Gomselga virus replication was not detected by real-time PCR in any mammal-derived cell lines.

Discussion

Like the pathogenic Rift Valley virus, or other sandfly/mosquito-borne phleboviruses, MKWV can infect wild animals through tick bites [21, 22]. The habitat of ticks is extremely wide and covers almost the entire territory of Eurasia. Ticks are distributed throughout Russia, where there are about 60 species of ixodid ticks. In 2023, more than 450,000 people in Russia sought medical help after a tick bite [36]. A large number of phenuiviruses are now being discovered [13, 37–42]. Many of them are tick-specific viruses, but some of them may be arboviruses pathogenic to humans. It cannot be ruled out that tick-specific viruses may interfere with some viruses pathogenic to humans or, conversely, contribute to the spread of already known arboviruses, as has been shown for orthoflaviviruses [43].

NGS is a powerful tool for identifying latent viral diversity in different ecosystems. Through the use of metavirome and screening approaches, dozens of new viruses have been identified in recent years. Some of them feature latent pathogenic potential. Sequencing technologies are a large-scale and reliable tool for unveiling viral diversity in different vectors. An example of this are viruses of the genus Phlebovirus. Although MKWV was detected on Hokkaido Island (Japan) in 2018, its range is currently unclear [29]. Several factors make it a potentially active infectious agent: the previously discovered ability to replicate in mammalian cells; the presence of antibodies in animals; and transmission by vector [8, 44]. The existence of GenBank MKWV sequence for isolate NAC-Org5 (Jilin Province, China) from human liver spheroids indicates potential ability to infect humans. MKWV is likely capable of transmission to mammals through tick bites, with subsequent infection. This possibility is supported by viral growth in a human-derived cell line and mice, as well as by the anti-innate immune function of NSs in human cells. MKWV NSs significantly blocked beta interferon promoter activity induced by RIG-I signaling, but not ISRE activity, while SFTSV NSs inhibited both [8].

Sequences of a new phlebovirus, termed Gomselga virus, have been detected in Russia since 2012 [13, 14]. The M segment of these viruses has not been previously identified through Sanger sequencing. For some time, they were assumed to be M-segmentless viruses, unable to replicate outside insect cells [11, 45].

The approach of screening with further enrichment and NGS sequencing applied in this study enabled us to obtain ten complete genomes of the novel phlebovirus. Genomic fragments of the novel phlebovirus from Siberia and the Russian Far East were obtained earlier by other researchers and deposited in GenBank (NCBI). This made it possible to conclude that Gomselga virus is a variant of the Mukawa virus species according to ICTV criteria. This assertion is also supported by signs of reassortment events between the Gomselga and Mukawa genotypes. As such, it is possible to assume that variants of Mukawa virus are widely distributed in Russia, and their spatial features may coincide with those of its vectors.

A previous study analyzed adult ticks of various species, including Ixodes persulcatus, I. persulcatus, Ixodes ricinus, Dermacentor reticulatus, D. marginatum, D. silvarum, D. dagestanicus, Hyalomma scupense, H. marginatum, R. rossicus, and Haemaphysalis punctata. Ticks were collected in 16 Russian regions and were tested for the presence of the Gomselga virus [13–15]. To date in Russia, we have detected this virus only in ticks of the genus I. persulcatus, the most widespread species of ixodid ticks in most Russian regions. However, the Mukawa genotype has been found in ticks of other species (D. silvarum, H. concinna) in China. This suggests a wider, undetected viral prevalence. In addition, nothing is known at the moment about the prevalence of the virus in Europe due to the spread of ixodid ticks in Northern and East Europe.

Multiple sequence alignment was performed on each of the three MKWV segments showing genomic variability varying from segment to segment. At the amino acid level, the greatest variability was observed in the first half of RdRp sequence, including the catalytic domain. In the M segment, variability was higher in G1 glycoprotein, rather than in G2. The S segment was the least variable. The nucleocapsid protein amino acid sequence is the least variable among representatives of different genotypes, with synonymous substitutions present. Nonstructural protein shows high variability, which may correlate with pathogenic potential. The MKWV S segment fragment encoding nonstructural proteins (NSs fragment) has been found in all tick-borne viruses, but is absent in insect/sandfly-borne viruses. It features the highest homology with infection-causing tick-borne viruses [22].

Based on L segment fragments, MKWV identified in China were separated into three lineages, all of which were independent of the Japanese MKWV lineage [9]. Based on an expanded sample set, and previously obtained data on the detection of a Mukawa-like phlebovirus in Russia, we assert the presence of two genotypes (Mukawa and Gomselga), both of which circulate in Russia. The presence of two genotypes is confirmed by their grouping into separate clusters by all three segments. Phylogenetic clustering of strains by geographical location is seen, although not in every case. The similarity of L and M segment sequences with the reference in Russian isolates gradually decreases with geographical distance from China/Japan.

Previous studies have shown that the Mukawa virus is capable of replicating in mammalian Huh 7 cells [8]. However, our study suggests that the Gomselga virus does not exhibit the same replication ability under similar conditions. This difference may represent a key distinguishing characteristic between the two viruses, potentially reflecting variations in host adaptation or viral life cycle. Undoubtedly, studying the replication characteristics of newly identified potentially pathogenic viruses and their genotypes is a matter of priority. Further investigations, including expanding the range of cell lines and the sample of strains, will be provided. It is required to determine whether this distinction influences the ecological niche, transmission dynamics, or pathogenic potential of the Gomselga virus.

Multiple probable reassortment events of several viral segments were detected. As with many segmented viruses, reassortment is characteristic of genus Phlebovirus members and has been shown previously. Reassortment provides a number of advantages to viruses. These include allowing the “correction” of deleterious mutations and the formation of maximally adaptive viral forms.

Many segmented RNA viruses have the evolutionary benefits of genetic recombination: reassortment enables the ‘repair’ of deleterious mutations and provides access to extended sections of the genome that carry specific, established functions, which is unachievable through mutations alone [46]. RNA segment reassortment (exchange of a whole segment) occurs when cells are coinfected by two closely related viruses of the same genus or serogroup [47]. Reassortment between viral strains has been demonstrated experimentally in tissue cultures [48] and insects [49]. Naturally occurring reassortants have been suggested or demonstrated for phenuiviruses, previously called bunyaviruses [50–52]. Due to the lack of a sufficient number of complete MKWV genomic sequences, we had to use incomplete L segment sequences to construct phylogenetic trees in this study. This may have affected tree topology or reliability. Thus, the possibility of MKWV reassortment events requires further thorough research.

Based on data from other studies, including descriptions of the Mukawa virus circulating in China and Japan (Mukawa genotype), it can be said with some confidence that MKWV is a probable arbovirus according to several criteria. These include the facts that: it has been repeatedly detected in various tick species; there is a case of isolation from human tissues; and specific antibodies have been registered in mammals. To meet all of the criteria for classification as an arbovirus, the possibility of vertical transmission in tick populations, and the possibility of vector-borne transmission, must be demonstrated. Vertical transmission in ticks has not yet been shown experimentally, but the wide distribution of MKWV, and the presence of the virus in ticks of different species, indicate that it is an arbovirus since transmission between ticks of different species is most likely possible through a vertebrate host.

To date, it has not been possible to culture the Gomselga genotype in mammalian cells, and there are no cases of detection of this genotype in animals. This is probably due to peculiarities of this genotype, and it does not support an assertion of pathogenicity, but additional studies are required. However, reassortment could not have taken hold if there were only vertical transmission in ticks. The presence of reassortment in both genotypes is evidence in favor of the existence of a transmissive pathway. The co-circulation of two genotypes in a single vector or host may contribute to the emergence of highly adaptive virulent forms of the virus. As such, the possible emergence of a new viral variant with higher virulence for humans and animals cannot be ruled out and may pose a potential threat to public health.

Conclusion

The recently discovered novel phlebovirus, P. mukawaense, seems to be an arbovirus with a wide range. At the moment, at least two genotypes (Gomselga, Mukawa) exist within MKWV. The Mukawa genotype has been identified in China, Japan, and the Asian part of Russia. The Gomselga genotype has been identified only in Russia (both in the Asian and European regions). Taking into account that the main vector is I. persulcatus, the distribution of MKWV may be much wider and overlap with the area of its vectors. Geographic divergence of Mukawa virus genomes has been observed. We assert that the newly identified Mukawa virus likely represents an arboviral pathogen with unknown epidemic potential. Understanding its epidemiological features can help in the future development of diagnostic tools and prevention of human infections.
