The electrophysiological basis of resting-state fMRI hyperconnectivity in early Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Laure Spruyt, Tjaša Mlinarič, Nathalie Dusart, Mariska Reinartz, Marc M. Van Hulle, Stefan Sunaert, Koen Van Laere, Patrick Dupont, Rik Vandenberghe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveOne of the most intriguing findings in Alzheimer’s disease connectivity studies is resting-state fMRI hyperconnectivity in the preclinical and prodromal phase. We examined how this phenomenon relates to electrophysiological connectivity.MethodsResting-state fMRI, 128-electrodes EEG, and amyloid PET data were collected from 41 cognitively unimpaired amyloid negative, 17 cognitively unimpaired amyloid positive, and 14 prodromal Alzheimer’s disease participants. Based on the Shen50 atlas, global fMRI network measures – characteristic path length and global clustering coefficient – were calculated using the absolute values of the partial correlations as weights. EEG-based global graph measures were calculated for delta, theta, alpha and beta bands using the average undirected weighted Phase Lag Index in sensor space as weights. We assessed between-group differences in resting-state fMRI and in EEG connectivity and examined the associations between fMRI-derived and EEG-derived global graph measures across the delta, theta, alpha and beta bands.ResultsIn line with prior findings, prodromal Alzheimer’s disease patients showed decreased fMRI-derived characteristic path length. This correlated positively with EEG-derived characteristic path length within the delta band across the total group and within the cognitively unimpaired amyloid positive and prodromal Alzheimer’s disease subgroup. Conversely, in the alpha band, global clustering coefficient was decreased and the characteristic path length increased. No correlation was observed between either of these two graph measures in the alpha EEG band versus the corresponding resting-state fMRI connectivity measures.InterpretationIn conclusion, the electrophysiological correlate of the hyperconnectivity measured with resting-state fMRI is situated in the low-frequency delta band while the alpha frequency band shows opposite effects unrelated to resting-state fMRI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-02003-w.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease is a neurodegenerative disease leading to cognitive and functional impairments that have been attributed to a cascading network failure [1].Cognition results from an optimal cooperation of local information processing and interregional integration of this information [2]. This communication can be assessed by measuring functional connectivity [3], which ensues from the coordinated activity between various brain regions [4]. Graph theory offers a quantitative mathematical framework to analyze functional network connectivity, and model functional connections (edges) between all regions (nodes of the network) [5]. Two widely used global graph measures are global clustering coefficient, a segregation measure, and characteristic path length, an integration measure [6–10]. The characteristic path length decreases if, on average, fewer intermediary nodes have to be passed in order to connect two nodes. Global clustering coefficient increases if two connected nodes (neighbors) are more likely to have a connection with a same neighboring node. A decrease in characteristic path length and/or an increase in global clustering coefficient correspond to increased small-world metrics. In Alzheimer’s disease, disruptions in small-world network properties reflect changes in the balance between local segregated connectivity and global integration of the network [2, 8], [10–12].

Using EEG, the pattern of changes in small-world metrics is not always consistent between studies, in particular in the asymptomatic and prodromal stage of the disease. In the dementia stage, a decrease in global clustering coefficient and an increase in path length are consistently reported [5, 8]. In the prodromal stage, within the alpha band, a decrease in connectivity is also reported (for review see Babiloni et al. [8]), with less clear effects in the theta and delta range. A recent high-density EEG connectivity study in the asymptomatic and prodromal stage of Alzheimer’s disease, reported decreased global clustering coefficient and increased path length within the alpha band, corresponding to a loss of small-worldness [13]. This loss of connectivity in the alpha frequency range correlated with the degree of medial temporal tau aggregation [13]. In other frequency bands than the alpha band, opposite effects suggesting hyperconnectivity have been described: within the delta band, coherence analysis has revealed an increased pairwise synchronization [14, 15]. Coherence is a frequency-domain measure of the consistency of phase and amplitude relationships between two signals over time. An alternative functional connectivity measure, weighted Phase Lag Index, is more robust to volume conduction [16].

Resting-state fMRI studies across the Alzheimer’s disease continuum have also reported changes in network efficiency and organization [5, 17, 18]. Also here, the most consistent changes are seen in the dementia phase, with decreased global clustering coefficient and increased characteristic path length, signifying loss of small-worldness [5, 10],[19–21]. Less pronounced but similar changes in global clustering coefficient and path length have also been described in the prodromal stage [5]. Surprisingly, some studies have reported the opposite, i.e. fMRI hyperconnectivity, in the preclinical and prodromal Alzheimer’s disease stage. This has been frequently reported in seed-based or independent component analysis at the resting-state network level, e.g. within the default mode network, the saliency or the medial temporal network [22, 23]. Large-scale network analysis, which is not based on seed-based or independent component analysis, has found most often decreases in small-world metrics (global clustering coefficient and characteristic path length) in the preclinical and prodromal stage. However, some studies have reported increased connectivity also at the large-scale network level [21] For instance, Arpanahi et al. [5] found an increase in global clustering coefficient in mild cognitive impairment patients.

The electrophysiological basis of resting-state fMRI connectivity is complex. One of the prevailing hypotheses is that resting-state fMRI connectivity relates to infraslow neural rhythms [24]. In addition to infraslow electrophysiological rhythms, the electrophysiological basis of resting-state fMRI connectivity at a global and a local network level likely includes effects across the entire electrophysiological frequency spectrum [25].

The rationale for the current study was based on our recent graph analysis finding based on high-density EEG in the asymptomatic and prodromal stage of Alzheimer’s disease. In that study, as the only difference with matched amyloid negative controls, the global clustering coefficient was decreased and the characteristic path length was increased within the alpha band [13]. This implies a loss of connectivity in the early stage of Alzheimer’s disease. This stands in contrast with the theory of fMRI hyperconnectivity in prodromal or preclinical Alzheimer’s disease [5],[17–19], [21].

As described above, the large-scale network analyses based on resting-state fMRI and EEG have yielded inconsistent findings in the asymptomatic and prodromal Alzheimer’s disease stage, with examples of both increased and decreased small-world metrics. To gain further insight in the Alzheimer’s disease changes in the connectome, we recorded the two modalities within the same subjects and applied a similar graph based modelling framework to the two modalities. For EEG, edge strength was defined using the weighted phase lag index (wPLI), a robust measure less affected by volume conduction [26–28]. For fMRI, edge strength was defined using partial correlations between regional timeseries from a whole-brain parcellation atlas [29]. We focused on global graph measures, as they are more robust at the individual level than local metrics [29].

Our main objective was to evaluate in the asymptomatic and prodromal stage of Alzheimer’s disease to which degree resting-state fMRI and EEG, acquired within the same subjects, would yield concordant results with regards to the direction of the small-world metrics characteristic path length and global clustering coefficient. We hypothesized that the concordance depends on the EEG frequency band studied. Concretely, we hypothesized that we would find a decrease in characteristic path length and an increase in global clustering coefficient in the large-scale network analysis of the resting-state fMRI data, and that changes in the same direction would be found in the delta frequency band [14, 15].

Materials and methods

Participants

72 participants were included in this study (Table 1). The cognitively unimpaired participants (n = 58) are part of the Flemish-Prevent Alzheimer’s disease Cohort KU Leuven (F-PACK), a larger longitudinal study cohort of the Laboratory for Cognitive Neurology, recruited from the community through advertisements in local newspapers for seniors. Participants had to have an intact cognitive status based on a thorough neuropsychological assessment. Inclusion was stratified genetically according to a factorial design with APOEε4 carrier and Brain Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) codon 66 met carrier status as factors. Details of the inclusion criteria have been described before [13, 30, 31], and are also described in the Supplementary material.

The patients with prodromal Alzheimer’s disease (n = 14) were recruited from the Memory Clinic of University Hospital Leuven. Their amyloid biomarker positivity was ascertained during prior clinical work-up: 3 patients were positive for amyloid β based on amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) and 11 patients had a diagnosis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker proven Alzheimer’s disease.

The resting-state EEG data and amyloid PET data used in this study were also included in a previous study [13], which additionally comprised data from 11 subjects not included in the present analysis, due to lack of reliable resting-state fMRI data. The tau PET data which was also part of the study protocol, are not included in current analysis [13].

The Ethics Committee for Clinical Studies UZ/KU Leuven approved the study (S61444) and all participants provided written informed consent for the use of their clinical data for research purposes, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Image acquisition and processing

MRI

All participants underwent a structural MRI, acquired as coronal slices on a 3T Philips Achieva system dStream 32-channel headcoil (3D TFE, TR/TE = 9.6/4.6 ms, flip angle = 8º, TI = 900 ms, voxel size 1 × 1 × 1.2 mm3).

Resting-state fMRI data was also collected on the same scanner for 500 s (250 volumes, TR/TE = 2/0.03 s, multiband factor = 2, bottom to top, voxel size 2 × 2 × 2 mm3). Ten dummy volumes were acquired to allow magnetization to reach steady state and were discarded before analysis. Subjects were instructed to stay awake, keep their eyes open and focus on a cross.

The standard in-house preprocessing [29] was performed using Matlab (R2022b) and Statistical Parametric Mapping software (version SPM 12, Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK).

First, the origin was set to the anterior commissure and the orientation was adapted to make it more similar to the template in MNI space. Then, the 250 resting-state fMRI frames were realigned and corrected for slice timing. This was followed by coregistration of the structural and functional image. After segmentation of the structural T1 image, the same deformation field was used to warp the functional images to MNI space. To determine the volumes that require censoring, head movement was assessed and the translation threshold was set to 1.5 mm, rotation to 1.5 degrees and a cutoff value for framewise displacement [32] of 1 mm. When there was no 5 min interval with less than 20% of spikes above the threshold, the subject was excluded for further analyses. Next, the fMRI timeseries were linearly detrended and the physiological noise regressors were extracted and removed from this data. A bandpass filter was applied between 0.009 and 0.1 Hz. During this process, a bad volume was filled with an interpolation of the neighboring volumes, but for all other steps and further processing, these bad volumes were censored. Regions were defined based on the Shen50 atlas (https://www.nitrc.org/frs/?group_id=51). Partial correlations were calculated between the average timeseries in these regions. The absolute values were then used as weights [29]. Graph theoretical analysis was conducted to obtain the global clustering coefficient and characteristic path length [29].

Resting-state high-density EEG

The 72 participants underwent a resting-state high-density EEG, using 128 AG/AgCl electrodes placed on a cap following the extended international 10 − 5 system. The EEG signal was recorded at a sampling rate of 1 kHz using a CE certified Synamps RT amplifier and acquisition software Curry 7 (Compumedics, Australia) in 40 of the participants. For logistic reasons, the EEG was obtained for 32 participants using the actiChamp Plus amplifier and BrainVision Recorder acquisition software (Brain Products GmbH, Germany). There was no significant difference in graph measure outcomes between subjects measured on the two EEG systems. For all subjects, the EEG signal was obtained while resting comfortably in a seated position for five minutes with their eyes open and immediately thereafter for five minutes with their eyes closed. Further details on the procedure has been described before [13], and can be found in the Supplementary materials.

The first step of the preprocessing was to identify bad channels and to down-sample the data to 256 Hz, in order to reduce computational load, followed by re-referencing to the average of all electrodes. Next, a band-pass filter of [0.5–40 Hz] was applied and independent component analysis was performed to remove automatically detected artefacts, including eyeblinks and heartbeats [33]. To account for initial accommodation, the first 60 s of the five recorded minutes were discarded, resulting in 240 s of data per condition (eyes open and eyes closed), divided into 12 epochs of 20 s. The wPLI was calculated per epoch using the Welch algorithm (Hamming filter size 500 ms) [26, 28], within the four frequency bands (delta 1–4 Hz, theta 4–8 Hz, alpha 8–13 Hz, beta 13–30 Hz) and then averaged across epochs. These wPLI values gave rise to an undirected adjacency matrix between electrodes using the absolute value of the wPLI values as weights. From this adjacency matrix, we calculated weighted global graph measures in sensor space [34, 35], i.e. characteristic path length and global clustering coefficient [36].

Amyloid PET

Every cognitively unimpaired participant underwent an amyloid PET scan to determine whether they were amyloid β positive or negative. Because of changing tracer availability over time, 29 participants received a 60 min dynamic [11] C-Pittsburgh Compound B (PiB) PET scan, and 32 participants a static 20 min [18] F-NAV4694 PET (starting 50 min post-injection). One participant underwent a 20 min [18] F-florbetaben amyloid PET scan (starting 90 min post-injection) as part of another study in our lab.

The procedure for amyloid PET acquisition and analysis have been described before [13, 30, 37], and is also covered in the Supplementary material. The Standard Uptake Value Ratio (SUVR) was derived from the PET scans, using participant-specific cerebellar grey matter as reference region. The mean SUVR was calculated in a global composite VOI, which consisted of five bilateral cortical regions, namely frontal (AAL areas [38] 3–10, 13–16, and 23–28), parietal (AAL 57–70), anterior cingulate (AAL 31–32), posterior cingulate (AAL 35–36) and lateral temporal (AAL 81–82 and 85–90) [13, 39]. SUVRs were calculated between 40 and 60 min post-injection for the [11] C-PiB scans, 50 to 70 min post-injection for the [18] F-NAV4694 scans, and 90 to 110 min post-injection for the [18] F-florbetaben scan. Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted with R statistical software version 4.3.2.

The distribution normality of the global graph measures in the three subgroups was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Depending on the normality, we used either a Kruskal-Wallis test or ANOVA to determine whether the two global graph measures derived from EEG or resting-state fMRI, respectively, differed between the CU-, CU+, and prodromal Alzheimer’s disease subgroups. To account for multiple comparisons in the post-hoc analyses, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) FDR correction, setting the False Discovery Rate FDR threshold for significance at 0.05.

The objective of this study was to understand the relationship between global graph measures based on networks derived from two different modalities measured in the same subjects. A multiple linear regression analysis was performed for each EEG frequency band (delta, theta, alpha, and beta), resulting in eight different models. In four of the models, the outcome variable was the global clustering coefficient from resting-state fMRI, and the regressors were the global clustering coefficient from a separate resting-state EEG band, age, sex and APOEε4 carriership. In four other models, a similar analysis was performed with characteristic path length from resting-state fMRI as outcome variable and characteristic path length from each resting-state EEG band as regressor. To account for multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure to control FDR.

If a significant effect was found in the main analysis, an analysis of each of the three subgroups was performed to determine which groups were driving the significant effect. This analysis was not corrected for multiple comparisons, as it was based on a gatekeeping approach, where a more global analysis is first performed and more granular analyses are performed subsequently in hierarchical manner when the primary analysis reveals significantly results in order to better comprehend the primary effect.

When we observed significant effects, in order to evaluate the effect of amyloid load, we performed a multiple linear regression analysis with the global graph measure as outcome variable, and as regressors global amyloid load, age and sex in the cognitively unimpaired group (n = 58). A similar analysis was performed with delta EEG-based characteristic path length as outcome variable. Since we do not have amyloid PET measures of the prodromal Alzheimer’s disease, we restricted this analysis to the CU group.

As a further analysis, we ran a unified model incorporating all bands: a regression model was examined with the fMRI-based global graph measure as outcome value and the global graph measure from each of the 4 EEG bands as regressors.

Discussion

This multimodal study aimed to determine the concordance of changes in small-world metrics in the asymptomatic and prodromal stage of Alzheimer’s disease between two modalities: high-density EEG and resting-state fMRI. The two modalities were acquired within the same subjects and examined within a same mathematical graph based framework, focusing on global measures of connectivity. The fMRI-based characteristic path length was decreased in the asymptomatic phase of Alzheimer’s disease and even more so in the prodromal stage of Alzheimer’s disease. This is indicative of hyperconnectivity at the large-scale global brain level. In contrast, in the EEG alpha band, we observed a decreased global clustering coefficient and increased path length. This is indicative of hypoconnectivity. These seemingly contradictory findings are resolved by our main finding: resting-state fMRI hyperconnectivity correlates with increased delta band EEG connectivity, both across the entire group and within subgroups, in particular the prodromal cases and to a lesser degree the asymptomatic amyloid positive cases (Table 5). There is no correlation between fMRI connectivity and the alpha band hypoconnectivity. Hence the study reconciles the apparent discrepancy between the direction of the connectivity changes in the alpha band [13] and in resting-state fMRI [5] : characteristic path length derived from resting-state fMRI relates to the characteristic path length derived from the delta band rather than the alpha band.

A key strength of this study is the within-subject multimodal analysis of two distinct techniques for measuring brain connectivity within a same mathematical graph-theoretical framework. A further strength is the reliance on connectivity measures with proven validity and reliability at the individual level for both modalities [29, 40].

Babiloni et al. [41] report a relationship between delta rhythms in Alzheimer’s disease patients with dementia and composite cortical hypometabolism measured with [18] F-fluorodeoxyglucose PET. These classical delta wave findings are related to power density rather than connectivity. It should be noted that power density and functional connectivity, as measured in the current study, are operating at two different levels. Power density is calculated at the nodal/regional level while functional connectivity is defined among two nodes/regions.

Connectivity within the delta frequency band (in which slow cortical potentials are occurring) is generally considered to reflect long-range connectivity [24, 42, 43]. According to a laminar electrode recording study, the EEG delta rhythm is generated in the superficial cortical laminae and is implicated mainly in connections over a longer range [42]. In physiological circumstances, synchronous activity within the delta frequency band plays an important role when large-scale distant cortical networks coordinate their neural activity during decision making [43]. Connectivity in the slow cortical potentials band (2–5 Hz) is higher in states of consciousness (such as rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep, the awake state and the minimally conscious state) compared to nonconscious states (such as slow wave sleep and deep propofol anesthesia, vegetative and unresponsive wakefulness) [44].

The path length in the delta frequency band correlated with the path length derived from fMRI across the entire study group as well as within the amyloid positive cognitively unimpaired group and within the prodromal Alzheimer’s disease group. Both modalities were concordant and indicated decreases in characteristic path length. This is in agreement with prior studies reporting increased delta band connectivity in Alzheimer’s disease versus healthy matched controls based on spectral coherence analysis (for review see Babiloni et al. [45]).

More generally, previous between-group comparisons of delta band connectivity in Alzheimer’s disease have yielded inconsistent results [40]. Also in the current study, no significant between-group differences in delta band graph measures were found (Table 3). A plausible explanation for the presence of a significant correlation in the absence of a group effect is that the dysconnectome in Alzheimer’s disease is changing over a continuum from the early asymptomatic stage to the prodromal stage, rather than in a categorical manner.

Resting-state fMRI was more sensitive than EEG for detecting between-group differences: the resting-state fMRI characteristic path length showed a significant difference between the amyloid negative healthy controls and the prodromal Alzheimer’s disease patients. Resting-state fMRI did not reveal a difference between the amyloid positive cognitively unimpaired participants and the two other groups. This likely reflects the transitional position of this group in between the two ends of the spectrum we studied.

In contrast to characteristic path length, the global clustering coefficient did not show any significant effects, apart from a between-group difference within the alpha band. There may be theoretical reasons why global clustering coefficient is less sensitive than the characteristic path length. The latter derives directly from the average of the local node strengths while the global clustering coefficient requires extra steps of processing at the local network (cluster) level, which may reduce sensitivity especially in relatively small samples.

fMRI hyperconnectivity in early stages of Alzheimer’s disease has been widely reported (for review see Corriveau-Lecavalier et al. [46]) within intrinsic networks such as the default mode network [22] or the anterior or posterior medial temporal network [23], [47–50]. The pattern of brain connectivity across disease progression follows an inverted U-shaped trajectory. In early stages, amyloid-positive individuals with low neocortical tau levels exhibit hyperconnectivity. As tau pathology increases, this shifts toward reduced connectivity, marking a transition towards hypoconnectivity [7]. The pattern may depend not only on disease stage [51], but also on age of disease onset [8]. Various explanations for the hyperconnectivity have been put forward,1 and its significance is still poorly understood, e.g. with regards to whether it is beneficial or harmful [52]. The observed fMRI patterns may reflect a compensatory neuroplastic response in early stages, which diminishes as the disease progresses [18, 46]. There are several possible mechanisms for hyperconnectivity: it may be a downstream effect of medial temporal lobe excitability and the disruption of the excitation/inhibition (EI) balance [3], or a sign of decreased functional flexibility, resulting in an overrepresentation of specific functional networks, or a compensatory, system-level adaptation to neuronal injury [7].

It is thought that amyloid β pathology plays a role in hyperactivity and hyperconnectivity [53, 54]. In transgenic mouse models resting-state fMRI hypersynchrony between anterior and posterior cingulate cortex has been described prior to amyloid accumulation. Mechanistically it has been linked to astrocyte-mediated dysregulation of neuronal function and deficient calcium signaling in astrocytes [55]. A same molecular mechanism could theoretically underlie the resting-state fMRI hyperconnectivity we observe at a more global level in preclinical and prodromal Alzheimer’s disease. However, we did not find a correlation between path length and amyloid load for either of the two modalities in the cognitively unimpaired group.

With EEG, between-group differences in characteristic path length and global clustering coefficient were only found in the alpha band [13]. The alpha rhythm is mainly localized to the occipital and parietal regions and has a putative role in short-range connections according to invasive recording studies [56]. There were no effects within other bands than alpha or delta. This negative finding may be due to lack of sensitivity given the relatively low sample size and the high within-group variance of the experimental measures. Prior electrophysiological studies have reported changes of connectivity in these other frequency bands too. For instance, a MEG study described an ‘X model’ based on phase locking in the theta and beta band. Mild cognitive impairment cases who converted to dementia showed initial hypersynchronization in these frequency bands compared to the stable group while at follow up the pattern was reversed [57]. The delta band was not examined in that study. An EEG study reported reduced delta band synchronization with lower MMSE scores in Alzheimer’s disease [58].

With regards to clinical applicability and implications, the large interindividual variability and the overlap between groups makes it unlikely that the global connectivity measures based on resting-state fMRI or EEG can be used as diagnostic markers (Figs. 1 and 3). For that purpose other graph based analyses of EEG data have been proposed, e.g. classification based on the wPLI adjacency matrices as input [40].

The study also has other limitations. The study protocol comprised multiple modalities executed at a high level of quality, including resting-state fMRI, high-density EEG, amyloid PET in the healthy controls, as well as tau PET [13] Because of the high demands of the study protocol, the sample size is relatively limited, in particular in the CU + and the prodromal Alzheimer’s disease group. Second, our research focused on global connectivity measures rather than the resting-state network level. We opted for global connectivity measures as these have been proven to be more robust than local connectivity measures or modularity analyses at the individual subject level [29]. It is possible that resting-state fMRI hyperconnectivity at the network level, e.g. in the default mode network, would relate to other frequency band than the global resting-state fMRI connectivity measures. Source-based modeling would be needed to define networks based on EEG [59]. The number of possible networks and variability in defining the networks may lead to an inflation of possible comparisons, while the use of global measures allows one to restrict the number of possible comparisons. The mathematical framework for these global estimations is strictly comparable for the resting-state fMRI partial correlation coefficients and the EEG wPLI, respectively. Local measures would require additional modality specific processing steps such as source modeling for EEG and principal component analysis or other methods for defining resting-state fMRI networks [60]. As a further limitation, the current study is based on correlation between graph measures derived from two distinct modalities, which does not allow to establish a causal link. The study is also cross-sectional. Longitudinal examination may contribute further evidence about the link between the delta frequency based measures and resting-state fMRI connectivity. The increase in delta band coherence is reportedly more exaggerated with disease severity [15], whereas resting-state fMRI hyperconnectivity has generally been mostly related to early Alzheimer’s disease.

To conclude, the current study offers an explanation for the discrepancy between the increase in characteristic path length in the alpha band in asymptomatic and prodromal stage of Alzheimer’s disease and the decrease in characteristic path length in fMRI : the fMRI connectivity measures are more strongly linked to the delta frequency which shows connectivity changes in the same direction as resting-state fMRI. Future multimodal investigations combining high density EEG and fMRI in larger study populations are warranted to unravel the Alzheimer disconnectome.
