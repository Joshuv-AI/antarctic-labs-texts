The association of frailty with age and lifespan in mice differs by strain and sex
Authors: Dantong Zhu, Cara L Green, Sarah J Mitchell, Michael R MacArthur, Elise S Bisset, John Mach, Judy Z Wu, Brady A Samuelson, Anastasia Shindyapina, Alibek Moldakozhayev, Albina Tskhay, Alexander Tyshkovskiy, Vadim N Gladyshev, David A Sinclair, Susan E Howlett, Sarah N Hilmer, Dudley W Lamming, Alice E Kane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrailty indices have been assessed in mouse models for more than a decade, but the effect of sex and strain on frailty outcomes remains poorly understood. Here, we collated and harmonized item-level 31-item clinical frailty index (FI) and lifespan data from 17 independent cohorts, including 1,564 naturally aging mice (690 females, 874 males) across five commonly used mouse strains or substrains: C57BL/6JNIA, C57BL/6N, C57BL/6J, UM-HET3, and Diversity Outbred. A total of 3,665 observations were included across cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, some previously published, with 2,192 observations linked to known age at death. Across all cohorts, FI increased significantly with chronological age, but the rate of frailty accumulation differed by strain. Sex differences in age-associated frailty trajectories were evident only in C57BL/6JNIA mice. Significant strain- and sex-specific differences in both survival and health span were observed, with males generally outliving females, although sex effects varied by strain. FI was strongly associated with lifespan independent of age, although age-dependent effects emerged in specific strains with notable sex-specific patterns. At the level of individual frailty items, most health deficits showed strain-dependent associations with age and lifespan. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the relationships between frailty, chronological age, and lifespan are strongly modulated by strain and sex. This work highlights the importance of accounting for strain and sex as factors when investigating and utilizing frailty in preclinical models.

Introduction

Aging is a highly heterogeneous process in which individuals of the same chronological age can present substantial variability in health and function.1 Frailty captures this inter-individual variability and reflects the cumulative decline in physiological capacity that accompanies aging.2 Frailty indices (FI) are utilized to measure frailty and represent the proportion of age-accumulated health-related deficits present in an individual.3–5 Higher FI values indicate a greater degree of frailty and are associated with increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, including disease burden, disability, and mortality.6,7 FI has also been used to predict mortality risk and remaining lifespan, underscoring its value as a measure of biological aging.8,9

A typical FI in humans is constructed by quantifying the accumulation of health deficits, including diseases, symptoms, or abnormal lab results, and calculating the proportion of deficits present out of the total number of eligible items assessed.5,10 The same cumulative deficit approach was first adapted for mice in 2014.11 The most widely used mouse index is a 31-item mouse ‘clinical’ FI, encompassing 7 domains.11 This FI has been applied across mouse strains and sexes in different facilities,12–14 but the effect of these factors on the relationship of frailty with age and lifespan has not been investigated.

Sex differences in frailty are well established in human studies,15,16 but findings are less consistent in mice.17,18 Genetic background is also a major determinant of aging in humans,19 and recent evidence indicates its influence on frailty.20 Commonly used mouse strains in aging research include inbred C57BL/6 and the more genetically diverse UM-HET3 and Diversity Outbred strains.21 Within the widely used C57BL/6 strain, multiple substrains (e.g., C57BL/6J, C57BL/6N, and C57BL/6JNIA) have been established and are known to differ genetically and phenotypically.22,23 Previous studies have demonstrated that these substrains exhibit distinct aging trajectories, including differences in lifespan,24,25 metabolic regulation,26,27 immune function,28,29 and susceptibility to age-related pathologies.30 Additionally, the interaction between sex and genetic background may substantially influence frailty trajectories and their interpretation in aging research. Nevertheless, systematic studies that jointly examine frailty across both sexes and multiple strains or substrains remain limited.

In this study, we have assembled both published and unpublished data from 17 mouse cohorts, collected across multiple sites and investigators, covering 3 strains: C57BL/6 (including substrains 6JNIA, 6J, and 6 N), UM-HET3, and Diversity Outbred, with ages extending up to 1,200 days and frailty assessments at multiple time points. All these cohorts utilized the mouse clinical FI protocol.11 Using this integrated dataset, we compared survivorship and examined how FI scores and individual frailty items relate to chronological age and lifespan, across strains and between sexes. Individual-item level analyses were conducted on a refined 18-item subset, selected after excluding items that were unavailable in certain cohorts or exhibited minimal variance across the integrated dataset. Our analyses reveal strain- and sex-dependent differences in survival, rates of frailty accumulation, and the relationships between FI, age, and lifespan. Furthermore, item-level analyses demonstrate that associations between specific health deficits and aging or lifespan percentage (defined here as the proportion of the lifespan an individual has completed at the time of observation) vary substantially across strains and, in some cases, by sex and age. Together, these findings highlight the importance of genetics and sex in shaping frailty trajectories and underscore the need to account for strain- and substrain-specific effects in mouse studies of aging and frailty.

Methods

Mouse data

We collated a total of 17 cohorts of naturally aging mice from either published work or unpublished experiments (Table 1). These cohorts were selected based on the availability of item-level data for the 31-item Clinical Frailty Index, through a targeted literature search and direct outreach to primary investigators. Our goal was to leverage a large, diverse, and harmonized dataset to identify biological trends that persist across different experimental environments. Some of the included cohorts also contained experimental intervention groups; however, only control mice were included in this analysis, and animals subjected to interventions were excluded prior to pooling across cohorts. Each cohort of mice belongs to one of the following substrains (strains) of mice: C57BL/6 (including substrains 6JNIA, 6 N, and 6J), UM-HET3, and Diversity Outbred. For published work, mouse husbandry conditions and ethical approval information can be found in the original references (Table 1).

Frailty assessment

The mouse clinical frailty index (FI)11 that contains 31 health-related items was utilized in each cohort, with some adaptations as below. Overall, mice were scored either 0, 0.5, or 1 for the degree of deficit they showed in each item, with 0 representing no deficit, 0.5 representing a mild deficit, and 1 representing a severe deficit. FI was calculated by summing scores for each deficit and then dividing by the number of items that are available for the sample.

Frailty data processing

As not all items were available across all cohorts, FI items that were not assessed or available in at least one cohort were omitted from further analyses to ensure consistency across datasets (Table S1). These items included temperature score, body weight score, malocclusions, hearing loss, and mouse grimace scale. Next, items with minimal variance across all mice in the training set (i.e., nearly invariant items) were excluded from further analyses, including dermatitis, tumors, cataracts, corneal capacity, nasal discharge, rectal prolapse, vaginal/uterine/penile prolapse, and diarrhea. For the remaining 18 items (Table S2), 33 missing values were imputed using the median of the corresponding age and sex specific group.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of survivorship across strains were performed using a stratified log-rank test. Pairwise comparisons of survivorship between strains and comparisons between sexes within each strain were conducted using the log-rank test. We used Frailty-Adjusted Mouse Year (FAMY) to measure the healthspan of mice in the study, following the previously published method.32 Briefly, for each mouse, 1 - FI was plotted against chronological age in days. The FAMY is then calculated by subtracting the area under the FI curve (AUC) from the total lifespan, then divided by 365 to convert the unit, representing the equivalent years lived in a deficit-free state. Strain-wise difference was tested by the Kruskal-Wallis test, and the difference between sexes within each strain was tested using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

We considered two aging outcomes in association analyses of FI and individual FI items: chronological age (days) and lifespan percentage. The latter was calculated by dividing chronological age by total lifespan to normalize for the differing lifespans across strains. The FI score is a bounded measure of accumulated health deficits and is typically non-normally distributed. Log transformation has been used in prior research33 to linearize the relationship between frailty and age and to model exponential patterns of deficit accumulation. Because the frailty index included zero values, a small constant (0.0001) was added prior to log transformation. Overall, the FI score was log-transformed (log-transformed FI + 0.0001) to account for its skewed distribution, better approximate normality (Table S3) and improve the linearity of its relationship with aging outcomes. To examine associations between the FI score and these two outcomes, linear mixed-effects models were applied, with FI as the dependent variable and chronological age or lifespan percentage as the independent variable, adjusting for strain and/or sex as appropriate for each subgroup analysis. An interaction term between age and lifespan percentage was included to test their combined effects.

For analysis of individual FI items, likelihood ratio tests were performed by including item-by-strain interaction terms to assess whether associations between individual items and chronological age or lifespan percentage differed by strain. Subsequently, linear mixed models were fitted within each strain- and sex-specific group, with chronological age or lifespan percentage as the dependent variable and the individual item score as the independent variable. For analyses involving lifespan percentage, an additional interaction term between lifespan percentage and age was included. All analyses were completed in R v. 4.4.2.

Results

Mice cohorts

We collated data from mouse studies with frailty and lifespan measurements across 17 cohorts including 5 mice strains or substrains (hereafter collectively referred to as ‘strains’ for simplicity), C57BL/6JNIA (cohort 1-6, hereafter 6JNIA), C57BL/6N (cohort 7, hereafter 6 N), C57BL/6J (cohort 8-12, hereafter 6J), UM-HET3 (cohort 13-16, hereafter HET3), and Diversity Outbred (cohort 17, hereafter DO) (Table 1). In total, 1,564 naturally aging mice (690 females and 874 males) were studied, with 576 mice (244 females and 332 males) tracked until death.

FI measurements, defined as the clinical deficit accumulation index based on the 31-item protocol, were collected at least once, and up to 11 times, per mouse, resulting in 3,665 samples (observations). Most samples were aged between 300 to 900 days (Figure 1A), with FI scores ranging from 0 to 0.68 (Figure 1B), the submaximal limit for FI observed in previous mouse and human studies.10,34 DO mice, the most genetically heterogeneous strain, exhibited significant lower (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < .001; post hoc Dunn’s test, p < .001 for all pairs) FI distributions compared to the rest of the strains.

Strain differences in lifespan and healthspan

We then examined whether survival differed across mouse strains and between sexes, in the 2,192 observations with known age at death. We found significant differences in survival curves (Figure 1C) (stratified log-rank test, χ2(3) = 58.1, p < .001) across strains and between strain pairs. Male 6J substrain lived longest, followed by male 6JNIA and female 6J. The C57BL/6 strain (including three substrains) showed no significant difference in lifespan when compared to the HET3 strain (χ2(1) = 0.9, p = .3), wherein the 6J strain outlived HET3 mice, and 6JNIA and the latter two showed no significant difference (Table S4). Sex differences in survivorship were also evident (χ2(1) = 49.3, p < .001), that is, male mice generally lived longer than female mice. When looking into the strains, a sex difference in survivorship was detected in 6JNIA (log-rank test, χ2(1) = 50.4, p < .001) and 6J (χ2(1) = 14.9, p < .001) but not in HET3 mice (χ2(1) = 2, p = .2).

The longitudinal nature of the FI data also enabled us to investigate healthspan using the novel Frailty-Adjusted Mouse Years (FAMY) method, which calculates years lived in ‘good health,’ based on the human-equivalent quality-adjusted life years (QALY).33 We observed significant strain differences in FAMY (Kruskal-Wallis test, H(6) = 183.8, p < .001) (Figure 1D), where, as observed for lifespan, the 6J strain had the longest healthspan, followed by 6JNIA and HET3. Additionally, 6JNIA (post hoc Dunn’s test; Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p < .001) and 6J (p < .05) strains exhibited sex differences in healthspan, with males having higher FAMY values than females. Overall, these results suggest that males generally have longer healthspans and lifespans than female mice.

Frailty associated with age and lifespan

Given the observed strain- and sex-specific differences in survival and FAMY values, we next asked whether these differences were reflected in patterns of frailty accumulation across age and progression through the lifespan. From the combined data from the 17 cohorts, we found chronological age was significantly associated with (log-transformed) FI, after controlling for sex and strain (linear mixed model, t(3625) = 30.661, p < .001; raw data and trend lines regardless of the longitudinal nature are visualized in Figure S1). In addition, we found the rate of relative FI accumulation differed among strains, as indicated by the significant age by strain interaction term. To illustrate these subgroup-specific trajectories, we then estimated age-associated changes in FI within each strain and further in sex-stratified subgroups, with effect sizes summarized in Figure 2A. Frailty was strongly associated with chronological age across strains, with greater effect sizes observed in the C57BL/6 substrains, followed by HET3 and DO mice. Notably, sex differences in age-FI association were strain-dependent and were only significant in 6JNIA, suggesting that female C57BL/6NIA mice generally accumulate deficits faster than males.

We next investigated the associations between FI and lifespan percentage. Lifespan percentage was strongly associated with FI after controlling for sex and strain (linear mixed model, t(1804) = 20.720, p < .001 (Figure 2B); raw data and trend lines regardless of the longitudinal nature are visualized in Figure S2). A trend was observed that at the end of lifespan, females presented lower FI scores than males (Figure S2A). Inclusion of a lifespan percentage by age interaction term revealed a non-significant interaction term, indicating that the association between log-transformed FI and lifespan percentage across strains is independent of age, and FI was not significantly associated with lifespan percentage after adjusting for age (Figure 2B), indicating that FI does not provide substantial information beyond chronological age in predicting lifespan in the overall cohort. However, the significant lifespan percentage: age: strain interaction indicated heterogeneity among strains, motivating strain-specific analyses. We found significant lifespan percentage by age interaction terms in 6JNIA and 6 N substrains, suggesting that in these strains the association between FI and lifespan percentage varies with age. Moreover, in 6JNIA, the age-dependent relationship between FI and lifespan percentage was also influenced by sex, where specifically for male mice the association between FI and lifespan increased as age increased, while for female mice the association was age-independent (Figure 2B). Interestingly, for 6 N mice (where we have only females), the opposite trend was seen, and the association between FI and lifespan actually decreased as the mice got older. For 6J mice, the relationship was consistent across ages, but differed between males and females, with males even showing a negative association between FI and lifespan. In contrast, HET3 mice exhibited neither age-dependent nor sex-specific differences in the relationship between FI and lifespan percentage. Overall, these results highlight that the relationship between frailty and age or lifespan percentage is sex- and strain-dependent.

Associations of frailty items with age and lifespan

In addition to the overall FI score, we tested the association of specific FI items with age and lifespan percentage. First, we performed a likelihood ratio test of the item by strain interaction term. Of the 18 items tested, 13 presented strain-dependent associations with age. In contrast, loss of whiskers, vestibular disturbance, eye discharge/swelling, microphthalmia, and vision loss did not show strain differences. As for lifespan, 12 items exhibited strain differences, whereas distended abdomen, kyphosis, and the latter four items (i.e. vestibular disturbance, eye discharge/swelling, microphthalmia, and vision loss) that were not age-associated showed no strain differences. Within each strain, we then investigated sex differences. While the majority of the items showed similar positive associations with age (Figure 3A) and lifespan percentage (Figure 3B), across males and females, some showed sex differences, suggested by significant interaction terms of FI item by sex (indicated by ‘†’ in Figure 3). Notably, for the majority of these items, especially for JNIA mice, female mice had greater effect sizes of association with age or lifespan than those of male mice (Figure 3). However, there were exceptions, such as body condition score for HET3 mice, which was more strongly associated with both age and lifespan in male mice, and menace reflex, coat condition, loss of whisker, and alopecia were more strongly associated with lifespan in male mice in at least one strain (Figure 3B). Additionally, we added age to the lifespan percentage models and found several items presented significant interaction terms, as well as strain differences (Table S5), suggesting the associations between some items and lifespan percentage depend on age. Altogether, these results suggest that although the majority of FI items are consistently associated with age and lifespan, there are sex and strain differences in these relationships.

Discussion

This study leveraged frailty and lifespan data from an extensive collection of naturally aging mice to characterize how genetic background, specifically sex and strain/substrain, impacts frailty progression. Across strains and sexes, the frailty index was strongly associated with both chronological age and lifespan percentage, supporting its validity as an integrative measure of biological aging in mice. However, sex- and strain-dependent differences were evident in both survivorship and frailty accumulation, as well as the progression of discrete health deficits.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to complete a pooled cohort study of frailty and lifespan across mouse strains/substrains. Although this analysis integrated both cross-sectional and longitudinal data, such an approach is supported by evidence that both data types capture comparable associations between frailty and aging.35 We demonstrated that strain-specific patterns exist in both lifespan (survival curves) and healthspan (Figure 1C and D). Notably, the C57BL/6 substrain 6J examined in this study exhibited a greater average lifespan than HET3 mice, whereas 6JNIA and N had similar or shorter average lifespan. While HET3 mice are generally considered to exhibit superior longevity compared to the C57BL/6 mouse strain, our results are consistent with recent studies highlighting substantial inter-site variability in survival outcomes. In multiple Interventions Testing Program (ITP) cohorts, median lifespans of male and female control HET3 were reported as ∼24 and ∼29 mo, respectively.36 These values are directly comparable to the C57BL/6 median lifespan observed in this study (∼23.6 to 31 mo; Table S4), which also aligns with the ∼29 mo median lifespan reported in another study.37 These findings also underscore the diversity among C57BL/6 substrains. On the other hand, lifespan estimates are cohort dependent and influenced by specific experimental and environmental conditions. We also applied the novel FAMY measure of healthspan32 and found that the 6J strain shows greater average healthspan compared to 6JNIA and HET3 mice. Together, our findings provide more evidence of mouse strain heterogeneity.

We further identified strain-dependent variation in the associations between frailty, age, and lifespan. Although FI was significantly associated with lifespan in the overall cohort, this association became non-significant after adjusting for age, indicating that FI largely reflects chronological aging at the population level rather than providing independent predictive information about lifespan. However, this pattern was not uniform across strains. In some strains, such as 6J and HET3, the FI-lifespan associations were not age-dependent. Notably, the associations between lifespan and frailty decrease with aging in the 6 N strain, likely due to survivorship bias, whereby more vulnerable and frailer individuals die earlier. Consequently, individuals surviving to older ages represent a selectively robust subset, which also explains the negative association between lifespan and FI observed in male 6J and HET3 mice (Figure 2B). In contrast, this pattern is not observed in 6JNIA mice, suggesting it is strain-specific. One possible explanation for this scenario is that the clinical FI was developed using 6JNIA mice, potentially limiting its accuracy in other strains. Importantly, these differences were not limited to the composite FI score, and most individual frailty items showed strain-dependent associations with both age and lifespan percentage. Collectively, this heterogeneity implies that distinct biological mechanisms, for instance, the integumentary system (piloerection) and the ocular system (menace reflex), may drive the aging process in different strains.

Sex differences are apparent in both aging and frailty for humans and animal models.15 Here, we found males generally exhibited longer lifespans than females, and these differences were pronounced in 6JNIA and 6J mice, suggesting a common mechanism. Interestingly, this is the opposite of what is seen in humans, but consistent with previous studies of C57BL/6 mice, which have reported mixed results regarding which sex lives longer.38 For HET3 mice, we observed no sex differences in lifespan, despite previous studies that have found females have longer lifespans than males across multiple study sites.39 Furthermore, sex modified frailty trajectories in a strain-specific manner, with significant sex differences in the relationship between age and FI primarily detected in 6JNIA mice, where females accumulate frailty more rapidly than males with increasing age. Similar sex-specific patterns of frailty accumulation have been reported in previous mouse studies, and this pattern mirrors observations in humans, where women tend to be frailer than men across ages.40 Interestingly, we see the opposite effect of the sex-frailty paradox observed in humans in female 6JNIA mice, which have both higher rates of frailty accumulation and shorter lifespans. Future work should explore this further, as these findings raise the possibility that the sex-frailty paradox in humans may be influenced not only by biological mechanisms but also by complex sociobehavioral factors that are difficult to model in laboratory mice.41 At the level of individual frailty items, most deficits increased similarly in males and females, yet a subset of measures, notably coat condition, body condition score, alopecia, and loss of whiskers, more frequently displayed sex differences in their associations with age or lifespan, especially in the C57BL/6 strain (Figure 3). This suggests these specific items may show sex-dimorphic effects in aging, potentially related to androgen-dependent mechanisms, and these domains could be explored further to better understand sex differences in aging and frailty mechanisms. Together, these findings align with a growing body of evidence indicating that sex differences in aging are context-dependent and that environmental and husbandry conditions have a significant effect on outcomes, including lifespan.42,43 Importantly, sex differences were not consistent across strains or outcomes, and were largely restricted to specific strain-trait combinations. This suggests that the influence of sex on frailty is strongly modulated by genetic background rather than representing a uniform effect across mouse models. In addition to genetic background, the manifestation of these traits can be highly sensitive to study design, for instance, clinical FI scores are higher for females than males in cross-sectional studies but not longitudinal ones.35 By pooling these data types into a blended trajectory, our approach integrates these design-dependent nuances while still successfully capturing distinct, strain-specific sex variations in frailty accumulation.

When considering the total score, FI was associated with age and lifespan across sex and strains. However, a more granular analysis of individual FI items revealed a more complex scenario. Of the 26 items that were consistently measured across the 17 cohorts, 8 items showed little variance, even considering multiple mouse strains and studies, suggesting they have limited contribution to frailty assessments. For the remaining 18 items, associations heavily depended on strain, sex, or age. Items that were particularly variable in their associations across subgroups included coat condition, body condition score, alopecia, and loss of whiskers. Crucially, these individual deficits are not causative drivers of frailty, and they function as discrete indicators of systemic biological decline. This complexity suggests that while the composite FI serves as a powerful summary metric, its constituent items may reflect divergent biological processes with variable relevance across populations. This 31-item FI was originally developed and validated primarily in C57BL/6 mice,11 and although it has since been widely applied, it may not be equally optimized for other strains. Instead, our findings suggest a need for strain-aware calibration of the scoring criteria or thresholds for specific items, and a ‘one-size-fits-all’ threshold may inadvertently overlook signs of frailty in some strains while overestimating them in others.

It should be noted, however, that a limitation of this study is that mice of different strains and sexes were not perfectly age-matched across all cohorts, which may contribute to the observed variability in deficit progression. Additionally, mice of different strains and sexes were not perfectly age-matched across all cohorts, which may contribute to the observed variability in deficit progression. Combining cohorts across multiple facilities likely introduced artificial variation into the frailty measurements. Because husbandry conditions, housing types (e.g., SPF vs. conventional), diets, and research teams varied considerably across facilities, these environmental factors could have influenced frailty scores and potentially affected our conclusions. While this study was constrained by the nature of the available data, future work should ideally compare these strains and sexes within a single facility under identical conditions to eliminate such environmental confounding. Furthermore, certain substrains were also much more represented in our data, and we had a particularly small sample size for HET3 and DO mice, especially at older ages. This relative sparsity of FI measurements at advanced ages, particularly in DO mice, may influence downstream analyses by biasing estimates of late-life frailty accumulation, potentially leading to overestimation of healthspan or obscuring nonlinearities in frailty trajectories and FAMY. We also did not have lifespan data available for every mouse, so our power for these analyses was reduced. Finally, although frailty trajectories may exhibit non-linear dynamics, particularly at advanced ages, the log-transformation of FI improved the linearity of its relationship with aging outcomes in our dataset; however, we cannot exclude the possibility that more complex age-related patterns were not fully captured by the linear modeling approach. Ideally, future work would include a direct comparison of sex and strains within a single facility. It would also be beneficial to extend the analysis to other frailty measures, such as the frailty phenotype.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that frailty accumulation and its relationship to lifespan are shaped by a tripartite interplay of strain, sex, and age. These results emphasize the importance of incorporating biological diversity into preclinical aging studies and suggest that strain- and sex-specific analyses are essential for understanding the mechanisms of frailty.
