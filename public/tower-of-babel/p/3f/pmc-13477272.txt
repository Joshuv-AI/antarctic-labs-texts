ECG‐Based Deep Learning to Predict Incident Atrial Fibrillation Burden
Authors: Thomas Sommers, Sam F Friedman, Shinwan Kany, Steven A Lubitz, Mahnaz Maddah, Patrick T Ellinor, Shaan Khurshid
Published: 2026

Undiagnosed atrial fibrillation (AF) is highly morbid. Although screening may enable prompt detection, contemporary efforts have not meaningfully reduced heart failure, stroke, or death. A key limitation is detection of low‐burden paroxysmal AF, 1 with less clinical impact than persistent or high‐burden AF. 2

Artificial intelligence (AI)‐based ECG models can estimate AF risk to guide screening. 3 However, it remains unknown whether existing ECG‐based AI AF risk models identify individuals more likely to develop persistent or high‐burden AF. Here, we deploy a validated ECG‐based AI algorithm (ECG‐AI), 3 trained in >100 000 patients from Mass General Brigham, to assess whether ECG‐based AI AF risk estimates associate with future persistent or high‐burden AF on outpatient rhythm monitoring.

We analyzed a Mass General Brigham primary care and cardiology cohort, 4 including patients without prevalent AF, ≥1 ECG within 3 years before cohort start of follow‐up, and ≥1 Holter, event, or patch monitor during follow‐up. Monitor‐detected AF and burden were extracted using regular expressions and classified into persistent (AF burden ≥98%) versus paroxysmal (AF burden >0% but <98%, or >1 discrete episode). One author (T.S.) validated random reports with an extraction (n=50 persistent and n=50 paroxysmal; positive predictive value, 100% [95% CI, 93–100]) and reports without AF (n=100; negative predictive value, 100% [95% CI, 93–100]). In a case–control design, we compared discrimination of (1) monitor‐detected AF (versus no monitor‐detected AF), and (2) persistent AF (versus paroxysmal), using age and sex; age, sex, and logit‐transformed ECG‐AI probability (ECG‐AI AS); and elements of the Cohorts for Heart and Aging Research in Genomic Epidemiology Atrial Fibrillation (CHARGE‐AF) score reweighted in the original ECG‐AI training set. Patients from the original ECG‐AI training set with monitors (n=2979) were used to fit coefficients for age and sex and ECG‐AI AS but excluded from evaluation. Discrimination was quantified by AUC and compared using DeLong's test. We tested associations between ECG‐AI and AF burden/type using linear regression adjusted for age and sex, assessed AF discrimination using a ≥10% burden cutoff, 5 and quantified associations between AF type and incident heart failure and stroke using proportional hazards models. The Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board approved the study with a waiver of informed consent. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

We analyzed 2761 patients with ≥1 monitor, including 269 incident AF cases (9.7%), comprising 225 paroxysmal (83.6%) and 44 persistent (16.4%; Figure A1A). Of 44 persistent AF cases, 28 (63.6%) had non‐AF rhythm on ECG in the preceding year. Baseline characteristics are shown in the Figure B1B. Left atrial size was modestly larger among persistent (43.7 mm) versus paroxysmal (40.2 mm) cases (P<0.01). Median interval between ECG and monitor‐detected AF was 10.1 years (interquartile range, 5.6–13.7). ECG‐AI AS discriminated monitor‐detected AF (AUC, 0.722 [95% CI, 0.692–0.752]). Among AF cases, ECG‐AI AS had favorable discrimination of persistent AF (AUC, 0.680 [95% CI, 0.598–0.762]) versus age and sex (95% CI, 0.605 [95% CI, 0.514–0.696]; P=0.03). ECG‐AI AS had similar discrimination of ≥10% burden (95% CI, 0.673 [95% CI, 0.595–0.753]). Among 2343 with complete data, ECG‐AI AS had higher discrimination than reweighted CHARGE‐AF for monitor‐detected AF (AUC, 0.727 [95% CI, 0.692–0.762] versus 0.693 [95% CI, 0.655–0.731]; P=0.06) and persistent AF (AUC, 0.674 [95% CI, 0.573–0.775] versus 0.636 [95% CI, 0.533–0.739]; P=0.40), though differences were not significant (Figure C1C). ECG‐AI was associated with persistent AF in models adjusted for age and sex (odds ratio, 1.49 per 1‐SD difference [95% CI, 1.11–2.00]) and reweighted CHARGE‐AF (odds ratio, 1.48 [95% CI, 1.07–2.05]; Figure D1D). ECG‐AI was also associated with higher continuous AF burden when adjusted for age and sex (15.6% per 1‐SD difference [95% CI, 6.3–24.9]) and reweighted CHARGE‐AF (11.3% [95% CI, 1.3–21.3]), but not when persistent AF was excluded (1.2% [95% CI, −3.9 to 6.3]; 0.37% [95% CI, −5.8 to 6.5]). Persistent AF was associated with higher heart failure and stroke incidence versus paroxysmal AF (hazard ratio, 3.10 [95% CI, 1.10–8.71]; Figure E1E).

Study summary.

The study design and results are summarized: (A) summarizes patient flow; (B) tabulates baseline characteristics for individuals with incident AF detected by monitor; (C) plots receiver operating characteristic curves for discrimination of persistent AF using ECG‐AI AS vs age and sex alone (gray) and ECG‐AI AS vs CHARGE‐AF (orange). (D) depicts the adjusted odds of persistent AF per one SD increase in logit‐transformed ECG‐AI probability for models adjusted for age and sex (dark green) and CHARGE‐AF (light green); (E) depicts the cumulative incidence of subsequent HF and stroke after monitor‐detected paroxysmal (red) vs persistent AF (blue), with the shaded area representing 95% CIs. AF indicates atrial fibrillation; AUC, area under the curve; BP, blood pressure; CHARGE‐AF, Cohorts for Heart and Aging Research in Genomic Epidemiology Atrial Fibrillation; ECG‐AI, ECG‐based artificial intelligence; ECG‐AI AS, ECG‐based artificial intelligence plus age and sex; and HF, heart failure.

Among ≈3000 individuals without baseline AF, we identified monitor‐detected incident AF and quantified AF type and burden in >250 patients. Our validated ECG‐AI AF risk model 3 discriminated monitor‐detected AF and showed strong associations with greater AF burden, driven by persistent AF.

Current AF screening approaches are inefficient, partially due to detection of low‐burden AF. Here, we demonstrate that ECG‐AI may select screening candidates more likely to develop persistent or high‐burden AF. Such individuals may derive particular benefit from prompt AF diagnosis and preventive therapies given their higher risk for adverse outcomes. 1 Indeed, we observed a >3‐fold hazard of stroke and heart failure with monitor‐detected persistent versus paroxysmal AF.

Limitations are noted. Discrimination of persistent AF (ie, a task more challenging than AF identification) was moderate. Given the small sample size, we assessed a validated ECG‐AI model without modification. Precision of estimates remains limited, and future work should investigate model fine‐tuning and training specifically for AF burden. AF classification used monitor‐based burden rather than specific durations. We cannot distinguish frequency of long‐standing persistent or permanent AF, but most individuals had non‐AF rhythm within 1 year before persistent AF diagnosis. Intermediate AF burdens were not well‐represented. We did not evaluate serial ECG‐AI predictions, which may inform optimal ECG follow‐up.

A validated ECG‐based AI AF risk model classifies individuals more likely to develop persistent or high‐burden AF. Future work is warranted to assess whether ECG‐based AI may improve the effectiveness of AF screening and preventive interventions.
