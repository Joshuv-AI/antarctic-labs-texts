Investigating the relationship between blood factors and HDL-C levels in the bloodstream using machine learning methods
Authors: Somayeh Ghiasi Hafezi, Marzieh Hosseini, Mahtab Panahi, Sahar Arab Yousefabadi, Amin Mansoori, Iman Atighi, Mark Ghamsary, Gordon Ferns, Habibollah Esmaily, Majid Ghayour-Mobarhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe study investigates the relationship between blood lipid components and metabolic disorders, specifically high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), which is crucial for cardiovascular health. It uses logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), XGBoost (XGB), and neural networks (NN) algorithms to explore how blood factors affect HDL-C levels in the bloodstream.MethodThe study involved 9704 participants, categorized into normal and low HDL-C levels. Introduction

Recent advances in understanding high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) functionality have revolutionized its role beyond the traditional label of “good cholesterol.” HDL-C, comprising 25–30% of bloodstream proteins, is intricately involved in lipid transport and cardiovascular health, with emerging evidence suggesting its broader influence on metabolic and inflammatory pathways [1, 2]. These insights have shifted the research focus toward exploring dynamic interactions of HDL-C with physiological systems, particularly its interplay with hematological parameters, which are increasingly recognized as key players in cardiometabolic health [3, 4]. Hematological factors—such as white blood cell (WBC) counts, platelet indices, and inflammatory markers like the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII)—are not only prognostic indicators in blood disorders but also potential modulators of lipid metabolism [3, 5, 6]. This evolving understanding underscores the clinical significance of hematological parameters in shaping HDL-C levels and their collective impact on conditions like metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease.

Despite these advances, existing studies on the relationship between HDL-C and hematological factors reveal critical limitations. Many investigations rely on traditional statistical methods that struggle to capture the multidimensional and nonlinear nature of these interactions [7, 8]. Cross-sectional designs often dominate, limiting insights into causality, while population-specific variations in lipid profiles—such as differences between men (42 mg/dL) and women (46–50 mg/dL) globally and in regions like Iran—complicate generalizability [9, 10]. Moreover, conflicting findings, such as the paradoxical association of higher HDL-C with increased mortality in some cohorts [11], highlight the need for more robust analytical approaches to disentangle these complex relationships. Traditional methodologies often fall short in handling large datasets with multiple variables, leaving subtle patterns and predictive trends undetected—a gap particularly evident in lipidomic research where intricate biological networks are at play.

The current study introduces an innovative approach to address these challenges by leveraging machine learning (ML) algorithms— logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), XGBoost (XGB), and neural networks (NN)—to investigate the association between HDL-C levels and hematological factors. Unlike conventional methods, ML excels in modeling nonlinear, multidimensional data, offering superior predictive power and the ability to uncover hidden patterns critical for personalized medicine [12–16]. For instance, LR provides interpretable odds ratios, DT offers intuitive hierarchical insights into variable importance, and RF enhances accuracy through ensemble learning, reducing overfitting and improving generalization [17–19]. These algorithms have proven effective in similar contexts, such as predicting metabolic syndrome using hematological markers [20] or identifying disease trends in large cohorts [12], demonstrating their value in biomedical research. By applying these tools to a large cohort from the Mashhad Stroke and Heart Atherosclerotic Disorder (MASHAD) study, this research pioneers their use in exploring HDL-C dynamics, offering a scalable framework for lipidomic and beyond.

This study investigates the influence of hematological factors, such as the platelets-to-HDL-C ratio (PHR) and SII, on HDL-C levels. It examines gender-specific patterns in these associations and explores how ML-driven insights can advance our understanding of cardiometabolic health. These questions hold theoretical significance by deepening our grasp of inflammation-lipid interplay and clinical value by identifying novel markers (e.g., PHR) for disease prevention and treatment. By systematically integrating advances in HDL-C research, the clinical relevance of hematological parameters, and the power of ML, this study not only overcomes the limitations of prior work but also sets a new standard for investigating lipid-related metabolic disorders.

Method

Study population

The MASHAD study, a cross-sectional study from northeastern Iran, involved 3,885 male and 5,819 female participants. Normal serum HDL-C levels were defined as > 40 mg/dL in males and > 50 mg/dL in females, while low HDL-C levels were ≤ 40 mg/dl in males and ≤ 50 mg/dl in females [16]. The study found a prevalence of low serum HDL-C in the Mashhad population of 57.4% in males and 73.8% in females (see Fig. 1). Participants completed written consent forms and the Ethical Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences approved the study protocol.

Baseline examination

Blood samples were collected from participants between 8 and 10 a.m. after a 14-hour overnight fast via venipuncture of an antecubital vein. In accordance with a conventional methodology, samples were collected in vacuum tubes (20 ml) from people seated in a chair. All blood samples were centrifuged at room temperature for 30–45 min to separate the serum from the whole blood into six aliquots (0.5 ml each). They were later delivered to the Bu Ali Research Institute in Mashhad. To preserve the samples for future study, serum aliquots were frozen at -80 °C. If serum triglycerides (TGs) concentrations were less than 400 mg/dl, LDL-C was calculated from TGs, serum total cholesterol (TC), and HDL-C concentrations expressed in mg/dl using the Friedewald formula [10]. Dyslipidemia was defined as TC > 200 mg/dl, LDL-C ≥ 130 mg/dl, TGs ≥ 150 mg/dl, or HDL-C < 40 mg/dl in men and 50 mg/dl in women [11].

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using R Statistical Software (v4.1.2; R Core Team 2021), using Python Software (v3.13.2 (default, Jun 28 2025, 15:17:03) [GCC 11.4.0]; Python Software Foundation) and IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27). Continuous and normal variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables are expressed as median (lower and upper quartiles) and frequency (percentage). We regarded a P-value less than 0.05 to be statistically significant. We utilized the t-test for continuous and normal data, the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous and non-normal variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables to compare the mean, median, and percentage of HDL-C levels. HDL-C levels of ≤ 50 for men and ≤ 40 for women were considered excessive. We tested for multicollinearity among independent variables using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and correlation coefficients, noting that a correlation greater than 0.95 suggested a high possibility of multicollinearity.

The odds ratios (OR) with their 95% confidence intervals were computed using LR based on three models that included the variables Sex, WBC, LHR (Lymphocyte/high-density lipoprotein), NHR (neutrophil/high-density), and BMI (Body Mass Index). LR yielded low and normal HDL-C (model A). This model was evaluated independently for each gender (models B and C).

Machine learning models

In this study, we employed a comprehensive ML framework to analyze the data and predict the target outcome. Multiple algorithms, including DT, KNN, LR, XGB, RF, and NN, were utilized to ensure robust and diverse modeling approaches. KNN facilitated similarity-based predictions, LR established linear feature-outcome relationships, XGB enhanced predictive performance through gradient boosting, RF improved accuracy via ensemble DTs, and NNs captured complex non-linear patterns. To optimize model reliability, the dataset was partitioned into training (75%) and testing (25%) sets to mitigate overfitting. Additionally, statistical significance was assessed using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction via the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (implemented with R’s p.adjust function), with adjusted P-values < 0.05 considered statistically robust. This multi-model approach ensured a rigorous evaluation of predictive performance while maintaining methodological rigor in controlling for overfitting and multiple testing biases.

K-nearest neighbors

The KNN algorithm is widely recognized as a robust baseline model for binary classification tasks [21, 22]. KNN’s simplicity and effectiveness in capturing local patterns within data make it a popular choice for initial experimentation and comparison in research studies. It is a robust benchmark against which more complex algorithms can be evaluated. Moreover, its intuitive nature allows for straightforward interpretation of results, aiding in understanding underlying relationships within medical datasets.

Logistic regression model

LR is a widely utilized statistical method employed for binary classification tasks. Unlike linear regression, LR predicts the probability of occurrence of a binary outcome based on one or more predictor variables. It models the relationship between the independent variables and the probability of the outcome using the logistic function, which ensures that the predicted probabilities fall within the range of 0 and 1. LR provides interpretable coefficients that signify the impact of each predictor variable on the likelihood of the outcome. These coefficients can be exponentiated to obtain odds ratios (OR), representing the change in odds of the outcome for a one-unit change in the predictor variable. This feature aids in understanding the magnitude and direction of the association between predictor variables and the outcome, enhancing the interpretability of the model within the medical context [23].

Random forest model

Ensemble learning is a ML technique in which multiple models, often of the same type or different types, are combined to improve predictive performance over any individual model. Random Forest is a powerful ensemble learning technique. It operates by constructing multiple decision trees during the training phase. Each tree in the forest is trained on a random subset of the training data and a random subset of the features, promoting diversity and reducing overfitting. The final prediction is determined by aggregating the predictions of all trees, resulting in a robust and accurate classifier capable of handling large datasets with high-dimensional feature spaces [24].

XGB model

Our classification task employed an XGB model for Extreme Gradient Boosting. This ensemble learning technique leverages the power of multiple decision trees to create a more robust and accurate predictor. XGB builds these trees sequentially, with each new tree focusing on correcting the errors made by the previous ones. This is achieved by employing a gradient boosting framework, where the training process iteratively minimizes a loss function that measures the difference between predicted and actual outcomes. XGB’s strength lies in its ability to handle complex relationships within the data and its effectiveness in reducing overfitting through regularization techniques. This makes it a powerful tool for various classification problems [25].

Decision tree model

The DT was drawn using a data mining approach to find a predictive model of HDL-C measurements [5, 26, 27]. In current study the CHIAD technique in DT was applied using chi-square tests. The successive splits specified the order of significance of the predictor variables. The tree was structured with a maximum of three levels, where the parent node contained 100 samples, and each child node contained 50 samples. The Pearson chi-square statistic was used to assess associations between predictor variables, and the Bonferroni method was applied to adjust the significance values, reducing the likelihood of Type I errors. The DT consists of components, nodes, and branches. So that, there are three types of nodes: (1) a root node represents the result of subdividing all records into two or more exclusive subsets. (2) The internal nodes represent a possible point in the tree structure connected to the root node from the top and the leaf nodes from the bottom. (3) Leaf nodes that show the tree’s final results in dividing records into target groups. Branches in the tree indicate the chance of placing records in target groups that emanate from the root node and the internal nodes. DT algorithm uses the Gini impurity index for selecting the best variable.

Neural network model

The second classification algorithm employed in this study was a NN. NNs are inspired by the structure and function of the human brain and consist of interconnected layers of artificial neurons. Each neuron receives weighted inputs from the previous layer, performs a simple mathematical operation on those inputs, and then passes the activation value to the next layer. This allows the network to learn complex, non-linear relationships between the input features and the desired output classes [28, 29]. In the context of classification, the final layer uses a sigmoid activation function, which transforms the outputs into a probability distribution across the two final classes. This paper used a 4-layer NN with 32, 16, 4, and 1 neuron, respectively.

Explanations and feature importance

We utilize SHAP values to have integrated explanations between models. SHAP values borrow from game theory to explain how individual features contribute to a model’s prediction. Imagine each feature is a player in a team effort, and the prediction is the outcome. SHAP values calculate the average marginal contribution of each feature by considering all possible orders in which features are included in the model. This way, it assigns fair credit to each feature for its impact on the final prediction [31]. In the case of this paper, we use the SHAP python package to calculate the SHAP values of our features, and we can interpret them as the feature importance of a model. We also could use the legacy feature, which comes from some classical ML models, but in this way, we cannot have a balanced and fair comparison across different models.

This suggests that the XGB model relies more heavily on “NHR” when making predictions than the LR model. Also, the table in female shows that “LHR” has a higher SHAP importance score for the LR model (69.339) than for the XGB model (5.8208). This suggests that the XGB model relies more heavily on “LHR” when making predictions than the LR model.

Discussion

This study investigated the association between hematological parameters and HDL-C levels using a multi-algorithmic ML framework, including LR, DT, RF, XGB, KNN, and NN models. The incorporation of these advanced techniques enabled a more comprehensive exploration of predictive features, classification accuracy, and potential non-linear interactions.

Novel indices derived from hematologic and lipid ratios—such as PHR, NHR, MHR, LHR, and SII—demonstrated strong associations with HDL-C levels and served as accessible surrogate biomarkers of low-grade systemic inflammation [26, 33]. These indices were prioritized across multiple models and aligned with established evidence suggesting that chronic inflammation contributes to lipid dysregulation and cardiovascular risk.

The LR model demonstrated the highest predictive performance overall, with an AUC-ROC score of 0.99 and high precision, recall, and specificity, making it the most balanced and interpretable algorithm in this analysis. In SHAP-based feature attribution, NHR and LHR emerged as dominant predictors of HDL-C levels in males and females, respectively, highlighting gender-specific differences in hematologic-lipid interactions. These indices showed a strong positive correlation with the risk of lower HDL-C, aligning with previous studies that reported inverse associations between neutrophilic inflammation and HDL-C function [3, 7].

The SHAP bee swarm analysis further confirmed that increased levels of WBC, NEUT, and LYM contributed to decreased HDL-C in both sexes, though the magnitude of impact varied. In males, NHR was the most influential predictor across LR and XGB models, while LHR dominated in the female subgroup, followed by LYM. These findings emphasize the importance of immune cell profiles in modulating lipid homeostasis and support existing evidence that HDL-C particles are functionally altered in inflammatory states through mechanisms such as cytokine-driven displacement of apolipoprotein A-I by acute-phase proteins like serum amyloid A [8, 27].

The SII index was consistently identified as an important predictor across all models. This reinforces prior findings indicating that elevated SII is associated with lower HDL-C levels and heightened cardiovascular risk [34, 35]. Given HDL-C’s role in reverse cholesterol transport and endothelial protection, the inverse relationship with systemic inflammation markers like SII is biologically plausible [36].

The gender-stratified DT analysis revealed NHR, NEUT, and WBC as critical predictors of HDL-C in males, while LHR and LYM were most important in females. These results support prior findings that females typically have higher HDL-C levels than males, which may be influenced by hormonal, genetic, or immunological factors [37]. The use of DT allowed for clinically relevant rule-based stratification, which could aid in identifying high-risk individuals based on specific hematologic thresholds.

PDW was also highlighted as a significant predictor in both sexes and across multiple models. An inverse relationship between PDW and HDL-C was consistently observed, in line with prior studies associating PDW with platelet activation and cardiovascular events [38, 39]. This suggests that elevated platelet reactivity may accompany dyslipidemia and further link hematologic activation with atherosclerotic processes.

Our results collectively suggest that hematological inflammation plays a pivotal role in HDL-C homeostasis. While causality cannot be confirmed, the repeated identification of inflammation-related indices (e.g., NHR, LHR, SII, PDW) across diverse ML models and interpretability frameworks highlights their biological and clinical relevance. This supports the potential utility of these indices not only in risk prediction but also as therapeutic targets in cardiometabolic disorders.

From a methodological perspective, this study benefits from several strengths. First, the use of six ML models enabled robust comparative evaluation and provided insights into both linear and non-linear associations. Second, SHAP analysis added interpretability to black-box models like NN and XGB, allowing transparent ranking of predictors. Third, the inclusion of a large, gender-balanced cohort (N = 9,704) enhanced the generalizability of findings and allowed exploration of sex-specific hematologic-lipid patterns.

Collectively, the findings underscore a complex but consistent relationship between hematological inflammation and HDL-C levels. ML models, particularly LR and XGB, effectively identified key predictive indices such as NHR and LHR, which may be clinically useful for early risk detection. These results suggest that low-cost, routinely available hematological parameters can enrich lipid-related cardiovascular risk stratification. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are warranted to validate these markers as therapeutic targets and to refine gender-specific risk models for precision medicine applications in cardiovascular health.

Conclusion

In general, our study shows that inflammation can affect the relationship between mixed blood factors, and the identification of these factors can help in the preventive treatment of inflammation and metabolic disorders. There are several limitations to the current body of evidence. Cause-effect relationships were not firmly established due to the inherent limitations of cross-sectional and retrospective analyses. Prospective investigations are needed to validate reported associations and determine if modulating hematological inflammation causally impacts outcomes. Characterizing temporal relationships using longitudinal follow-up could provide mechanistic insights. Elucidating specific pathways like ApoM-S1P signaling that link inflammation with HDL-C regulation and disease pathogenesis represents a promising avenue. Further exploring therapeutic potential of modulating ABC transporter activity is merited. Incorporating -omics approaches could provide a more comprehensive perspective on interactions between inflammation, hematopoiesis and disease endpoints. Defining minimal clinically important differences for hematological markers would support their adoption into practice. Larger, more diverse populations are required to strengthen generalizability. Additionally examining diet, lifestyle, and nutritional factors could uncover modifiable risk correlates. Determining prognostic ability independent of traditional metrics would support clinical adoption.
