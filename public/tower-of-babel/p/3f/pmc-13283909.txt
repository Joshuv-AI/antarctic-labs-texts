Characterizing treatment initiation with central nervous system–active polypharmacy among adults with dementia
Authors: Donovan T. Maust, Rachel C. Davis, Julie Strominger, Steven C. Marcus, Hyungjin Myra Kim, Tanner Caverly, Frederic C. Blow, Lauren P. Wallner, Sarah Krein, Sarah E. Vordenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCentral nervous system (CNS) –active polypharmacy, defined as concurrent use of antidepressants, antipsychotics, anti‐seizure medications, benzodiazepines, nonbenzodiazepine benzodiazepine receptor agonists, opioids, or skeletal muscle relaxants for ≥ 31 consecutive days, poses significant risks patients with dementia.METHODSAmong community‐dwelling patients in Medicare plans aged ≥ 65 with dementia and Medicare Part D coverage in 2021, we determined the rate of CNS‐active polypharmacy initiation as well as medication and prescribing clinician characteristics.RESULTSAmong beneficiaries with dementia (n = 1,214,928, mean age 81.6 years), 7.8% (n = 94,190) experienced incident polypharmacy. Antidepressants were the most frequent class (92.3%), and quetiapine (30.9%), gabapentin (29.0%), and trazodone (28.3%) the top individual medications. At the time of initiation, 43.8% of patients were prescribed all CNS‐active medications by a single clinician; primary care clinicians accounted for most polypharmacy prescriptions.DISCUSSIONInterventions to address polypharmacy should focus on development of guidelines targeting individual prescribers to help clarify appropriate use of CNS‐active medications.

BACKGROUND

Approximately 10% of adults over the age of 65 years in the United States have dementia, 1 and the absolute number of Americans with dementia will more than double to nearly 14 million by 2060. 2 These individuals often have multiple chronic conditions and, along with those conditions, they experience a higher burden of polypharmacy than other older adults. 3 The American Geriatrics Society (AGS) Beers Criteria identify central nervous system (CNS) –active polypharmacy—concurrent use of three or more medications, including antidepressants, antipsychotics, anti‐seizure medications, benzodiazepines, nonbenzodiazepine benzodiazepine receptor agonists (i.e., “z‐drugs”), opioids, and skeletal muscle relaxants—as a specific type of polypharmacy that is potentially inappropriate for older adults. 4 While increased risk of falls is cited as the specific rationale, these classes by themselves or in combination are associated with additional risks, including impaired cognition 5 and respiratory suppression or death (especially when involving opioids). 6

Given these risks, the prevalence of CNS‐active polypharmacy among adults with dementia is startling: in prior work, we found the prevalence in the community to be approximately 14%. 7 , 8 Moreover, the median length of polypharmacy among individuals with dementia who are exposed was 193 days, which means patients are exposed to these potentially harmful combinations for a prolonged period of time. However, this prior work examined cross‐sectional prevalence rather than polypharmacy at the time of initiation, understanding of which is essential to promote more appropriate prescribing. In addition, this prior work did not include Medicare Advantage, which now enrolls for more than half of Medicare beneficiaries. 9

Given the difficulties of stopping medications once initiated, 10 efforts to promote appropriate prescribing may benefit from a more nuanced understanding of how CNS‐active polypharmacy starts. To explore this, we created a cohort of all community‐dwelling Medicare fee‐for‐service and Medicare Advantage beneficiaries with dementia in the United States at risk for incident CNS‐active polypharmacy. First, we determined the amount of new (incident) polypharmacy during 1 year of observation. Then we characterized patients with and without incident polypharmacy and compared their demographics, clinical characteristics, and service utilization. We hypothesized that patients already prescribed CNS‐active medications at baseline would be at higher risk of polypharmacy initiation, along with those with more unique clinicians who prescribed their baseline medications. Finally, we characterized incident polypharmacy by examining contributing drug classes, specific medications, and prescribing clinicians.

METHODS

Cohort identification

The study cohort was drawn from patients aged 65 or older with 12 months of continuous Medicare coverage (fee‐for‐service or Medicare Advantage) in 2020 (Appendix Figure 1). We identified individuals with dementia using International Classification of Diseases – 1oth Revision (ICD‐10) diagnosis codes from healthcare encounters in 2020 using the Bynum‐Standard algorithm, which requires patients to have one or more inpatient claim or two or more outpatient claims at least 7 days apart. 11 We then limited the cohort to community‐dwelling patients by excluding those with a quarterly or annual Minimum Data Set assessment in 2020. 12 Because our analysis included baseline characteristics related to prescription medications, we required patients to have Medicare Part D prescription drug coverage in the final 3 months (i.e., October–December) of 2020.

Follow‐up began among individuals who were alive on January 1, 2021. Because our outcome of interest (CNS‐active polypharmacy initiation) required prescription drug coverage to be observed, we required Part D coverage on January 1, 2021. We excluded patients who were already experiencing polypharmacy on January 1, 2021, because they were not at risk of incident CNS‐active polypharmacy.

Outcome

Our outcome of interest was CNS‐active polypharmacy initiation. CNS‐active polypharmacy was defined as concurrent exposure to three or more medications from the following classes: antidepressants, antipsychotics, antiseizure medications, benzodiazepines, nonbenzodiazepine benzodiazepine receptor agonists (“z‐drugs”), opioids, and skeletal muscle relaxants (Appendix Table 1). 4 Daily medication exposure was measured using medication name, fill date, and days’ supply from the Medicare Part D Event file. Overlapping prescription fills of the same medication were pushed forward under the assumption that a patient would not use the new prescription until they finished the previous one. As in our prior work, we required polypharmacy to have more than 30 consecutive days of concurrent exposure, 7 , 8 that is, any 30‐day prescription would require a refill to reach day 31, suggesting the overlapping prescriptions were part of ongoing treatment rather than just short‐term treatment. Polypharmacy initiation occurred on day 31 of medication overlap.

Patients were followed from January 1, 2021, until they experienced polypharmacy initiation, lost Medicare Part D coverage, entered a skilled nursing facility, entered hospice, died, or observation ended (i.e., January 31, 2022). Because we excluded patients who were already experiencing a polypharmacy episode on January 1, 2021, the earliest date that three or more overlapping medications could begin was January 2, 2021; therefore, because January has 31 days, the earliest date that a patient could experience polypharmacy initiation (defined as day 31 of overlap) was February 1, 2021. To allow for 365 days of follow‐up during which patients could experience the outcome, we ended follow‐up on January 31, 2022 (i.e., 365 days after February 1, 2021). After identifying patients who experienced polypharmacy initiation, we examined medication and clinical characteristics on the day of initiation.

Baseline characteristics

We measured baseline (i.e., 2020) patient characteristics including age, race, Medicare Advantage enrollment, and low‐income status (defined as Medicaid enrollment or being eligible for the Part D Low‐Income Subsidy) using the Medicare Master Beneficiary Summary File. Rurality was computed using Rural‐Urban Commuting Area Codes, where a code of 7 or higher was considered rural. We identified the presence of clinical diagnoses including anxiety, depression, insomnia, and pain using ICD‐10 diagnosis codes from Medicare claims/encounters. Prescription characteristics, including CNS‐active medication exposure and number of clinicians who prescribed a medication during baseline, were computed using the Part D Event file, which contains information about which medications each patient was prescribed and which clinician prescribed each medication. Medicare visit information was used to identify patients with an emergency room visit or an outpatient visit with a neurologist or psychiatrist during baseline.

Statistical analysis

We examined the distribution of baseline characteristics including demographic, clinical, prescription, and service use characteristics among patients who experienced polypharmacy initiation compared to those who did not. Standardized differences were computed to determine the magnitude of the differences in distributions between the two groups 13 ; we considered an absolute standardized difference < 0.1 to be negligible. 14 In addition, we used Cox proportional hazards to examine factors associated with time to incident polypharmacy, adjusting for baseline characteristics.

Among patients who experienced CNS‐active polypharmacy, we characterized medications prescribed on the day of initiation. Specifically, we counted the number of CNS‐active medications and classes, computed the number of unique prescribing clinicians, and identified each prescribing clinician's specialty by linking each clinician's National Provider Identifier (NPI) from the Part D Event file to their taxonomy code from the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System. 15 Using polypharmacy initiation day information, we computed the top 10 most common CNS‐active medications, medication combinations, and medication classes. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4.

RESULTS

Our cohort included 1,214,928 Medicare beneficiaries with dementia at risk for CNS‐active polypharmacy in 2021 (Table 1). Mean age was 81.6 years (standard deviation 7.5); 68.1% were non‐Hispanic White, and 7.7% were rural. Overall, while 197,816 (16.3%) of patients experienced at least one day of concurrent exposure to 3 or more CNS‐active medications, 94,190 patients (7.8%) met our definition of CNS‐active polypharmacy initiation with > 30 days of concurrent exposure in 2021.

There were limited baseline demographic differences between those who did and did not experience polypharmacy initiation, though the latter were slightly younger (79.1 vs. 81.8 years, standardized mean difference [SMD] 0.36]). All clinical conditions were more prevalent among those for whom polypharmacy was initiated. During the last 3 months of 2020, patients initiating polypharmacy received prescriptions (of any type) from more unique clinicians (3.2 vs. 2.5; SMD 0.40) than those without polypharmacy initiation. In the adjusted model (Appendix Table 2), non‐White ethnicity, increasing age, and increasing Elixhauser score were all associated with lower risk of initiation. The measures of baseline CNS‐active exposure had the largest risks associated with initiation (e.g., baseline antidepressant exposure was associated with an adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] of 3.957 [95% confidence interval (CI) 3.893, 4.022]), while having more clinicians prescribing at baseline was also associated with higher risk of initiation (aHR 1.015, CI 1.011, 1.019).

While most patients (82.7%) who initiated polypharmacy did so with exposure to three CNS‐active medications, 14.5% had overlapping exposure to four medications, and 2.9% to five or more (Table 2). The most common contributing medication class was antidepressants (92.3% of those initiating polypharmacy), followed by antiseizure medications (54.5%), antipsychotics (49.8%), and benzodiazepines (28.9%). The top five contributing medications were quetiapine (30.9%), gabapentin (29.0%), trazodone (28.3%), sertraline (24.0%), and mirtazapine (21.2%).

Among patients who had polypharmacy initiated, the CNS‐active medications were prescribed by one or two clinicians for 43.8% and 44.1% of patients, respectively; three clinicians were responsible for 11.5% of patients. Only 0.6% of patients had medications prescribed by four or more clinicians. The most common prescribers of incident polypharmacy were primary care physicians (62.6% of patients) and primary care nurse practitioners or physician assistants (33.7%); 15.9% and 15.5% received prescriptions from psychiatrists and neurologists, respectively.

DISCUSSION

In this national analysis of all community‐dwelling Medicare beneficiaries with dementia in both traditional Medicare and Medicare Advantage, we found that, during 1 year of follow‐up, 7.8% experienced new CNS‐active polypharmacy of more than 30 days of overlapping prescriptions of three or more antidepressant, antipsychotic, antiseizure, benzodiazepine, z‐drug, opioid, or skeletal muscle relaxants medications. This builds on our prior work that established prevalence in two key ways: 7 focusing on the critical initiation phase and including the majority of Medicare beneficiaries that are enrolled in Medicare Advantage.

Considering the specific medications prescribed, these incident CNS‐active polypharmacy findings align with our prior work, with antidepressants, antiseizure medications, and antipsychotics the top classes. Both benzodiazepines and opioids, however, are less common as contributors to incident polypharmacy (28.9% and 18.3%, respectively) compared to prevalent polypharmacy (40.7% and 32.3%), 7 which may suggest that clinicians are aware of the heightened risks of these classes and at least do not initiate polypharmacy with them. Supporting this, we found that, even though 23.0% of patients with dementia exposed to incident CNS‐active polypharmacy had been exposed to an opioid during the last 3 months of 2020 (i.e., baseline exposure), just 18.3% had an opioid as part of incident polypharmacy. In contrast, all other medication classes were more common contributors to polypharmacy compared to their extent of baseline use, most notably antipsychotics (49.8% vs. 31.8%), antiseizure medications (54.5% vs. 38.6%), and antidepressants (92.3% vs. 77.0%).

It is striking that, among those that experienced incident polypharmacy, 92.3% are being prescribed at least one antidepressant as a component. There is minimal evidence to support their use for agitation 16 —most notably for citalopram, 17 which only contributed to one of the most common combinations—and evidence does not support their use for depression in dementia. 18 , 19 Trazodone and mirtazapine are potentially being used for sleep disturbances, but only trazodone has some evidence to support this indication. 20 It is unclear why quetiapine would be the most prevalent individual medication contributing to incident polypharmacy when other antipsychotics have more evidence supporting use for behavioral symptoms of dementia. 21 , 22 Similarly, a potential justification for gabapentin, the second‐most‐common polypharmacy contributor, is even less clear, as it has no placebo‐controlled studies for agitation in patients with dementia. 23 , 24 It is likely that these contributing medications are being prescribed for an array of off‐label indications other than agitation, such as insomnia (e.g., quetiapine, trazodone, mirtazapine) or chronic pain (e.g., gabapentin, duloxetine).

The clinician‐specific findings are notable: 43.8% of patients that experienced incident polypharmacy had CNS‐active medications prescribed by a single clinician; a similar percentage received medications from two individuals. This is in contrast to overall polypharmacy (i.e., not limited to CNS‐active medications) among older adults, which clinicians suggest is difficult to address because of diffusion of responsibility across multiple prescribers, 25 , 26 with potential solutions entailing better coordination between clinicians and settings. 27 While CNS‐active polypharmacy may reflect complex clinical situations, our findings suggest that solutions to facilitate more appropriate prescribing can begin by focusing on individual clinicians rather than on addressing coordination between multiple prescribers.

We found that more than 60% of beneficiaries who are prescribed polypharmacy received at least one medication from a primary care physician, and over 30% received at least one prescription from primary care nurse practitioners and non‐surgical physician assistants. This reflects the reality that the vast majority of clinical care provided to patients with dementia is provided by these primary care clinicians—in the outpatient setting, psychiatrists and neurologists account for just 2.4% and 1.7%, respectively, of clinicians who care for patients with dementia. 28 This suggests that supporting primary care clinicians, including training on the appropriate use of CNS‐active medications, may be a good first step, given that they care for a significant number of patients with dementia. However, implementation is likely to be constrained by existing barriers, such as limited time during medical appointments and insufficient reimbursement. 29 , 30

An alternative systems‐level strategy that has been proposed is comprehensive medication reviews to reduce the rate of CNS‐active polypharmacy, particularly as it was added as a display measure (i.e., not affecting plan ratings) for Medicare Part D in 2021. 31 However, in 2021, only one‐third of eligible beneficiaries with CNS‐active polypharmacy received this service, and it did not result in a reduction in the number of these medications. 32 , 33 Unfortunately, the lack of interoperability between community pharmacies and the traditional healthcare system decreases pharmacists’ ability to evaluate the appropriateness of medications and share recommendations with other members of the healthcare team. 34

This study has limitations, beginning with using claims to identify individuals with dementia, which have limited sensitivity and may not capture those with milder disease. Prescription claims reflect neither actual prescriptions (i.e., we may undercount polypharmacy if the patient pays out‐of‐pocket for a medication) nor medication consumption (i.e., patients fill a prescription but do not take the medication). In addition, we do not know the prescribing indication for each medication or patient's symptom burden, which limits our ability to comment on the appropriateness of the prescribing. Finally, we identified incident prescribing in 2021, relatively early in the coronavirus disease 2009 (COVID‐19) pandemic, though changes in psychotropic prescribing during the pandemic were relatively limited. 35

Our findings characterize the incident CNS‐active polypharmacy that nearly 8% of all community‐dwelling Medicare beneficiaries with dementia in the U.S. experience. The risks of such combination pharmacotherapy almost certainly outweigh the benefits given the vast majority of evidence does not support the efficacy of these medications in dementia. Given the growing population of older adults with dementia and the difficulty stopping medications once started, it is critical to limit initiation of this potentially inappropriate pharmacotherapy.
