ALG14 variants contribute to a congenital disorder of glycosylation characterized by congenital myasthenia and epilepsy

ABSTRACT
SummaryAsparagine-linked glycosylation 14 (ALG14) is a UDP-GlcNAc transferase that catalyzes the second sugar addition in the synthesis of the dolichol-linked oligosaccharide precursor in N-linked glycosylation, ultimately contributing to the glycosylation of a wide array of proteins. Bi-allelic pathogenic variants in ALG14 have been suggested to lead to a congenital disorder of glycosylation (CDG) due to incomplete lipid-linked oligosaccharide synthesis and ultimately hypoglycosylation of N-linked glycoproteins. In a cohort of nine previously unreported individuals with bi-allelic variants in ALG14 from eight unrelated families, we demonstrate evidence of a CDG that manifests as a congenital myasthenic syndrome, epilepsy, joint contractures, and dysmorphic features. Using a Xenopus knockdown model of disease, we explored the ramifications of alg14 depletion on early neurodevelopment and used this model as a platform to test the function of previously identified ALG14 missense variants, as well as missense variants from the individuals reported in this study. Investigations of Xenopus model tissue and tissue from an affected individual elucidated a mechanism by which dysfunctional glycosylation may lead to neurological disease and establish this as a bona fide CDG. In the future, these findings may guide the evaluation of individuals with suspected ALG14-CDG and targeted therapeutics such as those being used in other CDGs.Keywords: ALG14, congenital disorder of glycosylation, monogenic disease, myasthenia, epilepsy, Xenopus tropicalis

Introduction

A broad array of proteins requires the addition of glycans to facilitate proper folding, localization, and/or function.1,2,3 Asparagine-linked glycosylation 14 (ALG14) is a transmembrane endoplasmic reticulum (ER) protein that is in a heterodimeric complex with ALG13 and catalyzes the addition of the second GlcNAc-1-P from cytoplasmic UDP-GlcNAc in the synthesis of the dolichol-linked oligosaccharide precursor in N-linked glycosylation. Defects in this process of glycosylation at asparagine amino acids (N-linked glycosylation) seem to variably affect many different tissues.4,5,6,7 Indeed, variants in genes that encode proteins involved in N-linked glycosylation lead to a spectrum of disorders termed congenital disorders of glycosylation (CDGs).8,9 The severity of disease ranges from mild manifestations limited to a single organ system to complex multi-system disease.10,11,12,13

Variants in ALG14 have recently been described to contribute to a possible CDG in fewer than 30 individuals.14,15,16,17 This manifests clinically as a congenital myasthenic syndrome (CMS) or a broader progressive syndrome that can include epilepsy, joint contractures, and/or dysmorphic features, often in addition to a CMS. The subset of genetic causes of CDG that can result in a myasthenic syndrome includes those resulting from pathogenic variants in DPAGT1, ALG2, GFPT1, and GMPPB.14,18,19,20,21 While the resulting CDG can disrupt N-linked and/or O-linked glycosylation pathways, the resulting defects in glycosylation have been hypothesized to disrupt the structure and function of key proteins within the neuromuscular junction.22,23 One such protein, the acetylcholine receptor (AChR), has been evaluated in cases of these CDGs and appears to be decreased in abundance.14,18 Glycosylation is essential for proper folding, stability, and trafficking of AChR subunits and other synaptic proteins to the postsynaptic membrane.24,25 This could lead to impaired neuromuscular transmission and the clinical features of CMS resembling myasthenia gravis due to autoimmune effects on this same receptor.

While previous clinical reports are suggestive of bi-allelic pathogenic variants in ALG14 leading to a CDG, our ability to clinically diagnose this condition, even with the increasingly available clinical implementation of sequencing-based assays, is severely limited. The limited number of reported cases, incomplete clinical phenotyping, gaps in functional characterization of variants, and limited understanding of pathophysiological mechanisms hamper our ability to detect and diagnose this condition even when there is a high clinical index of suspicion. This has led consortia such as ClinGen to classify ALG14 as having only limited evidence for disease association.26 At the molecular level, functional studies in cell systems have demonstrated that some previously identified pathogenic variants in ALG14 disrupt the formation of the ALG14-ALG13 complex.27 While these approaches offer insights that will be invaluable in understanding the pathogenesis of ALG14-related disease, they are predicated on assaying potential impacts of variants on the ALG14-ALG13 complex and function. If a subset or even all variants in ALG14 result in disease through an alternative mechanism, these assays would be unlikely to faithfully evaluate the pathogenicity of a variant. Additionally, previous efforts have not provided us with insights into the organismal or even organ-level impacts of specific variants that might improve our ability to assign disease causality, and specifically CDG causality, to variants in ALG14.

In this study, we sought to bridge these gaps in our knowledge about ALG14-related disease and the potential for bi-allelic pathogenic variants in ALG14 to cause a CDG. To accomplish this, we identified an additional cohort of nine individuals with suspected ALG14-CDG from eight unrelated families and established a vertebrate model of ALG14-CDG by using morpholino oligonucleotides (MOs) to achieve knockdown of alg14 in Xenopus tadpoles. We used this model to assess for disease phenocopy and assess for resultant dysfunction in N-glycosylation of proteins and leveraged it to test the pathogenicity of the ALG14 variants detected in previous reports, as well as in this cohort.

Subjects and methods

Cohort identification

Individuals were identified through international collaborations, including through members of the Frontiers in Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation Consortium and Matchmaker Exchange. Inclusion was based on the presence of bi-allelic variants in ALG14 identified via exome or genome sequencing, along with a history of epilepsy, joint contractures, congenital myasthenia, and/or abnormal brain morphology.

Genetic testing

Genomic DNA underwent next-generation sequencing (Table 1) and copy-number variant calling in clinical laboratories. Individual VIII-3 underwent targeted testing for known familial variants. Sequencing reads were aligned to reference sequences based on NCBI RefSeq transcripts and the human genome GRCh37 hg19, except for sequencing reads for individual VII, which were aligned to GRCh38 hg38. Variants are described in relation to reference GenBank: NM_144988.4 for transcript changes and GenBank: NP_659425.1 for protein changes.

Chart review

Imaging and photos of individuals were obtained from their medical charts, with explicit permission provided by their legal guardians following informed consent.

RNA analysis in patient-derived lymphoblastoid cells

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-transformed lymphoblastoid cell lines were established from proband VII and a healthy control subject and cultured in triplicate in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Dominique Dutscher, South America Origin #S00G210001) and 1% ZellShield (Minerva Biolabs). Cultures were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2 with or without puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich) (40 μg/mL, 5 h) to inhibit nonsense-mediated decay (NMD). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sanger sequencing was performed as previously described.28

Immunofluorescence analysis in patient-derived lymphoblastoid cells

Immunofluorescence was performed in triplicate experiments on cytospin preparations of EBV-transformed lymphoblastoid cells from individual VII and a control subject, with and without puromycin treatment. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 20 min at room temperature (RT), incubated for 10 min in 50 mM NH4Cl (Sigma-Aldrich), and permeabilized in 2% PBS/BSA (Thermo Fisher Scientific)/0.3% Triton (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 7 min at RT. Cells were then incubated in PBS/1% BSA/0.01% Tween 20 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stained with anti-ALG14 antibody (antibody information included in Table S1) overnight at 4°C in a humid chamber. After three washes in PBS, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti-rabbit antibody (Invitrogen A11036) for 1 h at RT. After three washes in PBS, DAPI nuclear counterstain (Sigma-Aldrich1/2000) was added for 15 min at RT, followed by mounting in Permafluor (Epredia). Images were acquired using a Zeiss AX10 fluorescence microscope.

Xenopus husbandry

Xenopus tropicalis were raised, housed, and cared for in-house. We induced ovulation and collected embryos by in vitro fertilization as previously described.29 Embryos were raised in 1/9MR + gentamicin. Staging of Xenopus tadpoles was performed as previously described.30,31 Xenopus were raised at 25°C.

Xenopus mRNA and morpholino injection

Using standard protocols, we injected one-cell stage Xenopus embryos with 10 ng MO (alg14_splice-blocking_1: 5′-TCCTTTTTGTTTAATCACTCACCGT-3′, alg14_splice-blocking_2: 5′-GTGGACTACTTTAGAACTCACCTGA-3′, or control: 5′-CCTCTTACCTCAGTTACAATTTATA-3′; Gene Tools) or this same mixture along with 100 pg ALG14 mRNA generated from clone HsCD00358297 or mRNA encoding one of the ALG14 variants described in our cohort was synthesized using the T7 mMessage machine kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For glycosylation and subcellular localization studies in Xenopus, we injected 50 pg Chrna6-GFP mRNA (from Addgene plasmid #50486) into one-cell-stage Xenopus embryos following synthesis using the T7 mMessage machine kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s instructions, along with either control or alg14-targeting MOs.

Evaluation of alg14 depletion

The morpholinos used were designed to bind pre-mRNA and induce exon skipping, leading to a predicted frameshift and premature stop codon. While such events often trigger NMD, we evaluated Alg14 protein levels by immunoblotting to confirm the extent of depletion (antibody information included in Table S1).

Measuring tadpole movement and seizure activity

Stage 45 tadpole movement was assessed as previously described.32,33 Briefly, tadpoles were individually placed into wells of a standard 48-well culture dish. Prior to imaging, individual wells were aligned for video capture, and tadpoles were allowed to reach a resting state for at least 5 min. For myasthenia assays, tadpoles were gently prodded using a capillary pipette tip. Tadpoles were then allowed to reach a resting state for 1 min. Videos of tadpole movement were captured over 30 s after this second stimulation. Time points at initial stimulation and cessation of visible movement were used to determine movement time. For seizure assays, tadpoles were maintained in standard growth medium or exposed to a minimally phenotypic dose of pilocarpine (50 mM) in their growth medium and scored every 30 s over the course of 30 min to determine the percentage of time spent demonstrating seizure behavior as previously described.34,35 Videos were analyzed using Kinovea v.2023.1.2 (https://www.kinovea.org).

Splicing assays for evaluation of UPR

xbp1 splicing was assayed in stage 24 embryos as previously described32,36 with primers modified for use in Xenopus (5′-GACTGCTCGGGACAGGAAAA-3′ and 5′-GCCCAACAAGAGATCAGACTCA-3′). XBP1 splicing was assayed in muscle tissue from individual II as previously described37 with primers (5′-TGCTGAGTCCGCAGCAGGTG-3′ and reverse 5′-GCTGGCAGGCTCTGGGGAAG-3′).

Immunoblot analysis

Tissue lysis was completed in RIPA buffer, and immunoblots were performed with Bolt 4%–12% Bis-Tris plus gels and running buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using standard methods. Antibodies are listed in Table S1.

Fluorescence imaging

Samples were first fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, then permeabilized in 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS. Samples were mounted in Prolong Glass (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fluorescently labeled tissues were imaged on an Olympus APEXVIEW APX100 (tails, goblet cells, and muscle biopsy tissue) or a Zeiss LSM900 confocal microscope (brains and subcellular localization of Alg14). Reagents used for fluorescence labeling are listed in Table S1.

Image analysis

All fluorescent micrographs were processed and analyzed using Fiji.38 Quantitation of fluorescence was carried out by comparing the mean fluorescence of equal-sized regions of the embryonic epidermis (lectin labeling), single neuromuscular junction arcs (nicotinic AChRs [nAChRs]), or cortices (GABA receptors [GABARs]) between control and morphant embryos.

PCR analysis of receptor subunit expression

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Zymoresearch). Total RNA (0.5–1 μg) was reverse transcribed using the PrimeScript reverse transcriptase kit (Takara). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was carried out using the SYBR Premix ExTaqII SYBR Green mix (Takara). Primer concentrations of 300 nM were used. For statistical analysis, 3 biological replicates were completed for each experiment using the ΔΔCT method and normalized to odc1 reference gene expression. We used previously reported primers: odc1(5′- AGGCCACACTGGCAACTCA-3′ and 5′-TGCGCTCAGTTCTGGTACTTCA-3′),39 chrna7 (5′-TAGAATTCTTTGATCTCCTGCGATGGGAGGA-3′ and 5′-ATCTCGAGTAGAACTGCAGAACCCTAAGCAA-3′),40 and gabra1 (5′-CCCATGACTGTTCTCCGACT-3′ and 5′-TCCCGGTTTAGGTATGTTGC-3′).41

Sex as a biological variable

Both male and female Xenopus tadpoles were included in experimental analyses and used in approximately equal proportions based on the sex of tadpoles across clutches, although sex was not determined for every embryo utilized in this study.

Statistics

Statistical significance of differences measured between two groups was tested using Mann-Whitney U tests. ANOVA with post hoc Tukey’s test was used for comparison between multiple groups. p values less than 0.05 were deemed significant.

Study approval

The study protocol for inclusion of human subjects was approved by a central Clinical and Basic Investigations Into Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation IRB (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04199000) with local context review at Seattle Children’s Hospital.

Results

Genetic investigation

We identified nine individuals from eight unrelated families in whom genetic sequence analysis identified variants of interest in ALG14 (Figure 1). Fourteen of the 18 identified variants are missense, and the remaining variants are an insertion, a splice-site altering variant, and two nonsense variants. Eight variants have not been previously observed in individuals with suspected ALG14-CDG. Individual VIII-3 was born to a family with a history of previously reported affected children.17 Comprehensive clinical information regarding the probands can be found in Table 1. Of the nine individuals, two (individuals VI-2 and VII) underwent only prenatal evaluation. All identified probands who were evaluated postnatally (7/7) demonstrated hypotonia and developmental delay/intellectual disability. Muscle fatiguability and myasthenia were noted in 5/7 individuals evaluated postnatally. Epilepsy was present in 5/7 individuals evaluated postnatally. Abnormalities noted on intracranial imaging consisted of cerebral volume loss that could be observed both postnatally and prenatally. No clear craniofacial dysmorphic pattern was evident across probands. Contractures could also be observed both postnatally and prenatally. Those with carbohydrate-deficient transferrin testing (4/4) had results within the reference range. Notably, carbohydrate-deficient transferrin testing is often within the normal range in CDGs resulting from deficits early in the synthesis of lipid-linked oligosaccharides, such as in ALG2-CDG, ALG13-CDG, and DPAGT1-CDG.42 While a subset of CDGs can present with coagulopathies,43,44 this was not evident in the histories or laboratory evaluations of the individuals evaluated postnatally.

NMD escape can occur with nonsense variants in ALG14

As the c.68G>A (p.Trp23∗) variant identified in individual VII occurs early within the ALG14 coding sequence, it was thought to conceivably result in potential escape of the resulting transcript from NMD. To investigate this, we performed functional analyses using EBV-transformed lymphoblastoid cell lines derived from individual VII compared with a healthy control subject. These assays aimed to assess the impact of the variants on ALG14 transcript stability and protein accumulation to gain a window into the pathogenicity of the c.68G>A (p.Trp23∗) variant.

Surprisingly, Sanger sequencing of cDNA from proband-derived lymphoblastoid cells revealed the presence of both c.68G>A (p.Trp23∗) and c.310C>T (p.Arg104∗) across all three biological replicates, under both puromycin-treated and untreated conditions. Although the peak intensity of the mutant alleles was slightly reduced in the absence of puromycin, both transcripts remained readily detectable, indicating that neither variant is subject to complete degradation by NMD in this cellular context (Figure S1A).

Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated well-defined ALG14 staining in cells derived from individual VII and control cells, despite the antibody putatively recognizing a domain within amino acids 27–216. No significant differences in staining pattern or intensity were observed between puromycin-treated and untreated conditions. This further supports the persistence of ALG14 despite the presence of nonsense variants (Figure S1B).

A myasthenic phenotype occurs with alg14 depletion

Importantly, a major barrier to studying ALG14-CDG has been the severe phenotype in full knockout animals or cell lines, which is lethal. We hypothesized that we could model dysfunction in Xenopus by using a morpholino antisense oligonucleotide to diminish the abundance of alg14 mRNA in a manner that could recapitulate disease phenotypes while maintaining animal viability (Figure S2).

As CMS has been the most frequently described manifestation of ALG14-CDG, we sought to test the validity of our strategy by first assessing for myasthenia in alg14 morphant tadpoles. Following an initial external stimulus, stage 45 tadpoles were allowed to rest for 30 s prior to a second external stimulus. Time spent moving in response to this second stimulus was then measured. This allowed us to gauge the fatiguability of these tadpoles in a way that might mirror congenital myasthenia. Remarkably, alg14 morphant tadpoles demonstrated a drastically increased fatiguability compared to their control counterparts. Their average post-stimulus movement time was 0.98 s, while control tadpoles had a post-stimulus time of 5.64 s (Figure 2; Videos S1, S2, S3, and S4; p < 0.0001). Similarly, their average post-stimulus movement distance was 1.10 mm, while control tadpoles had a post-stimulus time of 14.39 mm (Figure 2; Videos S1, S2, S3, and S4; p < 0.0001).

Seizure activity occurs with alg14 depletion

Previous studies have characterized atypical tadpole movement or positioning as characteristic of seizure activity.34,35 These appear as unprovoked “C-shaped” positioning, “circling,” or “darting” (Figure 3). We assessed the prevalence of these behaviors in alg14 morphants as compared to control tadpoles as a negative control and in tadpoles treated with pilocarpine as a positive control. The alg14 morphant tadpoles displayed 13% cumulative seizure behavior on average as opposed to 2% for control tadpoles. Pilocarpine-treated alg14 morphant tadpoles displayed 58% cumulative seizure behavior on average as opposed to 34% for control tadpoles.

Contractures in alg14 morphants limit observable seizures

While characterizing both the myasthenic and seizure phenotypes noted in alg14 morphants, we observed atypical tail contractures (Figure 4). These contractures consisted of tail curvature with a ventrally angulated appearance that was somewhat reminiscent of abnormalities seen in exposure to pesticides, including triphenyltin and malathion, that disrupt nAChR signaling in the neuromuscular junction.45,46,47 Yet, in evaluating tadpoles noted to have less severe contractures, these also appeared to perdure in seizure activity states for longer periods of time, while those with more severe contractures spent less time displaying seizure activity (Figure 4). Thus, it seems that while seizure propensity may exist for a broader number of alg14 morphant tadpoles, the contracted tail morphology may inhibit the movements that we aimed to detect as a sign of seizure activity.

Proteins are hypoglycosylated in alg14 morphants

ALG14 loss of function has been reasonably proposed to result in a CDG based on the previously characterized function of ALG14.48 To begin to understand the mechanism of disease pathogenesis in our tadpole model, we first assessed for evidence of decreased glycosylation in alg14 morphants. The goblet cells of developing tadpoles are rich in highly glycosylated transmembrane proteins.49,50 These glycosylated proteins can be detected through the affinity of lectins for glycosylated residues. While this property has previously been used to label goblet cells,51 we used lectin-based detection to compare the relative abundance of glycosylated proteins in tadpole goblet cells. The Erythrina cristagalli lectin (ECL) used in this assay preferentially binds to Galβ1-4GlcNAc.52 Thus, the glycosylated goblet cell proteins detected through this approach serve as a proxy for the broader process of N-linked glycosylation. There was a clear decrease in glycosylated proteins detected by this method in alg14 morphants when compared to controls (Figure 5).

nAChRs are depleted in alg14 morphant neuromuscular junctions

A critical component of neuromuscular function is the nAChR. This receptor is known to require N-linked glycosylation for proper folding and insertion into the cell membrane.53,54 In fact, this receptor is often compromised in the pathogenesis of myasthenic syndromes.55,56 To test whether this might be the case in the context of alg14 depletion, we performed fluorescence labeling of nAChR in tadpole tails. Upon examination of tail regions that are enriched for neuromuscular junctions, nAChR signals were sharp arcs across somitic muscle in controls but discontinuous in alg14-depleted embryos (Figure 5). Transcription of chrna7, encoding a representative component of nAChRs, was not significantly altered in alg14-depleted embryos, further suggesting that the resulting impact on nAChRs occurs post-transcriptionally (Figure S4). We characterized how nAChR protein localization within tadpole midbrain neurons was impacted by alg14 depletion. While the neuronal soma of control tadpoles demonstrated broadly distributed GFP-tagged α6 subunit signal, alg14-depleted soma showed a polarized distribution of GFP-tagged α6 subunit signals indicative of atypical protein trafficking and/or accumulation (Figure S5A). Assessment of protein glycosylation of α6 subunits via changes in protein mobility within a sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel also demonstrated a change in distinct forms of this protein upon alg14 knockdown (Figure S5B). This is consistent with atypical decreased glycosylation of this protein in tissue with decreased alg14 levels.

Gamma-aminobutyric acid receptors are depleted in the cortex of alg14 morphants

While a mechanistic understanding of how ALG14 deficiency might lead to seizures is lacking, a previous murine model of ALG13-CDG provided some insights that we hoped to build on.57 Indeed, as both ALG13 and ALG14 cooperate within the same step in N-linked glycosylation,48 we hypothesized that their overlap in terms of seizure phenotype may share similar roots. While previous work suggested that knockout of Alg13 caused a decrease in GABAARs due to changes at the gene expression level,57 it seemed more intuitive that these receptors might be depleted at the protein level, as both GABAAR and GABABR subunits require proper glycosylation to carry out their functions.58,59 Further, the previous knockout of Alg13 was specifically of the longer isoform with unclear implications for the glycosylation role of ALG13.57 Our assessment of gabra1, encoding a component of GABARs, was not significantly altered in alg14-depleted embryos, further suggesting that the resulting impact on nAChRs occurs at the protein level (Figure S4). However, when we assessed levels of GABARs within the cortex of tadpole brains through fluorescence labeling, it was apparent that there was a decrease in these receptors in the context of alg14 knockdown compared to controls (Figure 5). Despite this deficit in receptors, the overall brain morphology appeared intact, with no obvious change in cortical thickness or ventricle size (Figure S2).

The unfolded protein response is activated in alg14 morphants

Our results in Xenopus tissues indicated that multiple proteins seemed to be depleted downstream of alg14 knockdown. We hypothesized that this was most likely due to atypical glycosylation and resultant protein misfolding and mislocalization. Under conditions of excess protein misfolding and concomitant ER stress, the unfolded protein response (UPR) is activated to aid in excess protein degradation. During this response, Ire1 (inositol-requiring enzyme1) is activated, and its ribonuclease function leads to the atypical RNA splicing of xbp1 that activates ER-associated degradation (ERAD). We assayed for the presence of this alternate isoform of xbp1 to test for activation of the UPR. In morphant embryos, we detected an increase in the atypically spliced xbp1 product, indicating an increase in the UPR that could lead to the degradation of hypoglycosylated proteins through ERAD (Figure 5D). We posited that differences in the relative abundance of atypically spliced xbp1 could be due to differing degrees of efficiency of knockdown at the protein level. This is supported by immunoblotting assays of evaluating remaining Alg14 levels in embryos treated with MOs, as the degree of decrease in Alg14 correlated with atypical xbp1 splicing (Figure 5E).

Functional testing of clinically ascertained variants supports their role in disease

With multiple phenotypic assays and evidence of a plausible mechanism by alg14 depletion can lead to these phenotypes, we endeavored to test the pathogenicity of patient missense variants and a truncating variant (p.Trp23∗) predicted to escape NMD. Most of the identified putative disease alleles for ALG14 are missense variants of uncertain significance with in silico metrics that at least partially support disruption of ALG14 function in a manner that could lead to disease (Table S2). Our vertebrate tadpole model serves as a platform to test these predictions. Previous work has identified multiple variants in ALG14 that have been considered likely to lead to an ALG14-CDG.14,15,16,17 Combining these with the variants that we report in this study, we were equipped to test a robust set of variants ascertained from individuals with varying phenotypes and disease severity. To accomplish this, we used the assays of myasthenia and seizure frequency that we had previously used to assess the phenotype of alg14 knockdown. We used wild-type ALG14 mRNA as a positive control with the hypothesis that this, along with any variants that did not result in detriment to ALG14 function, would rescue the phenotypes observed in morphants. For both assays, only mRNA from the wild-type and c.605T>C (p.Leu202Pro) variants appeared to consistently rescue tadpole movement and seizure phenotypes (Figure 6).

Human ALG14-CDG tissue has abnormalities similar to alg14 knockdown tadpoles

Based on our observations of decreased nAChR abundance in alg14 knockdown tadpoles, we wondered if this could reflect the physiological cause of hypotonia and/or myasthenia in the individuals we report here. We assessed the overall morphology of residual muscle tissue from one individual who had a previous muscle biopsy from the left vastus lateralis for clinical testing. Compared to control tissue, we noted regions of muscle fibers that varied in size with an appearance of bimodal small polygonal fibers and large round fibers in tissue from individual II (Figure 7A). Rare regions also demonstrate widened spaces between fibers (Figure 7A, bottom). This is similar to the described abnormalities in other forms of CMS, though this represents a minority of regions in assessed tissue. We further assayed nAChR abundance based on conjugated α-bungarotoxin labeling. In tissue from individual II, we saw a smaller frequency of observed α-bungarotoxin-labeled puncta compared to control tissue (Figure 7B). Assessing for UPR activation in muscle tissue also demonstrated an increase compared to control tissue based on XBP1 splicing (Figure 7C). Yet, ALG14 localization was not disrupted in myoblasts from this individual harboring compound heterozygous missense variants in ALG14 and appeared to largely overlap with ceramide, a marker of the Golgi apparatus (Figure 7D). These findings in primary tissue from an affected individual highly correlate with other forms of CMS and likely explain why response to pyridostigmine has been reported to be beneficial for individuals with suspected ALG14-CDG. Further, they may provide a useful diagnostic basis for evaluating future individuals with suspected ALG14-CDG.

Discussion

The importance of ALG14 in human health has been suggested by the multiple variants reported in individuals with suspected congenital neurological disorders consisting of atypical development, myasthenia, and seizures. Bi-allelic variants in ALG14 have been proposed to lead to a CDG because of the role of ALG14 in N-linked glycosylation in cooperation with ALG13. However, a mechanistic connection between these findings was lacking. In fact, the pathogenicity of many described variants was unclear, given that they are missense variants seen in a very limited number of individuals without a readily available method for assessing changes to protein function.

Our functional studies using EBV-transformed lymphoblastoid cells derived from individual VII carrying compound heterozygous nonsense variants in ALG14 provide several important insights. The consistent detection of both mutant transcripts, together with ALG14 exhibiting normal subcellular localization, supports the conclusion that these truncated protein products are indeed expressed.

To evaluate ALG14 dysfunction for evidence of a bona fide CDG, we tested the knockdown of alg14 in a Xenopus model. Remarkably, this recapitulated the myasthenia, seizure, and contracture phenotypes of individuals with putatively damaging bi-allelic variants in ALG14. In fact, given our observations about the relationship between contracture severity and seizures, it is likely that we underestimate the severity of the seizure phenotype caused by ALG14 in our model (Figure 4). In our search for a mechanism to explain these phenotypes, we evaluated protein glycosylation in Xenopus goblet cells, which appeared to be broadly diminished upon alg14 depletion (Figure 5). As this suggested that the mechanism of disease was tied to defective glycosylation, we took a candidate approach to evaluating proteins that could explain specific facets of ALG14-CDG. Both nAChR and multiple subunits of GABARs require proper N-linked glycosylation to carry out their functions.53,54,58,59

nAChRs mediate the transduction of neuroelectrical signals into chemical signals at the neuromuscular junction and serve as the prototypical point of disruption in myasthenia.24,25,60,61 Thus, this was a likely protein to mediate myasthenia in both individuals with pathogenic bi-allelic ALG14 variants and alg14-deficient tadpoles. The decreased abundance of nAChRs at the neuromuscular junctions in alg14 morphants and in affected muscle tissue provides a critical insight into not only the mechanisms of myasthenia in affected individuals but also therapeutic interventions (Figures 5 and 7). While pyridostigmine has been empirically trialed in individuals with suspected ALG14-CDG, our results support its potential benefit in affected patients, as this agent would allow for perdurance of acetylcholine in neuromuscular junctions to take advantage of whatever residual nAChRs are available to mediate neuromuscular signaling. Yet, the benefits and risks of this therapy should be weighed carefully. While administration of pyridostigmine in individuals VIII-1, -2, and -3 resulted in increased facial expressions and decreased ventilator needs, individual VIII-3 developed hypertrophic pyloric stenosis. Meanwhile, individuals VIII-1 and -2 exhibited hypersecretion and indigestion. While individual VIII-3 required surgical intervention for this complication, individuals VIII-1 and -2 had marked improvement of hypersecretion and indigestion with discontinuation of this therapy.

GABARs mediate inhibitory signals to reduce neuronal excitability through ligand-based control of resting potential (GABAA) or G protein-mediated inhibition of neurotransmitter release (GABAB). GABAR subunits require glycosylation for function, proper folding, and cell-surface expression.62,63,64,65,66,67 This was an attractive candidate for mediating seizures in individuals with pathogenic bi-allelic ALG14 variants, as variants in genes encoding these receptors have been implicated in seizure disorders.68,69 Testing whether a similar mechanism could underlie seizures in ALG14-CDG could aid in determining what antiepileptic medications might be most beneficial for individuals with this CDG. Indeed, a mouse model of the related ALG13-CDG suggested a deficiency in GABAARs as mediating seizures.57 Our results in alg14 morphant tadpoles are consistent with a decrease in this class of receptors contributing to seizures in ALG14-CDG, albeit through a decrease of GABARs at the protein level rather than at the gene expression level as seen in the aforementioned mouse model of ALG13-CDG (Figure 5).

Since glycosylation is understood to influence proper protein folding, we assayed UPR activity in control and morphant tadpoles, as tadpoles depleted of alg14 demonstrated increased UPR commensurate with decreased Alg14 abundance (Figure 5). The reduction in nAChRs and GABARs in alg14-depleted embryos supports a role for glycosylation in receptor trafficking, consistent with the activated UPR observed in this study. We therefore propose a model in which dysfunctional glycosylation compromises the ability of proteins, including nAChRs and GABARs, to adopt the necessary conformations to carry out their functions. Thus, ALG14-CDG may in part be understood as a congenital foldopathy.

Importantly, we also established a robust set of assays in Xenopus for testing ALG14 variants for pathogenicity. This same method could assist in the future diagnostics of patient alleles that are not clearly detrimental to function. In this assay, most variants appeared to be dysfunctional alleles when tested in our Xenopus system (Figure 6). One exception, c.605T>C (p.Leu202Pro) was also capable of demonstrating rescue in both myasthenia and seizure assays, but while the amino acid residue affected is conserved in Xenopus, it is in a less well-conserved region (Figure S3). Thus, the results of the assays suggesting retained functionality of this variant are difficult to interpret and could be consistent with a divergence of requirements at these amino acid residues between alg14 in Xenopus and ALG14 in humans. It is also possible that the effects of this variant are subtler than what could be captured as dysfunction in the assays utilized in this study, though this is challenging to evaluate, as this variant was ascertained from a prenatal case of suspected ALG14, and thus, we do not know if the affected fetus would have exhibited either seizures or congenital myasthenia, which our assays were designed to capture. Yet, this variant could also lead to a functional version of ALG14, and the corresponding patient may have had an alternative cause for their prenatal clinical course that is distinct from the suspected ALG14-CDG. These results highlight both the strength and potential limitations of functional testing of putative disease-causing variants in vertebrate models. Although limited to tissue from one affected individual, the observed bimodal muscle fiber morphology and decreased nAChR abundance in muscle biopsy tissue may be useful as markers of disease when evaluating an individual for a suspected ALG14-CDG.

While our assay did not suggest the presence of genotype-phenotype correlations based on individual variants, it was limited to testing of one variant at a time rather than the specific combinations of variants seen in individuals. This likely does not capture the subtleties of the diverse combination of variants that are reported in our cohort and in previous studies. Yet, in our review of all available ALG14 variants ascertained in individuals with overlapping neurodevelopmental phenotypes, we noted a trend in clinical severity based on the reported variants. Individuals who are compound heterozygous for variants in ALG14 and in whom only one of the variants is a splice-altering or truncating variant appear to have a trend toward a somewhat more limited clinical presentation (Table 1). Their disease course is characterized by a predominance of seizure disorders and related complications rather than the congenital myasthenia and accompanying severe movement disorders observed in individuals with bi-allelic missense ALG14 variants. While this may seem counterintuitive, because splice-altering and truncating variants are typically more severe loss-of-function alleles, if this correlation is consistent as additional individuals with ALG14-CDG are identified, this may suggest an avenue for further studies into the pathogenesis of this disorder. Along with the observation that complete knockout of Alg14 is lethal in animal models and cell lines, this supports the idea that even variants resulting in early stop codons may allow production of a residual amount of ALG14, potentially through alternative start codons or splicing. While ALG14 appears to be haplosufficient, glycosylation studies have not previously been completed in individuals heterozygous for truncating variants to detect subtle effects that may not manifest as overt clinical phenotypes. Our observation that truncating variant transcripts may escape NMD could provide some insight into this apparent phenomenon, though it is unclear how the resultant partial proteins might impart the function of ALG14. We speculate that this bypass of NMD could result from the increased ER stress evidenced by increased UPR (Figures 5D and 7C). While investigations into this phenomenon are limited, these sorts of shifts in ER stress have been linked to modifying NMD efficiency.70

Although ALG14-related disease has been described in the literature under distinct phenotypic designations, including CMS15, intellectual developmental disorder with epilepsy and coarse facies, and myopathy with epilepsy and progressive cerebral atrophy, our findings support the interpretation that these presentations likely represent a single clinical entity with variable expressivity. We propose using the unifying term “ALG14-CDG,” which has previously been suggested15 to best reflect our now-improved understanding of the shared underlying molecular mechanism and to facilitate consistent recognition and classification of this disorder.

In summary, our results are consistent with the disruption of ALG14 resulting in a CDG. This CDG manifests as a neurodevelopmental disorder through disrupted protein folding of hypoglycosylated proteins, including nAChRs and GABARs. This is supported by a battery of assays in our high-throughput Xenopus model. While we tested a subset of receptors in hopes of understanding how ALG14 dysfunction might lead to disease, we expect that future broad assays of protein glycosylation will identify additional affected proteins that may provide additional rich avenues for further inquiry in the hopes of optimizing treatment for individuals with ALG14-CDG. Indeed, many emerging therapeutic strategies, such as glycosylation-enhancing drugs, cofactor supplementation, and chaperone molecules, show great promise for their impact on CDGs.71,72,73
