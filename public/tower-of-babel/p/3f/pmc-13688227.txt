Follow‐Up and Outcomes After Thermal Ablation Treatment Among HIV‐Infected Women From Resource‐Limited Countries With Positive HPV‐Based Screening: Results of the AIMA‐CC Cohort (ANRS 12375)
Authors: Pierre Debeaudrap, Firmin Nongodo Kabore, Limsreng Setha, Joseph Tegbe, Brahima Doukoure, Moeung Sotheara, Olivier Segeral, Korn Aun, Eugène Messou, Matthieu Chicaud, Kim Sothea, Pierre Vassilakos, Armel Poda, Evelyne Kasilé Poda, Antoine Jaquet, Adolphe Some, Patrick Petignat, Gary Clifford, Apollinaire Horo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrimary screening for human papillomavirus (HPV) followed by treatment with thermal ablation (TA) is an important component of the global strategy to eliminate cervical cancer. However, evidence on post‐TA outcomes in women living with HIV (WLWH) remains limited. The aim of this study is to evaluate the risk and determinants of post‐TA CIN2+. As part of the AIMA‐CC study, WLWH from Côte d'Ivoire, Burkina Faso and Cambodia were screened using HPV testing (GeneXpert). All HPV‐positive women were biopsied and those with positive triage (positive visual inspection and/or HPV‐161845) were treated with TA using a flat probe and followed for 12 months with HPV testing and biopsies. Among the 456 WLWH (age: 30–59 years) treated with TA and with valid histology, 396 (86%) attended the 12‐month follow‐up visit. HPV persistence (HPV of same GeneXpert channel at baseline and 12‐month post‐treatment) was 40% and was associated with baseline HPV16 positivity (p = 0.007), age ≥ 40 years (p = 0.03) and CD4 ≤ 500 cells/mm3 (p = 0.02). CIN2+ was detected at 12 months in 12 (4%) of the 318 WLWH with valid histology and was associated with 12‐month HPV positivity (OR: 5.29 [1.46–28.46]). The risk of CIN2+ was particularly high among women with HPV‐16 at 12 months (OR: 15.53 [3.49–91.17]). HPV positivity at 12 months had a sensitivity for detecting CIN2+ of 83% but a specificity of 54%. HPV‐16 positivity had lower sensitivity (42%) but high specificity (> 90%).In conclusion, there is an urgent need to improve TA management in WLWH who are at high risk of CIN2+ re‐occurrence post‐treatment.Keywords: cervical cancer, HIV, HPV, resource‐limited countries, screening

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) is the most common cause of cancer and a leading cause of death in women living with HIV (WLWH) from resource‐limited settings [1]. However, effective methods to prevent CC are available, and there has been global mobilization over the last decade to reduce the burden of CC worldwide, including in resource‐limited contexts. In 2021, the WHO launched its strategy for CC elimination as a public health problem, which includes primary screening with an HPV test whenever possible and prompt treatment of women screening positive [2]. In the resource‐limited context where histology is not readily available and contacts with the health care system remain scarce, a single‐visit approach to CC screening has been recommended [3]. Women who screen positive are treated after confirmation by visual inspection of their eligibility for immediate ablative treatment. Although the effectiveness of this approach over the short term has been demonstrated in a large clinical trial [4], there is still limited evidence on the use of thermal ablation (TA), which has become the preferred ablative method in resource‐limited countries because of its safety profile and feasibility [5]. TA is expected to eliminate precancerous lesions and facilitate HPV clearance. While some studies reported suboptimal efficacy of TA compared to excisional methods [6], others show non‐inferiority in WLWH [7, 8].

As the risk of cervical cancer remains high after treatment for high‐grade lesions [9, 10], close follow‐up is needed to identify and treat these lesions [3]. However, the best strategy for posttreatment follow‐up needs to be determined, especially in resource‐limited countries where most women are treated without histologic confirmation. Several studies have demonstrated that HPV testing has high sensitivity and negative predictive value for detecting post‐treatment lesions [11, 12] and, therefore, would be a good test of cure [13]. Partial HPV genotyping may improve risk stratification for posttreatment follow‐up, as it is the case for primary CC screening. Good performance of type‐specific HPV persistence has been reported in several studies, although evidence remains limited [14, 15, 16]. In many resource‐limited contexts, only grouped partial genotyping is available (e.g., HPV 16, HPV 18/45, and HPV31/33/35/52/58), and the performance of HPV persistence within these groups has not been assessed.

With imperfect treatment options, participants' adherence to posttreatment follow‐up is crucial for the success of CC prevention strategies. However, it has received less attention from researchers than the other aspects of the screening and treatment process.

The primary objective of this paper is to report on post‐treatment CIN2+ and to examine the associated risk factors using data from the AIMA‐CC ANRS 12375 cohort of WLWH. This work also includes the analysis of the follow‐up completion and HPV persistence in this cohort, two aspects related to posttreatment CIN2 +.

Methods

WLWH were recruited from three HIV care services: the CEPREF in Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire; the HIV daycare centre in Bobo‐Dioulasso, Burkina Faso; and the HIV care service of Calmette Hospital in Phnom Penh, Cambodia. Participants were eligible if they were living with HIV‐1 (based on medical files), had received antiretroviral therapy (ART) for ≥ 12 months, were aged 30–49 years, and provided informed consent. Women who were pregnant, had undergone a previous hysterectomy, or had been treated for cervical precancer in the previous 12 months were excluded. After providing informed consent, participants underwent cervical cancer screening using the Xpert platform and a self‐collected sample, followed by triage with visual inspection after colouration with acetic acid (VIA) and lugol (VILI) if the HPV test was positive. HPV‐positive women infected with HPV 16, 18, or 45 and/or with abnormal VIA (based on the ABCD criteria [17]) were treated with TA when eligible ((TZ) transition zone fully visible or accessible, lesion ≤ 75% of the cervical surface and not extending into the cervical canal or vaginal wall) [18] or were referred for another treatment (e.g., loop electrosurgical excision procedure [LEEP]) when needed. Biopsies were performed in all HPV‐positive participants, primarily targeting visible lesions. In the absence of a visible lesion, random biopsies were taken at the transformation zone at 6 and 12 o'clock [19]. Women were screened and treated in a single visit when possible (“screen‐and‐treat” approach). However, this was not possible for some participants because of logistic or medical reasons, and they had to complete the screening process over several visits. More details on the primary CC screening are available in a previous paper [20].

After treatment, the participants were followed for 12 months with a phone call at week one and visits at months 6 and 12. All transportation expenses were reimbursed. The overall objective of this cohort was to evaluate post‐treatment outcomes, primarily the risk of CIN2+ post‐treatment. The week one phone call aimed to evaluate for absence of adverse events and to assess participants' satisfaction. The 6‐ and 12‐month evaluations included an HPV test performed with the Xpert platform using a cervical sample collected by a clinician and a VIA/VILI evaluation. In addition, biopsies were performed at the 12‐month visit for all participants to assess for the presence of CIN2+ lesions. Biopsies were performed on visible lesions and in the absence of a visible lesion, random biopsies for disease verification were taken at the transformation zone (TZ) at 6 and 12 o'clock. Participants who needed additional treatment were referred for another treatment.

TA

Treatment was performed using a flat probe 17 mm (WISAP; Medical Technology GmbH, Brunnenthal, Hofolding, Germany) which was heated at 100 C and applied on the cervix for 60 s after Lugol's iodine application to delimitate the TZ. If necessary, the application was repeated two or more times until the entire TZ was treated. For this study, no additional shallow nipple tip (shallow nipple, 17 × 5 mm) or narrower 7 mm‐wide (7 mm wide × 5 mm) probe from WISAP were used.

Histology

Biopsies were fixed with a neutral 10% formaldehyde solution, embedded in paraffin and cut into thin slices with a microtome. The slides were analysed by two independent pathologists who were aware of the participants' HPV status. The first evaluation was performed locally (pathology laboratories at Calmette Hospital for participants in Cambodia and at Felix Houphouet Boigny University for those in Africa). The second evaluation was performed by pathologists at the Simone Veil Hospital, France. The results were reported using the CIN system [21]. Discrepancies between the pathologists were resolved through discussion and/or immunohistochemistry.

Participation in Post‐Treatment Follow‐Up

The factors analysed as potential determinants for post‐treatment follow‐up attendance were as follows: (1) participants' characteristics; including age, social characteristics (education, marital status, economic level, disability), and HIV‐related characteristics (CD4‐cell count at ART initiation and ART duration); (2) factors related to the screening process and outcomes, including the number of visits to complete screening, the screening duration (i.e., time in hours between self sampling and screening completion when it was performed in a single visit), the occurrence of any adverse event (during treatment) and participation in the 6‐month visit; and (3) perceptions of the CC screening and treatment proposed (belief in CC severity, screening importance and efficacy, satisfaction with screening duration, process and TA).

HPV Persistence and Post‐Treatment CIN2+

HPV persistence was defined as the detection of HPV at 12 months from the same GeneXpert channel as the baseline channel [22] as follows: (1) HPV16; (2) HPV18/45; (3) HPV31/33/35/52/58; (4) HPV51/59; and (5) HPV39/56/66/68. In the case of multiple infections with different genotype groups, persistence was considered when at least one group at 12 months matched one of the baseline values. The risk factors considered for both HPV persistence and CIN2+ at 12 months were age [23], parity [24], nadir CD4‐cell count [25], ART duration and baseline HPV‐16 positivity [26] (according to the GenExpert). Moreover, any HPV persistence, HPV 16 persistence, and any HPV or HPV 16 positivity at 12 months (i.e., irrespective of the baseline HPV genotype) were also included as potential risk factors for CIN2+ at month 12 [13, 26, 27, 28]. The evaluation of CIN2+ detection at 12 months was conducted both with all treated WLWH (as this is the actual situation with the simplified approach to CC screening) and only among WLWH with CIN2+ at baseline. Persistent/residual disease has been defined as the detection of a histopathological CIN2+ diagnosed from a punch biopsy or a subsequent specimen (including hysterectomy or LEEP) at any interval after the initial treatment was performed.

Statistical Analysis

The study population included all HPV‐positive participants who were treated with TA and a valid baseline histology result. Patients with cancer at baseline were treated with surgery and not included in this analysis. Associations between potential risk factors and 12‐month visit attendance, 12‐month HPV persistence and 12‐month CIN2+ status were assessed using odds ratios (ORs) calculated by logistic regression adjusted for study site. A robust standard deviation with the robust sandwich method was used to account for the correlation between the three study sites and adjust the 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Because of the small sample size in the CIN2+ analysis, logistic regression with Firth's method was used [29]. The sensitivity and specificity of HPV persistence and the ability of the HPV genotype at 12 months to detect post‐treatment CIN2+ were computed for the two populations (all WLWH treated irrespective of the baseline histopathology; and the subset of WLWH treated and with proven CIN2+ at baseline). All data analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1).

Between March 2019 and December 2021, 2253 WLWH were enrolled and screened for CC. Among the 932 who were HPV positive, 478 were treated with TA because of a positive VIA (n = 363, 76%) and/or HPV 16/18/45 identification (n = 196, 41%). Among them, 456 (95%) had valid histology results at baseline and were included in the analytic population (Figure 1). The characteristics of the study population are provided in Table 1.

Post‐Treatment Follow‐Up Completeness

Overall, 87% of participants attended the 12‐month post‐treatment follow‐up visit (n = 396), with significant heterogeneity in completeness across the three sites (Côte d'Ivoire: 85%, Burkina Faso: 99%, Cambodia: 80%, p < 0.001). The follow‐up completeness among participants excluded from this analysis was 95% (p = 0.15). Notably, 6‐month visit completeness was low (64%) because of the COVID‐19 pandemic. As displayed in Table 1, women who had previous screening experience and those who completed the 6‐month post‐treatment follow‐up visit were more likely to complete the 12‐month post‐treatment follow‐up visit, whereas those who started ART in the past 2 years were less likely (p = 0.004). By contrast, none of the socio‐economic characteristics of the study participants was associated with follow‐up completeness.

HPV Persistence

Among the 394 participants who completed the 12‐month post‐treatment follow‐up visit (after exclusion of two without month 12 HPV test results), 198 (50%) were still HPV positive, and 160 (40%) had persistent HPV (detection of an HPV from the same GeneXpert channel as the baseline channel). In addition, 34 (8.5%) patients were HPV16 positive both at baseline and at 12 months (Table 2). The factors associated with HPV persistence are displayed in Table 3. According to multivariate analysis, the risk of HPV persistence was greater among WLWH older than 40 years (OR: 1.66, 95% CI: 1.04–2.65), among those with HPV‐16 at baseline (OR: 2.18, 95% CI: 1.25–3.77 compared to HPV other than HPV 16, 18, or 45 and 2.0, 95% CI: 1.18–3.39 compared to HPV other than HPV‐16) and those with a nadir CD4‐cell count ≤ 500 cells/mm3 (OR: 2.82, 95% CI: 1.17–6.79). There was also a negative association of borderline statistical significance (p = 0.09) with ART duration. As displayed in Table S1, ART duration greater than 10 years (OR 1.74, 95% CI: 1.15–2.65) and HPV‐16 at baseline (OR compared to type other than 16, 18, or 45: 1.76, 95% CI: 1.05–2.96) were associated with 12‐month HPV positivity (irrespective of the baseline HPV genotype group).

Post‐Treatment CIN2+

Among the 54 participants treated for a documented CIN2+ lesion at baseline, 11 (20%) still had CIN2+ lesions at 12 months (9 CIN3 and 2 CIN2). In addition, a CIN3 lesion was detected in one participant who did not have a CIN2+ lesion pretreatment. As shown in Table 4, HIV‐related factors and baseline HPV type were not associated with the risk of CIN2+ lesions at 12 months. In contrast, women with any HPV infection at 12 months (OR 5.29, 95% CI: 1.46 28.46) or with any HPV persistence (OR 5.10, 95% CI: 1.54–21.27) were more likely to have CIN2+ lesions. In addition, an increased risk of CIN2+ at 12 months was observed among women with HPV‐16 at 12 months (OR 15.53, 95% CI: 3.49–91.17) or with persistent HPV‐16 infection (OR 8.72 95% CI: 1.50–65.77), but not among WLWH with persistent other HPV types. Similar results were found in the subset of women with confirmed CIN2+ at baseline (Table S2).

The performance of 12‐month HPV positivity, HPV 16 positivity, any HPV persistence and HPV‐16 persistence in detecting 12‐month lesions is summarized in Table 5 for the overall population of women treated and for the subset of women treated with confirmed CIN2+ at baseline. HPV positivity (any type) at 12 months had the greatest sensitivity (83%, 95% CI: 55–95) but low specificity (54%, 95% CI: 48–59). Using persistent HPV‐16 resulted in greater specificity (93%, 95% CI: 90–96) but lower sensitivity (42%, 95% CI: 19–68). Similar results were found in the subset of women with confirmed CIN2+ at baseline (sensitivities of HPV any type and HPV‐16 persistence were 91%, 95% CI: 62–98 and 45%, 95% CI: 21–72, respectively, while specificities were 44%, 29–59 and 90%, 95% CI: 76–96, respectively).

Discussion

This prospective multi‐country cohort study provides important results on three key and interconnected aspects of the post‐treatment follow‐up among WLWH in resource‐limited contexts: participation in post‐treatment follow‐up, HPV persistence and the presence of post‐treatment CIN2+ lesions. The participation rate at the 12‐month post‐treatment follow‐up visit was high (90%), despite the recent COVID‐19 epidemic, which disrupted health care [30]. Surprisingly, there was no association between participants' social characteristics or perceptions of the screening and attendance at post‐treatment follow‐up visits, although there was significant heterogeneity across study sites reflecting the importance of contextual factors. In this study, transportation for the visits was free, which is an important facilitating factor for screening participation that may have contributed to this good adherence to follow‐up [31, 32]. In addition, it should be noted that the population living with HIV is already engaged in regular medical follow‐up, which could also facilitate follow‐up after the treatment of cervical precancer [33]. The lower attendance among women who recently started ART could be explained by the difficulties in managing both health challenges [34].

Over the 12‐month post‐treatment follow‐up, we found a high rate of persistent HPV infection and a significant proportion of treated women still presented with high‐grade lesions, which confirms the need for (1) intensive follow‐up post‐treatment and (2) reconsider the treatment options in this population. HPV persistence is an important treatment outcome, as HPV is the causal factor for cervical pre‐cancerous and cancerous lesions, and HPV persistence after treatment is deemed to promote the recurrence of CIN2 + [35]. Although HPV clearance at 12 months after TA in our study was lower than that reported in studies involving women in the general population, which vary between 70% and 84% [24, 26, 36, 37], it was in the range of the results reported in studies focusing on WLWH, which vary between 39% and 55% [25, 38, 39, 40, 41]. Despite the increasing evidence of a lower clearance rate associated with HIV, the actual mechanisms resulting in impaired clearance among WLWH remain to be determined. We identified three independent risk factors for lower HPV clearance after TA: age, nadir CD4‐cell count and HPV type 16. The greater HPV clearance observed among women with nadir CD4‐cell counts above 500 reflects the role of immune suppression in HPV elimination [42]. Older age has been identified as a risk factor for impaired HPV clearance in several studies assessing HPV persistence after excisional treatment in the general population [43, 44], but not among WLWH or after ablative treatment. Consistent with our results, different studies involving excisional as well as ablative methods have reported increased persistence rates with HPV type 16 [26, 43, 44]. This is an important result for optimizing post‐treatment follow‐up, especially in contexts with limited resources.

The primary outcome of this research was the rate of CIN2+ 12 months post‐treatment, and our results align with those of the few studies involving WLWH and TA [25, 38, 39, 45, 46]. A strength of this research is to provide clinical‐oriented evidence on the risk of CIN2+ re‐occurrence (i.e., focusing on women with confirmed CIN2+ when treated) as well as program‐oriented evidence taking into account the actual situation of many screening programs in resource‐limited contexts where histopathology is not readily available. Interestingly, the only factors associated with the risk of post‐treatment CIN2+ were those related to HPV infection. Other studies reported a higher risk of post‐treatment CIN2+ among older women [47, 48] or women with lower CD4‐cell counts [49, 50]. Nevertheless, in this study, as in several other studies, persistent HPV infection was the strongest predictor of post‐treatment CIN2 + [13, 25]. HPV persistence and any HPV positivity at 12 months had high sensitivity, ranging from 75% to 90%, similar to the results reported by Chung et al. among WLWH treated with TA [25] or in studies conducted with women in the general population and using mainly excisional methods [12, 51, 52, 53]. In contrast, HPV 16 persistence had a lower sensitivity, approximately 45%, but greater specificity [25]. Notably, the performances of these different options were similar when the analysis was conducted in the overall population of treated women or the subgroup with confirmed CIN2+ lesions.

In resource‐limited contexts, a simple and cost‐effective strategy for post‐treatment follow‐up is needed that considers the context and balances the cost and performance of the tests with the consequences of false positives and negatives (i.e., overtreatment and missing a potential cancer). For example, in settings such as those of this study, participants testing HPV negative could resume usual follow‐up, those testing HPV positive with a type different from HPV 16 could be followed annually, and those with HPV 16 identified could be referred for additional investigations and treatment. Notably, the transformation zone is modified after TA, which results in poorer performance of visual inspection with acetic acid to identify precancerous lesions [54]. Future studies should determine the safety and effectiveness of such risk‐based management strategy in different resource‐limited contexts.

It is important to highlight that TA remains the best treatment option in many resource‐limited contexts where access to excisional treatment is limited [55]. At the time of this study, TA was performed by one or several applications of the heated probe as described above without specific endocervical treatment. This may be one of the factors contributing to the high rate of post treatment CIN2+. Yet several probes with different shapes may be used successively for treatment of the squamocolumnar junction (one small tip for reaching into the endocervical canal for deep lesion and then a flat probe for covering the cervix), resulting in a more comprehensive ablation [56].

The main limitation of this study is related to its restricted sample size with respect to the analysis of the post‐treatment CIN2+ lesions and of the sensitivity/specificity of HPV test results to detect post‐treatment CIN2+. This is a limitation in several prospective studies reporting on post‐treatment outcomes, as they are usually powered to assess outcomes related to screening. However, collaborative work with pooled analysis of the data collected in different studies would be a useful initiative to provide more robust analyses. The absence of full HPV genotyping is another limitation of this research, as we could not assess type‐specific persistence as a risk factor for post‐treatment CIN2+. However, full genotyping is seldom available in resource‐limited countries, and although it is a more coarse‐grained indicator, persistence is more adaptable to the actual situation of many settings.

Recommendation & Conclusion

In summary, we found a significant rate of posttreatment CIN2+ among WLWH treated diagnosed with CIN2+ and treated with TA. In contexts where a simplified screen‐and‐treat strategy is used, resulting in overtreatment, innovative and effective posttreatment follow‐up strategies are needed to reduce the residual CC burden and achieve the goal of CC elimination. We found that cross‐sectional HPV testing at 12 months has a high negative predictive value even in the absence of histology, while the use of HPV 16 partial genotyping could be used as a triage test to prompt additional treatment (e.g., LEEP). There is an urgent need for additional data with longer follow‐up periods and for clinical trials assessing different monitoring strategies and/or treatment protocols.
