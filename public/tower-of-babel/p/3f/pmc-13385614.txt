Abdominal binders improve colonoscopy outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials with BMI-stratified subgroup analysis
Authors: Yu-Kuang Kang, Tien-Yu Huang, Meng-Chuan Lu, Peng-Jen Chen, Po-Feng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Abdominal binders have been studied as an adjunctive device for colonoscopy, but trial-level evidence is heterogeneous and existing meta-analyses have either not stratified by body-mass index (BMI) or used only linear meta-regression, leaving BMI dependence of binder benefit unresolved.Objectives:To quantify the effect of abdominal binders on cecal intubation time (CIT), post-procedural pain, manual pressure, and position change during adult colonoscopy and to examine BMI dependence using a pre-specified stratified approach.Design:Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.Methods:Seven academic databases plus ClinicalTrials.gov were searched (primary April 30, 2025; updated February 28, 2026). Two reviewers screened records and assessed risk of bias (Cochrane RoB 2) independently. Effects were pooled with random-effects (DerSimonian–Laird) models: mean difference (CIT), Hedges’s g (pain), and risk ratio (manual pressure, position change). BMI was a pre-specified stratified subgroup across three study-level tertiles (<26, 26–28, >28 kg/m²); robustness was assessed by leave-one-out sensitivity analyses.Results:Fourteen RCTs (n = 2834) were included after two post hoc exclusions (predatory publication; unverifiable randomization). Abdominal binders significantly reduced CIT (MD −1.576 min; p < 0.001; k = 14), pain (Hedges’s g −0.853; p < 0.001; k = 8), manual pressure (RR 0.522; p < 0.001; k = 11), and position change (RR 0.569; p < 0.001; k = 11). BMI-stratified analysis revealed a non-monotonic pattern for CIT and manual pressure (with a parallel pattern for pain whose intermediate stratum rests on a single trial): lean (<26) and obese (>28) strata showed benefit while the intermediate stratum (26–28) did not. Position-change benefit reached significance only in the lean stratum. Effects were robust on leave-one-out.Conclusion:Abdominal binders consistently improve four colonoscopy-related outcomes; the non-monotonic BMI response pattern suggests two distinct mechanistic pathways and is hypothesis-generating, warranting confirmation in BMI-stratified randomized trials.Trial registration:Prospectively registered with International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols on May 15, 2025 (registration number INPLASY202550043; DOI: 10.37766/inplasy2025.5.0043).

Introduction

Colonoscopy represents the gold standard for colorectal cancer screening, with demonstrated efficacy in reducing colorectal cancer incidence by up to 89% in screening populations. 1 Procedural quality directly impacts diagnostic accuracy, with loop formation during colonoscope insertion constituting a major technical challenge. Looping occurs in up to 90% of colonoscopies, resulting in patient discomfort, prolonged cecal intubation time (CIT), and potential complications including splenic injury and perforation. 2 Current quality benchmarks recommend cecal intubation rates of ⩾90% for all colonoscopies and ⩾95% for screening procedures, yet difficult colonoscopies may represent 5%–20% of cases, with obesity as a significant contributing factor.3,4

Conventional approaches to mitigate looping include manual abdominal pressure and position changes, required in up to 50% of procedures.2,5 These techniques necessitate additional personnel and may be inconsistently applied, compromising effectiveness. Abdominal binders have emerged as a potential standardized solution by providing consistent external compression across the abdomen, theoretically preventing sigmoid and transverse colon loops while supporting redundant mesentery and preventing mesenteric stretching. 6

Beyond procedural time, several other parameters are important for assessing colonoscopy quality and patient experience. Pain scores reflect patient comfort and can be influenced by looping, excessive manipulation, and sedation strategy. Manual abdominal pressure and patient position changes are surrogate measures of procedural difficulty, reflecting the need for operator maneuvers to overcome looping and redundancy. Reducing these interventions can improve staff ergonomics, decrease procedure complexity, and enhance patient satisfaction.

Although prior pooled syntheses have consistently demonstrated benefit, the body mass index (BMI) dependence of binder benefit has not been resolved. The 2019 meta-analysis by Nishizawa et al. 7 (n = 5 RCTs) demonstrated significant reductions in CIT by 1.31 min (95% CI −2.40 to −0.23, p = 0.02) and decreased need for manual compression (odds ratio (OR) 0.35, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.17–0.70). Similarly, Zafar et al. 8 confirmed these benefits across seven RCTs, showing shortened CIT by 0.76 min (p = 0.04) and reduced position changes (OR 0.46, 95% CI 0.27–0.78). Neither analysis, however, formally tested BMI as a moderator of binder efficacy.

Individual studies suggest differential effectiveness based on patient characteristics. Subgroup analysis from Crockett et al. 2 observed significant benefits specifically in patients with BMI 30–40 kg/m2. Subgroup analysis from Toyoshima et al. 9 noted advantages in those ⩾45 years. Inadequate statistical power in existing studies has prevented establishment of definitive selection criteria.

Despite the consistent overall benefit reported by prior pooled syntheses, the most recent comprehensive meta-analysis 10 (CMA; 16 RCTs, 3098 participants) explicitly identified the inability to perform a BMI-stratified subgroup analysis as a core methodological limitation, and prioritized “the identification of patient subgroups, such as those with high BMI” as a research priority for future work. The present systematic review and meta-analysis is designed to address that gap directly. We re-analyze the colonoscopy abdominal-binder literature in 14 randomized controlled trials using a pre-specified, tertile-based BMI subgroup framework with data-informed tertile cut-points (lean < 26 kg/m2, intermediate 26–28 kg/m2, obese >28 kg/m2; see section “Deviations from the registered protocol,” first deviation) applied to 4 primary procedural outcomes—CIT, post-procedural pain, the need for manual abdominal pressure, and the need for patient position change—and complement this with leave-one-out sensitivity analyses and 3 study-level moderator analyses (sedation depth, endoscopist experience, and binder type). We hypothesized that the BMI response of the abdominal binder is non-monotonic rather than linear, and we interpret the resulting pattern through a mechanistic model in which two anatomically distinct pathways operate with different relative dominance across the BMI range.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered with the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (INPLASY) on May 15, 2025 (registration number INPLASY202550043; DOI 10.37766/inplasy2025.5.0043). The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement 11 ; the completed PRISMA checklist is provided as Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

This meta-analysis was structured according to the PICO framework: Population—adults (⩾18 years) undergoing elective diagnostic or screening colonoscopy; Intervention—application of an abdominal binder, abdominal compression device, or support belt during colonoscopy; Comparison—no abdominal binder or standard care without compression; Outcomes—procedural efficiency (CIT; cecal intubation length (CIL)), patient comfort (post-procedural pain), and operator maneuvers (need for manual abdominal pressure; need for position change). The four primary outcomes were CIT, post-procedural pain, manual abdominal pressure, and need for position change; CIL was analyzed as a secondary outcome.

Inclusion criteria: (1) randomized controlled trials in adults; (2) abdominal binder or compression device versus standard care without such a device; (3) reporting of at least one of the listed outcomes. Exclusion criteria: (1) non-randomized studies; (2) pediatric, emergency, or therapeutic colonoscopy as the primary setting; (3) participant overlap with previously published trials.

Information sources and search strategy

Two authors (P.-F.H. and Y.-K.K.) independently searched seven academic databases—PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Web of Science, ClinicalKey, ProQuest, and ScienceDirect—and the ClinicalTrials.gov registry. The primary search (PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, Web of Science, ClinicalTrials.gov) was executed on April 30, 2025; a supplementary update covering ClinicalKey, ProQuest, and ScienceDirect was executed on February 28, 2026 to align the executed search with the seven academic databases listed in the registered protocol. Per PRISMA 2020 guidance on search updates, this update yielded no new candidate trials. Reference lists of relevant reviews and of all included trials were hand-screened. No language restriction was applied. Database-specific Boolean strings, field tags, filters, and per-source yields are presented in Table S2 (total 603 records before deduplication).

Study selection and data extraction

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote 21 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) for deduplication. Two reviewers (P.-F.H. and Y.-K.K.) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts in two separate EndNote libraries; disagreements were resolved by discussion or, where consensus could not be reached, by a third reviewer (M.-C.L.). Data were extracted onto a pre-defined structured form covering trial characteristics (country, year, sample size, setting), participant characteristics (age, sex, mean BMI), intervention characteristics (binder type, timing of application, pressure range if reported), comparator details, and all primary and secondary outcomes.

Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality was independently assessed in duplicate by P.-F.H. and Y.-K.K. using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool 12 across the five standard domains (randomization process; deviations from intended interventions; missing outcome data; measurement of the outcome; selection of the reported result), followed by an overall judgment of low, some concerns, or high risk of bias. The per-protocol effect-of-assignment framework was used for “deviations from intended interventions” given that binder efficacy depends on actual device application. Domain-level judgments are presented in Figure 1 (Özkan et al. 13 alternating allocation rationale detailed in Figure 1 caption). Risk-of-bias visualizations (per-trial traffic-light plot and weighted summary plot) were generated using the robvis web application. 14

Effect measures and synthesis

All analyses used a random-effects model with DerSimonian–Laird between-study variance estimation, 18 implemented in CMA version 4.0 (Biostat, Englewood, NJ, USA). A continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to trials with zero-event arms for dichotomous outcomes. Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. 19 For continuous outcomes, mean difference (MD) was used for CIT (minutes) and CIL (centimeters), and Hedges’s g was used for post-procedural pain owing to instrument heterogeneity (10-cm/10-pt VAS, 11-pt VAS, Facial VAS, and 4-point discomfort/distension scales). For dichotomous outcomes (manual pressure, position change), the risk ratio (RR) was used; the use of RR rather than the odds ratio originally specified in the protocol is addressed in section “Deviations from the registered protocol.” All pooled effects are reported with two-sided 95% confidence intervals; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Pain was operationalized across heterogeneous post-procedural-discomfort instruments and standardized to Hedges’s g. Yu et al. 5 reported abdominal distension on a 4-point ordinal scale; this was included in the Pain pool because distension during scope insertion is a recognized component of post-procedural discomfort, and analyzing ordinal scales as continuous data is standard meta-analytic practice (Cochrane Handbook v6.5, §10.7). 17 Robustness to this decision is shown in the leave-one-out analysis (Figure S3(B)).

BMI subgroup analysis

BMI was analyzed as a pre-specified effect modifier using a stratified subgroup approach; the linear meta-regression also specified in the registered protocol was de-emphasized as a complementary analysis after inspection of the primary outcome data indicated a non-monotonic BMI dose–response (rationale in section “Deviations from the registered protocol,” Third deviation; structural limitation of linear regression discussed in section “The BMI paradox: From a monotonic to a bimodal relationship”). Study-level mean (or median, when mean was not reported) BMI of included trials was partitioned into three tertile-based strata: lean (<26 kg/m2), intermediate (26–28 kg/m2), and obese (>28 kg/m2). Per-trial BMI sources, the single trial without tertile-compatible BMI reporting (Gomaa et al. 15 ; “Unspecified” stratum in Figure 2), and the three median-substituted trials20–22 are detailed in the Table 1 footnote. Within-stratum pooling used the same random-effects model; between-stratum differences were not formally tested and are described qualitatively (overlapping confidence intervals and consistent direction of effect). The rationale for tertile cut-points differing from the WHO-standard cut-points is given in section “Deviations from the registered protocol.” Single-study strata handling and τ2 imputation mechanics are described in the Figure 2 caption.

Sensitivity analyses

Robustness of each pooled effect was examined using leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. Three additional pre-specified sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) exclusion of trials at high risk of bias in RoB 2 Domain 1; (2) exclusion of Miclea 2025 on publication-integrity grounds (see section “Post hoc exclusions”); and (3) exclusion of Tsutsumi et al. 16 from the Pain pool. Full leave-one-out estimates are reported in Figure S3 and Table S3.

Post hoc exclusions

Two studies identified by the systematic search were excluded post hoc from the quantitative synthesis: Miclea 2025 on publication-integrity grounds (predatory-publishing indicators), and Tsutsumi et al. 16 owing to inability to verify randomization against our INPLASY-registered RCT-only eligibility criterion. Journal-level and source-availability findings supporting each exclusion are detailed in Results section “Study selection and characteristics” and Table S3; implications for inference are discussed in section “Limitations.”

Deviations from the registered protocol

Three deviations from the INPLASY-registered protocol occurred during execution and are disclosed in full. (1) BMI cut-points—WHO-standard cut-points (Normal <25; Overweight 25–29.9; Obese ⩾30 kg/m2) were replaced with data-informed tertile-based cut-points (<26, 26–28, >28 kg/m2) because study-level mean BMI of all 14 trials clustered between approximately 23 and 32 kg/m2, which under WHO cut-points would have left the Obese stratum represented by a single trial. (2) RR versus OR—Dichotomous outcomes were pooled using RRs rather than the protocol-specified odds ratios, per current Cochrane Handbook guidance (Chapter 6, v6.5) 24 for non-rare outcomes (baseline event rates ~27%–91%, mean ~58%–62% across the two 11-trial pools (Manual Pressure includes Zhang et al. 13 ; Position Change includes Amin et al. 25 ; pool union k = 12)). (3) Stratified subgroup over linear regression—The BMI effect-modifier analysis is presented primarily as a stratified subgroup analysis rather than as the linear meta-regression also specified in the protocol; the stratified approach was already pre-specified, so this is a re-ordering of analytic emphasis, not the introduction of an unplanned analysis. The publicly accessible INPLASY record (INPLASY202550043) lists two authors (P.-F.H. and Y.-K.K.); three co-authors (T.-Y.H., M.-C.L., P.-J.C.) joined post-registration, and rather than amending the registry, all post-registration changes—methodological and authorship—are disclosed within this manuscript.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots for the four primary outcomes (Figure S1). Formal asymmetry testing (Egger’s regression, trim-and-fill) was not performed because three of the four primary outcomes (Pain k = 8; Manual Pressure k = 11; Position Change k = 11) are at or below the Cochrane Handbook-recommended minimum of k ⩾ 10 studies 26 for informative inference, at which the Type II error rate of these tests typically exceeds 80%. 27 Funnel-plot visualization and benchmarking against prior-synthesis search yields (described in Results section “Publication bias”) constituted our publication-bias assessment; the inability to formally exclude small-study effects is acknowledged in section “Limitations.”

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Searches were conducted in two phases: a primary search (PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, Web of Science, ClinicalTrials.gov) on April 30, 2025, and a supplementary update (ClinicalKey, ProQuest, ScienceDirect) on February 28, 2026. Full executed search strings, field tags, and per-source yields are presented in Table S2. Reference lists of relevant reviews and of each included trial were hand-screened.

Two studies otherwise meeting eligibility were excluded post hoc per Methods section “Post hoc exclusions”: Miclea (2025; publisher URF Publishers exhibited predatory-publishing indicators—6-day submission-to-publication interval, no documented peer review) and Tsutsumi et al. 16 (full text not retrievable despite institutional and interlibrary-loan requests; PubMed publication type “Comparative Study” rather than “Randomized Controlled Trial”; abstract used “divided into two groups” without specifying randomization). Both exclusions are recorded in Table S3 and discussed further in section “Limitations.” After these exclusions, 14 randomized controlled trials comprising 2834 patients were included2,5,6,9,13,15,20–23,25,28–30 (PRISMA flow in Figure 4; trial characteristics in Table 1; technical heterogeneity in Table S5).

Risk of bias

Risk of bias was assessed with RoB 2 12 across five domains plus an overall judgment. Most trials were judged at low or some-concerns risk. One trial 13 used alternating allocation rather than true randomization and was judged at high risk in Domain 1; this trial was retained in the primary analysis and excluded in a pre-specified sensitivity analysis. The RoB 2 summary is presented in Figure 1 ((a) and (b) captions).

Primary outcomes

Cecal intubation time

Fourteen trials (k = 14) reported CIT. Abdominal binder application was associated with a significant reduction in CIT (pooled MD −1.576 min, 95% CI −2.303 to −0.848; p < 0.001; I2 = 94.98%; Figure 5(a)). The direction of effect favored the binder in 13 of 14 trials (favored ≡ point estimate < 0); Özkan et al. 13 (MD +0.34 min) was the only direction-against trial. Both Özkan et al. 13 and the near-null Crockett et al. 2 (MD −0.02 min) enrolled cohorts with mean BMI in the intermediate stratum (26–28 kg/m2), foreshadowing the pattern in section “BMI-stratified subgroup analysis.”

Post-procedural pain

Pain was reported in 8 trials (k = 8; Tsutsumi et al. 16 excluded per section “Post hoc exclusions”). Effects were pooled as Hedges’s g owing to instrument heterogeneity. The pooled standardized MD was −0.853 (95% CI −1.253 to −0.454; p < 0.001; I2 = 93.54%; Figure 5(b)). All eight trials favored the binder in direction, although Crockett et al. 2 (g = −0.040) and Toyoshima et al. 9 (g = −0.270) did not reach within-study significance. Because Yu et al. 5 contributed a 4-point distension score, a leave-one-out analysis omitting this trial preserved the direction and significance of the pooled effect (Hedges’s g = −0.771; Figure S3(B); Methods section “Effect measures and synthesis”).

Need for manual abdominal pressure

Eleven trials (k = 11) reported manual pressure. Binder use was associated with a significant reduction (pooled RR 0.522, 95% CI 0.395–0.690; p < 0.001; I2 = 78.84%; Figure 5(c)). Ten of 11 trials showed RR <1; Özkan et al. 13 was the single study with RR >1 (1.333).

Need for patient position change

Eleven trials (k = 11) reported position change. Binder use was associated with a significant reduction (pooled RR 0.569, 95% CI 0.469–0.689; p < 0.001), with the lowest heterogeneity among the four primary outcomes (I2 = 15.77%; Figure 5(d)). Nine of 11 trials favored the binder in point-estimate direction; the 2 exceptions were Özkan et al. 13 (RR = 2.000, favoring control) and Gündeş et al. 20 (RR = 1.000, neutral). Six of 11 individual 95% CIs crossed unity (Figure 5(d)).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out analyses were performed for all four primary outcomes (Figure S3). The direction and statistical significance of each pooled effect were preserved after removal of any single trial; every iteration remained statistically significant (p < 0.001 throughout). Per-outcome ranges and pivot trials are reported in the Figure S3 caption; no single trial was required to sustain any pooled effect.

BMI-stratified subgroup analysis

Trials were partitioned into three BMI tertiles (lean < 26, intermediate 26–28, obese >28 kg/m2); Gomaa et al. 15 lacked tertile-compatible BMI reporting and is displayed under “Unspecified” in Figure 2 (not interpreted as a pooled estimate). Per-stratum forest plots are shown in Figure 2(a)–(d); cross-stratum trends for the three multi-study outcomes are visualized in Figure 3.

Bimodal pattern (CIT, manual pressure, post-procedural pain)

CIT and manual pressure exhibited a non-monotonic, bimodal BMI response: both lean and obese strata reached significance, while the intermediate stratum did not (Figure 3(a) and (b)). For CIT, lean MD −2.413 min (p = 0.001; k = 5) and obese MD −1.711 min (p = 0.044; k = 4) both reached significance, while the intermediate stratum (MD −0.656 min, p = 0.426; k = 4) did not. Manual pressure showed mirror-image tail-stratum point estimates (RR 0.426 in both lean and obese strata, p ⩽ 0.007; non-significant 0.710 at 26–28). Pain replicated the same bimodal numerical structure across the tail strata (lean Hedges’s g −0.914; obese Hedges’s g −1.067; both p < 0.001); the intermediate stratum contains only Crockett et al. 2 and is reported as hypothesis-generating (Figure 2(b) caption).

Monotonic pattern (position change)

Position change followed a monotonic pattern: only the lean stratum reached significance (RR 0.535, 95% CI 0.459–0.623, p < 0.001); neither the intermediate nor the obese stratum reached significance (Figure 3(c)). This asymmetry—bimodal for three outcomes, absent for position change—motivates the dual-pathway mechanism in sections “Extending the splinting framework: A dual-pathway mechanistic model” and “Triangulating the dual-pathway model through secondary outcomes.”

Study-level subgroup analyses

Three study-level subgroup analyses (sedation depth, endoscopist experience tier, binder type) were performed to address residual heterogeneity beyond the main BMI stratification. Stratum pooled estimates, forest plots, and within-stratum k are reported in Table S6 and Figure S4(a)–(c). Across all three dimensions, every subgroup retained the same effect direction as the overall pool, and the majority of within-stratum comparisons reached significance; non-significant strata contained either single studies or small numbers of trials with inflated between-study variance rather than consistent null or adverse signals. Three findings warrant emphasis: (1) the Conscious-Sedation stratum (k = 2 across Pain/Manual Pressure/Position Change) was the only stratum failing to reach significance for those three outcomes—mechanistic interpretation in section “Heterogeneity”; (2) Single High-Volume operators detected the binder benefit as readily as Multi-operator mixed teams, indicating the effect is not contingent on endoscopist experience level; (3) the Commercial-vs-Generic contrast was non-significant for any outcome with overlapping confidence intervals, indicating that circumferential abdominal compression rather than any specific design feature is the active principle.

Publication bias

The rationale for not performing formal asymmetry tests (Egger’s regression, trim-and-fill) is detailed in Methods section “Publication bias.” Two empirical findings argue against systematic publication bias: (1) the pool grew organically from k = 8 in the only prior colonoscopy-specific synthesis 34 to k = 14 with the contemporaneous addition of six trials,15,20,22,23,25,28 and these added trials span a wide range of effect sizes (large pain and manual-pressure benefits, with small-to-moderate CIT effects, in Marmolejo et al. 22 and Zhang et al. 23 ; modest or null benefits in Adalı 28 and Gündeş et al. 20 ), arguing against selective enrichment; (2) funnel plots for all four primary outcomes (Figure S1) show no pronounced asymmetry. Small-study effects cannot be formally excluded at the present k.

Secondary outcome: CIL

Three trials5,22,29 reported CIL. Pooled MD was −4.312 cm (95% CI −9.394 to 0.770; p = 0.096; non-significant; Figure S2). Given the small k and non-significant pooled effect, CIL is reported in the Supplemental Material only (Figure S2).

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (2834 patients)—after two post hoc exclusions disclosed in the Methods section “Post hoc exclusions”—abdominal binder use during colonoscopy produced a statistically significant benefit across all four primary outcomes (pooled estimates and CIs reported in the Results section “Primary outcomes”; robust across leave-one-out iterations). The central new contribution of this analysis is the demonstration that the BMI response is non-monotonic: the benefit of binder use is significant in both the lean (<26 kg/m2) and obese (>28 kg/m2) strata, but not in the intermediate stratum (26–28 kg/m2) for CIT and manual pressure, and is confined to the lean stratum for position change. We interpret this pattern through a dual-pathway mechanistic model (section “Extending the splinting framework: A dual-pathway mechanistic model”) and triangulate it against the three secondary endpoints (section “Triangulating the dual-pathway model through secondary outcomes”); the framework reconciles previously discrepant trials and explains the borderline findings of prior pooled analyses (section “Comparison with previous meta-analyses”).

The BMI paradox: From a monotonic to a bimodal relationship

Our analysis extends the findings of Abdallfatah et al., 10 who conducted the most comprehensive pooled assessment of abdominal compression devices during colonoscopy to date (16 RCTs, 3098 participants) and examined BMI as a moderator through linear meta-regression. Their regression detected a small positive BMI coefficient for post-procedural pain (β = 0.10; p = 0.049) and for position-change frequency (β = 0.09; p = 0.03) but no significant BMI modifier for CIT, manual pressure, or complete colonoscopy rate. The authors themselves concluded that subgroup analysis was not applicable across their dataset because of inconsistent BMI cut-points and prioritized the identification of high-BMI subgroups in their Future Directions. Our tertile-stratified re-analysis addresses precisely this gap.

Applying a pre-specified three-stratum partition to study-level mean BMI, we uncovered a non-monotonic, bimodal BMI-response pattern for two of the four pooled outcomes (CIT and manual pressure; per-stratum point estimates and CIs in Results section “BMI-stratified subgroup analysis”/Figure 2). The bimodal pattern recasts three trials previously appearing mutually inconsistent—Toros et al. 6 (lean cohort, one of the larger CIT reductions and the largest within the lean-BMI stratum), Özkan et al. 13 and Adalı et al. 28 (intermediate-BMI cohorts, null or near-null CIT)—as fully consistent: each falls at the BMI position predicted by the dose-response curve.

The failure of previous linear meta-regression approaches to detect this pattern reflects a structural limitation rather than a methodological error: averaging across a non-monotonic relationship produces a slope attenuated toward zero, and the pattern is invisible until the data are partitioned. Future work should examine whether restricted cubic splines or individual-patient-data meta-analysis formally recover the non-linearity.

Extending the splinting framework: A dual-pathway mechanistic model

The notion that external abdominal compression facilitates colonoscope insertion is not new. Shah et al., 35 using magnetic endoscope imaging in 91% of patients studied, characterized manual abdominal pressure as a “splinting” maneuver intended to limit sigmoid looping. What has remained ambiguous is how the same external compression could yield benefits at very different ends of the BMI spectrum—a question sharpened by our bimodal finding. We propose that the splinting concept be elaborated into two anatomically and physiologically distinct pathways operating with different relative dominance across the BMI range.

Pathway 1—Mesenteric-fixation pathway (predominant at low BMI)

In lean patients, the mesentery is long and mobile and the abdominal wall is thin; redundant sigmoid loops form preferentially because there is little anatomic restraint opposing mesenteric displacement. External compression applied through an abdominal binder functions here as a broad, distributed fixator of the mesenteric root, stabilizing it against displacement and limiting loop formation. Convergent evidence supports this mechanism: Shah et al. 35 reported that manual pressure resolved only a minority of loop episodes versus position change, consistent with broad-area fixation being mechanically superior to a focused point force; Pasternak et al. 36 documented an inverse BMI–loop relationship across 647 patients; and Chung et al. 37 identified BMI <23 kg/m² as an independent predictor of both procedure difficulty and intra-procedural pain.

Pathway 2—Wall-stiffening pathway (predominant at high BMI)

At higher BMI, the dominant anatomic feature is attenuated abdominal-wall compliance. Malbrain et al. 38 formally defined abdominal compliance and listed obesity as a canonical cause of reduced compliance; El Bojairami et al., 39 using a finite element model, showed that poor abdominal elasticity and thickening fat layers produce low-compliance abdominal walls in which insufflation and probe advancement encounter greater resistive force. An abdominal binder in this context concentrates the force vector against a wall that is already less yielding. Convergent empirical support: Abdallfatah et al.’s 10 own positive BMI coefficients for pain and position-change frequency indicate that the benefit of binder use attenuates but does not disappear as BMI rises—precisely the linear-projection signature predicted by a non-monotonic curve whose downslope begins in the intermediate BMI range.

Why the intermediate stratum shows the weakest effect

Between these two regimes lies a transitional zone (≈26–28 kg/m2) in which neither pathway is strongly operative: the mesentery is sufficiently restrained that loop formation is less frequent than in the lean group, but abdominal compliance is not yet low enough that wall-stiffening augmentation provides substantial additional force transmission. This is the structural explanation for the non-significant pooled effect in the 26–28 stratum across our bimodal outcomes.

Triangulating the dual-pathway model through secondary outcomes

The dual-pathway model predicts outcome-specific signatures at the three secondary endpoints, and both signatures are observed (per-stratum estimates in Figure 2 and Results section “BMI-stratified subgroup analysis”). (1) Bimodal outcomes—post-procedural pain and manual pressure. Both display the bimodal BMI signature predicted by the model; the intermediate-stratum attenuation indicates that binder use is a sufficient substitute for manual pressure only where one of the two pathways is strongly active. (2) Asymmetric outcome—position change. Position change is the maneuver specifically used to reposition a redundant or looping sigmoid—a problem of the fixation pathway—and the overall RR is driven predominantly by the lean stratum. The wall-stiffening pathway should not produce a position-change signature, and it does not.

Comparison with previous meta-analyses

Three previous meta-analyses examined abdominal-binder efficacy with methodological choices that constrained their conclusions. Abuelazm et al. 34 (k = 8) reported borderline manual-pressure significance (RR ≈ 0.65, p = 0.05); our larger pool (k = 11; RR 0.522, 95% CI: 0.395–0.690, p < 0.001) places this outcome firmly inside significance, and the BMI-stratified analysis recasts the earlier borderline finding as the averaged signature of two responder strata and one non-responder stratum. Abdallfatah et al. 10 (k = 16), the largest prior synthesis, reported a CIT effect close to ours; the present analysis’s methodological contribution lies in resolving the BMI-moderator question their linear meta-regression could not address—an aim their Future Directions explicitly identified (section “The BMI paradox: From a monotonic to a bimodal relationship”). Zafar et al. 8 (k = 7) reported non-significant patient comfort, attributable to WMD-vs-Hedges’s-g instrument standardization and inclusion of Tsutsumi et al. 16 (excluded by us per section “Post hoc exclusions”). The six RCTs newly added since Abuelazm et al. 34 (Gomaa et al., 15 Gundes et al., 20 Marmolejo et al., 22 Zhang et al., 23 Amin et al., 25 Adal, 28 ) span a wide bidirectional range of CIT effects (Marmolejo et al. 22 /Liu et al. 21 large reductions; Adalı et al. 28 null intermediate-BMI), providing the non-formal reassurance against small-study effects described in section “Publication bias” (with rationale in section “Publication bias”).

Heterogeneity

Substantial between-study heterogeneity in our pooled analysis (CIT I2 = 94.98%; Pain I2 = 93.54%; Manual pressure I2 = 78.84%) is a feature, not a confound, of this literature and was the motivation for our pre-specified BMI stratification. These I2 values are broadly concordant with the most recent prior pooled analyses.8,10 Position change—the most homogeneous outcome (I2 = 15.77%)—is also the outcome in which the dual-pathway mechanism predicts the least BMI-dependent variation, an internal consistency we describe in section “Extending the splinting framework: a dual-pathway mechanistic model.”

To interrogate residual heterogeneity after accounting for BMI, we conducted three additional study-level subgroup analyses (sedation depth, endoscopist tier, binder type; Figure S4(a)–(c) and Table S6). Across the three dimensions, every subgroup estimate retained the same effect direction as the overall pool, and the majority of within-stratum comparisons reached significance. Where single-stratum estimates failed to reach significance, the pattern reflected either small within-subgroup k or directional disagreement among the contributing trials, rather than a coherent null signal.

Two observations are worth emphasizing. First, the Commercial vs Generic contrast is not significant for any outcome, with overlapping confidence intervals—arguing against the hypothesis that elaborated engineering features of purpose-designed Commercial devices are required to obtain the mechanical effect and reinforcing the section “Extending the splinting framework: A dual-pathway mechanistic model” framework that circumferential abdominal compression, not any specific design feature, is the active principle. Second, the endoscopist-tier analysis shows that Single High-Volume operators detect the effect as readily as Multi-operator mixed teams, reassuring for generalizability.

Beyond the null Commercial-vs-Generic statistical contrast, three theoretical dimensions of binder design plausibly influence compression efficiency and patient comfort, though the present dataset does not permit formal between-device testing. First, compression-pressure distribution profile. Generic elastic corsets and obstetric binders deliver a quasi-uniform circumferential pressure determined by material stretch and patient circumference; in our included trials, Toros et al. 6 used an inexpensive commercial elastic textile corset and Yu et al. 5 used a generic abdominal obstetric binder. Purpose-designed devices employ region-specific architectures: the N-Doe Pillow used by Schulman et al. 30 is a hands-free device delivering multi-zone segmental compression; ColoWrap used by Crockett et al. 2 supplies a focal-compression secondary strap directed at the sigmoid region; Marmolejo et al. 22 employed the Revive 3-in-1 commercially-available postpartum support belt (KeaBabies); and the inflatable device used by Zhang et al. 23 delivers an adjustable, pneumatically variable pressure (60–100 mmHg, the only trial in our pool reporting quantitative pressure). These design differences plausibly translate into different topographic compression maps, yet the observed effect distributions across our four primary outcomes overlap, suggesting that the colonic mechanical response is sensitive to circumferential pressure of any topographic profile sufficient to engage either pathway in the dual-pathway model—consistent with finite-element evidence that abdominal compliance is dominated by wall thickness, abdominal radius, and tissue elasticity. 39 Second, achievable pressure range. Passive elastic devices apply pressure determined by material stretch and patient circumference rather than by active control; active pneumatic systems 23 extend the achievable pressure range substantially upward and permit graded adjustment. The fact that the included passive-elastic trials and the single pneumatic trial yield comparable pooled effects across our four primary outcomes is compatible with a pressure-threshold rather than a monotonic dose-response model—a hypothesis testable in future trials with standardized quantitative pressure reporting. Third, patient comfort and tolerability. Generic devices are reusable, inexpensive, and broadly available but lack standardized, patient-specific pressure titration; Commercial devices theoretically aim to distribute load more evenly, yet the clinical impact of these design differences on patient tolerance remains unclear. Inference about between-device comfort must be considered preliminary because few trials reported binder-related discomfort and those that did used heterogeneous instruments. Per-trial hardware descriptors are tabulated in Table S5.

On the sedation-depth axis, the Conscious-Sedation stratum (k = 2 across Pain/Manual Pressure/Position Change) was the only stratum failing to reach significance for those three outcomes; Deep-Sedation and No-Sedation strata each reached significance. We interpret this pattern as a three-state mechanistic gradient across the conscious-, deep-, and no-sedation states defined by the American Society of Anesthesiologists Task Force 2018, 40 in which the marginal benefit of the binder is modulated by the patient’s intrinsic abdominal-wall muscle tone—which we propose varies with sedation depth.

Under deep sedation—typically propofol-based with loss of voluntary motor control—the diaphragm and abdominal-wall musculature relax substantially. The associated drop in baseline intra-abdominal pressure (Malbrain et al., 41 Part 2: muscle relaxation significantly lowers the resting intra-abdominal pressure Pv0, p = 0.001, though without significantly altering abdominal compliance or elastance per se) and loss of cooperative muscle tone facilitate lateral wall displacement and loop formation during shaft advancement. Concordantly, Roberts-Thomson and Teo 42 note that unconscious sedation impairs the patient’s ability to cooperate with positional maneuvers; the binder’s circumferential pressure substitutes for this iatrogenic loss of tone and reduces both manual pressure and position-change requirements, predicting a large marginal benefit consistent with our pooled estimates in this stratum.

Under conscious sedation—commonly midazolam-fentanyl or low-dose propofol with preserved voluntary control—the patient retains spontaneous abdominal-wall tone; Roberts-Thomson and Teo 42 note that mildly sedated patients more readily complain of pain, prompting endoscopists to use alternative techniques rather than “pushing through” loops. The preserved patient cooperation and muscle tone partially substitute for the binder’s mechanical role and reduce the marginal benefit—the mechanistic reading of our Conscious-stratum non-significance, complementary to (rather than competing with) the small-k statistical interpretation.

Under no sedation, preserved muscle tone is engaged simultaneously with full visceral pain perception; the binder reduces both the mechanical loop formation that produces visceral stretching and the perception of that stretching, yielding a significant pooled effect through dual-channel benefit.

The binder’s overall pooled effect across all four primary outcomes remained significant irrespective of sedation regimen; we therefore interpret the sedation interaction as a modulator of effect magnitude rather than as a determinant of clinical utility. The clinical implication for practicing endoscopists is that binder use is supported across the entire sedation spectrum, with the largest marginal benefit anticipated under deep sedation and the largest dual-channel benefit anticipated in unsedated procedures.

Limitations

Ecological inference

BMI stratification was performed at the study level; the bimodal BMI response is therefore an ecological finding requiring confirmation by individual-patient-data meta-analysis or BMI-stratified randomized trials. The intermediate (26–28 kg/m2) BMI subgroup for post-procedural pain is contributed by a single trial 2 and is not interpreted as a pooled estimate (Results section “BMI-stratified subgroup analysis”; Figure 2 caption); Gomaa et al. 15 (“Unspecified” stratum) and Tsutsumi et al. 16 (excluded per section “Post hoc exclusions”) are similarly affected by BMI-reporting limitations.

Protocol deviations

Three deviations from the registered protocol—tertile-based BMI cut-points; RR rather than odds ratio; stratified subgroup analysis prioritized over linear meta-regression—are disclosed in Methods section “Deviations from the registered protocol” and grounded on (1) imbalance of the WHO cut-points across the 14-trial mean BMI distribution, (2) Cochrane Handbook v6.5 Chapter 6 guidance for non-rare outcomes, and (3) the structural inability of linear regression to detect a non-monotonic pattern.

Post hoc exclusions

Miclea et al. (2025) and Tsutsumi et al. 16 were excluded post hoc under our pre-registered RCT-only eligibility criterion; rationales are detailed in Methods section “post hoc exclusions.” Sensitivity analyses (Methods section “Sensitivity analyses”; Figure S3) confirmed the robustness of all four pooled effects to either exclusion.

Technical heterogeneity

Binder type, application timing, pressure range, sedation regimen, and endoscopist experience varied substantially across included trials (Table S5). Residual within-stratum heterogeneity reflects this variability, and our GRADE certainty ceiling is comparable to that reported by Abdallfatah et al. 10 (primarily low-to-very-low).

Publication bias

Formal asymmetry testing was not pursued for the reasons in Methods section “Publication bias”; the funnel-plot visual inspection and the organic expansion of the pool from k = 8 to k = 14 with bidirectionally dispersed effect sizes are described in Results section “Publication bias.” Small-study effects cannot be formally excluded at the present k.

Blinding

Most included trials were open-label or single-blind by necessity; a sham binder was used by only three trials, of which two22,29 achieved double-blinding and one 2 was single-blind with sham. Performance bias on subjective outcomes cannot be fully excluded.

Clinical implications

The clinical findings of this analysis should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The BMI-response pattern was derived from study-level mean BMI rather than individual-patient BMI, the intermediate stratum was limited for two outcomes, and the 26 and 28 kg/m2 cut-points are empirical rather than physiologically anchored. Within these constraints, three implications can be drawn cautiously.

First, abdominal binder use is supported as an adjunctive device in lean patients (BMI < 26 kg/m2), for whom the fixation pathway predicts—and our data confirm—a reduction in CIT, manual pressure, and the need for position change. In this population, the binder may meaningfully substitute for repeated position-change maneuvers during insertion, a finding with workflow value.

Second, in obese patients (BMI > 28 kg/m2), binder use is supported primarily for the outcomes driven by the wall-stiffening pathway: reductions in CIT, post-procedural pain, and the need for manual pressure. The pooled point estimates in this stratum are of comparable magnitude to those in the lean stratum, although confidence intervals are wider because of the smaller number of contributing trials.

Third, in patients in the intermediate range (BMI 26–28 kg/m2), a uniformly positive recommendation is not supported by the present data; we suggest that adjunctive use be considered at the discretion of the endoscopist on the basis of per-case judgment of sigmoid mobility and abdominal-wall tone rather than as a default.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 randomized controlled trials (2834 patients) demonstrates that abdominal binder use during colonoscopy reduces CIT, post-procedural pain, the need for manual abdominal pressure, and the need for position change. The BMI-response in our pooled analyses appeared non-monotonic, with significant benefit in lean (<26 kg/m2) and obese (>28 kg/m2) strata and attenuated effect in the intermediate range (26–28 kg/m2). This pattern, which should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating given the limited number of contributing trials per stratum, reconciles previously discrepant trials and is consistent with a dual-pathway mechanism (mesenteric fixation predominant at low BMI; wall stiffening predominant at high BMI), to be confirmed by individual-patient-data meta-analysis or BMI-stratified randomized trials. Future work should pursue three priorities of decreasing analytical urgency and increasing translational specificity: (1) an individual-patient-data meta-analysis of the existing trial pool, the most direct test of whether the study-level bimodal pattern is reproduced at patient-level resolution; (2) a BMI-stratified randomized trial in obese patients (BMI ⩾30 kg/m2) with adenoma detection rate as the primary endpoint, the most clinically actionable extension of the wall-stiffening pathway; and (3) hardware-and-protocol standardization across future RCTs—uniform reporting of binder type, application timing, anatomical placement, and quantitative pressure—to enable formal device-class subgroup analyses in subsequent syntheses. Beyond obesity, BMI-stratified or technique-stratified trials in other high-risk subpopulations (elderly patients, prior incomplete colonoscopy, anatomical predictors of difficult procedures) merit parallel investigation.
