Misdiagnosis of restrictive cardiomyopathy averted by a stethoscope: a case report
Authors: Shyam S Kothari, Sagar Ghetiya, Vishal Sharma, Prarthi Shah, Nirav Panchal, Vincenzo Nuzzi, Deborah Cosmi, Sumit Sohal, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIatrogenic arteriovenous (AV) fistula is a rare but treatable cause of high-output heart failure that can mimic restrictive cardiomyopathy and may lead to inappropriate listing for heart transplantation.Case summaryA 37-year-old man presented with 7 months of progressive pedal oedema, tense ascites, and exertional dyspnoea. The baseline ECG showed sinus tachycardia with right axis deviation, and chest radiograph revealed cardiomegaly with right pleural effusion. Transthoracic echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated right atrial and ventricular enlargement and severe functional tricuspid regurgitation with preserved biventricular systolic function. A provisional diagnosis of restrictive cardiomyopathy was considered, and the patient was referred for transplant evaluation. Bedside examination identified a continuous murmur loudest over the left iliac fossa. Contrast CT aortogram confirmed a large iliocaval communication between the left common iliac artery and the inferior vena cava. Surgical closure with vascular grafting was performed. At 1-month follow-up, the symptoms resolved and echocardiography showed normalization of right heart size with disappearance of tricuspid regurgitation.DiscussionThis case highlights the enduring value of focused history and physical examination in detecting extracardiac, reversible causes of heart failure.Keywords: Case report, High-output heart failure, Arteriovenous fistula, Restrictive cardiomyopathy, Tricuspid regurgitation, Heart transplant

Introduction

It is reasonable to consider heart transplant in a patient who remains severely symptomatic despite guideline-directed medical therapy. With dwindling clinical skills among physicians and increasing emphasis on imaging investigations, misdiagnosis of a treatable cause of heart failure might occur. We present a case of refractory heart failure diagnosed as restrictive cardiomyopathy where the use of a stethoscope and careful history unravelled a treatable cause of heart failure, and the patient was cured of his ailment.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old man had gradually progressive pedal oedema, abdominal distention, and breathlessness for the last 7 months. The patient was treated with diuretics but worsening clinical status needed hospital admission. His haemogram, liver function, and renal function tests were normal. The pro-BNP levels were elevated (2472 pg/ml). The ECG was unremarkable, and the chest X-ray showed significant cardiomegaly (Figures 1 and 2). A 2D echocardiogram revealed normal left ventricular function (LVEF 60%), but the right ventricle (RV) and right atrium were dilated with severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR Gmax 32) (Figure 3). The tricuspid valve was non-coapting (see Supplementary material online, Video S1). Transmitral inflow velocities measured Ve = 1.32 m/s and Va = /0.55 m/s with a calculated E/A ratio of 2.41. Tissue Doppler imaging showed medial and lateral mitral annular e′ velocities of 0.15 and 0.18 m/s, respectively. Left atrium measures 4.2 cm in parasternal long axis view, consistent with mild left atrial enlargement. Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion was 13 mm. A cardiac MRI was also done that revealed dilated atria (right atrial volume, 73 ml/m2; left atrial volume, 50 ml/m2), dilated right ventricle (RV end diastolic volume, 139 ml/m2; RV end systolic volume, 55 ml/m2), and mildly dilated left ventricle (LV end diastolic volume, 113 ml/m2; LV end systolic volume, 44 ml/m2). The systolic function of both ventricles was normal.

The ECG shows sinus tachycardia at a rate of approximately 106 b.p.m. with rightward QRS axis but without chamber hypertrophy, conduction block, or ischaemic changes.

The posteroanterior chest X-ray demonstrates marked cardiomegaly with a prominent right heart border, right-sided pleural effusion, and pulmonary venous hypertension.

Preoperative transthoracic echocardiogram: the apical four-chamber view demonstrates marked dilation of the right atrium and ventricle, non-coapting tricuspid leaflets, and severe tricuspid regurgitation.

No evidence of fibrosis was seen on cardiac MRI as there was no significant late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) in either ventricle. Elevated regional native T1 mapping values were noted in the basal and mid cavity inferior and inferoseptal and anterolateral segments, raising the possibility of subclinical myocardial fibrosis. T2 mapping values were suboptimal with no evidence of oedema. The patient did not improve with usual medical therapy and was referred to our hospital for possible cardiac transplant. The referring working clinical diagnosis was idiopathic restrictive cardiomyopathy with predominant right ventricle involvement.

On examination, the patient had a heart rate of 90/min, blood pressure 110/70 mm Hg, good volume pulse, markedly elevated jugular venous pressure with tall V waves, and gross ascites. On auscultation, a systolic murmur of tricuspid regurgitation and right ventricular S3 were heard. Most remarkably, a continuous murmur was heard all over the back. The murmur, however, was loudest at the left iliac fossa on gradual inching of the stethoscope. A diagnosis of arteriovenous fistula was made, and the patient was investigated. A cardiac catheterization revealed the right atrial pressure of 20 mm Hg, raised right and left ventricular end diastolic pressures (16 and 18 mmHg, respectively), and moderately elevated pulmonary artery pressures (PA mean: 27 mm Hg). The IVC saturation was 90%, whereas the SVC was only 40%. The calculated cardiac index was 4.84 L/min/m2. The high IVC saturation suggested an arteriovenous shunt in the abdomen. He denied any history of trauma or accident but revealed that he had undergone spine surgery for a prolapsed disc at the L3–L4 level approximately 8 months ago. A CT angiogram revealed a large communication from the left common iliac artery to the inferior vena cava (Figure 4). The continuous murmur from the fistula was heard all over the back as well, possibly due to radiation along the spine! The patient was operated on for the same with a ligation of the fistulous communication. In view of very dense adhesions to surrounding tissue due to previous surgical procedure, closure of the fistulous segment with aortic iliac bypass with Dacron graft was performed. At 1-month follow-up, his symptoms had completely resolved, and severe tricuspid regurgitation had disappeared (Figure 5) (see Supplementary material online, Video S2).

Coronary reconstructed CT angiographic image demonstrates a large arteriovenous fistulous communication between the left common iliac artery and the IVC with critical stenosis distal to the site of the fistula.

Follow-up apical four-chamber transthoracic echocardiographic view obtained 1 month after surgical repair demonstrates well-coapting tricuspid valve leaflets and complete resolution of tricuspid regurgitation indicating reversal of ventricular volume overload.

Discussion

This case underscores the importance of clinical evaluation by a cohesive history and physical examination in every patient in the clinical context. The patient had high-output heart failure from an iatrogenic injury during the spine surgery. The findings of high cardiac output were evident on examination. The misdiagnosis of RCM could have been avoided, if the high cardiac output findings were recognized earlier, as patients with RCM typically have a low cardiac index. Fortunately, simple clinical examination with an open mind solved what appeared to be a mysterious heart failure, and his recovery was complete. Similar cases of arteriovenous fistula following a spinal surgery have been reported in the past.1–5 Once the possibility was considered, everything fell in place for the diagnosis in this patient.

A simple examination of such a good volume pulse and presence of a continuous murmur were enough to point to the correct diagnosis. While advances in multimodality imaging have made tremendous progress, patients’ story (his-story), and physical examination remain relevant even in the current era.
