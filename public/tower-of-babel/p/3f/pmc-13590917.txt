3-D landmarking repeatability emphasizes challenges in scan positioning during weight bearing CT of the knee
Authors: T.D. Turmezei, A. Boddu, T. Whitmarsh, N.H. Degala, J.A. Lynch, N.A. Segal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMaintaining repeatability of positioning during knee weight bearing CT (WBCT) acquisition can be challenging. We evaluated a manual bone landmarking system and automatically derived angulation metrics to advance understanding of joint positioning repeatability.DesignBilateral knee WBCTs for 14 recruited individuals taken at a mean ± SD interval of 14.9 ± 8.1 days using a frame and laser guidance for positioning were used in this technical study. One observer manually placed 33 bony landmarks at each knee, verified by a second observer who also placed landmarks at each end of the scan range in the centre of the bone. Landmark repeatability error was the mean ± SD absolute distance (mm) between baseline and follow-up. Automated methods for knee flexion angle (KFA), valgus and vertical positioning alignment were developed from the landmarks.ResultsMost repeatable landmarks were around the patella and proximal fibula with best performance at the proximal fibula tip (1.3 ± 0.5 mm). Least repeatable landmarks were along shallow curves, e.g. central tibial margins and around the trochlea, with worst performance at the tibial tuberosity (5.5 ± 4.7 mm). Mean repeatability for 85% of placed landmarks was in the 1–4 mm range. Valgus alignment absolute repeatability error ranged from 0.1–2.1° and for KFA was 0.3–15.5°, neither dependent on KL grade severity. Worst vertical scan range placement error was ∼60 mm.ConclusionDespite guidance systems, repeatability of KFA and vertical positioning could be optimised at the time of acquisition. This would provide a robust platform for evaluation of metrics such as meniscal extrusion and 3-D joint space width from WBCT that have shown promise in the evaluation of knee OA.

Introduction

Weight-bearing computed tomography (WBCT) is being increasingly used in osteoarthritis (OA) imaging research with growing popularity at the knee after initial development at the foot and ankle [1]. Unlike conventional radiography, WBCT provides 3-D imaging of a joint under physiological load, thereby capturing functional alignment and bone morphology at a radiation doses lower than conventional multidetector CT and similar to a series of plain radiographs involving multiple views of both knees [2]. These features make WBCT well-suited to the evaluation of joint space, alignment, and bone architecture in vivo, showing promise as a potential replacement for radiographs in assessing structural changes of OA, for example as observed at patellofemoral joint [3].

In addition, WBCT offers complementary information to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), such as 3-D bone morphology, bone thickness and bone attenuation measures as well as quantitative joint space mapping [4,5]. Recent advances include the introduction of a new extremity cone-beam CT system and a dedicated WBCT protocol in the MOST cohort [6]. However, even with these advantages, reproducibility and repeatability remain recognised challenges for WBCT acquisition [7].

Bone surface landmarks represent anatomical features that are key for understanding joint relationships and biomechanics. At the knee, these landmarks provide important biomechanical information for measuring alignment of both the tibiofemoral and the patellofemoral joints, as well as for tracking bone kinematics during movement, particularly within the patellofemoral joint. Biomechanical factors at the patellofemoral joint have been associated with pain and with progression of lateral patellofemoral OA [[8], [9], [10]]. Similarly, varus-valgus malalignment at the tibiofemoral joint is an established risk factor for development and progression of OA, as well as being a consequence of the disease process [11]. These findings highlight the potential for using bone surface landmarks in assessment and analysis of knee joint positioning and alignment using WBCT.

Established automated and semi-automated methods exist for knee joint landmarking in several imaging modalities. Xue et al. (2015) proposed a statistical shape model-based approach applied to MRI data from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI), defining 8 femoral, 10 tibial, and 6 patellar landmarks [12]. Their method, trained on 12 and tested on 31 datasets, achieved a mean localisation accuracy of 1.64 ± 1.03 mm. More recently, Park et al. (2025) implemented an artificial intelligence (AI)-based approach using dual-energy CT in 30 individuals from the MOST cohort, identifying 21 landmarks (19 automated, 2 manually placed) with excellent inter- and intra-rater reproducibility. Barbosa et al. (2024) also developed a U-net model for anterior knee landmarking on MRI in 95 individuals, focusing on patellofemoral instability metrics such as sulcus angle and lateral trochlear inclination, demonstrating good to excellent reliability compared to the AI model [13].

The objectives of this technical study were: (i) to develop a methodology for 3-D bone surface landmarking from knee WBCT imaging data from which recognized knee positioning metrics could derived; (ii) to demonstrate the feasibility of this new system; and (iii) to retrospectively evaluate test–retest repeatability of these knee joint positioning metrics from imaging taken with the latest Multicenter Osteoarthritis Study (MOST) WBCT image acquisition protocol.

Methods

Participants

Following Institutional Review Board approval of the protocol and informed consent process (STUDY00160530), participants who were age 50–79 years, had Kellgren and Lawrence (KL) grade 0–4 knees, were not pregnant, had a body mass <230 kg (weight limit for step stool), met the MOST4 inclusion criteria, defined as having frequent knee pain or at least one established risk factor for osteoarthritis (e.g., overweight status, prior knee injury, or previous knee surgery),18 and were able to safely stand upright for 5 min with support were recruited and consented at the University of Kansas Medical Center. The full range of KL grades was included in the convenience sample for this technical study in order to test the repeatability of our measures across different severities of structural osteoarthritis. All were scheduled for baseline and two-week follow-up WBCT imaging. All datasets were anonymized to participant identifiers and scan timepoint prior to analyses by one researcher not taking part in the analysis and then unblinded for the analysis.

Image acquisition

All WBCT scans were acquired using the same XFI WBCT scanner (Planmed Oy, Helsinki, Finland, Fig. 1) and knee WBCT image acquisition protocol used in the MOST Study, and then repeated 2 weeks later in order to perform test-retest repeatability analysis (mean ± SD interval of 14.9 ± 8.1 days). Knee positioning was standardised using a Synaflexer™ device (originally Synarc Inc, California, USA, now Clario, Pennsylvania, USA) to position feet in 5° of external rotation with approximately equal weight on each foot, patellae and both thighs touching a vertical plate to maintain the tips of the great toes, patellae and anterior thighs coplanar. A tri-planar laser guidance system was used to aim to centre the vertical scan range on the tibiofemoral joint line [6]. Acquisition parameters were 96 kV tube voltage, 51.4 mA tube current, and 3.5 s exposure time. Reconstructions were performed with a standard bone algorithm, producing 0.3 mm isotropic voxels with a 17 cm diameter field of view over a 21 cm vertical scan range. Landmarking

The landmarking system used was extended from an approach described by Xue et al. (2015) with additional landmarks thought to be useful for biometric analysis that the original schema did not include [12]. In order to minimize error in landmark placement so that we could test positioning metric repeatability with as little of this error as practicably possible, a team of two observers were used to mark and verify landmark placement according to our updated schema. The first observer (AB) placed 33 (10 femoral, 12 tibial, 7 patellar, and 4 fibular) landmarks using Stradview (software from the Cambridge University Engineering Department, Cambridge UK, free to use for research purposes, https://mi.eng.cam.ac.uk/Main/StradView). Stradview provides standard axial, coronal and sagittal reformatted viewing windows as well as an additional 3-D window that can used to review the imaging data set as a 3-D rotatable volume. All these review windows were all used to optimise landmark placement (Fig. 2). The second observer (TT) reviewed these landmarks and placed additional landmarks at the extremes of the vertical scan range within the medullary cavity centres, i.e. at the most proximal femur and most distal tibia. For each bone, the centre of gravity (CoG) was defined as the average position of all landmarks excluding those at the bone ends. Image processing and registration

Automatic bone segmentations, with minor manual corrections, were generated in ScanXM (Minogame Ltd, Cambridge, UK). These segmentations were used for rigid registration of follow-up to baseline landmarks for each bone individually in wxRegSurf (software from the Cambridge University Engineering Department, Cambridge UK, free to use for research purposes, https://mi.eng.cam.ac.uk/~ahg/wxRegSurf/). Follow-up to baseline femoral registration was also applied to tibial coordinates to assess for differences in joint positioning between the two timepoints. Baseline to follow-up bone-to-bone rigid registrations were performed, followed by mesh-to-mesh distance analysis (closest vertex method) using a custom script in MATLAB (MathWorks, MA, USA) to evaluate the potential confounding effect of segmentation error on landmark repeatability. Mean closest vertex values for each bone were compared with average mesh edge values as a yardstick for error, with representative 3-D maps of whole-bone averages reviewed to look for any heterogeneity in error across the bone surfaces.

Outcome measures

Landmark repeatability was quantified as the mean absolute error distance ± SD, with a minimum and maximum (all in mm) between baseline and follow-up for each landmark, including for CoGs. Excluding CoGs and F00/T00 (see Fig. 3), the five best and worst landmarks for repeatability error were highlighted for more detailed evaluation. An automated method was developed using a custom MATLAB script to extract knee flexion angle (KFA) and valgus alignment from the landmarking data at each knee. These measures were defined as the angle between the line connecting the extreme scan range landmarks (F00 and T00) and the CoG of the remaining landmarks within the same bone. Valgus alignment was calculated from the anterior view (<180° laterally = valgus), while KFA was measured from the lateral view according to the acquisition frame of reference (<180° as knee flexion). Results were presented as the mean absolute error ± SD, with absolute minimum and maximum values (all in °): therefore, an absolute minimum difference value would be closest to zero and an absolute maximum difference value would be farthest from zero, irrespective of sign. Although this loses directionality from baseline to follow-up data, it provides a more realistic measure of repeatability and avoids a large negative error value being averaged out to zero by a large positive error value. Vertical positioning repeatability error was taken as the absolute unsigned error in the vertical scan range positioning from the extreme F00 and T00 landmarks z-axis co-ordinate only, with results presented as values averaged from both knees and across the F00 and T00 values to deliver a single metric from both knees (in mm). The same MATLAB script was able to provide visual validation of the KFA, valgus alignment and vertical positioning differences from figure plots showing the overlay of the bone segmentations at baseline and follow-up for each participant (Figure S1). A further custom MATLAB script was used to compare KFA and valgus alignment repeatability differences between combined groups of KL grade 0 and 1, KL grade 2, and KL grade 3 and 4, using a linear mixed effects model to account for within-subject correlation between left and right knees. Group differences were evaluated using marginal ANOVA with pairwise comparisons between groups performed using post hoc linear contrasts with Holm correction for multiple testing.

Results

53 participants were approached for inclusion in the study, with 30 declining to participate and 3 of which had total knee arthroplasty. Out of these 20 individuals, 4 did not have repeat imaging and 2 were excluded after imaging on account of imaging artefact. Thus 14 individuals provided satisfactory baseline and follow-up images. These participant demographics were: mean ± SD age 61.3 ± 8.4 years, mean ± SD body mass index (BMI) 30.7 ± 4.3 kg/m², and male: female ratio 8:6. Both knees were included in all analyses (knee N = 28). The median (IQR) interval between baseline and follow-up imaging was 14 (14, 14) days. Breakdown of KL grading was: KL0 = 5; KL1 = 7, KL2 = 6, KL3 = 6, KL4 = 4, showing a range of structural disease across the study cohort.

The error mean, SD, minimum and maximum values for each landmark and the bone CoGs is given in Table 1 along with the landmark code with a verbal description for reference to Fig. 3.

As was expected from the justification of their generation, calculated CoG landmarks were more repeatable than any of the manually placed landmarks due to the averaging effect with mean ± SD repeatability being: FCG (femur) 1.2 ± 0.6 mm; BCG (fibula) 1.2 ± 1.7 mm; PCG (patella) 0.9 ± 0.5 mm; TCG (tibia) 1.3 ± 0.7 mm. Outside of these bone CoGs, the best five mean ± SD repeatability error performance was seen at: the proximal tip of the fibula (B01, 1.3 ± 0.5 mm, min. 0.3 mm, max. 2.5 mm); the central aspect of the fibular articular surface (B04, 1.5 ± 1.5 mm, min. 0.1 mm, max. 6.8 mm); the posterior central patella (P05, 1.4 ± 1.7 mm, min. 0.4 mm, max. 3.1 mm); the anterior inferior patella (P06, 1.4 ± 0.8 mm, min. 0.4 mm, max. 3.6 mm); and the central tibial facet for the fibula (T11, 1.6 ± 0.9 mm, min. 0.4 mm, max. 4.0 mm). These landmarks were notable as being around the proximal tibiofibular joint and patella, smaller bones with more easily defined topology.

Outside of the vertical positioning F00 and T00 landmarks, the worst five mean ± SD repeatability error performance was seen at: the tibial tuberosity (T12, 5.5 ± 4.7 mm, min. 0.8 mm, max. 16.9 mm); the superior medial trochlear margin (F03, 5.2 ± 3.6 mm, min. 0.3 mm, max. 10.7 mm); the central anterior tibial condyle (T01, 4.8 ± 4.7 mm, min. 0.4 mm, max. 20.6 mm); the central posterior tibial condyle (T09, 4.5 ± 3.8 mm, min. 0.8 mm, max. 13.2 mm); and the base of the trochlear groove (F05, 4.4 ± 2.8 mm, min. 0.4 mm, max. 10.9 mm). These landmarks were notable as being on shallow curved surfaces along at least one dimension.

Mean repeatability error for 85% of placed landmarks (28 out of 33, i.e. excluding landmarks at bone ends and CoGs) was in the 1–4 mm range.

The mean ± SD vertical placement error was 24.1 ± 15.1 mm (min. 6.6 mm, max. 59.6 mm), showing that the best repeatability for positioning of the knee in the vertical scanner range is in the sub-centimetre range, with worst performance up to nearly 60 mm, however this individual was a statistical outlier to a x3 SD threshold. The next largest error was 44 mm, itself a borderline outlier, leaving all other vertical placement errors within 30 mm. Discussion

In our landmarking system, less well-defined anatomical landmarks along surfaces with shallow curves in at least one plane (such as the tibial plateau margins, the tibial tuberosity, the base of the trochlear groove, and the superior medial trochlear margin) demonstrated poorest repeatability, with the largest mean repeatability error of around 5 mm. Best landmarking performance was seen at the patella and around the proximal tibiofibular joint with mean repeatability error around 1–2 mm. Centre of gravity repeatability error was understandably the best from the averaging effect with values between 0.9 and 1.3 mm, justifying their use as markers in the evaluation of valgus alignment and KFA.

The analysis of KFA repeatability showed variation from a lowest absolute error value of 0.3° up to a highest value of 15.5°, highlighting the challenge in sustaining repeatable KFA positioning that could impact on the repeatability of quantitative measures such as 3-D joint space width, meniscal extrusion and patellofemoral alignment. However, there was little variation in absolute valgus alignment error ranging from a lowest value of 0.1° to a highest value of 2.1° absolute difference between baseline and follow-up, providing reassurance that this could be a robust and reliable metric for future analyses. Statistical analysis with linear mixed effects analysis showed that there was no significant difference in absolute valgus alignment of KFA repeatability according to radiographic grouping, suggesting that our repeatability results are applicable across the full range of structural disease severity.

We also identified poor repeatability for aligning the knee joint line in the centre of the vertical scan range, varying up to ∼60 mm in vertical difference between scans at worst, but noting that two of the 14 study participants were statistical outliers or on the borderline for being an outlier, the rest with error within 30 mm (Fig. 7). These vertical placement errors suggest there could be repeatability issues for automated analyses that rely on having consistent coverage of the distal femur and proximal tibia, e.g. for shape modelling and bone registrations. However, it is also notable that in random sampling of 15 MOST participants’ knee WBCT images, the absolute error in the medial tibiofemoral joint line placement was 5.7 ± 5.3 mm from the vertical centre of the scan field of view, averaging 2.6 mm below the vertical centre. Our findings are consistent with previous work highlighting the challenges of achieving consistent joint positioning during WBCT [7]. It is of note that poor repeatability was seen for some of these metrics despite the use of a frame-supported protocol. One option to increase accuracy of KFA could be to introduce a digital inclinometer to the setup in addition to the existing laser-guidance system for the Planmed XFI, liaising with scanner manufacturers to consider incorporating this method that has proven to be more reliable than a manual approach [14]. Further augmentation could be considered to improve accuracy of vertical knee joint positioning, for example with manual markup on the skin of the knee joint line from clinical examination immediately prior to imaging in order to match the triaxial laser guidance to positioning once in the scanner.

While these repeatability errors highlight the importance of optimising positioning protocols (that could involve increasing the number of anatomical anchor points for the lower limb and pelvis [15]), our present landmarking system shows potential for further development. In particular, the approach could be adapted into an automated, machine learning–assisted model, such as the AI model outlined by Park et al. 2025 [16]. In addition, it could serve as a basis for developing additional biomechanical measures of value in understanding risks for conditions such as patellar maltracking and trochlear dysplasia using the tibial tubercle–trochlear groove (TT-TG) distance, patella height, and trochlear morphology (Barbosa et al., 2024 [17]. Similarly, these landmarks could be extended to support the development of dynamic models of the knee joint [18], improving the accuracy of biomechanical measures that currently rely mainly on manual techniques [19].

With respect to the limitations of our study, we recognise that we had a small sample size of 14 individuals able to provide valid imaging data of both knees at baseline and follow-up. We understand that this was a convenience sample with no a priori sample size calculation undertaken because no prior study of this type had been performed to be able to provide an estimate of the variance of the primary outcome measure. Thus, quantification of measurement error itself was a goal of this study. Furthermore, our landmarking repeatability could have been influenced by multiple interacting factors, including observer reliability, bone segmentation differences and rigid registration error in addition to patient positioning. We attempted to mitigate these issues by establishing repeat segmentation and registration error (which was at worst an average of 0.3 mm and therefore unlikely to influence landmarking error) and by verifying all landmark placement around the joint using two observers as the most practicable way to reduce error in this process. The use of angulation measures from CoGs as an average of other landmarks with around 1 mm accuracy supported the robustness of the KFA and valgus angulation repeatability metrics. We also recognize that an ideally controlled repeatability study would only have one metric varying between baseline and follow-up to avoid confounding interactions. However, we believe we devised the most practicable evaluation to minimize error from this using the data available, with visual validation of differences in knee joint positioning between baseline and follow-up able to provide reassurance of this (Figure S1).

Finally, while this landmark-based evaluation provides insights into the strengths and weaknesses of knee WBCT repeatability using the Planmed XFI system, it is not only this system that would benefit from ensuring repeatability, and additional work is required to optimise all such prospective positioning protocols. Such improvements will be essential for the reliable evaluation of other parameters such as meniscal extrusion and three-dimensional joint space width (JSW) [5,20] that have shown promise in the evaluation of OA from WBCT as well as providing a robust platform for quantitative analysis of patellofemoral alignment.
