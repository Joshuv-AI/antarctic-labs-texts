Carotid-carotid bypass with novel bilateral extracorporeal carotid shunting: A hybrid open and endovascular technique
Authors: Kareem Siada, Mohammed Dairywala, Maher Megaly, David Clausen, Jimmy Haouilou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarotid-carotid bypass is a revascularization option for symptomatic proximal carotid or innominate artery occlusive disease. We report the case of a 77-year-old woman with innominate in-stent occlusion who sustained an acute right hemispheric stroke during hospitalization. Cerebral angiography demonstrated that the right hemisphere was perfused solely through a circuitous, stenotic vertebrobasilar collateral pathway, with absent anterior crossflow, making carotid clamping immediately catastrophic. A three-way extracorporeal shunting circuit—established under fluoroscopic guidance before carotid clamping—maintained continuous bilateral internal carotid perfusion during simultaneous carotid clamping and carotid-carotid bypass, achieving zero cerebral ischemia time. The patient remained neurologically intact at discharge.Keywords: Carotid-carotid bypass, Extracorporeal carotid shunt, Cerebral ischemia, Innominate occlusion, Hybrid vascular technique, Vertebrobasilar collateral

Carotid-carotid bypass is an extra-anatomic revascularization technique for patients with symptomatic proximal common carotid or innominate artery occlusive disease not amenable to direct endovascular repair or transthoracic reconstruction.1 The procedure tunnels a conduit—most commonly polytetrafluoroethylene or autologous saphenous vein—through the retropharyngeal space with end-to-side anastomoses to each common carotid artery (CCA).1 Published series report 5-year primary patency rates exceeding 90% with combined perioperative stroke and death rates below 5%.2,3

Standard carotid-carotid bypass employs sequential unilateral clamping: the first anastomosis is performed under unilateral carotid occlusion, flow is restored, and the second anastomosis is then completed on the contralateral side. This approach is generally well tolerated because the contralateral internal carotid artery (ICA) remains perfused throughout each clamping interval.2 However, in patients with critically impaired collateral circulation, even unilateral clamping may produce ipsilateral hemispheric ischemia, and conventional intraluminal shunts placed after arteriotomy still require a brief ischemic interval before cerebral protection is established.4,5

We report the case of a patient whose collateral anatomy rendered any carotid clamping immediately catastrophic. Using a hybrid open-endovascular approach, we established a three-way, pressure-driven extracorporeal shunting circuit under fluoroscopic guidance before any carotid clamping. This enabled the operating team to safely clamp both carotid arteries simultaneously—a departure from standard sequential technique—while maintaining uninterrupted bilateral internal carotid perfusion throughout carotid-carotid bypass, achieving zero cerebral ischemia time. Written informed consent for the publication of this report, including operative and imaging details, was obtained from the patient before submission (Video 1, online only).

Case report

A 77-year-old woman with hypertension, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, hyperlipidemia, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation on anticoagulation, right subclavian steal syndrome, and previous right innominate artery stenting with subsequent in-stent stenosis requiring balloon angioplasty presented following a mechanical fall with left femoral and right humeral fractures. She underwent intramedullary nail fixation; on postoperative day 1, she developed acute altered mental status and dysarthria.

Computed tomography perfusion imaging demonstrated a large right hemispheric perfusion mismatch. Cerebral angiography revealed occlusion of the previously stented right innominate artery. Left CCA injection (Fig 1, A) confirmed brisk antegrade flow to the left hemisphere with critically absent anterior crossflow to the right hemisphere. Left vertebral artery injection (Fig 1, B) demonstrated that the right hemisphere was perfused entirely through a circuitous collateral chain: left vertebral artery → basilar artery → retrograde flow through a multisegmented stenotic right vertebral artery → right subclavian artery → right CCA → right middle cerebral artery territory. This stenotic, reverse-flow pathway was the sole source of right hemispheric perfusion.

A, Preoperative cerebral angiogram, lateral projection, left common carotid artery injection. The left internal carotid artery fills with brisk antegrade flow; no crossover perfusion to the right hemisphere is demonstrated, confirming critically absent anterior collateral circulation from left to right. B, Preoperative cerebral angiogram, anteroposterior projection, left vertebral artery injection. Flow ascends through the left vertebral artery to the basilar artery, from which retrograde perfusion traverses a multisegmented stenotic right vertebral artery into the right subclavian artery and right common carotid artery, supplying the right middle cerebral artery territory. This circuitous, high-resistance vertebrobasilar pathway represented the sole source of right hemispheric perfusion and would be eliminated by right common carotid artery clamping.

This anatomy created two independent ischemic hazards: (1) clamping the right CCA would eliminate the terminal outflow of the vertebrobasilar collateral pathway, ending all right hemispheric perfusion; and (2) clamping the left CCA would eliminate the direct aortic inflow to the left hemisphere. Simultaneous bilateral clamping without cerebral protection would produce complete bilateral anterior hemispheric ischemia; even sequential clamping was unsafe, as right CCA clamping alone would immediately eliminate the right hemisphere’s sole perfusion source. The patient underwent evaluation by the neurointerventional service, which attempted to recanalize the occluded innominate artery stent; however, the lesion could not be safely crossed. Given the complex vascular anatomy, severe cerebral hypoperfusion, and perceived risk of further endovascular intervention, open carotid-carotid bypass was selected as the safest and most durable method of cerebral revascularization. Vascular surgery therefore proceeded with a preemptive extracorporeal shunting strategy before any carotid clamping.

With the patient in the supine position, the bilateral neck and upper chest were exposed. Oblique incisions were made anterior to the bilateral sternocleidomastoid muscles. Following platysma division and sternocleidomastoid retraction, the vagus nerve was identified and protected bilaterally without manipulation. Both CCAs were dissected circumferentially from behind the clavicle to several centimeters above the bifurcation. A retropharyngeal tunnel was created with blunt and sharp dissection without injury to the esophagus, trachea, or pharynx; a Penrose drain was placed to guide graft passage. Systemic heparin was administered.

The extracorporeal shunting circuit was established under fluoroscopic guidance before any arterial clamping (Fig 2, A-C). Using standard micropuncture needles and microwires, vascular access was first obtained before upsizing to the final sheath configuration. A 6F sheath (inflow cannula) was inserted retrograde into the proximal left CCA, with the sheath tip positioned at the origin of the left CCA from the aortic arch, using direct aortic pressure as the inflow source. Two 5F sheaths (outflow cannulae) were advanced under wire guidance into the bilateral proximal ICAs, with final placement confirmed by contrast angiography through each sheath. These smaller-caliber sheaths were intentionally selected to minimize the risk of carotid dissection and access-related arterial injury while still providing adequate cerebral perfusion. A 6F inflow sheath was chosen to maximize inflow from the aortic source while maintaining a low-profile access strategy.

The three sheaths were sutured to the drapes to prevent inadvertent migration or dislodgement, and connected via a three-port cardioplegia cannula adapter (LivaNova CA-40010; LivaNova); the circuit was sequentially flushed to eliminate all air, and excellent antegrade ICA perfusion was confirmed fluoroscopically before any vascular clamping.6,7

With confirmed bilateral ICA perfusion, both CCAs were simultaneously occluded with soft, noncrushing vascular clamps, allowing both anastomoses to be constructed without intervening clamp-release cycles. A longitudinal arteriotomy was created in the right CCA; a 7-mm polytetrafluoroethylene graft (Gore-Tex; W. L. Gore & Associates) was anastomosed end-to-side to the right CCA using running 6-0 polypropylene suture. The graft was passed through the retropharyngeal tunnel, trimmed to length, and anastomosed end-to-side to the left CCA. The system was deaired, all vascular clamps were removed, and excellent pulsatile flow was confirmed through the bypass graft.

Before sheath removal, the access sites were allowed to backbleed and flush before the previously placed 5-0 polypropylene U-stitches were secured, minimizing the risk of thrombotic or fibrin debris. The ICA sheaths were then removed with access sites secured by preplaced 5-0 polypropylene U-stitches; the 6F sheath was subsequently removed similarly. Completion angiography (Fig 3) demonstrated brisk bilateral ICA and intracranial perfusion, with the carotid-carotid graft providing robust antegrade flow to the right CCA and right ICA. Protamine reversed anticoagulation. Direct laryngoscopy confirmed bilateral vocal cord mobility. The patient was extubated neurologically intact, moving all extremities and following commands, and was discharged to a subacute rehabilitation facility without new neurological deficits.

Brisk bilateral internal carotid artery and intracranial perfusion is demonstrated, with the carotid-carotid bypass graft providing robust antegrade flow to the right common carotid artery and right internal carotid artery, confirming technical success and uninterrupted cerebral perfusion.

Discussion

We describe a hybrid open-endovascular technique in which fluoroscopically guided extracorporeal carotid shunting established bilateral ICA perfusion before any carotid clamping, enabling safe simultaneous bilateral CCA occlusion during carotid-carotid bypass. The critical insight was that this patient’s collateral anatomy made even sequential unilateral clamping unsafe: right CCA clamping alone would immediately eliminate the right hemisphere’s sole perfusion source, regardless of the left CCA status. The extracorporeal circuit, established before any clamp was placed, resolved this by delivering continuous aortic inflow to both ICAs and additionally permitted simultaneous bilateral clamping throughout reconstruction.

In this patient, alternative endovascular strategies, including attempted recanalization of the occluded innominate artery stent, were unsuccessful because the lesion could not be safely crossed, making open cerebral revascularization the preferred treatment strategy. The most directly comparable previous technique is the ENROUTE transcarotid arterial sheath (Silk Road Medical) used as the outflow limb of a femoral-to-carotid extracorporeal circuit during retrograde innominate stenting, providing antegrade cerebral perfusion during a great vessel intervention.7 In both cases, an endovascular sheath in the CCA maintains ICA perfusion during a supra-aortic intervention. In the ENROUTE-based technique, the femoral artery serves as inflow, requiring groin access and extending the operative field inferiorly. In our technique, the aorta is accessed via a retrograde sheath in the left CCA—a vessel already in the operative field—eliminating groin access entirely. A further distinction is the three-way configuration, which simultaneously perfuses both ICAs from a single aortic inflow, providing bilateral cerebral protection that a femoral-to-unilateral-carotid circuit cannot achieve.

Extracorporeal femoral-carotid shunting with a centrifugal pump for complex supra-aortic reconstruction has also been described by Gosh et al.6 Our passive, pressure-driven circuit eliminates the pump, using native aortic pressure as the sole driving force; the connecting manifold—a cardioplegia cannula adapter (LivaNova CA-40010) applied off-label—is widely available at low cost.

Limitations include the absence of real-time shunt flow quantification, reliance on adequate native aortic perfusion pressure through the retrograde left CCA inflow cannula, and the lack of intraoperative cerebral oximetry or transcranial Doppler monitoring during clamping. Long-term bypass graft patency data are not yet available. Future work should quantify shunt flow rates, evaluate neuromonitoring as a confirmatory adjunct, and define patient selection criteria. This case demonstrates that a fluoroscopically guided, three-way extracorporeal carotid shunting system can enable simultaneous bilateral CCA clamping during carotid-carotid bypass with zero cerebral ischemia time in patients with critically compromised hemispheric collateral circulation.
