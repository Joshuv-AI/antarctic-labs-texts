Egg-derived sphingomyelin induces ferroptosis in cancer cells, depending on intracellular labile iron pool levels
Authors: Chiharu Iwase, Atsushi Nishikawa, Chie Umatani, Yutaka Miura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSphingomyelin (SPM), a sphingolipid abundant in animal cell membranes and animal-derived foods, plays a crucial role in maintaining cell membranes and intracellular signaling. Additionally, although previous studies have suggested that SPM has anticancer effects, the molecular entity underlying its effect on cancer cell viability remains unclear. Moreover, the effects of exogenous SPM on cancer cells have not yet been thoroughly explored. In this study, we investigated the mechanisms underlying SPM-induced cell death in cancer cell lines. For the analyses of cell death, we used five cancer cell lines—COV362, AH109A, HepG2, A549, and Colo201—as cancer models and primary cultured rat hepatocytes and normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDF) as normal cell models. We demonstrated that SPM induces a type of cell death, ferroptosis, in several cancer cell lines, including COV362, AH109A, HepG2, and A549. Furthermore, SPM application increased the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and induced ferroptosis. Since one of the key factors regulating ferroptosis sensitivity is the intracellular labile iron pool (LIP), we examined LIP levels in each cell and found that cells with higher LIP levels were more susceptible to SPM-induced cell death; exogenous iron further enhanced this effect, confirming that LIP plays a crucial role in ferroptosis induction. Our findings demonstrate that SPM can selectively induce ferroptosis in cancer cells through ROS production and, possibly, iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. These findings may provide a potential therapeutic strategy for the selective induction of ferroptosis in cancer cells.Keywords: Cancer, Reactive oxygen species, Cell death, Ferroptosis, Labile iron pool, Sphingomyelin

Introduction

Sphingomyelin (SPM) is an amphiphilic sphingolipid comprising ceramide and phosphocholine. SPM is a major component of eukaryotic cell membranes and is specifically localized in the outer leaflet. Beyond its role as a structural component of membranes, SPM has garnered attention for its potential biological effects, including regulation of cell growth, modulation of immune responses, and influence on lipid metabolism. SPM is abundantly contained in animal-derived foods, such as egg yolk and milk [1]. Previous studies have reported that dietary SPM suppresses the growth of colorectal cancer induced by 1,2-dimethylhydrazine [2,3], which highlights the potential anticancer effects of SPM. SPM also plays important roles in various intracellular signaling pathways. SPM metabolism generates secondary messengers involved in multiple signaling pathways, including apoptosis, cell adhesion, cell differentiation, and cell proliferation. [[4], [5], [6]]. Ceramides from SPM have been reported to act as secondary messengers and cause cell death [7,8]. Although these findings suggest that dietary SPM suppresses cancer cell viability, it remains unclear whether this effect is mediated by SPM itself or its metabolic products, such as ceramides.

It has previously been reported that high concentrations of SPM are associated with the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and induce cell death in cultured neurons with acid sphingomyelinase deficiency [9,10]. Because ROS are the major drivers of ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death, SPM-induced ROS production may trigger ferroptosis. Ferroptosis, first reported in 2012, is a form of iron-dependent regulated cell death driven by the accumulation of lipid peroxides and is distinct from other types of cell death, such as apoptosis and necrosis [[11], [12], [13]]. Although previous studies have demonstrated that exogenous SPM induces ROS production and cell death in cultured abnormal neurons, no studies have investigated the effects of exogenous SPM on cancer cells.

In the present study, we directly applied SPM to various cancer cell lines and normal cells to investigate whether SPM can induce cell death and to explore the involvement of ferroptosis in this process. We used rat ascites hepatoma cells (AH109A), human hepatoma cells (HepG2), human ovarian epithelial-endometrioid cancer cells (COV362), human lung cancer cells (A549), and human colorectal cancer cells (Colo201) as cancer cell models and primary cultured rat hepatocytes and normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDF) as models of normal cells. We specifically investigated the relationship between SPM-induced cell death and intracellular labile iron pool (LIP) levels since ferrous iron (Fe2+) plays a crucial role in the mechanism of ferroptosis. A key factor regulating ferroptosis sensitivity is the LIP [14], which is defined as a low molecular weight pool of weakly chelated iron [15]. LIP is crucial for ferroptosis because it facilitates the Fenton reaction, resulting in the generation of hydroxyl radicals that induce oxidative damage [15,16], a hallmark of ferroptosis. Finally, we investigated whether elevated LIP levels enhanced SPM-induced cell death. We found that cells with high LIP levels were generally more susceptible to SPM-induced ferroptosis, although the correlation was not absolute in all cell lines. These findings provide valuable insights into the potential of SPM as a therapeutic agent for selectively inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells with high LIP levels while minimizing damage to cells with low LIP levels.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Wistar rats were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan). The rats were individually housed under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 2°C, 60 ± 5% humidity) with a 12 h light (8:00–20:00)/dark (20:00–8:00) cycle. The rats were fed a standard diet of chow and provided tap water ad libitum. All the rats were maintained and used in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Experimental Animals of the Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology (R06-104).

Materials

SPM (#860061P) was purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Birmingham, UK). Deferoxamine mesylate (#14595) and ferrostatin-1 (#17729) were purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA) and dissolved in DMSO (#D2650, Sigma-Aldrich Co., St. Louis, MO, USA). Iron (II) sulfate heptahydrate (#094–01082) was purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co. (Osaka, Japan) and dissolved in distilled water.

Preparation of SPM micelles

To obtain the SPM solution, SPM was dissolved in a serum-free medium at a 1 mg/mL concentration, heated at 50°C for 15 min, and then sonicated for 30 min, resulting in emulsion formation.

Primary hepatocyte isolation

Rat hepatocytes were prepared using the collagenase perfusion method, as previously described [17]. The hepatocytes were suspended in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (#05919, Shimadzu Diagnostics, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with 3.5 g/L d(+)-Glucose (#049–31165, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (#173012, Sigma-Aldrich Co., St. Louis, MO, USA), Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution (×100) (#168–23191, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan), 60 mg/L Kanamycin Sulfate (#115–00342, Fujifilm Wako, Osaka, Japan), 1.0–1.4 g/L NaHCO3 (pH 7.1–7.4) (#191–01305, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan) and 600 mg/L l-Glutamine (#078–00525, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 10,000 cells/well and incubated for 4 h in an incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37°C for cell attachment.

Cell culture

AH109A cells were obtained from the Cell Resource Center for Biomedical Research at Tohoku University (Miyagi, Japan). AH109A and COV362 (#EC07071910, KAC, Hyogo, Japan) cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 3.5 g/L d(+)-Glucose, 10% FBS, Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution (×100), 60 mg/L Kanamycin Sulfate, 1.0–1.4 g/L NaHCO3 (pH 7.1–7.4), and 600 mg/L l-Glutamine.

NHDF (#PCS-201-101, ATCCⓇ, Virginia, USA) and HepG2 cells (#RCB1886, RIKEN BRC, Ibaraki, Japan) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution (×100), 1.0–1.4 g/L NaHCO3 (pH 7.1–7.4), and 600 mg/L l-Glutamine.

A549 cells (#EC86012804, KAC, Hyogo, Japan) were cultured in Ham's F–12K medium (#080–08565, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% FBS and Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution (×100).

Colo201 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (#05918, Shimadzu Diagnostics, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with 10% FBS, Penicillin-Streptomycin Solution (×100), 1.0–1.4 g/L NaHCO3 (pH 7.1–7.4), and 600 mg/L l-Glutamine.

All cells were maintained in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured using a WST-8 kit (#343–07623, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Briefly, cells (2,000–5,000 cells/well) were plated in 96-well plates and treated with SPM. After incubating for 48 or 72 h, cells were treated with WST-8 reagent. After 3–4 h at 37°C, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (LB917 Apollo, BERTHOLD, Bad Wildbad, Germany).

ROS measurement

Cells were treated with SPM and incubated for 24 h at 37°C. After incubation, the cells were harvested, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then seeded at 30000 cells/well in 96-well plates. The cells were loaded with 5 μM DCFH-DA (#D399, Invitrogen, Oregon, USA) for 20 min. ROS-dependent fluorescence was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (GloMax® -Multi + Detection System, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at excitation/emission wavelengths of 490/515 nm, respectively.

Measurement of LIP

LIP was measured as described previously [16]. Briefly, cells were loaded with 0.5 μM Calcein-AM (#349–07201, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan), dissolved in DMSO, for 15 min at 37°C. After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with 100 μM deferiprone (#324–65151, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan) for 1 h at 37°C or left untreated. Calcein-dependent fluorescence was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader at excitation/emission wavelengths of 490/515 nm, respectively. LIP levels were calculated from the difference in mean cellular fluorescence with and without deferiprone incubation.

Statistical analysis

Student's t-test was used for the statistical comparison of the two groups. Data were first analyzed for multiple comparisons using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by appropriate tests. After that, Dunnett's test or Tukey–Kramer's multiple comparison test was used. All statistical analyses were performed using Statcel3 software, and each graph was displayed using R Studio (ver. 4.4.2). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 or P < 0.01.

Results

1SPM reduces cancer cell viability in a dose-dependent manner

SPM reduces cancer cell viability in a dose-dependent manner

We investigated the effects of SPM on the proliferation of COV362, AH109A, HepG2, A549, and Colo201 as tumor models. As shown in Fig. 1A, cell viability after 48 h of treatment with 80 μg/mL of SPM decreased to approximately 56% in COV362 cells, 32% in AH109A, 70% in HepG2, and 2% in A549 cells. In contrast, SPM did not significantly affect the viability of Colo201 (Fig. 1A). To further examine whether SPM exerts similar cytotoxic effects on normal cell lines, we examined its impact on rat hepatocytes and NHDF. As shown in Fig. 1B, the treatment with 80 μg/mL of SPM for 48 h did not cause significant changes in rat hepatocytes and NHDF cells (Fig. 1B).

SPM induces cell death in cancer cell lines except for Colo201. (A) Cell viability of COV362, AH109A, HepG2, A549, and Colo201 treated with 80 μg/mL SPM for 48 h. Data are presented as mean ± SEM of n = 8 for each group. ∗∗ on the graph indicates a statistically significant difference using Student's t-test. (P < 0.01) (B) Cell viability of hepatocytes and NHDF cells treated with 80 μg/mL SPM for 48 h. Data are presented as mean ± SEM of n = 8 for each group. (C–F) Cell viability of COV362 (C), AH109A (D), HepG2 (E), and A549 (F) cells treated with 40 or 80 μg/mL SPM for 48 h. All data are presented as mean ± SEM of n = 8 for each group. ∗∗ on the graph indicates the statistically significant difference compared with the control using Dunnett's test. (P < 0.01).

Next, we treated COV362 (Fig. 1C), AH109A (Fig. 1D), HepG2 (Fig. 1E), and A549 (Fig. 1F) with 40 or 80 μg/mL of SPM for 48 h to analyze dose-dependency. These results demonstrated that SPM induced a dose-dependent decrease in the viability of these cell lines. Furthermore, the effectiveness of SPM varied across cell lines, with the most significant effect observed in lung cancer cells (Fig. 1C–F). These results suggested that SPM does not affect the viability of normal cells such as hepatocytes and NHDF, while it significantly inhibits the viability of cancer cell lines except the colorectal cancer cell line Colo201.

2SPM treatment leads to ROS accumulation and ferroptosis

SPM treatment leads to ROS accumulation and ferroptosis

A previous study has reported that SPM induces oxidative stress and triggers cell death in cultured neuronal cells [9]. To investigate the effect of SPM on intracellular ROS levels, we measured ROS levels in COV362, AH109A, HepG2, and A549 cells 24 h after SPM treatment with 2′, 7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). The exogenously added SPM significantly increased the intracellular ROS levels in all of these cell lines (Fig. 2A).

Ferroptosis inhibitors suppress cell death induced by SPM. (A) Relative ROS levels measured using DCFH-DA in COV362, AH109A, HepG2, and A549 cells treated with 80 μg/mL SPM for 24 h. Data are presented as mean ± SEM of n = 8 for each group. ∗∗ on the graph indicates a statistically significant difference using Student's t-test. (P < 0.01) (B) Cell viability of COV362 cells co-treated with 80 μg/mL SPM and 70 μM DFO or 50 μM Fer-1 for 48 h. (C) Cell viability of AH109A cells co-treated with 40 μg/mL SPM and 1 μM DFO or 5 μM Fer-1 for 48 h. (D) Cell viability of HepG2 cells co-treated with 40 μg/mL SPM and 1 μM DFO or 20 μM Fer-1 for 72 h. (E) Cell viability of A549 cells co-treated with 80 μg/mL SPM and 1 μM DFO or 5 μM Fer-1 for 48 h. (B–E) All data are presented as mean ± SEM of n = 8 for each group. Different alphabets indicate the statistical difference using Tukey-Kramer's multiple comparison test. (P < 0.05).

Recently, ferroptosis has been reported as a form of cell death associated with increased ROS levels, which occurs because of the accumulation of lipid peroxides in an iron-dependent manner [11,12]. To examine whether SPM-induced cell death was ferroptosis, we co-treated cells with SPM and the ferroptosis inhibitors, deferoxamine (DFO) or ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1). As shown in Fig. 2B–D, SPM-induced cell death was almost entirely diminished in the presence of Fer-1, an inhibitor of lipid peroxidation, across all tested SPM-sensitive cell lines. DFO, which chelates iron, also tended to suppress SPM-induced cell death. On the other hand, its efficacy varied considerably among the cell lines. While DFO significantly suppressed cell death in COV362 and HepG2 cells, this effect was not statistically significant in AH109A and A549 cells (Fig. 2B–D). These findings suggest that SPM increases intracellular ROS levels and induces ferroptosis, with the degree of iron dependency showing cell-type-specific differences.

3High LIP levels are required but not sufficient for SPM-induced ferroptosis

High LIP levels are required but not sufficient for SPM-induced ferroptosis

As described above, SPM decreased in cell viability in certain cancer cell lines (Fig. 1A), and cell death was suppressed by the iron chelator DFO (Fig. 2B–E). Thus, it is possible that the intracellular iron ion levels in each cell affected cell viability in the medium containing SPM. To evaluate the dependence of SPM-induced cell death on the amount of free intracellular iron, we measured LIP levels in each cell line. LIP is an intracellular pool of iron that is chelatable and has redox activity. It generates oxygen radicals via the Fenton reaction, resulting in oxidative cell damage [16].

Using the Calcein-AM method (see Methods section), we found that high LIP levels were observed in COV362, AH109A, HepG2, and A549 cells, in which cell death was induced by SPM, whereas low LIP levels were observed in NHDF and Colo201 cells, in which cell death was not induced (Fig. 3A). A549 cells showed the lowest LIP levels among the four cell lines in which cell death occurred, although SPM exhibited the strongest effect (Fig. 3B). Considering this result for A549 cells, it can be inferred that the strength of the SPM effect does not necessarily correspond to the LIP levels. Taken together, these results suggest that sensitivity to SPM is not solely determined by LIP levels, while high LIP levels are required for SPM-induced cell death.

SPM-induced cell death is regulated by intracellular LIP levels. (A) Relative LIP levels were measured using the Calcein-AM method. The difference in the mean cellular fluorescence with and without deferiprone incubation reflects the amount of LIP. Data are presented as mean ± SEM of n = 6 for each group. Different alphabets indicate the statistical difference using Tukey–Kramer's multiple comparison test. (P < 0.05) (B) Relationship between relative LIP levels and cell viability. Each symbol represents a different cell line: COV (■), AH109A (▲), HepG2 (◆), A549 (●), NHDF (○), and Colo201 (△).

To further examine the involvement of LIP in SPM-induced cell death, we determined whether increased intracellular LIP levels augmented the effects of SPM. We loaded 0.2 μM iron (II) sulfate heptahydrate into AH109A cells to elevate intracellular LIP levels. The measurement of LIP levels at each time point after iron treatment showed a significant increase in LIP levels after 24 h (Fig. 4A), which is consistent with previous reports indicating that LIP levels increase upon iron loading in HepG2 cells [18,19].

Increasing LIP levels can enhance cell death. (A) LIP levels were measured at 0, 6, 24, and 48 h after treatment with 0.2 μM Fe (II) in AH109A cells. Data are presented as mean ± SEM of n = 6 for each group. ∗∗ on the graph indicates the statistically significant difference compared to the control using Dunnett's test. (P < 0.01) (B) Cell viability of AH109A cells co-treated with 40 μg/mL SPM and 0.2 μM Fe (II) for 48 h. Data are presented as mean ± SEM of n = 6 for each group. Different alphabets indicate the statistical difference using Tukey–Kramer's multiple comparison test. (P < 0.05) (C) Cell viability of HepG2 cells co-treated with 80 μg/mL SPM and 0.25 μM Fe (II) for 72 h. Data are presented as mean ± SEM of n = 6 for each group. Different alphabets indicate the statistical difference using Tukey–Kramer's multiple comparison test. (P < 0.05).

Finally, we measured the cell viability after treating SPM with AH109A and HepG2 cells loaded with iron. The results revealed that the groups co-treated with SPM and iron exhibited increased cell death compared with the groups treated with SPM alone (Fig. 4B and C). These results demonstrate that SPM-induced cell death depends on intracellular LIP levels.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that SPM induced cell death in several cancer cell lines, including COV362, AH109A, HepG2, and A549, with minimal effects on hepatocytes, NHDF, and Colo201 (Fig. 1). These results suggest that SPM may induce cell death in some cancer cells. Additionally, our results suggest that SPM-induced cell death is mediated by ferroptosis (Fig. 2). Furthermore, we demonstrated that intracellular LIP levels were a key factor in SPM-induced ferroptosis (Fig. 3). Finally, elevated LIP levels induced by exogenous iron enhanced SPM-induced cell death (Fig. 4), supporting the conclusion that LIP is critical for SPM-induced ferroptosis. These findings suggest that SPM-induced ferroptosis is closely linked to intracellular iron homeostasis and oxidative stress.

Our findings are consistent with previous reports showing that SPM can induce oxidative stress [9,10]. These studies have demonstrated that SPM increases ROS levels, leading to apoptotic cell death in neurons. Although the cell death pathway may vary depending on cell type, our findings provide further evidence that SPM induces ferroptosis in multiple cancer cell lines. Ferroptosis is characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [11], and our data suggest that SPM promotes oxidative stress by increasing ROS levels. The inhibition of SPM-induced cell death by DFO and Fer-1 indicates that iron-dependent lipid peroxidation is the primary mechanism underlying the observed cytotoxicity.

While lipid peroxidation is a hallmark of ferroptosis, the precise role of SPM in this process remains unclear. Previous studies have reported that ferroptosis can be induced by polyunsaturated fatty acids with multiple double bonds, such as arachidonic acid [20] and docosahexaenoic acid [21], as well as by phospholipids composed of polyunsaturated fatty acids [22]. SPM is a phospholipid composed of ceramide and phosphocholine, and ceramide can have varying carbon chain lengths, some of which may contain unsaturated bonds that are susceptible to peroxidation. However, the SPM used in this study primarily consists of ceramides with saturated carbon chains, suggesting that SPM is unlikely to undergo peroxidation and directly induce ferroptosis. This indicates that SPM peroxides are not responsible for triggering ferroptosis; instead, SPM induces ferroptosis via a distinct mechanism.

Our findings reveal that SPM-induced cell death exhibits cell-type specificity and differential sensitivity to ferroptosis inhibitors. While Fer-1 consistently suppressed SPM-induced cell death in all sensitive cell lines, the iron chelator DFO significantly inhibited cell death in COV362 and HepG2 cells but had minimal effect in AH109A and A549 cells (Fig. 2B–D). These results indicate that although iron-dependent lipid peroxidation is a key mechanism, the extent to which ferroptosis depends on intracellular iron may vary between cell types. In particular, ferroptosis sensitivity is known to be regulated by factors other than iron availability, such as glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [23,24], ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) [25,26], and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) [27,28]. Given that A549 cells exhibited weak responses to DFO treatment, their ferroptosis sensitivity may be more dependent on alternative pathways of these or other relevant regulators rather than LIP levels alone. Therefore, further investigation into how SPM modulates core ferroptosis regulators could help clarify the molecular basis for differential SPM sensitivity.

Furthermore, although high LIP levels were necessary for SPM-induced cell death, sensitivity to SPM did not strictly correlate with LIP levels in all cancer cell lines. For example, A549 cells, which exhibited the strongest response to SPM, had lower LIP levels than other SPM-sensitive cell lines such as COV362, AH109A, and HepG2 cells (Fig. 3B). This implies that other cell-specific factors modulate sensitivity to SPM. Such factors may include antioxidant capacity, membrane lipid composition, or the expression and activity of ferroptosis regulators.

Another significant aspect of cell-type-specific responses is the observed resistance of the human colorectal cancer cell line Colo201 to exogenous SPM, which contrasts with previous reports that dietary SPM suppresses tumor growth, particularly in colorectal cancer models [2,3]. This discrepancy may be due to differences in SPM metabolism. Dietary SPM is not absorbed as an intact molecule but is instead hydrolyzed into ceramide and phosphocholine, which are subsequently absorbed. Therefore, the anticancer effects observed in dietary SPM studies may be mediated by metabolic products rather than the SPM itself. Indeed, previous studies have shown that the oral administration of ceramide analogs suppresses tumor growth [29], and the addition of ceramide and sphingosine to colorectal cancer cells reduces cell viability [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Therefore, the absence of anticancer effects in our study may be explained by the fact that, unlike dietary SPM, the SPM used in our experiments was not metabolized into ceramide, which is known to exert cytotoxic effects on colorectal cancer cells. The micellized SPM used in this study was likely taken up directly by cells without prior metabolism, suggesting that its effects may be due to the SPM itself or to its intracellular metabolites. Further studies, which distinguish between the effects of SPM itself and its metabolic products, will help to understand the specific molecular pathways underlying SPM-induced ferroptosis.

In our study, SPM selectively induced cell death in some cancer cell lines but not in hepatocytes, NHDF, and Colo201. Understanding the determinants of this selectivity, such as lipid composition, ferroptosis-related enzymes, and metabolic pathways, will be critical for evaluating the potential of SPM as an anticancer agent. This raises the possibility that SPM could serve as a therapeutic agent for selectively targeting ferroptosis in specific cancer cells, thereby offering a strategy to induce cell death in cancer cells while sparing normal tissues. While our study provides compelling in vitro evidence that exogenous SPM induces ferroptosis in certain cancer cell lines, future studies using in vivo models, such as pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and toxicity of SPM in animal models, will help us to evaluate the efficacy and safety of SPM as a potential ferroptosis-inducing agent.

In summary, this is the first study to demonstrate that exogenous SPM induces ferroptosis in cancer cell lines by increasing ROS levels and leveraging intracellular LIP. These findings suggest a potential therapeutic strategy for the selective induction of ferroptosis in cancer cells. Elucidating the molecular pathways involved and identifying biomarkers for SPM sensitivity and the effectiveness of SPM in animal models will also be key steps toward its potential as a ferroptosis-inducing agent in cancer therapy.
