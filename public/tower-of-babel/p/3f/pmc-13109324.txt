UAV-LiDAR high-throughput time-series phenotyping and genome-wide association analysis reveal the genetic basis of plant height in peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.)
Authors: Jiangtao Tan, Shiyuan Liu, Guowei Li, Weiguang Yang, Minmin Liang, Xi Li, Yifei Chen, Huanxin Zou, Baixiang Wang, Zhi Pan, Jianguo Wang, Yubin Lan, Tingting Chen, Lei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plant height (PH) is closely linked to yield potential, lodging resistance, and mechanized harvesting efficiency in peanut cultivation. However, breeding efforts for optimized PH are hindered by limited understanding of its genetic architecture. In this study, we utilized a UAV-based high-throughput phenotyping platform to monitor the dynamic growth of 241 peanut accessions across four trials. Using UAV-LiDAR data, we precisely measured time-series PH and applied Gaussian fitting and principal component analysis (PCA) to extract five dynamic growth parameters: parameter a (maximum plant height), b (time to reach maximum height), c (variation extent of PH), PC1 (interpreted as average height), and PC2 (growth rate). Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) identified 1133 candidate genes associated with parameters a, b, c, PC1 and PC2, and differential expression of genes (DEGs) analysis combined with weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) further identified Arahy.1026BX as a candidate gene. This gene is involved in the shikimate pathway and is crucial for the synthesis of auxin and lignin. Reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) and virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) experiments validated the significant effect of Arahy.1026BX on peanut PH. Overall, our study integrates advanced UAV-LiDAR time-series phenotyping with genome-wide association study to identify potential candidate genes associated with PH, which providing valuable breeding insights for developing peanut varieties with ideal PH and improving peanut yield.

Introduction

Peanut (Arachis hypogaea L.) is a major oil crop widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical areas [1]. The significance of peanut as a plant-based protein source has had a profound impact on global food security [2]. The search for superior phenotypes of the peanut plant has been ongoing, with the aim of improving yield. A reduction in height has been linked to enhanced yield and yield stability in numerous crop varieties [3]. The breeding of crops including wheat [3], rice [4], maize [5], and oilseed rape [6,7] with semi-dwarf genes has resulted in not only a significant reduction in PH but also a substantial increase in production potential and resistance to downfall. In China, peanuts serve as a vital source of vegetable oil for the population and a significant cash crop for enhancing farmers' income and alleviating poverty [8]. As the majority of cultivated peanut varieties are upright, the application of growth inhibitors such as polyconazole and aliconazole is typically required 2–3 times throughout the growing season. This not only increases production costs but also results in environmental pollution. Thus, investigating the molecular mechanisms that regulate peanut plant height and breeding semi-dwarf peanut cultivars becomes crucial. Nevertheless, the genetic basis of peanut PH remains elusive. In recent years, although some studies have been conducted to identify PH quantitative trait loci (QTLs) in peanut [[9], [10], [11]], no genes closely related to PH have been cloned in peanut to date. Accordingly, the development of more appropriate PH based on an enhanced understanding of the genetic basis of peanut PH has become a pressing necessity in order to ensure global food security.

In recent years, the advent of genomics has significantly improved the efficiency of plant breeding [12,13]. Despite the advent of sequencing technology, which has provided an almost unlimited number of high-density genetic markers, traditional phenotyping methods have become a significant bottleneck in modern crop breeding. This is due to the labor-demanding, time-intensive, expensive, and low-efficiency nature of these methods [14]. This has significantly constrained our capacity to comprehensively elucidate the genetic underpinnings of large-scale, rapid, and precise phenotypes [[15], [16], [17]]. The advent of robotics and imaging analysis has opened new avenues for high-throughput screening, with drone technology playing a pivotal role in crop PH monitoring [18,19]. Among these, UAV-mounted LiDAR provides high-resolution 3D crop canopy information and accurately measures PH, offering extremely superior accuracy and productivity for crop growth monitoring, which has been widely used in agricultural research [[20], [21], [22]]. Combining phenomics and genomics has further facilitated the identification of candidate genes in crops [23,24]. Phenomics has been extensively utilized in the phenotypic analysis of peanut in the field, with a primary focus on the phenotypic monitoring of seedling emergence [25], chlorophyll content [26], leaf area index [27], disease [28] and yield [29].

Plant growth is a dynamic, complex trait regulated by multiple genetic/QTLs throughout the plant life cycle [30]. Conventional genetic investigations tend to concentrate on static phenotypic analyses, neglecting the dynamic characterization of phenotypes in time series. This somewhat constrains our comprehension of the intricacies of genetic mechanisms [31]. The use of time series data allows for the more effective identification of QTLs and provides greater insight into the phenotypic impact of specific alleles at particular loci [32]. UAVs enable the collection of time-series phenotypic data, thereby facilitating a more comprehensive analysis of phenotypic dynamics. Li et al. [33]conducted a UAV-mounted RGB camera to obtain time-series data on canopy cover (CC) for a large-scale soybean accession. This approach led to the identification of 35 loci associated with CC dynamics through GWAS. In addition, 10 loci were associated with known QTLs for flowering time and PH. Ye et al. [34]monitored PH time series data of land cotton by RGB camera, identified key loci regulating PH, and revealed novel genetic mechanisms underlying PH in upland cotton. Ding et al. [35] utilized the combination of UAV technology and GWAS for genomic analysis of nitrogen response and variety classification in wheat. This involved the use of dynamic phenotyping and machine learning modelling. Yu et al. [36]monitored the senescence dynamics of 565 wheat accessions using spectral indices. This approach enabled the discovery of genetic loci and candidate genes linked to greening retention traits. Nevertheless, there is a paucity of research on the utilization of UAVs for peanut time-series field phenotyping. Furthermore, few studies have been conducted on UAV time-series phenotyping data with GWAS for peanut genetic analysis.

In this study, an UAV carrying a LiDAR system was utilized to obtain field point cloud data of 241 peanut accessions cultivated in four trials. The point cloud data, obtained approximately 40 days after sowing (DAS) and extending to the pre-harvest period, was processed to generate digital elevation model (CHM) images. This allowed the time-series PH data for peanuts to be obtained. Subsequently, 5 indicators related to PH (parameter a, b, c, PC1 and PC2) were integrated using Gaussian fitting and PCA for GWAS, resulting in the mining of a total of 1133 candidate genes, followed by enrichment analysis on them. Furthermore, the target gene Arahy.1026BX, which was significantly associated with PH, was identified and subsequently validated through the integration of DEGs analysis and WGCNA of publicly available transcriptome data, in addition to haplotype analysis. By integrating UAV-based time-series phenotyping with genetic analysis, this study establishes a theoretical foundation and identifies potential candidate genes for peanut breeding, which can improve the development of peanut varieties with optimal PH, thereby enhancing the stability and yield of peanut production.

Materials and methods

Plant accessions and field trials

The study employed a natural population comprising 241 peanut germplasm resource accessions, as detailed in Table S1. All accessions were deposited at South China Agricultural University (SCAU) and were manually planted in the early (2023E, sown on March 10) and late (2023L, sown on August 10) seasons of 2023 ​at the Zengcheng Experimental Farm of SCAU, located in Guangzhou, Guangdong, China (23.1N, 113.4E) (Fig. S1). The seeds were sown on 10 March and August 10 and harvested at DAS116 and DAS102, respectively. All field trials followed a randomized complete block design, with four replications established (2023E1, 2023E2, 2023L1 and 2023L2). Each resource was planted in three rows, with six plants per row, with a plant spacing of 20 ​cm and a row spacing of 50 ​cm. Field management followed conventional peanut production protocols, and all 241 accessions successfully completed their full life cycle in each trial.

UAV-based LiDAR data acquisition and field data collection

The DJI M300 RTK UAV, carrying the DJI ZENMUSE L1 (DJI L1) LiDAR system, is utilized for the collection of point cloud data, while the aerial image acquired by its mapping camera serves as an auxiliary plot segmentation. The DJI L1 system integrates a Livox LiDAR module coupled with high-accuracy inertial guidance, aerial mapping cameras, and a three-axis stabilization platform. It is capable of capturing high-quality real-time 3D data under various weather conditions, thanks to its strong anti-magnetic interference capabilities and accurate positioning capabilities. Data collection takes place during sunny noon or during cloudy and breezy daytime periods to ensure optimal point cloud quality. The UAV maintains a flying altitude of 15 ​m and moves at a speed of 1 ​m per second, with a sampling rate of 240 ​kHz. To ensure proper slicing adjustment, the laser point cloud overlap ratio is set to 10 ​%. The point cloud data were collected utilizing a double-pulse scanning mode, with a point cloud density of 9428 points per square meter. The specific hardware parameters are detailed in Table S2.

In the 2023E and 2023L trials, the collection of UAV LiDAR data commenced at approximately DAS40 and continued until the point of harvest. The time interval between flights was between 4 and 21 days, with data acquired on 9 and 7 occasions, respectively (Table S3). On the day of several of these UAV flights, PH measurements were manually obtained using a ruler, recording vertical distances from ground level to canopy apex. Manual measurements were taken at the sample plots, with three to five peanut plants measured and averaged for each plot. A total of 429 and 126 randomly selected sample plots in 2023E (DAS49 and DAS67) and 2023L (DAS41 and DAS88) were used to measure PH.

The processing of LiDAR point cloud data and the extraction of PH

The point cloud data processing was divided into two stages: (i) point cloud data stitching and (ii) pre-processing. The stitching was conducted using the DJI Terra software (version 4.0.10) (Fig. 1a). The software automatically reads the point cloud data, inertial navigation logs, tracking files and other associated information, performs a Position and Orientation System (POS) analysis, adds color to the point cloud and performs stitching, and then generates a Laser Scanning (LAS) point cloud file. To circumvent the potential impact of point cloud noise in the LAS file on the subsequent PH extraction, we employed the LiDAR360 software (GreenValley International, Berkeley, CA, USA, V5.2.2.0) to execute a series of de-noising, smoothing, and other preprocessing operations (Fig. 1b), which proved indispensable for accurate PH extraction [37].Fig. 1Workflow for Extracting Peanut PH Using UAV-based LiDAR. To extract the PH, the preprocessed LAS data was obtained and the crop targets were identified with the assistance of a computer vision algorithms, which enabled precise segmentation of vegetation from soil background. Given that point cloud data have red, green and blue bands and that the green to red ratio vegetation index (G/R) provides a rapid, straightforward and precise estimate of crop growth [38], the G/R threshold was set to 0.99 for the segmentation of plants from the ground (Fig. 1c). Subsequently, the digital surface model (DSM) of the study area and the DSM of the ground points were generated in the LiDAR360 software (Fig. 1d). The difference between the two was then used to obtain the canopy height model (CHM) of peanut plants with a grid resolution of 0.02 ​m ​× ​0.02 ​m, which was subsequently used for PH extraction.

In order to extract the PH value of each plot, polygons were digitized on the aerial orthophoto acquired by the mapping camera on the QGIS software (version 3.28.13), and shape files were exported. The CHM of each plot was then segmented in combination with Python (version 3.8). The 95th to 98th percentile of the largest is typically selected as the apex of the main stem heigh [39]. In this study, the average of the 95th-100th percentile of the vegetation CHM was chosen as the PH value (Fig. 1e).

The quality assessment and statistical analysis of PH based on UAV LiDAR

In order to ascertain the consistency between the PH values recorded by the UAV and those obtained manually, a linear regression model was employed for the analysis. To evaluate the precision of PH measurement by the UAV, three metrics were utilized: R2, RMSE and NRMSE. R2 is a measure of the proportion of variance in the model's explanatory variables, with values closer to 1 indicating a superior fit. RMSE reflects the average discrepancy between the predicted and actual values, with smaller values indicating greater predictive accuracy. NRMSE is a metric that facilitates comparisons across datasets by standardizing the RMSE to be independent of the range of relative values. The formulas for these three parameters are provided below:R2=1−∑i=1n(xi−yi)2/∑i=1n(xi−x¯)2,RMSE=∑i=1n(yi−xi)2/n,NRMSE=RMSE/(ymax−ymin),where xi is the PH value obtained by manual measurement, x¯ is the average measured PH value, yi is the CHM extracted PH value, ymax is the CHM extracted PH maximum value, ymin is the CHM extracted PH minimum value, and n indicates the number of data samples.

To determine whether genetic diversity underlies the phenotypic variation in PH, we estimated the broad-sense heritability (H2) of PH at all time points of the trials in 2023E and 2023L. The following formulae were used:H2=δg2(δg2+δε2L),where L is the number of trials, and δg2 and δε2 indicate the genotypic and error variances, respectively. The variance components (δg2 and δε2) were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) in a linear mixed-effects model with genotype as a random effect and trial environment as a fixed effect, implemented in the lme4 package (v1.1.35.5) in R.

Extraction of phenotype-related metrics based on dynamic trait analysis and PCA analysis

Given that dynamic or longitudinal phenotypes offer more detailed insights into plant-environment interactions [40], we inferred dynamic phenotypic traits from the static phenotypic datasets gathered across various growth phases. This approach allowed us to avoid relying solely on arbitrary time points for scoring growth patterns. Inspired by previous research [41], we opted to quantify dynamic phenotypes by fitting curves, despite the potential absence of some phenotypic data points. Given that the height of peanut plants tends to decrease in the later stages of growth, a variety of fitting functions were tested, including linear and logarithmic functions. Ultimately, a Gaussian function was chosen to fit the PH variation [35,42]. Based on the Gaussian fitting function, three key parameters were extracted: a, b, and c, which were then estimated through nonlinear least-squares fitting using the ‘curve_fit’ function from Python's SciPy library (v1.8.0) to characterize PH variation. Subsequently, PCA was conducted on the PH measured by the LiDAR system using the built-in function “prcomp” in R 4.3.2 [34] to decompose the differences between the fitted curves. The first two PCs explained approximately 90 ​% of the variance in the four trials and were utilized for trait analysis in the GWAS. The following formulae was used:Gaussian(x)=a∗e−(x−b)22c2,where a is the amplitude of the Gaussian curve, indicating the maximum height of the plant, b is the center of the curve, indicating the point in time when the plant reaches its maximum height, and c is the width of the curve, reflecting the duration and growth rate of the plant.

GWAS and prediction of candidate genes

In a previous study [43], 241 peanut accessions were genetically characterized, with subsequent quality control, genomic variation, investigation of population architecture, and evaluation of linkage disequilibrium (LD). The GWAS analyses were performed using the IIIVmrMLM method, which was performed with the R package “IIIVmrMLM” downloaded from the GitHub repository (https://github.com/YuanmingZhang65/IIIVmrMLM) [44,45]. The single-environment module (Single_env) was employed to identify QTNs of the 5 indicators (parameter a, b, c, PC1, and PC2) associated with PH in each trial. In contrast, the multi-environment module (Multi_env) was utilized to detect the QTNs (Qs) and QEIs of the aforementioned indicators. The threshold for statistical significance for QTNs and QEIs was set at 0.05/m ​= ​2.0 ​× ​10−8, where m represents the number of markers (2,110,659). Furthermore, to avoid the exclusion of potential candidate genes, QTNs and QEIs with a LOD score of 3 or higher but not reaching statistical significance were considered as suggested QTNs and QEIs. In the aforementioned analyses, the Q matrix, which pertains to population structure, and the K matrix, which Covariates related to individual relatedness were employed in the analyses, with the Q matrix, representing 5 genetic clusters identified in previous study [43], computed using the STRUCTURE software (version 2.3.4), and the K matrix calculated automatically by the IIIVmrMLM software.

Similarly, genotype data were assembled and annotated based on the peanut reference genome (arahy.Tifrunner.gnm1.KYV3), which demonstrated an LD decay distance of approximately 50 ​kb (r2 ​= ​0.2) [43]. The candidate genes were researched from the PeanutBase database website (https://dev.peanutbase.org/) and annotated using the aforementioned peanut reference genome. Candidate gene regions were identified as sequences located within 50 ​kb upstream and downstream of the obtained QTNs and QEIs. To better understand the predicted genes' potential functions of the predicted genes in plant growth and development, we combined the annotation files with GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses using TBtools-II software [46].

Differential expression of genes analysis and weighted correlation network analysis

In this study, transcriptome data (SRP287054) (Table S4) of peanut stems with contrasting heights were downloaded from the NCBI public database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.gov/geo/) for differential expression of genes (DEGs) and gene co-expression network analyses (WGCNA) to support candidate gene identification. DEGs analysis was conducted to identify genes that are significantly up- or downregulated between tall and dwarf phenotypes, while WGCNA was applied to detect gene modules correlated with plant height. These complementary approaches enhanced the prioritization of candidate genes identified through GWAS.

To analyze the DEGs, the raw data were initially quality assessment with FastQC (v0.12.1) [47], followed by data filtering with Trim Galore (v0.6.10) [48]. Subsequently, a reference genome index was constructed and sequence comparison was performed using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) [49]. Transcript assembly and merging were conducted using StringTie (v2.2.1) [50], and the data were subsequently analyzed quantitatively based on FPKM values. All of the aforementioned operations were conducted on a Linux system. Ultimately, the data were analyzed using the “DESeq2” (v1.44.0) [51] package in R (v4.3.2), applying thresholds of adjusted P-value ≤0.05 and |log2FC|≥1 for significance [52].

The WGCNA was conducted using the R plug-in “WGCNA shiny” for TBtools-II [53,54]. First, the gene count data, which were subjected to a Variance Stabilizing transformation (VST) for normalization, were entered into an expression matrix and the correlation between genes was calculated using Pearson's coefficient to determine the similarity of co-expression. Subsequently, the β-value of the neighbor matrix weighting parameter was selected to align with the criteria for a scale-free network distribution. A topological overlap matrix (TOM) was generated to perform module clustering. Finally, phenotype-module associations were then evaluated to pinpoint biologically relevant network modules.

RNA extraction, cDNA preparation and RT-qPCR analyses

For quantification of target gene expression, six peanut accessions exhibiting significant differences in PH were selected, including three short-stature accessions (CX417, CX432, and CX436) and three tall-stature accessions (DL089, DL098, and DL100). Total RNA extraction was performed on all samples employing the FastPure Universal Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (RC401-01, Vazyme, https://www.vazymebiotech.com/, Nanjing, China). Reverse transcription reactions were conducted employing HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR Kit (R323-01, Vazyme). RT-qPCR was performed on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix Kit (Q711-02, Vazyme). The following reaction program was employed: pre-denaturation at 95 ​°C for 30 ​s, then 40 cycles of 95 ​°C for 10 ​s (denaturation) and 60 ​°C for 30 ​s (annealing), and finally analysis by the instrument's default melting curve program. The relative expression levels of the genes in the three biological replicates were quantified by the 2-(ΔΔCt) method [55] and normalized with the internal reference gene Actin (LOC112783800, Arachis hypogaea actin-7) [56]. All primer sequences are provided in Table S5.

VIGS of Arahy.1026BX

The impact of Arahy.1026BX gene silencing was examined using two distinct VIGS systems: the tobacco rattle virus (TRV) VIGS system [57] and the peanut whole plant VIGS system [58]. In summary, the partial coding sequence (CDS, 307 bp) of the Arahy.1026BX gene was amplified from cDNA and inserted into the pTRV2 vector to produce the pTRV2:1026BX plasmid. Subsequently, the pTRV1, pTRV2, and pTRV2:1026BX vectors were introduced into A. tumefaciens GV3101 competent cells via freeze-thaw transformation, as in previous study [59]. The transformed Agrobacterium cells were cultured overnight at 28 ​°C in LB medium containing 50 ​mg/L kanamycin (Kana) and 25 ​mg/L rifampicin (Rif). Following the collection of the cells, they were suspended in an infiltration solution comprising 10 ​mmol/L 2-(N-morpholinyl) ethanesulfonic acid (MES), 150 ​μmol/L acetosyringone (AS), and 10 ​mmol/L MgCl2. Following transformation, equal volumes of Agrobacterium cultures harboring pTRV1 (for auxotransformation) and pTRV2-derived vectors were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and incubated for 3–5 ​h at 24–28 ​°C in the absence of light. Subsequently, the combined Agrobacterium suspension was introduced into the leaf and main stem growth regions of peanut seedlings. The injected seedlings were kept in the dark for 24 ​h, then cultivated at 28 ​°C with a 16-h light and 8-h dark regime. At 14 days post-infection, plants carrying pTRV1+pTRV21026:BX and pTRV1+pTRV2 (negative control) were subjected to phenotypic observations. Three biological replicates were used per treatment group. For each replicate, the main stem height was measured from the base to the apex using a ruler; leaf area was measured using a flatbed scanner (ScanMaker i800plus, Microtek, China); and the angle of the first pair of lateral branches was measured with a protractor. The main stem internodes were then excised, cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 ​°C until subsequent experiments. The primers utilized for vector assembly are presented in Table S6.

Results

Comparison between UAV-based LiDAR and manual PH measurement

With the assistance of UAV-based LiDAR, we performed a phenotypic analysis of 241 cultivated peanut accessions across four replicate field trials (2023E1, 2023E2, 2023L1, and 2023L2) (Fig. S1). The peanut PH values were derived from digital elevation models (DEMs) generated using UAV-based LiDAR point cloud data (Fig. 1). To evaluate the LiDAR-derived PH values, we conducted manual measurements of PH at various plots by ruler at DAS49 and DAS67 in 2023E, and DAS40 and DAS89 in 2023L. Linear regression analysis was employed to investigate the relationship between UAV-derived LiDAR and manual PH measurements. The R2 for the correlation between UAV-derived and manual PH measurements was 0.91 for both 2023E and 2023L trials. The RMSE were 3.24 ​cm and 2.02 ​cm, with NRMSE values of 0.07 and 0.06, respectively (Fig. 2a and b). These results demonstrate a close agreement between LiDAR-derived and manually measured PH values. Consequently, UAV-based LiDAR can be utilized for precise and high-throughput phenotypic analysis of peanut PH. Phenotypic variation analysis of peanut PH under time series

Based on the UAV LiDAR point cloud data, digital elevation model images were generated to extract PH data for further analysis of the PH distribution among peanut varieties. Across all experimental time points, the 241 accessions showed a continuously varying PH, demonstrating a rich natural variation with coefficients of variation (CV) ranging from 0.176 to 0.304, indicating significant phenotypic variability (Table S7). The PH at all time points followed a normal or near-normal distribution, a typical feature of quantitatively inherited traits controlled by multiple genes (Fig. 2c–S2).

Pairwise correlation coefficients of PH phenotypes measured at different time points in each replicate trial were calculated using Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 2d–S3). The Pearson correlation coefficients confirmed a significant positive correlation of PH data across different time points, with adjacent time points showing higher correlations. These results also indicate a high degree of variability in PH among these 241 accessions. The heritability analysis showed that the broad-sense heritability (H2) of PH at all time points was greater than 0.5, ranging from 0.535 to 0.825, suggesting that PH is primarily controlled by genetic factors, with some influence from environmental factors (Table S7).

Dynamic analysis of PH using curve fitting and parameters extraction

UAV-based phenotyping may be affected by challenging field environments, including natural lighting, rainfall, strong winds, or gusts [60]. To address this, we utilized static PH phenotypes measured at different time points (Table S7) and established profile curves using Gaussian, Linear, Gompertz, Monomolecular and Logistic curve fitting functions (Fig. 2e–S5). Evaluation of the fitting functions showed that the Gaussian fit yielded the best R2 (Fig. S4a), the smallest RMSE and NRMSE errors (Fig. S4b and c).

Based on the Gaussian fitting function, we extracted 3 key parameters: a, b and c, to describe the characteristics of PH variation. The parameter a represents the maximum height of the plant during the growth process. The parameter b indicates the time point at which the plant reaches its maximum height. The parameter c measures the extent of variation in PH. Descriptive statistics for these parameters are presented in Table S8, with the distributions of parameters a, b, and c approximating normality or near-normality (Fig. S6). Analysis of the standard deviation for each parameter revealed differences in PH variation among varieties, suggesting their potential as valuable materials for GWAS.

Dimensionality reduction of the PH time series using PCA

PCA was employed to decompose the variation among the fitted curves. In four trials, the first 2 ​PCs explained more than 85 ​% of the total variation, with specific values of 92.68 ​%, 86.33 ​%, 98.49 ​%, and 97.71 ​% (Fig. 2f–S7). To interpret the biological meaning of PC1 and PC2, we examined their temporal loading patterns. PC1 exhibited relatively uniform and positive loadings across all time points, indicating that it captures consistent variation in overall plant height and can be interpreted as average height. In contrast, PC2 showed opposing loadings between early and late stages, suggesting that it reflects differences in how rapidly accessions increased in height over time, and is therefore considered to represent growth rate. This interpretation is further supported by prior studies applying PCA to time-series plant trait data [44,45]. The line graphs (Fig. 2g–S8) further illustrate those accessions in the top 10 ​% of PC1 exhibit significantly higher average heights across the entire fitted curve compared to those in the bottom 10 ​% of PC1. Conversely, a marked difference in average growth rate is observed between the top 10 ​% and bottom 10 ​% of PC2 (Fig. 2h–S8). These findings underscore that PC1 serves as a robust indicator of accession height, while PC2 is effectively suited for characterizing a growth rate of accession height.

Given the first 2 ​PCs account for most of the variation in plant growth patterns over time, these components were utilized in subsequent GWAS to identify genes associated with height variation. The descriptive statistics for these PCs (Table S8) show that the CV for PC1 ranges from −3.631 to 25.083, and for PC2, from −29.104 to 6.307, with both exhibiting distributions that are approximately normal or near-normal (Fig. S9). These findings provide valuable phenotypic data for further GWAS, facilitating more precise exploration of the genetic basis underlying variation.

Detection of QTNs and QEIs related to peanut PH using IIIVmrMLM

Based on the above 5 indicators mined from the Gaussian fitting and PCA (parameters a, b, c, PC1 and PC2), the GWAS was conducted on the above indicators in 241 peanut accessions under four trials. 447 significant/suggested QTNs (including colocalized QTNs) were identified for the 5 traits across the four trials through the single-environment detection model. These QTNs were distributed across all 20 chromosomes (Figs. S10 and S11; Table S9). The LOD scores of QTNs calculated by the R package IIIVmrMLM for parameters a, b, c, PC1, and PC2 ranged from 3.883 to 58.617, 4.456 to 54.355, 3.228 to 66.799, 3.844 to 39.228, and 3.979 to 48.494, respectively, with corresponding average r2 values of 1.83 ​%, 1.84 ​%, 1.79 ​%, 1.81 ​%, and 1.81 ​%. Candidate genes mining and genes enrichment analysis

To identify candidate genes, we defined candidate regions as 50 ​kb upstream and downstream of all QTN and QEI, based on the LD decay distance reported in previous studies [43]. In total, 1133 candidate genes were identified (Table S12). First, we performed GO enrichment analysis on these 1133 candidate genes (Fig. 3d; Table S13). The GO enrichment analysis indicated that the candidate genes were primarily enriched in protein binding and lipid binding within molecular functions. The cellular components were mainly enriched in the plasma membrane, vacuole, and endosome, while biological processes were largely enriched in processes such as transport and anatomical structure development. These results indicate the potential importance of candidate genes in plant growth and development. Subsequently, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the 1133 candidate genes (Fig. 3e; Table S14), which showed that the biosynthesis pathway of sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis had the highest level of significance, suggesting that it was highly enriched in the candidate genes. In addition, other significantly enriched pathways included metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides, anthocyanin biosynthesis, the secretion system, and the ubiquitin system, which further implies their involvement in plant growth and development.

Identification of target genes for analysis and validation through integrated DEGs analysis and WGCNA

To further identify genes and conduct functional analysis and validation, this study utilized transcriptome data of peanut plants with varying stem heights, which was downloaded from the NCBI public database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.gov/geo/, SRP287054). DEG analysis and WGCNA were performed, and the results were combined with GWAS data to identify target genes for subsequent analysis and validation.

In the DEG analysis (Fig. S12), PCA was first conducted on the samples. PC1 explained 75 ​% of the variance, and PC2 explained 11 ​%, indicating significant differences between samples, which are suitable for further analysis (Fig. S14a). The criteria for DEG analysis were set to adjusted P-value ≤0.05 and |log2FC|≥1, resulting in the identification of 683 genes with significant differential expression between tall and short stem samples, including 445 upregulated genes and 238 downregulated genes (Fig. S14b and c; Table S15).

For WGCNA (Fig. S15), lowly expressed genes were removed based on FPKM values, resulting in 24,793 highly expressed genes. A hierarchical clustering tree was then generated using gene expression profiles (Fig. S15a), and no significant outliers were observed. Subsequently, WGCNA was performed, and a soft threshold of β ​= ​10 (Fig. S15b) was chosen for co-expression network construction. A gene clustering tree was generated based on gene expression correlation, as shown in Fig. S15c, with branches representing gene clusters characterized by high co-expression, ultimately resulting in 40 WGCNA modules. The correlation between the 40 modules and phenotypes is illustrated (Fig. S15d). Modules with higher absolute correlation coefficients and smaller p-values were considered highly associated with phenotypes, and 17 modules with |r| > 0.5 were identified [61], comprising a total of 16,022 genes.

By integrating the results of DEG analysis (683 genes), WGCNA (16,022 genes), and GWAS (1133 genes), three target genes were co-localized (Fig. 3f; Table S16): Arahy.A37Y50, Arahy.1026BX, and Arahy.G5K9LY.

Functional analysis and validation of the Arahy.1026BX gene

Based on the above analysis, we identified three target genes. Comparative analysis with the reference genome revealed that no SNPs were detected in the exons of the Arahy.A37Y50 and Arahy.G5K9LY genes, which may indicate that these genes have not undergone significant mutations and are therefore not the focus of further analysis. In contrast, a detailed investigation of Arahy.1026BX involved an LD analysis of its significant marker (12–115037909) and the near regions upstream and downstream. The results showed the formation of several LD blocks in this region (Fig. 4a). Additionally, LD analysis within the gene region of Arahy.1026BX also revealed the formation of an LD block (Fig. 4b), indicating a high degree of linkage disequilibrium among the internal variations of Arahy.1026BX. Subsequently, we used the JBrowse2 tool on PeanutBase website (https://www.peanutbase.org/tools/jbrowse2) to visualize the gene structure of Arahy.1026BX (Fig. 4c). SNP annotation revealed a non-synonymous mutation in the 2645 bp exon of Arahy.1026BX, where a lysine (Lys) was replaced by an arginine (Arg) (Fig. 4c). The difference in PC2 between materials with the ‘AA’ genotype and those with the ‘GG’ genotype was highly significant (P ​< ​0.0001) (Fig. 4d), and in four trials, plants with the ‘AA’ genotype exhibited significantly greater PH compared to those with the ‘GG’ genotype (Fig. 4e–h). To further validate these findings, we selected three tall and three dwarf accessions for RT-qPCR analysis of Arahy.1026BX expression (Fig. 4i). The gene showed significantly lower expression in dwarf accessions than in tall ones during nutrient and reproductive growth periods. Discussion

PH is a key agronomic trait due to its ease of measurement and its strong association with biomass, lodging resistance, and yield [62]. However, as plant development progresses, PH undergoes dynamic changes, and traditional phenotyping throughout the developmental stages remains a major bottleneck in genetic studies. In this study, we employed high-throughput UAV-based LiDAR phenotyping to collect time-series data on PH for 241 peanut accessions across multiple trials, aiming to explore the dynamic regulation of PH. Compared with previous studies on peanut PH, our time-series data provide deeper comprehensive understanding of growth dynamics, capturing key growth stages that are often overlooked in static phenotypic studies. Furthermore, using VIGS technology, we validated the role of the Arahy.1026BX in regulating peanut PH, offering new insights into the genetic basis underlying peanut growth.

Advantages of UAV-based LiDAR for phenotyping

Traditional PH measurements typically depend on labor-intensive approaches, resulting in low efficiency, high cost, and limited in accuracy [63,64]. This issue is particularly pronounced in large-scale agricultural fields, where manual measurements are slow, labor-intensive, and lack timely precision. Additionally, manual measurements are susceptible to human error, leading to significant data variability [65]. In contrast, UAV platforms provide a more efficient, high-throughput solution for phenotypic analysis of crop traits. UAV technology has become increasingly popular for phenotyping plant structural traits in agricultural fields, offering advantages in terms of efficiency, cost, and precision that effectively address the limitations of traditional manual methods. It has thus emerged as a crucial technological tool in modern agricultural research and management [34,66]. Compared to visible, multispectral, or hyperspectral sensors, LiDAR technology offers significant advantages in measuring PH [37]. By capturing the precise three-dimensional structure of plants through laser pulses, LiDAR can generate high-resolution CHM, providing highly accurate PH data [21]. Its ability to penetrate dense vegetation and capture detailed structural information, especially in complex or densely vegetated agricultural fields, offers a level of detail that visible and spectral sensors often fail to capture. In this study, we selected 241 peanut accessions for analysis. UAVs were able to capture plant images from large field areas in a very short amount of time at specific time points. In our research, UAV technology enabled accurate monitoring of PH dynamics during rapid growth. The rapid monitoring capability of this technology enables researchers to capture critical traits at key periods of plant development. Furthermore, the R2 value between UAV-based LiDAR measurements and manual measurements exceeded 0.9, indicating that UAV technology is highly reliable in terms of measurement precision.

Reliable gene identification through UAV-based dynamic phenotyping combined with genomic analysis

High-quality phenotyping is essential for genetic analysis of field traits, which has driven a recent trend toward integrating UAV-based phenotyping technology with genomics, such as GWAS. Traditional studies on PH typically focus on specific growth stages; however, PH exhibits dynamic changes throughout plant development [32]. Using a non-destructive, high-throughput UAV-based phenotyping platform, we collected PH data across multiple growth stages that traditional methods often fail to capture. Although a large amount of imagery and sensor data has been obtained, extracting meaningful information from such big data remains a challenge [67]. In this study, we followed the approach of Sun et al. [42] and Ding et al. [35] first calculating static phenotypes of peanut at different growth stages as data points and then selecting appropriate curve-fitting functions based on these points. Using the fitted curves, outliers due to phenotyping errors were detected and excluded, and dynamic indicators (e.g., fitting parameters) were extracted. This approach not only provided a deeper understanding of the dynamic changes in PH but also reduced the workload for phenotypic analysis and improved the reliability of assessing phenotypic variation among different genotypes. In addition, we applied PCA to extract the first two PCs, thereby reducing the dimensionality of time-series PH data.

Based on these five indicators (fitting parameters a, b, c, and the first two PCs), we performed a GWAS analysis and identified 1133 genes, including previously reported genes related to peanut PH (Fig. S16) [11,[68], [69], [70], [71]]. Furthermore, we performed pathway enrichment analyses on the identified genes. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the candidate genes are primarily involved in processes such as substance transport within cellular structures like the plasma membrane, vacuole, and endosome, as well as protein and lipid binding and the regulation of plant growth and development. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the sesquiterpenoid biosynthesis pathway could be crucial for plant development. Although the sesquiterpenoid pathway is typically associated with secondary metabolism and stress resistance, based on our results, we hypothesize that these candidate genes may influence PH by regulating sesquiterpenoid biosynthesis. Previous studies have shown that complex regulatory interactions may exist between secondary metabolites and plant hormone networks (e.g., auxin, ethylene, and jasmonic acid), which may affect cell division, elongation, and differentiation, thereby regulating PH [72,73]. Moreover, the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway has also been shown to be closely associated with plant development [74,75], suggesting that these pathways may serve as promising candidates for future research on PH regulation. It should be noted that both GO and KEGG enrichment results are presented as potential functional context and as hypotheses for future validation. These results require further experimental validation and additional data to confirm their biological significance and clarify their specific roles in PH regulation.

Candidate genes provide new insights into the genetic basis of peanut PH

PH is a complex trait controlled by numerous small-effect loci [76]. Aromatic amino acids, as products of the shikimate pathway, are involved in the synthesis of auxin and lignin [77], both of which are essential for PH regulation. Tryptophan is a precursor of auxin, which has a pivotal function in regulating PH. The synthesis and signaling of auxin significantly affect PH, with changes in the expression of auxin-related genes directly influencing stem length and growth rate [78,79]. Phenylalanine is a key intermediate in the lignin biosynthesis pathway. Wang et al. [80]revealed that the MYB110 transcription factor regulates lignin-related genes such as Os4CL3 and OsPAL3, altering lignin accumulation in rice stems, thereby affecting both PH and lodging resistance. Most semi-dwarf mutants in peanuts have been generated through radiation-induced mutagenesis [81,82]. While these extreme mutants have provided valuable insights into the mechanisms regulating PH, breeders typically prefer using relatively mild variations in natural populations for practical breeding purposes. Such variations are generally more stable and allow for the optimization of PH traits without adversely affecting other agronomic traits. In this study, we combined UAV-based dynamic phenotyping with GWAS, DEGs, and WGCNA to identify Arahy.1026BX, a candidate gene associated with PH. This gene encodes a multifunctional enzyme (DHD/SHD) involved in several key steps of the shikimate pathway. In a previous study, Ding et al. [83]used RNA interference to suppress the expression of the plastidial DHD/SHD gene in tobacco leaves, resulting in significantly reduced enzyme activity and severe growth inhibition, accompanied by a decrease in aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine and tyrosine) and their downstream products (lignin and chlorogenic acid).

Although we have preliminarily validated the function of Arahy.1026BX through RT-qPCR and VIGS, its precise molecular regulatory mechanisms still require further confirmation using gene editing techniques such as CRISPR. Further investigations are needed to uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying peanut PH regulation, particularly through integrating gene functional validation with multi-omics strategies like metabolomics and proteomics, to further elucidate the complex regulatory networks governing PH traits. Additionally, this time-series framework can also be applied to other dynamic traits, enhancing understanding of gene–environment interactions in crop development.

Limitations and future directions

Our study combined UAV-based LiDAR high-throughput phenotyping with multi-omics analyses to identify and validate the potential role of Arahy.1026BX in regulating peanut PH, but some limitations remain. The experiments were conducted at a single location within one year, and although both spring and summer sowing dates were included, the design inevitably confounded sowing date with seasonal climate effects, which restricts the general applicability of the conclusions. Future studies should therefore incorporate multi-location and multi-year trials for further validation. In addition, while this study primarily focused on Arahy.1026BX, we acknowledge that other candidate genes (such as Arahy.A37Y50 and Arahy.G5K9LY) may also contribute to PH variation and warrant systematic evaluation in future work. Finally, although the non-synonymous SNP in Arahy.1026BX showed strong association with PH and was supported by expression and VIGS analyses, the observed expression differences may also result from regulatory variants in the promoter or downstream non-coding regions acting together with the exonic mutation. These considerations are crucial for further elucidating the molecular regulatory mechanisms of PH and for advancing molecular breeding in peanut.

Conclusion

In summary, this study leveraged a UAV-based LiDAR high-throughput phenotyping platform to monitor the dynamic growth of 241 peanut accessions across four trials, providing precise time-series PH measurements. Through GWAS, a total of 1133 candidate genes associated with key dynamic growth parameters were identified. Further integrative analysis using DEGs analysis and WGCNA pinpointed Arahy.1026BX as a key candidate gene, which is crucial in the shikimate pathway, influencing auxin and lignin biosynthesis. Functional validation through RT-qPCR and VIGS confirmed its critical role in PH regulation. This study highlights the effectiveness of combining advanced UAV-LiDAR phenotyping with genomic approaches to uncover the genetic basis of PH in peanut, offering guidance for peanut breeding strategies targeting PH optimization and yield improvement.
