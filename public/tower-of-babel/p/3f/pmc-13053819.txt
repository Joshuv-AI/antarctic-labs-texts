Tumor-Associated Macrophage Infiltration and PD-L1 Expression in Gastric Cancer According to a Modified TCGA-Based Classification
Authors: Boram Song, Dong-Hoe Koo, Eo Jin Kim, In-Gu Do, Jinah Chu, Kyungeun Kim, Hyebin Lee, Min-Jung Kwon, Jung Ho Park, Byung Ho Son, Chang Hak Yoo, Seoung Wan Chae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAlthough gastric cancer (GC) exhibits significant genomic heterogeneity, the clinical implications of its immune microenvironment remain poorly understood.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively evaluated patients with GC who underwent gastrectomies between 2011 and 2014. The tumors were analyzed for Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (CD3), tumor-associated macrophages (CD68 and CD163), and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression. Tumors were classified using the modified The Cancer Genome Atlas scheme, and their clinical characteristics were compared.ResultsA total of 567 patients were classified into EBV (6%), MSI-H (10%), chromosomal instability-like (36%), and genomically stable-like (48%) subtypes. EBV tumors exhibited the highest PD-L1 expression (85%) and immune infiltration by CD3+ T cells (86%), CD68+ macrophages (58%), and CD163+ macrophages (40%). High CD68+ macrophage tumors were associated with advanced stages and worse 5-year disease-free survival (83% vs. 95%; P<0.001); however, this association was not independently significant after adjusting for the tumor-node-metastasis stage. PD-L1 expression did not significantly affect the survival outcomes.ConclusionsGC subtypes have distinct immune microenvironments that influence prognosis. Our findings highlight the prognostic and therapeutic potential of immune profiling in GC.

INTRODUCTION

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common cancer and fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. In 2020, more than one million new cases of GC were diagnosed, and approximately 760,000 deaths were attributed to GC globally [1]. Despite significant advancements in diagnosis and treatment, recurrence and mortality rates remain high, indicating that GC is a clinically heterogeneous disease [23].

Traditionally, GC has been classified based on histological criteria [4]. The Lauren classification divides gastric adenocarcinoma into intestinal and diffuse types [5], while the World Health Organization (WHO) system categorizes GC into papillary, tubular, mucinous (colloid), and poorly cohesive carcinomas [6]. Recently, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project identified 4 molecular subtypes of GC through integrative genomic and proteomic analysis [3]: Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-associated, microsatellite instability (MSI) due to MLH1 hypermethylation, chromosomal instability (CIN) with frequent copy number aberrations enriched in intestinal-type tumors, and genomically stable (GS) enriched among diffuse-type tumors.

Each of these subtypes is associated with distinct tumor microenvironment (TME) characteristics and clinical outcomes. For instance, EBV and MSI subtypes exhibit heightened inflammation and increased programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression [78]. Tumor-infiltrating immune cells, particularly tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), play a significant role in immune tolerance and tumor progression [9]. TAMs express immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-L1, which suppress adaptive immunity and promote tumor growth. TAMs are a heterogeneous population with plasticity encompassing both the anti-tumoral (M1) and pro-tumoral (M2) phenotypes. The M2 phenotype has been implicated in immune tolerance, tumor progression, angiogenesis, and metastasis [10].

This study aimed to investigate the clinicopathological characteristics of each TCGA subtype in relation to the immune microenvironment by focusing on TAMs infiltration and PD-L1 expression using immunohistochemistry (IHC).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient selection

We consecutively enrolled 673 patients who underwent gastrectomy for GC at Kangbuk Samsung Hospital between January 2011 and December 2014. Of these, 45 were excluded due to an insufficient number of dissected lymph nodes, leaving 628 patients for inclusion in this study. Clinical data, including patient age, sex, and follow-up findings, were retrieved from electronic medical records. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Kangbuk Samsung Hospital (approval No. 2020-11-033).

Pathologic diagnosis and tumor samples

Surgically resected specimens were macroscopically examined and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. Representative tumor sections were embedded in paraffin using standard protocols and reviewed based on the WHO [6] and Lauren classification [5] and tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stages as per the 2018 American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system [11]. For Lauren classification, tumors were categorized as intestinal type (well-differentiated [WD] and moderately differentiated [MD] papillary and tubular adenocarcinomas), diffuse type (poorly cohesive carcinoma and signet ring cell carcinoma), mixed type (when intestinal and diffuse components coexisted in similar proportions), or indeterminate type (solid-type poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas that could not be clearly assigned to intestinal or diffuse categories) [12]. Lymphovascular invasion and perineural invasion were categorized as mild (1–2 foci) or marked (≥3 foci) based on the number of observed foci [11]. The most representative tumor areas consistent with histologic type of pathological diagnosis were marked on the slides. Two 2-mm-diameter tissue cores were obtained from each block and manually arrayed into recipient TMA blocks following IHC and in situ hybridization (ISH) staining.

Modified TCGA classification of GC

GCs were categorized into 4 subtypes based on TCGA molecular and histologic criteria (Fig. 1A): (1) EBV confirmed by ISH of EBV-encoded RNA (EBER); (2) microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), assessed by IHC for mismatch repair proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6); (3) intestinal, as a surrogate for CIN type, associated with chronic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, atrophy, and glandular dysplasia; and (4) diffuse, as a surrogate for the GS type, characterized by the absence of gland-forming intestinal components, signet-ring cells, or excessive extracellular mucin pools, and without precursor lesions such as chronic gastritis or intestinal metaplasia (Fig. 1B). Histological evaluation to categorize the subtypes was based on pathological diagnosis by evaluating the entire tumor specimen. For Lauren indeterminate or mixed cases, the classification was determined based on the predominant component after an independent pathological review by 2 gastrointestinal pathologists, and these were categorized as CIN-like (intestinal) or GS-like (diffuse).

IHC analysis

IHC analysis of the TME included staining for the pan-macrophage marker CD68, the M2 macrophage marker CD163, and the T cell marker CD3. IHC was performed using an avidin-biotin-peroxidase detection system on a Ventana BenchMark Ultra Stainer (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The following primary antibodies were used: MLH1 (Clone M1, Ready-to-use; Roche, Basel, Switzerland), PMS2 (Clone A16-4, Ready-to-use; Roche), MSH2 (Clone G219-1129, Ready-to-use; Roche), MSH6 (Clone SP93, Ready-to-use; Roche), EBER ISH (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), CD3 (Clone LN10, 1:200; Leica), CD68 (Clone 514H12, Ready-to-use; Leica), CD163 (Clone 10D6, 1:100; Leica), PD-L1 22C3 PharmDx (Clone 22C3, Ready-to-use; Dako, Glostrup, Denmark), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2, Clone SP3, 1:200; LabVision Corporation, Fremont, CA, USA), and p53 (1:5,000; Cell Marque, Hot Springs, AR, USA). For p53 IHC interpretation, we used a pattern-based approach [13]. Tumors showing diffuse, strong nuclear staining in ≥90% of tumor cells or complete absence of nuclear staining in tumor cells in the presence of positive internal non-neoplastic controls (null pattern) were classified as having an aberrant (mutant-type) p53 pattern. Tumors with heterogeneous intermediate nuclear staining were classified as having a wild-type p53 pattern.

Interpretation of the tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), TAMs, and PD-L1 staining

Digital images of stained TMA slides were obtained using a GT 450 scanner (Leica Biosystems, Nußloch, Germany). Quantitative analysis of TILs and TAMs was performed using QuPath software (v0.3.0; https://qupath.github.io/). Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined as tumor areas, excluding necrotic and normal structures. The total number of 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB)-positive cells within the ROI was counted automatically. The intensity thresholds for pixel detection were standardized for all samples. Positive cell density was calculated as the percentage of DAB-positive cells out of all cells and the number of positive cells per mm2. Median percentages were used as cutoff values for TAMs (CD68, 10%; CD163, 3%) and TILs (CD3, 10%). PD-L1 staining was assessed based on circumferential membrane staining of tumor cells and membrane or cytoplasmic staining of tumor-associated mononuclear inflammatory cells. PD-L1 positivity was defined as a combined positive score (CPS) ≥1, calculated as the total number of PD-L1-positive cells (tumor cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages) divided by the number of viable tumor cells, multiplied by 100.

Statistical analysis

Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the interval from surgery to documented disease recurrence or death, whichever occurred first. Patients without disease progression or death were censored at the last follow-up visit. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from surgery to death, censored at the last follow-up, for surviving patients. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Multivariate analysis was performed using the Cox proportional hazards regression model with the Enter method. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P-value of <0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics according to TCGA subtypes

The MSI status was available for 567 of the 628 patients. Accordingly, 567 patients were identified and classified into the following 4 biological subtypes based on their molecular and histological features: EBV (6.3%), MSI-H (9.7%), CIN-like (36.4%), and GS-like (47.6%; Fig. 1C). The characteristics and final distribution of the patients are summarized in Table 1. EBV-type tumors, predominantly located in the upper stomach (86%), exhibited lymphoid stroma (53%), high CD3+ TIL levels (86%), and high PD-L1 expression (85%). MSI-H tumors had deeper T stages (T3/4, 44%), larger tumors (>5 cm, 43%), greater lymphovascular invasion (51%), and more advanced stages (II/III, 58%). Most CIN-like tumors demonstrated WD/MD histology (89%), p53 mutant pattern (61%), HER2 overexpression (16.2%), and low perineural invasion (9%). GS-like tumors were more often found in females (48%) and younger patients (<65 years; 76%) and predominantly exhibited signet ring cell histology (84%).

PD-L1 expression according to TCGA subtypes and TAMs

PD-L1 IHC data were available for 533 tumor samples. High PD-L1 expression (CPS ≥1) was observed in 221 tumors (42%). Among these subtypes, EBV-type tumors exhibited the highest PD-L1 expression (85%), followed by MSI-H tumors (55%). The CIN-like and GS-like subtypes exhibited positivity rates of 40% and 34%, respectively. High PD-L1 expression was associated with fewer signet ring cell components (35%), greater lymphatic invasion (37%), and higher densities of CD68+ (63%) and CD3+ (53%) cells (Table 3).

Survival outcomes according to the TCGA subtypes and CD68+ TAMs

The median follow-up duration of 508 living patients was 60.6 months (interquartile range, 53.6–73.2), with unreached median DFS and OS. The 5-year DFS and OS rates were 88.2% (95% confidence interval [CI], 85.5–90.9) and 88.8% (95% CI, 86.2–91.4), respectively. For stages I, II, and III, the 5-year DFS rates were 99.4% (95% CI, 98.6–100.0), 87.6% (95% CI, 81.2–94.0), and 48.9% (95% CI, 39.6–58.2), respectively (P<0.001; Fig. 3). The corresponding OS rates were 99.6% (95% CI, 98.9–100.0), 88.0% (95% CI, 81.7–94.3), and 51.7% (95% CI, 42.4–61.0), respectively (P<0.001). By subtype, the 5-year DFS rates were 100.0% (EBV), 75.7% (MSI-H; 95% CI, 64.4–87.0), 90.3% (CIN-like; 95% CI, 86.3–94.3), and 87.5% (GS-like; 95% CI, 83.6–91.4) (P=0.010). The corresponding OS rates were 100.0% (EBV), 84.1% (MSI-H; 95% CI, 74.4–93.8), 91.2% (CIN-like; 95% CI, 87.3–95.1), and 86.5% (GS-like; 95% CI, 82.4–90.6) (P=0.069). High CD68+ TAM infiltration was associated with worse DFS rates (82.6% at 5 years; 95% CI, 78.3–86.9) than low CD68+ TAM infiltration (94.6%; 95% CI, 91.8–97.4; P<0.001). PD-L1 expression (CPS ≥1 vs. CPS <1) did not significantly affect the DFS or OS. In the multivariate analysis, TNM stage was the only independent prognostic factor. Excluding TNM stage, lymphatic and perineural invasion were significant prognostic factors for DFS (Table 4), with high CD68 infiltration showing borderline significance (P=0.052).

Further stage-and TCGA subtype-stratified subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the prognostic impact of CD68 expression (Supplementary Fig. 1). No significant prognostic differences were observed within each TNM stage, which likely reflects the strong prognostic influence of TNM stage itself, making it difficult for CD68 infiltration to further discriminate outcomes within the same stage. However, when stratified by TCGA subtype, CD68-high tumors showed significantly worse DFS in the MSI-H (P=0.022), CIN-like (P<0.001), and GS-like (P=0.018) subtypes, whereas no events were observed in the EBV group. Moreover, CD163 infiltration and PD-L1 expression were evaluated according to TCGA subtype. CD163 showed similar trends without reaching statistical significance, whereas PD-L1 expression did not show any significant prognostic differences among the subtypes.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we examined the clinicopathological and immunological characteristics of GCs categorized according to a modified TCGA classification system using IHC staining, focusing on tumor-infiltrating immune cells and their prognostic implications. Our findings confirmed the heterogeneity of GC, revealing distinct immune microenvironments and clinicopathological features across the EBV, MSI-H, CIN-like, and GS-like subtypes, as previously reported [2314]. In particular, EBV-type tumors exhibit a unique immune profile characterized by a high density of CD3+ T cells and CD68+ macrophages and increased PD-L1 expression. These observations not only reaffirm previous reports linking EBV-positive GCs to pronounced lymphoid stroma and immune activation but also suggest that these tumors may be particularly responsive to immunotherapeutic strategies.

The differential distribution of immune infiltrates across subtypes was noteworthy. EBV-associated tumors, predominantly in the upper stomach, were enriched in immune cells and exhibited high PD-L1 expression. The immune-rich microenvironment supports the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors in this subgroup. This finding aligns with emerging clinical data suggesting favorable responses in patients with EBV-positive GC. In contrast, MSI-H tumors also demonstrated elevated PD-L1 expression and robust T cell infiltration. Notably, while both EBV and MSI-H subtypes exhibit high immune cell infiltration, the pattern differs substantially: lymphoid stroma, characterized by dense lymphocytic aggregates forming a prominent stromal component that can obscure tumor cells, was predominantly observed in EBV-associated tumors (52.8%) but was rare in MSI-H tumors (1.8%). This distinction reflects the different mechanisms of immune activation in these subtypes, with EBV-driven tumors eliciting a characteristic Crohn-like lymphoid reaction, whereas MSI-H tumors demonstrate diffuse TILs without the organized lymphoid stromal architecture typical of EBV-associated GC [151617]. Therefore, these MSI-H tumors were associated with more aggressive pathological features (e.g., deeper invasion, larger size, and increased lymphovascular invasion) and comparatively poorer DFS. This discordance between immune activation and clinical outcomes in MSI-H tumors suggests that additional factors within the TME or host immunity may modulate the impact of immune cell infiltration on disease prognosis. One potential explanation for this is the presence of a highly immunosuppressive macrophage milieu. MSI-H tumors show high infiltration of CD68+ TAMs, with a substantial CD163+ subset suggestive of M2-polarized macrophages, which may counterbalance the favorable prognostic impact of ‘immune-hot’ CD3+ T cell infiltration. These findings raise the hypothesis that dominant M2-like TAM polarization could blunt the survival benefits typically associated with MSI-H status, underscoring the need for future studies to elucidate the functional interactions between TAM subsets and T cell responses in MSI-H GC.

Our findings also highlighted notable differences in the distribution of TCGA subtypes when comparing our cohort to the original TCGA dataset [3]. Specifically, the proportions of EBV-positive (6% vs. 10%) and MSI-H tumors (10% vs. 20%) were lower in our cohort, whereas the proportion of GS-like tumors was higher (47% vs. 20%). Similar trends have been reported in other East Asian populations, including Japanese and Korean cohorts [1819], whereas Western studies have shown subtype distributions that align more closely with TCGA cohort [2021]. These discrepancies may reflect underlying racial and environmental differences rather than methodological variations stemming from the use of IHC staining. Importantly, despite these differences in subtype proportions, the clinicopathological characteristics in our cohort were largely comparable to those observed in TCGA dataset. This supports the validity of our modified TCGA classification using IHC staining as a practical alternative to routine clinical practice, enabling robust subtype categorization without relying on extensive molecular analyses.

This study highlighted the crucial role of TAMs in the immunosuppressive TME of GC. TAMs facilitate tumor progression by secreting cytokines and growth factors and enhancing the expression of inhibitory immune checkpoint proteins such as PD-L1 [22]. Owing to their functional plasticity, TAMs are capable of exhibiting both anti-tumoral (M1) and pro-tumoral (M2) phenotypes, with M2 being primarily associated with increased tumor progression, angiogenesis, and metastasis [9]. We assessed TAM populations using IHC staining with CD68 as a pan-macrophage marker and CD163 as an M2-specific marker. Our findings revealed that GC cases with high CD68+ TAM densities significantly correlated with various adverse prognostic factors, including advanced T stage, larger tumor size, diffuse-type histology, poor differentiation, lymphovascular invasion, and perineural invasion, further underscoring the role of TAMs in promoting tumor aggressiveness. Moreover, patients with high CD68+ TAM infiltration had a significantly worse DFS than those with low infiltration. Although PD-L1 expression did not independently affect DFS or OS in our cohort, the observed association between high TAM density and aggressive tumor behavior supports the growing body of evidence implicating TAMs in promoting tumor progression and immune evasion. These findings highlight the potential of targeting TAMs as a therapeutic strategy for GC, either alone or in combination with other modalities, such as checkpoint blockade [2324].

Interestingly, despite the prognostic significance of high CD68+ TAM infiltration in univariate analysis, multivariate analysis identified TNM stage as the only independent prognostic factor for DFS. When TNM stage was removed from the model, lymphatic invasion, perineural invasion, and high CD68+ infiltration emerged as significant or borderline predictors of poor prognosis [24]. This suggests that, while traditional clinicopathological parameters remain strong predictors of outcomes, the TME, especially the macrophage component, may offer additional, though more nuanced, prognostic information. The borderline significance of CD68+ TAMs in the absence of TNM staging highlights the need for further studies to refine the predictive value of immune biomarkers and investigate their potential integration into existing staging systems. Future investigations should explore the mechanisms of TAM-mediated tumor progression and evaluate the potential of macrophage-targeted or combination immunotherapies (e.g., programmed death-1 [PD-1] blockade with TAM modulation), particularly in populations with distinct molecular subtype distributions.

Our findings highlight the importance of incorporating immune microenvironment markers into the prognostic evaluation of GC. The differences observed in TCGA subtype proportions between our cohort and Western cohorts underscore the necessity of considering racial and regional variations when interpreting GC biology. The EBV and MSI-H subtypes are well-established candidates for immune checkpoint inhibitors, whereas the CIN subtype frequently harbors targetable alterations such as HER2 amplification or receptor tyrosine kinase. Moreover, the modified TCGA classification using IHC staining provides a practical and cost-effective approach for routine clinical stratification of relevant targeted or immunotherapeutic strategies. Therefore, our findings support the rationale for exploring macrophage-targeted therapies or combination immunotherapies (e.g., PD-1 blockade with TAM modulation) for this subset of GC. Future investigations should examine the mechanistic underpinnings of TAM-mediated tumor progression and assess the potential of targeting TAMs as part of integrated therapeutic strategies, particularly in populations with distinct molecular subtype distributions.

Our study has several strengths. By employing a modified TCGA classification that integrates molecular and histological criteria, we identified distinct subtypes of GC with unique immunological profiles. Quantitative digital image analysis of TILs and TAMs using the QuPath software improved the objectivity and reproducibility of our immune cell assessments. However, this study has certain limitations. First, the retrospective, single-institution nature of the study may have limited the generalizability of the findings. Second, because the original TCGA classification was derived from comprehensive genomic analyses, our histopathology-based approximation does not fully correspond to molecular subtypes and should be viewed as a surrogate rather than a direct substitute. Some GS-like tumors exhibited mixed or transitional histological features or contained extracellular mucin-rich components, which were classified as WD/MD according to conventional histopathological criteria. This discrepancy reflects histological heterogeneity and limitations of the histopathology-based TCGA classification approach. Although TMAs are practical for high-throughput analysis, they may not fully capture the spatial heterogeneity of the TME. Consequently, IHC-based analyses of TMAs have inherent limitations in capturing the spatial and functional complexities of the TME. Future studies employing whole-slide imaging and spatial transcriptomics could offer comprehensive insights into the distribution and functional states of immune GC. Finally, although our study provides a glimpse into the immune landscape at the time of surgery, the dynamic evolution of the TME during treatment remains unclear.

In conclusion, our data revealed that GCs exhibited distinct immune profiles across TCGA subtypes. EBV-associated tumors exhibit significant immune cell infiltration, PD-L1 expression, and high CD68+ TAM density as aggressive disease markers. These findings have important clinical implications, particularly for immunotherapy and macrophage-targeted strategies. Prospective studies are warranted to validate these results and explore the therapeutic potential of modulating TME in GC.
