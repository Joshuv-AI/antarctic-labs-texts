2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of elevated blood pressure and hypertension – How practical is it for clinical practice?
Authors: Elisabeta Badila
Published: 2024

The presentation of the new guidelines at the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Congress started with the long-awaited guideline dedicated to hypertension. After the publication in 2023 of the document of the European Society of Hypertension, preparing a guideline with updates and new recommendations seemed quite difficult.

The first aspect that attracts attention is the title change. We are no longer discussing the management of arterial hypertension, but rather elevated blood pressure (BP) and hypertension (HTN) [1]. It is even recommended to remove the term arterial and to use only the one of hypertension, citing that arterial hypertension can also occur in the pulmonary arteries. The introduction in the title of the idea that elevated BP correlates with the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) even before reaching the threshold values that define hypertension was to be expected after the communication of the results of the meta-analysis showing that for each 5 mmHg reduction in systolic BP, the risk of cardiovascular events decreased by 10 %, and even a modest BP reduction can lead to significant gains in preventing incident or recurrent CVD [2].

The guideline proposes a new classification, but keeping the classic definition of hypertension (office systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg or office diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg). Two new terms are introduced: elevated BP (office BP 120–139 for systolic or 70–89 mmHg for diastolic) and non-elevated BP (office systolic BP < 120 or diastolic BP < 70 mmHg) to aid treatment decision. Beyond the aspect related to the difficulty of translating the term non-elevated BP into different European languages keeping its meaning from the guideline, one of the problems of this new classification is the absence of defining normal values for BP. Both for the clinicians, as well as for the healthy population and for the patients, the existence of cut-off values that define the normality of a parameter is imposed through the prism of the desire to reach it.

To measure BP we can use automated oscillometric monitors, but in patients with atrial fibrillation or in pregnant women these devices are not always accurate, so auscultatory measurements are recommended. The out-of-office measurement (home BP monitoring and ambulatory BP monitoring) can be used for diagnosis and ongoing management of hypertension because increasing evidence for the stronger relationship with outcomes and the ability to detect white-coat and masked hypertension, but none of the new cuffless modalities are recommended for routine clinical use.

The new ESC guideline no longer classifies hypertension according to degrees of severity, nor does it take into account the three stages of the disease mentioned in the 2018 guidelines [3]. The previous classification drew attention in particular to the increased risk in those patients with associated clinical conditions, in which the event has already occurred, starting even from high-normal BP. In the current guideline, it is insisted on the risk assessment based on SCORE-2, SCORE2-OP or SCORE2-Diabetes among individuals who are not at already considered at increased risk. We have a new term “sufficiently high CV risk conditions”, somehow difficult to perceive in clinical practice, to describe a person with 10-year CVD risk of ≥10 % (rather than the terms “high risk” or “very high risk "). Assessing the risk of a person with elevated BP is quite complicated according to the algorithm recommended by the guideline if the 10-year predicted CVD risk is between 5 and 10 % and difficult to apply in clinical practice. Shared risk modifiers are taken into account first, and then sex-specific modifiers (class IIa) and, if still uncertain, shared decision-making informed by risk tools (class IIb) by any of these abnormal criteria: coronary artery calcium score, carotid or femoral plaque, hs cardiac troponin, NT-proBNP, pulse wave velocity. If these modifiers are present or we have changes in the mentioned investigations, it is recommended to change the lifestyle for 3 months and then pharmacological therapy for those with BP ≥ 130/80 mmHg.

The identification of increased BP values during the screening in the office implies the subsequent confirmation of the diagnosis of hypertension or elevated BP, preferably through home BP monitoring or ambulatory BP monitoring, but the recommended time interval for specifying the diagnosis is quite lax. Thus, for those with HTN with values of 160–179/90–109 mmHg, the “prompt” confirmation of the diagnosis is recommended, but the prompt term is quite loose considering that it is mentioned that this should be done in the following month. For those patients with increased blood pressure values ≥ 180/110 mmHg, the first recommended step is to rule out a hypertensive emergency. Only the idea of hypertensive emergency remains, the urgency disappears, most of the patients in this category actually have uncontrolled hypertension.

There is a new screening recommendation for primary aldosteronism by renin and aldosterone measurement that should be considered in all adults with confirmed hypertension (BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg) with class IIa indication, which is not possible in current clinical practice. Doppler examination of the renal arteries also appears with a IIa class indication in hypertensive patients with chronic kidney disease to exclude the renovascular component. It is extremely difficult to apply screening methods for secondary HTN to all patients. It remains that clinical judgment to prevail, to think correctly about each patient presented with HTN and to assess if there is a probability of having a secondary form of HTN and what therapeutic implications it would have, before doing the recommended screening.

As a newer therapeutic aspect, we note the idea that all hypertensive patients are considered at sufficiently high risk so that they benefit from immediate antihypertensive treatment. The therapeutic target of 120–129 mmHg for systolic BP is considered a new recommendation and is justified by reducing the risk of CVD. On the other hand, if we refer to the guideline from 2018, for most adult hypertensives, the target for SBP was the same, 120–129 mmHg (even if it was obtained in two steps) with the mention “if tolerated”, which is still maintained now. The diastolic BP target of 70–79 mmHg represents only a class IIb indication in the current guidelines. Probably one of the best recommendations of the current ESC guideline is the application of the ALARA principles - target to BP As Low As Reasonably Achievable - whenever possible. The indication of monotherapy is maintained for frail, very elderly patients or patients with elevated BP. For most patients, it is recommended to start the therapy with a combination of two antihypertensives in a low dose, so that in the next step, three classes are combined in a low dose and only in step 3, maximally tolerated triple combination therapy, preferably in a single pill. Beta-blockers, although they do not appear as a first-line recommendation, are still indicated in specific situations at any step if there are compelling indications. In case of lack of control, the indication to add spironolactone is maintained, as it was in the previous guideline, this can be replaced with eplerenone, and there is a sequencing of the medication classes that can be added (a beta blocker, central agonist, alpha blocker, hydralazine, K+ sparing diuretics). More specific guidance is provided for managing hypertension in particular populations, such as the elderly and those with diabetes or chronic kidney disease. While the guidelines encourage early use of combination therapy, there are concerns about potential overmedication and the associated risks, especially in older populations.

Last but not least, self-measurement at home is recommended and encouraged to achieve better BP control and for positive effects on the acceptance of a diagnosis of hypertension, patient empowerment, and adherence to treatment.

In conclusion, some of the main messages of 2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of elevated blood pressure and hypertension could be: accurate BP measurement out-of-office, defining elevated BP with high CV risk conditions, targeting the threshold of 120–129 mmHg for one-step systolic BP, keeping combinations, in resistant hypertension taking drugs in a specified sequence, no urgency, just emergency, passing some of the responsibility to the patient. And a very important message is to follow the ALARA principle!
