A retrospective observational study on management of penetrating gunshot and migrating projectile injuries to the heart during the war in Ukraine
Authors: Stepan Maruniak, Yurii Hutsuliak, Ihor Mokryk, Oleh Zelenchuk, Serhii Sudakevych, Ihor Kuzmich, Andriy Batchinsky, Justyna Swol, Borys Todurov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Penetrating cardiac injuries caused by firearms are associated with extremely high prehospital and early in-hospital mortality. During wartime, civilian cardiac surgery centers are increasingly involved in the management of complex gunshot and fragment-related cardiac injuries, yet published evidence from nonmilitary practice remains limited.METHODS:We conducted a retrospective observational study of civilian and military patients with penetrating cardiac gunshot or fragment injuries treated surgically at a tertiary civilian cardiac surgery center between February 2022 and December 2025. Patients with severe cardiogenic shock or rapidly progressing tamponade were typically managed at frontline hospitals; referred patients who survived initial resuscitation underwent definitive cardiac surgery at our institution. Demographic, clinical, imaging, operative, and outcome data were analyzed descriptively.RESULTS:Among 69 patients undergoing cardiac surgery for combat-related injuries, 25 required surgical removal of intracardiac foreign bodies. Median age was 38 years [interquartile range (IQR): 32–47], and 92% were males; 72% were military personnel. Metallic fragments accounted for 92% of foreign bodies, most commonly located in the ventricles. All procedures were performed in a hybrid operating room; cardiopulmonary bypass was used in 32% of cases, while 68% were managed off-pump with bypass standby. Neodymium magnet-assisted extraction was used in 80% of procedures. Median operative time was 117 minutes (IQR: 89–133). Median intensive care unit stay was 3 days (IQR: 2–5), and hospital stay was 13 days (IQR: 8–17). In-hospital and 30-day mortality were both 4%.CONCLUSIONS:Surgical management of penetrating cardiac gunshot and fragment injuries can be safely performed in civilian cardiac surgery centers during wartime. A multimodal imaging strategy, individualized use of cardiopulmonary bypass, hybrid operating room infrastructure, and adjunctive magnet-assisted techniques facilitate effective treatment with favorable early outcomes. BACKGROUND

Penetrating cardiac injuries caused by firearms are associated with high prehospital and early in-hospital mortality, reaching 60% to 90% in some case series.1,2 Survival depends on rapid recognition, immediate transport, and prompt surgical intervention. Gunshot wounds require urgent operative management to prevent fatal complications such as cardiac tamponade, massive hemorrhage, acute valvular dysfunction, and hemodynamic collapse.3,4 The clinical complexity of these injuries increases when bullets or fragments are retained within the myocardium, cardiac chambers, or great vessels, where they may not be easily recognized or extracted.

While some retained intracardiac projectiles may be managed without surgery in selected asymptomatic patients, most authors advocate surgical removal in the presence of hemodynamic instability, arrhythmias, valvular involvement, infection risk, or potential for further migration.5 However, due to the rarity of these injuries in European countries, available evidence is limited to case reports and small case series, and standardized management algorithms are lacking. In contrast, data from the United States, where civilian access to firearms is widespread and penetrating trauma is encountered more frequently, provide a broader clinical experience but may not be directly generalizable to the European context.6

The ongoing war in Ukraine has resulted in a substantial increase in firearm-related injuries among both military staff and civilians.7 Nonmilitary cardiac surgery centers are increasingly involved in the management of complex penetrating cardiac trauma, often under emergent conditions of limited resources and logistical constraints. Available evidence about cardiothoracic surgical management of gunshot and fragment injuries during wartime in nonmilitary practice is still scarce and fragmented.

The aim of this study was to report our center's experience in the cardiothoracic surgical management of gunshot and fragment injuries in civilian and military patients treated at a civilian cardiac surgery hospital during wartime.

METHODS

Study Design

This is a retrospective observational study including civilian and military patients who sustained penetrating cardiac gunshot or fragment injuries between February 2022 and December 2025. Data were obtained from the institutional operative registry, radiology archives, and medical records. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (Supplemental Digital Content 6, http://links.lww.com/TA/F293) guidelines were followed to ensure appropriate reporting of the study methods, results, and discussion.

Patients with penetrating cardiac injuries and severe cardiogenic shock or rapidly progressing cardiac tamponade were typically managed at referring hospitals near the frontline. This study included patients referred to us from those centers after they survived the initial resuscitation and were transported to our center for advanced cardiac surgical treatment.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local institutional ethics committee (approval No. 1 from July 1, 2026). Given the retrospective design and anonymized data set, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived. For military staff, data handling followed institutional and national regulations regarding confidentiality and access restrictions.

Patient Selection

Inclusion criteria were: (1) civilian or military patients aged 18 years or older, (2) firearm-related penetrating injury with confirmed cardiac involvement, and (3) cardiac surgery for foreign body removal.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) non-firearm penetrating trauma, (2) extracardiac retained projectiles without evidence of intravascular migration, and (3) incomplete documentation.

Definitions

Gunshot or fragment cardiac injury was defined as a projectile-related penetrating injury involving the myocardium, cardiac chambers, or coronary vessels requiring cardiac surgical management.

Data Collection

Variables included demographics, civilian or military status, mechanism and number of gunshot wounds, prehospital care, time from injury to hospital arrival, radiologic findings, suspected vascular entry site, foreign body location, associated injuries, transfusion requirements, and need for damage-control procedures before definitive cardiac surgery.

Further, surgical approach (median sternotomy/anterolateral thoracotomy as applicable), pericardiotomy findings, repair technique, cardiopulmonary bypass use, cannulation strategy, method of foreign body extraction, concomitant procedures, operative time, and intraoperative complications were collected.

Postoperative data included intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, reexploration for bleeding, acute kidney injury according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes criteria, renal replacement therapy, neurologic complications like stroke, infections, and in-hospital and 30-day mortality.

Imaging and Diagnostic Workflow

Plain radiography was used for initial projectile localization. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed to assess pericardial effusion, tamponade, chamber injury, and ventricular function. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography was obtained in hemodynamically stable patients to define missile trajectory, vascular entry site, and location. Transesophageal echocardiography was used intraoperatively or perioperatively at the discretion of the treating team.

Surgical Approach

All procedures involving the removal of intracardiac bullets or fragments were performed in a hybrid operating room (Supplemental Digital Content, Fig. 1, http://links.lww.com/TA/F289, hybrid operating room setup during surgical removal of an intracardiac missile fragment). When intraoperative localization of the foreign body was challenging, fluoroscopy was used to assist in real-time identification and guidance during extraction. A multidisciplinary team of cardiac and trauma surgeons, anesthesiologists, and radiologists was involved in decision-making.

The individualized choice of approach and cardiopulmonary bypass is followed according to foreign body location and suspected intracardiac injury. In off-pump cases, cardiopulmonary bypass circuits were primed in stand-by for each patient, allowing immediate conversion in case of hemodynamic deterioration. In on-pump cases, standard cannulation was performed using an ascending aortic cannula for arterial inflow and bicaval venous cannulation of the superior and inferior venae cavae for venous drainage.

Neodymium magnets were used intraoperatively to facilitate localization and extraction of ferromagnetic bullet fragments or intact projectiles, allowing atraumatic retrieval and minimizing additional myocardial or vascular manipulation (Supplemental Digital Content, Video 1, http://links.lww.com/TA/F290, intraoperative demonstration of neodymium magnet–assisted localization and extraction of a ferromagnetic intracardiac bullet fragment). Chest drains were placed. All patients were transferred postoperatively to the ICU for monitoring.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was survival to hospital discharge. Secondary outcomes included successful extraction of the missile/fragment, major postoperative complications (reexploration for bleeding, acute kidney injury requiring renal replacement therapy, stroke, sepsis), and ICU/hospital length of stay.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, while categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. No missing data were identified for the variables included in the analysis. All statistical analyses were descriptive and performed using XLSTAT statistical software (Addinsoft).

RESULTS

Overall, 133 patients with combat-related injuries were treated at our center; among them, 67 patients did not require cardiac surgical intervention (Fig. 1). In these cases, comprehensive clinical assessment and multimodal imaging excluded hemodynamically significant cardiac injury or retained intracardiac foreign bodies. In the context of combat-related trauma, these patients either required concomitant management of cardiovascular conditions without direct cardiac injury or were found to have myocardial contusions, self-limited pericardial effusions, or extracardiac thoracic injuries that could be managed conservatively. Sixty-nine patients underwent cardiac surgery for combat-related injuries. Of these, 25 patients required surgical removal of retained intracardiac foreign bodies, whereas the remaining 44 patients underwent cardiac procedures not related to foreign body extraction, including surgical removal of pericardial fragments and management of extracardiac thoracic injuries requiring cardiac surgical involvement.

Patients characteristics due to combat-related cardiac injuries and their management.

Within the 25-patient cohort with intracardiac foreign bodies, the median age was 38 years (IQR: 32–47); majority were males (92%) and military personnel (72%; Supplemental Digital Content, Table 1, http://links.lww.com/TA/F291, individual case summary of patients with penetrating cardiac gunshot or fragment injuries). The median time from injury to admission to the cardiac surgery center was six weeks (IQR: 4–8), reflecting delayed referral following initial stabilization and evacuation (Table 1). Nineteen patients required damage-control interventions at forward or regional facilities before transfer, including pericardial drainage, temporary hemorrhage control, and hemodynamic stabilization. In contrast, the remaining patients were hemodynamically stable and did not require emergent surgeries before referral to our center.

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

Retained intracardiac foreign bodies resulted predominantly from fragment-related trauma (92%), while direct cardiac gunshot wounds were uncommon (8%). Foreign bodies were most frequently localized to the right ventricle (44%), followed by the left ventricle (36%) and right atrium (16%); involvement of the interventricular septum was rare (4%). Concomitant extracardiac injuries were common, with nearly half of the patients sustaining musculoskeletal trauma (48%) and one-fifth presenting with associated abdominal injuries (20%).

All procedures were performed in a hybrid operating room (Fig. 1), mostly using an off-pump approach, while cardiopulmonary bypass was performed in complex cases. Fluoroscopic guidance and neodymium magnet–assisted extraction were used to facilitate foreign body localization and retrieval, especially in off-pump procedures. Surgery duration and transfusion requirements varied according to procedural complexity and bypass use, and due to individualized intraoperative decision-making (Table 2).

Early Postoperative Outcomes

The single in-hospital death occurred in a patient with complex combined injuries, including fragment-related cardiac involvement associated with concomitant abdominal and musculoskeletal trauma. The postoperative course was complicated by sepsis with subsequent development of multiorgan failure, including acute kidney injury requiring renal replacement therapy.

DISCUSSION

We describe a single-center experience with direct cardiac gunshot and fragment injuries in civilian and military patients treated at a civilian cardiac surgery center during wartime. The ongoing war in Ukraine led to an increase in firearm-related injuries requiring advanced cardiac surgical care. Our findings highlight important aspects under constrained conditions.

Patient Selection During Wartime

Patients with penetrating cardiac trauma in profound cardiogenic shock or rapidly progressing cardiac tamponade were treated at primary levels of care, at contemporary military and civilian trauma systems with rapid damage-control intervention close to the point of injury.8,9 The described population represents patients who survived initial resuscitation and evacuation, referred for definitive cardiac surgery. This referral selection bias has been previously described during military and civilian conflict settings.10 This cohort included intracardiac foreign bodies and concomitant extracardiac injuries requiring advanced cardiac surgery.

Diagnostic Strategy and Role of Multimodal Imaging

Management of cardiac gunshot wounds and major projectile fragment injuries calls for a multimodal, stepwise diagnostic strategy tailored to hemodynamic stability, injury pattern, and resource availability. Accurate localization of retained missiles is a central part of the management. Single imaging modalities are not sufficient as diagnostic techniques in this setting.11 In our study, plain radiography enabled rapid initial two-dimensional projectile localization, while echocardiography was used for the detection of pericardial effusion, chamber involvement, and ventricular function.12 Contrast-enhanced computed tomography performed in hemodynamically stable patients delineated projectile trajectory, showing suspected vascular entry sites and final localization.13 Intraoperative fluoroscopy proved particularly valuable when preoperative imaging did not precisely define foreign body position or when projectile fragment migration was suspected.

Surgical Decision-making, Hybrid Operating Room, and Individualized Use of Cardiopulmonary Bypass

Surgical management of intracardiac missiles is controversial.14,15 Surgical projectile extraction was performed on bypass in cases associated with hemodynamic instability, arrhythmias, valvular involvement, infection risk, or potential for further migration. The choice between on-pump and off-pump surgery was guided by foreign body localization and suspected extent of intracardiac injury. Off-pump extraction was feasible in selected hemodynamically stable patients, provided that cardiopulmonary bypass standby was ensured, whereas on-pump surgery enabled safe exploration and repair in complex intracardiac injuries.

A distinctive feature of our approach was the consistent use of a hybrid operating room for all procedures involving intracardiac foreign body removal. This setting allowed seamless integration of surgical exposure with real-time fluoroscopic guidance, facilitating precise localization of missiles and reducing the need for extensive myocardial or vascular manipulation.16 In addition, the adjunctive use of neodymium magnets simplified retrieval of ferromagnetic bullets and fragments, enabling atraumatic extraction through limited cardiac incisions. These technical adjuncts contributed to shorter localization times and higher procedural control, which allowed, in most cases, to be managed without cardiopulmonary bypass. Although magnet-assisted strategies are still under-represented in mainstream cardiac trauma literature, recent war-related reports and surgical descriptions documented their feasibility as a low-cost adjunct for precise fragment localization and expedited extraction.17,18

Outcomes and Comparison With Existing Literature

Despite the constraints inherent to wartime practice, outcomes in the investigated cases were favorable and comparable to previously reported case series and reports on cardiac gunshot injuries.10,19

High rates of successful extractions and survival to hospital discharge with low incidence of major postoperative complications were achieved. These findings are consistent with prior reports indicating that, although penetrating cardiac trauma is associated with high prehospital mortality, patients who reach definitive care and undergo prompt surgical intervention can achieve favorable outcomes.10,19

Clinical Implications

Civilian cardiac surgery centers may have a pivotal role in the management of penetrating cardiac trauma during war, particularly as centralized referral hubs for patients who survive initial resuscitation and evacuation. The use of a structured, multimodal diagnostic algorithm combined with individualized surgical planning enables safe and effective treatment in rare and complex scenarios. Finally, access to hybrid operating rooms and the use of simple adjunctive tools, such as neodymium magnets, enhance operative efficiency, facilitate atraumatic foreign body extraction, and reduce reliance on cardiopulmonary bypass in selected patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and relative selection bias related to referral patterns during wartime. Long-term outcomes were not systematically assessed, limiting conclusions on late complications or functional recovery.

The generalizability of the present study is limited by its single-center, retrospective design and the relatively small, highly selected patient cohort. As a tertiary civilian cardiac surgery center located away from the frontline, our institution primarily received patients who survived initial resuscitation and evacuation, which may not reflect the full spectrum of penetrating cardiac trauma, particularly the most severe cases with early mortality. Consequently, the reported outcomes may be applicable to frontline or prehospital settings with some caution.

In conclusion, we report on the successful use of specialized civilian cardiac surgery centers as referral hubs for injured patients with complicated cardiac and fragmented migrating projectile injuries. Utilization of well-coordinated multidisciplinary teams, hybrid operating rooms, multimodal imaging, including magnets to facilitate localization and removal of projectiles, and individualized use of cardiopulmonary bypass, are key components determining favorable outcomes in these critically injured patients despite challenges of wartime medicine.
