Effect of intratumoral alpha radiation on tumor growth delay and tumor microenvironment in an orthotopic colorectal liver metastasis murine model
Authors: Oran Zlotnik, Anastasia Tsatoumas, Audrey Kapelanski-Lamoureux, Amatzia Gantz, Miran Rada, Stephanie K Petrillo, Jennifer Kalil, Shmuel Packer, Lisa Deutsch, Ronen Segal, Zu-Hua Gao, Anthoula Lazaris, Vered Domankevich, Peter Metrakos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer frequently spreads to the liver, and current treatments often damage healthy liver tissue, while targeting tumors. Diffusing Alpha-emitters Radiation Therapy (Alpha DaRT) disperses alpha-emitting atoms from an intra-tumoral implanted source to a range of a few millimeters in the tumor. Alpha radiation is highly efficient in killing cancer cells, compared to other radiation types, and its short range in the tissue, potentially enables sparing healthy tissue adjacent to the tumor. Alpha DaRT’s efficacy was previously demonstrated in subcutaneous tumors from various histotypes. Yet its effectiveness in treating tumors located in an internal organ, such as the liver, remains to be tested in animal studies. Here, we used a single liver metastasis orthotopic model by implanting MC38 colorectal metastatic tissue in mice livers. The metastasis was then treated either with a single radioactive or inert Alpha-DaRT source. Alpha-DaRT effectively delayed tumor growth without damaging the surrounding liver parenchymal tissue. F4/80 + immunosuppressive macrophages in the tumor-normal tissue interface were reduced following treatment. The findings suggest that this type of radiation therapy could be a promising option for treating patients with liver tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51264-w.Keywords: Alpha DaRT, Alpha particles, Radium-224, Radiation therapy, Liver tumors, F4/80 + macrophagesSubject terms: Cancer, Surgical oncology, Radiotherapy, Cancer, Cancer models, Cancer therapy, Gynaecological cancer, Metastasis, Tumour immunology

Introduction

Liver metastases occur in more than 50% of colorectal cancer patients and most of them face a poor prognosis1. The treatment of colorectal liver metastases represents a significant global health challenge, necessitating the development of innovative strategies to effectively target liver metastases while minimizing damage to healthy liver tissues. While advancements in radiotherapy—such as Stereotactic Body Radiotherapy (SBRT)—have improved safety of radiation delivery to liver metastases, its application remains limited due to potential toxicity to adjacent healthy tissue and organs and limited efficacy. These limitations restrict SBRT use to cases with a sufficient volume of non-irradiated liver or to locations away from luminal gastrointestinal tissues in patients with unresectable liver metastases2.

Alpha particles are a form of radiation with high Linear Energy Transfer (LET) and superior ability to create irreparable DNA damage3,4, and to kill cancer cells even under hypoxic conditions5. In addition to their high relative biological effectiveness (RBE), alpha particles have a potential safety advantage as they diffuse in a very short range (micron scale) in the biological tissue6. However, the short range of alpha radiation also limits its use in the clinic. In the last few years, novel technologies have emerged to utilize alpha radiation for cancer treatment despite its short range. For example, the use of radium-223 and its tendency to accumulate in the bones, have enabled the delivery and therapeutic efficacy of alpha radiation in prostate patients with bone metastases7. Nevertheless, the treatment is still accompanied by systemic toxicity following intravenous administration of the drug and its temporal accumulation in organs such as the intestine8. Thus, the need to deliver alpha radiation specifically to the tumor tissue was not accomplished until Diffusing alpha-emitters Radiation Therapy (“Alpha DaRT”) emerged.

Alpha DaRT utilizes implanted thin metal tubular sources loaded with Radium-224 (224Ra) atoms that are fixed to it. During radium decay (224Ra half-life =3.6 days) its daughter isotopes are released from the source and diffuse into the surrounding tissue, thereby emitting alpha particles in close proximity to the source. This creates a therapeutically effective diameter of a few millimeters around each source9. The effective diameter is determined by the diffusion coefficients, half-lives, and clearance times of the daughter isotopes around the source and is dominated by the first daughter isotope 220Rn, a noble gas that diffuses relatively freely in the tissue. Alpha DaRT dosimetry and effective diameter were extensively studied in multiple tumor models including MC38 subcutaneous tumors (the cell line used in the current study), confirming these observations10 . This relatively short-range effect could potentially allow the treatment of liver lesions while avoiding the limitations of other modalities, such as microwave and radiofrequency ablation, which may damage adjacent organs11–13.

A treatment strategy has been developed based on the implantation of a hexagonal configuration of sources at a typical spacing of ~4 mm, to deliver a lethal dose to the tumor14–16. Promising results and complete response rates have been observed in a feasibility and safety clinical trial targeting locally advanced recurrent squamous cell carcinomas (SCC) of the skin and head and neck15, and in a second study for the treatment of recurrent or unresectable skin cancer patients16. The latter study showed 100% complete response rates at 12 weeks following treatment, and a safety profile of no more than grade 1–2 adverse events, when over 85% tumor coverage rates were achieved.

In previous preclinical studies Alpha DaRT was shown to delay tumor growth in multiple indications such as glioblastoma17, pancreas18, and breast cancer19. Murine models of SCC20, colorectal21, pancreatic18, and triple negative breast cancer18 have demonstrated that Alpha-DaRT sources can also enhance antitumor immune response or augment immunotherapy. Synergistic effects on tumor growth delay and lymphocytes infiltration have been observed when Alpha DaRT was combined with immune checkpoint blockade in a murine model of SCC20. Interestingly, Alpha DaRT alone was able to induce interferon response and increase CD8/Tregs ratio.

In the current study we investigated Alpha DaRT feasibility and effectiveness in a single orthotopically implanted CRC liver metastasis mouse model. The model was chosen and designed to preserve the splenic function and was not aimed at ablating the tumor22, allowing optional future combinational studies e.g. with immunotherapy and mimicking potential clinical cases of partial tumor coverage. The goal of this study was to investigate the response to the treatment after Alpha DaRT source implantation including tumor growth delay, possible damage to the healthy liver parenchyma surrounding the tumor. In addition, we aimed to investigate the treatment potential to alter the immune microenvironment of the liver, that is characterized by immunoregulatory23–25, liver-resident macrophages (among other immune cells populations)23.

Results

FeasIbility of Alpha DaRT implantation in a murine orthotopic single lesion colorectal liver metastasis model

As this study marks the application of Alpha-DaRT implantation into the liver, it was important to use a suitable tumor model. The conventional approach to induce colorectal liver metastases in mice involves injecting tumor cells into the spleen, which then migrate to the liver through the portal circulation, resulting in multiple liver lesions26. This metastatic model was not suitable for the investigation of Alpha DaRT sources mainly because it requires the implantation of multiple sources into multiple liver lesions (5–10) in relatively small liver sizes (average weight of a mouse liver is 1.5 grams). In addition, it involved splenectomy, which may interfere with the mice immune response22 that was shown to be activated by Alpha DaRT and may mediate part of its effect20,21,27.

Instead, we used a liver orthotopic mouse model of a single well-defined colorectal liver metastasis. We performed intrasplenic tumor cell inoculation, harvested the liver metastasis, and inoculated tissue pieces intrahepatically into the left lobe of the mouse liver (see methods and Fig. 1A). A single lesion was successfully established and was visible by Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) (Fig. 1B). Following MRI and prior to source implantation, an Alpha DaRT inert or radioactive source was prepared for inoculation (Fig. 2A). Thereafter, mice underwent laparotomy for Alpha DaRT sources implantation. The skin and peritoneum were opened to expose the left liver lobe, and the tumor was identified (Fig. 2B). A loading device containing the source was then connected to a designated applicator with a 21 G needle to load the source into the applicator needle. The applicator needle was inserted into the middle of the tumor (inferior to superior). Then, the source was placed inside the tumor by fixation of the applicator’s internal stylet followed by applicator retraction (Fig. 2C). This process was performed on both mouse cohorts, as described below. No unexpected distress was observed following radioactive or inert source implantation or during the follow-up period.

Alpha DaRT implantation in a single liver colorectal metastatic lesion. (A) An Alpha DaRT source is preloaded in a designated loading device, that serves as a protective capsule. (B) A laparotomy is performed to expose the tumor. (C) Alpha DaRT is loaded from a loading device into a 21G needle attached to a designated applicator. The applicator is pointing in the direction where we wish to have the Alpha DaRT source inserted, preferably in the middle. Then the Alpha DaRT source is ejected from the applicator into the tumor.

MRI but not PET-CT can be used to image an alpha DaRT source and to quantify the liver tumor size in the mouse liver

Alpha DaRT comprises a metal tube and the experiments were performed in the liver. Therefore, it was important to establish a precise imaging methodology of the source in the liver to accurately determine the spatial positioning of the source, thereby ensuring its correct placement within the tumor, and in order to assess the therapeutic effect of Alpha DaRT by tumor size quantification. We evaluated two imaging methods: PET-CT and MRI. Both methods enabled the determination of the source location. As for tumor quantification, artifacts from adjacent tissues masked the tumor in PET-CT and it was not possible to achieve an accurate quantification of the tumor due to background signal from the liver and kidneys (Fig. 3A). The MRI method enabled an accurate quantification of the tumor despite a negligible artifact around the source (Fig. 3B). Tumor sizes were retrieved from MRI DICOM files both by manual measurements of three tumor dimensions and using ImageJ to measure the tumor area- that were strongly correlated (Table S1, Fig. S1, r=0.78; p=0.0079, Pearson’s correlation coefficient).

Evaluation of imaging methods for Alpha DaRT source location and tumor size inside the mouse liver. (A) PET-CT or (B) MRI images of mouse liver tumor implanted with Alpha DaRT source.

Alpha DaRT significantly reduced tumor size of a MC38 lesion in the mouse liver

We then examined the impact of Alpha-DaRT sources on the size of individual colorectal metastases. In the first experiment (“Experiment 1”), mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups and either radioactive or inert sources were implanted into the liver tumor twelve days after tumor inoculation. During the procedure the initial longest diameter of the tumor was manually measured and recorded. Seven days after source implantation, MRI was performed and daily monitoring of animal health was documented. The experiment was terminated 8- 9 days after implantation, and mice were euthanized and livers were collected for further analysis (Fig. 4A).

Tumor growth delay following radioactive vs. inert Alpha DaRT source implantation in the liver. (A) Schematic representation of Experiment 1. (B) Bar chart of the mean ± SEM of the initial longest diameter (upper panel) and the follow-up tumor volume (lower panel), for mice treated with Alpha DaRT (n = 5) or inert (n = 5) sources in Experiment 1. P-value of student’s t-test is specified in the graph. C. Representative MRI images.

It was shown that despite no significant difference in the initial longest diameter between the treatment groups, the tumor size on MRI, 7 days following treatment, was significantly lower on average by about 3-fold in the radioactive group compared to the inert group (Fig. 4B, Table S1; two-sided t-test p<0.05). Representative images of tumors in MRI scan are presented in Fig. 4C.

Interestingly, when inspecting the MRI scans, it was shown that in one individual mouse the Alpha-DaRT source was located in the margins of the tumor. As expected, given the localized nature of the treatment, this tumor was relatively less affected by the treatment (Fig. 4C, blue arrows).

Radiation emitted from alpha DaRT did not damage the adjacent untreated healthy mouse liver tissue

We further investigated the effect of the radioactive Alpha-DaRT source on the surrounding liver tissue (at distances larger than ~3 mm from the source the dose drops below ~1 Gy and is dominated by beta electrons)28. We performed an additional experiment “Experiment 2”. Experiments 1 and 2 differed in the duration of the follow-up period after Alpha DaRT insertion. In Experiment 1 the Alpha DaRT sources were inserted on day 12 and, as the size of the lesion at that time was larger, resulting in a follow-up period that was relatively short (8, 9 days) whereas in the second experiment Alpha DaRT was inserted earlier (day 8) to allow a longer follow-up period (17 days) and capture potential late effects. When experiments were terminated tumors were harvested and stained (Fig. 5A).

Liver architecture following Alpha DaRT sources insertion. Representative images (20 × magnification) of liver parenchyma and tumor tissue stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin. Samples were taken from Experiment 2 (A) inert treated (B) radioactive treated.

As expected from the short diffusion range of the alpha emitters (a few millimeters) and the low contribution by beta electrons, the liver parenchyma, adjacent to the tumors where the Alpha-DaRT sources were inserted, demonstrated no histopathological changes compared to the inert sources (Fig. 5B). This included preservation of portal triads, hepatocyte morphology, sinusoids and stroma. Similar observations were observed in Experiment 1 (data not shown). In addition, no change was observed in the tissue sections when liver parenchyma was stained for apoptotic cells using the TUNEL assay (Fig. S2).

Alpha DaRT effects on innate immune cells in tumor microenvironment of the mouse liver

To assess the effect of Alpha DaRT on the immune cell microenvironment we evaluated both adaptive and innate immune cells from samples of both experiments (Fig. 6). We detected very low amounts of CD8+ cells in both experiments. In Experiment 1 we observed a non-significant tendency of CD8+ to increase in the tumor and a tendency of about 2-fold increase in the CD8+/F4/80+ ratio in the tumor core and interface (Fig. 6A). In Experiment 2 (representing a longer follow-up period after source implantation, Fig. 6B), no change in CD8+ was observed, however a significant decrease in F4/80 + was demonstrated in the interface (Figs. 6B, 7A), and a tendency of F4/80+ to decrease in the tumor and liver was observed. The CD8+/F4/80+ ratio was increased by about 2-fold in the tumor and interface in the radioactive group compared to inert control (Fig. 6B), corresponding to the observations of Experiment 1.

To investigate the activation state of the macrophages, we performed immunofluorescence (IF) using an M2 specific antibody CD206 (immunosuppressive macrophage marker). We observed colocalization of CD206, but not iNOS, a M1 specific antibody, with F4/80+ immune cells in the inert group, and similar staining (but less abundant) in the radioactive treated group (Fig. 7B), suggesting that macrophages were reduced after treatment but maintained their immunosuppressive state.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate for the first time the feasibility of Alpha-DaRT sources insertion into a tumor located in the liver in an animal model, and the tumor response to the treatment on this site. To achieve this goal, we studied a single colorectal liver metastasis in mice with an intact immune system. This model was well-suited for experiments involving locoregional therapies against liver metastases due to the presence of a biologically well-defined single lesion. Additionally, the model allowed for the single lesion size to be controlled and for the growth of the tumor to be monitored using imaging techniques (MRI).

As previously shown in multiple preclinical studies in mice, a single Alpha DaRT source is not expected to eradicate the tumor due to the short effective diameter of the source (few mm around the source). In this study, we used a single source, mimicking the human case of partial coverage. The treatment with radioactive Alpha-DaRT sources resulted in reduced tumor size compared to inert sources, without affecting healthy liver tissue adjacent to the tumor. In addition, the Alpha-DaRT implantation site architecture and cellular appearance was preserved (Fig. 5B).

The immune microenvironment in the liver exhibits immunoregulatory characteristics often referred to as “immune tolerant” to both self and foreign antigens23–25. Liver immune regulation is, among others, mediated by Kupffer cells, the liver-resident macrophages, which can downregulate the activity of cytotoxic T lymphocytes23. In the current study, we observed a reduced number of suppressive macrophages in the liver-tumor interface in the group treated with the Alpha DaRT sources compared to control inert sources. Qiao et al. have shown that in MC38 metastases in the liver there is a gradual increase of F4/80 in the tumor liver interface during the development of the lesion over time29. Since Alpha DaRT delayed tumor growth this might reduce this accumulation compared to the control group. The reason for the reduced number of macrophages in the liver-tumor interface and the consequences of these effects warrant further investigation.

By staining the macrophages, we showed that no change in their polarization occurred. In addition, we saw a tendency of CD8+ cells to increase and a corresponding ~2-fold increase in the CD8+/F4/80+ ratio in the radioactive vs. inert group. These findings warrant further investigation, including a time course to evaluate the mutual dynamics of these cell populations. Such investigation may examine the hypothesis that the reduction in macrophages was not directly due to the killing effect of radiation, as in case of a direct cell killing effect of radiation, we would have expected a decrease also in CD8+ numbers in early follow-up, and no reduction in cell number count 17 days after source implantation, in which the source is considered inert. This hypothesis is aligned with the well-established previously published mechanisms of macrophages reduction following radiotherapy and its effect on CD8+ cells23. According to these previous publications, CD8+ cells undergo apoptosis via interaction with suppressive macrophages in the liver, leading to CD8+ cell “desert”. Thus, since radiotherapy reduces suppressive macrophages, CD8+ cell survival is increased. Interestingly, the paper showed that radiotherapy decreased intrahepatic myeloid cells by altering the hepatic cytokine milieu. It was previously published that Alpha DaRT induces interferon response20. There is a strong published evidence that such a response in the liver, for example under viral attack, influences the tissue resident macrophages (F4/80⁺ in mice during viral infection and inflammation), and affects their persistence or depletion30. This may suggest that the interferon signaling induced by Alpha DaRT may mediate macrophages reduction. We propose this research direction for future investigation. In addition, a comparison between Alpha DaRT and x-ray is another possible future direction, yet due to the difference in dosimetry, such comparisons may require complex calibrations studies that are not in the scope of the current study.

Mare et al.,20 previously showed promising effects when Alpha DaRT was combined with other systemic therapies such as chemotherapy in other models, e.g. GBM (Temozolomide)17, colorectal cancer (5-FU)31, SCC (cisplatin)32, and pancreatic cancer (gemcitabine)33, or with the antiangiogenic agent Bevacizumab in GBM models17. Additional studies are required to confirm these findings in the liver.

Considering the availability of other ablative technologies and advancements in liver surgery, Alpha DaRT is particularly advantageous for specific anatomical locations, for which other treatments may be less effective or pose higher risks. One such location is liver tumors located next to critical structures, such as the hepatic veins or bile ducts, for which resection or ablation poses significant risks. The precision of Alpha DaRT makes it an ideal candidate as a localized treatment in such anatomically complex regions. In addition, in post-resection margins of clinical cases for which achieving clear margins surgically is challenging, Alpha DaRT might be implanted in the tumor bed (following surgical resection of the tumor) to target microscopic residual disease while preserving functional liver parenchyma. This is particularly advantageous for patients with underlying liver disease or limited liver reserve, where additional tissue loss could lead to liver failure. Another interesting case is large tumors. Since traditional ablation techniques, such as radiofrequency or microwave ablation, are generally limited to tumors up to 3 cm, the application of Alpha DaRT using suitable applicators for deploying multiple sources across the tumor may be advantageous.

The current study served as an initial translational step towards initiating a human clinical trial (NCT05829291) involving Alpha DaRT implantation for the treatment of colorectal liver metastases and towards future preclinical and clinical combinational studies. Alpha particles, due to their short range in tissue and high LET values, are a promising alternative to conventional photon or hadron therapy in the future. Using alpha radiation in the setting of a major internal organ with minimal damage to nearby organs and healthy tissue, and without the need of installing special expensive equipment, may provide a valuable tool towards expanding the treatment options in colorectal metastatic patients and the use of alpha radiation for cancer treatment.

Methods

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the Research Institute of the McGill University Health Centre (RI-MUHC) (Institutional Review Board Number 7841, Research institute of the McGill University Health Center), and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All procedures followed the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care. All procedures were reported as described by the ARRIVE guidelines34. C57BL/6 male mice weighing approximately 24 g and aged 8 weeks were sourced from Charles Rivers Laboratories and housed in the animal facility. Mice were induced with 4% isoflurane and maintained at 2% for surgical procedures and received carprofen (20 mg/kg every 24 hours) during and post-surgically (3 days). At the experimental endpoint, animals were euthanized via CO2 chamber followed by cervical dislocation, consistent with the recommendations of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals.

Tumor cell line

The cell line MC38 (obtained from Dr. P Brodt laboratory, QC, Canada), murine colon carcinoma, was used in this study. The cells were cultured in DMEM (Wisent Inc., #319-005-CL) supplemented with 10% FBS (Wisent Inc., #085-150) and 1% Penicillin-streptomycin solution (Wisent Inc, 450-201-EL) and maintained at a temperature of 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Intrasplenic tumor cell inoculation and tumor harvesting

Mice underwent intrasplenic inoculation of MC38 tumor cells as previously described35. Briefly, under general anesthesia, a left subcostal incision was made through the peritoneum, and the spleen was exposed. The spleen was then inoculated with 4×105 MC38 tumor cells in a volume of 20 μl using a 29-gauge needle, followed by spleen resection. The incision was closed, and the mice were allowed to recover. Eighteen days after the intra-splenic inoculation, the mice were sacrificed, and the liver metastases were aseptically harvested. The harvested liver tumors were cut into 1 mm3 pieces and placed in 10% DMSO + 90% FBS and stored in liquid nitrogen.

Tumor intrahepatic orthotopic inoculation

Under general anesthesia, a midline incision was made in a separate group of C57BL/6 mice. The left liver lobe was exposed after making incisions in the skin and peritoneum. A 2 mm deep incision was created in the left liver lobe, and a single 1 mm3 tumor piece was inserted into the liver parenchyma of each mouse. GELITA-CEL (Gelita Medical, GC-507) a hemostatic dressing, was applied to cover the inoculation site, the peritoneum and skin were sutured and closed.

Preparation and insertion of 224Ra-loaded sources

Stainless steel (316 LVM) Alpha-DaRT sources with a diameter of 0.37 mm and a length of 10 mm were either inert or loaded with 224Ra atoms. The loading process involved electrostatic collection, similar to the method described in reference9. The radium was fixed to the source using a subsequent heat treatment. The desorption probability of 220Rn (the probability of 220Rn atom emission from the source following 224Ra decay) was approximately 45%. The activity of 224Ra was approximately 2 µCi on the day of treatment. Mice were randomly selected from the cage and alternately assigned to either radioactive or inert treatment groups. Sources were implanted using a 21-gauge needle, which was attached to an insertion applicator.

MRI protocol

Tumor live imaging was conducted using an animal MRI system at the Research Institute McGill University Health Centre (RI-MUHC) Small Animal Imaging Laboratory (SAIL). The Bruker 7T MRI Biospec 70/30 USR system was used, and for the scans, a rat head surface coil was utilized. T2-weighted imaging was performed using a Turbo RARE (Rapid Acquisition with Relaxation Enhancement) pulse sequence. The following scan parameters were employed: TE/TR (Echo Time/Repetition Time) = 18/3500 ms, excitation angle = 90°, axial slice orientation, slice thickness = 0.5 mm, respiration gating, fat suppression, resolution = 0.156 mm × 0.156 mm, and an average of 4 scans. The animals undergoing MRI scans were placed under general anesthesia, and their vital signs were continuously monitored throughout the procedure. MRI images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Positron emission tomography: computed tomography (PET CT)

PET CT scans were performed to confirm tumor metabolic uptake, and to validate that the source was located within the tumor. All PET/CT experiments were performed on nanoScan PET/CT for small animals (Mediso Medical Imaging Systems, Budapest, Hungary) at the RI-MUHC SAIL platform. The radiotracer 18F-FDG was performed by a bolus injection in the tail vein (5 MBq in 50 µl), and animals were kept awake for 45 min before the scan. Afterwards, they were placed in the scanner, with the brain at the center of the field of view. Each PET/CT session consisted of a 30-min PET emission scan, followed by a 10-min CT transmission scan. PET/CT scans were conducted under anesthesia (isoflurane 2%, medical air (0.6 L/min) delivered by a nose cone. Temperature and heart rate were monitored throughout the procedure using the Mediso system.

Tumor size measurement

Tumor size was calculated based on the MRI DICOM files or based on measurements extracted manually from MRI DICOM files using the following formulas: area = Vertical × horizontal diameter; Volume = π /6 × horizontal × vertical × superior-inferior. Tumor length in the source’s insertion day was obtained by manual measurement of the tumor with a digital caliper.

Histopathology

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) mouse livers with orthotopically implanted tumors treated with Alpha-DaRT radioactive sources or control inert sources were used in these experiments and processed as previously described26. Following complete decay of radioactivity in the specimens (~4 weeks), and sources removal, immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) was performed36. Briefly, tumors were washed in PBS, fixed in formalin (Epredia 10% Neutral Buffered Formalin, Fisher Scientific, 22-050-104) for 24 hours and placed in 70% ethanol until processed, and embedded in paraffin (Surgipath Paraplast Plus, Lecia Biosystems, 39601006). The blocks were cut into 4-μm serial sections and mounted on charged glass slides (Superfrost Plus; ThermoFisher Scientific, 1255015), baked at 37 °C overnight, and stored at 4°C until staining. The first serial section of each block was then stained with Hematoxylin (Fisher Scientific, #SH26-4D) and Eosin (Anatech Ltd Eosin-Y, Fisher Scientific, NC9686037) for initial histopathological assessment. Slides were visualized using the Aperio software (ver.11.2.0.780) following scanning with the Aperio AT Turbo System.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Previously cut and mounted 4-μm thick sections of FFPE murine liver and tumors were baked at 60 °C for 1h prior to performing immunohistochemistry (IHC) using the following antibodies: F4/80 (70076, Cell Signaling Technology) at a dilution 1:500, and CD8 (ab217344 Abcam, ab217344) at a dilution of 1:2500. The sections were deparaffinized with xylene (Leica Biosystems, #3803665) and rehydrated with 100% and 95% ethanol (Fisher Scientific, A962-4) and double-distilled water (ddH2O). Heat induced antigen retrieval for 20 minutes in a steamer was done using Sodium Citrate (10 mM, pH 6.0) and Tris/EDTA (10 mM, pH 9.0) for F4/80 and CD8, respectively. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using Dual Endogenous Enzyme Block (Dako, #S2003). Sections were then incubated with blocking solution (5% goat serum in 1xPBST) for 1h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. The following day, the sections were washed using 1x PBS supplemented with Tween (Thermo Fisher, #BP-337) and incubated with a secondary antibody Dako EnVision+System-HRP Labelled Polymer Anti-Rabbit (Dako, #K4003) for 1 hour at room temperature. After washing, the sections were incubated with diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (Dako, Burlington, ON, Canada, #K3468) for 5 minutes, counterstained with Hematoxylin for 2 minutes, washed with distilled water and dehydrated with ethanol and xylene. Coverslips were then mounted using Permount Mounting Medium (Fisher Scientific, SP15-100) . Slides were viewed using the Aperio software (ver.11.2.0.780) following scanning with the Aperio AT Turbo System.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence, similar steps were taken to deparaffinize and rehydrate the samples. Heat induced antigen retrieval was performed using Tris/EDTA (10 mM, pH 9.0), permeabilization using PBST buffer (PBS/0.5% Triton-X100) and then slides were blocked using PBST with 5% goat serum for one hour. Following this both primary and conjugated antibodies: F4/80 (70076, Cell Signaling Technology) at a dilution 1:400, CD206-AF555 (Cell Signaling Technology, 76220) at a dilution 1:200, iNOS-AF647 (Abcam, ab209027) at a dilution of 1:50 were applied for two hours. Slides were then washed with 1x PBST and the secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, ab150077), was applied for 1 hour followed by DAPI for 10 minutes (ThermoFisher Scientific, 62248,) at a dilution 1:1000 at RT. The slides were then mounted using Fluoromount (Invitrogen, 5018788) and images were captured using the Zeiss LSM780 Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope.

IHC quantification

All IHC slides were scanned at 20x magnification using the Aperio AT Turbo System and images were viewed using QuPath ver.0.4.4 as described previously36. For each block of tumor and adjacent liver 3 areas of three different regions were analyzed: within the tumor, in the adjacent normal liver and at the interface- defined as the total width of about 250 µm of tumor and liver parenchyma in the tumor margins.

To calculate the number of F4/80+ and CD8+ stained cells, QuPath ver0.4.4 was used. The slides were analyzed with the positive cell detection setting and the intensity threshold parameters set to 0.4 and the smooth boundaries setting disabled for all slides.20,36Additionally, the number of positive cells per mm2 was automatically generated by the QuPath positive cell detection function and the average of the three areas selected in triplicate for each region of interest was averaged.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

A TUNEL assay which allows for the recognition of apoptotic nuclei in FFPE tissue was performed on both radioactive and inert murine liver and tumors. For the labeling reaction, 1μl TdT Enzyme was added to 39μl TdT Labeling Reaction Mix and placed on each specimen which were then covered with a coverslip to avoid evaporation. Slides were then incubated in a humid chamber for 1.5 hours. To end the labeling reaction, the slides were submerged in 1× TBS to remove the coverslip. Following this, Stop Buffer was warmed to 37 °C for 5 minutes, and each slide was covered in Stop Buffer for 5 minutes. Slides were then rinsed in 1× TBS and then incubated with Blocking Buffer for 10 min. The 25× conjugate was diluted 1/10 in Blocking Buffer and slides were then covered with the diluted 1× conjugate for 30 minutes and then rinsed in 1× TBS for 5 minutes. DAB solution 1 was then added to DAB solution 2 at a 1/30 dilution and applied to the slides and incubated for 15 minutes, slides were then rinsed for 5 minutes in dH20. To conclude the assay, Methyl Green Counterstain was added to each slide for 3 minutes and slides were subsequently submerged in 100% ethanol and xylene. Glass coverslips were then mounted onto the slides using Permount Mounting Medium (Fisher Scientific, SP15-100). The analysis of the TUNEL assay was done using QuPath ver0.4.4. Three areas of identical size were selected in the liver parenchyma and analyzed with the Hematoxylin H-DAB setting and the positive cell detection tool. The nucleus parameter sigma value was set to 1.5µm. Optical density sum was used for analysis; the intensity threshold was set to 0.5 and nucleus DAB OD max setting was used to analyze each slide. The output of the three regions of interest was combined and averaged to obtain the number of positive cells per mm2 per section. Slides were viewed using the Aperio software (ver.11.2.0.780) following scanning with the Aperio AT Turbo System. A pathologist was consulted to confirm the findings of the TUNEL assay and to assess the integrity of the liver parenchyma.

Statistical analysis

This study was planned as a feasibility study and, as such, was not powered to test a pre-specified hypothesis. That said, a sample size of minimum 5 animals per group for statistical analysis was chosen according to standard practice for such pre-clinical trials to expose a minimal sample for evaluating potential meaningful differences37.

Data from both groups were initially assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, supplemented by visual inspection of Q-Q plots and histograms. For data that adhered to a normal distribution, parametric statistical tests were used. Specifically, an independent samples two-tailed t-test was performed to compare the radioactive and inert groups. All technically successful stained samples were used in the statistical analyses. The individual values are plotted in the chart bar for each stain type and the numbers of samples used in the analysis are specified in the figure’s legends. Data is summarized with a mean ± standard error (SEM). Correlation between two continuous variables was assessed with Pearson’s correlation coefficient. When statistical comparisons are performed, a P-value of 0.05 or less were considered statistically significant. Nominal unadjusted p-values are presented as they are considered descriptive in this feasibility study. All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism V10.6.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) or Microsoft Excel V2601 (Microsoft Canada Inc, Mississauga, ON, Canada) or SAS® V9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).
