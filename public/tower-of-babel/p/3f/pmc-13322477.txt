Patient-Specific Medial Patellofemoral Ligament Femoral Footprint Mapping Using Compressed Lateral and Sagittal Sequence MRI in Patients With Trochlear Dysplasia
Authors: E. Alonso Domenech, G. Thürig, M. Barrera Uso, R. Panadero-Morales, J. López-Pascual, M. Tannast, J.M. Schwab, D. Petek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Accurate identification of the medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) femoral footprint is essential for successful MPFL reconstruction. Standard tunnel placement techniques, such as the Schöttle method, rely on fixed sagittal radiographic landmarks and may not account for morphological patient variability, particularly in patients with trochlear dysplasia. The Compressed Lateral and Sagittal Sequence (CLASS) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) method has been validated for assessing the native MPFL insertion and has been used to map its location in normal knees. However, it has not yet been applied to assess anatomical variability in dysplastic knees. The objective of this study was (1) to determine whether the MPFL femoral footprint differs between knees with and without trochlear dysplasia and (2) to assess whether the position of the MPFL footprint in trochlear dysplasia differs from the Schöttle point.Methods:MRI data from 58 dysplastic knees and 80 control knees were analyzed. The femoral MPFL footprint was identified using the CLASS method and projected onto a 2D coordinate system defined by the posterior femoral cortex (y-axis) and a perpendicular line intersecting the most posterior point of Blumensaat’s line (x-axis). Statistical comparisons were made between groups and relative to the Schöttle reference point. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.Results:The femoral MPFL footprint in trochlear dysplasia was more anterior (2.05 ± 3.61 mm vs. 0.83 ± 3.39 mm; p = 0.044) and distal (–1.84 ± 4.09 mm vs. 1.44 ± 2.66 mm; p < 0.001) compared with knees without dysplasia. When referenced to the Schöttle point, a distal deviation was observed (mean difference: 4.84 ± 4.09 mm; p < 0.001), while there was no anteroposterior difference (0.75 ± 3.61 mm; p = 0.119).Conclusion:Trochlear dysplasia alters the femoral MPFL footprint, producing a significant systematic distal displacement compared with both normal knees and the Schöttle point, with a mean distal difference of 3.28 mm vs. normal knees and 4.84 mm relative to Schöttle point, emphasizing the anatomical variability overlooked by standardized landmarks. This supports the use of the CLASS algorithm for individualized tunnel placement in MPFL reconstruction.Level of Evidence:Level IV, diagnostic. Introduction

The medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) is the main passive stabilizer of the patella during the first 30° of flexion1-3. It is disrupted in more than 90% of patients following lateral patellar dislocation, and without surgical treatment, recurrence rates can reach up to 44%.4,5 MPFL reconstruction has therefore become the standard approach for recurrent patellar instability.6-8 However, accurate femoral tunnel placement is important in MPFL reconstruction, as nonanatomic positioning may alter graft length-change behavior, patellofemoral kinematics, and contact pressures.4,9-11

Fixed radiographic landmarks of the MPFL, such as the Schöttle point (Fig. 1), may not consistently represent its anatomical origin across different knee anatomies.12-19 Small deviations from the native insertion site can alter graft tension and lead to abnormal patellofemoral kinematics, increasing the risk of its failure.9,20-23 This highlights the interindividual variability in MPFL anatomy and the limitations of a fixed landmark approach.12-19 Recent advances in imaging enable more precise, individualized anatomical assessment. The Compressed Lateral and Sagittal Sequences (CLASS) method is a validated magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based technique that projects volumetric knee data onto standardized lateral radiographic views using reproducible anatomical landmarks such as the posterior cortex and Blumensaat’s line.24-27 As a result, CLASS enables individualized 2D mapping of the femoral MPFL insertion. The image-processing workflow used by the CLASS method is illustrated in Appendix, Figure A1. Barrera Uso et al.12 demonstrated considerable variability in the MPFL footprint among anatomically normal knees, underscoring the importance of patient-specific mapping.

Trochlear dysplasia, characterized by a flattening of the trochlear groove, is one of the main anatomical risk factors for recurrent patellar instability.26-30 Whether trochlear dysplasia further affects the position of the femoral MPFL insertion, and its alignment with the Schöttle point, remains unclear.

Based on this background, we aimed to address the following research questions.1 How does the position of the MPFL femoral footprint, according to CLASS, differ between knees with and without trochlear dysplasia?2 How does the position of the MPFL femoral footprint, according to CLASS, compare with the Schöttle Point in knees with trochlear dysplasia?

Methods

Patient Selection

This retrospective study was approved by the local ethics committee (Approval No. 2024-00003) with a waiver for informed consent. A prior sample size calculation was performed for the primary study aim of comparing MPFL coordinates between dysplastic and nondysplastic knees. Assuming an alpha level of 0.05, a known mean distance from the posterior cortex of 3.66 ± 1.83 mm, and 80% power, a sample of 50 knees was estimated to detect a difference of 0.73 mm. A retrospective search of the institutional radiology database at the Department of Orthopaedic Surgery and Traumatology, Fribourg Hospital, University of Fribourg, Switzerland, was performed to identify knee MRIs performed in patients with patellofemoral instability and trochlear dysplasia between January 2017 and December 2024, identifying a total of 89 knees (83 patients). Exclusion criteria were applied as follows: poor image quality (n = 23), open distal femoral physis (n = 4), presence of osteoarthritis defined on available plain radiographs as Kellgren–Lawrence grade ≥ 2 (n = 3), withdrawn informed consent (n = 1), history of knee joint fracture (n = 0), prior knee surgery (n = 0), or childhood knee deformity (n = 0). After applying these criteria, a final sample of 58 knees (54 patients) was included for analysis. (Appendix, Figure A2)

Study Group Definition

Dysplastic knees were classified according to the Dejour classification system based on MRI findings31. Of the included knees, 7 were classified as type A, 20 as type B, 14 as type C, and 17 as type D. The mean age of the dysplasia group was 20 ± 7 years, and 65% were female. Race and ethnicity were not recorded in the source database and therefore could not be assessed or analyzed in this study.

For comparison with nondysplastic knees, a control group of 80 anatomically normal knees was used from a previously published institutional database12. The control group had a mean age of 33 ± 11 years, and 56% were female. There was a statistically significant age difference between groups (p < 0.001), while gender and laterality distributions were comparable (p = 0.32; p = 0.054). (Table I)

Imaging Technique

All MRI scans were acquired using a standardized protocol on a 1.5T Optima MR360 Advance scanner (GE Healthcare), including a sagittal proton density fat-saturated isotropic 3D sequence with an isovoxel resolution of 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.6 mm.

The femoral attachment of the MPFL, the adductor tubercle, and the medial epicondyle were independently identified by 2 orthopaedic surgeons (G. Thürig and E. Alonso Domenech) each with more than 4 years of experience in musculoskeletal MRI using the multiplanar reformatting tool in Materialise Mimics software (Materialise NV).

Anatomical landmarks were identified following the methodology described by Dirim et al.32 and verified in compressed 2D radiographic-like reconstructions of the knee in lateral and anteroposterior views, created using the CLASS algorithm.

The MPFL femoral insertion point was then translated into a 2D coordinate system based on the y-axis, distance from the posterior femoral cortex (Fig. 2, Line 1) and the x-axis, distance from the posterior edge of Blumensaat’s line (Fig. 2, Line 2). These coordinates were used for subsequent comparisons.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.0 [2025-04-11]). Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To address our first study aim, the comparison of CLASS-derived MPFL femoral insertion coordinates (x and y) between the control and dysplasia groups, independent samples t tests were used for normally distributed variables with equal variances, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were applied otherwise. The Levene test was performed to assess the equality of variances. A heatmap of the anatomical landmarks was generated in R by plotting the coordinate density of each landmark on the standardized 2D reference system.

To address the second study aim, assessing whether the femoral insertion in dysplastic knees deviates from the Schöttle point, we computed the coordinate differences (delta) between each CLASS-derived insertion point and the reference Schöttle point. These deltas were analyzed using 1-sample t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, depending on data normality, to test whether the mean deviation differed significantly from zero. The results are reported as mean ± SD (range), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Accurate femoral tunnel placement is a critical determinant of successful MPFL reconstruction, as deviations from the native footprint can disrupt graft isometry and alter patellofemoral biomechanics. These alterations may increase cartilage stress and contribute to chondral injury, potentially requiring concomitant treatment.4,9-11,20-22,33 While the Schöttle point remains the most widely used reference for tunnel placement, it assumes a uniform MPFL insertion among all patients34. A wide range of anatomical and imaging studies has investigated the femoral MPFL footprint, collectively demonstrating heterogeneous results (Appendix, Table A1). More recently, substantial interindividual variability has been reported even among anatomically normal knees.12 This raised the question whether trochlear dysplasia influences the location of the MPFL footprint and its alignment with the Schöttle point. This study expands upon these findings by evaluating whether trochlear dysplasia, a recognized morphological risk factor for patellar instability, influences the position of the MPFL femoral insertion.

The primary aim of our study was to determine whether the position of the MPFL femoral footprint, as assessed by CLASS, differs between knees with and without trochlear dysplasia. Our findings revealed a consistent directional shift in dysplastic knees, characterized by a mean anterior displacement of 1.22 mm and a mean distal displacement of 3.28 mm compared with control knees (Fig. 2). This suggests that trochlear morphology itself may influence footprint location, underscoring the importance of accounting for underlying anatomy in surgical planning. However, patellofemoral instability in dysplastic knees is often multifactorial, and associated factors such as patella alta or rotational malalignment were not independently analyzed in this study. In addition, although a difference in the location may be relevant, its direct clinical relevance in the setting of femoral tunnel placement cannot be determined from the present data.

The second research question addressed whether the CLASS-defined MPFL footprint in dysplastic knees deviates from the Schöttle point. We observed a significant mean distal shift of 4.84 mm, whereas the mean anteroposterior difference was 0.75 mm and did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3). This distal displacement may reflect both altered trochlear morphology and changes in the spatial relationship between bony landmarks used to define the reference point. These findings reinforce the need for individualized mapping strategies in surgical planning, particularly for patients with dysplasia.

There are several limitations to this study. Although the overall sample size is among the largest MRI-based cohorts investigating MPFL anatomy, the number of knees within each Dejour subtype was insufficient to support a robust subgroup analysis according to dysplasia severity. As a result, we could not determine whether the location of the footprint differs between different degrees of trochlear dysplasia. Future studies with larger subtype-specific cohorts are needed to address this question. The CLASS method, while validated in anatomically normal knees, has not been externally validated in dysplastic cases; however, as it relies on patient-specific anatomical references, its application in this context is conceptually reasonable. In addition, distal femoral morphology, including the size and shape of the medial femoral condyle, was not independently assessed and may have influenced the relative position of the measured footprint coordinates. Another limitation is that femoral torsion was not assessed. Increased femoral torsion may influence the position and reliability of radiographic landmarks for MPFL reconstruction13. Because standard knee MRI does not capture the full femoral axis, torsional parameters could not be evaluated. Future studies incorporating whole-limb imaging protocols should address this variable. This was a purely imaging-based anatomical study, and therefore, no direct conclusions can be drawn regarding functional outcomes or graft behavior. In addition, given the cross-sectional imaging design, this study could not determine whether the altered femoral footprint is causally involved in patellar instability or simply associated with dysplastic anatomy. In addition, the analysis was performed by orthopaedic surgeons with anatomical expertise, but not by musculoskeletal radiologists, which may introduce observer bias. Finally, there was a statistically significant age difference between groups, which could influence anatomical development, although its clinical relevance in adult skeletal anatomy remains uncertain.

Finally, the authors recognize that while the CLASS method appears to provide precise patient-specific localization of the MPFL femoral footprint, this study does not establish whether reconstruction in dysplastic knees should prioritize the MRI-defined anatomical footprint or the most isometric femoral point. The findings from our study, however, provide a potential additional data point to be considered by the surgeon, specifically in the cases where an MPFL reconstruction using traditional localization methods for graft anchoring points, has failed. Until further clinical data are available to support MPFL reconstruction using the MPFL footprint derived from CLASS, our findings support the need for a critical evaluation of how we identify optimal graft placement. In addition, we did not evaluate how trochleoplasty or subsequent CLASS-guided reconstruction might influence patellofemoral tracking or contact pressures. Further validation is required to assess patellofemoral tracking and contact pressures in CLASS-guided ligament reconstruction. This would help determine whether the CLASS-defined insertion truly represents the optimal site for reconstruction. Moreover, clinical outcome data following MPFL reconstruction guided by the CLASS algorithm are not yet available, and future prospective studies are necessary to evaluate its therapeutic impact. Despite these limitations, the present findings highlight the considerable anatomical variability in the MPFL insertion, particularly in the setting of trochlear dysplasia, where the footprint was found to be located more distally compared with both nondysplastic knees and the classical Schöttle point. These results underscore the potential shortcomings of standardized radiographic landmarks in accurately guiding femoral tunnel placement. The CLASS algorithm offers a reliable, MRI-based method to identify individualized MPFL anatomy, and may serve as a useful patient-specific anatomical planning tool in patellofemoral instability.

In conclusion, the femoral MPFL footprint is altered in the setting of trochlear dysplasia, producing a systematic distal displacement compared with both normal knees and the Schöttle point, emphasizing the anatomical variability overlooked by standardized landmarks. The CLASS MRI-based method enables patient-specific mapping of the MPFL insertion and may serve as a useful anatomical planning tool in MPFL reconstruction in patients with patellofemoral instability and trochlear dysplasia.
