Nrf2 attenuates neuroinflammatory injury in intracerebral hemorrhage via an ASK1/JNK-related signaling pathway
Authors: Yuting Gu, Hua Yuan, Guang Zhao, Yuyang Chen, Zhiqiang Guo, Mei Zeng, Qiupeng Feng, Ping Zhou, Zhe Chen, Hua Liu, Xiaohua Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a clinically prevalent cerebrovascular disorder in which neuroinflammation is a major contributor to secondary brain injury. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a key regulator of intracellular redox homeostasis, whereas apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1) is involved in stress-associated inflammatory and apoptotic signaling. However, the relationship between Nrf2 signaling and ASK1/JNK pathway activity in ICH-related inflammatory injury remains incompletely understood. In this study, we established a rat ICH model and used molecular, histological, and behavioral approaches to examine the association between Nrf2 signaling and ASK1/JNK-related neuroinflammatory changes. We found that ICH was accompanied by increased Nrf2 and phosphorylated ASK1 expression. siRNA-mediated modulation of Nrf2 or ASK1 was associated with reciprocal changes in ASK1/JNK pathway activation. In parallel, changes in CD80/CD206 expression and inflammatory cytokine levels suggested that Nrf2 signaling was associated with altered inflammation-related responses, potentially involving ASK1/JNK pathway activity. Histological analyses, including TUNEL staining, Nissl staining, and brain water content measurement, further showed that preservation of Nrf2 signaling was associated with reduced neuronal injury and cerebral edema after ICH. Behavioral assessments showed corresponding changes in locomotor and anxiety-related performance. Overall, these findings support an association between Nrf2 signaling and ASK1/JNK-related neuroinflammatory responses following ICH and provide a basis for further mechanistic investigation of this pathway in hemorrhagic brain injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52793-0.

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a critical cerebrovascular condition associated with high mortality, disability, and recurrence rates1,2. Its poor prognosis is largely attributed to early brain injury (EBI)3,4. EBI involves a series of multidimensional pathophysiological cascades, including excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and activation of neuroinflammatory pathways, all of which contribute to neurological deterioration. Extravasated blood components (e.g., erythrocytes, leukocytes, macrophages, and hemoglobin) infiltrate the brain parenchyma through a compromised blood-brain barrier (BBB), activating resident microglia and astrocytes, thereby triggering neuroinflammatory cascades5–7. Activated immune cells release pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and free radicals, which ultimately cause irreversible damage to the neurovascular unit-manifested as BBB disruption, cytotoxic edema, and neuronal death8. Importantly, the dynamic balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory responses during EBI underscores the need for therapeutic strategies that modulate excessive inflammation and restore immune homeostasis.

Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), a master regulator of cellular redox homeostasis, mitigates post-ICH brain injury by transactivating downstream antioxidant and detoxifying genes9. Preclinical studies have shown that targeting the Keap1/Nrf2 axis significantly ameliorates neurological damage in ICH models10. While Nrf2 activation in macrophages provides protection through enhanced phagocytosis, oxidative stress suppression, and blockade of pro-inflammatory signaling, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood11,12. Apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1) acts as a critical node in orchestrating neuroinflammation and apoptosis by polarizing microglia/macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, thereby exacerbating neuronal death13–15. In subarachnoid hemorrhage models, inhibition of the ASK1/MAPK pathway has been shown to effectively reduce neuronal apoptosis and improve outcomes16,17. Given the pivotal roles of Nrf2 and ASK1 in neuroinflammation, their potential interaction in modulating ICH prognosis remains unexplored. To date, no studies have elucidated the regulatory crosstalk between these two factors in ICH pathogenesis.

Based on preliminary evidence, we propose the novel hypothesis that “Nrf2 suppresses post-ICH neuronal inflammation by negatively regulating the ASK1/JNK signaling cascade” and aim to systematically validate this mechanism through integrated experimentation.

Methods

Animal

Female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats of specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade, weighing approximately 240–260 g, were used in this study. The animal facility maintained controlled ambient temperature, humidity, and noise levels, with rats housed in individually ventilated cages (IVC) at a density of 4–5 animals per cage. Throughout the experiment, the rodents had ad libitum access to standard rodent chow, water, and bedding materials, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted with continuous access to food and water. The animal protocols for this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of The First People’s Hospital of Kunshan and strictly adhered to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines for animal care and use.

ICH Model

Following intraperitoneal anesthesia with pentobarbital sodium, rats were secured in a stereotaxic apparatus. After disinfection, a midline scalp incision was made to expose the skull, and periosteal tissue was removed to visualize the bregma. Hemostasis was achieved prior to drilling a 2-mm diameter cranial window (coordinates: 0.2 mm anterior to bregma, 3.5 mm lateral, 5.5 mm deep) using a high-speed dental drill until the dura mater was visible, targeting the striatal region. A microinfusion pump was used to slowly inject collagenase into the site, with the needle remaining in situ for 5 min post-injection before gradual withdrawal. The craniotomy was sealed with bone wax, and the wound was closed meticulously. Hematoma formation at the injection site was visually confirmed to ensure consistency. All procedures were performed under aseptic conditions, with postoperative monitoring until full recovery.

Transfection

To achieve targeted suppression of Nrf2 and ASK1 expression, rats received daily intracerebroventricular (ICV) injections of siRNA complexes (2µL/day) for three consecutive days prior to ICH induction. Using stereotaxic guidance, Lipofectamine™ 3000-complexed siRNAs were infused into the lateral ventricle using a microsyringe. The stereotaxic coordinates (relative to bregma) were: anteroposterior (AP) = 0.8 mm, mediolateral (ML) = 1.5 mm (right), and dorsoventral (DV) = 2.4 mm (measured from the dura after the burr hole was made. The needle was left in situ for 10 min post-injection to prevent reflux, followed by slow withdrawal. Surgical sites were sealed with bone wax, disinfected with povidone-iodine, and sutured in layers. The siRNA sequences used were: si-Nrf2 (5′-GCAGGACAUGGAUUUGAUUTT-3′) and si-ASK1 (5′-GCAGACGAUUGGCUUCUAUTT-3′).

Western Blot

Perihematomal brain tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer to obtain total protein lysates, and equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred to nitrocellulose (NC) membranes via semi-dry transfer. After blocking with 3% BSA, membranes were incubated with diluted primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following TBST washes, the membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at 37 °C. After additional washes, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and quantified by densitometry.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Perihematomal brain tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 4 µg RNA per sample was reverse-transcribed into cDNA and performed qPCR analysis. Primer sequences for target genes were obtained from PrimerBank. Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To assess inflammatory cytokines after ICH, levels of TNF-α (ab236712) and IL-6 (ab234570) were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s protocols. For plasma cytokine measurements, whole blood was collected from the tail vein into anticoagulant-coated tubes. Samples were collected and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Flow Cytometry

After washing with PBS, microglial cells were resuspended to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL, Cell suspensions were transferred to microcentrifuge tubes and incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated primary antibodies against CD80 and CD206. Unbound antibodies were removed by PBS washes, followed by incubation with diluted fluorochrome-labeled secondary antibodies under light-protected conditions. After final washes, cells were resuspended in PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry. The percentage of CD206⁺ cells and the CD206/CD80 mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) ratio were quantified using FlowJo™ software (v10.8.1).

ELISA

Nissl staining

Following intraperitoneal pentobarbital sodium anesthesia, rats underwent transcardial perfusion, initiated by thoracotomy and insertion of a cannula through the left ventricular apex into the ascending aorta. Sequential perfusion was performed with saline (0.9%) followed by ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were decapitated, post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, and rinsed under running tap water. Tissues were dehydrated through graded ethanols (70% → 80% → 90% → 95% → 100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin at 60 °C before being stored at 4 °C.

Serial 5-µm sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanols (100% → 95% → 80% → 70% → ddH₂O), and stained with 1% cresyl violet/thionin. After rinsing in ddH₂O, sections were differentiated in 70% ethanol under microscopic monitoring, dehydrated (70% → 80% → 95% ethanol, 5 min each), cleared in absolute ethanol (2 × 5 min), and xylene (2 × 10 min), then mounted with DPX. Nissl bodies staining deep purple, and cell nuclei appeared pale purple under light microscopy.

TUNEL staining

Following xylene deparaffinization and graded ethanol rehydration (100% → 95% → 90% → 80% → 70%, 1 min per step), sections were digested with Proteinase K at room temperature. For TUNEL labeling, experimental groups received 50 µL of TdT enzyme mixed with 450µL of fluorescein-dUTP, while control groups included: (1) Negative control: 50µL of fluorescein-dUTP only; (2) Positive control: 100 µL of DNase I pretreatment (10 min, room temperature) followed by the TUNEL mixture. After air-drying, sections were incubated with 50 µL of the reaction mixture under coverslips in a humidified dark chamber at 37 °C for 1 h. Sections were then washed 3 times with PBS (5 min per wash) and imaged using fluorescence microscopy (488/530 nm), with DAPI counterstaining for apoptotic cell quantification.

Open-field-test (OFT)

Prior to experimentation, the open field arena was thoroughly cleaned to remove any fecal residues and urine from previous trials, ensuring an odor-neutral environment. Behavioral tracking software (e.g., ANY-maze™) was set up with preset parameters, and subject details (animal ID, date, health status) were documented. Animals were gently transferred from their home cages using cupped-handling techniques, ensuring dorsal orientation toward the experimenter. Each subject was then placed in the central quadrant of the arena (50 × 50 cm zone), and the experimenter promptly exited the testing room. Video recording began immediately for a standardized 15-minute session, during which automated tracking recorded locomotor parameters (e.g., total distance, center duration).

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

Prior to testing, animals were acclimated to the laboratory environment for at least 3 h in their home cages with ad libitum food and water to minimize novel-environment stress. During testing, rodents (rats/mice) were gently transferred using dorsal-orientation handling and placed at the central hub of the elevated plus maze (EPM), facing an open arm. The experimenter then exited the room immediately and quietly. Behavioral tracking commenced via SuperMaze/VisuTrack software for 5 min, recording the following parameters: Open arm entries (OE%) = (open arm entries / total arm entries) × 100%; Open arm time ratio = (time spent in open arms / total time) × 100%.

CT/MRI examination

After the ICH model was established, hematoma volume was calculated using MRI/CT scans (slice thickness 0.5 mm). The hematoma volume was estimated using the formula: Hematoma volume = 1/2 × length × width × number of slices.

Neurological function score

According to the modified Bederson score, a score of 0 indicates almost normal function of the forelimb nerve. A score of 0.5 points reflects flexion of both the anterior and posterior limbs, with a positive tail lift suspension test. A score of 1 point indicates any flexion of the forelimb, while 1.5 points refers to flexion of only one forelimb. A score of 2 points is assigned when there is decreased resistance to a lateral push (positive in the lateral push test) along with forelimb flexion. A score of 2.5 points is given when resistance to the lateral push is further reduced, with both forelimb and hindlimb flexion, though turning behavior does not occur. A score of 3 is assigned when spontaneous rotation occurs, in addition to the behavior observed at score 2. Lower scores indicate less nerve damage, while higher scores indicate more severe nerve damage.

Brain water content measurement

After ICH, the entire brain was harvested through a craniotomy under deep anesthesia. The wet weight of the brain was then measured. Following this, brain samples were dried in an oven at 100 °C for 72 h to determine their dry weight. The percentage of brain water content was calculated using the formula: ((wet weight - dry weight) / wet weight) × 100%.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0) was used to calculate the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) for the data. One-way ANOVA and Two-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons or a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was performed to analyze the data sets. Formal correlation analyses were not performed because the present study was designed primarily for group-wise comparisons across predefined experimental conditions rather than for correlation-based inference using matched continuous variables. Probability (p) values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. ****P < 0.0001.

Results

ICH model significantly activates both Nrf2 and P-ASK1, with peak hematoma volume observed 48 to 72 h post-induction

As is well-established, the first week following intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a critical period characterized by peak cerebral edema development. This study aimed to investigate temporal changes in Nrf2 and phosphorylated ASK1 (P-ASK1) expression within this 7-day window using a rat ICH model. Western blot analysis revealed significant upregulation of both Nrf2 and P-ASK1 in the ICH group, indicating activation of these pathways due to hemorrhagic injury. Maximum expression levels were observed between 48 and 72-hour post-ICH (Fig. 1A-C). Supporting these findings, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed peak hematoma volume during the same 48-hour period (Fig. 1D and E). Based on this convergent temporal profile, 48 h was selected as the optimal time point for subsequent experimental observations.

Modulation of Nrf2 expression is associated with reciprocal changes in P-ASK1 signaling following ICH

To clarify the regulatory relationship between Nrf2 and ASK1 signaling after ICH, we first validated the effectiveness of siRNA-mediated knockdown under prolonged in vivo administration. Rats subjected to ICH received intracerebroventricular delivery of si-Nrf2 or si-ASK1, with mRNA and protein expression were examined at 1, 3, and 5days post-induction. We first confirmed that si-Nrf2 and si-ASK1 produced sustained and target-specific reductions in Nrf2 and ASK1 mRNA levels, respectively, indicating effective and persistent gene silencing throughout the experimental period (Fig. 2A-B). Knockdown efficiency in perihematomal tissue was validated at the protein level by western blot in Fig. 2C-D.

Having established the efficacy of siRNA intervention, we next examined alterations in ASK1 signaling activity. Compared with ICH controls, Nrf2 knockdown resulted in a marked increase in ASK1 phosphorylation (p-ASK1), whereas total ASK1 protein levels were only modestly affected. In contrast, ASK1 knockdown significantly reduced p-ASK1 levels, confirming ASK1 as the upstream kinase responsible for its phosphorylation. Notably, ASK1 depletion was also accompanied by an unexpected increase in Nrf2 protein abundance, suggesting that suppression of ASK1 signaling may enhance Nrf2 stability under hemorrhagic stress conditions (Figs. 2C-F). Together, these data indicate a reciprocal association between Nrf2 expression and ASK1 activation at the pathway level, in which loss of Nrf2 enhances P-ASK1 signaling, while attenuation of ASK1 activity is associated with stabilization of Nrf2 protein.

Consistent with the findings of Huang et al.18., ASK1 is a well-established upstream activator of the JNK pathway under oxidative and inflammatory stress. Therefore, to further assess the functional consequences of Nrf2–ASK1 interaction, we examined the phosphorylation status of JNK1 as a downstream signaling readout. Western blot analysis revealed that Nrf2 knockdown significantly enhanced JNK1 phosphorylation, consistent with the observed increase in P-ASK1 levels. Conversely, ASK1 knockdown markedly suppressed P-JNK1, concomitant with reduced ASK1 activity and elevated Nrf2 protein expression (Fig. 2G). This promotes Keap1 binding and ubiquitin-dependent degradation, ultimately suppressing Nrf2 activity and reducing antioxidant capacity. Conversely, ASK1 depletion alleviates this inhibition, stabilizing Nrf2. Based on these results, we propose that Nrf2 and P-ASK1 exhibit a potential reciprocal association at the signaling pathway level under hemorrhagic stress conditions.

Nrf2 attenuates the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines following ICH by modulating the P-ASK1/JNK signaling pathway

Neuroinflammation is a central pathological feature of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and critically contributes to secondary brain injury. Given the established role of Nrf2 in redox regulation and stress adaptation, we next examined whether Nrf2–ASK1 signaling is associated with inflammatory modulation following ICH. To assess inflammation-related changes at the cellular level, we first performed flow cytometry analysis of microglial activation markers. CD80 and CD206 were used as representative markers associated with pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory tendencies, respectively. Compared with sham controls, ICH induction resulted in a marked shift toward a CD80-enriched microglial profile. Notably, Nrf2 knockdown further increased the CD80/CD206 ratio, indicating a bias toward a more pro-inflammatory microglial state. In contrast, ASK1 knockdown significantly reduced the CD80/CD206 ratio, suggesting an attenuation of inflammation-associated microglial activation (Fig. 3A).

To further validate these observations at the molecular level, we quantified inflammatory cytokine expression in perihematomal brain tissue. qPCR analysis revealed that ICH induced robust upregulation of IL6 and TNFα mRNA, which was significantly exacerbated by Nrf2 knockdown. Conversely, ASK1 silencing partially suppressed the ICH-induced elevation of these cytokines, consistent with a dampened inflammatory response (Fig. 3B). Because neuroinflammation after ICH may extend beyond the local lesion site, we additionally assessed circulating cytokine levels using ELISA. In line with the localized nature of brain injury, plasma IL-6 and TNF-α levels exhibited more modest but significant increases following ICH. Importantly, Nrf2 knockdown further elevated circulating cytokine levels, whereas ASK1 knockdown partially reversed these changes, mirroring the trends observed in brain tissue (Fig. 3C).

Collectively, these findings indicate that Nrf2 and ASK1 signaling are associated with the regulation of inflammatory responses after ICH, with Nrf2 deficiency amplifying, and ASK1 suppression attenuating, both local and systemic inflammatory changes. Rather than defining a fixed microglial phenotype, our data support a model in which the Nrf2-ASK1 axis modulates inflammation-related signaling states following hemorrhagic brain injury.

Nrf2 is associated with reduced neuronal injury, cerebral edema, and improved functional outcomes following ICH

To determine whether modulation of Nrf2–ASK1 signaling influences tissue injury and functional recovery after intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), we assessed neuronal damage, cerebral edema, and neurological performance in parallel.

Neuronal injury was first evaluated using TUNEL and Nissl staining in perihematomal brain regions. Compared with sham controls, ICH induction resulted in pronounced neuronal loss and increased apoptotic cell death. Nrf2 knockdown significantly exacerbated neuronal damage, as evidenced by reduced Nissl-positive neurons and increased TUNEL-positive cells. In contrast, ASK1 knockdown was associated with attenuated neuronal injury, concomitant with elevated Nrf2 protein levels (Figs. 5A–B). These findings suggest that preservation of Nrf2 signaling is linked to reduced neuronal vulnerability following hemorrhagic injury.

Given the close relationship between structural brain injury and functional impairment, we further assessed neurological outcomes using a battery of behavioral tests. Imaging analysis demonstrated marked hematoma formation after ICH, which was accompanied by significant neurological deficits as reflected by hemiplegia index scores (Fig. 5A-B). We examined cerebral edema formation during the acute phase of ICH. Brain water content analysis revealed a significant increase in edema within the first week after ICH compared with sham animals. Notably, Nrf2 deficiency further aggravated edema severity, whereas ASK1 silencing partially alleviated brain water accumulation, indicating an association between Nrf2 signaling status and edema progression. Animals with ASK1 knockdown exhibited reduced hematoma burden and improved neurological scores, whereas Nrf2 knockdown was associated with worsened hemiparesis (Figs. 5C).

Consistent with these observations, open field testing showed that ICH markedly reduced locomotor activity and exploratory behavior. These deficits were exacerbated by Nrf2 knockdown but partially reversed by ASK1 silencing, as indicated by changes in total distance traveled. In addition, performance in the elevated plus maze test revealed that Nrf2 deficiency was associated with reduced open-arm exploration, while ASK1 knockdown promoted increased open-arm dwell time and alternation frequency, suggesting improved behavioral performance (Figs. 5D-E).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that modulation of Nrf2–ASK1 signaling is associated with coordinated changes in neuronal injury, cerebral edema, and neurological function following ICH. Rather than implying direct causality, our findings support a model in which preservation of Nrf2 signaling correlates with reduced tissue damage and improved functional outcomes during the acute phase of hemorrhagic brain injury.

Discussion

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a cerebrovascular disorder characterized by high incidence, disability, and mortality rates, affecting over 3 million people worldwide each year. It poses a significant burden on patients, families, and society19. ICH-induced injury includes both primary and secondary brain damage. Despite interventions such as hematoma evacuation surgery, blood pressure control, and anticoagulant therapy, the mortality rate for ICH remains as high as 68%20,21. As a result, increasing research has focused on the pathological processes underlying secondary brain injury. Neuroinflammation is recognized as a central mechanism driving brain injury and edema following ICH, and targeting inflammatory responses offers potential for mitigating ICH-induced brain damage22,23. Microglia, as resident immune cells in the central nervous system, play a crucial role in the pathophysiology of ICH24,25. In the context of ICH, microglia act as key drivers of neuroinflammation, and their activation status directly influences the progression of inflammatory responses and the severity of secondary brain injury26. Studies have shown that microglia depletion post-ICH reduces neuronal loss and alleviates vasospasm, whereas excessive microglial activation worsens neuronal damage27,28.

This study provides evidence supporting a potential reciprocal association between Nrf2 and ASK1 signaling in an ICH model, extending previous observations of Nrf2–ASK1/JNK interactions reported in other neurological and inflammatory disease contexts Specifically, Nrf2 activation significantly suppresses P-ASK1 expression, while ASK1 promotes Nrf2 ubiquitination and degradation through JNK pathway activation, forming a complex homeostatic feedback loop. This discovery aligns with findings by Huang et al. in other disease models18,29, yet holds particular significance in the context of ICH: during oxidative stress induced by cerebral hemorrhage, early-phase Nrf2 activation rapidly inhibits the ASK1/JNK pro-inflammatory axis, establishing an endogenous protective feedback loop. However, sustained stress triggers ASK1-mediated Nrf2 degradation, weakening cellular antioxidant defenses and initiating a harmful cycle. This bidirectional regulatory mechanism provides novel insights into the dynamic progression of secondary injury following ICH.

Notably, the role of ASK1 in neurological disorders exhibits remarkable cell-type specificity. Gao’s research group demonstrated in traumatic brain injury (TBI) studies that ASK1 in endothelial cells primarily activates JNK to promote apoptosis, while in microglia, it simultaneously activates both the JNK and p38 inflammatory pathways30. Our study further confirms that in ICH models, ASK1 predominantly regulates microglial polarization—ASK1 inhibition significantly increases the proportion of anti-inflammatory M2 microglia and reduces pro-inflammatory M1 microglia. This immunomodulatory effect outweighs its direct impact on neuronal apoptosis. These cell-specific regulatory mechanisms suggest that targeting ASK1 may offer broader neuroprotective effects by remodeling the neuroimmune microenvironment.

From a translational perspective, the central role of the Nrf2-ASK1 axis provides a theoretical foundation for combination therapy. Some researches indicates that TAK1 inhibitors alleviate oxidative stress and neuronal pyroptosis post-ICH through Nrf2 activation31. Our findings further demonstrate that ASK1 inhibition similarly relies on upstream Nrf2 regulation. This evidence positions Nrf2 as a convergent node integrating multiple stress pathways, making it an ideal therapeutic target for multi-target intervention strategies.

The regulation of neuroinflammation by Nrf2 through the ASK1/JNK pathway constitutes a central mechanistic finding of this study. Our data clearly demonstrate (Fig. 3) that Nrf2 knockdown enhances the activation of both P-ASK1 and downstream P-JNK, whereas ASK1 knockdown suppresses P-JNK activation, accompanied by Nrf2 upregulation. More importantly, flow cytometric analysis of microglial/macrophage phenotypes (Fig. 3B) provides direct physiological evidence: Nrf2 knockdown, which results in ASK1/JNK overactivation, significantly increases the proportion of pro-inflammatory M1 microglia; conversely, ASK1 knockdown, leading to Nrf2 upregulation and JNK inhibition, markedly promotes the polarization of anti-inflammatory M2 microglia. These results definitively establish that Nrf2, by suppressing the ASK1/JNK signaling axis, effectively drives the transition of microglia/macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby inhibiting the pro-inflammatory cytokine storm and mitigating neuroinflammatory damage.

The neuroprotective effects of the Nrf2/ASK1 axis were validated at multiple levels. Histological analysis showed that Nrf2 inhibition exacerbated neuronal apoptosis and Nissl body loss following ICH, while simultaneously worsening cerebral edema (Fig. 4). In contrast, ASK1 inhibition (via Nrf2 upregulation) significantly ameliorated these pathological changes. More importantly, these molecular and cellular protective effects translated into substantial improvements in neurological function (Fig. 5). These behavioral outcomes strongly highlight the preclinical potential of targeting the Nrf2/ASK1 axis to improve post-ICH neurological dysfunction.

Our neurobehavioral assessment identified a critical therapeutic window: Nrf2 activation produced the most significant improvement in motor function during the 48–72 h period post-ICH, which corresponds to the peak phase of secondary injury (Fig. 5C-E)32. The open field test and elevated plus maze primarily reflect locomotor activity and anxiety-related behavior, and thus should be interpreted cautiously in the context of overall neurological recovery after ICH. This temporal pattern closely mirrored the dynamics of cerebral edema—brain water content peaked at 72 h33, and Nrf2 activation effectively delayed edema progression (Fig. 4C). Limitations should be noted. First, although our study delineates the Nrf2-ASK1/JNK axis primarily in an in vivo ICH model where neuroimmune interactions are preserved, we did not include an in vitro hemorrhage-mimicking system (e.g., hemin/oxyhemoglobin stimulation in microglia or neuron–glia co-cultures) to further dissect cell-autonomous mechanisms under controlled conditions. Importantly, the present study did not directly test a biochemical interaction between Nrf2 and ASK1. In addition, rescue experiments or overexpression-based validation were not performed. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as supporting an association between Nrf2 signaling and ASK1/JNK-related activity in the ICH context, rather than establishing a direct molecular interaction or a definitive causal regulatory mechanism. Future studies will incorporate such in vitro paradigms to validate and refine the mechanistic framework alongside the in vivo findings. In addition, although reciprocal changes in Nrf2 and ASK1 signaling were observed, the present study does not establish a direct molecular interaction between these proteins. Future studies employing biochemical and genetic approaches will be required to delineate the precise mechanisms underlying this association.

In summary, this study uncovers a novel mechanism through which Nrf2 exerts its protective effects following ICH: by bidirectionally regulating ASK1 activation, Nrf2 negatively controls the ASK1/JNK signaling pathway, thereby effectively suppressing microglia-mediated neuroinflammation, reducing neuronal apoptosis and cerebral edema, and ultimately improving neurological outcomes. This discovery not only enhances our understanding of the inflammatory injury mechanisms following ICH but also provides a solid experimental foundation and a promising direction for developing combined therapeutic strategies based on “Nrf2 activation and ASK1 inhibition.”

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings support an association between Nrf2 signaling and ASK1/JNK-related neuroinflammatory responses following intracerebral hemorrhage. Changes in this pathway were accompanied by corresponding alterations in inflammation-associated markers, histological injury, cerebral edema, and behavioral outcomes. These results extend current understanding of secondary brain injury after ICH and provide a foundation for future mechanistic and intervention-focused studies.
