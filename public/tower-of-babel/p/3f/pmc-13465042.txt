Medical students with specific learning disabilities: mixed-methods systematic review of the prevalence, academic performance, challenges, and perceived impact of support interventions
Authors: Bernarda Pavletič, Milena Košak Babuder, Vesna Homar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpecific learning disabilities (SpLDs)—including dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia, and dyspraxia—affect approximately 5–8% of medical students. The demands of medical education may exacerbate these challenges, while stigma and inconsistent institutional support contribute to ongoing barriers. This mixed-methods systematic review synthesised evidence on prevalence, academic performance, challenges, and support needs to inform more inclusive medical education practices.MethodsA mixed-methods systematic review was conducted following Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology and PRISMA 2020 guidelines. PubMed and EBSCOhost were searched in October 2024. Primary studies on undergraduate medical students with formally diagnosed SpLDs were included. Fifteen studies met the inclusion criteria (eight quantitative, seven qualitative). Quantitative data were synthesised using meta-analysis and narrative approaches, qualitative data using meta-aggregation, and findings were integrated using a convergent segregated approach.ResultsThe prevalence of SpLDs among medical students ranged from 2% to 11.5% (mean 5.5%). Meta-analysis of three studies showed no statistically significant difference in multiple-choice assessment performance between students with and without SpLDs when accommodations, most commonly additional examination time, were provided (standardised mean difference d = 0.045; 95% CI: −0.094 to 0.183), although evidence was limited and context-dependent. Evidence for other assessment formats was inconsistent, with some indication of lower performance. Qualitative findings identified persistent academic challenges and psychosocial impacts, alongside unmet support needs, including early identification, flexible assessment design, structured mentoring, and increased staff awareness.ConclusionsLimited quantitative evidence suggests that medical students with SpLDs may achieve comparable performance in some accommodated MCQ-based assessments, but findings remain context-dependent. Qualitative findings consistently indicate substantial additional effort, emotional burden, and systemic barriers, suggesting that comparable outcomes may not necessarily reflect equitable learning conditions. More inclusive, flexible, and proactive educational approaches are needed to support equity, inclusion, and wellbeing in medical education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09574-0.

Background

Specific learning disabilities (SpLDs), including dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia, and dyspraxia, are neurodevelopmental disorders affecting information processing without lower intelligence or motivation [1, 2]. In higher education, SpLDs present unique challenges that require targeted support and appropriate educational adjustments to ensure equitable academic success [3, 4].

The reported prevalence of SpLDs has increased over recent decades, from 0.5 to 2.5% in studies of the late 20th and early 21st centuries [5, 6] to 4–6% in recent research among university students [7–9]. However, due to stigma and concerns about institutional understanding of these conditions, they remain underreported [10, 11].

In medical education, prevalence estimates range between 5 and 8% [12, 13]. The intensity and pace of the medical curriculum can exacerbate the impact of SpLDs, with previously effective compensatory strategies becoming insufficient [14, 15], thereby affecting not only academic performance but also emotional and social well-being [16]. Persistent myths and negative assumptions about disability in medicine [17, 18] continue to contribute to stigma [19], creating systemic barriers across the medical education continuum, from admissions to graduation [20].

Despite the increasing recognition of SpLDs in medical education [21–23], gaps in research and support systems persist, with systemic barriers emerging early in the admissions process [21]. Medical schools often lack consistent implementation of guidelines for educational adjustments, leading to variability in access and unequal support across institutions [24]. Since a comprehensive approach is not consistently implemented [25, 26], it is crucial to understand both the challenges and support needs to ensure equitable access, promote inclusion and prepare a diverse and competent medical workforce for the future.

This review aimed to synthesize existing evidence on the prevalence, academic performance, challenges, and perceived impact of support interventions among medical students (MS) with SpLDs, in order to identify barriers and inform inclusive educational practices in medical education. The specific objectives were to: (1) report the prevalence of SpLDs among MS across countries and institutions; (2) evaluate their academic performance compared to peers without SpLDs; (3) identify key academic, social and emotional challenges; and (4) explore students’ perceptions and experiences of support interventions.

Methods

Review registration and reporting guidelines

The review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for mixed-methods systematic reviews, using the Convergent Segregated approach for data synthesis and integration [27]. Reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [28]. The review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251053411 [29]. A completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Additional file 5. Following PROSPERO registration, the only modification made was a minor editorial revision of the review title to enhance clarity. No changes were made to the review objectives, eligibility criteria, methodological approach, or planned analyses.

Eligibility criteria for study inclusion

Eligibility criteria were defined using the PICO framework [30].

Included were: (1) primary studies (qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-method) on undergraduate MS with a formal SpLD diagnosis (e.g., dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia, dyspraxia); (2) studies examining experiences, challenges, barriers, support needs, or inclusive educational strategies; (3) studies conducted within undergraduate medical education (classroom teaching, clinical placements, or broader university learning specific to medicine); (4) peer-reviewed articles published in English, with no date restrictions.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) studies on general learning difficulties, cognitive impairments, or neurodevelopmental disorders without isolated SpLD data; (2) studies on ADHD as the sole diagnosis; (3) studies outside undergraduate medical education; and (4) non-English publications.

Information sources

Primary documents were identified through searches of the PubMed database [31] and the EBSCOhost research platform [32]. These sources were selected to capture literature spanning both biomedical and educational research domains relevant to undergraduate medical education. PubMed provides comprehensive coverage of biomedical and health professions education literature, while the EBSCOhost platform includes several education- and social science–focused databases relevant to disability and learning research. Within EBSCOhost, all available databases (excluding PubMed) were searched to maximise retrieval of relevant studies. To include relevant articles that might have been missed by the search engines, author (BP) also manually checked the reference lists of the included articles. Table 1 lists the included databases.

We searched the PubMed database on October 24, 2024 and the EBSCOhost database on October 25, 2024. We exported the PMID numbers of the articles and then, on October 26, 2024, imported all search results into Zotero [33], where we removed duplicates. Before conducting a detailed analysis of all the articles, we re-examined both databases to check if any new articles had emerged.

Search strategy

The search strategy included MeSH terms and other subject headings, as well as relevant keywords and synonyms. Each search string was applied across the entire bibliographic record. The search strategy was designed and implemented by the first author (BP), informed by established systematic review methodology. Table 2 presents the search terms used for querying the databases. The same search terms were applied across all databases.

The strategy primarily focused on population-related terms referring to formally diagnosed SpLDs and related conditions in order to maximise sensitivity and ensure that potentially relevant studies addressing educational experiences, challenges, or support needs were not missed due to restrictive outcome-related terminology. Broader neurodiversity-related terms (e.g., neurodivergent, neurodiverse) were not included because the review aimed to examine evidence specifically concerning SpLD diagnoses within medical education.

Selection and data collection process

Screening of titles and abstracts was conducted independently by BP and VH to determine eligibility. Agreement between reviewers during the title and abstract screening stage was monitored throughout the process, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. For studies meeting the inclusion criteria, full texts were retrieved and reviewed by both reviewers. Additionally, citation searches were screened by BP after the full-text review stage. No automation tools were used in the selection process.

Data extraction was carried out by two authors (BP, VH), who independently collected relevant data from each included study. There was no need to contact study investigators for additional data. The relevance of the extracted data was reviewed and confirmed by the third author (MKB).

Data items

Data were extracted according to four primary outcome domains aligned with the review objectives. Additional data items included study characteristics (authors, year, country, design and publication type) and participant details (sample size, type of SpLD, study year and disclosure status, where available). Where applicable, we also extracted descriptions of support interventions, recruitment methods, analytical approaches, and outcome measures used.

Given the diversity of study designs, not all items were available in each study. Relevant data were extracted when clearly reported and aligned with the review aims. In cases where outcome data were aggregated or not stratified by SpLD status, these data were excluded unless disaggregation was possible. Missing or unclear information (e.g. unspecified type of SpLD) was recorded as “not reported” Funding sources and declared conflicts of interest were noted where available to support risk of bias assessment.

Risk of bias assessment

To ensure methodological quality, risk of bias was assessed for all included studies using design-specific tools. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklists were used as the primary appraisal framework, including the JBI Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies and the JBI Checklist for Cohort Studies [34, 35], as well as the JBI Checklist for Qualitative Research [36]. In addition, corresponding Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklists were applied to provide complementary assessment of methodological rigor and reporting transparency across study designs [37–39].

One reviewer (BP) performed all assessments, while two additional reviewers (VH, MKB) independently appraised a subset of studies to ensure consistency, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus. Risk of bias was not used as an exclusion criterion but informed the interpretation of findings.

Effect measures

For quantitative comparative studies, standardised mean differences (Cohen’s d) with 95% CIs were calculated using a random-effects model, restricted to studies with comparable assessment tasks and standardised scores.

Synthesis methods

Quantitative studies reporting comparable academic performance outcomes between students with and without SpLDs were considered eligible for meta-analysis, while studies reporting heterogeneous outcomes were synthesised narratively.

Where required, reported effect measures were converted to standardised mean differences (Cohen’s d) to allow pooling. Missing or insufficient summary statistics precluded inclusion in the meta-analysis and were therefore synthesised narratively. Results of individual studies and quantitative syntheses were tabulated and summarised using structured summary tables and forest plots where applicable. Quantitative data were synthesised using random-effects meta-analysis, with statistical heterogeneity assessed using tau2. All quantitative analyses were conducted using appropriate statistical software. Due to the limited number of studies, formal subgroup analyses, meta-regression, and sensitivity analyses were not performed.

Qualitative findings were synthesised using meta-aggregation [40]. Two authors (BP and VH) independently reviewed the full qualitative studies and extracted reported findings related to academic challenges, learning experiences, psychosocial impact, support needs, and perceived strengths of MS with SpLDs. Extracted findings with similar meanings were grouped into descriptive categories through iterative comparison and discussion. Areas of overlap and discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through consensus. These categories were then aggregated into overarching synthesised findings aligned with the review objectives. A detailed overview of the extracted qualitative categories and synthesised findings is provided in Additional file 4.

Quantitative and qualitative findings were first synthesised separately and subsequently integrated using the JBI Convergent Segregated approach [27]. During the integration phase, findings from both evidence strands were compared to identify areas of convergence, divergence, and complementarity across academic performance, learning experiences, psychosocial impact, and support needs. Integrated interpretations were then developed in relation to the predefined review objectives.

Reporting bias assessment

Potential reporting bias, arising from missing results in the included studies, was assessed by examining selective outcome reporting and publication bias. For quantitative studies, funnel plots were visually inspected to identify asymmetry that could indicate missing studies; formal statistical tests (e.g., Egger’s regression) were not performed due to the small number of studies. For narrative syntheses and qualitative studies, bias was evaluated based on the completeness of outcome reporting and the availability of study protocols. Any identified risks of reporting bias were considered in the interpretation of findings and in assessing the overall quality of evidence.

Certainty assessment

Where possible, the certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE approach for quantitative findings [41] and GRADE-CERQual for qualitative findings [42]. For outcomes where formal assessment was not feasible due to limited data or heterogeneity, a narrative evaluation was performed. Integration of both strands informed an overall judgment of confidence, highlighting areas of higher certainty and those requiring further research.

Results

Study selection

After removal of duplicates, the remaining records were screened based on titles and abstracts, with the majority excluded at this stage due to lack of relevance to the review objectives. A total of 43 studies were retrieved for full-text assessment. Of these, 22 publications were theoretical or conceptual papers without primary empirical data and were therefore not eligible for inclusion in the systematic review; however, several of these papers were used to inform the background and discussion sections.

Twenty-one studies that initially appeared to meet the inclusion criteria were excluded for specific reasons. Studies focusing exclusively on institutional policies, legal frameworks, or administrative descriptions of disability services without reporting empirical data on students’ educational experiences, challenges, or outcomes (n = 9) were excluded [43–51]. Two studies (n = 2) included mixed student populations in which data specific to MS could not be isolated [52, 53]. Some (n = 9) focused only on general special needs or mental health-related disabilities and SpLDs could not be isolated [13, 54–61], and one study (n = 1) addressed postgraduate education [62], which was outside the scope of this review.

A total of 15 studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising 8 quantitative [63–70] and 7 qualitative [71–77] study designs. The majority (n = 9) of included studies were published within the last decade, indicating increasing research attention to the experiences of MS with SpLDs. Risk of bias in studies

The included studies varied in methodological rigor. Most quantitative cohort and cross-sectional studies reported clearly defined populations, valid outcome measurements, and appropriate statistical analyses. Some quantitative studies had limitations related to confounding and comparability between groups [68, 69], while some qualitative studies provided limited methodological detail or had constraints related to small sample size and reporting transparency [70, 77]. One study [76] lacked sufficient methodological detail and was classified as high risk. Individual risk of bias assessments for each study are summarized in Additional file 1.

Results of individual studies

Quantitative studies

Across the included quantitative studies, the reported prevalence of MS with SpLDs ranged from 2% to 11.5%. Multiple-choice question (MCQ) formats were the most frequently reported assessment type. Across the included studies, the most commonly reported adjustment was additional examination time, typically granted following formal disclosure of a learning disability. Where reported, overall differences in performance between students with SpLDs and their peers were small or not statistically significant. Data on non-MCQ assessments (e.g., OSCE, SAQ, MEQ) were less frequently reported and showed heterogeneous outcomes, with some evidence of lower performance in certain first-year assessments.

Qualitative studies

The included qualitative studies consistently reported that MS with SpLDs encounter challenges across multiple academic and psychosocial domains. Key areas of difficulty included knowledge acquisition, assessment performance, emotional well-being, social integration, and systemic barriers. Support needs were multifaceted, spanning academic adjustments, teaching modifications, personal support, and broader institutional strategies. Students emphasized the importance of early identification of learning disabilities, individualized learning strategies, flexible assessment methods, social support, mentorship, and increased staff awareness.

A detailed summary of individual qualitative studies, including specific challenges and support needs, is presented in Additional file 3.

Results of synthesis

Synthesis 1: quantitative studies

Prevalence

The prevalence of MS with SpLDs varied significantly across the included studies, with an average prevalence of 5.5%. The highest reported prevalence was 11.5% [63], while the lowest was 2.0% [69].

Multiple-choice questions based assessment performance

A meta-analysis was conducted including three studies reporting MCQ-type assessment results: McKendree (1st and 2nd year, MCQ/EMQ with additional examination time as an approved accommodation) [64], Ricketts (progress test, multiple years, UK, additional examination time) [66], and Meeks (USMLE Step 1 and Step 2, US, additional examination time) [67].

Using a random-effects model, the pooled standardized mean difference was d = 0.045 (95% CI: −0.094 to 0.183), indicating no statistically significant difference in MCQ performance between students with and without SpLDs. Statistical heterogeneity was low (τ² = 0.022), suggesting limited variability in effect estimates across studies. Nevertheless, important clinical and methodological differences were present, including variation in assessment formats (e.g., progress testing versus high-stakes licensing examinations such as USMLE) and differences in educational contexts and implementation of accommodations. No sensitivity analyses were conducted due to the small number of included studies.

Overall, these findings suggest that, under specific conditions—particularly when assessment accommodations such as additional examination time are provided—students with SpLDs may achieve comparable performance in MCQ-based assessments; however, these results should be interpreted with caution.

Academic performance

For complex assessments, including extended matching questions (EMQ), short answer questions (SAQ), and objective structured clinical examinations (OSCEs), results varied across studies. One study [63] reported significantly lower scores for students with SpLD in EMQ, SAQ, and OSCEs (data and clinical image interpretation). Another study [64] reported no significant differences in SAQ and OSCE performance.

Additional examination time was the primary accommodation reported for students with SpLDs in six studies [63–68]. In two studies [63, 64], additional examination time was granted for all assessments except OSCEs, where time limits remained unchanged. No other adjustments were reported in any of the included studies.

Reported academic outcomes included lower first-year assessment scores across various formats in one study [63], delayed graduation and higher leave-of-absence rates in another [67], and improved performance following early diagnosis with accommodations versus poorer final-year outcomes following late diagnosis in a separate study [65].

Synthesis 2: qualitative studies

Qualitative studies [71–77] reported that MS with SpLDs experience difficulties across multiple domains, including knowledge acquisition, lectures, assessments, emotional well-being, social relationships, and systemic barriers. Students described challenges such as reading and writing difficulties, time pressure, and coping with large volumes of information, alongside anxiety, frustration, and feelings of isolation. These challenges were often intensified by stigma and limited institutional understanding.

Despite these barriers, several strengths were identified, including creativity, empathy, and strong communication skills that positively influenced patient care. Reported support needs focused on flexible assessments, personalized learning strategies, early identification, and greater staff awareness. A detailed summary of the qualitative findings and results of the meta-aggregation is provided in the Additional file 4.

Integration of findings (mixed-methods synthesis)

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings identified areas of convergence, divergence, and complementarity across academic performance, assessment experiences, psychosocial impact, and systemic factors.

Academic performance and assessment experiences

Quantitative studies suggested that MS with SpLDs may perform comparably to their peers in some MCQ-based examinations when accommodations such as additional examination time are provided [64, 66, 67]. Results for other assessment formats, including SAQs and OSCEs, were more variable, with some evidence of poorer performance or delayed progression [63–65, 67]. Qualitative studies reported that students required greater effort, additional preparation time, and compensatory strategies to achieve academic outcomes [71–74, 76]. Across both strands, time pressure and rigid assessment structures were consistently identified as barriers [63–65, 70–76].

Psychosocial impact

Quantitative studies reported limited data on psychosocial outcomes, although some evidence indicated higher rates of burnout, leave of absence, and challenges related to the learning environment [67, 68]. Qualitative findings described self-doubt, anxiety, frustration, isolation, stigma, and fear of disclosure as commonly reported experiences [70–77]. Negative attitudes from peers or faculty, including bullying and limited understanding, were also reported [68, 70, 72, 73]. Some studies additionally identified perceived strengths, including empathy, creativity, and communication skills [72–74, 76].

Systemic factors and support

Across both evidence strands, additional examination time was the most frequently reported accommodation, with limited reporting of other adjustments [64–67, 70–74]. Quantitative studies identified variability in prevalence estimates, timing of diagnosis, and access to accommodations, including associations between later diagnosis and poorer academic outcomes [65, 67]. Qualitative evidence reported delayed or absent diagnosis, limited staff awareness, stigma, and inflexible curricula as factors influencing students’ experiences [70–75].

Integrated synthesis

Overall, quantitative findings indicated comparable performance in some accommodated MCQ-based assessments [64, 66, 67], while qualitative findings reported increased effort, time demands, and challenges across academic and psychosocial domains [70–77]. Academic outcomes appeared to be shaped by assessment format, the availability and implementation of accommodations, and broader systemic barriers. Taken together, these findings highlight that comparable assessment outcomes may be achieved alongside substantial compensatory effort and should not be assumed to indicate equitable learning conditions. Reporting biases

Risk of bias due to missing results was assessed for each synthesis. For the quantitative synthesis of MCQ performance, completeness of outcome reporting was checked across included studies, with no indications of selective non-reporting; funnel plots were visually inspected, but formal tests (e.g., Egger’s regression) were not performed due to the small number of studies. For the qualitative synthesis, all studies reported outcomes on academic, social, and emotional challenges and support strategies, with no evidence of selective reporting. Overall, risk of bias from missing results is considered low.

Certainty of evidence

Certainty for MCQ performance was rated low-moderate using an adapted GRADE approach, downgraded slightly due to variations in assessment type and accommodations [64, 66, 67]. For qualitative findings, certainty was rated moderate using GRADE-CERQual, reflecting coherence and relevance across studies [71–77]. Where formal grading was not possible, a narrative assessment was made. Overall, the evidence provides low to moderate confidence that MS with SpLDs may achieve comparable performance in some accommodated MCQ-based assessments. Nevertheless, qualitative findings indicate that substantial academic and psychosocial challenges persist, while targeted support strategies are perceived as helpful by students.

Discussion

Growing research attention to neurodivergence in health professions education has been reflected in recent evidence syntheses [21–23]. Two scoping reviews focused on neurodivergent learners in medical and health professions education [21, 22], while one systematic review examined the experiences of MS and doctors with dyslexia across the continuum of medical training [23]. The present review extends this literature by focusing specifically on undergraduate MS with SpLDs as a broader diagnostic category and by integrating evidence on prevalence, academic performance, challenges, and support needs using a mixed-methods approach.

By combining quantitative and qualitative findings, this review distinguishes between performance outcomes and the learning processes and experiences through which these outcomes are achieved. Performance outcomes refer to measurable indicators such as examination scores, progression, and graduation-related outcomes, whereas learning processes and experiences include the effort, strategies, cognitive load, emotional burden, and institutional barriers that shape students’ participation in medical education. Comparable performance in some accommodated assessment contexts therefore represents only one dimension of students’ educational experiences and should not be interpreted as evidence of equitable learning conditions.

However, the predominance of UK-based and dyslexia-focused studies should be considered when interpreting these findings, particularly given national and institutional differences in undergraduate medical education, as well as in the availability and implementation of accessibility accommodations. This distribution may partly reflect the restriction to English-language publications.

Academic challenges and learning environments

Integrated evidence suggests that comparable academic outcomes for students with SpLDs may mask substantial underlying effort and persistent challenges. This distinction between performance outcomes and learning processes is important: similar grades or examination scores may reflect successful compensation under accommodated conditions rather than the absence of educational disadvantage. Learning processes described in the qualitative studies included additional preparation time, reliance on individualized compensatory strategies, difficulties with processing large volumes of information, and the need to navigate learning environments that were not consistently designed with accessibility in mind.

Although accommodations may support performance in some contexts [64, 66, 67], they do not fully address the cognitive and emotional load associated with time management, processing complex information, and adapting to unfamiliar academic demands [64, 66, 70, 72, 73]. The structure and pace of medical education may further exacerbate these difficulties, particularly in learning environments characterised by high information density and time pressure [22–24, 72].

These findings indicate that current support systems may be insufficiently proactive, often relying on students’ own initiative to develop compensatory strategies such as visualization, organization, and discussion-based learning [70–73, 75, 76]. Although adaptive tools and tailored study environments can be beneficial [70–73], their availability and effective use remain inconsistent. This highlights the need for more systematic, evidence-informed approaches to inclusive curriculum design that address hidden workload and reduce inequities in learning conditions.

Assessment-related barriers

Assessment outcomes for MS with SpLDs should be interpreted in relation to the assessment format, the accommodations provided, and the broader educational context in which assessment takes place. Assessment performance should therefore be distinguished from assessment experience, as similar scores may be achieved under substantially different learning conditions.

In a small subset of studies, comparable performance was observed in MCQ-based assessments when accommodations such as additional examination time were provided [64, 66, 67]. However, the limited number of studies and contextual heterogeneity constrain the interpretation of this finding. Included studies differed in assessment formats, ranging from progress tests to high-stakes licensing examinations such as the USMLE, as well as in educational contexts, implementation of accommodations, and institutional support systems, which may limit the comparability and transferability of findings despite low statistical heterogeneity.

Integrated evidence further indicates that parity in multiple-choice examinations does not necessarily extend to other assessment formats. Assessment performance represents only one dimension of educational equity and should not be treated as a sufficient indicator of whether students experience fair and accessible learning conditions. OSCEs, essay-based tasks, and high-stakes examinations, defined as assessments with major consequences for progression or qualification, often reveal persistent challenges, including difficulty articulating thoughts under time pressure, coping with noisy or crowded testing environments, and navigating rigid assessment structures (63–65,70,72,73).

These findings suggest that reliance on performance metrics alone may underestimate underlying systemic inequities. While quantitative data support the effectiveness of additional examination time [64–67], qualitative evidence indicates that additional or alternative adjustments—such as oral examinations, digital adaptations, or semi-private testing environments—may be required to better align assessment conditions with diverse learning needs [70–73, 75]. Early performance discrepancies, delayed graduation, and higher rates of academic leave [63, 65, 67] highlight the cumulative impact of these barriers, reinforcing the need for more inclusive and flexible assessment design.

Social and emotional impact

Beyond academic performance, MS with SpLDs experience substantial emotional and social burdens. Integrated evidence indicates that stigma, fear of disclosure, and experiences of exclusion contribute to frustration, self-doubt, and reduced confidence, even when performance appears comparable [70, 72–76]. These psychosocial challenges are closely linked to structural factors, including limited faculty awareness, inconsistent mentoring, and insufficient peer support [70–75].

Importantly, these findings suggest that emotional strain is not an inherent consequence of SpLDs, but rather reflects how medical education environments are structured and culturally framed. This highlights the role of institutional responsibility in shaping students’ experiences, including the need for coordinated support systems and inclusive educational practices. Structured peer and faculty mentoring programmes have been shown to support the social and emotional development of MS with SpLDs, promoting inclusion and self-confidence [79–81]. In the absence of such support, students may face cumulative disadvantages that affect both wellbeing and long-term academic engagement.

Delayed diagnosis and systemic barriers

A further important finding is the delayed identification of SpLDs, with many students only recognizing their condition after entering the demanding medical curriculum. Within this high-pressure environment, previously effective compensatory strategies may become insufficient, requiring students to develop more complex coping mechanisms [65, 70, 82]. This suggests that medical education environments may inadvertently obscure underlying learning difficulties, as early academic struggles can be misattributed to lack of effort rather than unmet learning needs.

Evidence indicates that a substantial proportion of MS with dyslexia are identified only during medical school, highlighting uncertainty regarding the number of students who remain undiagnosed or choose not to disclose their learning difficulties [70]. Similar patterns of delayed identification and challenges related to disclosure have been reported in recent research on dyslexia in medical education [23]. Limited access to appropriate expertise within medical schools may further delay recognition and support [65]. Late identification of SpLDs has been associated with poorer academic outcomes, increased stress, and a higher risk of burnout among MS [65]. Without proactive screening and early support, students may experience cumulative academic and psychosocial disadvantages, underscoring the need for greater awareness, earlier identification, and more accessible support structures.

Support needs and strengths: an underexplored area

This review highlights a persistent gap in addressing the broader support needs of MS with SpLDs. While assessment adjustments, most commonly additional examination time, are relatively well established [64–67], integrated evidence indicates that strategies targeting academic, social, and emotional support remain inconsistent and limited [71–77]. Students reported needs for adaptable curricula, accessible resources, assistive technologies, early identification, staff awareness, and more individualized forms of academic and psychosocial support [71–77], suggesting that current provisions address only part of the challenges they face.

Although several forms of support were identified, the evidence base provides limited information on how these are implemented, evaluated, or experienced over time [71–77, 79–81]. This limits conclusions about their effectiveness and highlights the need for future research to examine which support strategies are feasible, acceptable, and beneficial in different medical education contexts. Such work should consider not only academic outcomes, but also students’ workload, wellbeing, disclosure experiences, and sense of belonging [70–77].

Perceived strengths among MS with SpLDs were also reported, including communication skills, creative problem-solving, empathy, and positive contributions to patient care [72, 74, 76]. Considering these strengths is important for moving beyond a solely deficit-oriented understanding of SpLDs in medical education. However, such findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be presented as universal or inherent characteristics of all students with SpLDs. Future research should examine perceived strengths alongside support needs, while also considering the structural barriers that shape students’ educational experiences.

Limitations and strengths of the review

This mixed-methods synthesis integrates quantitative and qualitative evidence, addressing a relative gap in the literature where studies have predominantly relied on single-method approaches. By combining meta-analytic findings with qualitative synthesis, the review enables a more comprehensive understanding of both academic outcomes and underlying processes.

However, several limitations should be considered. The evidence base was geographically concentrated, with most included studies conducted within UK medical education and focused on dyslexia, which limits the transferability of findings to other educational systems and to students with other types of SpLDs. Differences in the structure and definition of undergraduate medical education across countries should also be considered when applying the findings beyond the contexts represented in the included studies. In addition, variability in definitions of SpLDs, institutional support systems, assessment accommodations, and approaches to disclosure across contexts may affect the comparability and generalisability of findings. The small number of included studies, overlap in authorship, and small qualitative sample sizes further constrain the robustness of conclusions. In addition, the meta-analysis included a limited number of studies (n = 3), requiring cautious interpretation of pooled estimates. Variability in assessment formats, outcome measures, and reporting may also have influenced comparability across studies.

Despite these limitations, the integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence allowed identification of consistent patterns and gaps not readily apparent in single-method studies.

Implications for the field

Integrated insights underscore that medical schools must implement comprehensive strategies addressing academic, emotional, and systemic needs of students with SpLD. An early identification, mentoring, flexible curricula, assistive technologies and faculty training are central to promoting equitable access and wellbeing. Recognizing the hidden effort behind comparable outcomes can inform policy, curriculum design, and resource allocation, fostering a more inclusive and resilient healthcare workforce.

Future research directions

Future research should examine the longitudinal impact of SpLDs on academic progression and wellbeing, while also identifying and evaluating the most effective accommodations to support student success. Mixed-methods designs remain essential for capturing both measurable outcomes and lived experiences. Importantly, further work could investigate how medical schools can better recognize and leverage the strengths noted in MS with SpLDs, such as communication skills, empathy, and broad, integrative thinking, which represent highly valued professional attributes in medicine.

Conclusions

This mixed-methods review indicates that approximately 5.5% of MS have SpLDs, yet their experiences are not adequately reflected by performance outcomes alone. Limited quantitative evidence suggests that, with accommodations such as additional examination time, students with SpLDs may achieve comparable results in some MCQ-based assessments. However, qualitative findings show that comparable outcomes may mask substantial additional effort, emotional burden, cognitive load, and persistent systemic barriers, particularly in high-pressure, clinical, written, and inflexible assessment contexts. Equity therefore requires a shift beyond reactive individual adjustments toward proactive, inclusive, and flexible educational design that supports accessibility, progression, and wellbeing in medical education.
