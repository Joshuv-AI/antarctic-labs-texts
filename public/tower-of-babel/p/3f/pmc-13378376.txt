The role of ucOCN in aerobic exercise induced amelioration of autism spectrum disorder phenotypes
Authors: Yaxin Shi, Tikang Zhu, Shuting Wang, Yao Sun, Yutong Liu, Shuangshuang Chen, Wanying Qiao, Ding Zhou, Zuyue Wang, Lili Fan, Hongmei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder closely associated with synaptic dysfunction that contributes to core behavioral deficits. Aerobic exercise (AE) serves as a promising adjuvant intervention for diverse neuropsychiatric conditions, and accumulating evidence suggests favorable effects of AE on ASD-related behaviors and physiological traits. The bone-derived hormone undercarboxylated osteocalcin (ucOCN) induced by AE can cross the blood-brain barrier to regulate synaptic plasticity. Mendelian randomization analysis suggests an association between genetically predicted higher physical activity levels and lower ASD risk, as well as between physical inactivity and higher ASD risk. In BTBR mice (a classic ASD animal model), AE intervention was associated with improvements in ASD-like phenotypes, increased cortical ucOCN, GPR158, BDNF, and synaptic proteins, and altered MAPK/ERK/Akt2 signaling. This work provides potential mechanistic clues for AE-associated changes in synaptic plasticity, supporting non-pharmacological intervention strategies for ASD.Subject areas: molecular biology, neuroscience, behavioral neuroscience

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a multifaceted neurodevelopmental condition that generally presents symptoms during early childhood and continues into adulthood. Key features of ASD include persistent challenges in social communication and interaction, along with repetitive and restricted patterns of behaviors, interests, or activities. Notably, there has been a significant increase in the global prevalence of ASD, with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) predicting an estimated occurrence of approximately 1 in 31 individuals in the United States by 2025,1 The condition’s poor prognosis and substantial disease burden have raised it to a position of significant concern in global public health. While the precise causes remain largely unknown, various studies indicate that synaptic dysfunction is integral to the fundamental pathology of ASD, which is intricately linked to cognitive deficits, social challenges, and stereotypical behaviors.2

Currently, there are two main approaches for the rehabilitation treatment of ASD: one is pharmacotherapy3 which has significant side effects and low compliance from both children and parents. Another mainstream rehabilitation training method is behavioral intervention and skills training,4 which requires experienced professionals to provide one-on-one treatment for children with ASD, resulting in high treatment costs. Among various rehabilitation intervention methods, aerobic exercise (AE) is favored for its low exercise intensity, long duration, slow pace, and high compliance. AE is an effective strategy for improving neural and cognitive functions.5 It has been reported that long-term AE can prevent cognitive deficits.6 Studies indicate that AE can alleviate neuroinflammation in the central nervous system and reduce oxidative stress; these improvements in the neural microenvironment help protect neurons and synapses.7 A randomized controlled study demonstrated that engaging in moderate-intensity AE for 30 min, three times weekly, significantly diminished repetitive behaviors and challenges in social communication among children diagnosed with ASD.8 Research conducted by Pan et al. revealed that consistent swimming training notably improved social responsiveness and lessened stereotypical behaviors in children diagnosed with ASD.9 A meta-analysis indicated that participants in the AE group exhibited superior performance compared to the ASD control group on tasks assessing cognitive flexibility.10 However, the exact mechanisms by which AE improves ASD phenotypes remain unclear.

In recent years, studies have found that a variety of drugs acting on relevant receptors have been confirmed to improve the pathological state of ASD by regulating related molecular pathways. It has been verified that resveratrol treatment reduces the expression levels of C-C chemokine receptor (CCR) and C-X-C motif chemokine receptor (CXCR) in the spleen and brain tissues of BTBR mice, thus making it a potentially effective candidate drug for the treatment of ASD.11 Adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR) agonists can ameliorate the neuroimmune dysfunction observed in BTBR mice.12 In addition, studies have indicated that peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-β (PPARδ) agonist GW0742 can improve the abnormal neurobehavioral phenotypes of BTBR mice by exerting anti-inflammatory effects.13

In recent years, it has been discovered that AE can regulate brain function through signaling molecules secreted by peripheral organs.14 Among these, undercarboxylated osteocalcin (ucOCN) has garnered significant attention due to its ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and directly affect neurons. ucOCN is secreted by osteoblasts and, in addition to regulating glucose and lipid metabolism and energy metabolism, it also acts on the brainstem, midbrain, and hippocampus, modulating neurodevelopment, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive abilities.15 Research indicates that ucOCN can enhance neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity by activating the cAMP-PKA-CREB pathway through its binding with GPR158, thereby improving cognition.16 Previous research has indicated that disruptions in ucOCN signaling are associated with cognitive decline, and the injection of ucOCN in AD model mice can reduce Aβ plaques and improve cognitive function.17 However, no studies have directly explored whether ucOCN can act on relevant receptors to participate in the regulatory mechanism of ASD after AE intervention.

This study employs Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to explore the causal relationships between physical activity and ASD. In addition, an AE intervention was carried out using the BTBR mouse model of ASD to assess its influence on key phenotypic traits. Moreover, through molecular experiments and RNA sequencing analysis, the function of ucOCN and its receptors in the modulation of synaptic activity was clarified, revealing the possible mechanisms through which AE enhances social and cognitive deficits in ASD via the ucOCN pathway. This research provides theoretical foundations and potential targets for exercise interventions in ASD.

Results

Vigorous physical activity and heavy DIY (do it yourself) activities are inversely associated with ASD risk

MR analysis

Studies indicate that when total energy expenditure is equivalent, there is no statistically significant difference in health benefits between engaging in fitness exercises and participating in everyday physical activities.18 MR analysis results indicate an association between three exposures—vigorous physical activity, heavy DIY, and not engaging in physical activity in the past four weeks—and ASD. Among these, vigorous physical activity and heavy DIY are protective factors for ASD, while not engaging in physical activity in the past four weeks is a risk factor for ASD (Figure 1A). This indicates a possible causal link between the level of physical activity and the likelihood of developing ASD.

Sensitivity analysis

To ensure the reliability of the previously mentioned causal connections, this research carried out a range of sensitivity analyses. The results of Cochran’s Q test indicate consistency among the various IVs (p > 0.05), suggesting that there is no significant heterogeneity. The MR-Egger regression intercept test reveals that all p values exceed 0.05 (Table 1), suggesting a lack of significant horizontal pleiotropy. This indicates that the instrumental variables mainly affect the outcomes via exposure factors instead of alternative pathways. Furthermore, the leave-one-out analysis validated that the overall effect estimates remained stable, even when any single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) was excluded (Figures 1B–1D), demonstrating that the research results are not overly influenced by individual genetic loci. Notably, in the multivariable MR analysis, the associations of vigorous physical activity and heavy DIY behavior on ASD remained statistically significant even after adjusting for other potential confounding factors (Figure 1E). To conclude, various sensitivity analyses bolster the reliability of the MR findings, providing additional support for the observed associations between physical activity-related traits and ASD risk.

Aerobic exercise improves the ASD-like phenotype in BTBR mice

Based on MR analysis suggesting a correlation between high levels of physical activity and reduced risk of ASD, we further selected AE, which is the most common and experimentally feasible, to intervene in the ASD model BTBR mice. This was done to verify its effects on social functioning, cognition, repetitive stereotyped behaviors, and anxiety-related phenotypes.

Aerobic exercise alleviates anxiety in BTBR mice

In the open field test, the total distance traveled by mice was employed as a critical index to evaluate their basal spontaneous locomotor activity, whereas the access frequency and the duration spent in the central zone were used to assess anxiety levels. The results revealed no statistically significant differences in total distance traveled between the C57 exercise group and the C57 control group, between the BTBR control group and the C57 control group, as well as between the BTBR exercise group and the BTBR control group (Figures 2A and 2B). Compared with the C57 control group, the C57 exercise group showed no significant differences in either the number of entries into the central zone or the time spent in it (Figures 2A and 2B). In contrast, the BTBR control group exhibited significantly fewer central zone entries and markedly shorter residence time in the central zone (Figures 2A and 2B). After AE training, the BTBR exercise group presented a prominent increase in both the number of entries into and the time spent in the central zone relative to the BTBR control group (Figures 2A and 2B). Collectively, these findings indicate that AE training does not modify the spontaneous locomotor activity of C57 and BTBR mice, nor does it influence the anxiety levels of C57 mice, but it effectively alleviates anxiety-like behaviors in BTBR mice.

Aerobic exercise enhances social behavior in BTBR mice

Social impairment represents a fundamental characteristic of ASD. The social behavior of BTBR mice was evaluated both prior to and following an AE intervention utilizing a three-chamber test. Findings from the social ability assessment indicated that control and exercise-trained C57 mice dedicated considerably more time to investigating the stranger 1 in comparison to their exploration of the empty cage (Figures 2C and 2D); in contrast, BTBR mice did not exhibit a notable difference in the duration spent with stranger 1 versus the empty cage. Following AE training, BTBR mice exhibited a notable rise in the duration they spent investigating stranger 1 when compared to the empty cage (Figures 2C and 2D). In the evaluation of social novelty preference, control and exercise-trained C57 mice spent significantly greater time observing stranger 2 rather than stranger 1 (Figures 2E and 2F); however, no significant difference was found in the time that BTBR mice devoted to exploring stranger 2 in contrast to stranger 1. After the AE training, BTBR mice spent significantly longer examining stranger 2 compared to stranger 1 (Figures 2E and 2F). These findings suggest that the social function of BTBR mice is effectively improved after AE training.

Aerobic exercise improves cognitive impairments in BTBR mice

The water maze experiment is utilized to evaluate the cognitive capabilities, along with the learning and memory functions, of mice. Results from the navigation task related to platform positioning showed that, as the number of training sessions increased, the duration required for control and exercise-trained C57 mice to find the platform typically diminished (Figure 2H); in contrast, BTBR mice exhibited no notable change in their latency to find the platform post-training. After participating in AE training, BTBR mice showed a notable decrease in the time taken to find the platform (Figure 2H). Additionally, spatial probe test results demonstrated that exercise-trained C57 mice exhibited no significant difference in the number of crossings over the original platform site relative to control C57 mice, whereas BTBR mice showed a significantly decreased number of platform crossings (Figure 2I). Following AE training, the frequency with which BTBR mice crossed the platform improved significantly (Figure 2I). This confirms that the cognitive impairment in BTBR mice was effectively improved following AE training.

Aerobic exercise reduces repetitive stereotyped behaviors in BTBR mice

The main techniques used to assess stereotyped behaviors in mice are the burying behavior test and the grooming behavior test. Findings from the burying behavior test indicate that exercise-trained C57 mice exhibited no significant alteration in the number of buried marbles relative to control C57 mice (Figure 3A), while BTBR mice showed a marked increase in marble burying behavior (Figure 3A). Furthermore, after undergoing AE training, there was a notable reduction in the number of buried marbles among BTBR mice (Figure 3A). Additionally, grooming behavior test results showed that AE training had no significant effect on grooming duration or frequency in C57 mice (Figure 3B), whereas BTBR mice exhibited significantly longer grooming duration and higher grooming frequency than C57 mice (Figure 3B). Following AE training, BTBR mice showed a notable reduction in grooming duration and the frequency of grooming incidents (Figure 3B). These results validate that the repetitive stereotyped behaviors of BTBR mice are effectively improved following AE training.

Aerobic exercise upregulates the levels of ucOCN and its receptor GPR158 in the cortex of BTBR mice, and improves the expression of synaptic-related proteins

Previous research has demonstrated that prolonged AE can elevate serum levels of ucOCN. GPR158, identified as a primary receptor for ucOCN, exhibits high expression levels in brain tissue.19 The compound ucOCN is capable of traversing the BBB and binding to GPR158, which in turn activates downstream signaling pathways that influence neurogenesis in the hippocampus and synaptic plasticity. In the current investigation, we initially assessed the ucOCN levels in the cortex of BTBR mice. The ELISA findings revealed that the ucOCN level in the BTBR mouse cortex was significantly lower than that found in C57 mice (Figure 3D). Furthermore, AE training resulted in a significant rise in ucOCN levels among the BTBR mice (Figure 3D). Additionally, the results from qRT-PCR analyses indicated that GPR158 mRNA levels in BTBR mice were significantly reduced in comparison to C57 mice (Figure 3E). After the exercise intervention, there was a notable upregulation of GPR158 mRNA levels (Figure 3E).

In line with earlier research, ucOCN influences BDNF expression in hippocampal neurons,19 and BDNF is closely related to synaptic function.20 We further examined the relevant synaptic proteins. Western blot results indicated that, compared to C57 mice, BTBR mice exhibited significantly lower levels of PSD-95 and Synapsin-1 (SYN1) (Figures 3G and 3H). Following exercise intervention, both proteins were significantly elevated (Figures 3G and 3H). Similarly, BDNF mRNA levels were also upregulated (Figure 3F). Collectively, these results indicate that AE partially ameliorates the pathological reduction of synaptic-related proteins in BTBR mice, potentially involving the ucOCN/GPR158/BDNF signaling axis, though the ASD-specificity of this molecular response warrants further investigation.

Screening of hub genes after aerobic exercise intervention in BTBR mice

Identification of differentially expressed genes in BTBR mice post-aerobic exercise

The cerebral cortex serves as the principal integrative center of the nervous system, handling sensory processing, motor control, and advanced cognitive functions.21 In mouse models of ASD, the cortex frequently displays both structural and functional irregularities,22 aligning closely with the neuropathological characteristics observed in human ASD.23 To investigate the impact of AE on the cortical transcriptome in BTBR mice, we conducted an extensive analysis of genes that were differentially expressed between the two groups. Our results revealed that 3,277 genes exhibited differential expression in the cortical tissues of both C57 and BTBR mice. Specifically, 1,562 genes showed increased expression while 1,715 demonstrated decreased expression, as illustrated by the volcano plot for the DEGs in Figure 4A. In the cortical tissues of BTBR mice subjected to AE, we identified 2,695 DEGs. Within this subset, 1,287 genes showed increased expression, while 1,408 genes exhibited decreased expression, as illustrated by the volcano plot of the DEGs displayed in Figure 4B.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes following aerobic exercise intervention in BTBR mice

To enhance the identification of genes linked to the BTBR phenotype affected by AE, we conducted an intersection analysis on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the “C57 vs. BTBR” and “BTBR vs. BTBR + aerobic exercise” groups.24 This evaluation revealed a total of 1,295 DEGs that intersect (Figure 4C). To determine the functional implications of these differentially expressed genes, we subsequently carried out GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. The results of the GO enrichment analysis (Figure 4D) indicated that terms corresponding to cellular components (CCs) and molecular functions (MFs) were predominantly found in ribonucleoprotein granules, synapses between neurons, binding to ubiquitin-like protein ligases, and the activity of protein serine/threonine kinases. Regarding biological processes (BPs), notable entries encompassed the production of precursor metabolites and energy, the positive regulation of cell projection organization, RNA splicing, and the development of dendrites. The KEGG enrichment analysis (Figure 4E) shows that the primary pathways enriched include the MAPK signaling pathway—Mus musculus (house mouse), the Neurotrophin signaling pathway, and the AMPK signaling pathway, among others. Prior research has indicated that the phosphorylation levels of ERK1/2 (MAPK3/MAPK1) are markedly increased in ASD mice.25 Furthermore, abnormal activity of the ERK pathway in the prefrontal cortex of BTBR mice is associated with repetitive behaviors and social deficits, and MAPK inhibitors can ameliorate these behavioral abnormalities.26 Therefore, we chose to validate the MAPK pathway.

Akt2 is closely related to the improvement of the ASD phenotype in BTBR mice due to aerobic exercise

To explore the relationships among genes within the specified intersection, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was created using the STRING database, with a combined score threshold set at ≥0.9. This PPI network pertaining to cortical tissue comprises 240 nodes and 360 edges. The Cytoscape software utilized the MCODE plugin to identify two significant modules: Module 1 (score = 7.0, containing 7 nodes and 21 edges) and Module 2 (score = 6.0, which also includes 7 nodes but has 18 edges). Additionally, the leading ten hub genes were determined using five different algorithms (MNC, Degree, BottleNeck, Closeness, and EPC) found in the cytoHubba plugin, as shown in Table 2. Venn analysis (Figure 4F) indicated that Akt2 is the hub gene recognized by all five algorithms.

Aerobic exercise can modulate the expression of ERK/Akt2-related proteins in BTBR mice

To validate the earlier noted results from transcriptomic and network assessments and to enhance our comprehension of the potential molecular mechanisms by which AE improves the traits of BTBR mice, we analyzed the protein expression related to the Akt2/ERK signaling pathway. The western blot analysis results showed that the phosphorylation level of Akt2 was notably higher in BTBR mice compared to C57 mice (Figure 5A). Following AE training intervention, the phosphorylation level of Akt2 was significantly reduced (Figure 5A). Additionally, the phosphorylation level of ERK1/2 was significantly increased in BTBR mice (Figure 5B). Following the AE training intervention, a significant reduction in the phosphorylation levels of ERK1/2 was observed (Figure 5B). Collectively, these findings indicate that the ERK/Akt2 pathway is inappropriately activated in BTBR mice, and that AE can effectively correct this abnormality, suggesting that the ERK/Akt2 signaling may be involved in the behavioral improvement process mediated by AE.

Discussion

ASD is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder in which synaptic dysfunction plays a critical role in its pathogenesis. Although prior studies have confirmed that AE can ameliorate ASD-associated behavioral phenotypes, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying its therapeutic effects remain to be elucidated. In this study, we first verified a causal link between insufficient physical activity and elevated ASD risk via MR analysis; subsequent animal experiments further validated that AE improves core ASD symptoms (including anxiety-like behaviors, repetitive stereotyped behaviors, and social-cognitive deficits). Notably, this study is the first to identify the ucOCN/GPR158/BDNF signaling axis as a key pathway mediating the therapeutic effects of AE, with the involvement of Akt2 and ERK1/2 signaling pathways in regulating synaptic plasticity—thus providing valuable insights for targeted ASD interventions.

Clinical studies have consistently demonstrated that physical activities such as jogging, swimming, rhythmic gymnastics, table tennis, yoga, and horseback riding can significantly improve communication skills, social interaction abilities, and learning-memory capacities in children with ASD.32 Epidemiological evidence further supports a dose-dependent relationship between physical activity and ASD severity: shorter physical activity duration,33 lower activity intensity,34 and longer sedentary time35,36 in ASD children and adolescents correlate with exacerbated disease symptoms. Studies have found that a single 20-min jogging intervention can significantly reduce stereotyped behaviors in ASD children.37 Regarding exercise intensity, interventions of low,38 moderate,39 and high intensity all alleviate autistic behavioral symptoms, while a recent meta-analysis confirmed that moderate-intensity AE yields the most significant therapeutic efficacy.40 Moreover, research involving animals has suggested that engaging in AE may help reduce neuroinflammation and oxidative stress within the central nervous system, improve the neuroenvironment, protect neuronal and synaptic functions, and foster cognitive enhancements.41 Collectively, these findings underscore exercise as a promising non-pharmacological strategy for ASD management, but the key mediators linking AE to neural improvements have long been unclear.

Emerging evidence highlights the role of peripheral factors in mediating the central effects of exercise. For instance, the myokine irisin protects brain tissue by preserving BBB integrity;42 while fibroblast growth factor 21 modulates microglial polarization to reduce neuroinflammation.43 Among these peripheral regulators, ucOCN has attracted increasing attention due to its unique ability to cross the BBB and regulate neural functions. Notably, AE also upregulates the expression of ucOCN—a bone-derived mechanosensitive hormone with pleiotropic neuroregulatory functions.44 Epidemiological studies show that regular exercisers exhibit significantly higher circulating ucOCN levels than sedentary individuals,45 and animal experiments confirm that ucOCN exerts antidepressant effects via “bone-brain crosstalk.”46 Mice deficient in ucOCN display hypoactivity, anxiety, and memory impairments, whereas long-term exogenous ucOCN supplementation reverses cognitive deficits and anxiety-like behaviors.47 Reduced circulating ucOCN levels are also strongly associated with heightened anxiety and impaired spatial learning in animal models.19 In line with these findings, our study demonstrated that BTBR mice (a validated ASD model) have decreased ucOCN levels, which were markedly restored by AE intervention—suggesting that low ucOCN levels may contribute to cognitive impairment and anxiety phenotypes in ASD.

GPR158 is one of the key receptors for ucOCN, which can cross the BBB and bind to GPR158, activating downstream signals that influence hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity. GPR158 is the primary receptor for OCN in the brain, predominantly expressed in neurons in regions such as the hippocampus, cortex, and cerebellum of rodents, mediating the effects of ucOCN on mood and cognitive functions. Recent research has indicated that GPR158 has a significant and intricate function in the onset and progression of different neurological disorders.48 Currently, there is considerable research on GPR158 in the context of the pathological mechanisms of depression, with its alterations playing a key role in depressive-like behaviors.49 Some studies have observed changes in the expression levels of GPR158 in the brains of AD models or patients,50 but its function in neurodevelopmental disorders including ASD has not been reported. This study is the first to fill this gap, confirming that AE significantly upregulates GPR158/BDNF expression in the brains of BTBR mice; combined with the finding that “AE promotes ucOCN secretion,” our study identifies the ucOCN/GPR158 axis as a previously unrecognized regulatory pathway mediating AE-induced improvements in ASD symptoms. This finding has unique advantages over existing pharmacological intervention strategies for ASD: For example, resveratrol ameliorates ASD-like phenotypes by reducing the expression levels of CCR and CXCR in the spleen and brain tissues of BTBR mice;11 the PPARδ agonist GW0742 improves neurobehavioral abnormalities in mice by exerting anti-inflammatory effects.13 Studies have shown that exercise-induced β-hydroxybutyrate (β-HB) can effectively alleviate age-related learning and memory impairments by activating its receptor GPR109A (HCAR2) and initiating the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) signaling pathway.51 Compared with these drug-based approaches, targeting the ucOCN/GPR158 axis through AE offers distinct benefits of high safety and good compliance, especially for pediatric ASD populations who are more vulnerable to drug side effects. In contrast, our study is the first to define the ucOCN/GPR158 axis as a non-pharmacological intervention target—considering the limitations of drug-based therapies in pediatric patients with ASD, this target holds significant clinical translational value. From a mechanistic perspective, the regulatory effects of the ucOCN/GPR158 axis on BDNF are closely associated with two key pathways involved in cell survival, synaptic protein synthesis, and neurogenesis—the ERK1/2 and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways. Existing evidence has confirmed that BDNF can simultaneously activate both pathways: The PI3K/Akt pathway regulates local synaptic protein translation and dendritic spine stability by modulating the activities of mTOR and GSK-3β;52,53 the ERK1/2 pathway, on the other hand, mediates the phosphorylation of CREB and MNK1 to regulate early gene expression and receptor phosphorylation, thereby converting short-term stimulation signals into long-term synaptic adaptations.54 Our study further extends these conclusions, verifying that AE significantly modulate the phosphorylation levels of Akt2 and ERK in BTBR mice—indicating that the ERK1/2 and PI3K/Akt pathways are critical downstream effector pathways through which the ucOCN/GPR158/BDNF axis regulates ASD pathogenesis. Consistent with this, our study observed that AE significantly upregulates the expression levels of synaptic-related proteins in BTBR mice, suggesting that AE may improve synaptic plasticity in ASD mice via ucOCN-mediated regulation of these signaling pathways.

This study confirms a significant causal relationship between physical exercise and the amelioration of ASD-like phenotypes. AE upregulates bone-derived ucOCN, which activates the GPR158/BDNF axis and downstream Akt2/ERK1/2 signaling to enhance synaptic plasticity. This bone-to-brain pathway effectively mitigates cognitive impairments, anxiety, repetitive behaviors, and social deficits in ASD models. Ultimately, these findings provide a biological rationale for targeting the ucOCN signaling axis in non-pharmacological ASD interventions.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. Only one AE protocol was examined, and the effects of different exercise intensities, durations, and types were not evaluated. In addition, this study focused only on male ASD model mice and did not evaluate the influence of sex on the experimental outcomes. This design was adopted because ASD is more prevalent in males than in females, and sex-related biological differences, including the estrous cycle in female mice, may introduce additional variability in behavioral and molecular analyses. However, the use of male mice alone may limit the generalizability of our findings, and future studies should further evaluate the effects of AE in female ASD model mice. Moreover, molecular data from exercised wild-type mice were not included, making it difficult to determine whether the observed changes in the ucOCN/GPR158/BDNF pathway are specific to ASD or reflect a general response to exercise. This issue should be addressed in future animal studies. The specific cortical cell types mediating ucOCN/GPR158 signaling were also not fully identified. Further studies are needed to define the cellular basis of this pathway, validate these findings in human cell models and clinical cohorts, and optimize exercise paradigms to strengthen their translational relevance.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Two mouse strains were included in this study: 4-week-old male C57BL/6J (C57) mice, purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., were used as the wild-type control; 4-week-old male BTBR T + Itpr3tf/J (BTBR) mice were employed as the ASD model. Only male mice were used to reduce variability related to sex differences in behavioral and molecular analyses. However, the absence of female mice limits our ability to assess sex-specific responses to aerobic exercise, which should be addressed in future studies. At this age, mice present high cerebral synaptic plasticity and stable behavioral phenotypes, which is well suitable for exercise intervention and mechanistic research. All mice were housed in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care Guidelines (No. 201701134) of the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University. The mice were individually housed in a temperature-controlled, clean, and dust-free environment with adequate ventilation, and were given ad libitum access to food and water. The specific grouping scheme was as follows: 20 male C57BL/6J wild-type mice were randomly divided into a wild-type control group and a wild-type exercise group (n = 10 per group); 20 male BTBR ASD model mice were randomly divided into an ASD model control group and an ASD model exercise group (n = 10 per group). For the intervention protocol, mice in the wild-type exercise group, ASD model exercise group, wild-type control group and ASD model control group were all placed on a stationary treadmill without exercise intervention. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University, Daqing Campus and conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines for animal care and use.

Method details

Mendelian randomization analysis

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed in this study based on three core assumptions: that selected SNPs are strongly associated with the exposures of interest, independent of confounding factors, and related to ASD outcomes only through the exposure pathways rather than via direct effects. All data were obtained from publicly available databases and published literature, and thus no additional ethical approval was required. Summary statistics for physical exercise were derived from the UK Biobank and a previous study by Klimentidis et al.,55 while ASD summary-level data were obtained from the iPSYCH-PGC consortium through the IEU OpenGWAS platform below table. For instrumental variable selection, SNPs strongly associated with exposure at a threshold of p < 5 × 10−8 were selected, and linkage disequilibrium was controlled by setting r2 < 0.001 and a window distance of 10,000 kb to ensure independence among instrumental variables. Multiple MR approaches were applied, including MR-Egger, weighted median, and inverse variance weighting (IVW), with IVW used as the primary method due to its high estimation precision under valid SNP assumptions. The Wald ratio method was used for individual SNP analysis. To detect potential horizontal pleiotropy and violations of instrumental variable assumptions, we carried out the MR-Egger intercept test.56 Cochran’s Q test was also applied to assess heterogeneity in causal estimates, and a leave-one-out validation strategy was performed to exclude bias driven by any single SNP. Protective exposures identified in two-sample MR were further incorporated as observational parameters in multivariable MR (MVMR) analysis to better account for pleiotropic effects, with final conclusions primarily based on IVW estimates supported by MVMR.

Bioinformatics analysis

Bioinformatics analysis was performed on 9 samples from BGI’s multi-omics system, including 3 brain tissues from C57 mice, 3 from BTBR mice, and 3 from BTBR mice subjected to treadmill aerobic exercise; first, data quality checks and logarithmic transformation normalization were conducted to eliminate batch effects, and the “limma” package in R software (version 4.4.1, Vienna, VIE, Austria)57 was used to screen for differentially expressed genes between experimental and control groups. Genes with p ≤ 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes. Volcano plots of differentially expressed genes were also generated using R software; next, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were carried out using the clusterProfiler package,58 with GO analysis covering three main categories (Biological Process [BP], Molecular Function [MF], and Cellular Component [CC]) and significance thresholds set at q < 0.05 for GO and p < 0.05 for KEGG; then, the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed via the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING)59 online database, visualized using Cytoscape software,60 and significant modules in the PPI network were identified using the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE)61 plugin with parameters set as degree cutoff = 2, maximum depth = 100, and k-score = 2, while hub genes were determined using the Cytohubba62 plugin in Cytoscape software, and the top ten hub genes were obtained through five algorithms (MNC, Degree, BottleNeck, Closeness, and EPC) within this plugin; finally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (http://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea) was used to identify significant changes in biological pathways related to core genes,63 with the dataset sourced from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/msigdb/) (m2.all.v2023.2.Mm.symbols.gmt).

Aerobic exercise

This study utilized a treadmill exercise regimen with a 0-degree incline and moderate exercise load. In the first week, the exercise group underwent a three-day acclimatization training on the treadmill at a pace of 5 m/min, lasting for 5 min each session. After three days, formal training commenced, lasting 30 min each day. The formal training protocol began with a 0-degree incline at an initial speed of 3 m/min for 10 s, followed by a speed of 8 m/min for 20 s, and then a speed of 12 m/min for 30 min, conducted once daily for 5 days a week over a continuous period of 4 weeks. The control group received the same stimuli on the treadmill for the same duration but remained in a stationary position.

Open field experiment

The experimental setup for the open field comprises an open field enclosure, a video camera system, and software for analyzing recordings. At the beginning of the experiment, the animal is gently removed from its breeding cage and placed in the center of the open field, facing away from the experimenter, allowing it to explore without restriction. Video recording starts right away to capture the animal’s behavior in the open field for a duration of 10 min. After the experiment concludes, the mouse is returned to its breeding cage, and thorough sanitation of the base, objects, and walls of the open field setup is performed to remove any leftover substances (including feces, urine, or scents) from previous trials that might affect the results of future tests. Generally, a solution of 75% alcohol or a lab-grade disinfectant is employed to clean the entire open field apparatus, which is then left to dry before reintroducing the mouse, allowing the process to be conducted again.

Three-chamber social experiment

The experimental setup consists of a box partitioned into three separate sections. The central section is typically kept empty. In the left and right sections, unfamiliar mice are kept in small cages. At the outset, the experimental mouse is placed in the central section, where it is allowed to explore for a period of 10 min. After it acclimatizes to the environment, its typical behaviors can be observed. This is followed by the phase for assessing social interaction abilities. During this phase, a new mouse is secured in a small cage placed in one of the side sections, with the other chamber remaining vacant. The test mouse is then introduced into the central section of the box, giving it the chance to traverse all three areas for another 10 min. The amount of time the test mouse spends in the chamber with the unfamiliar mouse compared to the empty chamber is documented. In the next stage, known as the social preference assessment, a different unfamiliar mouse is placed into the previously unoccupied chamber, while the test mouse is once more placed in the central chamber, allowing it to explore all three sections freely for an additional 10 min. This portion aims to assess the mouse’s preferences for new versus familiar social interactions. After each experimental phase, the apparatus is sanitized using 75% alcohol to remove any scents or residues that could influence the subsequent mouse’s behavior.

Water maze experiment

Locomotion Navigation Experiment: In this study, clean water is introduced into the pool beforehand, and the platform is situated 1 cm beneath the surface of the water in any designated quadrant, maintaining the water temperature at around 24°C. The duration of the locomotion navigation experiment spans 4 days, with two training sessions held each day at consistent times. A specific quadrant is chosen, and the mice are released into the water oriented away from the platform. The process includes watching and recording the route followed and the duration needed for the mice to recognize the platform, particularly focusing on their latency in locating it. Spatial Exploration Experiment: Following the completion of the locomotion navigation experiment, the platform is taken out of the water. The mice are reintroduced to the water from the same starting point, and their latency to reach the former platform location, as well as the frequency of crossings over that area, is recorded.

Marble-burying test

First, prepare a clean and dry breeding box, and lay a 5-cm-thick layer of bedding at the bottom. Then, evenly distribute 15 standard glass marbles on the bedding. Next, gently place the mice in one corner of the box for a 30-min test period. A marble is considered buried if more than two-thirds of it is covered by the bedding. A higher number of buried marbles indicates more severe stereotypical behavior in the mice.

Grooming experiment

The mice were allowed to acclimatize for 1 h while being monitored in a tranquil, softly illuminated, and temperature-regulated observation chamber. Throughout the experiment, an individual mouse was introduced into a sanitized, clear observation box, where both the frequency and duration of its grooming behavior were documented over a span of 10 min. An increased frequency and longer duration of grooming suggest a greater presence of stereotyped behavior in the mice.

qRT-PCR

RNA isolation and quantification Cortex RNA extraction was performed using FreeZol Reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) supplemented with isopropanol precipitation and 75% ethanol washes. Purified RNA was resuspended in nuclease-free aqueous solution for subsequent analysis. Spectrophotometric quantification (Nanodrop 2000, Thermo Scientific) confirmed nucleic acid integrity, with 260/280 nm absorbance ratios maintained above 1.9 as quality control criteria. cDNA synthesis was executed under standardized thermal cycling protocols. Quantitative PCR amplification was subsequently performed on a LightCycler480 system, with cycle threshold values determined through manufacturer-recommended fluorescence quantification parameters. Post-amplification validation included confirmation of both amplification and melting curves. All primer sequences used in this experiment were rigorously validated for target specificity: GPR158: 5′-TGCTCCAAGAAGGGCTTAGG-3′/Antisense 5′-TCCCTGTATTCCCTGAGGACT-3′. Western blot

Protein extracts (30 μg per lane) were subjected to separation utilizing precast gels, after which they were transferred onto PVDF membranes. These membranes underwent treatment with primary antibodies, and subsequently, they were incubated with secondary antibodies linked to HRP. Chemiluminescent signals were captured and quantified using Image-Pro Plus. Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test were performed to verify data normality and homogeneity of variance. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Welch’s corrected t test was used for two-group comparisons. For multi-group analyses, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied, while two-way ANOVA was analyzed with Tukey’s or Šidák’s post hoc test according to pre-specified hypotheses.
