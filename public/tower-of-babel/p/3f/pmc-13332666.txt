Organizing Hematoma Involving a Native Tricuspid Valve
Authors: Zaid Naseer, Venu Peddibhotla, Sebhat Erqou, Aaron Schatz, Anna Tomdio, Daniel Ruiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBehçet disease (BD) is a multisystem vasculitis with variable cardiovascular involvement. Hughes-Stovin syndrome (HSS), featuring pulmonary artery aneurysms and venous thrombosis, is considered a rare variant within the BD spectrum. Intracardiac mass formation in either vasculitis is exceptionally rare.Case SummaryA 32-year-old man with prior mucocutaneous ulcers presented with hemoptysis and chest pain. Multimodality imaging revealed pulmonary arterial thrombosis and lobulated intracardiac mass attached to the native tricuspid valve (TV). Histopathology showed inflammatory organizing hematoma. Despite anticoagulation and valve replacement, the patient developed pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysms and died from fatal hemorrhage.DiscussionEight similar cases in the literature revealed a pattern of TV masses, pulmonary artery aneurysms, and hemoptysis in young male patients, suggesting a cardiac-predominant BD-HSS overlap syndrome.Take-Home MessagesAn under-recognized syndrome shares overlapping features of BD and HSS, a common denominator being unusual TV masses. In patients with pulmonary artery involvement, immunosuppressive therapy is critical, and anticoagulation requires caution given aneurysmal rupture risk.

History of Presentation

A 32-year-old African American man was admitted to the intensive care unit with intermittent chest pain and large-volume hemoptysis. His symptoms had evolved over several months and included a previously unevaluated episode of hemoptysis, followed by 1 month of drenching night sweats and weight loss. He had recently been incarcerated for 1 month. After release, he continued to experience poor appetite and recurrent hemoptysis. He had tested negative for tuberculosis (TB) prior to incarceration. The patient reported having tattoos done while incarcerated; he denied intravenous drug use. On admission, laboratory studies were largely unremarkable, with no leukocytosis and only mild anemia (hemoglobin: 11.2 g/dL). The comprehensive metabolic panel was within normal limits. Vital signs revealed sinus tachycardia with a heart rate of 130 beats/min and elevated blood pressure (143/95 mm Hg). He was afebrile, had a respiratory rate of 14 breaths/min, and maintained an oxygen saturation of 95% on room air. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the chest revealed a 4.0-cm mass within the right ventricular chamber, bilateral pulmonary emboli, and multiple bilateral wedge-shaped pulmonary nodules up to 2.0 cm in size (Figure 1). Past Medical History

The patient had multiple presentations in 2022 for painful genital ulcers and stomatitis. Infectious work-up, including testing for herpes simplex virus and syphilis, was negative. He was prescribed antibiotics but never filled the prescription. Lesions would self-heal and then recur sporadically.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included tuberculous endocarditis, intracardiac thrombus, Behçet disease, Hughes-Stovin syndrome, myxoinflammatory fibroblastic sarcoma, and inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor.

Investigations

Two sets of blood cultures were drawn from the left arm and hand, both yielding Staphylococcus epidermidis. Further review suggested polymicrobial colony morphology, favoring contamination. No subsequent blood culture yielded positive results.

QuantiFERON-TB Gold test was positive. However, acid-fast bacilli cultures from the patient's sputum demonstrated no growth.

Negative hypercoagulable work-up, including factor studies, von Willebrand factor, prothrombin gene mutation, and antiphospholipid antibodies.

Rheumatology evaluation including testing for systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren syndrome, and ANCA-associated vasculitis, was unremarkable.

Erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein were elevated at 36 mm/h and 5.4 mg/dL, respectively.

Transesophageal echocardiography identified 2 echodense masses: one irregular lesion partially adherent to the base of the anterior tricuspid valve (TV) leaflet and right ventricular (RV) free wall, and another attached to the interventricular septum with moderate tricuspid regurgitation (TR) (Figure 2). Histopathology of the excised cardiac mass revealed granulation tissue, fibroblast proliferation, and hyalinization, consistent with an exuberant inflammatory process such as organizing hematoma.

Management

Given the leading differentials were thrombus and endocarditis, the patient was started on a heparin infusion, empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics (vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam), and empiric antituberculosis therapy with rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. To aid in diagnosis, he was transferred to an outside hospital for resection and biopsy of the cardiac mass. Intraoperative evaluation revealed the large mass to be continuous with the septal and anterior leaflets of the TV. Because of the inability to salvage the TV, the leaflets were resected for better exposure. The mass, which was firm and large, extended from the septum into the subvalvular apparatus and apex. There was no clear plane separating the mass from the endocardium. Given the mass's encasement of the subvalvular apparatus, a free margin could not be achieved in any direction. A portion of the mass was sent for a frozen section, resembling myxoid tissue with vascular proliferation; and after complete excision of the mass, a 33-mm St Jude Epic bioprosthetic TV was implanted. Postoperatively, the patient's condition deteriorated, with multiple complications and large-volume hemoptysis requiring emergent intubation. Bronchoscopy revealed extensive airway clot burden, including large obstructive thrombi in the right middle and lower lobes. Patient was transitioned to fondaparinux subcutaneously daily after developing bilateral upper extremity deep vein thrombosis with an obstructive thrombus in the left subclavian vein.

Repeat CTA of the chest revealed a new right middle lobe pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm measuring 8.1 mm (Figure 5). Subsequent imaging demonstrated 3 discrete aneurysmal dilations of the segmental and subsegmental pulmonary arteries in the right upper and middle lobes measuring up to 10.0 mm, suggestive of pulmonary artery vasculitis. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient continued to report intermittent hemoptysis. Repeat CTA of the chest 2 weeks later showed no significant change in the pulmonary artery thrombi, with complete occlusion of all basal segmental arteries in the lower lobes despite continuing fondaparinux. Three stable aneurysmal dilations in the right upper and middle lobe segmental pulmonary arteries were again noted, situated just proximal to chronically occluded vessels. One month later, he presented to the emergency department and died from fatal hemoptysis suspected from pulmonary artery aneurysm rupture. Retrospective analysis revealed striking similarities to existing literature on Hughes-Stovin syndrome (HSS). The clinical course mirrored the pathognomonic 3 stages of HSS: thrombophlebitis, pulmonary artery aneurysm formation, and fatal aneurysmal rupture. Our patient met the international criteria for Behçet disease (BD) with a score of 6: oral aphthae (+2), genital aphthae (+2), skin lesions (+1), vascular lesions (+1). The most likely diagnosis was determined to be a variant of both disorders.

Discussion

BD and HSS remain poorly understood and are likely overlapping entities.1,2 HSS has classically been defined by pulmonary artery aneurysms, venous thrombosis, and hemoptysis, whereas BD is characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcerations with comparatively infrequent cardiac involvement.2 Cardiovascular manifestations of BD include pericarditis, myocarditis, intracardiac thrombus, and endomyocardial fibrosis.1 In contrast, widely cited definitions of HSS emphasize the absence of mucocutaneous features.2,3 Our patient demonstrated features of both disorders: recurrent oral and genital ulcerations and intracardiac involvement consistent with BD, alongside pulmonary vascular pathology and a clinical course mimicking HSS. In our patient, the question arises whether BD evolved into an HSS-like phenotype, given his mucocutaneous symptoms preceded his cardiopulmonary vasculitis. Mycobacterial infections have been proposed as immune triggers in BD,4 which is characterized by pathergy—an exaggerated inflammatory response to minor trauma. His recent incarceration, latent TB status, and high-risk tattoo exposure introduce potential immune stressors that may have acted as amplifying factors. Valvular surgery could have also acted as an iatrogenic trigger, reactivating vascular inflammation and contributing to aneurysm formation, as previously reported in postoperative BD patients without immunosuppressive therapy.1,5

To our knowledge, vasculitis is not widely recognized as a cause of intracardiac hematoma. Although established mechanisms include trauma, myocardial infarction, instrumentation, thrombolysis, and anticoagulation, vasculitis can disrupt endothelial integrity and coagulation homeostasis,1 creating a paradoxical prothrombotic and hemorrhagic state that may organize into a mass-like lesion. Within the realm of BD and HSS, prior descriptions of cardiac involvement have largely focused on intracardiac thrombosis and endomyocardial fibrosis.1 Furthermore, neither the existing literature on BD nor HSS includes inflammatory cardiac masses as a common feature. Such lesions are often misdiagnosed as thrombus, vegetation, or neoplasm. In our case, pathology revealed hemorrhagic, friable tissue without defined margins or intact endocardium. We propose that severe vascular inflammation caused bleeding within structurally compromised tricuspid valve tissue, with subsequent organization, neovascularization, and fibrosis forming a hemorrhagic mass.1,2,6

Management in our case was delayed by anchoring bias toward infective endocarditis. In patients with intracardiac masses that do not respond to anticoagulation or antibiotics, vasculitis should be strongly considered. Despite progressive pseudoaneurysm formation and recurrent hemoptysis, anticoagulation was maintained with heparin, bivalirudin, apixaban, and ultimately fondaparinux. This strategy likely contributed to the fatal outcome. It is important to recognize that filling defects in BD and HSS are the result of intra-aneurysmal mural thrombus formed in situ rather than pulmonary embolism.3 This inflammation-induced venous thrombosis is indicative of active vasculitis and requires immunosuppressive therapy, rather than anticoagulation alone.

We identified 8 previously published cases with strikingly similar presentations involving young men (ages 15-35) presenting with hemoptysis, frequently accompanied by painful oral or genital ulcers. In each case, further evaluation revealed an unusual right-sided intracardiac mass, most often attributed to cardiovascular involvement of BD (Table 1). Across these reports, the combination of anticoagulation and immunosuppressive therapy consistently resulted in clinical improvement, although surgical valve replacement was often required. Conclusions

The recognition of a potential BD-HSS overlap provides basis to the longstanding view that these entities are not distinct.2 Our patient demonstrated characteristic features of both disorders, and several recently reported cases describe strikingly similar presentations—young male patients with cardiac masses involving the TV, pulmonary artery aneurysms, and a rapidly progressive course masquerading as endocarditis. Collectively, these observations support the possibility of a fulminant cardiac-predominant subtype within the Behçet disease–Hughes-Stovin syndrome spectrum.
