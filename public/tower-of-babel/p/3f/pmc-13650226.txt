Integrative machine learning reveals a TLS signature and CCL5-CCR1 axis-associated immune remodeling in breast cancer
Authors: Chuyi Wang, Pengcheng Ding, Wei Ouyang, Yunhai Ding, Meilin Lu, Tianyi Li, Yunxing Xu, Liangfeng Zheng, Kai Chen, Feiyue Ji, Min Dai, Guiyuan Gu, Xiaomin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe transition from ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) to invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) is a critical but poorly understood step in breast cancer progression. This study characterizes the dynamic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME) during this transition, focusing on tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) and chemokine signaling.MethodsUsing an integrated multi-omics approach—including multiplex immunofluorescence of a clinical cohort, public single-cell and spatial transcriptomics data, and a longitudinal syngeneic mouse model (EO771)—we investigated spatiotemporal TME evolution.ResultsOur findings reveal that TLS reorganization during the DCIS-to-IDC shift is closely associated with the CCL5–CCR1 axis, which is implicated in macrophage-CD8+ T cell interactions and correlates with terminal T-cell exhaustion. Longitudinal modeling confirmed CCL5 network dysregulation alongside progressive CD8+ T cell dysfunction. Through an integrative machine-learning framework, we developed a robust 21-gene TLS signature that independently predicted patient outcomes in multiple cohorts, even after adjusting for clinical confounders. Finally, molecular docking identified cucurbitacin derivatives as candidate compounds nominated by exploratory in silico analysis targeting the CCL5 network. ConclusionsThis work highlights CCL5–CCR1 axis-related TLS dysregulation as a key spatiotemporal feature of invasion, provides a clinically applicable prognostic signature for early risk stratification, and nominates actionable targets to intercept invasive progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13402-026-01264-9.

Introduction

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is defined by clonally expanded neoplastic epithelial cells confined within the ductal system by an intact basement membrane [1–4], exhibiting cytological malignancy without stromal invasion. Progression to invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) follows a multistep trajectory, driven by the acquisition of driver mutations, dysregulation of key molecular pathways, and dynamic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment—notably through cancer-associated fibroblast activation and infiltration of immunosuppressive cell populations [5–7]. These biological alterations, including nuclear pleomorphism and the development of comedo-type necrosis, collectively mark the transition to invasive disease [8–10]. The transition to invasive disease is accompanied by marked clinical heterogeneity in progression risk. Early detection facilitates prompt identification of high-risk DCIS cases, enabling targeted interventions including surgical excision, radiotherapy, and endocrine therapy to effectively halt progression to IDC and minimize recurrence and mortality [11–16]. Left untreated, DCIS that progresses to metastatic invasive disease presents significant therapeutic challenges and is associated with markedly diminished survival rates. Elucidation of the underlying molecular mechanisms driving this transition may uncover clinically actionable biomarkers, permitting refined risk stratification and personalized treatment strategies to optimize outcomes while reducing unnecessary interventions.

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) are organized ectopic lymphoid formations that arise within the tumor microenvironment. These structures contain spatially coordinated aggregates of immune cells, specifically B cells, T cells and dendritic cells, along with stromal components including fibroblasts and endothelial cells [17–19]. Accumulating evidence has identified these structures as master regulators of breast cancer progression, with specific involvement in DCIS to IDC transition [20–23]. The formation dynamics and functional properties of TLS have thus emerged as a key research focus in modern cancer immunology, providing critical insights into tumor immune evasion mechanisms and guiding the development of targeted immunotherapeutic strategies [24–26]. Functionally mature TLS displaying distinct germinal center formation, prominent follicular helper T cell infiltration and memory B cell accumulation are significantly associated with attenuated invasive progression in DCIS [25, 27]. These organized lymphoid aggregates recruit naive T and B cells through chemokine signaling pathways involving CXCL13 and CCL21, initiating adaptive immune responses via dendritic cell-mediated antigen presentation [27–29]. This integrated immune response mediates both CD8+ cytotoxic T cell-dependent eradication of neoplastic ductal epithelial cells and tumor antigen-specific antibody production via terminal plasma cell differentiation, collectively preserving basement membrane integrity through coordinated immune surveillance.

The C-C chemokine receptor type 1 (CCR1) plays a pivotal role in tumor progression, where its overexpression facilitates neoplastic growth, metastasis, and immune evasion through multiple mechanisms [30–33]. CCR1 mediates the recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) into the tumor microenvironment via interactions with ligands CCL3, CCL5, and CCL7, thereby suppressing CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity and fostering an immunosuppressive niche [34, 35]. The CCL5–CCR1 axis promotes tumor cell migration and invasion by activating PI3K-Akt and ERK signaling pathways, consequently driving distant metastasis. Notably, combined blockade of CCR1 and PD-1 demonstrates synergistic enhancement of antitumor immune responses, offering a promising combinatorial immunotherapy approach [36, 37]. Despite these insights, it remains unclear whether chemokine-driven intercellular crosstalk within TLS directly potentially contributes to T cell exhaustion to permit the breach of the basement membrane in DCIS. Elucidating these spatiotemporal dynamics is crucial not only for understanding tumor invasion but also for identifying viable pharmacological targets to intercept this process.

To bridge these translational gaps, this study presents a comprehensive spatiotemporal analysis of TLS remodeling during the DCIS-to-IDC transition. Recently, cutting-edge machine learning algorithms have demonstrated unparalleled advantages in decoding high-dimensional multi-omics data and identifying robust clinical biomarkers. By capturing complex, non-linear biological interactions within the tumor microenvironment, these data-driven approaches offer broad application prospects for precision oncology and early risk stratification [38, 39]. By integrating in-house multiplex immunofluorescence with publicly available spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA sequencing datasets, we delineate the critical role of the CCL5–CCR1 axis in mediating macrophage-driven CD8+ T cell exhaustion within TLS. To overcome the lack of longitudinal clinical samples, we established an in vivo syngeneic mouse model to prospectively track the spatiotemporal dysregulation of chemokine networks during progressive stages of tumor invasion. Furthermore, leveraging an integrative machine learning framework of 101 algorithms, we developed and validated a 21-gene TLS prognostic signature. Crucially, we employed a combination of different algorithms to confirm its independent prognostic value across clinical confounders, and utilized molecular docking to identify cucurbitacin derivatives as exploratory candidate compounds targeting the CCL5 network. Together, our findings provide a robust spatiotemporal framework for early risk stratification and nominate in silico predicted candidate compounds for further pharmacological investigation in breast cancer. Methods

Cohort selection

The experimental workflow, including sample processing and analytical steps, is summarized in Fig. 1. This study involving human samples and data strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All included patients provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Haian People’s Hospital (approval number: 2025-K003-01) and the Ethics Committee of Hubei Cancer Hospital (approval number: LLHBCH2025YN-100). Study participants were female patients with breast cancer who met two core inclusion criteria: (1) complete clinical data, including blood routine tests, tumor markers, and other laboratory results, as well as breast ultrasound, mammography, MRI, and other imaging data; (2) specimen availability, with archived formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) breast tumor specimens. We retrospectively enrolled 55 FFPE specimens from young women with untreated primary breast cancer. To minimize confounding factors, samples were rigorously matched by two criteria: (1) tumor type, categorized as DCIS or IDC; (2) immunohistochemical (IHC) subtype, classified into four classic subtypes according to the St. Gallen consensus based on the expression of ER, PR, HER2, and Ki67: Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-positive, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC).

To validate the reliability and generalizability of our findings, we further obtained independent cohorts from public databases for external validation: TCGA-BRCA cohort, which provides comprehensive genomic, transcriptomic, and clinical follow-up data from multi-center, multi-subtype breast cancer samples; GSE181254 and GSE195861 from the Gene Expression Omnibus GEO database, used to validate gene expression and tumor immune microenvironment features; 10X Genomics Visium ST datasets, used to validate mechanistic findings at spatial resolution.

The scRNA-seq datasets were acquired from GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). For DCIS and IDC analysis, two datasets—GSE181254 and GSE195861—were processed, encompassing 15 tumor samples: 9 DCIS and 6 IDC. Samples were selected based on predefined criteria, with only tumor tissue retained for downstream analysis. ST datasets generated using the 10x Genomics Visium platform were obtained for one DCIS sample (https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets/human-breast-cancer-ductal-carcinoma-in-situ-invasive-carcinoma-ffpe-1-standard-1-3-0) and two IDC samples (https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets/human-breast-cancer-block-a-section-2-1-standard-1-1-0; https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets/human-breast-cancer-visium-fresh-frozen-whole-transcriptome-1-standard). All ST datasets are publicly available through 10x Genomics.

Cell lines

EO771 cells were purchased from BosterBio (Wuhan, China) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Animal tumor models

To establish an orthotopic mouse model of breast cancer, 1 × 106 EO771 cells were injected into the mouse mammary fat pad under stereomicroscopic guidance. Mice were randomly assigned into specific time-point groups (n = 4 per group). Mice were monitored twice daily, once in the morning and once in the evening. All animals were euthanized at 14/21/28 days post-injection. Tumor dimensions were measured using calipers. The investigators responsible for histological quantification and molecular analyses were blinded to the grouping.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIHC) staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections (4 µm) from DCIS and IDC specimens were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval in EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (pH 9.0) at 99 °C for 30 min. After blocking, sections were incubated sequentially with primary antibodies—anti-CD8 (Zsbio, ZA-0508), anti-CD68 (CST, 76437T), anti-CD4 (Zsbio, ZM-0418), anti-CD20 (CST, 48,750), anti-CD21 (Zsbio, ZA-0525), and anti-DC-LAMP (CST, 47,778)—at room temperature for 60 min. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 20 min at room temperature. TSA was performed using fluorophore-conjugated tyramides as follows: CF430 (Biotium, 96,053) for CD8, AF488 (AAT, 11,070) for CD68, AF594 (AAT, 11,082) for CD4, Cy3 (AAT, 11,065) for CD20, Cy5 (AAT, 11,066) for DC-LAMP, and XTSA690 (AKF, AXT6710000) for CD21, incubation at room temperature for 10 min. After each TSA cycle, antibodies were stripped by incubating sections in EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (pH 9.0) at 99 °C for 8 min. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Histological evaluation of TLS

The identification and quantification of TLS were primarily performed manually based on H&E morphological features. In cases where multiplex immunofluorescence (mIHC) staining was performed, it was utilized as supplementary confirmation. The evaluation was independently conducted by two experienced pathologists who were completely blinded to the patients’ clinical and transcriptomic data. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. TLS counts were quantified per square millimeter of tumor tissue to account for variations in tissue size.

Quality control of scRNA-seq and ST data

scRNA-seq data encompassing 9 DCIS and 6 IDC samples across two datasets underwent quality control (QC) using Seurat (v5.1.0). Standard QC metrics were independently applied to each dataset to ensure data integrity. Low-quality cells were excluded using the following thresholds: <1000 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) per cell, <400 genes detected per cell, >6000 genes detected per cell, or mitochondrial gene content > 20%. Batch effects from individual patients were mitigated using Harmony to integrate major cell populations. For the CD8+ T cell subset, integration was performed via canonical correlation analysis (CCA) followed by the IntegrateData function in Seurat. For Visium ST data, QC was conducted using Scanpy. Low-quality spots were filtered using thresholds of < 500 UMIs per spot, >3000 UMIs per spot, or mitochondrial gene content > 20%. Additionally, lowly expressed genes were filtered out, retaining only those expressed in at least 10 spots.

TLS score calculation for single cells and spatial spots

TLS scores for individual cells across the 15 scRNA-seq samples were derived using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function, leveraging a predefined 12-chemokine gene signature (comprising CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL8, CCL18, CCL19, CCL21, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, and CXCL13) originally established for evaluating breast cancer outcomes [40, 41]. Cells with TLS scores exceeding a 0.5 threshold were classified as high TLS score populations for downstream analysis. For Visium ST data, TLS scores per spot were estimated via the sc.tl.score_genes algorithm in Scanpy, with the identical 0.5 threshold applied to identify high TLS score spatial regions.

Cell clustering and annotation

Dimensionality reduction and unsupervised cell clustering were executed using the Seurat framework. To effectively mitigate inter-patient technical batch effects while retaining true biological variation, specific integration strategies were dynamically tailored to different cell-type hierarchies. For the global integration of major cell populations, the first 30 principal components (PCA dims = 1:30) were computed based on highly variable genes. Clusters were identified using the FindClusters function with a Louvain clustering resolution of 0.5, followed by visualization via Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) utilizing dimensions 1:30. For the fine-grained sub-clustering of immune cells in TLS-associated microenvironments, optimized dimensionalities and resolutions were introduced to robustly identify subtle cellular states. The TLS-associated T cell subset was integrated using the Harmony algorithm, with downstream clustering performed using 15 PCA dimensions (UMAP dims = 1:15) at a resolution of 1.0. For the deeper dissection of the CD8+ T cell compartment, batch correction was conducted via Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) followed by Seurat’s IntegrateData function; subsequent sub-clustering was guided by 15 PCA dimensions (UMAP dims = 1:15) at a higher resolution of 1.2 to adequately separate transitional and exhausted phenotypes. Similarly, the TLS-associated Macrophage subset was aligned via Harmony, utilizing 20 PCA dimensions (UMAP dims = 1:20) and clustered at a resolution of 1.2. Marker genes defining each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function, followed by comprehensive manual curation based on established canonical literature.

Differential gene expression and GO enrichment analysis

Differential gene expressions between high and low TLS scores regions were analyzed using the Seurat’s FindMarkers function. Genes were deemed statistically significant if they met the thresholds: log2 FC > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.01. GO enrichment analysis for biological processes was conducted on these DEGs using the enrich GO function from the ClusterProfiler package (v4.12.6), with significance defined as p-value < 0.01.

Analysis of cell-cell communication

To analyze cell-cell communication networks, we used CellChat (v1.6.1), a tool optimized for inferring signaling interactions from single-cell data. We first initialized the analysis pipeline with the create CellChat function, which integrates a gene expression matrix with cell-type metadata to form a structured analysis object. For ligand-receptor (L-R) interaction mapping, we relied on the CellChatDB.human database as our reference and applied the subset DB function to narrow our focus to secreted signaling pathways—this allowed us to prioritize extracellular factor-mediated crosstalk between cells. To quantify how individual L-R pairs contribute to inferred signaling pathways, we used netAnalysis_contribution, a module that calculates the relative importance of each interaction within a pathway. For visualization, we generated specific L-R communication networks using netVisual_individual, which highlights direct interactions between cell types. We also represented intercellular signaling strength across defined cell clusters as bubble plots via netVisual_bubble, a standard format for comparing signaling activity between groups. For spatial transcriptomic data—where spatial context is critical—we shifted to stLearn to assess ligand-receptor communication strength. This tool is designed to integrate spatial coordinates with gene expression, enabling us to map signaling interactions to specific regions of the tumor microenvironment.

Constructing a TLS-associated prognostic signature via integrative machine learning

To develop a TLS-based prognostic model for breast cancer, we first performed differential gene expression analysis between high and low TLS score regions, identifying 162 upregulated genes in DCIS and 184 in IDC (FDR < 0.05). To capture core TLS-associated genes conserved during disease progression, we extracted the intersection of these two gene sets. Subsequently, we combined this intersected gene sets with the predefined panel of 21 chemokine genes utilized for TLS scoring. This merging strategy yielded a final, curated cohort 51 candidate genes, which served as the core feature set for downstream integrative machine learning modeling. The 101 machine learning algorithms initially performed univariate Cox regression analysis to identify genes with significant prognostic value, and only statistically significant genes were utilized for prognostic model construction. We then applied an integrative machine learning approach with these algorithm combinations for model construction and screening using this refined candidate gene set, and ultimately developed a prognostic signature based on the StepCox[forward] and plsRcox algorithm. To develop a reproducible prognostic framework and rigorously eliminate the risk of information leakage, a strict data-isolation protocol was enforced. Prior to any gene filtration, rescaling, or model fitting, the consolidated TCGA-BRCA cohort (N = 1059) was cross-sectionally partitioned into a training dataset (N = 600) and an independent internal validation dataset (N = 459). Dimensionality reduction and model parameterization were conducted exclusively within the isolated training matrix. Initially, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed strictly on the training data to screen candidate features (p < 0.05). Subsequently, the stepwise regression algorithm StepCox[forward] was implemented on the filtered training subset to optimize the feature combination under the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Finally, Partial Least Squares Risk Cox (plsRcox) regression was applied to calculate the finalized unstandardized regression coefficients (βi). The mathematical architecture of the signature was entirely frozen on the training dataset. The prognostic Risk Score for each individual patient was formalized as a linear combination of gene expression levels weighted by their frozen model coefficients, calculated using the standard mathematical summation formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$RiskScore = \mathop \sum \limits_{i = 1}^{21} ({\beta _i} \times {X_i})$$\end{document}

Where (βi) represents the unstandardized regression coefficient for genei derived strictly from the training cohort, and (Xi) represents the relative mRNA expression level of the corresponding gene. The internal validation cohort and the independent external cohort (GSE58812) were never accessed during any stage of feature screening or coefficient estimation, serving purely as blinded, held-out validation series to evaluate the generalizability of the frozen signature.

Survival analysis was performed using the R packages “survival” and “survminer” to estimate overall survival probabilities, fit Cox proportional hazards models, and generate publication-quality Kaplan-Meier curves. Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted to visualize survival differences between risk groups, and the log-rank test was employed to assess the significance of these differences. Additionally, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis was conducted using the “timeROC” package to evaluate the sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve of the prognostic signature at multiple time points in the training, internal validation, and external validation sets.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10 and R v4.1.2 within RStudio. For histological evaluation, TLS metrics were analyzed at the patient level and normalized to tissue area. Comparisons between two independent groups were analyzed using the unpaired Student’s t-test or the nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Comparisons among three or more groups with a single independent variable were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), whereas data involving two independent variables were evaluated using two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post-hoc tests. For prognostic modeling, Hazard Ratios and 95% Confidence Intervals were calculated. All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates. All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments. The exact sample sizes (n) are specified in the respective figure legends. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Clinicopathologic characteristics of DCIS and IDC patients

Emerging evidence underscores the critical role of TLS in breast cancer pathogenesis (Fig. 2A). Comprehensive H&E analysis and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIHC) revealed striking spatial differences in TLS organization between disease states (Fig. 2B). While IDC exhibited significantly fewer total TLS (independent samples t test p < 0.001), including reduced peritumoral and intratumoral TLS counts (p < 0.001), they paradoxically demonstrated larger individual TLS areas compared to DCIS (p < 0.01, Fig. 2C). TLS immune remodeling features CD8+ T cell enrichment and B cell depletion during DCIS-to-IDC progression

Through mIHC analysis of TLS cellular composition, we observed profound immunological reprogramming within the microenvironment during the transition from DCIS to IDC (Fig. 2D). Quantitative analysis revealed that TLS in IDC harbored significantly elevated proportions of CD8+ T cells (independent samples t test p < 0.05), CD68+ macrophages (p < 0.001), and DC-LAMP+ dendritic cells (p < 0.01) compared to those in DCIS (Fig. 2E). Conversely, this progression was accompanied by a severe depletion of B-lineage populations, evidenced by markedly reduced infiltration of both CD20+ mature B cells and CD21+ follicular dendritic/B cell networks (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2E). These compositional shifts reflect a coordinated structural and functional remodeling of TLS during malignant transformation. Specifically, the concurrent accumulation of cytotoxic effector cells and immunosuppressive myeloid populations, alongside the deterioration of the B-cell compartment, suggests that these ectopic lymphoid niches dynamically evolve to differentially modulate cellular and humoral immune responses, potentially facilitating tumor immune evasion.

To further delineate the molecular basis of TLS remodeling during DCIS-to-IDC progression, we analyzed published spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA sequencing datasets. Applying Prabhakaran’s 12-chemokine signature, we computed TLS scores across three independent breast cancer cohorts. Spatial mapping of these scores onto matched H&E-stained sections confirmed the signature’s robust accuracy in localizing histologically defined TLS regions in both DCIS and IDC tissues (Fig. 2F). Having validated this spatial scoring system, we subsequently applied it to preprocessed scRNA-seq datasets to investigate TLS-associated transcriptional programs at single-cell resolution.

To delineate TLS-associated cellular niches at single-cell resolution, we projected the validated 12-chemokine TLS signature onto the single-cell atlas (Fig. 3F). Crucially, quantitative cellular composition analysis confirmed that the TLS-high microenvironment was predominantly occupied by classical TLS constituents—specifically T cells, B cells, myeloid cells, and plasma cells—whereas epithelial cells were strictly sequestered to the TLS-low compartment (Fig. 3G, Supplementary Fig. 2A). This distinct segregation aligns well with the non-epithelial nature of ectopic lymphoid aggregates. Statistical comparisons further demonstrated significantly elevated TLS signature activities within these core immune subsets (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001) (Fig. 3H), confirming the robustness of the scoring module.

Differential gene expression analysis between TLS-high and TLS-low populations revealed a marked upregulation of potent immune effector molecules, including GZMB, PRF1, NKG7, and CCL5 in TLS-associated regions (Fig. 3I). Consistent with this, GO enrichment analysis demonstrated that TLS-high cells were heavily enriched for pathways governing immune response-activating signaling, leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, and leukocyte-mediated immunity (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

To address the trajectory and directionality of TLS-associated alterations during tumor evolution, we directly compared the transcriptomic profiles of TLS-high populations between IDC and DCIS (Fig. 3J). This comparative analysis uncovered a distinct subset of genes that were significantly dysregulated in the IDC context. To further characterize this functional transition, GO enrichment analysis of TLS-high populations was performed for both DCIS and IDC (Supplementary Fig. 2B). These distinct GO enrichment profiles revealed that while TLS in DCIS maintained active immune signaling and leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, the TLS-associated niche in IDC exhibited altered regulatory patterns. Together, these transcriptomic and pathway-level alterations suggest a potential fundamental functional shift in the microenvironment from an immune-competent state in DCIS to a progressively exhausted and immunosuppressive niche in invasive disease.

Single-cell profiling of CD8+ T cell and myeloid states within TLS-associated regions

As pivotal cellular components of TLS, T cells play critical roles in TLS formation and TLS-mediated antitumor immunity. To delineate the compositional heterogeneity and functional states of T cell subsets within TLS-associated regions during DCIS-to-IDC progression, we subclustered the T cell compartment into five major lineages: CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, NKT cells, cycling T cells, and MAIT (Fig. 4A, B). Furthermore, differential gene expression analysis of the overall T cell population between IDC and DCIS revealed a profound transcriptomic shift, highlighted by the marked dysregulation of key immune-modulatory genes during tumor invasion (Fig. 4C). By integrating canonical marker gene expression with pseudotime trajectory inference, we further dissected the CD8+ T cell populations into seven distinct subsets: naive/central memory CD8+ T cells (CD8_T_naive/centr_mem), effector memory CD8+ T cells (CD8_ T_effector_mem_1/2), effector/exhausted CD8+ T cells (CD8_T_effector/exh_1/2), exhausted CD8+ T cells (CD8_T_exh_1/2) (Fig. 4D–G). Within TLS-associated regions, the broader myeloid compartment encompassing macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), and neutrophils functionally synergize with B and T cells to orchestrate critical immunomodulatory processes. Single-cell profiling of DCIS and IDC datasets classified myeloid compartment into nine distinct subsets based on signature gene expression: CCL7+ macrophages, ISG5+ macrophages, CCL5+ macrophages, CHIT1+ macrophages, IL1B+ macrophages, cDC2, Neutrophils, CD209+ DC and proliferating myeloid cells (Fig. 4I, K). Comparative compositional analysis demonstrated a preferential and statistically significant expansion of CCL7+ macrophages and neutrophils within the TLS-high regions of IDC (p < 0.05). Conversely, the proportions of CHIT1+ and IL1B+ macrophages were markedly depleted relative to their DCIS counterparts (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4J). These profound compositional shifts within the myeloid compartment suggest a dynamic remodeling of innate immune phenotypes, which likely plays a pivotal role in establishing an immunosuppressive TLS niche to facilitate invasive progression.

Enhanced CCL5–CCR1 ligand-receptor axis activity during DCIS-to-IDC progression

To elucidate the intercellular communication networks governing CD8+ T cell and macrophage crosstalk within the TLS microenvironment, we employed the CellChat computational framework to infer and quantify ligand-receptor interactions. Focusing on the CCL chemokine signaling pathway, our single-cell interactome analysis revealed active communication networks primarily driven by CCL5+ macrophages, CCL7+ macrophages and IL1B+ macrophages targeting effector memory and effector/exhausted CD8+ T cell subsets in the DCIS state (Fig. 5A). Notably, during the transition to IDC, while the interacting cellular subsets remained consistent, the overall signaling intensity and network density of these macrophage-T cell interactions were markedly amplified (Fig. 5B, D). To pinpoint the core molecular drivers responsible for this enhanced crosstalk, we systematically evaluated the communication probabilities of individual ligand-receptor pairs. This analysis unequivocally identified the CCL5–CCR1 axis as the predominant signaling pathway mediating macrophage-dependent T cell recruitment in both disease states (Fig. 5C, E). Crucially, to directly address the dynamic alterations of this network during tumor evolution, we performed a quantitative comparative analysis between IDC and DCIS. Statistical evaluation confirmed that the communication strength of the CCL5–CCR1 axis was significantly upregulated in IDC-associated TLS microenvironments compared to their DCIS counterparts (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5F). To spatially validate these single-cell predictions, we mapped the inferred ligand-receptor interaction scores onto Visium spatial transcriptomics datasets. Consistent with our computational interactome models, CCL5–CCR1 interactions exhibited highly compartmentalized enrichment specifically within and immediately adjacent to histologically annotated TLS regions in both DCIS and IDC, compared to non-TLS tumor areas (Fig. 5G).

Within the CXCL chemokine signaling pathway, CCL7+ macrophages, IL1B+ macrophages and ISG5+ macrophages maintain their crucial roles, while cDC2 also demonstrate significant regulatory functions (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Distinct from the CCL pathway where CD8+ T cells predominantly initiate signaling, the CXCL pathway features macrophages actively recruiting CD8+ T cells through chemokine secretion. During the DCIS phase, macrophages primarily engage with effector memory CD8+ T cell and effector/exhausted CD8+ T cell subsets, exhibiting limited communication with terminally exhausted CD8+ T cells. Remarkably, as progression to IDC occurs, macrophages markedly intensify interactions with nearly all CD8+ T cells, particularly establishing novel communication networks with terminally exhausted CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3B, E).

To delineate core ligand-receptor interactions within the CXCL signaling pathway, we systematically quantified their communication strength. We observed enhanced interactions between CCL7+ macrophages, IL1B+ macrophages and ISG5+ macrophages and effector/exhausted CD8+ T cell subsets via the CXCL16–CXCR6 ligand-receptor pairs (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D, F). However, their contributions to macrophage-T cell crosstalk were less salient than the prominent role of the CCL5–CCR1 axis, which remained a major correlate of immune cell recruitment and localization within TLS.

Construction of a TLS-associated prognosis signature based on integrative machine learning

To develop a TLS-associated prognostic model for breast cancer, we performed differential gene expression analysis between high TLS score and low TLS score regions in DCIS and IDC, identifying 162 upregulated genes in DCIS and 184 in IDC. Intersection analysis yielded 105 core genes consistently upregulated in TLS regions across both DCIS and IDC stages. We systematically analyzed these TLS-associated prognostic genes using an integrative framework of 101 machine learning algorithms implemented in Mime (v2.1.0), an open-source ML library for precision oncology modeling. The TCGA-BRCA cohort was randomly partitioned into training and internal validation sets, with GSE58812 serving as the external validation set. We computed concordance index (C index) across all datasets (Fig. 6A). Among the top 11 models ranked by mean C index, those built with Random Survival Forest (RSF) and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) algorithms demonstrated satisfactory training performance but failed in both validation cohorts (C index < 0.6), leading to their exclusion due to overfitting. Subsequent evaluation revealed that plsRcox, and StepCox[forward] + plsRcox maintained robust predictive accuracy across all datasets. As a result of comprehensive screening, StepCox[forward] + plsRcox was ultimately selected as the optimal model, achieving balanced predictive accuracy (mean C index = 0.654) and translational potential. Utilizing the integrated StepCox[forward] + plsRcox machine learning pipeline on the frozen training matrix, we successfully identified a consolidated panel of 21 core TLS-associated prognostic features. Discussion

The transition from DCIS to IDC constitutes a pivotal threshold in breast cancer progression, yet the immunological determinants governing stromal invasion remain incompletely resolved. Our integrated multi-omics analysis establishes that TLS remodeling associated with CCL5–CCR1 signaling serves as a critical biological bridge between pre-invasive and invasive disease states. Through spatial transcriptomics and single-cell profiling, we demonstrate that DCIS-to-IDC progression triggers profound TLS reorganization, characterized paradoxically by diminished TLS density yet increased individual TLS size. This architectural shift coincides with fundamental alterations in immune constituency: TLS microenvironments in IDC exhibit significant enrichment of CD8+ T cells and macrophages alongside depletion of B-lineage cells, suggesting a potential transition from adaptive immune-competent niches toward microenvironments favoring T cell dysfunction. Crucially, TLS-associated CD8+ T cells in IDC predominantly adopt effector/exhausted phenotypes marked by co-expression of TOX, PDCD1, and HAVCR2 – molecular hallmarks of terminal exhaustion that cripple antitumor cytotoxicity.

Central to this pathological reprogramming is the dysregulated activity of the CCL5–CCR1 chemokine axis. Ligand-receptor interactome mapping identified CCL5–CCR1 as the dominant signaling pathway, mediating crosstalk between CCL7+/1L1B+ macrophages and exhausted CD8+ T cells within TLS. Spatial transcriptomic validation confirmed compartmentalized enrichment of CCL5–CCR1 interactions specifically within TLS-associated regions, with signaling intensity significantly amplified during progression to IDC. This mechanistic link provides insights into how TLS – traditionally regarded as antitumor immune hubs – might potentially acquire immunosuppressive characteristics: CCR1-mediated recruitment of myeloid populations establishes feedforward loops that correlate with CD8+ T cell exhaustion, thereby potentially reflecting an impaired immune surveillance linked to basement membrane breach. Our findings imply chemokine-mediated alterations in TLS functionality as a potential contributor to invasive progression, revealing previously uncharacterized context-dependent properties that may reconcile discordant associations between TLS and clinical outcomes across breast cancer stages.

To translate these biological insights into clinical utility, we leveraged machine learning to construct a TLS-centric prognostic framework. Screening 101 algorithms against conserved TLS-core genes identified the StepCox[forward] + plsRcox model as the optimal predictor, incorporating a 21-gene signature (including the mechanistically pivotal CCL5). The model demonstrated robust risk stratification across validation cohorts, achieving consistent C-indices and time-dependent AUC values. Critically, high-risk patients defined by this signature exhibited significantly reduced overall survival, providing a molecular tool to address the pressing clinical challenge of identifying DCIS cases destined for aggressive progression. This signature bridges biological mechanisms with patient management by enabling early triage of individuals who may benefit from intensified surveillance or neoadjuvant interventions.

While the 21-gene signature provides robust predictive accuracy, we acknowledge the clinical demand for simplified assays. Feature importance analysis derived from the plsRcox framework (Supplementary Fig. S5A) revealed that downstream immune effectors, such as CD2, SIT1, CD52, and GZMB, carry the highest predictive weights. This hierarchy suggests that future translational applications could explore isolating these top-weighted features to develop more targeted, streamlined assays for patient triage. Notably, this analysis also highlights an interesting biological dynamic: although the upstream chemokine CCL5 exhibits a relatively modest independent mathematical weight within this specific collinear regression model, our spatial transcriptomics and in vivo validations suggest it acts as a critical biological modulator of TLS remodeling. Consequently, downstream effectors may serve as superior mathematical predictors for survival stratification, whereas upstream regulators like the CCL5 axis represent potential actionable targets for therapeutic intervention.

Several limitations of our study warrant consideration and provide avenues for future research. While our multi-omics and in vivo data reveal a robust association between CCL5–CCR1 signaling and TLS-associated immune remodeling, the current study is observational. Specifically, direct validation of the proposed CCL5–CCR1 signaling mechanism at the protein or functional level in human clinical tissues is still lacking. Direct functional experiments, such as strictly controlled in vivo blockade or genetic knockout models, are required to definitively establish whether this axis causally drives CD8+ T cell exhaustion and invasive progression. We recognize that our study evaluated TLS as a collective entity without strictly stratifying them into mature and immature states. Given that mature and immature TLS, as well as non-specific immune aggregates, carry distinct biological implications and prognostic values, the inability to precisely classify these sub-states represents a limitation of our current quantitative analysis. Future studies employing highly multiplexed spatial panels targeting specific germinal center markers are warranted to dissect the maturation-specific immune dynamics during invasive progression. Furthermore, while our 21-gene signature exhibits robust prognostic value in breast cancer, it did not significantly predict therapeutic response or survival in an external urothelial carcinoma immunotherapy cohort (IMvigor210; Supplementary Fig. S5B, 5C). This context-dependent performance highlights the profound microenvironmental and immunological heterogeneity across different malignancies. Consequently, rather than serving as a universal pan-cancer biomarker, our signature appears highly specialized to capture the unique microenvironmental dynamics of early breast cancer progression. Additionally, our clinical analyses rely on cross-sectional cohorts representing distinct disease states rather than longitudinal tracking of individual patients. Moreover, while the syngeneic EO771 model captures progressive immune remodeling during tumor outgrowth, it cannot fully recapitulate the biological complexity of breaching the ductal basement membrane unique to human DCIS. Finally, while our machine learning framework implemented strict data isolation to mitigate overfitting, we must honestly acknowledge the limitations in its long-term predictive performance. Although the 21-gene prognostic signature demonstrated robust short-to-medium-term accuracy in the training set, its predictive accuracy decreased substantially in the validation datasets, particularly for long-term prediction. Specifically, the 5-year AUC in the internal validation set dropped to 0.569. This explicit decline suggests that the signature currently possesses limited robustness for direct clinical implementation. Future iterations incorporating larger multi-center cohorts and additional multi-omics layers are necessary to refine the signature’s long-term predictive robustness before it can be considered for routine clinical practice.

The prognostic model offers a preliminary framework to inform precision prevention strategies, potentially supporting risk-adapted management for DCIS patients. Our mechanistic observations provide biological rationale for investigating CCL5–CCR1 axis inhibition – particularly considering previously reported synergy between CCR1 and PD-1 blockade. Hypothetically, modulation of macrophage-T cell crosstalk within TLS niches may alleviate exhaustion phenotypes and enhance immune surveillance, potentially delaying invasive progression. Future studies should validate the 21-gene signature across diverse cohorts and further evaluate the biological feasibility of targeting the CCL5 network in high-risk DCIS. Although cohort size limitations necessitate further verification, our work contributes to evolving understanding of immune evasion mechanisms, suggesting TLS serve as dynamic niches for chemokine-mediated immunosuppression during cancer transformation, with associated molecular signatures reflecting disease trajectory.
