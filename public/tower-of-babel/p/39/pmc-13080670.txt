Complex Aortic Plaques and Polycythemia Vera Presenting as Blue Toe Syndrome Without Peripheral Arterial Disease
Authors: Sneha Chebrolu, Shannon O'Connor, Sean Doherty, Arjun Aggarwal, M. Octavia Rangel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtherosclerosis can cause cardiovascular complications, but the risk is further exacerbated by comorbid prothrombotic conditions.Case SummaryWe describe a case of a 58-year-old man who presented with distal digit pain and was found to have complex aortic atherosclerosis and polycythemia vera that resulted in recurrent distal thromboembolism in the absence of peripheral artery disease. He had a prolonged course that required serial imaging studies and laboratory tests to elucidate the diagnosis.DiscussionThis case highlights the importance of addressing prothrombotic conditions in patients with complex aortic plaque. Clinical stability was achieved only through the combination of lifestyle modifications, anticoagulation, antiplatelet therapy, and appropriate treatment of polycythemia vera.Take-Home MessagesCurrent guidelines do not adequately address the complexities involved in managing patients with atherosclerotic disease and hematologic prothrombotic comorbidities. Research on antiplatelet therapy and direct oral anticoagulation for treating distal embolization from unstable aortic plaques is needed.

History of Presentation

A middle-aged man presented with 1 week of progressive pain in the left fourth toe. On physical examination, there was bruising of the distal digit with thickening of the overlying nail. He had tenderness to palpation with palpable dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial artery pulses. He was referred to podiatry and was treated conservatively. However, he developed worsening pain with purulent exudate requiring drainage and prolonged antibiotics for osteomyelitis. Although ankle brachial indices (ABIs) were normal, he was referred to vascular surgery given ongoing pain. Runoff computed tomography angiography (CTA) demonstrated small vessel disease, but there was no culprit plaque as a source for atheroembolism. Consequently, surgical intervention was deferred.Take-Home Messages•Serial multimodal imaging is helpful in elucidating the diagnosis and treatment response of complex aortic atherosclerosis.•Patients with aortic atherosclerosis and high-risk prothrombotic comorbidities may benefit from early initiation of antiplatelet and anticoagulation at the time of diagnosis.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of smoking and was incidentally found to have ischemic cardiomyopathy with reduced ejection fraction and polycythemia vera (PV). His family history was significant for premature death from myocardial infarction in 2 first-degree relatives.

Differential Diagnosis

The patient was thought to have early small vessel thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger disease) following his presentation with distal digit necrosis and significant smoking history. Given the development of bilateral lower extremity involvement and lack of peripheral artery disease (PAD), there was later concern for cholesterol emboli. Although he had no culprit lesion on initial imaging, subsequent imaging identified complex aortic plaques. He was also found to have PV, which has an increased risk of thromboembolic events and is the leading cause of mortality in patients with this condition.1,2

Investigations

The patient had a subsequent episode of toe pain 1 year after his initial presentation. Repeat ABI remained normal. He had continued smoking and was intermittently compliant with aspirin and statin. A referral was made to preventative cardiology for hypercholesterolemia (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]: 178 mg/dL). He was instructed to make dietary modifications and continue statin therapy. A few months later, the patient experienced worsening pain, now in his left third toe. Magnetic resonance imaging showed cellulitis without evidence of osteomyelitis, which was treated with ciprofloxacin. He ultimately underwent amputation of the left third toe for gangrene. Intraoperative cultures grew Staphylococcus aureus, which was treated with cephalexin. ABI remained normal. He clinically improved and successfully stopped smoking.

Several months after surgery, the patient developed pain in his right second toe. Repeat angiography (CTA-2) now showed extensive noncalcified atheromatous plaque involving the aortic arch and descending thoracic aorta, with penetrating ulcers (Figure 1A). Transthoracic echocardiography showed a reduced ejection of 25% to 30% with wall motion abnormalities in the right coronary artery and left circumflex distribution. The duration for which his ejection fraction was reduced was uncertain, as this was the first transthoracic echocardiogram obtained. Further evaluation with transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed a mobile, filamentous structure concerning for left ventricular (LV) thrombus. He was noted to have worsening thrombocytosis (platelet count: 752 × 103/μL) and was referred to hematology. Upon review, the patient had elevated platelets since his initial presentation, but this was thought to be due to infection. Further work-up with bone marrow biopsy and next-generation sequencing was diagnostic of PV.

TEE-2 a few months later showed severe atherosclerotic plaque in the descending aorta with a large mobile plaque (1 × 0.5 cm) as well as moderate plaque in the arch (Figure 2). CTA-3 was obtained for potential surgical intervention given the lack of improvement on anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy. Imaging now showed thoracic aorta thrombi and redemonstrated diffuse atherosclerosis with aortic penetrating ulcers and complex plaques in the abdominal aorta and iliac vessels (Figure 1B). It was thought that distal embolization of adherent material from an endograft or stent would outweigh the benefit of any intervention, so the patient remained on medical management. CTA-4 after several months showed scattered noncalcified atherosclerotic plaque and multiple shallow penetrating atherosclerotic ulcers within the ascending and descending thoracic aorta (Figure 1C). He later underwent left heart catheterization for evaluation of presumed ischemic cardiomyopathy, which showed chronic total occlusions of the right coronary artery and left circumflex artery (Figure 3). Cardiac magnetic resonance completed before catheterization showed thinning and near-transmural late gadolinium enhancement of the right coronary artery and territories. Management

The patient was initiated on aspirin after evaluation by vascular surgery, although he was poorly compliant. He later started clopidogrel after CTA-2 demonstrated extensive atherosclerotic disease. Given initial concern for LV thrombus on TEE-1 as a potential etiology, he was started on apixaban, and clopidogrel was discontinued. He was prescribed hydroxyurea after diagnosis of PV. CTA-3 later showed progression of atherosclerotic disease, so antiplatelet therapy was intensified with clopidogrel instead of aspirin.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient has not had recurrence of blue toe syndrome and remains clinically stable on clopidogrel, apixaban, statin, and hydroxyurea after markers of active PV normalized and aggressive medical management for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) was achieved.

Discussion

Complex atherosclerotic plaques can result in thromboembolism and distal infarct that mimic other small vessel arterial disease. Atherosclerotic plaque formation occurs after endothelial damage; subsequent oxidation and consumption of lipids by macrophages result in plaque formation. While normal endothelial cells inhibit the clotting cascade, this ability is lost with endothelial damage. Activation of the clotting cascade can lead to the formation of an overlying thrombus, which poses risk of embolization to downstream arteries. The management of atherosclerosis can be further compounded by the presence of hematologic prothrombotic comorbidities. This case illustrates several teaching points regarding the clinical manifestations, complications, and management of patients with atherosclerotic disease, particularly in the setting of PV.

Blue toe syndrome occurs when cholesterol emboli result in distal vessel occlusion and microvascular ischemia, causing discoloration of the toes.3 This can mimic thromboangiitis obliterans, but it has a different pathophysiology and management. Aortic plaque has a high risk of stroke and an even higher risk of peripheral emboli.3 The diagnosis of cholesterol emboli is largely clinical, however imaging can be used to confirm. The initial CTA did not extend enough cranially to reveal the atherosclerotic plaque that was found on repeat imaging. Hypereosinophilia can also be observed in the setting of cholesterol emboli, but this was not seen in our patient. The management of aortic atherosclerotic plaque includes lipid-lowering therapies, blood pressure and glycemic control, and antiplatelet therapy. There is a Class 1a recommendation in patients with symptomatic PAD for combination therapy with low-dose aspirin and low-dose rivaroxaban for the reduction of adverse ASCVD events.4 In this case, the patient maintained normal ABI, making the decision for aspirin and direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) initiation less straightforward, as he did not have PAD. There is also a Class IIa recommendation for low-dose rivaroxaban in combination with aspirin for medical management of stable coronary artery disease at high risk of recurrent ischemic events.5 This patient was initially placed on a full-dose DOAC for presumed LV thrombus that was later determined to be a loose aberrant mitral cord. Given his increased prothrombotic risk with PV and descending aortic thrombus, he remained on full-dose DOAC with apixaban. There is no current recommendation for antiplatelet therapy specifically to prevent cholesterol emboli. Plaques with high-risk features, such as thrombus overlying ruptured atheroma, are often treated with systemic anticoagulation. Nonetheless, there is a paucity of evidence regarding the type of anticoagulation that should be used. This management approach is only described in case reports or small studies and is not in any guidelines.6,7

This case was complicated by the diagnosis of PV, a type of myeloproliferative neoplasm associated with thromboembolic events and ASCVD. Thrombus formation in PV occurs because of hyperviscosity of blood and increased platelet aggregation. Consequently, both treatment and prevention of thrombosis are critical in patients with PV. Cytoreduction, targeting hematocrit to <45%, has been demonstrated to decrease thrombosis and cardiovascular mortality.1,8 Treatment options include phlebotomy, interferon, or hydroxyurea, as was used for this high-risk patient (defined by age >60 years and history of thrombosis before diagnosis).9 The patient had a hematocrit level of >45% until being on treatment. Antiplatelet and anticoagulants are used for arterial and venous thrombosis, respectively. Daily low-dose aspirin is recommended because PV is associated with increased thromboxane synthesis, which is inhibited by aspirin, resulting in reduced platelet activation.9 Vitamin K antagonists are the medication of choice for anticoagulation, particularly for splenic vein thrombosis, though there is ongoing research on the efficacy of DOACs in this population.9,10 Studies have shown that the combined treatment of cytoreduction and antiplatelet or anticoagulation has more benefit compared with any of these treatments alone.1 While our patient was initially started on apixaban for a presumed LV thrombus as opposed to venous thrombosis, this addition to clopidogrel and hydroxyurea appears to have controlled his symptoms. This patient also had aortic atherosclerosis, predisposing him to clot formation. We suspect that PV exacerbated the development of thrombosis in the setting of plaque rupture, increasing the risk for distal embolization.

PV is associated with ASCVD, thus management of ASCVD risk factors is important.11 The patient had a significant smoking history, which is known to contribute to atherosclerosis through endothelial damage and inflammatory processes, likely contributing to the development of aortic plaque and penetrating ulcers. He was able to discontinue smoking, which is important, as smoking is also associated with increased arterial thrombosis risk in PV patients.12 He also had hyperlipidemia and started statin therapy early in his course. There is no specific LDL-C goal for PV patients, but given their high risk for ASCVD, it is suggested that a goal of <70 mg/dL is appropriate.11,13 Our patient was aggressively treated for a goal of <55 mg/dL per provider preference, and he met this goal with an LDL-C of 47 mg/dL.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates the complexities of atherosclerotic thromboembolism with concomitant myeloproliferative disease. There is a need for further research and guidelines around the role of anticoagulation, particularly DOACs, and antiplatelets in the management of complex atherosclerotic lesions in high-risk patients.
