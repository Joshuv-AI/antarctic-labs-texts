Impact of a patient-centred levothyroxine decision support tool on rapid achievement of target dosage and improved patient outcomes: protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Solveig Robertsen Konst, Lars Hellemo, Beate Hennie Garcia, Vegard Heimly Brun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe hormone replacement drug levothyroxine is the most common therapy for people with reduced function of the thyroid gland. The optimal dosage varies considerably between individuals, and reaching the correct dosage is often a time-consuming task. To reduce the time needed for dose adjustments, we have developed a model for calculating each patient’s optimal dosage and an associated decision support tool (DST). We present the study protocol for a randomised controlled trial using the DST in initiation and adjustment of levothyroxine therapy for thyroidectomised patients. The aim of the study is to assess the tool’s efficacy on therapeutic target achievement and to investigate whether faster dose adjustments lead to better patient-reported outcomes on symptoms and health-related quality of life (HRQoL).Methods and analysisWe will conduct a randomised, controlled, multicentre, non-blinded, parallel arm trial with three intervention groups and one control group. We will include 240 patients undergoing total or completion thyroidectomy in three Norwegian hospitals. Patients allocated to the three intervention groups will have the option to use the DST in dose adjustments after the operation. Each intervention group will test a different version of the DST. The control group will follow standard care practice. The randomisation ratio will be 1:1:1:1. Our primary outcome is proportion of patients within biochemical target at 8 weeks postoperatively. Secondary outcomes are distance from biochemical target at 8 weeks, mean time to achieve biochemical target, change in HRQoL, adverse events and number of days absence from work.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Norwegian Medical Products Agency (CIV-23-02-042436), The Norwegian Ethics Committee for Clinical Trials on Medicinal Products and Medical Devices (547311), and the patient protection officer at the University Hospital of North Norway. All participants will give written informed consent prior to inclusion. Introduction

The hormone replacement drug levothyroxine is the most common therapy for people with reduced function of the thyroid gland. Despite the fact that 4% of the population in many countries uses this drug daily,1 reaching the optimal dosage in a timely manner still poses a clinical challenge.2 Standard clinical practice to identify the optimal dosage is to calculate an empirical or weight-based dose, which is consecutively adjusted according to thyroid function tests every 6–8 weeks until the patient is within the desired therapeutic level.3 Long half-life, variable bioavailability and narrow therapeutic window make this a potentially time-consuming task, requiring multiple doctor’s appointments over the course of months or even up to a year.4 5

Total thyroidectomy is a procedure performed mainly in patients with thyroid cancer, symptomatic goitre and therapy-resistant Graves’ disease. Whereas large goitres have become less frequent with iodisation programmes,6 thyroid cancer has had nearly a threefold increase in incidence over the last 40 years, with a yearly incidence of 14.3 per 100 000 in 2015.7 Patients undergoing total thyroidectomy have, in theory, the most predictable hormone requirement, as they do not have any residual endogenous hormone production. But even in this situation, several studies have shown that only one out of three patients becomes euthyroid with the first dose calculation, and time to achieve euthyroidism can be several months or even up to a year.8,10 The time to reach optimal dosage can be associated with symptoms of hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism and reduced quality of life.11,13

To overcome this challenge, several authors have introduced alternative dosage regimens.9 10 14 15 Using algorithms with different combinations of age, weight, body mass index, gender and lean body mass, they have been able to predict the optimal dose for up to 40% of patients.9 10 15 With various regression models and machine learning techniques, some authors have reached an accuracy in dosage prediction of 51–65%5 16 17 but are yet to undergo testing in a prospective setting.

Between 2016 and 2020, we developed a model for calculating the optimal levothyroxine dosage for patients following thyroidectomy.18 This model, along with an associated decision support tool (DST), was tested in a randomised controlled trial, demonstrating an accuracy of 40–59% in dose calculation for goitre and thyroid cancer patients, using narrow thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) targets. Additionally, the model reduced the dose adjustment period by 58 days in the cancer group compared with the control group.

With this project, we seek to improve the health and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) for thyroidectomised patients by shortening the time to achieve correct levothyroxine dosage. In this study, we will employ a new and improved version of the DST. The underlying model has been refined to improve predictive accuracy, and we will examine whether the model remains reliable with a reduced number of blood samples. Study outcomes will be assessed not only biochemically but also using patient-reported outcome measures. This approach will provide important information on whether the tool produces clinically meaningful benefits for patients. Furthermore, the study is designed with fully digital follow-up, which is expected to simplify study administration, enable faster communication between study centres and facilitate more efficient follow-up of study participants.

Objectives

The main objective is to evaluate the effect of our DST on obtaining biochemical TSH targets after thyroidectomy. Secondary objectives include investigating if a reduced time to achieve correct dosage corresponds to fewer symptoms of underdosage and overdosage, better HRQoL and decreased absence from work.

Methods and analysis

This protocol is developed in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials 2025 statement.19

Study design and setting

This is a randomised, controlled, multicentre, non-blinded, parallel arm trial, with three intervention groups and one control group. We will recruit a total of 240 patients undergoing total or completion thyroidectomy in three Norwegian hospitals (University Hospital of North Norway, Haukeland University Hospital and Oslo University Hospital). The three hospitals have a combined catchment area of 1.3 million people and conduct a total of 630 thyroid surgeries yearly.

Eligibility criteria

We will include patients ≥18 years with planned total or completion thyroidectomy for benign goitre or thyroid cancer. We will exclude patients with Graves’ disease, toxic nodules, anaplastic thyroid cancer, distant metastasis from thyroid cancer, pregnancy and patients who are unable to sign digital informed consent.

Recruitment and randomisation

Eligible patients will be recruited consecutively from the outpatient clinic prior to surgery. They will receive information about the study and be provided with a link to the digital consent form. A copy of the participant consent form can be found in online supplemental file 1. After inclusion, they will be randomly assigned to one of the four study groups in a 1:1:1:1 relationship (figure 1). We will use blocked randomisation stratified by sex and diagnosis (benign goitre or thyroid cancer). We will apply block sizes of 8 and a computer-generated allocation sequence.

The decision support tool

The DST to be applied in the three intervention groups is a medical software that predicts each patient’s individual serum TSH and free thyroxine (FT4) values in response to different doses of levothyroxine.18 20 Results from postoperative measurements of TSH and FT4, along with data on age, sex, height, weight, diagnosis and levothyroxine dosages, are fed into a custom hierarchical Bayesian model using Markov Chain Monte Carlo simulation.20 The model predicts each patient’s TSH–FT4 response and calculates the levothyroxine dosage needed to reach the specified TSH target. In addition to the original model, we have developed an improved version of the DST, exploiting data from the former clinical trial18 to further optimise the predictive properties of the model. For the new model, applied in groups 2 and 3, we have also included an expected delay in TSH response, and this delay is calculated for each patient. Information on the development of the original model can be found in the papers by Hellemo and Brun and Brun et al.18 20 Information on the development of the new model will be published in a separate paper. In the current study, both versions will be applied.

Medical therapy, dosage and treatment goal achievement

All patients will be started on levothyroxine before discharge from the operating hospital. Initial dosage is either weight-based or empirical and decided by the surgeon. The desired therapeutic level and target serum-TSH is decided by the surgeon and based on the patient’s diagnosis, risk-of-recurrence profile and comorbidities, in accordance with national and international guidelines. Postoperative dose adjustments will be conducted by the patient’s general practitioner or by an endocrine surgeon, depending on the hospital’s existing routines for follow-up.

Control group

Patients allocated to the control group (n=60) will be following standard care procedures for dose adjustments. This involves contacting their general physician for thyroid function tests (TSH, FT4, free triiodothyronine (FT3)) and dose adjustment 8 weeks after surgery. If dose adjustment is necessary, the general practitioner or endocrine surgeon calculates and prescribes the new dose. Dose adjustments will repeat every 6–8 weeks until the patient has reached the desired TSH target.

Intervention groups

Patients allocated to one of the three intervention groups (n=180) will give two or four blood samples for measuring TSH and FT4 during the first 2 weeks after surgery and use two different versions of the DST. Group 1 will give four blood samples and use the original version of the DST as in Brun et al.18 Group 2 will give four blood samples and use the new version of the model that includes TSH response delay. Group 3 will also use the new version of the DST but give only two blood samples during the first 2 postoperative weeks. This allocation enables direct comparison between the original and improved versions of the DST and investigates whether it is beneficial for the model to include more than two blood samples.

For the intervention groups, the participants will receive precompleted blood test requisition forms upon discharge from the hospital. They will then be instructed to bring these forms to their nearest laboratory for blood sampling. Test results will be transmitted automatically and electronically to the study personnel. The patient is asked to spread the blood samples evenly over the first 2 weeks following surgery. Specific sampling days are not mandated, as blood collection is often not available on weekends or national public holidays.

2 weeks after surgery, when results from the initial tests are ready, the patient has a follow-up from their general physician or endocrine surgeon. The physician will apply the DST and get a dosage suggestion. The physician then decides whether to adjust the dosage and if the suggestion from the DST should be applied or not. 8 weeks after surgery, new measurements of thyroid function are done. If the patient has reached the biochemical target, the dosage adjustment is finished. If another dose adjustment is necessary, they will consult the DST again. This cycle of adjustment every 6 weeks will continue until the patient has reached the target TSH (figure 2).

Follow-up

All patients will be followed until they reach target TSH or for a maximum of 18 months. Patients who have not achieved the biochemical target at 18 months will be referred to their general practitioner or an endocrinologist for further follow-up. All patient, independent of biochemical target achievement or not, will be asked to give additional blood samples and answer questionnaires on symptoms and HRQoL at 12 months and 18 months.

Treatment goal achievement

Dosages and biochemical target areas are in line with national and international guidelines.7 21 The initial TSH target is decided by the endocrine surgeon based on preoperative and perioperative assessment. The TSH target can be adjusted throughout the study according to histopathological features, risk profile and the patient’s response to treatment (table 1).

The patients can access their own record via a mobile phone app with secure two-step verification. Baseline characteristics are manually self-recorded into the medical record by the patient. Laboratory data are transferred electronically or manually by a researcher. All study-related questionnaires are distributed and answered digitally within the record.

Data management

All study data will be collected in a secure, cloud-based digital research record, which serves as the study’s electronic case report form. Access is restricted to authorised users with individual login credentials and two-factor authentication. Only personnel specifically invited to the research record will have access to the data, and all logins and data modifications are fully logged. On study completion, data will be de-identified and exported to Microsoft Excel for statistical analysis. De-identified data will be stored on the University Hospital of North Norway’s secure research server for 10 years, in accordance with international regulatory requirements for clinical investigations of medical devices. A separate anonymised dataset will be made publicly available. The study does not have an independent data monitoring committee, but monitoring will be conducted as stated under the section ‘Study Governance’.

Risk assessment and adverse events

The project has been assessed as low risk. The dosing tool is designed as a clinical DST and is intended to supplement, not replace, the clinician’s judgement. The treating physician may override the tool’s recommendations at any time without consequences for the participant’s continued involvement in the study or for data quality. Participants may experience short periods of overdosing or underdosing; however, such fluctuations are common in routine clinical practice. All AEs will be recorded, and any SAE will prompt an evaluation of whether the DST may have contributed and whether continuation of the study is appropriate.

Statistics

Sample size calculations

Sample sizes are derived from insight gained in the study by Brun et al,18 where 35% of patients in the DST group reached their target dosage within 8 weeks, compared with only 15% in the control group. Applying an α of 0.05, a power of 80% (β 0.2) and an enrolment ratio of 3:1 for use of the DST versus standard care, we calculated a requirement for approximately 50 patients in each group. Accounting for up to 20% drop-out rate, we plan for group sizes of 60.

Data presentation

A participant flow diagram will be prepared following Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials 2025 guideline.24 Patient demographics and baseline characteristics will be summarised by randomised treatment arm and overall using descriptive statistics.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be conducted by the research team according to a prespecified statistical analysis plan finalised prior to database lock, to minimise analytical bias. The full analysis set (FAS) will include all patients randomised to a treatment group who have undergone surgery and had at least one postoperative assessment of thyroid function. Participants will be analysed according to their randomised allocation. The FAS will serve as the primary analysis population for efficacy outcomes.

The intention-to-treat (ITT) population will comprise all randomised participants who provided informed consent and were entered into the study database. The ITT population will be used for participant flow descriptions and sensitivity analyses where appropriate.

The per-protocol population will include all randomised participants who completed the dose adjustment process without major protocol deviations, either until biochemical target achievement or for a maximum follow-up of 18 months if the target was not reached.

The primary analysis will be conducted in the FAS. The results from the three intervention groups will be pooled and compared with the control group. All statistical tests will be two-sided and evaluated at a 5% significance level, and effect sizes will be reported for all analyses. The primary outcome will be analysed using a χ² test, with treatment effects reported as risk ratios and risk differences with 95% CIs. Missing data for the primary binary outcome will be handled using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption, with imputation models including treatment group and relevant baseline covariates. Sensitivity analyses will include complete case analysis and worst-case/best-case scenarios to assess the robustness of the results.

All secondary outcomes will be regarded as exploratory, and no adjustment for multiplicity will be performed. Continuous secondary outcomes will be analysed using Student’s t-test, while patient-reported outcome measures will be analysed using mixed-model analysis of variance.

Subgroup analysis

The following preplanned subgroup analysis will be performed: Difference in patient-reported outcomes in patients with (1) benign diagnosis versus malignant diagnosis, (2) within biochemical target versus not in target at 2 months and 6 months. To examine the accuracy of the model, we will also do a separate subgroup analysis for patients who followed the suggestion of the DST. We will compare the different versions of the model, and the impact of the number of blood samples taken during the first 2 weeks.

Patient and public involvement

A representative from the Norwegian Association for Thyroid Diseases has been consulted throughout the planning of the study. All written material has been presented and adjusted according to his feedback. The representative, along with two pilot patients and six healthy adults, has tried and tested the electronic health record app and found that the burden and time required to participate in the research is manageable and does not exceed the benefits from participation.

Study governance

University Hospital of North Norway is the study sponsor. Study monitoring will be carried out by the Department of Clinical Research at University Hospital of North Norway. The monitoring will be according to ISO 141555 and focus especially on patient safety and quality of data collection.

The study has been approved by the Norwegian Medical Products Agency (CIV-23-02-042436), The Norwegian Ethics Committee for Clinical Trials on Medicinal Products and Medical Devices (547311), and the patient protection officer at the University Hospital of North Norway. Discussion

Optimising levothyroxine dosing after thyroidectomy remains a clinical challenge, as standard empirical or weight-based approaches often require multiple dose adjustments over several months. Prolonged time to biochemical target may result in symptoms of undertreatment or overtreatment and reduced HRQoL. This randomised controlled trial is designed to evaluate whether a model-based DST can improve early biochemical target achievement and patient-reported outcomes compared with standard care.

A major strength of the study is its prospective, randomised, multicentre design, allowing evaluation of the DST in a real-world clinical setting. By including only patients undergoing total or completion thyroidectomy, the study focuses on a population without residual endogenous thyroid function, thereby reducing biological variability and enabling a more precise assessment of the tool’s performance.

A key innovation of the current study is the comparison of different versions of the DST, including an updated model that incorporates an individualised delay in TSH response. This update showed improved predictive qualities in preclinical testing. The current study will also investigate whether the model is beneficial with a reduced number of postoperative blood tests. This will provide insights into the trade-off between burden for patients and model accuracy.

Beyond biochemical outcomes, the inclusion of validated patient-reported outcome measures and registry-based data on work absence allows assessment of whether faster dose optimisation translates into clinically meaningful benefits for patients. The fully digital study platform represents another important strength. The absence of scheduled in-person study visits reduces participant burden and study costs and may facilitate inclusion of patients living in remote areas.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study is non-blinded, which may introduce performance or expectation bias, particularly for patient-reported outcomes. However, blinding in this study would require all participants to provide all four blood samples, which would result in increased cost and increased burden for half of the participants. Second, medication adherence and the use of concomitant medications known to affect levothyroxine absorption, such as calcium or iron supplements, are not registered. This may introduce residual confounding. Third, patients with Graves’ disease and toxic nodules are excluded because persistent TSH receptor antibody activity and delayed TSH recovery may impair the model’s assumptions. While this exclusion enhances internal validity, it limits generalisability to these patient groups.

In summary, this trial will provide important evidence on the clinical utility of a personalised, model-based DST for levothyroxine dosing after thyroidectomy. If effective, the intervention has the potential to shorten time to biochemical target, improve patient-reported outcomes and streamline follow-up in a large patient population requiring lifelong thyroid hormone replacement.
