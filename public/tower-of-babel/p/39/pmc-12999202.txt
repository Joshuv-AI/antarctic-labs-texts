Obstetric trauma and its association with race and immigration: a population-based cohort study in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Irina I. Oltean, Parnian Hossein-Pour, Maya Rajasingham, Francis Nguyen, Susitha Wanigaratne, Rohan D’Souza, Giulia M. Muraca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The factors involved in the association between obstetric trauma and the race of the birthing parent are uncertain. We sought to assess the association between race and obstetric trauma, and the impact of immigration-related factors on this association.Methods:We performed a retrospective, population-based cohort study of people with singleton, cephalic, vaginal births at 20 weeks’ gestation or later in Ontario, Canada, between 2012 and 2021. The race of the birthing parent was recorded by care providers in prespecified categories (Asian, Black, White, other, and missing). The primary outcome was a composite measure of obstetric trauma (severe perineal, cervical, or high vaginal lacerations; injuries to the pelvic organs). We used multivariable modified Poisson regression to quantify the association between race and obstetric trauma. We assessed variation in this association by immigration status and duration of residence in Canada (among immigrant birthing parents).Results:The study included a total of 487 158 birthing parents, of whom 26.0% were recorded as Asian, 6.1% as Black, 61.0% as White, and 4.6% as other; 2.3% were missing information on race. Overall, the frequency of obstetric trauma was 5.5%; the frequency was higher among Asian (7.5%) and lower among Black (3.3%) people than among White people (4.9%). After adjustment, the risk of obstetric trauma remained higher among Asian people (adjusted relative risk [RR] 1.46, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.40 to 1.52) and lower among Black people (adjusted RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.78 to 0.94) than among White people. The association between race and obstetric trauma varied by immigration status and duration of maternal residence in Canada (p for interaction < 0.0001 for both). The risk of obstetric trauma was higher among Black refugees than among Black people in other immigrant classes. Among all immigrants, the risk of obstetric trauma decreased with increasing duration of residence in Canada.Interpretation:Rates of obstetric trauma were higher among Asian people and lower among Black people than among White people. Racial disparities in obstetric trauma varied by immigrant class and were attenuated as duration of maternal residence in Canada increased.

Obstetric trauma — defined as severe injury to the perineum, cervix, vagina, or surrounding pelvic structures during childbirth — occurs in 4.6% to 6.0% of vaginal births in Canada.1,2 These injuries increase the risk of longer-term complications, including bowel incontinence and pelvic floor disorders.3,4 Several studies have reported an association between race and obstetric trauma.5–8 A meta-analysis of 27 studies including more than 2.3 million individuals found that Asian people in high-income, non-Asian countries had a 1.6-fold higher rate of obstetric trauma than White people, with the highest rates observed among those of South Asian origin.5 Studies have reported that South Asian people have a 2 to 4 times higher risk of obstetric trauma than White people in high-resource settings.6–8 Conversely, lower rates of obstetric trauma have been reported among Black people.9,10

These disparities have not been explained by differences in characteristics such as parental stature, perineal length, mode of delivery, episiotomy, or infant birth weight.6,11,12 Asian people living in Asian countries have a lower risk of obstetric trauma than Asian immigrants living in non-Asian countries13,14 and, despite the lower observed risk among Black people overall, higher risk has been reported in Black immigrant subpopulations,15 suggesting that immigration-related factors may contribute to these disparities. Individual and structural discrimination can cause health disparities.16,17

In accordance with the theory of intersectionality,18,19 we sought to quantify the association between a birthing parent’s race and obstetric trauma and to assess variation in this association by immigrant status and duration of residence in Canada.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective population-based cohort study of people who accessed prenatal genetic screening and vaginally delivered a liveborn or stillborn infant 20 weeks’ gestation or later in Ontario between Apr. 1, 2012, and Mar. 31, 2021. We did not include those who did not access prenatal genetic screening, since we obtained information on maternal race from the genetic screening requisition forms. Prenatal screening is publicly funded for pregnant people in Ontario; about 65% to 70% of pregnant people participate in the program.20,21 We excluded those with missing, duplicate, or invalid study identifiers; immigrants with multiple landing dates; non-Ontario residents; those with ineligible health card numbers; and those with induced abortions, multiple gestations, and noncephalic births. We did not have information on gender. As all births were listed as being to females in administrative data, we hereafter use female-centric terms. We acknowledge that birthing parents may not have identified as women. We adhered to the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data checklist (Appendix 1, Table S1, www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250633/tab-related-content).22

Data sources

We conducted the research at ICES, an independent, nonprofit research institute whose legal status under Ontario’s health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze health care and demographic data, without patient consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. We used a combination of data from several data sources (Appendix 1, Table S2); data sets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES.

Exposure and outcome

Our main exposure was the reported race of the pregnant person, recorded on prenatal screening requisitions by care providers in prespecified categories, namely Asian, Black, White, other (none of the above or more than 1 race), and missing. We considered race as a proxy measure for potential systemic or individual racism.23,24

Our primary outcome was obstetric trauma, defined by the Canadian Institute for Health Information as at least 1 of the following: obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI) involving third- or fourth-degree perineal tears; cervical or high vaginal laceration; injury to the bladder or urethra; broad ligaments of the uterus; and other pelvic trauma, including obstetric injury to the pelvic organs, pelvic joints, or ligaments, as well as pelvic hematoma (Appendix 1, Table S3).25,26

Covariates

We selected study covariates through literature review,27–29 expert consultation, and our conceptual framework about how race, immigration status, and other factors were related to obstetric trauma (Appendix 1, Figure S1). We included maternal characteristics (age, parity, primary language, prepregnancy body mass index); maternal neighbourhood-level socioeconomic indicators, including material deprivation quintile (a validated index closely related to poverty)30 and residential population density, defined as rural (≤ 10 000 population) or urban (> 10 000 population); 31 health behaviours (tobacco use in pregnancy, drug and substance exposure in pregnancy); antenatal factors, including conception type (spontaneous v. assisted), first-trimester prenatal visit (yes v. no), any diabetes, any hypertension, and previous cesarean delivery; and labour and delivery characteristics, including induction, augmentation, duration of the second stage of labour, episiotomy (mediolateral, midline, none), mode of birth (spontaneous vaginal birth, forceps, or vacuum), and infant birth weight. We examined total and direct effects using crude and adjusted estimates to isolate the effect of maternal race unexplained by clinical or labour factors.

Effect modifiers

We examined maternal immigrant class and duration of residence in Canada separately as effect modifiers. We defined immigrant class as economic, sponsored family, resettled refugee or protected person, other immigrant, and nonimmigrant or long-term resident. The latter category included those born in Canada and those who immigrated before 1985, since information on immigration was available only from 1985 onward. We calculated duration of maternal residence as the difference between the date of the index birth and date of landing during the period when immigration records were available until the end of the study period (Jan. 1, 1985, to Mar. 31, 2021).

Statistical analysis

We compared the distribution of related characteristics by maternal race using frequencies, proportions, medians, and inter-quartile ranges (IQRs). We used multivariable modified Poisson regression models to quantify the association between maternal race and obstetric trauma to obtain adjusted relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We also calculated risk differences and adjusted risk differences. We defined White birthing parents as the reference category since this group is the least likely to experience racial discrimination. We assumed that missingness was informative and applied the missing indicator approach to variables with more than 3% missing observations (maternal prepregnancy body mass index, primary language, first prenatal visit, and type of conception).32

To evaluate potential effect modification of the association between maternal race and obstetric trauma, we included interaction (product) terms for race by immigration status and race by duration of residence in 2 separate regression models. The latter model included only immigrants, and duration of residence was modelled as a continuous mean-centred variable measured in years. We assessed effect modification by using the likelihood ratio test to compare models with and without the interaction term, and visually by plotting the probability of obstetric trauma by duration of residence in each race group.

We conducted all analyses in SAS Enterprise 8.3 (SAS Institute).

Sensitivity analyses

We compared the distribution of all relevant characteristics and outcomes among parents who did not access prenatal screening with those who did, using standardized differences. We considered a difference of 0.1 or greater to indicate a meaningful imbalance.33

Given that a parent’s mother tongue may indicate how they are perceived within the health care system independent of their race or country of birth,34–36 we explored variation in rates of obstetric trauma by maternal mother tongue and mode of birth among Asian and Black immigrants. Mother tongue (defined as the first language learned at home during childhood and still understood at the time of immigration) was collected by Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada, whereby all immigrants selected their mother tongue from a prespecified dropdown menu or in a free-text field.37,38 We mapped each mother tongue to the world region where it is predominantly spoken, identified using publicly available data sources (Appendix 1, Table S4).39,40 Previous studies have used mother tongue to examine potential differences in outcomes among race or ethnicity groups.35,36

Ethics approval

The project was approved by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (no. 16576).

Results

There were 743 379 singleton, cephalic, vaginal births at 20 weeks’ gestation or later in Ontario between Apr. 1, 2012, and Mar. 31, 2021. We included 487 158 (65.5%) with prenatal screening records (Figure 1). The proportion of Asian, Black, White, and other parents was 126 744 (26.0.%), 29 733 (6.1%), 297 292 (61.0%), and 22 235 (4.6%), respectively; 2.3% were missing information about race. Overall, 86.5% of births were spontaneous, 3.1% involved the use of forceps, and 10.4% involved vacuum extraction. The proportion of spontaneous vaginal births was highest among Black parents, and the proportion of vacuum extractions was highest among Asian parents; however, the absolute differences in mode of birth were small (Appendix 1, Figure S2).

Compared with White parents, Asian parents were more likely to speak a primary language other than English or French (22.6% v. 3.7%), be sponsored family immigrants (34.6% v. 5.1%), have diabetes (pre-existing: 2.6% v. 1.2%; gestational: 12.7% v. 4.8%), have vacuum-assisted births (13.7% v. 9.1%), and have a mediolateral episiotomy (17.5% v. 10.3%) (Table 1). Black parents were more likely to be multiparous, in the most materially deprived quintile, and have a shorter second-stage labour duration (Table 1). Both Asian and Black parents were less likely than White parents to live in rural areas, smoke during pregnancy, and have babies weighing 4500 g or more. The median duration of maternal residence in Canada was lowest among Asian immigrants (6.1 yr, IQR 2.6 to 12.4 yr) (Table 1).

Association between maternal race and obstetric trauma

Obstetric trauma occurred in 5.5% of the total population; OASI accounted for more than 80% of obstetric trauma (Appendix 1, Table S5). Compared with White parents (4.9%), crude frequencies of obstetric trauma were higher among Asian (7.5%) and lower among Black (3.3%) parents, and similar among those in the other race group (4.8%). After adjustment, Asian parents had a 46% higher risk of obstetric trauma than White parents (adjusted RR 1.46, 95% CI 1.40 to 1.52); the risk was lower among Black parents (adjusted RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.78 to 0.94) (Table 2).

We found variation in the association between maternal race and obstetric trauma by immigrant class and duration of maternal residence in Canada (p for interaction < 0.0001 for both). Specifically, the risk of obstetric trauma was higher among Black refugees (adjusted RR 1.33, 95% CI 1.16 to 1.53) and Black economic immigrants (adjusted RR 1.23, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.49), compared with Black parents in the other immigrant classes and White non-immigrants or long-term residents (Figure 2 and Appendix 1, Table S6). The risk of obstetric trauma decreased among all immigrants with increasing duration of residence (Figure 3). Among Asian immigrants, each additional year of residence was associated with a 2% lower risk of obstetric trauma (adjusted RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.98 to 0.99) compared with White immigrants.

Sensitivity analyses

Thirty-five percent of parents did not access prenatal genetic screening. The incidence of obstetric trauma was lower (4.6%) in this population than in the main analytic cohort. Those who did not access prenatal genetic screening were more likely to be multiparous (63.5% v. 55.1%), be younger than 25 years or 40 years or older, be a nonimmigrant or long-term resident (80.1% v. 68.2%), reside in a rural area (16.0% v. 7.2%), and use tobacco during pregnancy (10.5% v. 5.2%) (Appendix 1, Table S7).

Among Asian immigrant parents, those whose mother tongue was from South Asia (predominantly Punjabi) had higher crude rates of obstetric trauma than those whose mother tongue was English and nonimmigrants or long-term residents (Appendix 1, Figure S3A). Among Black immigrants, parents whose mother tongue was from East Africa (primarily Somali) had higher crude rates of obstetric trauma than those whose mother tongue was English (Appendix 1, Figure S3B).

Interpretation

In this population-based cohort study of parents with singleton vaginal births who accessed prenatal genetic screening in Ontario between 2012 and 2021, we found that the risk of obstetric trauma was nearly 1.5-fold higher among Asian parents than among White parents. The relationship between maternal race and obstetric trauma varied by immigrant class. Among immigrants, the risk of obstetric trauma decreased with increasing duration in Canada; we found a steeper decline among Asian immigrants than among White immigrants. Exploratory analysis suggested higher rates of obstetric trauma among Asian immigrants with South Asian mother tongue and Black immigrants with East African mother tongue; these results should be interpreted with caution, as the methods were exploratory.

Our finding of an increased risk of obstetric trauma among Asian parents aligns with previous population-based analyses demonstrating increased risk among Asian parents, driven by high obstetric trauma rates among South Asians.29,41 The finding that Black parents had a lower risk of obstetric trauma than White parents is also consistent with previous studies.42,43

We found an interaction between maternal race and immigration factors. Black immigrants who were refugees or economic immigrants had a higher risk of trauma than their White counterparts and Black parents in other immigrant classes. International studies have reported higher odds of obstetric trauma among immigrants from Africa (e.g., Eritrea, Ethiopia, Sudan) than among nonimmigrants.7,15 In this analysis, we were able to include duration in Canada, a social factor that may affect the risk of experiencing language or accent discrimination among immigrants. Indeed, immigrants with shorter duration of residence in Canada had a higher initial risk of obstetric trauma, and the risk declined with increasing duration. This could be explained by better prenatal care, earlier interventions, informed decision-making, more integration, and improved English language skills.44,45

Our exploratory analysis suggested that the frequency of obstetric trauma varied among subgroups of Asian and Black immigrant parents based on world region of maternal mother tongue. Prior studies have suggested that parents whose mother tongue is not English have a twofold higher risk of obstetric trauma than those whose primary language is English. 46 Moreover, linguistic discrimination may impede timely intervention47,48 and result in communication challenges, leading to poor patient understanding of prenatal care and guidance throughout labour and delivery.28 An evaluation of whether culturally or racially concordant care can attenuate the high rates of obstetric trauma among childbearing individuals in Canada is warranted, as evidence suggests that having health care providers of the same racial, ethnic, or linguistic background can enhance communication, trust, and overall health care experiences for patients.49,50

Limitations

Recognition and documentation of obstetric trauma may vary; however, indicators for obstetric trauma have been shown to be highly accurate in Canadian health administrative databases.51 Care providers may have assigned maternal race categories that did not align with patients’ self-identified race, resulting in potential misclassification. External validity of our findings is compromised because we ascertained race only among people who accessed prenatal genetic screening. Currently, race-disaggregated data on pregnancy outcomes in Canada are scarce; efforts are ongoing to address this, but it will be years before these data are available. Although our cohort was limited to people who underwent prenatal genetic screening, they represented around 65% of the population and closely reflected the racial composition of Ontario’s overall population. 52 Our analyses were constrained by the limited classifications of race (e.g., the term “Asian” includes many different races and ethnicities) and the absence of information about Indigenous identity. However, we did conduct intersectional analyses assessing heterogeneity in obstetric trauma rates within race groups by immigration-related factors and maternal mother tongue. A person’s mother tongue does not necessarily reflect whether they are perceived as belonging to a linguistic minority, and thus this exploratory analysis should be interpreted with caution. Lastly, the absence of data on English fluency, maternal education, and income may have influenced the observed associations.

Conclusion

We found that the racial disparities in the frequency of obstetric trauma differed by immigrant class and diminished as the duration of maternal residence in Canada increased, suggesting that obstetric trauma is modifiable through factors such as improved health care access, communication, or acculturation. These findings could inform policies to reduce disparities earlier after immigrants arrive in Canada, such as targeted prenatal care programs for newcomers, and the development of culturally competent and equitable care strategies.
