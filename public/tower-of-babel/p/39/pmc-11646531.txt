Outcomes of Adults With Unrepaired Partial Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Return and Intact Atrial Septum
Authors: Alexander C Egbe, Ahmed Younis, Ahmed E Ali, Snigdha Karnakoti, Elizabeth H Stephens, Joseph A Dearani, Christopher Francois, Heidi M Connolly
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study was to describe the correlates and outcomes in adults with unrepaired partial anomalous pulmonary venous return and intact atrial septum (PAPVR‐IAS).Methods and ResultsWe identified adults with PAPVR‐IAS who received care at the Mayo Clinic, while those with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS comprised the reference group. Clinical indices (New York Heart Association class, peak oxygen consumption, and NT‐proBNP [N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide]) and echo‐derived right heart indices (right atrial [RA] volume, RA reservoir strain, right ventricular [RV] free wall strain, RV end‐diastolic area, and RV systolic pressure) were assessed at baseline and 3‐year and 5‐year follow‐up. There were 80 patients and 38 patients with unrepaired versus repaired PAPVR‐IAS, respectively. The clinical predictors of surgical repair were the number of anomalous veins, RA volume, and RV end‐diastolic area. The PAPVR‐IAS risk score, derived from these clinical predictors, was associated with surgical repair (adjusted odds ratio, 1.37 [95% CI, 1.24–1.65] per unit increase in risk score; area under the curve, 0.742). Among patients with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS with 3‐year (n=73) and 5‐year follow‐up (n=36), there was no temporal change in clinical indices (New York Heart Association class, predicted peak oxygen consumption, and NT‐proBNP) and right heart indices (RA volume index, RA reservoir strain, RV end‐diastolic area index, RV free wall strain, and RV systolic pressure).ConclusionsThe PAPVR‐IAS risk score can be used to assess the odds of requiring surgical repair. Furthermore, there was no temporal deterioration in clinical and right heart indices during follow‐up in adults with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS.Keywords: anomalous pulmonary vein, atrial septal defect, left‐to‐right shuntSubject Categories: Congenital Heart Disease

Isolated partial anomalous pulmonary venous return (PAPVR) is a rare congenital heart disease (CHD) that is characterized by an abnormal connection of ≥1 pulmonary veins to a systemic vein or the right atrium (RA). 1 , 2 This leads to left‐to‐right shunt, right heart volume overload, and right heart remodeling. 3 The severity of right heart volume overload and remodeling is dependent on the number of anomalous veins and the presence of an atrial septal defect (ASD). 3 The American and European guidelines for the management of adults with CHD recommend surgical repair of PAPVR in patients with significant left‐to‐right shunt defined as the ratio of pulmonary blood flow to systemic blood flow (Qp/Qs) ≥1.5, right ventricular (RV) enlargement, or functional impairment. 4 , 5 Surgical repair of isolated PAPVR involves rerouting of anomalous vein(s) to the left atrium using an intra‐atrial baffle or direct reimplantation to the left atrial appendage, and closure of a concomitant ASD. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Although, the surgical repair of PAPVR is associated with low risk of operative death, 5% to 10% of the patients may experience complications such as sinus node dysfunction, superior vena cava stenosis, or pulmonary vein stenosis. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 This low, but not negligible, risk of complications supports conservative management in patients with PAPVR without significant right heart volume overload. 4 , 5

About 20% of patients with PAPVR have an intact atrial septum (PAPVR‐IAS), and these patients have a smaller volume of left‐to‐right shunt as compared with patients with PAPVR and ASD, with similar number of anomalous veins. 3 As a result, patients with PAPVR‐IAS should have less right heart remodeling, which in turn would influence the threshold for surgical intervention. 3 While the outcome of surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS has been described, there are limited data about outcomes of adults with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS. 3 , 9 The purpose of this study was, therefore, to describe the clinical outcomes of adults with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS and to identify the clinical characteristics associated with undergoing surgical repair.

Methods

Study Population

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board approved this study. In this retrospective study, we identified adults (aged≥18 years) with PAPVR‐IAS who received care at the Mayo Clinic from January 1, 2003, to December 31, 2022. Of 118 patients that met the study inclusion criteria, the diagnosis of PAPVR‐IAS was based on transesophageal echocardiogram in 104 patients (88%), chest computed tomography angiography in 77 patients (65%), and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMRI) in 41 patients (35%). From this cohort, we identified consecutive patients who did not undergo surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS during follow‐up, and these patients comprised the study group. The patients who underwent surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS comprised the reference group. The institutional criteria for surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS include any of the following: (1) Qp/Qs ≥1.5 based on CMRI or invasive hemodynamic assessment; (2) right heart volume overload defined as moderate or greater RV dilation based on echocardiography or CMRI; or (3) functional or aerobic impairment defined as New York Heart Association functional class >II or predicted peak oxygen consumption <50%. The clinical characteristics and outcomes of the patients who underwent surgical repair of PAPVR at the Mayo Clinic has been previously described. 6 , 7 , 9 The first clinical encounter in the adult CHD clinic before surgical intervention was considered the baseline encounter.

Study Objectives

The aim of this study was to describe the clinical and hemodynamic characteristics of patients with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS and identify the predictors of surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS (clinical and hemodynamic indices associated with surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS) using data derived from the baseline characteristics of the cohort. These predictors of surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS were used to create PAPVR‐IAS risk score (see Statistical Analysis section for details). The purpose of the PAPVR‐IAS risk score is for risk stratification to identify patients likely to require surgical repair of PAPVR. We also aimed to assess temporal changes in clinical and right heart indices in patients with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS.

Data Collection

Clinical Indices

The following clinical indices were assessed: (1) functional capacity assessed using the New York Heart Association functional class; (2) aerobic capacity as measured by predicted peak oxygen consumption; and (3) neurohormonal activation as measured by NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide). These clinical indices were assessed at the baseline encounter (within 6 months from the first encounter in the adult CHD clinic), 3‐year follow‐up (2–4 years from baseline encounter), and 5‐year follow‐up (4–6 years from baseline encounter).

Right Heart Indices

Right heart structure, function, and hemodynamics were assessed using 2‐dimensional, Doppler, and speckle tracking echocardiography. 10 , 11 , 12 For the purpose of this study, we assessed the following right heart indices: (1) RA size as measured by RA volume index; (2) RA function as measured by RA reservoir strain; (3) RV size as measured by RV end‐diastolic area index; (4) RV systolic function as measured by RV free wall strain; (5) pulmonary hypertension as measured by RV systolic pressure. Similarly, these right heart indices were assessed at the baseline encounter (within 6 months from the first encounter in the adult CHD clinic), 3‐year follow‐up (2–4 years from baseline encounter), and 5‐year follow‐up (4–6 years from baseline encounter). Additionally, we reviewed CMRI reports and cardiac catheterization reports in patients who underwent these assessments within 6 months from the baseline encounter.

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as mean±SD, median (interquartile range), and count (%). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test of normality. Between‐group comparisons were performed using the unpaired t test, Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, ANOVA, and Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

The clinical characteristics associated with surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS (the clinical and right heart indices associated with undergoing surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS) were determined using logistic regression analysis. First, we performed univariable logistic regression analysis assessing the relationship between undergoing surgical repair and the following covariates: (1) demographic indices (age, sex); (2) anatomic indices (number of PAPVRs [assessed as categorical variable using the subgroup with single PAPVR as the reference group]; side of PAPVR [assessed as categorical variable using right‐sided PAPVR as the reference group]; and PAPVR drainage site [assessed as categorical variable using right superior vena cava as the reference group]); (3) right heart indices (RA volume index, RA reservoir strain, RV end‐diastolic area index, RV free wall strain, and RV systolic pressure). The covariates with P<0.1 on univariable analysis were then used to create a multivariable logistic regression model, and the final covariate selection was based on stepwise backward variable selection with P<0.1 required for a covariate to remain in the model. The covariates with statistically significant association with surgical repair on multivariable logistic regression analysis were used to create a PAPVR‐IAS risk score by assigning a score of 1.0 for each unit of odds ratio (OR). For ease of application in routine clinical practice, we dichotomized RA volume and RV end‐diastolic area using sex‐specific cutoff points. 11 , 13 The PAPVR‐IAS risk score for each patient was calculated as a sum of the risk factors, with a maximum score of 5. The diagnostic performance of the PAPVR risk score was assessed using multivariable logistic regression analysis (with surgical PAPVR repair as the outcome) using the combined cohort of patients with repaired and unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS (n=118). The probability of undergoing surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS was calculated as the quotient of number of patients that underwent surgical repair and total number of patients within the risk score.

The assessment of temporal changes in clinical and right heart indices was restricted to the patients with available data at the prespecified time points: (1) baseline and 3‐year follow‐up (n=73; 91% of cohort); and (2) baseline and 3‐year and 5‐year follow‐up (n=36; 45% of the cohort). All statistical analyses were performed with BlueSky Statistics software version. 7.10 (BlueSky Statistics LLC, Chicago, IL), and JMP statistical software version 17.1.0 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC). P value <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

There were 118 patients with PAPVR‐IAS who received care at Mayo Clinic within the study period. Of the 118 patients, 38 (32%) and 80 (68%) patients had repaired versus unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS, respectively. Of the 38 patients with surgical repair, 14 (37%) received an intracardiac baffle, 21 (29%) underwent a Warden procedure, and 13 (34%) had reimplantation of anomalous vein(s) to the left atrium.

Of the 80 patients with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS, the mean age at baseline encounter was 41±15 years, and 37 (46%) were men. Of the 80 patients, 65 (81%) had a single anomalous vein, while the other 15 patients (19%) had 2 PAPVRs. The most common PAPVR drainage site was the right superior vena cava in 55 (69%) patients, followed by the left innominate vein in 23 (29%) patients.

Table 1 shows a comparison of the baseline clinical characteristics of the patients with unrepaired versus repaired PAPVR‐IAS. Compared with the patients with repaired PAPVR‐IAS, the unrepaired group were more likely to have a single PAPVR (81% versus 42%; P<0.001), more likely to have PAPVR drainage to the right superior vena cava (69% versus 50%; P=0.04), and less likely to have PAPVR drainage to the left innominate vein (29% versus 37%; P=0.02) (Table 1). The unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS group also had less neurohormonal activation (NT‐proBNP, 114 pg/mL [interquartile range, 61–342] versus 169 pg/mL [interquartile range, 96–984]; P=0.009), better functional capacity (New York Heart Association functional class 1 in 89% versus 36%; P<0.001), but similar aerobic capacity (predicted peak oxygen consumption 88%±19% versus 79±17; P=0.2; Table 1).

Clinical and Hemodynamic Indices Associated With Surgical Repair

Table 3 shows the clinical and hemodynamic indices associated with surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS. Compared with patients with a single PAPVR, those with 2 PAPVRs (OR, 2.05 [95% CI, 1.19–5.77], P<0.001), and those with 3 PAPVRs (OR, 3.62 [95% CI, 1.20–8.91], P=0.004) had greater odds of requiring surgical PAPVR repair (Table 3). Similarly, larger RA volume index (OR, 1.03 [95% CI, 1.02–1.04] per mL/m2; P=0.01), and larger RV end‐diastolic area index (OR, 1.02 [95% CI, 1.01–1.03] per cm2/m2; P=0.04) were associated with greater odds of requiring surgical repair (Table 3).

Temporal Change in Clinical and Right Heart Indices in Patients With Unrepaired PAPVR

Of the 80 patients with unrepaired PAPVR, 73 (91%) had clinical and echocardiographic assessments at baseline and 3‐year follow‐up, while 36 (45%) had clinical and echocardiographic assessments at baseline and 3‐year and 5‐year follow‐up. There was no statistically significant change in clinical indices (New York Heart Association class, predicted peak oxygen consumption, and NT‐proBNP) and right heart indices (RA volume index, RA reservoir strain, RV end‐diastolic area index, RV free wall strain, and RV systolic pressure) during follow‐up (Table 5).

Discussion

In this case study, we assessed the clinical characteristics associated with surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS, and the temporal changes in clinical and right heart indices of patients with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS. The main findings are as follows: (1) The predictors of surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS were the number of PAPVRs, RA volumes, and RV end‐diastolic areas. The PAPVR‐IAS risk score derived from these indices had robust diagnostic performance to predict surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS with a 37% increase in the odds of undergoing surgical repair for every unit increase in PAPVR‐IAS risk score. (2) There was no significant temporal deterioration in the clinical and right heart indices of the patients with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS over 5‐year follow‐up.

PAPVR is a rare CHD characterized by anomalous drainage of one or more pulmonary veins directly to the RA, or indirectly through a systemic vein, leading to left‐to‐right shunt and right heart volume overload. 3 , 14 , 15 PAPVR‐IAS represents an uncommon variant of PAPVR with less left‐to‐right shunt because of the absence of an ASD. 3 , 14 , 15 In a recent paper from our center, Graham et al reported the midterm outcomes after surgical repair of PAPVR‐IAS in 45 adults. 9 There was no surgical death. However, 13% of the cohort develop new‐onset postoperative atrial fibrillation, and 5% required transcatheter reintervention for pulmonary venous baffle stenosis within 5 years after repair. 9 These estimates were consistent with results of previous surgical series reporting 5% to 10% risk of systemic or pulmonary venous baffle stenosis, 3% to 5% risk of sinus node dysfunction requiring pacemaker implantation, and 10% to 15% risk of new‐onset postoperative atrial fibrillation. 6 , 7 , 16 , 17 , 18 These findings suggest that while the risk of death is low after surgical repair of PAPVR, the risk of other postoperative complications was not insignificant.

To minimize the risks associated with surgical intervention, while avoiding the long‐term consequences of right heart volume overload, the American and European guidelines for the management of adults with CHD recommend surgical repair of PAPVR only in patients with significant left‐to‐right shunt as defined as Qp/Qs >1.5, RV dilation, or symptoms attributable to right heart volume overload. 4 , 5 In the absence of an ASD, the severity left‐to‐right shunt is determined by the number of anomalous veins as well as the type of PAPVR. 3 Based on CMRI analysis of 104 patients with PAPVR‐IAS, Hatipoglu et al showed that PAPVR involving the right upper pulmonary vein was associated with a higher Qp/Qs (1.6 versus 1.4) and larger RV end‐diastolic volume index (114 versus 90 mL/m2) as compared with other anomalous pulmonary veins. 3 This underscores the central role of CMRI for risk stratification and clinical decision making regarding the timing of surgical repair of PAPVR. While CMRI remains the gold standard for RV volumetric assessment, the PAPVR‐IAS risk score proposed in the current study suggests that transthoracic echocardiogram can also provide valuable anatomic and hemodynamic data to guide clinical decision making, especially when CMRI data are not available in all patients.

Although the practice guidelines recommend conservative management in patients with PAPVR‐IAS not meeting criteria for surgical repair, the optimal frequency of clinical and imaging follow‐up is unclear because of the paucity of outcome data in patients with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS. 4 , 5 The current study aimed to bridge this knowledge gap by demonstrating that patients with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS do not have progressive right heart enlargement or clinical deterioration during follow‐up. This is also consistent with a recent study by Shah et al showing that CMRI‐derived RV volumes remained stable over a 5‐year follow‐up period among 15 adults with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS. 8 A possible explanation for these findings may be that in those with right heart enlargement from PAPVR‐IAS, the dilation occurred early in life before adulthood, and hence progressive right heart enlargement was unlikely to occur afterwards. If this hypothesis is supported by empirical data, then less frequent clinical and imaging surveillance may be required in this population. This would, in turn, reduce health care resource usage and the financial burden for the patient.

Limitations

This is a retrospective single‐center study, and it is therefore prone to selection and ascertainment bias. The study was limited by a small sample size and limited follow‐up duration. The criteria for performing surgical repair of PAPVR was not standardized but was based on the clinical judgment of the primary cardiologist and the cardiac surgeon. As a result, the use of case–control design in this study introduces a selection bias. Finally, we relied on echocardiography for the assessment of RV enlargement, instead of CMRI, which is the gold standard for RV volumetric assessment because of limited data availability.

Conclusions

A simple risk score derived from the number of PAPVRs, RA volumes, and RV end‐diastolic areas can guide risk stratification regarding the probability of requiring surgical intervention in adults with PAPVR‐IAS. This suggests that echocardiographic indices can be used for clinical decision making in the absence of CMRI‐derived volumetric indices. Furthermore, we did not observe progressive right heart remodeling or clinical deterioration during follow‐up among the patients with unrepaired PAPVR‐IAS. While clinical recommendations cannot be based on a single retrospective study with relatively short‐term follow‐up, these data suggest that perhaps a less stringent imaging surveillance may be required in this population.
