Exposure to multifaceted violence and subjective health complaints in adolescents: the remit of school health services, a cross-sectional study in Sweden
Authors: Marlene Makenzius
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite free student health services in Sweden, subjective health complaints (SHC) and violence remain common among adolescents. Evidence on how different forms of violence relate to these symptoms is limited. This study examined associations between multifaceted violence and SHC among adolescents by sex and sexual orientation.MethodsA cross-sectional digital survey (2024) was conducted among first-year upper-secondary students in a sparsely populated county of Sweden, covering approximately one-tenth of the national land area (n=668; 76%). SHCs measured using the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children symptom checklist. past-year victimisation (physical, psychological and sexual), childhood witnessing of violence and perpetration were assessed using items adapted from the Sexual health Identification Tool. Demographic variables included sex, place of birth and sexual orientation. Descriptive statistics and sex-stratified hierarchical regression analyses were conducted.ResultsOverall, 15.3% reported past-year violence exposure, more commonly among girls than boys (17.1% vs 12.4%, p<0.001). Boys more often reported repeated physical victimisation and physical perpetration, whereas girls more frequently reported psychological victimisation and witnessing psychological violence during childhood. Non-heterosexual adolescents reported higher violence exposure scores and higher levels of several SHC compared with heterosexual peers. In sex-stratified regression analyses, experienced violence was independently associated with higher SHC scores among both girls (β=0.245, p<0.001) and boys (β=0.175, p=0.004). Witnessed violence during childhood was also independently associated with higher SHC among girls (β=0.245, p<0.001) and boys (β=0.133, p=0.029). Sexual orientation remained independently associated with higher SHC only among boys (β=0.220, p<0.001).ConclusionsViolence exposure patterns differed by sex, while sexual-minority adolescents reported greater violence exposure and symptom burden. Both experienced and witnessed childhood violence were independently associated with higher SHC, indicating that violence-related health consequences extend beyond direct victimisation. Further research and prevention efforts should better recognise diverse violence experiences, including boys who are often primarily framed as perpetrators rather than victims and sexual-minority youth.

Introduction

Globally, violence exposure among children and adolescents represents a major public health concern, with estimates suggesting that up to one billion children experience some form of violence annually. Psychosomatic complaints, such as headaches, sleep problems and anxiety, are also widely reported across regions and constitute important indicators of adolescent health and well-being.

Psychosomatic subjective complaints have increased markedly among Swedish adolescents, particularly girls. Analyses of Swedish Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) data (1985–2022) show rising prevalence across the distribution of symptom severity.15 Compared with neighbouring Nordic countries, Swedish adolescents, especially girls, report some of the highest levels of psychosomatic complaints despite comparable welfare systems and education.2 However, Stattin and Eriksson6 argue that the striking similarity in the shapes of time trends in psychosomatic complaints among Nordic adolescents indicates that shared cross-national conditions drive the increases, whereas differences in levels likely reflect country-specific factors. They further suggest that the aggregate technique, by separating trend shape from level, may enable empirical tests of general and country-specific explanations in this and other similar fields.

In person-oriented analyses by Eriksson and Stattin,2 adolescents in all Nordic countries were grouped into five mental-health profiles: adequate health, perceived good health, perceived poor health, high psychosomatic complaints and ‘dual’ problems (high complaints plus poor perceived health). The profiles showed similar trends across countries, such as in 2002, adequate health was most common for both sexes, but by 2022, the high-psychosomatic profile was most common among girls. The dual-problem group had the poorest psychological and social adjustment. Because the high-symptom group doubled while the dual-problem group rose only modestly, increases in reported symptoms do not necessarily signal a general deterioration in adolescent mental health.6

However, a Finnish study reported adverse effects of COVID-19 on youth mental health that may persist; a notable exception was a marked improvement among transgender adolescents.7 The proportions of young people with generalised anxiety, depression or social anxiety did not return even close to pre-pandemic levels among boys and girls. Kiviruusu et al7 therefore concluded that the crisis requires both adequate clinical support and broader, society-level solutions developed in partnership with young people. Similar findings were observed in the Netherlands.8

Socio-economic conditions, family structure and prior mental health problems are also important potential confounders, as they may influence both exposure to violence and reporting of psychosomatic symptoms.9 However, Ottová-Jordan et al3 found that the strongest and most consistent individual-level associations with adolescent health complaints were being female, being bullied, school pressure and smoking, suggesting that proximal environments are more influential than macro-level conditions, though country-level factors remain relevant.4 In addition to gender, sexual minority youth report higher rates of bullying, victimisation and discrimination than their heterosexual peers. Longitudinal data from Dutch lesbian, gay and bisexual (LGB) adolescents demonstrated that LGB youth reported more internalising problems and greater use of cigarettes and marijuana than heterosexual peers, with disparities explained by peer victimisation and parental rejection (minority stress framework) but not by psychological mediation processes.10 These disparities highlight the importance of considering sexual orientation alongside gender when studying violence and adolescent health.

While the impact of victimisation on adolescent health is well established, few studies examine victimisation, witnessing and perpetration together or assess how these roles vary by sexual orientation among youths. In response to this gap, the SEXual health Identification Tool (SEXIT) was developed as a structured approach to identify youth at risk of sexual ill health including exposure to violence or perpetration.11 12 Piloted in Swedish youth clinics, it showed feasibility and promoted disclosure of sensitive experiences.13 Implementation in school health services was feasible but contingent on an enabling infrastructure: formal collaboration with municipal social services, clear referral pathways and supportive leadership.13 However, a limitation of the SEXIT tool is that violence-related response categories are grouped rather than separated, making it harder to distinguish types of physical, psychological and sexual violence in a detailed manner.

Sweden’s school health services are universal, school-based and free, with a primarily preventive and health-promoting remit rather than long-term treatment.14 A multidisciplinary team, comprising a nurse and a doctor alongside a psychologist, school counsellor and special educational needs staff, provides scheduled health visits, delivers the national immunisation programme, offers brief psychosocial support and identifies emerging mental-health, learning and safeguarding concerns (eg, bullying or violence), referring on to primary care, child and adolescent mental health services, youth clinics and social services as required.

The school health service also supports whole-school policies that foster inclusion, safety and a positive climate and contributes to health education (eg, sexual and reproductive health, substance use and digital safety). Care is delivered under healthcare confidentiality rules, although capacity and the availability of doctors and psychologists can vary by municipality and the service is not a substitute for specialist care.14

Despite free and easy access to school health services, psychosomatic complaints and violence experience are common among school youths. The impact of victimisation on adolescent health is well established but less is known about the relationship between victimisation, perpetration, witnessing of violence during childhood and psychosomatic symptoms or how these vary by gender and sexual orientation. Accordingly, the contribution of the present study is not to establish new associations but to examine how brief practice-oriented violence indicators, similar to those used in school health services, capture overlapping exposure patterns.

Aim

The aim was to investigate the associations between violence exposure and SHC in youth and assess how these relationships vary by sex and sexual orientation.

Materials and methods

This was a cross-sectional digital classroom survey among first-year students at upper secondary schools in Sweden’s third-largest county by land mass. The study was planned and reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.15 All data were collected (April to June 2024) using a structured digital questionnaire administered in classroom settings.

The study was conducted in 13 upper secondary schools within a Swedish county. The region is one of the largest in Sweden, covering approximately one-tenth of the national land area and characterised by low population density. The participating schools represented the eight municipalities in the county and included both municipal-funded and government-funded independent schools, offering theoretical (university-preparatory) and vocational programmes. Data collection occurred during scheduled school hours on a single designated survey day in the 2023/2024 academic year. The participating schools reflect typical upper-secondary school settings with a diverse socio-economic composition; however, detailed school-level data (eg, school climate, staffing or prior violence-prevention initiatives) were lacking.

Target population

The target population comprised first-year upper-secondary students. To reduce selection bias, all eligible first-year students were invited (n=1763) enrolled across 13 upper-secondary schools in the region, including both municipal-funded (n=1607) and government-funded independent schools. Four schools declined participation due to concurrent ongoing surveys and were therefore not included in the study (figure 1). Data collection was carried out during regular class time to maximise participation.

Flow diagram of participant recruitment, exclusions and inclusion in the analytical sample.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were enrolment as a first-year student and presence in school on the survey day. Exclusion criteria applied at the student level were as follows: students absent due to off-site field training in vocational programmes, students enrolled in preparatory programmes as determined by school administration and students attending special-needs schools requiring additional ethical procedures. On the survey day, 262 students were absent, leaving 876 students present and invited to complete the questionnaire. Of the 876 students present on the survey day, 682 completed the questionnaire (77.9%). Fourteen questionnaires with only 1–3 items completed were excluded, resulting in a final analytic sample of 668 students (386 girls, 282 boys).

Recruitment was coordinated through the regional public health department in collaboration with school managers. A contact person at the regional public health authority and a designated school liaison facilitated communication with participating schools

Patient and public involvement

The questionnaire (online supplemental file 1) was co-developed with school health service professionals and upper-secondary students with the aim to strengthen the content validity, before it was pilot-tested with nine students (five girls, four boys). The students’ feedback improved clarity, cultural relevance and acceptability of items, leading to minor revisions in wording and question order. In the final result, internal missing data were <5%, indicating good acceptability and comprehension of the questionnaire items.

Variables and outcomes

No formal a priori sample size calculation was conducted, as the study aimed to include all eligible students.

Experienced violence: physical, psychological and sexual victimisation during the past 12 months were designed as brief screening indicators measured with 10 items adapted from the validated SEXIT instrument,10 with a four-point Likert scale (0=never, 1=unsure, 2=once, 3=repeatedly).

Witnessed violence: exposure to physical, psychological or sexual violence within the family during childhood, measured with three items (yes, no and unsure).11

Subjective health complaints during the past month, measured with the eight-item HBSC symptom checklist (eg, headache, stomachache, back pain, feeling low, anxiety, irritability, sleep difficulties and dizziness),16 with a five-point scale (0=never to 4=almost daily) was used.

Composite scores were calculated for the total frequency of experienced violence (range 0–30) and psychosomatic symptoms (range 0–32).

Covariates: demographic variables included age, sex (sex registered at birth), sexual orientation (heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual, other and unsure) and place of birth (Nordic vs outside Nordic countries).

Perpetration of violence: two items assessed self-reported physical and psychological violence perpetration during the past 12 months (yes, no and unsure).11

Continuous total scores were computed for violence exposure and psychosomatic symptoms. Gender and sexual orientation were treated as categorical (binary/dichotomous for group comparisons). In regression analysis, sex was coded as boy=0, girl=1, sexual orientation as heterosexual=0, non-heterosexual=1, and place of birth as Nordic countries=0, outside Nordic countries=1. Violence exposure scores (0–32) were entered as continuous independent variable. For descriptive tables, violence exposure items were presented in frequency categories (never, unsure, once and repeatedly).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise socio-demographic variables and main study variables. Group differences were tested using independent-samples t-tests for sex and sexual orientation comparisons of mean (M) exposure and symptom scores. Kruskal–Wallis tests were conducted for comparisons across perpetration categories (no, unsure and yes). Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted for sex and sexual orientation differences in psychosomatic symptom frequency.

Hierarchical multiple regression analyses stratified by sex were conducted to examine associations between SHC, experienced violence and witnessed violence during childhood. In step 1, sexual orientation (0=heterosexual; 1=non-heterosexual), place of birth and total experienced violence were entered. In step 2, a combined dichotomous variable representing witnessed violence during childhood was added to evaluate additional explanatory power. The variable was coded as no witnessed violence versus unsure/any witnessed physical, psychological or sexual violence during childhood. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.29.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (reference no. 2023–05473-01). Participants were aged 15–19 years. According to Swedish ethical regulations, adolescents aged ≥15 years can provide informed consent for minimal-risk research; therefore, parental consent was not required. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Before data collection, teachers read an information letter aloud in class, and the same information was presented on the first page of the digital questionnaire. By choosing to proceed after the information page and submitting the questionnaire, participants provided implied informed consent.

Results

A total of 668 students completed the questionnaire (figure 1), corresponding to a response rate of 76%.

Most students were 16–17 years old (92.4%; median age, 17 years, IQR 16–17). Girls comprised 57.8% of the sample and boys 42.2%. Self-defined gender identity followed a similar distribution, with 55.7% identifying as girls, 42.2% as boys and 2.1% as non-binary or other. Most participants identified as heterosexual (79.7%) and were born in Sweden or another Nordic country (89.1%). Further characteristics of the sample by sex are in online supplemental file 2).

Among the 668 students, 102 (15.3%) reported having been exposed to physical, psychological or sexual violence in the past 12 months (table 1). Of these, 66 were girls (17.1% of all girls), and 35 were boys (12.4% of all boys), with a significant difference between sexes (p<0.001).

Table 1 shows that boys reported repeated experiences of being slapped more often than girls (40.0% vs 12.1%, p<0.001) and pushed (58.8% vs 27.3%, p=0.006). Table 1 also includes reports of having, during childhood, experienced that someone in the family was exposed to violence. Girls reported more often having witnessed psychological violence within the family (14.0% vs 4.3%, p<0.001). They also more often reported witnessing physical violence (6.2% vs 4.3%, p=0.005). Reports of witnessing sexual violence within the family were uncommon for both sexes and did not differ significantly (p=0.167).

Perpetration of psychological violence was also reported significantly more often by boys than by girls (p=0.004) (table 2).

Table 3 also presents psychosomatic complaints during the past month. Headache (48.1%), sleeping difficulties (43.5%) and irritability (48.5%) were the most frequently reported symptoms occurring more than once a week or almost daily. Anxiety affected 61.6% at least once a week, and feeling low was reported by 56.9% at the same frequency.

Girls reported significantly higher levels of SHC than boys across all measures, except sleeping difficulties (p=0.482). Non-heterosexual students reported higher levels of several symptoms, including headache, stomachache, back pain, feeling low, anxiety and dizziness (p<0.001–0.040). Students born outside the Nordic countries reported higher levels of feeling low (p=0.036) and irritability (p=0.005).

Among participants who had perpetrated psychological violence, only dizziness was significantly associated (p=0.001). For physical violence perpetration, irritability (p=0.014) and sleeping difficulties (p=0.010) were significantly associated.

Sex-stratified hierarchical regression analyses (table 5) showed that step 1 explained 11.0% of the variance in SHC scores among girls (adjusted R²=0.103, p<0.001) and 8.7% among boys (adjusted R²=0.076, p<0.001). In both groups, sexual orientation and experienced violence were independently associated with higher SHC scores.

Adding witnessed violence during childhood in step 2 (table 5) significantly improved the models for both girls (ΔR²=0.053, p<0.001) and boys (ΔR²=0.017, p=0.029), increasing the explained variance to 16.3% (adjusted R²=0.154) and 10.4% (adjusted R²=0.090), respectively. In the final models, experienced violence and witnessed violence during childhood were independently associated with higher SHC scores in both girls (β=0.245, p<0.001 for both) and boys (β=0.175, p=0.004; β=0.133, p=0.029, respectively). Sexual orientation remained independently associated with higher SHC scores only among boys (β=0.220, p<0.001).

Discussion

Exposure to violence during the past year was common and patterned by sex and sexual orientation. Girls reported greater overall exposure than boys, whereas boys more often reported repeated experiences of physical victimisation (slapping and pushing), and girls more often reported relational or emotional forms such as humiliation and insults. Sexual minority students reported higher violence exposure and more SHC than heterosexual peers. These findings are broadly consistent with existing literature.1721 The stratified regression analyses further showed that both experienced violence and witnessed violence during childhood were independently associated with SHC among both girls and boys, whereas sexual orientation remained independently associated with SHC only among boys. Therefore, the contribution of the present study lies primarily in examining overlapping roles (victimisation, perpetration and witnessing) and their independent associations with SHC using brief indicators within a school health service context.

Frequent SHC clustered with violence exposure, in line with meta-analyses showing increased risks of headaches and other SHC among victimised youth.22 23 Witnessed violence in childhood and adolescence was also associated with high symptom scores, consistent with evidence that witnessing parental violence independently predicts later depression and post-traumatic stress.24 25 Importantly, the present stratified analyses indicate that witnessed violence contributed independently to SHC even after adjustment for direct violence exposure and demographic factors, suggesting that indirect violence exposure should not be overlooked in school health assessments.

The observed sex differences reflect a well-documented pattern that boys are more often exposed to physical aggression, whereas girls are more likely to experience sexualised and relational victimisation.3 11 Our data mirror these findings demonstrating that boys more often reported repeated physical victimisation (eg, being pushed or slapped), whereas girls more frequently reported psychological victimisation (insults and humiliation). Nevertheless, boys showed the same overall pattern, with violence exposure associated with increased SHC. The stratified analyses further demonstrated that witnessed violence during childhood was independently associated with SHC among boys as well as girls, despite boys reporting lower overall exposure levels.

Gender norms often cast girls as victims and boys as perpetrators, rooted in hegemonic masculinity frameworks,26 yet these findings illustrate that boys also show the same pattern of association between violence exposure and SHC. This underscores the importance of broadening the perspective beyond victim–perpetrator stereotypes to ensure prevention and support address both sexes.

Although non-heterosexual students reported significantly more frequent experiences of violence and higher SHC in the bivariate analysis and sexual orientation in the sex-stratified analyses, sexual orientation remained independently associated with SHC among boys but not girls. This likely reflects sex-specific pathways in how minority stress and violence exposure relate to health complaints, meaning that the increased symptoms among sexual minority youth appear to be largely accounted for by their increased exposure to violence, rather than by sexual orientation per se. This pattern is consistent with the minority stress theory, which posits that the increased incidence of mental health problems in sexual minorities stem from higher exposure to external stressors (eg, victimisation and discrimination).26 Supporting this, a population-based study in Sweden found that experiences of victimisation and threats of violence—and lack of social support—partially mediated the association between sexual orientation and mental health outcomes.27

Students who reported perpetrating physical violence also reported higher victimisation scores, echoing ‘bully–victim’ literature that highlights compounded psychosomatic problems in youth who are both victims and perpetrators.28 29 The associations between witnessed violence and symptoms are in line with meta-analytic and population-based findings showing that witnessing violence independently predicts internalising problems.24 25 The present findings may extend this literature by demonstrating that witnessed violence remained independently associated with SHC when entered hierarchically alongside direct violence exposure.

Taken together, the findings indicate that different forms of violence cluster by sex, with boys more exposed to physical forms and girls more to bullying (relational, social exclusion, etc). Moreover, the stratified analyses suggest that the health consequences of violence exposure may be more similar across sexes than the exposure patterns themselves. This may reflect differences in peer aggression strategies, gendered norms around expression of violence and patterns of disclosure. The increased risks among sexual minority youth are likely linked to minority stress processes, including stigma and victimisation, underscoring the importance of lesbian, gay, bisexual and transgender (LGBTQ)-affirming support in schools.10 19

Strengths include standardised items adapted from the internationally used HBSC framework and analysis of both direct and witnessed violence in relation to SHC. Subjective health complaints alone or in combination with violence exposure may not provide a full picture of adolescent mental health, as affected youths represent a heterogeneous group. For example, those who also perceive their overall health as poor are more likely to experience severe mental health problems, whereas those who rate their health as good tend to face less severe adjustment issues.6 Data were collected via anonymous self-administered questionnaires, which reduces interviewer bias but may introduce recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly for sensitive topics such as violence exposure.

Limitations include the cross-sectional design (limiting causal inference) and reliance on self-report. As the questionnaire was administered only in Swedish, participation was limited to students with sufficient Swedish proficiency. This may have introduced selection bias, and language-related differences in prevalence or reporting cannot be ruled out. However, meta-analytic evidence on ethnic group differences in peer victimisation indicates that minority status alone is not strongly associated with higher levels of victimisation.30 Instead, the effect of ethnic minority status appears to be context-dependent. Few students were identified as sexual minorities, which necessitated combining them into a single category. This approach is common in studies with relatively small samples, but it limits the ability to capture the diversity of sexual orientation across identity and behaviour. Nevertheless, existing evidence shows that LGBT students are disproportionately affected by prejudice-related bullying.31 32 However, the vulnerability of sexual minority youth may stem less from sexual orientation per se than from contextual factors, especially acceptance, inclusivity, family factors and the wider social climate.32 33

Given the limited socio-economic and demographic information collected, our ability to adjust for potential confounders and effect modification was restricted, as shown significant in the study by Due et al.34

Moreover, victimisation measures were based on brief screening items that did not capture severity, duration, timing or perpetrator relationship and should be interpreted as indicators of exposure rather than comprehensive measures. Treating ‘unsure’ as possible perpetration may have increased sensitivity but reduced specificity. Similarly, witnessed violence was assessed using single items, with ‘unsure’ combined with ‘yes’, which may have introduced misclassification.

Excluding students attending special schools is a significant limitation. Young people with special educational needs are consistently shown to be at higher risk of bullying, victimisation and social exclusion, partly owing to social–communication difficulties, reduced peer support and, in some cases, physical vulnerability.3537

Implications

The overlap between perpetration and victimisation highlights the importance of addressing school and online climates that normalise aggression and harassment, with particular attention to young people occupying both roles. The finding that witnessed violence independently contributed to SHC among both girls and boys further suggests that prevention and support efforts should move beyond narrow gendered assumptions about who is affected by violence and how violence-related distress manifests. SHC were associated with violence exposure; however, these symptoms should not be interpreted as diagnostic indicators but as prompts for sensitive, confidential enquiry within school health services. In practice, services may consider asking about safety and violence when students present with recurrent SHC, avoiding gender stereotypes in assessment, ensuring sexual minority-affirming communication and establishing clear referral pathways to external services.

Schools that prioritise ethos, relationships, community and culture—led with care—foster belonging, safety and secure attachments that protect adolescent health and well-being, particularly when other attachments are compromised.36 37 Programmes should be informed by young people’s distinct, intersecting identities and draw on relational, caring pedagogies (care, reciprocity, attentiveness, trust and acceptance). Educators must balance academic attainment with health promotion; ambidextrous organisational approaches may help achieve this dual mandate.37 However, as detailed school-context data were not collected, these recommendations should be interpreted as practice-informed rather than directly derived from measured school-level effects.

Future research should include special schools and ensure accessible recruitment and measures, use more detailed and validated assessments of violence exposure and incorporate socioeconomic, family and school-level factors. Longitudinal designs are also needed to clarify causal pathways and temporal ordering.

Conclusions

Violence exposure patterns differed by sex, with girls reporting greater symptom burden and psychological violence exposure, whereas boys more often reported physical victimisation and perpetration. Both experienced and witnessed childhood violence were independently associated with higher SHC after adjustment for the demographic variables included in the analyses. Although the findings should be interpreted cautiously because of measurement limitations and the cross-sectional design, they indicate the importance of recognising diverse violence experiences among adolescents and moving beyond stereotypical assumptions that frame boys primarily as perpetrators rather than also potential victims, as well as recognising the vulnerability of sexual-minority youth.
