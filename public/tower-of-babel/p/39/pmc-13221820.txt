An Examination of Digital Media and Device-Related Problems in Pediatric Hospital Visits for Psychiatric Crises
Authors: Meredith Gansner, Prakruthi Nikam, William Wang, Sharon Levy, Zev Schuman-Olivier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDespite growing concerns about mental health effects of youth digital media use, the frequency of digital media/device–related problems (DMRPs) amid pediatric psychiatric crises is unknown. This study leverages the electronic medical record system of a large pediatric hospital to provide a descriptive analysis of DMRPs associated with pediatric psychiatric crises, including different DMRP subtypes. Consistent with existing research on problematic digital media use, we expected to find greater frequency of presentations involving DMRPs in older adolescents as well as sex-based differences in specific DMRP subtypes.MethodA retrospective chart review was performed examining psychiatric consultation notes from all youth 6 to 17 years of age who presented to the hospital in psychiatric crisis in 2021 or 2022. Age, biological sex, race/ethnicity, and psychiatric diagnoses were recorded from each initial psychiatric consultation note. All encounters were assessed for presence of a DMRP within the presentation, coded under 5 primary subtypes. Relationships between variables of interest and DMRPs were assessed using regression models.ResultsThere were 3,710 encounters that met inclusion criteria, representing 2,889 unique patients (average age, 13.5 years). Of the total encounters, 711 (19.2%) featured a type of DMRP. Youth with presentations including DMRPs were significantly older in age and more likely to have a mood disorder diagnosis. Compared to male patients, female patients were significantly less likely to present with crises related to device/digital media access and excessive device use, but were more likely to have presented because of high-risk online sexual behaviors/relationships.ConclusionOur findings support the need to assess for problematic digital media and device use during pediatric crisis evaluations. In addition, demographic differences in crisis presentations that involve DMRPs can guide development of targeted screening recommendations for specific types of problematic digital media/device use.Key words: social media, electronic devices, emergency room, crisis evaluations

As adolescents spend greater amounts of time on the Internet and digital devices, risks associated with digital media use have become increasingly apparent. Cybervictimization, visualization of suicide-promoting online content, and excessive Internet use are just a few examples on a growing list of digital media–related risks impacting youth mental health.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6

Concurrently, the number of emergency room visits for pediatric mental health concerns has also been rising.7, 8, 9, 10 The rate of pediatric psychiatric hospitalizations increased steadily in the decade before the COVID-19 pandemic,11 and in 2021, the American Academy of Pediatrics, Children’s Hospital Association, and American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry jointly declared a national crisis in pediatric mental health.12 Although it has been suggested that adolescent screen and social media use contributed to this crisis,13 the extent to which digital media/device–related problems (DMRPs) factor into pediatric mental health emergencies has still not been investigated.

Several studies have explored the relationship between DMRPs and inpatient psychiatric admission, but they omit information about DMRPs in mental health crises that do not require subsequent psychiatric hospitalization.14, 15, 16 It is also unknown whether certain youth are more vulnerable to specific types of DMRPs. For example, problematic Internet use (excessive Internet use featuring functional impairment) is more frequently diagnosed in male youth,17 and female youth are more likely to share sexually explicit photos via digital media18; serious mental health outcomes associated with these DMRPs could demonstrate similar sex-based biases. Unfortunately, these gaps in the literature make it challenging for clinicians to provide personalized anticipatory guidance focused on DMRPs that may be of greatest risk to an individual patient.

This retrospective chart review leverages the electronic medical record system of a large tertiary pediatric hospital with the following aims: (1) to estimate what proportion of pediatric mental health crises included a digital media/device–related problem, and (2) to examine demographic factors, psychiatric diagnoses, severity of psychiatric symptoms, and treatment recommendations associated with these crisis presentations. We expected that DMRPs featured in crisis encounters would reflect known patterns of problematic digital media/device use seen in community-based samples of children and adolescents. During this national emergency in pediatric mental health, data highlighting opportunities for prevention of pediatric psychiatric crises could be of significant public health benefit.

Method

We performed a structured chart review of youth who presented to a pediatric hospital in psychiatric crisis over a 2-year period. This study was considered exempt by the hospital system’s Institutional Review Board.

Study Setting and Data Acquisition

The study was performed in a large, urban pediatric hospital where patients in psychiatric crisis are assessed by specialized teams of psychiatric clinicians (physicians, social workers, and psychologists), either in the emergency room or on hospital medical floors (in the event that a child in crisis also requires urgent medical stabilization). The hospital was located in a major city in the northeastern United States that offers robust psychiatric acute care services, including multiple hospital-affiliated psychiatric inpatient units. Pediatric patients in psychiatric crises frequently present from metropolitan areas surrounding the city, as well as from less-resourced rural areas in the state where the hospital is located and several surrounding states. Initial psychiatric crisis evaluations routinely collect information from both patient and guardian about circumstances contributing to the crisis presentation (ie, History of Present Illness), as well as review the patient’s current psychiatric symptoms, medical (including psychiatric) history, family history, and psychosocial circumstances (eg, school attended, family structure). Based on the information collected, clinicians produce both a diagnostic assessment and treatment recommendations, including level of care. This study team performed a retrospective chart review of all psychiatric consultation notes for youth 6 to 17 years of age who had presented to the hospital in psychiatric crisis from January 1, 2021, through December 31, 2022. A full list of encounters in this age group and time period was provided by the hospital’s Clinical Research Information Technology (CRIT) team. Study team members (MG, PN, WW, SK) reviewed all encounters to identify only those instances in which a psychiatric crisis had brought the patient to the hospital. Consultation notes for youth who had been hospitalized for a medical condition unrelated to a psychiatric crisis (eg, a child who expressed suicidal ideation while undergoing chemotherapy) were excluded. All encounter notes were then reviewed using a structured extraction tool (Supplement 1, available online) to identify whether crisis visits involved a digital media/device–related problem (DMRP).

Coding and Analysis

From electronic health records, the study team collected age, sex, race/ethnicity, and psychiatric diagnoses for all patients included in the study. To ensure adequate power for analyses, specific psychiatric diagnoses were sorted into categories of psychiatric disorder. Table S1, available online, shows which disorders were included under which broader categories. For patients who presented more than once in psychiatric crisis during the 2021 to 2022 period, a mean age was calculated by averaging their ages for all encounters. For each individual crisis encounter, we documented the presence of suicidal ideation, homicidal ideation, and whether inpatient hospitalization was recommended in the initial treatment plan. An encounter was designated as involving a DMRP if it included mention of at least 1 of 5 pre-determined DMRP subtypes in the History of Present Illness/Review of Psychiatric Symptoms/Interval History sections of encounter notes. To avoid reliance upon a sole clinician interviewer and because initial consultation notes do not always include history obtained from both guardian and patient (eg, a patient refused to participate in initial interview), Interval History sections in each subsequent psychiatric or social work progress note were reviewed until the patient was discharged. Five primary DMRP subtypes were developed based on prior research14, 15, 16 and were included: (1) unsafe thoughts or behaviors related to access of an electronic device or digital media (eg, suicidal ideation after a smartphone was taken away, or stealing to acquire a device or to make a digital media–related purchase); (2) unsafe thoughts or behaviors after conflict occurring through digital media (eg, suicidal ideation after being cyberbullied); (3) accessing high-risk digital media content (eg, non-suicidal self-injury online videos or content promoting disordered eating) or unsafe thoughts or behaviors after visualization of triggering digital media content (eg, suicidal thoughts worsened after watching a video on a social media platform), or posting or communicating unsafe thoughts or behaviors via digital media (eg, texting a suicide note to a group chat); (4) sexting or inappropriate online relationships (eg, sexual conversations with adults online, posting/texting sexually explicit selfies); and (5) excessive or problematic digital media use compromising a child’s daily functioning (eg, no longer sleeping because of playing video games all night). Examples of presentations falling within each category can be found in Table S2, available online. A sixth “other” category was created for the possibility of a DMRP subtype that had not been initially considered; however, all DMRP crisis encounters were able to be categorized within the 5 broad subtypes mentioned above.

A single encounter could be coded as having more than one DMRP associated with it. For example, a patient posting on a social media platform about their suicidal ideation in the context of experiencing cyberbullying would be coded both “2” and “3.”

A data collection tool was created to standardize data extraction. Team members (MG, PN, WW, SK) were trained to use the tool. Before reviewing charts, study team members practiced coding sample presentations to ensure appropriate use and alignment. All encounters were split evenly among the 4 study team members for initial review. For the first month of data collection, study team members presented all charts that were coded as involving a DMRP at a weekly research team meeting. Each encounter was discussed as to whether the presentation included a DMRP, and if so, which category or categories of DMRP. An encounter could be coded as involving a DMRP without the DMRP being considered the primary cause (or one of the primary causes) of the hospital presentation.

At weekly meetings, study team members reviewed any encounter that they had difficulty coding as to whether a DMRP was involved or which DMRP subtype. All disagreements were decided by consensus majority. Interrater reliability ranged between 86.0% and 88.4% in initial coding. After the initial round of coding, 2 team members (MG, PN) performed a second review of each encounter to decrease the likelihood that a DMRP was missed or a subtype coded incorrectly. Again, any disagreements between the first and second rounds of coding were decided by consensus majority. Finally, the study principal investigator (PI) personally reviewed each encounter coded as involving a DMRP to ensure that there was consistency in DMRP subtype coding.

Psychiatric diagnoses were classified within broader diagnostic categories (eg, depressive disorders, trauma-related disorders); this categorization can be found in Table S2 available online. Except for age, outcome and dependent variables were binarily coded as “0” or “1.” Natural language processing and artificial intelligence tools were not used.

T-tests assessed age-related differences between individuals who presented with DMRPs (overall and subtypes) in 2021 to 2022 and those who did not. Logistic regression models assessed relationships between other demographic variables, psychiatric diagnoses, level of care recommendation, suicidal/homicidal ideation, and presentations involving DMRPs. We also assessed whether outcomes were affected by a potential interaction between variables of sex and age. Regression analyses were controlled for age and biological sex. To account for multiple comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was implemented with a false discovery rate of 0.05,19,20 combining “families” of tests within a single demographic variable or diagnostic category (eg, all analyses examining relationships between a DMRP subtype and anxiety disorder diagnoses).

Results

A total 3,710 encounters met criteria for inclusion in the study, representing 2,889 unique patients, of whom 2,474 (85.6%) had been diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder before the crisis presentation. The average age was 13.5 years (SD = 2.6). The majority of patients (62.0%) were female, followed by male (38.0%). The most frequently documented racial/ethnic identification was non-Hispanic/Latine White (55.7%); however, race and ethnicity were not documented in the electronic medical record for 22.6% of included patients. Mood disorders were the most frequently documented psychiatric diagnosis (66.4%), followed by anxiety disorders (45.5%), and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (31.2%). The majority of encounters featured suicidal ideation (64.9%) and recommendations for psychiatric hospitalization (62.7%) (Table 1).

A total of 2,889 unique patients were represented in these 3,710 individual encounters, because of 533 patients (18.5%) having more than one crisis visit during the study period. Almost one-fifth of all encounters (n = 711, 19.2%) were determined to involve a digital media or device-related concern (Table 1). Presentations involving the viewing or posting of high-risk online content were most frequently documented (9.2% of all reviewed encounters), followed by those involving access to digital media or electronic devices (7.1% of all reviewed encounters) and online conflict (2.5% of all reviewed encounters) (Table 1).

On average, youth with DMRP crisis presentations were slightly older than youth without such presentations, the difference in mean age being significant but small (13.5 vs 13.7 years, p = .03) (Table 2). Youth with presentations involving viewing or posting risky/triggering online content were significantly older than youth without such presentations (14.0 vs 13.5 years, p = .001) (Table 3). Although no difference was observed in the likelihood of a DMRP crisis presentation between male and female patients, there were noted sex differences based on DMRP subtype. Female patients were significantly less likely to have presentations related to device/digital media access (OR = 0.66, 95% CI = 0.50-0.87, p = .003) or involving device/digital media use that was impairing daily functioning (OR = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.23-0.67, p = .001) (Table 3). However, female patients were more likely to have encounters associated with sexting or online sexual behaviors/relationships (OR = 2.96, 95% CI = 1.57-5.59, p = .001) (Table 3). The effect of being female on the odds of a DMRP crisis presentation decreased with age (OR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.83-0.96, p = .003) (Table 2), most significantly for presentations involving viewing or posting high-risk online content (OR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.78-0.95, p = .003) (Table 3).

Even controlling for the increased prevalence of mood disorder diagnoses among adolescents, patients with DMRP crisis presentations were more likely to have a mood disorder diagnosis than those without DMRP crisis presentations (OR = 1.60, 95% CI = 1.30-1.98, p < .001) (Table 2). Examining specific DMRP subtypes, youth with presentations involving posting/viewing high-risk content were more likely to have mood disorder diagnoses (OR = 2.03, 95% CI = 1.50-2.74, p < .001), whereas youth with access-related presentations were less likely to have anxiety disorder diagnoses (OR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.49-0.87, p = .003) (Table 4). Disruptive behavior–related disorders (eg, conduct disorder, oppositional defiant disorder) were more frequently found in youth with both access-related presentations (OR = 1.96, 95% CI = 1.20-3.21, p = .007) and those involving device/digital media use causing functional impairment (OR = 3.46, 95% CI = 1.61-7.42, p = .001) (Table 4). Although the analysis did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, trauma-related disorders were more likely to be found in youth who presented in regard to inappropriate online relationships (OR = 1.95, 95% CI = 1.13-3.37, p = .02) (Table 4).

Youth with DMRP-related presentations were significantly more likely to have had multiple presentations for psychiatric crises during the study period compared to those without (OR = 1.98, 95% CI = 1.61-2.44, p < .001). Discussion

Nearly 1 in 5 patients who presented to a large pediatric academic hospital in psychiatric crisis had a digital media/device–related problem associated with their crisis visit. To our knowledge, this is the first report examining DMRPs among youth presenting to an ED in psychiatric crisis. Although our incidence rate is lower than those reported in 2 prior cross-sectional studies of psychiatrically hospitalized youth,15,16 our study’s rate may be an underestimate. Like other major hospital systems, it is not standard practice to inquire about digital media use when performing emergent psychiatric assessments; it is possible that our findings were especially influenced by clinician variability and that the actual number of DMRPs associated with psychiatric crisis visits is significantly higher than the almost 20% uncovered here. The data for this study were extracted from psychiatric consultation notes that did not include a standardized assessment of digital media use, leading to the potential for missed data if a patient or family member did not mention, or a clinician did not record, problematic digital media behaviors. However, the majority of encounters included in this study featured assessment by multiple providers, particularly those that involved boarding for a higher level of care (n = 2,327; 62.7% of all encounters). By reviewing all behavioral health notes in each encounter, the study team attempted to minimize the number of DMRPs that may have been missed by a single clinician. However, reliance on medical record data from a single hospital system during a concrete time frame does limit the generalizability of the study findings. In 2021, the United States was still navigating COVID-19–related lockdowns, and extracurricular activities had not resumed in most places; many children and adolescents were still reliant upon digital media for recreation and socialization. Subsequent studies should examine whether our results might have been affected by our study’s unique time period.

We found a significant correlation between presenting with digital media–related crisis and older age, which is consistent with the well-known rise in daily screen time observed in adolescence21, 22, 23; however, the difference in age between the 2 groups was quite small. It is possible that a larger age difference was not observed because digital media access is more common among latency-aged youth with psychiatric illness. For example, children who struggle with emotional regulation are more likely to be given devices earlier in life as a mechanism for managing their behavior.24

Our findings concerning biological sex and DMRP presentations are also consistent with known differences in digital media use between male and female youth. The strongest association between DMRP subtype and biological sex involved sexting and inappropriate relationships in female adolescents, highlighting the need to explore further this potential sex-based vulnerability. Our results (OR = 2.96, 95% CI = 1.57-5.59, p = .001) support prevalence differences reported in the literature, where female patients endorse online sexual victimization more than twice as much as male individuals.25 Moreover, adolescent girls are more likely to experience adverse outcomes as a result of sexting26,27; boys who sext often perceive a boost in status or popularity, and girls who sext endure criticism and judgment from their peers.26, 27, 28 If parental response to sexting also differs depending on a child’s biological sex, girls may be more likely to be punished for sexting, and a higher incidence of interpersonal conflict (both parental and peer) stemming from these incidents could be contributing to the greater proportion of psychiatric crises involving sexing among female patients.

Although the medical records used for this analysis did not include a formal assessment of Internet addiction (IA),29 our subtype of “excessive or problematic digital media use affecting a child’s daily functioning” is similar, and, as with IA,17 we found that this DMRP was more common among male individuals. Furthermore, experience of significant distress when access to digital media is lost may be caused by uncomfortable symptoms of digital media withdrawal related to abstinence30; our finding that access-related crises were more common among male individuals could also be a reflection of a greater propensity for developing IA. Alternatively, similar to the self-medication hypothesis that has been implicated in the development of adolescent substance use disorders, research continues to suggest that youth use digital media as a coping skill to ameliorate distressing psychiatric symptoms such as anxiety or depression.31, 32, 33, 34 Reactive aggression after loss of Internet or device access could be a response to acute loss of a screen-based coping skill. Although sex-based differences related to access and excessive use DMRP subtypes were not as pronounced as those found for presentations involving sexting or inappropriate online relationships, future studies should explore whether male patients with mental illness are more likely to use digital media-centric activities to manage psychiatric symptoms.

Concerning limitations related to a single hospital system, our study sample mirrors national statistics on US youth who presented to emergency departments in psychiatric crisis during the same 2021 to 2022 time period.35 Nationally, the mean proportion of female to male patients who presented to emergency departments weekly for psychiatric crisis was ∼1.58 in 2021 and 2022, compared to 1.61 in our overall sample. The percentages of national crisis visits involving youth 12 to 17 years of age were 81.1% and 81.8% in 2021 and 2022, respectively, compared to ∼80% within our sample. The 3 psychiatric diagnoses observed most frequently among all US pediatric crisis visits were depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, and ADHD, which match our own study findings seen in Table 1. Thus, although this study’s findings should still be generalized with caution, national data suggest many US emergency departments see similar demographics of youth in crisis.

Psychiatric Diagnoses Associated with DMRP Presentations

The only diagnostic category associated with a greater likelihood of a DMRP presentation was mood disorders, which is consistent with the well-established association between mood disorder diagnoses and problematic digital media use.1,18,36, 37, 38, 39 Youth with mood disorder diagnoses in our study were nearly twice as likely to present in crisis after posting high-risk content online (eg, “I wish I were dead) or viewing high-risk content (eg, watching social media videos on how to attempt suicide). Depressed youth may turn to their devices to distract from/cope with internalizing symptoms via passive scrolling through online content, seek meaningful connection with others online to improve their mood, or look for a greater understanding of depressive symptoms. Suicidality is not uncommon among adolescents with mood disorder diagnoses,40 and digital media give teens a way to both explore suicidality through readily accessible suicide-related digital content41, 42, 43 and to access support for suicidal thoughts.

Even while adjusting for biological sex and age, moderate to high-strength associations were found between externalizing psychiatric conditions featuring dysregulation and poor impulse control (ie, disruptive behavior–related disorders) and DMRP presentations involving access-related conflict and inability to regulate device use. Given that reactive aggression toward self and others was a common manifestation of access-related presentations, these findings are not unexpected. Teens who have been diagnosed with disorders such as conduct disorder or oppositional defiant disorder may especially benefit from proactive planning for times when they might have to experience prolonged separation from devices or digital media (eg, parents could help children to identify alternative recreational activities before a device is taken away as punishment). Depressed youth with comorbid conduct-related disorders might also benefit from this proactive counseling if reliant upon digital media use to manage their symptoms.

Acuity of Presentations Connected to Digital Media/Device Use

Overall, DMRP presentations were associated with greater psychiatric acuity, with these encounters being more likely to involve suicidal ideation and recommendations for inpatient psychiatric treatment. Although this association does not prove a causal linkage between social media use and poor youth mental health, it does reinforce prior findings that high-risk digital media use is positively correlated with more severe psychiatric symptoms and other socioemotional deficits.16,18,39,44,45 Our observation that youth who presented with crises involving DMRPs were significantly more likely to have had multiple emergency psychiatric evaluations in a 2-year period also supports the hypothesis that severe psychiatric illness elevates risk of problematic digital media use.

Access-related crises were the only DMRP subtype associated with greater odds of homicidal ideation. However, this subtype did not appear to increase the odds of psychiatric hospitalization, supporting a characterization of access-related problems (and their associated homicidal ideation) as primarily impulsive or reactive in nature. By contrast, presentations citing online conflict or engagement with high-risk online content were more likely to be associated with suicidal ideation and to warrant psychiatric hospitalization. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that online conflicts such as cyberbullying are associated with suicidal behavior and ideation46,47 and are frequently cited as reasons for psychiatric hospitalization.31 The higher rates of psychiatric hospitalization among youth who posted or viewed high-risk online content may reflect a trend in youth with significant mental illness using social media and the Internet to further their understanding of their psychiatric symptoms and to connect with others who have similar mental health struggles.48,49

Compared to youth who presented with other DMRPs, patients with access-related presentations had significantly higher odds of re-presenting to the hospital because of the same DMRP. Taken together with our results showing a correlation between access-related presentations and reactive homicidal ideation that does not require hospitalization, these findings suggest that brief interventions about safe means of device removal (eg, use of time-limit warnings) could be offered in acute care settings such as emergency rooms to families experiencing access-related conflict. It is possible that standardized implementation of such an intervention could reduce a child’s risk of repeated access-related presentations.

Although our study was limited to a single hospital sample, it provides initial evidence for relationships between high-risk digital media use and psychiatric outcomes, and suggests a need for future prospective studies that include a standardized assessment of digital media use. However, the findings outlined here serve as an important first step toward the creation of targeted screening practices for high-risk digital media use among youth with psychiatric illness. For example, screens that prioritize questions about high-risk sexting in female adolescent patients and excessive device use in male adolescents could be efficient methods to identify high-risk behavior in busy acute care settings. Inquiring about the digital media habits of children and adolescents with mood disorders also appears to be particularly critical, including to what extent they use the Internet to explore and to express suicidal thoughts. Finally, because access-related crises were significantly associated with repeated digital media–related crisis presentations but less likely to require psychiatric hospitalization, interventions that proactively counsel parents and youth on safe methods of limiting device access could offer the potential for reducing psychiatric emergency room visits.

To improve existing guidance on healthy digital media practices for youth in mental health treatment, research needs to assess risky digital media use within cohorts of youth with underlying psychiatric diagnoses and to focus on clinically meaningful digital media–related outcomes, such as crisis presentations involving DMRPs.
