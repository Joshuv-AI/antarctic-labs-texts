Decoding cancer heterogeneity through super-enhancer landscapes: from subtype discovery to therapeutic opportunity
Authors: Shuyang Cai, Zhenchang Wang, Xiao Sun, Hao Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuper-enhancers (SEs) are clusters of enhancers with potent regulatory capabilities. They play a crucial role in shaping cellular identity and driving the progression of various diseases, including cancer. SEs exhibit significant heterogeneity across different cell types and cancer subtypes. Analysis of SE landscapes can recapitulate existing classification systems and unveil novel SE-driven epigenetic subtypes. In this review, we summarized the latest advancements in cancer subtype identification based on SE-related characteristics, outlined the typical analytical workflows adopted in such studies, and explored the biological and clinical significance of SE-driven subtypes. Furthermore, we discussed the field’s key challenges and emerging technologies to highlight future research directions. SE analysis provides a robust framework for dissecting cancer heterogeneity. This approach offers novel epigenetic perspectives and support for the realization of personalized medicine.Keywords: super-enhancer, cancer heterogeneity, cancer subtyping, epigenomic landscape, SE-driven classification

Introduction

The definition of super-enhancer

In 1980, Capecchi first observed what was later recognized as enhancer activity in cell transformation experiments [1]. Over the following years, the definition, composition, and functional characteristics of enhancers were gradually elucidated [2, 3]. Enhancers are a class of cis-acting DNA elements, typically ranging in size from 500 to 1500 base pairs (bp), which can regulate gene expression from distal locations. The orientation of enhancers on the DNA strand does not affect their regulatory function. The primary mechanism of enhancer activity involves the binding of transcription factors (TFs) and cofactors, facilitating the formation of DNA loops to bring the enhancer into proximity with the promoter region of the target gene, thereby enabling activation of gene expression. This regulatory relationship is not always one-to-one: a single enhancer can regulate the expression of multiple genes, and multiple enhancers can work synergistically to regulate a single gene.

Super-enhancers (SEs) are DNA regions composed of multiple clustered enhancer units, exhibiting significantly stronger regulatory activity compared to typical enhancers (TEs) [4–6]. Their genomic span is substantially larger, often exceeding 20 kilobases (kb). SEs are functionally similar to TEs but have higher TF binding density and stronger transcriptional activation capacity. Active SEs are typically marked by high levels of the acetylation of lysine 27 on histone H3 (H3K27ac) and mono-methylation of lysine 4 on histone H3 (H3K4me1). Many studies have found that SEs are often located near genes that are critical for regulating cell identity and function, contributing significantly to cancer initiation and progression by promoting oncogene overexpression, disrupting key signaling pathways, and modulating the tumor immune microenvironment [7–9]. A subset of active enhancers could be transcribed into enhancer RNAs (eRNAs), a class of non-coding RNAs whose expression levels are closely correlated to the activity of the corresponding enhancers [10]. Studies have shown that eRNAs can function in a variety of ways, including affecting chromatin accessibility, promoting the establishment and stabilization of enhancer-promoter interactions, and enhancing the recruitment of transcriptional cofactors in the promoter region [11–13]. However, the precise regulatory mechanisms and functions of eRNAs have not been fully elucidated and require further in-depth research. Figure 1 shows the regulatory mechanism of SE.

The regulatory mechanism of SE. SEs are characterized by high levels of active histone marks (H3K4me1, H3K27ac). They interact with target gene promoters through chromatin loops, where they recruit a high density of TFs, coactivators (e.g. CBP/P300, BRD4), and the mediator complex. SEs ultimately drive target gene transcription via RNA polymerase II (pol II). Furthermore, a subset of active SEs are transcribed into eRNAs, which can further stabilize this regulatory interaction.

Super-enhancers in normal development and cancer

SEs play a primary regulatory role in both normal cell lineage establishment and malignant dysregulation in cancer. During normal cell development and differentiation, they establish and maintain the functional state of specific cell types by driving high-level, stable expression of lineage-specific master TFs. For instance, in embryonic stem cells, SEs are enriched near core pluripotency genes such as Oct4, Sox2, and Nanog. These SEs, together with the proteins encoded by these genes, form a robust positive feedback loop to maintain the pluripotent state [5]. This evidence suggests that a cell’s SE landscape dictates the expression patterns of its core regulatory genes, thereby sustaining its normal physiological functions. In the process of cell lineage differentiation, SEs act as fate-determining switches. For example, during hematopoiesis, the master factor SCL/TAL1 directs common progenitor cells toward the blood lineage by selectively activating hematopoietic SEs while repressing cardiac-related SEs [14]. Similarly, during neurogenesis, a specific SE adjacent to the Sox2 gene precisely regulates its expression in a dose-dependent manner, ensuring the correct regional identity of neural progenitor cells is established [15]. Furthermore, SEs could serve as critical hubs for integrating external developmental signals. In embryonic stem cells, compared to TEs, SEs are highly enriched for the binding sites of downstream effector TFs of key signaling pathways like Wingless-INT (WNT), transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), and leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF). This allows SEs to directly translate spatiotemporal signals from the embryonic environment into transcriptional commands that control core cell identity programs [16].

In cancer, however, this regulatory machinery is systematically hijacked and reprogrammed. Tumor cells drive the aberrant expression of key oncogenes by forming de novo SEs. The proto-oncogene myelocytomatosis (MYC) is a classic example; tumor-specific SEs have been identified near its locus in various cancers, including multiple myeloma, T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, and lung cancer [9]. This reprogramming of the SE landscape essentially forges a new, pathological cell identity for cancer cells, characterized by uncontrolled proliferation and survival. Consequently, cancer cells often develop a “transcriptional addiction” to these SE-driven programs [17]. Cancer cells hijack SEs through several molecular mechanisms. These include: focal genomic amplification, which can form SEs through tandem duplications [9]; large-scale genomic rearrangements, such as chromosomal translocations that place SEs near proto-oncogenes; and somatic mutations in non-coding regions, such as point mutations or small indels, which can create novel TF binding sites that recruit the transcriptional machinery to form an SE [17]. Additionally, the constitutive activation of oncogenic signaling pathways, such as the WNT pathway in colorectal cancer (CRC) and the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway in renal cell carcinoma, is another major driver of aberrant SE formation [18]. In summary, reprogrammed SE networks act as key drivers in tumorigenesis and malignant progression. Therefore, cancer can be largely viewed as a fundamental disease of cell identity, driven by the reprogramming of the SE landscape.

The heterogeneity of SEs increases the complexity of cancer research.

The heterogeneity of SEs is a critical characteristic, observable in various aspects. Firstly, SEs exhibit heterogeneity across different cancer types and subtypes. For example, a study has shown that 7333 SEs were identified specifically in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) compared to non-triple-negative breast cancer (non-TNBC) [19]. In medulloblastoma, the four known molecular subtypes exhibit distinct SE repertoires, further supporting the crucial role of SEs in defining cancer subtype identity [20]. Moreover, SEs display pronounced tissue specificity, reflecting how cancers often preserve key transcriptional features of their cells of origin. SEs identified in primary neuroblastoma (NB) samples overlap by only 15% with SEs from other tissues [21], while in gastric cancer (GC), about 60% of SEs show tissue specificity [22]. These findings suggest that different cancers and cancer subtypes drive specific transcriptional regulatory programs through different SE repertoires. Notably, SEs have been used for the identification and classification of cancer subtypes, leading to the discovery of novel cancer subtypes or the refinement of existing cancer classification systems. For instance, in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), SE profiling has led to the identification of novel epigenomic subtypes that are not captured by traditional genetic classification [23].

Secondly, the heterogeneity of SEs is also reflected in their context-dependent reprogramming within the tumor microenvironment. For example, Zhou et al. [8] revealed an approximately 11% difference in the SE landscape between primary CRC and in vitro CRC models, and identified an SE activated by the inflammatory environment, whose regulated genes play a key role in the carcinogenesis of CRC. Compared with TEs, SEs exhibit higher sensitivity to chemical inhibition and environmental signals, which may be due to the enrichment of a large number of chromatin-associated proteins within their regions, including co-activators and kinases that play key roles in transcription [24, 25]. Notably, SE sensitivity to external stimuli is uneven and should be taken into account.

SEs exhibit certain plasticity throughout tumor progression. Studies have elucidated the role of SEs in the progression of bone-related cancers, including multiple stages such as tumorigenesis, growth, and metastasis [26]. SEs can also be reprogrammed in response to environmental stimuli or during distinct tumor development stages. Such reprogramming may involve de novo SE formation, chromatin-rearrangement-induced SE repositioning, and activity modulation due to mutations or single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). For example, a short insertion about 8 kbs upstream of the TAL1 oncogene introduced a binding site for the myeloblastosis (MYB) TF, thereby forming an SE that drives TAL1 expression [27]. In NB, a SNP within the intronic region of the LMO1 oncogene created a binding site for GATA3, thereby activating an SE and promoting LMO1 expression [28]. This plasticity makes cancer cells highly dependent on SE-driven transcriptional programs, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets. In fact, disrupting specific SE structures or interfering with their interactions with key cofactors has been proposed as a promising therapeutic approach [29].

While studies on SEs have yielded valuable insights into gene expression regulation and their roles in cancer [17, 30, 31], significant challenges remain in their functional validation and therapeutic application. The mechanisms by which SE heterogeneity influences different cancer types, subtype characteristics, and clinical outcomes are not yet fully understood [32]. Functional validation is complicated by the intricate and dynamic regulatory networks of SEs, and their landscapes exhibit considerable heterogeneity across patients, tumor types, and even within single tumor cell subpopulations. This diversity limits the generalizability of SE-targeted therapies. Consequently, while inhibitors targeting components like BRD4, CDK7, and p300 have shown some efficacy [33–37], their effectiveness is often inconsistent due to patient variability, drug resistance, and off-target effects leading to toxicity [32]. Despite these hurdles, leveraging SE heterogeneity provides new opportunities for cancer subtyping and targeted therapy. Accurately identifying subtype-specific SEs and their regulatory networks is foundational for developing more refined cancer classification systems and more effective therapeutic strategies in precision oncology.

Super-enhancer prediction tools

The development of SE prediction tools has developed through three distinct stages: from descriptive analysis relying on a single experimental signal, to machine learning models integrating multi-dimensional features, and finally, toward de novo prediction based on DNA sequence alone.

In the first stage, work centered on the Ranking of Super-Enhancer (ROSE) [5, 24] algorithm laid the foundation for the field. The ROSE workflow involves stitching constituent enhancers (typically identified from H3K27ac or MED1 ChIP-seq signals) that are spatially proximal, ranking them by total signal intensity, and using a geometric inflection point to distinguish SEs from TEs. This was the earliest method for SE identification and remains the gold standard. ROSE has been widely adopted in research and was implemented with a similar principle in the HOMER suite in 2014 (findPeaks–style super) [38]. Building on the ROSE framework, LILY (detection of super-enhancers in cancer data) [39] introduced a Copy Number Variation (CNV) correction step, significantly improving prediction accuracy in cancer samples. Despite the emergence of more complex models, the ROSE algorithm remains the most widely used tool today due to its simple logic, low data requirements, and clear interpretability. It provides a reliable and easy-to-implement standardized workflow and has become the benchmark against which new tools are compared.

The accumulation of multi-omics data led to the second stage of SE prediction, characterized by the use of machine learning models to integrate multi-dimensional data for more robust SE identification. imPROSE [40], a key tool of this phase, evaluated multiple algorithms, focusing on Random Forests. It integrated a wide array of features, including ChIP-seq data (histone modifications, TFs, coactivators/mediators/cohesins, RNAPII, and DNase-seq) and sequence characteristics (conservation, GC content, repeat ratios, and DNA motifs), to significantly improve the discrimination of SEs from TEs. Subsequently, DEEPSEN (a convolutional neural network based method for super-enhancer prediction) [41] extended this approach to deep learning, using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to automatically learn the non-linear patterns distinguishing SEs from TEs based on inputs containing dozens of epigenomic features. This shift transformed SE identification from a descriptive process based on signal sorting and inflection point delineation to a supervised predictive problem. Although integrating multi-dimensional features is theoretically the most robust approach, it demands extensive input data. To build and apply an effective predictive model, researchers must generate multiple types of high-quality datasets for the same sample, which presents significant barriers in terms of experimental cost and technical expertise. Consequently, while these methods are logically sound, their high data cost has limited their widespread adoption.

Currently, the field has entered a third stage—prediction based solely on DNA sequence. This shift aims to decipher the intrinsic regulatory rules encoded within the genome. Early work in this direction by DeepSE [42] first demonstrated the feasibility of using CNNs to process sequence embeddings for prediction. Later, SENet [43] developed a more sophisticated model that uses dna2vec for feature embedding, a convolutional network to extract local features, attention pooling for feature refinement, and a final Transformer module to capture contextual information. Building on this, MUlti-feature fusion for Super-Enhancer (MuSE) [44] proposed a multi-feature fusion strategy, representing DNA sequences in parallel with both one-hot and dna2vec encoding to simultaneously capture single-nucleotide information as well as local and global sequence features. The most recent advance, EnsembleSE (identification of super-enhancers based on ensemble learning) [45], utilized an ensemble learning strategy with a multi-angle feature representation method to extract both high-dimensional abstract relationships and key low-dimensional biological features from the sequence. This ensemble approach not only surpasses previous sequence-only models in accuracy and recall but also balances performance with biological interpretability by merging the strengths of different models. This stage of development highlighted a critical concept: the potential for SE formation is largely determined by the DNA sequence itself. Sequence-only models significantly lower the data barrier, greatly expanding the application of SE research where experimental data is scarce and providing a powerful tool for understanding gene regulation at a more fundamental level.

In summary, while models that integrate multi-omics data are theoretically superior, their high data costs limit their universal applicability. Conversely, although sequence-only prediction models are promising, their accuracy and biological interpretability are still evolving. Therefore, for practical applications, ROSE remains the primary recommended tool. It offers a reliable and easy-to-implement standardized workflow, and its results are not only readily comparable with the vast body of published literature but also provide a solid foundation for downstream analysis. Characterization of cancer subtypes based on super-enhancers

Clustering analysis based on super-enhancer and related features

Super-enhancer

Traditional cancer subtyping has primarily relied on transcriptomic profiles or DNA methylation patterns. With advances in epigenomic technologies, it is now possible to quantify the activity of gene regulatory elements at high resolution. SEs, as central regulators of transcription and cell identity, have shown strong potential in distinguishing cancer subtypes based on their activation landscapes.

In 2016, Lin et al. [20] identified enhancers exhibiting significant activity differences across four medulloblastoma subtypes using Analysis of Variance, accounting for 26% of all enhancers. To further explore the regulatory patterns and subtype-specific activation profiles, K-means clustering of all enhancers was applied and revealed six distinct categories:

Each subtype, WNT, sonic hedgehog (SHH), Group 3, and Group 4, corresponded to a unique category of enhancers;

Each subtype, WNT, sonic hedgehog (SHH), Group 3, and Group 4, corresponded to a unique category of enhancers;

WNT and SHH subtypes shared one category of enhancers;

WNT and SHH subtypes shared one category of enhancers;

Group 3 and Group 4 subtypes shared another category of enhancers.

Group 3 and Group 4 subtypes shared another category of enhancers.

To assess whether SEs alone could capture subtype-specific regulatory features, they performed hierarchical clustering based on SE signals. This approach successfully reproduced the four known subtypes defined by transcriptomics, indicating that the features of SE signals can accurately recapitulate established classifications. Based on the analysis of the SE regulatory network, the researchers further identified the main transcriptional regulatory factors responsible for these subtype differences. Particularly in the Group 4 subtype, LMX1A, EOMES, and LHX2 were identified as main TFs. These TFs exhibit overlapping and spatiotemporally specific expression patterns during early cerebellar development and are closely related to specific cerebellar regions. This finding provided important clues for revealing the potential cellular origin of the Group 4 subtype.

In addition to medulloblastoma, a similar classification approach has been extended to another central nervous system tumor, NB. In 2020, Gartlgruber et al. [21] investigated SE-driven subtypes and their associated core transcriptional regulatory programs in primary NB and NB cell lines. Using samples from 60 primary NB tumors and 25 cell lines, the study identified four SE-driven epigenetic subtypes through clustering analysis of H3K27ac signals. Among these subtypes, MYCNSE, MNA-HRSE, and MNA-LRSE were previously known, while MESSE was a novel subtype discovered in primary samples, exhibiting mesenchymal characteristics, induced by rat sarcoma (RAS) activation, and enriched in recurrent tumors. Applying the same clustering method to the SE target gene expression matrix reproduced these four subtypes. They further analyzed subtype-specific TF networks and found distinct activity patterns linked to each epigenetic subtype.

In 2017, McKeown et al. [23] described the SE landscape of 66 AML patients, aiming to uncover transcriptional regulatory circuits and potential therapeutic targets. They performed non-negative matrix factorization consensus clustering (NMF-C) on SE scores, revealing six distinct AML subtypes. Specifically, subtypes 1 and 2 exhibited SE characteristics closer to monocytes, while subtypes 3 to 6 exhibited stronger hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell (HSPC)-like characteristics. Further investigation of these SE-defined AML subtypes revealed that subtype-specific SEs typically involve target genes encoding core TFs, such as HOXA, HOXB, MEIS1, PROM1 (HSPC-specific), and IRF8 (monocyte-specific). These identified core TFs highlighted the underlying master regulatory circuits shared by these subtypes. The information provided by SE-based clustering analysis extends beyond what can be obtained through traditional mutational analysis, offering new perspectives for cancer research.

This approach is also applicable to more complex solid tumors. In 2021, Li et al. [46] analyzed the epigenomic landscape of CRC. They used principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce dimensionality on 73 paired CRC tissues and normal tissues, utilizing data types including gene expression, H3K27ac signal, and H3K4me3 signal. The results demonstrated that gene expression and H3K27ac signal effectively distinguished normal samples from tumor samples, whereas H3K4me3 signal failed to do so, confirming the utility of SE information for tumor identification. Furthermore, clustering analysis based on H3K27ac signals at variant super-enhancer loci categorized the samples into three subgroups. Notably, the majority of CMS2 subtype samples were classified into the G1 subgroup, indicating that the CMS2 group is more homogeneous compared to other subtypes and possesses the highest number of active SEs. They also identified multiple TFs associated with CRC and validated the roles of SE-regulated PHF19 and TBC1D16 in CRC.

In 2021, Huang et al. [19] conducted a detailed investigation into the SE landscape of TNBC cell lines. The researchers constructed an SE similarity network, where each node represented a cell line, and the edges between nodes represented the Jaccard similarity coefficient, reflecting the co-occurrence of SEs across cell lines. The research team then performed clustering analysis on the network using the Markov clustering algorithm (MCL), revealing two distinct clusters. The SE-based clustering analysis distinguished TNBC from non-TNBC subtype without relying on gene expression data or predefined subtype markers. Further analysis identified TNBC-specific SEs associated with oncogenes such as FOXC1, MET, and MYC. They also experimentally validated the functional impact of FOXC1 SE deletion on TNBC cell proliferation and invasiveness using Clustered Regularly Interspaced Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR)/Cas9 technology. In fact, as early as 2010, studies on DNA methylation profiles in breast cancer revealed that different molecular subtypes of breast cancer exhibit significantly distinct DNA methylation patterns [47]. Through unsupervised hierarchical clustering, the researchers categorized the samples into three main groups, and K-means clustering further confirmed the robustness of the results. Notably, the study found that tumors from BRCA2 mutation carriers exhibited higher methylation frequencies compared to other groups. These findings highlight the importance of epigenetic modifications in the development of breast cancer.

Following these findings, researchers conducted a detailed analysis specifically in TNBC. In 2025, Chen et al. [48] carried out a comprehensive investigation of SE heterogeneity in TNBC. They integrated H3K27ac ChIP-seq data from 15 TNBC cell lines, bulk RNA-seq profiles of 121 TCGA-BRCA and 206 Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC) TNBC patients, and single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data encompassing 28 317 tumor cells to generate SE activity and target-gene expression matrices. Each matrix underwent NMF clustering via the ButchR package across k = 2–6, with the optimal cluster number selected based on cophenetic correlation, average silhouette width, Frobenius reconstruction error, and average Amari distance. This yielded k = 3 clusters for both the cell-line and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets, k = 2 for METABRIC, and k = 4 for the single-cell cohort. A mesenchymal development subtype emerged consistently across all four analyses, characterized by hybrid epithelial-mesenchymal transition, marked enrichment of extracellular matrix pathways, and heightened cancer-associated fibroblast infiltration; this subtype correlated with significantly poorer overall and relapse-free survival in the METABRIC and TCGA cohorts. They also pinpointed a core regulatory circuit (CRC) driving this high-risk subtype, formed by a TNBC-specific SE (SE269) and the TF VAX2 in a positive-feedback loop. Functional experiments in vitro and in mouse xenograft models demonstrated that either VAX2 knockout or SE269 disruption robustly suppressed tumor-cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, and decreased sensitivity to BET inhibitors. To support clinical application, they then built and validated machine-learning classifiers based on the expression of 27 VAX2-regulated genes. These models achieved Area Under the Curve (AUC) > 0.7 in independent validation cohorts, offering a practical biomarker panel to identify TNBC patients most likely to benefit from BET inhibitor therapy.

Applying this framework to bladder cancer (BLCA) further demonstrated the broad applicability of SE-based classification. In 2023, Neyret-Kahn et al. [49] investigated the role of SEs in driving the transcriptional programs of different BLCA subtypes. This study first integrated multiple ChIP-seq datasets through the ChromHMM method to construct a comprehensive epigenomic map of BLCA. The map defined six major chromatin states (E1–E6):

E1: Regions with no epigenetic marks;

E1: Regions with no epigenetic marks;

E2: Regions enriched with H3K27ac modification, corresponding to active promoters and enhancers;

E2: Regions enriched with H3K27ac modification, corresponding to active promoters and enhancers;

E3: Regions simultaneously enriched with active and repressive epigenetic marks, corresponding to bivalent enhancers or bivalent promoters;

E3: Regions simultaneously enriched with active and repressive epigenetic marks, corresponding to bivalent enhancers or bivalent promoters;

E4: Repressive regions enriched with H3K27me3 and H3K9me3 modifications;

E4: Repressive regions enriched with H3K27me3 and H3K9me3 modifications;

E5: Regions only enriched with weak H3K9me3 modification;

E5: Regions only enriched with weak H3K9me3 modification;

E6: Heterochromatic regions enriched with H3K27me3 and H3K9me3 modifications.

E6: Heterochromatic regions enriched with H3K27me3 and H3K9me3 modifications.

Analysis of RNA-seq data revealed that gene expression was closely correlated with the expected chromatin states. Using multiple correspondence analysis (MCA), the research team successfully identified two BLCA subtypes: basal and non-basal, which aligned with known molecular subtypes of BLCA. Furthermore, PCA based on SE markers (H3K27ac modification signal) further validated the classification of these two subtypes. In the larger TCGA-BLCA dataset, hierarchical clustering of samples based on subtype-specific SE-associated gene expression successfully reproduced these molecular subtypes. Similar to the aforementioned breast cancer studies, the researchers validated the critical role of FOXA1 in maintaining BLCA subtype identity through CRISPR/Cas9 knockout experiments.

Collectively, these studies demonstrated the critical role of epigenetic modifications, particularly the characteristics of SEs, in defining cancer subtypes and driving their unique molecular and clinical features. The studies consistently highlighted the distinct SE landscapes specific to different cancer subtypes and revealed the complex networks of TFs regulated by SEs. Some studies further explored the functional roles of these networks in cancer transcriptional regulation and proposed their potential contributions to cancer progression. These findings supported the role of SEs in defining cancer subtypes and guiding potential therapeutic strategies.

Enhancer RNA

eRNAs are a class of long non-coding RNAs transcribed from enhancer regions, and their expression levels can indirectly reflect the activity of enhancers. In 2020, Chen et al. [50] utilized RNA-seq data to identify eRNA loci and characterized their activity in cancer. By analyzing the expression levels of eRNA loci in cancer samples, they have discovered that SEs are frequently dysregulated in cancer through various mechanisms. eRNA profiles offer insights into genotype–phenotype relationships. The response of melanoma to anti-PD1 immunotherapy was investigated. While no significant differential expression was observed in protein-coding genes, 164 eRNAs exhibited significant differential expression between responders and non-responders. To facilitate extensive research on eRNAs, the team developed the Cancer eRNA Atlas (TCeA), a data portal that provides comprehensive information on eRNA loci, expression levels, and their associations with various molecular and clinical characteristics of cancer. Additionally, a CAGE-based analysis revealed that, compared to mRNA, a larger proportion of eRNAs exhibit higher cell-type specificity [9]. This finding supports the potential of eRNA expression patterns as a basis for cancer subtyping.

In 2020, Qin et al. [51] focused on analyzing the role of eRNAs in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). By examining eRNA expression patterns in 80 Chinese LUAD patients, they identified 188 functional eRNAs and classified the patients into three subtypes based on eRNA expression using consensus clustering analysis. Subtype 1 exhibited genomic characteristics similar to normal tissue but was associated with SETD2 mutation. Subtype 2 was primarily composed of terminal respiratory unit group, while Subtype 3 was dominated by proximal proliferative transcriptional subtype. This study showed that eRNA-based clustering identified LUAD subtypes with higher invasiveness, supporting personalized diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

In 2022, Hu et al. [52] investigated the role of eRNAs in GC and identified three distinct immune subtypes of GC based on eRNA expression patterns. Using eRNA expression data from 349 TCGA-GC samples, the researchers performed consensus clustering analysis and classified the samples into two major clusters: cluster A and cluster B. Further analysis revealed that cluster B could be subdivided into a high immune score subtype and a low immune score subtype (cluster C). The three subtypes differed in survival, immune infiltration, and immunotherapy response. By performing differential expression analysis and integrating survival data and target gene information from the eRic database, the researchers identified 24 prognostically relevant eRNA-target gene pairs associated with the subtypes. To address the lack of eRNA expression data in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets, the team developed a machine learning approach based on random forest models, enabling the mapping of eRNA expression patterns to mRNA expression profiles. Furthermore, the researchers proposed an eRNA score to quantify patient eRNA activity levels. A higher eRNA score means improved overall survival and immunotherapy response rates. The eRNA score may help predict immunotherapy response and prognosis.

In 2022, Bu et al. [53] performed a comprehensive characterization of the eRNA landscape in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and assessed its relationship with tumor immune infiltration and intratumoral heterogeneity. Analyses were carried out on two publicly available GEO cohorts (total n = 122 HCC samples). Using the Pipeline for Enhancer Transcription, ribosomal RNA–depleted total RNA-seq data were quantified for eRNA expression (reads per kb of transcript per million mapped reads [RPKM] >0 in at least 10% of samples). Within each cohort, eRNAs detected in at least 50% of tumors were retained, and the top 50% most variable loci by median absolute deviation (MAD) (n = 1244) were selected as input features for NMF clustering. The cophenetic correlation coefficient indicated an optimal cluster number of k = 3. PCA and Subclass Mapping (SubMap) confirmed high concordance of subtypes C1–C3 across the two cohorts. Subsequent comparative analyses revealed that C1 exhibited a prototypical immune-enriched phenotype, C2 represented an immune-exhausted subtype characterized by pronounced activation of proliferation-related pathways and the poorest clinical prognosis, and C3 showed intermediate immune infiltration alongside enrichment of metabolic pathways, correlating with the best survival outcomes and the greatest predicted benefit from transarterial chemoembolization. Finally, they constructed a 51-eRNAs classifier using nearest template prediction (NTP) algorithm, which was validated in independent TCGA-LIHC and SYSUCC cohorts, demonstrating robust predictive power for subtype-specific prognosis, immune features, and therapy response.

Extending their work in HCC, Bu et al. [54] applied the same eRNA framework to CRC, revealing analogous subtypes. In 2024, they analyzed RNA-seq data of stage II/III CRC patients from TCGA to investigate eRNA expression profiles. They identified 237 prognosis-associated eRNAs and performed consensus clustering analysis based on these eRNAs, ultimately categorizing the samples into two subtypes. Significant differences in survival rates and clinical outcomes were observed between the two subtypes. To validate the distinctions between the subtypes, the researchers constructed a signature template using differentially expressed eRNAs and applied this signature to the TCGA CRC dataset using the NTP algorithm, which confirmed the previous analytical results. Compared to CMS subtypes, subtype C1 largely corresponded to CMS1, while subtype C2 aligned with CMS2. Notably, subtype C1, characterized by high immune infiltration, exhibited a potentially higher sensitivity to immunotherapy, particularly PD1 inhibitors. These results highlight CRC heterogeneity and may inform personalized immunotherapy.

In 2023, Yan et al. [55] investigated the role of eRNAs in identifying prognostically relevant subtypes of stomach adenocarcinoma. They first analyzed gene expression data from 40 stage IV and 16 stage IA patients to identify eRNAs that exhibited significant expression changes between early and late stages of GC. These differentially expressed eRNAs were then used as clustering input to classify 415 patients, ultimately identifying three subtypes: Cluster A, Cluster B, and Cluster C. Cluster A was characterized by higher tumor purity, Cluster B exhibited a higher immune score, and Cluster C displayed a higher stromal score. These subtypes reflect disease progression and reveal immune microenvironment differences. The distinct immune and stromal characteristics of Clusters A, B, and C may have significant implications for treatment strategies and prognostic assessment.

General workflow and practical pipeline for super-enhancer-based subtyping

Molecular subtyping using SEs follows a systematic workflow, as depicted in Fig. 4. This process offers a powerful framework to dissect disease heterogeneity from an epigenetic point of view. It guides researchers through data collection, processing, subtype discovery, biological analysis, validation, and ultimately, the exploration of translational applications in precision medicine.

A representative workflow for cancer subtyping based on SE-related features. The process includes six main stages: (1) data collection from public databases or wet-lab experiments; (2) data processing to identify SEs/eRNAs and build a feature matrix; (3) sample subtyping via unsupervised clustering; (4) subtype analysis to characterize various biological features and clinical relevance; (5) validation with independent datasets and experiments; and (6) exploration of clinical applications.

It details key steps: data preprocessing (quality control, alignment), feature construction (MACS2, ROSE, feature selection), subtype discovery (e.g. NMF, consensus clustering), subtype interpretation (differential, pathway analysis, etc.), and external validation (classifier building, wet-lab experiments).

The first step is constructing a feature matrix from raw data by quantifying enhancer activity. Using H3K27ac ChIP-seq data, this process starts with peak calling, typically with Model-based Analysis of ChIP-seq (MACS2) [56]. MACS2 identifies significantly enriched genomic regions by comparing ChIP samples to input controls. The most widely-used algorithm ROSE is then applied for SE identification. As mentioned before, ROSE ranks constituent enhancers by H3K27ac signal, stitches adjacent enhancers (typically within 12.5 kb) into larger domains, and uses a signal inflection point to distinguish SEs from TEs. For eRNA-focused analyses, standard normalization methods like transcripts per million, RPKM or fragments per kb of transcript per million mapped reads correct for sequencing depth and gene length, ensuring comparable expression levels. A subsequent filtering step retains reliable eRNAs based on length (e.g. > 50 bp) and expression levels (e.g. RPKM >0 in more than 10% of samples) [54]. For both data types, the final step is feature selection. To reduce noise and improve clustering, the top 10%–30% most variable features, identified by methods like MAD or variance, are selected for analysis.

The refined feature matrix is then used to discover molecular subtypes via unsupervised clustering. Non-NMF [57] decomposes the feature matrix into a signature matrix (W, latent features) and an exposure matrix (H, feature contributions per sample), simultaneously grouping samples and identifying key markers that define each subtype. Other advanced methods are also effective. The graph-based MCL simulates random walks on a sample similarity network to find robust clusters [58]. It is also applicable to SE-gene regulatory networks, TF regulatory networks, and other similar networks. For categorical data like chromatin states, MCA reduces dimensionality to separate biological groups. In the study, Dim 1 distinguished primary tumor samples from cell lines, while Dim 2 differentiated non-basal and basal subgroups [49]. Standard algorithms such as hierarchical clustering, k-means, and PAM are also common, often combined with consensus clustering. This resampling-based method assesses cluster stability, ensuring a robust final subtyping solution. In these studies, consensus clustering has been primarily applied to eRNA expression data.

After subtype discovery, analysis shifts to biological interpretation and validation. This usually begins with differential analysis between subtypes using tools like DESeq2 [59], edgeR [60], or limma [61] to find subtype-specific genes and regulatory elements. These features then drive downstream investigations, such as pathway enrichment, motif analysis, and regulatory network construction. Integrating these results with other omics data builds a holistic biological profile for each subtype. The final step is external validation. This involves building a classifier (usually with machine learning) for new samples and confirming key findings with targeted wet-lab experiments like CRISPR perturbations, immunoblotting, and xenograft studies.

In summary, the SE-based subtyping pipeline is a robust, integrative strategy. By shifting from gene-centric views to the landscape of transcriptional regulation, this approach uncovers deeper layers of disease heterogeneity. The successful execution of this pipeline, which combines rigorous computational analysis with essential experimental validation, can identify novel biomarkers, reveal therapeutic vulnerabilities, and advance precision medicine.

Available super-enhancer databases

To support related analyses, we briefly survey representative databases that curate SEs and eRNAs. The following five are the most practical databases.

SEdb v3.0 (http://www.licpathway.net/sedbv3/index.php) [62]: released in 2018 (v1.0), last updated 12 May 2025 (v3.0). SEdb contains 3 478 186 SEs from 5387 samples, including human, mouse, Arabidopsis thaliana, and maize. In addition to SE coordinates, it provides genetic and epigenetic annotations on SEs, supports search and bulk download, and offers a suite of online analysis tools, including overlap analysis, gene–SE analysis, SNP–SE analysis, etc. Collectively, SEdb is among the largest and most practically useful SE databases available.

SEA v3.0 (http://sea.edbc.org/) [63]: released in 2016 (v1.0), last updated on 3 June 2019 (v3.0). SEA compiles 164 545 SEs from 11 species, including human and mouse. It also catalogs 3 361 785 TEs and adds regulatory annotations (chromatin interactions, SNPs, TFBS, SpCas9 target sites). The site supports flexible filtering and search, SEA-Browser genome visualization, charting, download, and built-in analysis tools (e.g. overlap/annotation).

CenhANCER (https://chenzxlab.hzau.edu.cn/cenhancer/#/) [64]: released and last updated in 2021. It is a cancer-focused human enhancer resource built from H3K27ac ChIP-seq profiles, spanning 49 cancer types. It catalogs 29 815 953 TEs and 516 447 SEs in 805 primary tissues, 27 213 455 TEs, and 461 964 SEs in 671 cell lines. Beyond coordinates, CenhANCER integrates expression quantitative trait locus from Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) and PancanQTL, and risk SNPs from Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS), and reports TF enrichments across samples. The web portal provides browsing, searching, and bulk download options.

Note that dbSUPER [65] was the earliest widely used public SE database and remains important; however, it has not been updated for years and is probably out-of-date. EnhancerAtlas [66] is a major resource for TEs, but it does not provide SE data.

eRNAbase (https://bio.liclab.net/eRNAbase/index.php) [67]: released and last updated in 2024. It catalogs 9 155 797 eRNAs from 858 human samples and 1 244 131 eRNAs from 154 mouse samples. It also provides comprehensive genetic and epigenetic annotations, and links eRNAs to putative target genes using five complementary methods. The web portal supports browse and search by region, gene, tissue, or cell type, offers a genome browser built on JBrowse, and enables bulk download. It includes three built-in analyses for pathway regulation by eRNAs, interpretation of genetic variation in eRNA regions, and inference of eRNA related TF to target gene relationships. It is one of the most richly annotated eRNA databases.

eRic (https://hanlaboratory.com/eRic/) [68]: released and last updated in 2019. eRic collects a pan-cancer eRNA expression profile across TCGA samples. It also provides three other modules: clinical relevance, target genes, and drug response. In the original paper, 9108 detectable eRNAs were cataloged across TCGA cancers. The web portal provides detailed search and download options. eRic focuses on human cancers and uniquely offers rich clinical associations and drug-response data, making it especially valuable for moving eRNA research from basic discovery toward clinical translation.

Super-enhancer analysis provides insights into cancer subtype characteristics

SE analysis can unveil subtype-specific epigenetic landscapes and distinct core regulatory networks. For instance, in TNBC, Huang et al. [19] showed that TNBC displayed an SE landscape distinct from that of non-TNBC, with these SEs potently driving the expression of genes such as FOXC1 and MET, which are closely associated with invasion and metastasis. Chen et al. [48] subsequently used SE features to subdivide TNBC into functional subtypes, identifying a mesenchymal development subtype governed by a VAX2-centric CRC. This SE-VAX2 circuit is essential for maintaining the malignant phenotype and significantly enhances its sensitivity to BET inhibitors. In medulloblastoma, Lin et al. [71] systematically constructed the CRC for each subgroup, clearly identifying specific TF sets that determine subtype identity. By tracking the spatiotemporal activity of key regulatory TFs in Group 4, the study inferred the putative cells of origin for this subtype. Notably, these main TFs had not been implicated in medulloblastoma on the basis of transcriptomic data alone. In multiple myeloma, Alvarez-Benayas et al. [72] profiled chromatin states and identified de novo formed and re-commissioned enhancers, enabling the definition of subtype-specific master regulators and the construction of TF regulatory networks for each subtype. Network-centric analyses identified six pivotal TFs (CXXC1, BPTF, MAZ, KLF13, CBFB, RFX5) whose up-regulated expression correlated with poor patient prognosis. Together, these studies underscore the distinct advantage of SE analysis in uncovering subtype-specific regulatory networks and defining subtype identities.

eRNAs, the transcriptional products of active enhancers, reflect enhancer activity and exhibit pronounced cell type specificity. Cancer subtypes defined by eRNA expression display distinct patterns of immune infiltration. Bu et al. [53, 54] performed eRNA-based clustering in HCC and CRC, respectively identifying cancer subtypes with distinct tumor immune microenvironments and prognostic differences. These findings also informed potential therapeutic strategies. For instance, the HCC C1 subtype, characterized by immune infiltration enrichment, was suggested to be sensitive to immune checkpoint inhibitors, whereas the HCC C2 subtype, which displayed immune depletion and the worst prognosis, was identified as potentially benefiting from targeted therapy. Similarly, eRNA-based analyses of GC by Hu et al. [52] and Yan et al. [55] also discovered immune subtypes. Both studies showed that eRNA-defined immune subtypes hold prognostic value. Hu et al. [52] further developed an eRNA score that independently predicted overall survival and response to immunotherapy, with high-score patients exhibiting prolonged survival and an increased likelihood of therapeutic benefit. These findings underscore the potential of eRNAs as biomarkers for guiding immunotherapy.

In conclusion, SE-based subtype analysis offers new insights for understanding the complex molecular heterogeneity of cancer, not only contributing to the refinement of existing cancer classification systems but also revealing molecular mechanisms underlying tumor behavior, treatment response, and clinical outcomes. These findings support further research into tumor heterogeneity and clinical stratification.

Therapeutic strategies targeting super-enhancers driven regulatory networks

The pivotal role of SEs in driving aberrant transcriptional programs has established them as compelling therapeutic targets. Strategies have been developed to disrupt SE function by targeting key components of the transcriptional machinery that are highly enriched at these regulatory elements, offering a promising approach to selectively inhibit the programs that drive cancer growth.

One of the primary strategies involves the use of BET protein inhibitors. These small molecules competitively displace BET proteins (BRD2/3/4, and BRDT) from acetylated histones at SEs, thereby disrupting transcriptional activation. Among these, BRD4 interacts with MED1 and participates in transcriptional elongation regulation through assembly with RNA polymerase II, thus playing an important role in transcriptional activation [73]. JQ1, a pioneering tool compound in this class, has been shown to selectively inhibit MYC oncogene expression in various cancers by reducing BRD4 occupancy, MED1 recruitment, and RNA polymerase II elongation capability [74].

Another major therapeutic strategy targets Cyclin-Dependent Kinases (CDKs) that are critical for transcriptional regulation. CDK7/9 inhibitors target CDKs, CDK7, and CDK9, which phosphorylate the C terminal domain (CTD) of RNA polymerase II, a step that is essential for transcription initiation and elongation. THZ1, a covalent inhibitor of CDK7, functions by inhibiting RNA polymerase II CTD phosphorylation and selectively targets SE-driven transcriptional programs, including MYC-dependent transcriptional amplification and the expression of other cancer-specific oncogenic TFs and signaling molecules [75].

Although BET inhibitors and CDK7/9 inhibitors have shown therapeutic efficacy, resistance to single-agent treatment with either inhibitor has been reported in preclinical models, necessitating further research to understand resistance mechanisms [30].

In multiple myeloma, immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs) are commonly used therapeutics that function by inducing the degradation of TFs IKZF1 and IKZF3. Both IKZF1 and IKZF3 bind to SEs and regulate the expression of genes essential for normal B cell function and tumor suppression, including MYC and IRF4. P300, a transcriptional coactivator highly enriched in multiple myeloma SEs, can be targeted to disrupt SE function and reduce MYC and IRF4 expression. Studies have shown that combining IMiDs with P300 inhibitors produces synergistic effects, leading to substantial downregulation of MYC and IRF4 [81]. This combination approach demonstrates a broader therapeutic window compared to single-agent treatments. Additionally, this research uncovered a resistance mechanism involving BATF, where multiple myeloma cells can maintain essential oncogene expression through various pathways using different BATF family proteins, a dynamic regulatory capability termed transcriptional plasticity.

Beyond resistance, the clinical translation of SE-targeted therapies faces additional hurdles. A key issue is achieving sufficient therapeutic specificity, as these agents can affect SEs crucial for normal cellular homeostasis, leading to on-target toxicities. Furthermore, the scarcity of robust and validated biomarkers to identify patient populations most likely to respond poses a significant challenge for clinical trial design and patient stratification.

From a regulatory standpoint, small molecule inhibitors targeting SE-associated proteins largely follow traditional drug development pathways. However, emerging strategies that employ gene-editing technologies to directly modulate SEs would likely encounter greater ethical scrutiny and navigate more complex regulatory landscapes.

In conclusion, SE-targeted therapeutics have moved from theoretical research to clinical trials. While these approaches have shown potential, future research must critically address the persistent challenges of drug resistance, target specificity, and the development of predictive biomarkers to fully realize their clinical utility.

Challenges and opportunities

This review summarizes recent advances in cancer subtyping based on SE features, with a particular focus on epigenetic modification signals and eRNA expression patterns. Studies have demonstrated that cancer subtypes driven by distinct SEs exhibit significant differences across multiple biological levels, including but not limited to regulatory networks, the tumor immune microenvironment, and clinical outcomes. These differences provide insights for identifying potential therapeutic targets, further advancing the development of precision oncology.

Inconsistent definitions and multi-omics integration challenges in super-enhancer-based subtyping

A primary challenge in the study of SEs is the lack of a consistent definition, which complicates their identification and comparative analysis across different samples and conditions. The widely used tool ROSE [5, 24], defines SEs based on the signal intensity of a single epigenetic mark, typically H3K27ac. This method ranks all enhancers within a sample and establishes a classification threshold at a geometric inflection point. However, because this threshold is sample-dependent, a genomic region identified as an SE in one context may be classified as a TE in another that has a higher overall signal or a different signal distribution. This reliance on a relative, sample-specific cutoff is a fundamental source of inconsistency.

The limitations of this approach extend beyond definitional ambiguity. The common use of a fixed stitching distance (e.g. 12.5 kb) to merge constituent enhancers is an empirical parameter that can lead to inaccurate demarcation of regulatory regions. Furthermore, by focusing on a single histone modification, this method overlooks other critical features of enhancer function, such as co-activator and master TF binding, eRNA transcription, and three-dimensional (3D) chromatin architecture. Consequently, the identified SEs may not always correspond to functionally significant regulatory elements.

This inconsistency undermines the reliability of differential SE analysis, potentially leading to a significant overestimation of disease-specific SEs. For instance, in a study on TNBC, researchers found that many regions identified as tumor-specific SEs also exhibited active signals in normal tissues; these signals simply failed to meet the stringent SE threshold within the normal samples [82]. This suggested that without cross-sample normalization, observed differences may be technical artifacts rather than true biological variations, thereby limiting the comparative value of public databases. The binary classification of enhancers as either SEs or TEs also masks the continuous spectrum of enhancer activity, where minor fluctuations in signal intensity can create the illusion of significant SE reprogramming.

To address these issues, several computational strategies have been proposed. A straightforward approach involves applying a uniform cross-sample threshold or normalizing signal intensities, though selecting an appropriate threshold remains an empirical challenge. For cancer studies, LILY [39] mitigates the biasing effects of CNV by integrating a correction step. However, a more fundamental solution lies in developing new analytical paradigms that move beyond relative thresholds. Models such as imPROSE [40] and DEEPSEN [41] integrate multi-dimensional data, including various epigenetic modifications, transcriptomic data, and DNA sequence features, to train more robust SE classifiers. Alternatively, models like EnsembleSE [45] leverage machine learning to predict SEs from DNA sequence alone, operating on the hypothesis that the potential for SE formation is at least partially encoded in the genome.

The increasing availability of high-dimensional, SE-related datasets has accelerated the development of these innovative computational methods. Unsupervised clustering and dimensionality reduction are now routinely used to identify patterns in SE activity, while network-based models can illuminate key TF hubs within SE-driven regulatory circuits. However, significant challenges remain. Integrating multi-omics data requires harmonizing datasets with different scales and noise characteristics, and sophisticated models carry the risk of overfitting to technical artifacts. Therefore, rigorous validation using independent cohorts or experimental perturbations is essential to confirm the biological relevance of computational findings. Establishing robust computational standards will be crucial for translating SE-based analyses into reliable and broadly applicable insights, particularly in fields like oncology. The evolution from heuristic, single-marker models to more rigorous approaches based on statistical modeling and multi-modal data integration marks a critical advance in the field.

Single-cell technologies unraveling super-enhancer heterogeneity

Single-cell sequencing enables high-resolution analysis of SEs, revealing tumor heterogeneity, and cell-specific regulation. Single-cell ATAC-seq (scATAC-seq) profiles chromatin accessibility at single-cell resolution, revealing SE usage patterns and regulatory divergence that are often obscured in bulk analyses. By clustering cells based on accessibility landscapes, scATAC-seq identifies co-activated enhancers, and cell-specific transcriptional networks. Complementary scRNA-seq links enhancer activity to gene expression, including SE-associated oncogenes and eRNAs. Tools like Enhlink [83] use scATAC-seq data to infer context-specific regulatory links, and have uncovered previously unrecognized neuron-specific SEs.

Single-cell epigenomic methods like scChIP-seq and scCUT&Tag further extend this resolution by profiling histone modifications in individual cells. For example, scCUT&Tag for H3K27ac, a histone mark of active enhancers, identifies SE-related chromatin patterns in heterogeneous cell populations [84]. These methods reveal cell-type-specific SE activity and help identify subclusters with unique enhancer profiles.

However, single-cell SE analysis faces challenges, including data sparsity, technical noise, and high dimensionality. The limited read depth per cell complicates reliable SE calling and enhancer–gene association. Integrative analyses require sophisticated computational methods, such as dimensionality reduction, filling in missing data, and aligning different data types through shared feature spaces or network-based methods. Despite these challenges, ongoing technological and analytical advances are enabling the generation of high-resolution SE maps at the single-cell level, providing new insights into regulatory heterogeneity in tumor progression and therapy resistance.

Future directions

Despite significant progress, critical questions remain regarding the role of SEs in cancer subtypes. A central issue is causality: whether SEs actively drive subtype or emerge as a consequence of established transcriptional programs. Addressing this requires functional studies, such as CRISPR interference or targeted degradation of SEs or their constituent enhancers, to determine whether disrupting a subtype-specific SE can reprogram tumor identity or trigger compensatory mechanisms. At the same time, efforts to translate SE-based subtyping into clinical practice are gaining attention. While showing clinical potential, its predictive value for treatment response and outcomes requires further validation.

A deeper understanding of SE function also depends on elucidating their 3D genomic context. SEs rely on spatial organization to form chromatin loops and interact with target promoters. Techniques such as Hi-C, Hi-ChIP, and single-cell Hi-C are crucial for mapping these structures and clarifying their regulatory roles. SE activity is also shaped by the tumor microenvironment, for example through cytokine expression that affects stromal or immune cells, or through external signals that trigger the formation of new SEs during tumor progression. Integrating SE data with spatial and single-cell transcriptomic analyses will be important for revealing these context-dependent regulatory dynamics.

Targeting SEs therapeutically holds promise, but remains technically challenging. Current strategies, such as BET and CDK7 inhibition, exploit SE-dependent transcriptional vulnerabilities in certain cancers. Future approaches may focus on targeting SE-specific TF complexes or use epigenome editing for precise silencing of oncogenic SEs. One major challenge is targeting oncogenic SEs without disrupting normal SEs, which requires a precise definition of subtype-specific SE activity. Progress in high-resolution epigenomic profiling and computational integration of multi-omics data will be essential for addressing these challenges and fully leveraging SE-based regulatory mechanisms in precision oncology.

Conclusion

This review demonstrated that using SE-related features for cancer subtyping offers a powerful framework for dissecting tumor heterogeneity. Analysis of SE landscapes and their associated features can define novel cancer subtypes with unique biological characteristics and prognostic value, often transcending the limits of traditional classifications. While standard analytical workflows now exist, their accuracy is challenged by a reliance on single epigenetic marks and bulk-tissue data that can obscure important signals. The path forward lies in leveraging emerging single-cell technologies and advanced computational methods to resolve these issues. These advancements are crucial for developing SE-targeted therapies and fully realizing the potential of this approach in advancing precision oncology.
