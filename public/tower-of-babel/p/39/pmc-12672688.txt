Age interaction in associations between geriatric nutritional risk index and prognosis in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Kei Fujita, Hikaru Tsukasaki, Shin Lee, Tetsuji Morishita, Eiju Negoro, Kana Oiwa, Takeshi Hara, Hisashi Tsurumi, Takanori Ueda, Takahiro Yamauchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate whether age modifies the association between the geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI) and overall survival (OS) in patients aged ≥ 18 years with newly diagnosed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), we conducted a multi-centre retrospective study of 552 patients. Multivariable Cox regression with restricted cubic spline (RCS) modelling showed that GNRI was significantly associated with OS, but the relationship was non-linear (P for non-linearity = 0.0158). A three-dimensional Cox-RCS model revealed that older age was associated with a higher mortality risk. The association between lower GNRI and worse OS was consistent across all age groups, including younger patients. Among patients with low GNRI (< 92), the most common pattern of malnutrition involved normal albumin (Alb) and body mass index (BMI). This pattern accounted for approximately 40% of low-GNRI patients, regardless of age group (based on thresholds of 65, 70, or 75 years). Survival curves demonstrated that a low GNRI (< 92) predicted poor prognosis, regardless of whether Alb or BMI was the main contributing factor. There was no evidence of interaction between age and GNRI on prognosis. Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, representing a clinically heterogeneous disease with diverse presentations [1–3]. The prognosis of DLBCL is determined by various host- and disease-related factors [4]. Nutritional status is a representative host-related prognostic factor but has mainly been investigated in elderly patients, other than in investigations using the International Prognostic Index (IPI) [5–9].

The Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) is a simple and effective nutritional indicator consisting of albumin concentration (Alb) and body mass index (BMI). GNRI was initially reported as a predictor of mortality risk among hospitalized elderly patients [10]. We have recently demonstrated the usefulness of GNRI in predicting overall survival (OS) for elderly patients ≥65 years old with DLBCL [6]. The impact of malnutrition appears to be more severe in older adults with DLBCL than in younger adults [11], but no reports have specifically examined the interactions of age with the prognostic potential of the GNRI [5].

We visually verified whether the prognostic ability of GNRI for de novo DLBCL in patients ≥18 years old is affected by age interaction using two- and three-dimensional (3D) Cox modelling with a restricted cubic spline (RCS). We also investigated the possibility that the composition of malnutrition, low GNRI, based on Alb and BMI, may differ between younger and older adults.

Methods

Study population

We conducted this observational, multicentre, retrospective analysis at three tertiary institutions in Japan: University of Fukui Hospital; the Japanese Red Cross Fukui Hospital; and Matsunami General Hospital. The medical records and oncology pharmacy records of consecutive patients diagnosed with de novo DLBCL during the period from 2006 to 2024 were reviewed. Lymphoma was diagnosed according to the World Health Organization classification [1, 12]. Patients with newly diagnosed de novo DLBCL who were ≥18 years old were included in this study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: central nervous system involvement; composite pathology consisting of DLBCL plus indolent lymphoma (transformed DLBCL); methotrexate-related DLBCL; post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder; patients with human immunodeficiency virus infection; cases with missing data; or patients treated with any therapy not intended to be curative. Treatments without curative intent are included monotherapy with etoposide, rituximab or steroid. Palliative radiotherapy or best supportive care were considered as interventions without curative intent.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all protocols were approved by the appropriate institutional review boards. The need to obtain written, informed consent was waived, since this study used retrospective data obtained from hospital records.

The clinical and demographic characteristics of patients were collected by retrospective medical chart review. Background baseline data including Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (PS), number of extranodal sites, IPI, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level, Ann Arbor stage, Alb, B symptoms, tumour diameter (with bulky mass defined as maximum diameter > 7.5 cm), and soluble interleukin-2 receptor [13], and BMI were extracted. Comorbidities at diagnosis were assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [6, 9, 14, 15]. The GNRI was assessed as a nutrition assessment scale [10]. To compare the prognostic impact of GNRI in older and younger populations, we set three age thresholds for older age: 65, 70, or 75 years.

Calculation of GNRI

GNRI at the diagnosis of lymphoma was calculated using body weight (BW) and albumin (Alb) according to the following formula: 14.89 × albumin (g/dL) + 41.7 × (BW/ideal BW), where ideal BW was defined as 22 × [height (m)]2. When BW exceeded ideal BW, BW/ideal BW was set as 1. All patients were categorized into four groups based on GNRI: no risk, GNRI > 98; mild risk, GNRI 92 to 98; moderate risk, GNRI 82 to < 92; and severe risk, GNRI < 82 [10].

Outcome measures

In this study, the primary outcome of interest was OS. We also evaluated prognostic factors thought to affect the OS of patients with DLBCL. OS was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to the last follow-up visit or date of death from any cause. Retrospective chart reviews were used to collect all event dates.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are shown as medians and ranges and were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable Cox hazard modelling with 3-knot RCS was used to assess the nonlinear relationship between GNRI and survival. To determine if there was an effect modification of age on the association between GNRI and prognosis, we created three different multivariable Cox-RCS analyses: (1) a model comparing the effect of GNRI on mortality risk in older and younger groups defined using the three age thresholds of 65, 70, or 75 years; (2) a model comparing the effect of age on mortality risk in high- and low-GNRI groups; and (3) a 3D-Cox-RCS model to simultaneously assess the relationship of age and GNRI to mortality risk. In this study, the following parameters used in the multivariable analysis were determined a priori based on previous findings: age; sex; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status; serum LDH; Ann Arbor stage; number of extranodal sites [16]; CCI [6, 9, 14, 15]; and GNRI [5, 6].

A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for all-cause mortality risk was plotted. The optimal cut-off for GNRI was identified from ROC analysis using Youden’s index. Because the optimal GNRI cut-off for predicting mortality risk obtained from ROC analysis closely approximated previously reported GNRI cut-offs [10], we use cut-off values from previous reports to divide GNRI into binary groups in subsequent analyses in this study. To clarify whether differences exist between older and younger adults in terms of the pattern of parameter abnormalities leading to a low GNRI, we created scatter plots and density plots for Alb and BMI. The log-rank test was used to compare survival curves in Kaplan–Meier analyses for each group according to the combination of GNRI as well as the Alb and BMI components. P-values were two-tailed, with values of P < 0.05 considered significant. For interaction analyses, values of P < 0.10 were considered significant. Data analysis was performed using R version 4.2.1 or EZR version 1.55 [17], which is a graphical user interface for R [18].

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 645 patients were diagnosed with DLBCL. Forty-seven patients with CNS involvement, five patients diagnosed with transformed DLBCL, three patients diagnosed with methotrexate-related DLBCL, 33 patients who received best supportive care only, and five patients with missing data were excluded. The remaining 552 patients were enrolled in the present study (Supplementary Fig. 1). Table 1 presents a comparison of patient backgrounds between groups of deceased and surviving patients. The median age of analysable patients was 74 years (range, 18–96 years) and 206 patients (37.3%) were deceased. Among the patients, a total of 271 patients (49.1%) were classified to the low-GNRI group, which showed moderate or severe risk. GNRI was significantly better in the surviving group compared to the deceased group. The younger group had significant better GNRI score than the older group.

Prognostic impact of GNRI

Median duration of follow-up was 29.3 months (range, 0.07–171.2 months), during which time 206 patients died (37.3%). Among these, 113 deaths (54.8%) were due to lymphoma, 23 (11.2%) due to treatment-related adverse events, 47 (22.8%) due to other diseases including cancers, and 23 (11.2%) due to unknown causes. A multivariable Cox hazard proportional model for OS revealed GNRI as an independent prognostic factor for mortality risk (hazard ratio [HR] 0.979, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.967–0.992, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

A multivariable Cox-RCS model for the relationship between GNRI and mortality risk demonstrated that GNRI correlated significantly with mortality risk (Supplementary Fig. 2), but the relationship was not linear (P for effect of GNRI = 0.002, P for non-linearity = 0.016). When GNRI exceeded approximately 92, mortality risk decreased in proportion to improved nutritional status. When GNRI fell below approximately 92, mortality risk plateaued. Although mortality risk was high below approximately 92, that risk remained constant and did not further deteriorate as GNRI declined.

Effect modification of age on association between GNRI and OS

Figure 1 shows a multivariable Cox-RCS model assessing the impact of GNRI on mortality risk in the older group ≥75 years old and the younger group < 75 years old. Age ≥ 75 years and low GNRI both significantly increased mortality risk, but no interaction was present (P for effect of GNRI = 0.016, P for non-linearity = 0.087, P for interaction = 0.762). Regardless of GNRI, the blue band representing younger patients was consistently below the yellow band representing older patients, indicating lower mortality risk for younger patients compared to older patients. Similar results were obtained regardless of whether older adults were defined as ≥ 65 or ≥ 70 years old (Supplementary Figs. 3a and 3b). Pattern of parameter abnormalities leading to low GNRI

According to Supplementary Table 3, the proportion of patients with low GNRI (< 92) was higher in older adults than in younger adults for all of the age thresholds of 65, 70, or 75 years. Supplementary Table 4 shows the patterns of abnormal values leading to low GNRI. No significant differences between older and younger patients were seen regarding causes of low GNRI (< 92), irrespective of age thresholds used (65, 70, or 75 years).

Supplementary Fig. 5 presents scatter plots illustrating the relationship between Alb and BMI in both older and younger patients. The distribution in scatter plots was almost the same for those ≥75 years old and those < 75 years old. Density plots were created to illustrate the distribution of Alb and BMI (Supplementary Fig. 6). Black line represents the distribution of parameters in the patients younger than 75 years and blue line represents the distribution of parameters in the older patients. The shapes of distribution curve of Alb levels and BMI are almost the same between older and younger patients. Results from scatter plots and density plots were comparable with age thresholds of 65 and 70 years (Supplementary Fig. 7 for age threshold of 65 years; Supplementary Fig. 8 for the age threshold of 70 years). Discussion

We found a significant correlation between prognosis of de novo DLBCL and GNRI across all age groups, with no evidence of interaction by age. Although mortality risk increased with age, the impact of malnutrition on survival was consistent regardless of age. While the prevalence of malnutrition rose with age, the components of low GNRI (< 92) were similar between younger and older individuals. This result held true across all tested age thresholds (65, 70, and 75 years). The underlying patterns leading to low GNRI also did not differ by age. Notably, the most common profile in both age groups was a low GNRI despite normal Alb and BMI. These results suggest that relying on Alb or BMI alone is insufficient, and GNRI should be actively calculated.

We created a 3D-RCS-Cox model to assess how both age and GNRI affect mortality risk in the same model. The results showed that mortality risk increased with age, but the negative prognostic impact of malnutrition was the same for all ages. The GNRI is a nutritional scale originally established for the elderly [10]. As a result, few reports have examined the prognostic utility of the GNRI for patients with hematopoietic malignancies across adult populations of any age, not restricted to the elderly [5, 19]. Those few studies, focusing on adult DLBCL patients or those undergoing second allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, did not compare findings between younger and older patients [5, 19]. The value of the present study is that we demonstrate, for the first time, that age does not interact with the prognostic ability of the GNRI. At any age, DLBCL patients suffer equally from the negative prognostic impact of a low GNRI.

Among elderly patients, fat-free mass tends to decrease preferentially due to metabolic changes associated with aging [11]. We had therefore predicted that the negative prognostic impact of malnutrition in DLBCL would be a more serious issue in the elderly population than younger population. However, the negative prognostic implication of a low GNRI was surprisingly the same for both older and younger patients in the present study. This was an unexpected result but a robust conclusion, since the same result was demonstrated with different cut-offs for binary comparisons by age. Hypoalbuminemia, an important factor in lowering GNRI, is also known to reflect inflammation [20]. Some types of malignancy present an inflammatory condition [21]. The presence of severe inflammation associated with lymphoma, as typified by B symptoms, at the diagnosis of DLBCL is well known [22, 23]. Further, this inflammation is also associated with poor prognosis of lymphoma [22, 23]. In DLBCL, lymphoma-induced weight loss and inflammation-mediated hypoalbuminemia are likely to appear according to the aggressive clinical course of the lymphoma itself. Hence, the GNRI (comprising Alb and BMI) is expected to be low at the onset of DLBCL. Given the high chemosensitivity of DLBCL, chemotherapy can dramatically improve the general condition and prognosis, even in elderly patients [24, 25]. The absence of variation in the prognostic impact of malnutrition between older and younger patients in this study may have been due to the improvement in nutritional status associated with improvement in lymphoma following therapeutic intervention.

In the present study, no difference was seen in the pattern of parameter abnormalities leading to a low GNRI (< 92) between younger and older adults. In general, causes of malnutrition vary with age [26]. In a study among hospitalized patients, the main triggers of malnutrition were multiple medications and the presence of malignant disease in younger patients, but multiple medications and living alone in older patients [27]. Malnutrition in DLBCL can be attributed to the lymphoma itself [28]. A randomised controlled trial mainly focusing on cancer patients, of whom 27% were lymphoma patients, showed that nutritional intervention by early dietary counselling during chemotherapy had no beneficial effect on mortality [29]. The authors of that previous study attributed this lack of effect to cancer cachexia anti-anabolism [29]. The potential for nutritional intervention to enhance treatment tolerability in cancer patients has been discussed [30]. There is little evidence in lymphoma [31], given the high chemotherapeutic sensitivity of DLBCL, that nutritional intervention may improve prognosis by increasing treatment tolerability. Research into potential prognostic impacts of nutritional intervention in DLBCL is therefore needed.

We revealed that the pattern of low GNRI despite normal Alb and normal BMI was the most common state of malnutrition in both young and older patients, at any of the various age thresholds of 65, 70, or 75 years. The results suggest a potential risk of malnutrition in DLBCL. According to survival curves, classification based on GNRI reflects prognosis more accurately than categories based solely on Alb or BMI. Patients with normal Alb and BMI but low GNRI (< 92) displayed poorer prognosis, similar to other patients with abnormal Alb and BMI and a low GNRI (< 92), compared to those with high GNRI (≥ 92). Among 271 patients evaluated as having low GNRI (< 92), 116 patients (42.8%) showed both normal Alb and normal BMI. These patients would not be detected using Alb or BMI screening alone. We therefore need to calculate GNRI in clinical practice rather than substituting evaluations based on Alb or BMI alone.

Although GNRI was originally developed for the elderly [10], our study is the first to demonstrate that its prognostic significance is consistent across both younger and older adult patients with DLBCL. This suggests that the clinical utility of GNRI should not be confined to elderly populations, and it may serve as a valuable tool for nutritional risk stratification and prognostication in adult patients of all ages. Furthermore, future studies could investigate whether GNRI also predicts treatment-related adverse events, especially in younger patients who are often overlooked in geriatric assessments.

There are several limitations in the present study. Because this analysis was conducted at a tertiary care institution, selection and healthcare access biases may have been present. This study utilised a retrospective design. Although a certain amount of information bias exists, patient information was collected from electronic medical records to minimise this bias as much as possible. We acknowledge that the absence of a statistically significant interaction between age and GNRI may be partly attributable to limited statistical power rather than a true absence of effect modification. Therefore, further validation using external cohorts with larger sample sizes is warranted to confirm whether the association between GNRI and prognosis is consistent across age groups. As GNRI is calculated using only Alb and body weight [10], it may not capture other important domains of nutritional status, such as muscle mass, inflammatory markers, or dietary intake. Interpretation of the results of this study should take into account the influence of potential mechanisms such as sarcopenia [32], inflammatory conditions, and metabolic abnormalities [33]. This study assessed the patterns of parameter abnormality that led to low GNRI but did not analyse specific causes of malnutrition in DLBCL patients. Whether interventions to address the nutritional status of patients defined as undernourished are feasible thus remained unknown in this study.

In conclusion, the negative impact of low GNRI on prognosis in DLBCL was consistently observed across all age groups, with no significant difference between younger and older adults. Malnutrition was equally associated with poor prognosis at any age. Low GNRI despite normal ALB levels and BMI was the most frequent pattern of undernutrition among both younger and older adults. The calculation of GNRI in daily practice is recommended, since this aids in stratifying high-risk DLBCL patients who may not be identified through screening with either Alb or BMI alone, thus potentially improving prognostic assessment and clinical management.
