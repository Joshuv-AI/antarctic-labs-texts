Spatiotemporal trends and geographic disparities in spatial accessibility to maternal and child health services in Nanning, China: impact of two-child policies
Authors: Qinglian Qin, Bin Xu, Xinxuan Hu, Xue Wei, Yulu Su, Xianyan Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina’s family planning policies have experienced stages of one-child policy, partial two-child policy, and universal two-child policy. However, the impact of these policy shifts on the spatial accessibility to maternal and child health (MCH) services for women and children remains uncertain. This study aimed to evaluate the spatiotemporal trends and geographic disparities in spatial accessibility to MCH services in the context of two-child polices.MethodsThis study was conducted in Nanning prefecture, China, from 2013 to 2019. Data on the transportation networks, MCH institutes, the annual number of newborns, and the annual number of pregnant women in Nanning prefecture were collected. Gaussian two-step floating catchment area (Ga2SFCA) method was employed to measure the spatial accessibility to MCH services at county, township, and village levels. Temporal trends in spatial accessibility were analyzed using Joinpoint regression analysis. Geographic disparities in spatial accessibility were identified using geographic information system (GIS) mapping techniques.ResultsOverall, the spatial accessibility to MCH services showed an upward trend from 2013 to 2019 at county, town, and village levels, with the average annual percent change (AAPC) being 5.04, 4.73, and 5.39, respectively. Specifically, the spatial accessibility experienced a slight downward trend during the period of partial two-child policy for both parents only children (i.e., 2013–2014), a slight upward trend during the period of partial two-child policy for either parent only child (i.e., 2014–2016) and the early stages of universal two-child policy (i.e., 2016–2018), and a large upward trend in the later stages of universal two-child policy (i.e., 2018–2019). Spatial accessibility to MCH services gradually decreased from central urban areas to surrounding rural areas. Regions with low spatial accessibility were predominantly located in remote rural areas.ConclusionWith the gradual opening of the two-child policies, the spatial accessibility to MCH services for women and children has generally improved. However, significant geographic disparities have persisted throughout the stages of the two-child policies. Comprehensive measures should be considered to improve equity in MCH services for women and children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11415-z.Keywords: Maternal and child health services, Two-child policies, Spatial accessibility, Spatiotemporal trends, Geographic disparities

Introduction

China’s family planning policies have evolved from the one-child policy to the two-child policy, and to the three-child policy. Initially, the one-child policy was implemented to control population growth [1]. In 2011, this policy was adjusted to a partial two-child policy, allowing couples to have two children if both parents were born as the only child. In November 2013, the policy was further relaxed to allow couples to have two children if either parent was an only child [2]. In response to an aging population, China approved and formally implemented a universal two-child policy in January 2016 to further encourage and empower all couples to have two children [3]. This policy shift has led to a surge in cumulative fertility demand, potentially peaking birth rates in the short term [4]. Concurrently, the number of pregnant women with advanced maternal age and high-risk pregnancy has been increasing [5, 6]. Therefore, the demand for maternal and child health (MCH) services for women and children may increase, underscoring the need for an optimized allocation of health resources. The gradual opening of two-child policies might affect access to MCH services if the allocation of MCH resources does not keep pace with the adjustments in family planning policies.

Ensuring equitable access to healthcare services remains a critical policy challenge both in high and low resource countries. Previous research has demonstrated that access to healthcare services is a vital component affecting health and should not be overlooked [7, 8]. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) aim to achieve Universal Health Coverage (UHC) by 2030, with the goal of ensuring that all people and communities worldwide have access to healthcare services without facing financial difficulties [9]. Meeting this goal necessitates a strategic and equitable distribution of medical resources that aligns with the dynamics of supply and demand. Particularly vulnerable populations, like women and children, require more accessible medical services. Maternal and child health-related indicators are integral to the United Nations Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), reflecting a shared global focus and commitment to improving MCH outcomes [10].

Accessibility to healthcare services can be classified into spatial accessibility and nonspatial accessibility [11]. Spatial accessibility emphasizes the spatial distribution of healthcare services, whereas nonspatial accessibility focuses on the actual use of healthcare services in a given location. Spatial accessibility emphasizes the geographical barriers (e.g., travel distance or travel time) between healthcare providers (i.e., supply) and demanders (i.e., demand) [12] [13]. In this study, we focus on spatial accessibility, a critical measure that can reflect whether women and children have equitable access to MCH services from a spatial perspective. Identifying areas with limited spatial accessibility could help planners understand and optimize the geographical distribution of MCH resources, thereby enhancing the health status of women and children.

To date, China’s family planning policies have experienced stages of one-child policy, partial two-child policy, and universal two-child policy. However, there is a scarcity of studies examining the impact of the two-child polices on the spatial accessibility to MCH services. It is unclear whether the implementation of two-child policies shrinks the spatial accessibility to MCH services, and leads to inequity in spatial accessibility. To address above issues, we conducted this study in Nanning prefecture, China. We aimed to measure the spatial accessibility to MCH services for women and children at multiple spatial scales, and reveal spatiotemporal trends and geographic disparities in spatial accessibility. Our findings will provide evidence to guide the planning of maternal care resources and contribute to enhance the quality and equity of MCH.

Methods

Study setting

The study’s time frame extends from 2013 to 2019, a period that includes the critical phase of the implementation of China’s two-child policy. This period can be divided into partial two-child policy for both parents only children (i.e., 2013–2014), partial two-child policy for either parent only child (i.e., 2014–2016), and universal two-child policy (i.e., 2016–2019). The study was conducted in Nanning prefecture, the capital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, situated in Southwest China (Fig. 1). In 2019, Nanning prefecture’s healthcare infrastructure included 216 MCH institutes, which comprised 46 hospitals, 9 MCH centers, 44 community health service centers, and 117 township health centers (Additional File 1).

Data collection and pre-processing

Data on MCH institutes from 2013 to 2019 were collected from Nanning Information Center for Health and Family Planning Propaganda. This data mainly included names and addresses of MCH institutes, the number of obstetricians, the number of obstetric beds, the number of pediatricians, and the number of pediatric beds. Geocoding is the process where the physical addresses of MCH institutes are converted into geographical coordinates in the format of longitude and latitude. We geocoded addresses of MCH institutes utilizing Baidu Maps Geocoding Application Programming Interface (API). Population data for Nanning prefecture from 2013 to 2019 were obtained from Guangxi Bureau of Health Statistics. We extracted data on the number of pregnant women and the number of newborns for each county, town, and village.

In this study, travel time was chosen as the metric for distance decay instead of raw distance, as it’s a more realistic measure of spatial accessibility. The shortest travel times from residents’ locations to MCH institutes were calculated using the Origin-Destination (O-D) cost matrix function of the ArcGIS Network Analyst Extension. Data on the vector map and transportation network in Nanning prefecture were sourced from the Resource and Environment Science and Data Center of Chinese Academy of Sciences (http://www.resdc.cn/). We established a vector layer for the road networks, which includes national roads, provincial roads, expressways, subway lines, first-grade roads, second-grade roads, pedestrian roads, and other types (Additional File 1). Utilizing the established road network data, we calculated the travel times between supply and demand points.

Definition of relevant variables

MCH institutes include specialized MCH hospitals, general hospitals equipped with obstetrics and pediatrics departments, community healthcare centers that provide obstetric and pediatric services, and township hospitals with similar departments. Clinical doctors providing MCH services include obstetricians and pediatricians. An obstetrician is a physician with a medical practitioner or assistant physician certificate, specializing in the clinical services of patients within the obstetric department. Similarly, a pediatrician is a physician with the same qualifications, focusing on the clinical care of pediatric patients. Clinical beds for MCH services include obstetrics beds and pediatric beds. In theory, the supply capacity of MCH institutes should be evaluated using multiple integrated indicators [14]. Based on expert consultation and literature review, we developed a comprehensive indicator by assigning a weight of 67.27% for healthcare professionals and a weight of 32.73% for MCH beds [15].

Measurement of spatial accessibility to MCH services

Various methods have been employed to measure the spatial accessibility to healthcare services, including the provider-to-population ratio, the nearest distance method, gravity model, two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) method, and modified 2SFCA [16–18]. Given that residents are more likely to seek medical care close to their residences, capturing this tendency is crucial for measuring spatial accessibility. The Gaussian two-step floating catchment area (Ga2SFCA) method effectively captures this behavior by incorporating a Gaussian function, which plots an S-shaped curve. This curve shows that the rate of accessibility reduction accelerates at first and then decelerates as the distance from healthcare facilities increases [19, 20]. Joinpoint regression analysis to reveal temporal trends of spatial accessibility

Temporal trends in spatial accessibility were dissected into meaningful segments using the joinpoint regression analysis model [21]. A statistically significant trend is indicated when the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the annual percentage change (APC) or average annual percentage change (AAPC) does not include zero. Specifically, if the lower bound of the 95% CI exceeds zero, it suggests a significant upward trend for the specified interval. Conversely, if the upper bound of the 95% CI is below zero, it indicates a significant downward trend.

Multi-scale GIS mapping approaches to identify geographic disparities in spatial accessibility

Geographic disparities in spatial accessibility to MCH services were identified through geographic information system (GIS) mapping techniques. Spatial accessibility scores were categorized into quartiles, employing the minimum (Min), first quartile (Q1), median (Q2), third quartile (Q3), and maximum (Max) values. This categorization segmented spatial accessibility into four equal segments: Min to Q1, Q1 to Q2, Q2 to Q3, and Q3 to Max. Areas with low accessibility refer to spatial accessibility scores below Q1, while areas with high accessibility are defined by spatial accessibility above Q3.

Statistical analysis

The spatial accessibility was measured using Ga2SFCA approach in ArcGIS software (version 10.5, ESRI Inc., Redlands, USA). The temporal trends of spatial accessibility from 2013 to 2019 were revealed by the Joinpoint regression software (version 4.8.0.1, National Cancer Institute, Rockville, MD, USA). The geographic disparities in spatial accessibility to MCH services were visualized via multi-scale GIS mapping approaches in ArcGIS software. All the analyses were conducted at the county, town, and village levels. P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Temporal trends of spatial accessibility to MCH services

Overall, the average travel times from residential points to all MCH institutes were 28.33 min at county level, 30.89 min at town level, and 36.35 min at village level. In 2019, the median spatial accessibility scores peaked at 29.37 for the county level, 27.69 for the town level, and 28.64 for the village level, respectively (Table 1).

Statistical description of spatial accessibility scores

From 2013 to 2019, the spatial accessibility to MCH services exhibited an upward trend at levels of county, town, and village, with the AAPC of 5.04, 4.73, and 5.39, respectively (Table 1, Fig. 2). Specifically, during the period of partial two-child policy for both parents only children (i.e., 2013–2014), a slight downward trend was observed at levels of county (APC = -2.96, 95% CI: -4.52 to -0.56), town (APC = -4.93, 95% CI: -7.85 to -0.12), and village (APC = -0.56, 95% CI: -5.38 to 1.77) (Fig. 2). In contrast, during the period of partial two-child policy either parent only child (i.e., 2014–2016) and early stage of universal two-child policy (i.e., 2016–2018), there was a slight upward trend in spatial accessibility at the county, town, and village levels, with APC of 2.79 (95% CI: 2.31 to 3.52), 3.25 (95% CI: 2.38 to 4.84), and 4.20 (95% CI: 2.39 to 6.74), respectively (Fig. 2). During the continuous period of universal two-child policy (i.e., 2018–2019), we found a large upward at levels of county, town and village, with APC of 23.97 (95% CI: 21.29 to 26.00), 22.14 (95% CI: 17.56 to 26.01), and 22.50 (95% CI: 15.93 to 27.95), respectively (Fig. 2).

Geographic disparities in spatial accessibility at county level

At county level, regions with high spatial accessibility (spatial accessibility score, A > 27.31) were located in central urban areas of Liangqing district and Xixiangtang district (Fig. 3, Additional File 1). Conversely, regions with low spatial accessibility (A < 17.70) were mainly clustered in surrounding rural areas of Shanglin county and Yongning district (Fig. 3, Additional File 1). With the implementation of two-child policies, the spatial accessibility to MCH services for women and children has gradually improved. The number of regions with high spatial accessibility increased from 2 counties in 2013 to 6 counties in 2019. In contrast, the number of regions with low spatial accessibility decreased from 5 counties in 2013 to 1 county in 2019 (Fig. 3).

Geographic disparities in spatial accessibility at town level

At town level, significant geographic disparities in spatial accessibility to MCH services were evident. Spatial accessibility to MCH services gradually declined from central urban areas to surrounding rural areas (Fig. 4). Overall, regions with high spatial accessibility (A > 26.72) were predominantly located in central urban areas, such as Xinzhu street, Jianzheng street, Minsheng street, Nanhu street, and Jintou street (Fig. 4, Additional File 1). In contrast, regions characterized by low spatial accessibility (A < 16.28) were mainly clustered in surrounding rural areas, such as Chengxiang town, Jinchai town, Tanghong town, Shitang town, and Dujie town (Fig. 4, Additional File 1). After the implementation of universal two-child policy, the number of regions with high spatial accessibility scores increased from 24 towns in 2016 to 74 towns in 2019.

Geographic disparities in spatial accessibility at village level

At village level, significant geographic disparities in spatial accessibility were consistently observed throughout the implementation stages of two-child policies (Fig. 5). Notably, villages with high spatial accessibility (A > 25.80) were located near central urban areas, mainly including Sanjun village in Zhonghua town, Pingdong village in Wuxu town, Pingdan village in Wuxu town, and Tuandong village in Datang town (Fig. 5). A total of 21 villages characterized by low spatial accessibility (A = 0.00) were mainly clustered in surrounding rural areas, such as Hezhuang village in Gantang town, Pinglang village in Nanxiao town, and Xingyang community in Chengxiang town (Fig. 5). With the implementation of two-child policies, the number of regions with high spatial accessibility increased from 229 villages in 2013 to 935 villages in 2019 (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Principal findings

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the spatiotemporal trends and geographic disparities in the spatial accessibility to MCH services for women and children in the context of China’s two-child policy. Our findings revealed that spatial accessibility to MCH services exhibited an upward trend from 2013 to 2019 at levels of county, town, and village, with the AAPC being 5.04, 4.73, and 5.39, respectively. There has been a general improvement in spatial accessibility to MCH services as the two-child policy has been gradually relaxed. However, significant geographic disparities in spatial accessibility persisted throughout the study period, with spatial accessibility to MCH services observed to decline progressively from central urban areas to surrounding rural areas.

China’s two-child policy experienced a gradual opening, initially permitting couples where both parents were only children to have a second child, then relaxing the rule to include couples with one only child, and ultimately fully implementing the universal two-child policy in 2016, allowing all couples to have two children [22]. Our study showed that an overall enhancement in spatial accessibility to MCH services from 2013 to 2019, despite a modest decline during the period when the partial two-child policy was implemented for couples where both parents were only children (i.e., 2013–2014). The policy shift exerts a direct influence on fertility rates and subsequently transforms the demographic age structure [23, 24]. Previous studies have reported a significant increase in the percentage of advanced maternal age and in the number of births during the early stages of two-child policy [25, 26]. The precipitous surge in births and the number of pregnant women posed a potential strain on healthcare infrastructure and personnel in the short term, and MCH service resources have been challenged to the growing demand within a short time frame. With the further relaxation of the two-child policy, the government has implemented strategic initiatives, including the expansion of MCH institutes, improvements in the quality of medical services, and more efficient resource allocation, all aimed at easing the pressures on the MCH system [27, 28]. Concurrently, the fertility intention of residents was gradually decreasing under the influence of systematic factors such as huge economic pressure, changes in fertility concepts, and changes in family structure, thus the number of newborns decreased dramatically [29, 30]. Consequently, the spatial accessibility to access MCH services has generally improved.

Equitable access to MCH services remains a significant challenge worldwide. The allocation and fairness of MCH resources directly affect women and children access to MCH services. Our study revealed significant geographic disparities in the spatial accessibility to MCH services during the implementation of two-child policies. Notably, at village level, there were 21 villages where spatial accessibility was zero. Our findings are also consistent with prior research that vulnerable and remote populations often face barriers in accessing maternal health facilities [31–33]. The geographic disparities in the spatial accessibility to MCH services significantly impact the health outcomes of women and children, exacerbating health inequalities between urban and rural areas. Previous studies showed that the allocation of MCH resources was related to maternal mortality, preterm birth incidence, and low birth weight incidence [34, 35]. Therefore, addressing these gaps is of paramount importance for improving public health. This point is also emphasized in the United Nations’ final report on the MDGs in 2015, which notes that these goals have not yet been fully achieved and that inequalities persist [8].

Overall, the spatial accessibility to MCH services in Nanning prefecture gradually decreased from central urban areas to surrounding rural areas. The reasons underlying this spatial pattern were multifaceted. First, the central urban areas benefited from robust economic development, accessible transportation, and a well-concentrated distribution of MCH institutes. In contrast, the surrounding rural areas were marked by underdeveloped economies, inconvenient transportation, and insufficient allocation of MCH resources [36]. The sparse distribution of the limited MCH resources in these rural areas would exacerbate the barriers to accessing MCH services for women and children within a certain travel time. Second, the inferior health investment, remuneration, and professional development opportunities in remote rural areas were inferior to the central urban areas, resulting in brain draining of healthcare professionals [37]. Other studies have also found that areas with superior economic development and convenient transportation tend to exhibit higher spatial accessibility [38, 39]. This illustrates that economic development is a priority in attracting healthcare professionals and enhancing the quality of MCH services. In essence, achieving balanced economic development is of great significance for improving equity in the utilization of MCH services.

Policy implications

Within the framework of the SDGs, Goal 3.8 specifically emphasizes the importance of achieving universal health coverage. However, geographic disparities in spatial accessibility to MCH services pose a challenge to the realization of this goal. The inequality in MCH services significantly impedes progress towards Goals 3.1 and 3.2, which are aimed at reducing global maternal mortality, neonatal mortality, and mortality rates of children under the age of five. Therefore, to overcome these obstacles and achieve the SDGs, it is crucial to focus on rural and underdeveloped areas, ensuring that women and children have equal access to MCH services. First, to promote a more equitable distribution of MCH resources, it is imperative to integrate considerations of population density, geographical context, and travel times. Second, establish efficient transportation systems to minimize travel time and expenses for women and children accessing MCH services. Third, it is essential to create incentives for healthcare professionals to work in rural areas, such as offering higher salaries, better housing, and enhanced professional development opportunities. Moreover, this study holds profound implications for public policy in the context of China’s three-child policy. In May 2021, China announced the three-child policy allowing all couples to have up to three children [40]. To effectively promote the smooth implementation of three-child policy and achieve the anticipated demographic and social effects, it is necessary for the government and relevant institutions to further increase financial investment and optimize the MCH system.

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, we calculated the travel times based on a fixed road network, without considering the dynamic changes in traffic networks and actual road conditions, such as traffic congestion and waiting times at traffic lights. This approach could result in estimates that differ from real-world travel times. Second, this study did not take into account resident attributes, including age, gender, and income, which may have a significant impact on the selection of MCH services. Third, we did not conduct a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the uncertainties and limitations inherent in the empirical geospatial analysis of spatial accessibility in different parts of the study area. Lastly, although we evaluated the spatial accessibility to MCH services across multiple spatial scales, the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP), a limitation inherent in spatially aggregated data, remains unaddressed.

Conclusion

With the gradual loosening of the two-child policy, there has been a general improvement in the spatial accessibility to MCH services for women and children. However, significant geographic disparities were evident throughout the implementation of two-child polices, with spatial accessibility decreasing from central urban areas to surrounding rural areas. Geographic disparities in spatial accessibility to MCH services pose a challenge to the realization of SDGs. To address these disparities, it is essential to bridge the gap between rural and urban areas and to ensure the rational allocation and optimization of MCH resources.
