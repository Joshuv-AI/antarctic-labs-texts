Quantitative liver ultrasound: current status and challenges
Authors: Yunlin Huang, Yi Yu, Ying Wang, Yijie Qiu, Rui Cheng, Li Wei, Yi Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUltrasound is the most widely used imaging modality for liver evaluation because it is accessible, portable, affordable, and repeatable. Quantitative liver ultrasound should therefore be understood not as a single technology, but as a set of biomarker strategies at different stages of development. Ultrasound elastography is the most mature component of this field and has been incorporated into noninvasive pathways for fibrosis assessment. Ultrasound-based liver fat quantification is also advancing rapidly through attenuation, backscatter, and radiofrequency-based techniques. A newer area of investigation involves the evaluation of viscoelasticity and inflammatory activity using shear-wave dispersion and related biomarkers, particularly in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. The future value of quantitative liver ultrasound will depend on integrating multiple parameters to support clinical triage, monitoring, and, increasingly, treatment decision-making.Keywords: Quantitative ultrasound, Elastography, Attenuation coefficient, Backscatter coefficient, Shear-wave dispersion, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease

Introduction

For decades, conventional B-mode ultrasound (BMUS) has been valued for its availability and usefulness as a first-line imaging tool. However, the dominant clinical questions have changed. In diffuse liver disease, particularly metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), clinicians no longer need only to determine whether liver morphology appears abnormal [1]. Liver ultrasound is entering a biomarker era in which it is increasingly expected to assess fibrosis extent, fat accumulation, ongoing inflammatory activity, the development of portal hypertension, and disease progression or regression over time. This shift has exposed a fundamental limitation of BMUS: it is widely deployable but only partly quantitative [2–4].

This tension between accessibility and precision highlights the importance of quantitative liver ultrasound, which generates numerical biomarkers from acoustic properties rather than relying solely on subjective image patterns. These techniques aim to make ultrasound a scalable platform for tissue characterization, positioned between routine ultrasound and more costly methods such as magnetic resonance imaging proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) and magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) [3–6].

However, the field is not advancing as a single cohesive entity; its components are maturing at different rates. Elastography has already achieved broad clinical relevance for fibrosis assessment [4,7]. Ultrasound-based fat quantification is progressing rapidly and becoming more precise as MRI-PDFF is increasingly used as the noninvasive reference standard [8]. Biomarkers targeting inflammation and viscoelasticity are promising but remain in the early stages of validation [9,10]. Consequently, quantitative liver ultrasound has made substantial but uneven progress [3,4].

This review examines quantitative liver ultrasound from a clinical perspective. Rather than simply cataloguing technologies, it evaluates the maturity of different components of the field, identifies areas requiring improvement, describes emerging domains, and considers barriers to broader clinical implementation. The central thesis is that quantitative liver ultrasound should no longer be judged solely by technical ingenuity. Its future prospects will depend on whether it can provide robust, reproducible, and clinically interpretable measurements at scale.

Why Quantitative Liver Ultrasound, and Why Now?

Three developments have converged to make quantitative liver ultrasound particularly urgent. First, MASLD has transformed diffuse liver disease from a specialized hepatology concern into a widespread public health issue. Current guidance emphasizes case-finding and noninvasive assessment in people with cardiometabolic risk factors, especially those with obesity or type 2 diabetes [11]. Therefore, an inexpensive, repeatable, and widely available tool has clear strategic value if it can produce trustworthy biomarkers.

Second, the reference-standard landscape has changed. For steatosis assessment, MRI-PDFF has become the dominant noninvasive benchmark because it samples the liver more comprehensively and provides a continuous quantitative estimate of fat fraction [12]. At the same time, fibrosis evaluation has shifted toward combinations of serum-based scores and elastography rather than routine reliance on biopsy alone [2,3,11,13]. These developments have raised the evidentiary standard for ultrasound technologies. It is no longer sufficient for a method to correlate only with visual ultrasound findings or a small biopsy series; it must also demonstrate performance against modern quantitative comparators and fit into real-world clinical pathways.

Third, liver care is becoming longitudinal. The value of imaging increasingly lies not only in diagnosis, but also in repeated monitoring during lifestyle intervention, pharmacologic therapy, surveillance of advanced disease, and risk re-stratification over time. This shift places particular pressure on repeatability and inter-platform consistency [14]. A biomarker that performs well in cross-sectional studies but drifts substantially between visits or across vendors has limited value in routine care [15]. Quantitative liver ultrasound is therefore being asked to solve a more demanding problem than traditional ultrasound: not merely depiction, but dependable measurement.

Elastography: An Established Platform, But Not a Finished Story

Ultrasound elastography is the most developed quantitative liver ultrasound method. Techniques such as vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE), point shear-wave elastography, and two-dimensional shear-wave elastography are now widely used for noninvasive evaluation of liver fibrosis (Table 1, Fig. 1). Major guidance documents from the World Federation for Ultrasound in Medicine and Biology and the European Federation of Societies for Ultrasound in Medicine and Biology emphasize the role of elastography in diagnosis, risk stratification, and reduction of unnecessary biopsies, while also clarifying interpretation frameworks and reliability considerations [2,4,7,13]. Elastography has demonstrated the potential of quantitative liver ultrasound when physical measurement, biological rationale, and clinical need are aligned.

Ultrasound elastography shifted liver assessment from morphology alone toward evaluation of mechanical properties. This change was clinically important because fibrosis has a strong relationship with stiffness, enabling the establishment of clinically useful thresholds [16]. In practice, liver stiffness measured by ultrasound elastography is now often interpreted alongside serum-based fibrosis scores rather than in isolation, and this multimodal approach has become one of the most credible models for noninvasive liver assessment [17].

However, liver stiffness is not synonymous with fibrosis. Inflammation, cholestasis, venous congestion, food intake, technical acquisition factors, and disease etiology can all affect measurements [3,4,13,16,18]. Numerical outputs do not eliminate biological variability. Although elastography provides useful information, it cannot be regarded as a direct surrogate for histological evaluation [19,20]. This limitation should be acknowledged when newer quantitative ultrasound biomarkers are interpreted, especially when they are presented as “objective.”

Longitudinal robustness remains incompletely understood. Studies of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease indicate that liver stiffness measured by shear-wave elastography and VCTE has acceptable repeatability, although measurement variability remains relevant in clinical practice and should be considered during longitudinal assessment [15,21]. This issue is especially important when expected biomarker changes are small, as in early intervention studies or routine follow-up [22]. In this context, elastography is best regarded as an established technique with well-recognized limitations.

Steatosis Quantification: The Fastest-Moving Domain

Steatosis quantification is evolving rapidly. BMUS can suggest hepatic steatosis, particularly when fat accumulation is moderate or severe, but its sensitivity for mild steatosis is limited, and visual grading remains inherently subjective [8,23]. This limitation is increasingly problematic in settings where steatosis is prevalent, treatment effects are often modest, and imaging is expected to support quantitative longitudinal assessment.

The controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) was an early technique for quantifying liver fat using ultrasound signal attenuation rather than visual assessment. Its contribution was to demonstrate that steatosis could be estimated quantitatively in a clinically scalable manner. However, because CAP is not BMUS image-guided, its diagnostic performance has not resolved remaining uncertainties in ultrasound-based fat quantification [3,8,24]. This limitation has become more apparent with the introduction of newer attenuation coefficient (AC) algorithms implemented in ultrasound imaging systems for hepatic steatosis evaluation.

AC algorithms allow the region of interest (ROI) to be placed under direct BMUS visualization, addressing a key limitation of CAP and aligning measurements more closely with the standard sonographic workflow (Table 1). These techniques—including attenuation imaging (ATI), attenuation coefficient (ATT), tissue attenuation imaging (TAI), ultrasound-guided attenuation parameter (UGAP), and ultrasound attenuation imaging technology (Fig. 2)—have shown promising diagnostic performance, especially in patients with MASLD and type 2 diabetes [3,25–30]. A recent study of patients with MASLD showed that AC measured using UGAP correlated strongly with MRI-PDFF (r=0.77) and demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance for detecting and grading hepatic steatosis, with areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs) ranging from 0.88 to 0.91 [31]. In a prospective study of patients with biopsy-proven MASLD, AC measured using ATI correlated well with steatosis grade (r=0.72) and demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance for steatotic liver disease, with AUCs ranging from 0.77 to 0.97 [28]. AC measured using TAI also correlated significantly with steatosis grade. For diagnosing hepatic steatosis ≥S1, ≥S2, and S3, the AUCs for TAI were 0.844, 0.914, and 0.909, respectively. Steatosis grade was an independent determinant of AC measured using TAI (P<0.001) [32]. Together, these findings suggest that AC algorithm-based techniques may offer practical advantages for ultrasound-based steatosis assessment.

At the same time, attenuation-based techniques are not the only direction of development. Technical standardization efforts from the AIUM (American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine)–RSNA (Radiological Society of North America) Quantitative Imaging Biomarkers Alliance have emphasized the potential role of backscatter-based techniques and broader pulse-echo quantitative ultrasound frameworks [25,33,34]. These approaches extend beyond a single physical parameter and may allow more comprehensive characterization of tissue acoustic properties. In this context, liver fat assessment may ultimately rely on combinations of features rather than a single attenuation-derived measure. This possibility is reflected in growing interest in integrated biomarkers that combine AC and backscatter coefficient (BSC), such as ultrasound-derived fat fraction (UDFF) and ultrasound-derived estimated fat fraction (USFF) (Fig. 3). USFF is estimated using a two-dimensional convolutional neural network that incorporates BMUS images and quantitative ultrasound parametric maps, including TAI and tissue scatter-distribution imaging. In patients with MASLD, USFF correlated strongly with MRI-PDFF (r=0.86) and achieved an AUC of 0.97 for diagnosing hepatic steatosis, defined as MRI-PDFF ≥5%, significantly outperforming individual quantitative ultrasound parameters and visual BMUS grading. At a cutoff of 5.7%, USFF demonstrated 90% sensitivity and 91% specificity [35]. These findings indicate that USFF can provide accurate ultrasound-based liver fat quantification. UDFF, which is based on AC and BSC, is intended to provide a continuous estimate of hepatic fat content and has shown good agreement with MRI-PDFF in prospective studies [36–39]. Favorable intraobserver and interobserver reproducibility has also been reported, suggesting that radiofrequency-based approaches may be suitable for longitudinal monitoring after further standardization [36,40–43]. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because variability persists, and agreement does not necessarily indicate interchangeability across scanners, software implementations, or clinical settings [44,45].

Recent studies also indicate that fat quantification may be influenced by non-fat tissue characteristics. Advanced fibrosis was associated with overestimation of steatosis by quantitative ultrasound in individuals without true hepatic fat accumulation [46]. This observation underscores that acoustic biomarkers are affected by multiple tissue properties. In addition, acquisition factors such as ROI depth, ROI size, and threshold settings can influence attenuation-based measurements [36,37,47]. Further progress in ultrasound-based steatosis quantification will therefore depend not only on technical development, but also on more consistent acquisition practices and clearer quality standards [3].

Beyond Fibrosis and Fat Content: Inflammation, Viscosity, and Viscoelasticity

A major frontier for quantitative liver ultrasound is the assessment of inflammatory activity. This is particularly relevant in MASLD, where steatosis alone does not define prognosis and the distinction between fat accumulation and steatohepatitis remains clinically important [11]. Liver biopsy remains the gold standard for assessing inflammation, but it is invasive and unsuitable for large-scale population monitoring. Ultrasound is therefore of interest because even partial noninvasive assessment of inflammatory activity could address an important gap in current liver imaging.

The iLEAD study advanced this area by examining associations between shear-wave dispersion (SWD) (Fig. 4), related viscosity-oriented parameters, and inflammation in MASLD [48]. This approach may help distinguish inflammatory change from fixed fibrosis more effectively than traditional elastography techniques [48,49].

Although these findings are promising, they do not resolve the issue. The biological specificity of SWD remains incomplete, and emerging evidence suggests that fibrosis burden itself may influence the performance of inflammation-focused biomarkers [48,49]. In other words, the tissue complexity that makes SWD attractive also complicates its interpretation. Viscoelastic biomarkers should therefore not be overstated as direct inflammation sensors. At present, they are best viewed as informative but composite signals.

Reproducibility remains another significant challenge. Compared with stiffness and some fat biomarkers, dispersion-based measurements have not been standardized as consistently. Differences in implementation, ROI placement, acquisition settings, and vendor-specific algorithms can substantially affect results. In addition, SWD imaging is currently available from only a limited number of ultrasound vendors, which further restricts broader clinical implementation. This limitation does not diminish the importance of this research, but it does define its current stage. Inflammation-focused quantitative ultrasound is one of the most conceptually compelling areas in liver imaging, but it remains at an earlier phase of validation than elastography or steatosis quantification.

Multiparametric Liver Ultrasound: From Isolated Measurements to Integrated Phenotyping

A major recent shift has been the movement from isolated measurements toward multiparametric ultrasound. Liver disease is not one-dimensional: fibrosis, steatosis, inflammation, and changes in blood flow often coexist, progress at different rates, and influence how each process appears on imaging. Therefore, a clinically useful quantitative ultrasound examination should provide an integrated view of concurrent disease processes [3,8,50].

In practice, a multiparametric examination might include BMUS, hepatorenal index, liver stiffness, quantitative fat assessment, optional viscoelastic markers such as SWD, and, in selected patients, spleen stiffness or other signs of portal hypertension [24,50,51]. This integrated approach is especially relevant in MASLD (Fig. 5). BMUS provides structural information, elastography rules out fibrosis, a fat biomarker estimates steatosis, and additional markers may assess inflammation or portal hypertension. Such a framework would not replace all advanced imaging or biopsy, but it could improve triage, reduce diagnostic uncertainty, and provide a practical way to monitor patients over time [1–3,8,24,50].

The challenge is that multiple parameters can easily create interpretive confusion. A biomarker panel is valuable only if the results are clear, reproducible, and relevant to decision-making. Therefore, the next step is not merely to accumulate additional data, but to determine how best to integrate the data, identify which combinations are redundant or genuinely additive, and define what management changes should follow specific patterns. This transition—from technical accumulation to a coherent clinical framework—is pivotal for the field.

Hemodynamic Adjuncts and Advanced Disease Staging

As liver disease advances, the clinical emphasis often shifts from tissue properties to the consequences of structural change, particularly portal hypertension. At this stage, quantitative ultrasound may extend its utility beyond the liver itself. Although spleen stiffness and Doppler-derived hemodynamic parameters are not yet as common as liver stiffness measurement or fat quantification, their relevance is increasing in advanced chronic liver disease and MASLD-associated fibrosis [52]. Their value is based on a central concept: diffuse liver disease matters not only because of histological change, but also because it affects portal flow, splenic response, and the likelihood of decompensation.

This expansion represents an important conceptual advance in quantitative ultrasound. Elastography has shown that tissue mechanical properties can serve as clinically useful biomarkers, and measurements directed at portal hypertension suggest that downstream biomechanical and vascular effects may also be measurable [53]. However, these parameters are more contextual than universally applicable. They are likely to be most useful in patients already suspected of having advanced fibrosis or compensated advanced chronic liver disease, rather than in broad screening populations. Therefore, their role is best understood as a stage-dependent extension of the examination, not as a replacement for primary liver-based biomarkers.

This distinction has implications for workflow design. A frontline quantitative liver ultrasound examination for a patient with obesity and mildly elevated liver enzymes need not resemble an examination for a patient with suspected advanced fibrosis, thrombocytopenia, or signs of portal hypertension [54]. A modular approach, with a core module for fibrosis and steatosis and an advanced-disease module for hemodynamic context, spleen stiffness, and complication risk, may be more clinically realistic than an indiscriminate all-in-one protocol [55]. More broadly, the success of quantitative ultrasound should not be defined by the accumulation of measurable outputs. Rather, success depends on applying each parameter to the appropriate patient, at the appropriate disease stage, for the appropriate clinical decision.

Current Challenges

A further challenge, often underemphasized in technical papers, is implementation in routine sonography practice. A quantitative method may perform well in expert hands but still fail to scale if it disrupts workflow, depends on unstable ROI placement, or lacks intuitive reporting language. This issue is particularly relevant in abdominal ultrasound, where operator variability, patient body habitus, and time pressure are everyday realities rather than experimental nuisances. For quantitative liver ultrasound to achieve durable adoption, it must be operationally credible.

Operational credibility has several components. First, the acquisition sequence must be teachable. Sonographers and radiologists need practical answers to basic questions: How many measurements are sufficient? How should poor-quality acoustic windows be flagged? What constitutes a failed or unreliable examination? Second, the report must be interpretable and should link biomarkers to clinical domains such as fibrosis risk, steatosis, inflammation, and portal hypertension. Uncontextualized numerical outputs are unlikely to influence management. Third, implementation requires explicit quality standards. In conventional ultrasound, poor acoustic windows are often handled implicitly through operator judgment. Quantitative ultrasound benefits from a more explicit framework that includes quality indicators, reliability thresholds, and structured statements when technical conditions are suboptimal. Such discipline is not a bureaucratic extra; it is part of what makes a biomarker trustworthy. One of the most important lessons from the elastography era is that the reliability of a number depends as much on acquisition governance as on the underlying physics [4,13,15]. Fourth, quantitative liver ultrasound will almost certainly be strengthened by software assistance [56–59]. Artificial intelligence (AI)–based tools may help identify acceptable acoustic windows, suggest ROI placement, detect artifacts, and combine multiple biomarkers into standardized impression statements. Yet software assistance should support, not obscure, quantitative reasoning. The most useful systems will likely be those that improve consistency while preserving transparency about what was measured, how it was measured, and why the result should be trusted. In this respect, implementation science and human factors may matter as much as algorithmic innovation in the next phase of the field.

Standardization remains the most fundamental barrier to broader adoption. Quantitative ultrasound is often described using general terms such as attenuation, backscatter, stiffness, and dispersion, but actual implementations vary widely across vendors and platforms [44,60]. Signal processing, beamforming, ROI rules, quality metrics, and post-processing pipelines differ from one system to another [25,33,34,45,61]. As a result, values obtained on one machine may not be directly comparable to those obtained on another, even when both systems claim to measure the same physical property. This is not a minor technical issue; it is a major obstacle to multicenter comparisons, pooled data analysis, and the use of fixed cutoffs in practice. In addition, quantitative ultrasound still depends heavily on how acquisition is performed. Probe position, ROI placement, ROI depth, patient body habitus, breathing, fasting status, and local artifacts all affect measurement quality [3,8,15,47,62]. For attenuation methods, even small differences in ROI configuration can change diagnostic performance. For stiffness and viscoelastic measurements, acquisition-related variability can be clinically important, especially when patients are monitored over time. A numerical output does not eliminate operator dependence; it shifts that dependence into a more technical part of the process.

Another challenge is interpretive specificity. Liver stiffness does not reflect fibrosis alone. Quantitative fat markers can be influenced by fibrosis or other structural abnormalities [46]. Diagnostic standards are also changing, which complicates interpretation of the literature. In steatosis studies, MRI-PDFF is increasingly used as the noninvasive reference standard, whereas earlier ultrasound studies often relied on visual grading or biopsy in relatively small groups [24,26,27,41,63,64]. In fibrosis studies, histopathology remains the reference standard, and MRE is generally regarded as the most accurate noninvasive reference method. Other techniques, including liver stiffness measurement obtained using transient elastography and noninvasive serum biomarkers, may also serve as reference standards depending on study design [2–4,13]. For inflammation and steatohepatitis, histology remains essential but is difficult to scale. Thus, quantitative liver ultrasound is not simply validating technologies against a fixed truth; it is developing within a changing diagnostic reference framework.

The key clinically underdeveloped question is what constitutes meaningful change over time. For quantitative ultrasound monitoring, repeatability, minimal detectable difference, visit-to-visit variability, and treatment response thresholds are critical [15,41]. These concerns are especially important in MASLD, where treatments may reduce fat before affecting fibrosis or inflammation, which may not immediately alter tissue mechanics. Biomarkers that are useful for diagnosis at a single time point are not always effective for follow-up.

Finally, quantitative liver ultrasound must demonstrate not only technical validity, but also clinical impact. Does it reduce unnecessary referrals? Does it improve risk stratification compared with current workflows? Does it better identify which patients need MRI, biopsy, or treatment? Does it support better decision-making over time? These questions will determine whether quantitative liver ultrasound is adopted. The field has already shown that many measurements are feasible and often accurate. The next question is whether they change patient management in meaningful ways.

Future Directions

The most important future priority is the transition from novelty to robustness. The field no longer needs isolated demonstrations that a new parameter can be measured. Instead, it needs calibrated phantoms, vendor-neutral benchmarks, standardized acquisition protocols, explicit quality metrics, and prospective multicenter validation [25,33,34]. The development of elastography shows that guideline-driven harmonization can move a technology from technical interest to routine clinical application. Steatosis and viscoelastic biomarkers now require a comparable phase of disciplined consolidation.

A second priority is outcome-linked integration. Quantitative ultrasound parameters gain greater clinical significance when they are associated not only with histology or imaging surrogates, but also with outcomes and decision-making processes, such as incident advanced fibrosis, portal hypertension risk, treatment eligibility, or meaningful responses over time [1,2,50,52]. This priority is particularly important for multiparametric models, in which value lies not in generating additional data points, but in improving triage and management.

A third approach is intelligent simplification. The most effective quantitative ultrasound examination may not be the one that derives the maximum number of parameters, but the one that generates the clearest clinical phenotype with the least ambiguity. This strategy may involve combining a selected set of robust ultrasound biomarkers with serum markers and structured clinical data. Accordingly, the future direction of quantitative liver ultrasound is likely to be integrative rather than isolated: a scalable intermediary between initial imaging and precision liver care, rather than a replacement for all other diagnostic modalities or a merely additive adjunct.

AI may accelerate this integrative phase by helping unify physics-based biomarkers rather than replacing them. Potentially valuable applications include ROI placement, quality assurance, artifact detection, and integration of multiple ultrasound-derived parameters with clinical variables. These capabilities may be especially useful for large-scale screening, where standardization and operator support are as important as diagnostic precision. However, AI does not remove the need for biological validation. Instead, it increases the importance of ensuring that the underlying quantitative signals are interpretable and reproducible.

The future of quantitative liver ultrasound depends on integration into a trusted clinical ecosystem that includes guideline-based pathways, harmonized standards, monitoring frameworks, and explicit links between biomarkers and management. The field has moved beyond proof of concept and must now demonstrate clinical utility.

Conclusion

Quantitative liver ultrasound should be viewed not as a single technology, but as a family of biomarker strategies at different stages of maturity. Elastography is already an established clinical tool for fibrosis assessment. Ultrasound-based fat quantification is progressing rapidly through B-mode-guided attenuation, backscatter-informed frameworks, and radiofrequency-derived approaches such as UDFF. Biomarkers targeting inflammation and viscoelasticity are among the most promising developments, but they remain at earlier stages of validation and require greater standardization and clearer biological interpretation.

The central challenge is not whether the liver can be measured with ultrasound, but whether these measurements can become robust, interpretable, and actionable enough to influence care at scale. Once that threshold is crossed, quantitative liver ultrasound may become the most practical imaging platform for noninvasive liver phenotyping, positioned not at the extreme of technical sophistication, but at the optimal intersection of accessibility, repeatability, and clinically useful biological information.

Key points

Quantitative liver ultrasound encompasses several techniques that extract measurable tissue information rather than relying solely on visual interpretation. The field must establish robust quality control, biologically meaningful interpretation, clinically actionable thresholds, and reliable criteria for longitudinal response assessment.
