A bibliometric analysis of global research trends and emerging hotspots on suicide and depression among children and adolescents
Authors: Ziming Liu, Shumin Zhu, Yulan Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide and depression among children and adolescents represent critical global public health challenges. Despite a rapidly growing body of literature, the overall knowledge structure, research trends, and emerging directions in this field remain insufficiently synthesized.ObjectiveThis study aimed to perform a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of research on suicide and depression among children and adolescents, with a focus on thematic evolution, global research trends, and emerging areas to guide future research directions.MethodsA bibliometric analysis was conducted using the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database to identify relevant publications from 2005 to April 6, 2026. Original articles and review papers were included. Data were analyzed using Bibliometrix (R package), VOSviewer, and InCites to evaluate publication trends, contributing countries, institutions, journals, collaboration networks, and keyword co-occurrence patterns.ResultsA total of 8454 publications were included, demonstrating a steady increase in annual output, particularly after 2018, with a strong concentration in high-impact (Q1) journals. The United States and China were the leading contributors in terms of productivity, citations, and international collaboration. Core research themes included depression, suicide, and adolescent mental health, while five major thematic clusters were identified: clinical comorbidity, self-harm behaviors, social determinants and prevention, psychological mechanisms, and artificial intelligence-driven applications. Over time, research focus has shifted from traditional clinical and epidemiological approaches to integrative, data-driven frameworks. Emerging hotspots include machine learning, prediction models, ecological momentary assessment, and digital phenotyping.ConclusionsResearch on suicide and depression in children and adolescents has expanded substantially and is evolving toward interdisciplinary and precision-oriented paradigms. Advances in artificial intelligence and data analytics are reshaping early detection and prevention strategies. However, disparities in global research contributions and challenges in translating technological innovations into clinical practice persist. Future efforts should emphasize equitable collaboration, longitudinal data integration, and real-world implementation to enhance mental health outcomes in youth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44192-026-00538-w.Keywords: Suicide, Depression, Children, Adolescents, Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC), Bibliometric analysis

Introduction

Mental health disorders among children and adolescents represent a growing global public health challenge. Among these conditions, depression is one of the most prevalent and debilitating psychiatric disorders affecting young people [1]. Epidemiological evidence indicates that depressive symptoms frequently emerge during early adolescence and may persist into adulthood if left untreated, significantly affecting emotional development, academic achievement, and social functioning [2]. More concerning is the strong association between depression and suicidal behaviors in youth. Suicide is the second leading cause of death in young people worldwide and is responsible for about 52,000 deaths annually in children and adolescents aged 5–19 years [3, 4]. Prevalence for lifetime suicidal thoughts, suicide attempts, and nonsuicidal self-injury among preadolescents was 15.1, 2.6, and 6.2%, respectively, in community samples; and approximately 17.0% of preadolescents with suicidal ideation would transition to attempting suicide [5]. The development of suicide risk is complex, involving contributions from biological (including genetics), psychological (such as certain personality traits), clinical (such as comorbidities), social and environmental factors [6, 7]. Psychological problems such as avoidance, impulsivity, depression, and anxiety in youth can lead to suicide, with some of the most extreme groups of the population seeing death as a last resort to solve their problems [8, 9]. The coexistence of depression and suicide risk during critical developmental stages and antidepressant suicide not only poses immediate threats to life but also contributes to long-term psychological and societal burdens [10–12].

Over the past two decades, scientific interest in suicide and depression among children and adolescents has increased substantially. A growing body of research from psychiatry, psychology, neuroscience, and public health has sought to understand the etiology, risk factors, and prevention strategies related to youth suicidal behaviors and depression. Studies have highlighted a complex multifaceted interplay related to adolescent suicide and depression, including genetic vulnerability, family environment, peer relationships, academic stress, and exposure to adverse life events [13]. In recent years, rapid advances in digital health technologies and graph theory-based network analysis have expanded research directions, providing new approaches for early detection, risk prediction, and personalized mental health care. For example, machine learning models and digital behavioral data have been increasingly used to identify patterns associated with suicidal ideation and depressive symptoms among adolescents [14]. In addition, resting-state fMRI combined with graph theory analysis has revealed alterations in functional connectivity within key brain networks, including the sensorimotor network, default mode network, cognitive control network, and visual network, among adolescents with major depressive disorder and a history of suicide attempts [15].

Despite the rapid expansion of research in this area, the existing literature remains highly fragmented. Publications span diverse disciplines and journals, and the relationships among key research themes, influential authors, and institutional collaborations are not always well understood. Moreover, emerging topics such as the influence of social media, digital mental health platforms, and artificial intelligence-based predictive models are evolving quickly, making it difficult for researchers and policymakers to obtain a comprehensive overview of the field’s development. Without a systematic understanding of the knowledge structure and research trends, it becomes challenging to identify major scientific contributions, collaborative patterns, and future research priorities.

Bibliometric analysis offers a powerful approach to address these challenges by quantitatively evaluating large bodies of scientific literature. Through the analysis of publication outputs, citation networks, and keyword co-occurrence patterns, bibliometric methods can reveal the intellectual structure and evolution of a research field. Visualization tools such as VOSviewer and Bibliometrix further enable the mapping of collaborative networks and the identification of emerging research hotspots [16]. Such analyses are particularly valuable in interdisciplinary areas like youth mental health research, where integrating insights from multiple fields is essential for advancing knowledge and informing policy.

Therefore, the present study aims to conduct a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of global research on suicide and depression among children and adolescents using the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database. Specifically, this study seeks to (1) examine temporal trends and geographical distribution of publications, (2) identify influential authors, institutions, and journals, (3) analyze collaboration networks and knowledge structures within the field, and (4) detect emerging research hotspots and future research directions. By mapping the scientific landscape, this study aims to contribute to a deeper understanding of the evolving research dynamics surrounding depression among children and adolescents and to support more effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive, observational, and cross-sectional study was conducted through a bibliometric analysis of the worldwide scientific production of suicide and depression in children and adolescents published in journals indexed in WoSCC between database inception and April 6, 2026. The analysis included only original research articles and review papers. The purpose of the analysis was to explore the thematic evolution, global research collaborations, and emerging trends within the WoSCC research ecosystem.

Data collection and screening strategy

According to the PRISMA guidelines [17], we selected the Science Citation Index Expanded (SCIE), Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI), and Arts & Humanities Citation Index (A & HCI) subdatabases of the WoSCC as the bibliometric dataset for the standardized indexing structure and citation reliability [18, 19]. Data retrieval and analysis were completed on April 6, 2026.

An advanced topic search was performed using the following query: TOPIC= (“suicid*”) AND (“depress*” OR “MDD”) AND (“child*” OR “adolescent*” OR “teenager*” OR “school student*” OR “minors*” OR “toddler” OR “infant*”). The non-relevant terms of “ST depression” and “respiratory depression” was excluded. Finally, only English-language original articles and review papers published between 2005 and April 6, 2026, were included. Publications with the document types of Meeting Abstract, Editorial Material, Early Access, Proceeding Paper, Letter, Book Chapters, Correction, Retracted Publication, News Item, and Book Review were excluded.

The timespan covered was from 2005 to April 6, 2026. Two independent researchers conducted the search and screening process to ensure reliability. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Full bibliographic records and cited references were exported in plain text and InCites-compatible formats and a total of 9234 documents were retrieved for subsequent analysis. Bibliometric analysis and visualization

To minimize potential bias resulting from the continuous updating of bibliographic databases, all data extraction and bibliometric analyses were performed on the same date, April 6, 2026. The retrieved records were exported and analyzed using a combination of complementary bibliometric and visualization tools, including the Bibliometrix package (v5.0) in the R software environment (v4.5.2), VOSviewer (v1.6.20), InCites Benchmarking & Analytics (v2026), InCites Journal Citation Reports (JCR), and Microsoft Excel 2019®.

Data from the WoSCC dataset were imported into Bibliometrix, VOSviewer, and InCites Explorer. Prior to analysis, the datasets underwent rigorous cleaning and preprocessing using the built-in procedures of each platform. These procedures included duplicate record removal, standardization of author names and institutional affiliations, and normalization of keywords, thereby ensuring the accuracy, consistency, and reliability of the bibliometric results.

We used the R “Bibliometrix” package [20] for metadata extraction, descriptive statistics, visualization of theme evolution, and a graph of author keyword frequency trends, as well as to compute bibliometric indicators such as the h-index and m-index [21].

VOSviewer was used to analyze the co-occurrence of collaborating countries and author keywords via the full-counting method. The graphical interpretation is based on the grouping guidelines (keywords) and the distance between countries (co-occurrence networks) [22, 23]. We set the resolution as 1.00, miniest cluster as 3 items to generate 5–6 clusters, representing the main groups in the given field.

InCites JCR was used to obtain the Journal Impact Factor (JIF) quartiles. We also used InCites Benchmarking & Analytics [24] to graph the trend of publications with JIF quartiles, funding agencies, and funded studies. The publications in 2025 and 2026 were ranked by 2024 JIF quartiles.

These tools enabled a comprehensive, multidimensional analysis of the dataset, addressing both quantitative and qualitative aspects of bibliometric trends.

Multiple complementary indices were used to assess scholarly impact: the h-index, indicating that h publications have been cited at least h times; the m-index, calculated as the h-index divided by the number of years since first publication to account for career length [21, 25]. Journal impact was evaluated using the JIF in its category for the JCR year [26]. Citations, serving as both a marker of scholarly impact and a conduit for intellectual influence, were also used for impact on research within the field or across all fields [27].

Corresponding author affiliation was used to represent research leadership. Publications were categorized as Single-Country Publications (SCPs) or Multiple-Country Publications (MCPs) based on author affiliations. International collaboration was assessed using the number and proportion of MCPs, with MCP% calculated as MCPs divided by the total number of publications (SCPs + MCPs) for each country.

Additionally, Microsoft Excel 2019® was used for visualization of publication trend by JIF quartiles.

Ethics

The bibliographic data were downloaded from a public database and involved no human participants or identifiable personal information. Therefore, institutional ethical approval was not required.

Results

A total of 8454 articles addressing suicide and depression among children and adolescents were retrieved from the WoSCC database from the period of 2005 to April 6, 2026. These publications consisted of 7468 original articles and 986 review papers, with a 4.25% annual growth rate, 39.84 average citations per document, and an international collaboration rate of 27.48% (Table 1).

Research trends

Among the 9532 author keywords, 133 were selected for analysis in VOSviewer and were chosen on the basis of their occurrence in at least 30 documents (Fig. 4A). The most frequent co-occurrent author keywords used by the authors (300 and more frequency) were depression, suicide, adolescents, adolescent, suicidal ideation, mental health, anxiety, adolescence, machine learning, non-suicidal self-injury, and suicidality. The 132 author keywords were divided into five different groups according to keyword frequency and total link strength: (i) Clinical Comorbidity and Psychiatric Spectrum in Youth Suicide (suicide, bipolar disorder, suicidality, comorbidity, epidemiology, mood disorders, antidepressants, etc.); (ii) Self-Harm Behaviors and Psychosocial Risk in Adolescents (adolescents, suicidal ideation, adolescence, self-harm, suicidal attempt, suicidal behavior, depression symptoms, etc.); (iii) Social-Environmental Determinants and Public Health Prevention (adolescent, mental health, children, bullying, youth, transgender, suicide prevention, etc.); (iv) Psychological Mechanisms and Emotional Dysregulation Pathways (major depressive disorder, non-suicidal self-injury, childhood trauma, stress, resilience, esteem, inflammation, etc.); and (v) Screening, Prevention, and AI-Driven Clinical Applications (depression, anxiety, machine learning, prevention, covid-19, screening, pregnancy, etc.), representing the main research directions representing the main research directions in suicide and depression among children and adolescents over time.

To better understand the trend in theme change, we investigated the evolution of research over time using co-word network analysis and clustering (Figs. 4B and 5). From 2005 to 2026, the themes “depression” and “suicide” existed at all phases; research themes were relatively broad and clinically oriented pre-2021, with high frequent keywords including antidepressants, comorbidity, suicidal ideation, adolescents, and bipolar disorder; and the post-2021 themes such as mental health, major depressive disorder, nonsuicidal self-injury, risk factors, inflammation, treatment-resistant depression, suicidal ideation, machine learning, suicide prevention, covid-19, suicide risk, network analysis, and adolescence, represented the recent research focus and a movement toward multifactorial risk modeling and prevention frameworks. The thematic evolution demonstrates a progression from disease-level research toward integrated mental health, risk factor identification, and preventive intervention approaches.

Discussion

The present research represents an important comprehensive bibliometric analysis of the scientific literature on suicide and depression among children and adolescents. The WoSCC was chosen as the primary database because of its standardized indexing structure, comprehensive citation data, and recognized reliability for bibliometric research [18, 19].

Although the first publication in this field appeared in 2005 [38], research on suicide and depression among children and adolescents has received increasing attention over time. The annual growth rate of scientific production was 4.25%, which is far lower than that reported for pediatric hemiplegia research (8.69%) [39], psycho-oncology studies (13.9%) [40], family resilience in the context of children and adolescents (14.49%) [41], and mental disorders using machine learning (17.05%) [42]. Nevertheless, publication output has increased steadily, particularly since 2018, indicating growing scholarly attention to this topic.

The upward trend in publication output may be associated with increasing awareness of the burden of suicide and depression among children and adolescents, especially following the COVID-19 pandemic and its impact on mental health [43–46]. The expanding literature also reflects broader research interest in topics such as artificial intelligence [14, 47], cross-lagged panel network analysis [48], EMA [49], social media data [50, 51], digital phenotyping from wearable devices [52, 53], and multi-level datasets [50, 54]. These developments have been accompanied by a continuous increase in scientific output and a broader scope of research within the field.

The prolific documents were mainly published in psychiatry, psychology, multidisciplinary, or adolescent research journals, such as Journal of Affective Disorders, Frontiers in Psychiatry, PLoS ONE, and Journal of Adolescent Health, which are very highly impactful in this field with higher h-index, m-index, local citations, and global citations. The most cited documents mainly published JIF Q1 journals, for instance American Journal of Psychiatry, Archives of General Psychiatry, Lancet, JAMA, etc. Notably, the citation count for a journal did not seem to be similar to publication volume, and not all the most frequently cited documents were included in these top prolific journals. This may be due to citation bias, particularly the authors’ preference for citing papers from academic societies and high-impact journals [55]. The gaps between citation count and publication in the current study resulted from similar citation selection; the American Journal of Psychiatry, sponsored by American Psychiatric Association, contributed three highly cited documents, while The Lancet and JAMA were two top journals in all medical fields.

Globally, consistent with other areas of mental health research [56, 57], North America, Asia, and Europe contribute the majority of literature on adolescent suicide and depression, a pattern reflected in citation impact. Research productivity is concentrated in high-income countries with strong funding support, established infrastructures, and extensive international collaborations, with leading contributions from organizations such as Harvard University, University of Toronto, and University of London, and scholars including Brent DA, Mann JJ, Turecki G, Kaess M, and Apter A.

These disparities largely reflect unequal distribution of resources and collaborative networks across regions. While sustained investment and multi-countries collaboration enhance research capacity, knowledge exchange, and scientific influence [58], output remains limited in many low- and middle-income countries, particularly in Africa and parts of Latin America, where mental health research competes with other urgent public health needs. The publication gap is further shaped by contextual barriers, including shortages of mental health professionals, strained service availability, stigma, and challenges in diagnosis and access to evidence-based care. Broader social and environmental factors such as educational and family engagement, digital influences, cyberbullying, conflict, and forced displacement also influence both disease burden and research feasibility [59]. As a result, studies on prevention, early intervention, and service delivery remain underrepresented in these settings. In addition, database coverage and language bias in systems such as WoSCC may reinforce the predominance of high-income, English-speaking countries [60]. Encouragingly, initiatives such as Using Peer Support in Developing Empowering Mental Health Services (UPSIDES) [61] and The Lancet Psychiatry Commission on transforming mental health implementation research [62] highlight growing global efforts to strengthen mental health systems and research capacity. Sustained investment in international collaboration, workforce capacity building, and equitable resource allocation will be essential for addressing enduring disparities in mental health research, service delivery, and access across regions.

Within this dataset, the most frequently cited literature reflects the core concerns of the field. These highly cited, and profoundly influential studies delve deeply into various factors associated with suicidal and depressed adolescents. For instance, the most-cited article identifies the “Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale” (C-SSRS) as a valid instrument, applicable in both clinical and research settings, for assessing suicidal ideation and behavior in adolescents and adults [28]; the second-ranked article reports an association between antidepressant use in pediatric patients and a slight increase in suicide risk, recommending close patient monitoring to help manage this risk [31]; while the third-ranked article focuses specifically on the entire context of adolescent depression [30]. Meanwhile, the most recent highly cited article published in 2019 explores the association between gender and suicidal behavior, as well as gender-specific risk and protective factors [31]. These landmark and milestone studies reveal several common themes, encompassing the assessment of suicide severity, risk factors, epidemiology, and other contextual dimensions of suicide and depression, thereby laying a solid foundation for research into adolescent suicide and depression, and demonstrating the continuity of this line of inquiry and key priorities of this field of study.

The co-word and clustering analyses revealed a field rooted in enduring clinical concerns yet increasingly shaped by methodological innovation. Core keywords such as depression, suicide, adolescents, and suicidal ideation remained central, reflecting the persistent burden of these conditions. Importantly, depression is a major risk factor for suicidal ideation and behavior, whereas suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and completed suicide represent distinct but related stages of suicidal outcomes. Non-suicidal self-injury and self-harm, although often co-occurring, are conceptually distinct from suicidal behavior. The emergence of machine learning alongside constructs such as non-suicidal self-injury highlights growing integration between psychiatry and data science [63]. Together with the identification of five thematic clusters, these findings suggest a shift from diagnosis-focused research toward more integrative and interdisciplinary approaches. Consistent with this trend, recent studies increasingly emphasize multifactorial risk factors, biological mechanisms such as inflammation, advanced analytical methods, and digital technologies, reflecting a more nuanced understanding of depression and the spectrum of suicidal and self-injurious behaviors in children and adolescents [64].

This progression is even more evident in the evolution of emerging and persistent keyword trends. While established themes such as biomarkers, ketamine-based treatments, and adolescent mental health remain prominent, newer topics including latent profile analysis [48], prediction models [64], and EMA [49] highlight a growing emphasis on precision psychiatry and real-time monitoring. The rising attention to psychosocial and contextual variables, such as internet gaming disorder [65], LGBTQ+ issues [66], circadian rhythms [67], and caregiver dynamics [68], reflects a broader and more nuanced understanding of youth mental health that extends beyond symptomatology. By 2026, the incorporation of approaches like cross-lagged panel network analysis and EMA indicates a transition toward dynamic, longitudinal modeling of mental states [48, 49], enabling the capture of temporal and bidirectional relationships among risk factors. At the same time, the sustained interest in treatments ranging from ketamine and esketamine to mindfulness-based therapies [69, 70, 71] reflects ongoing efforts to address clinical complexity, particularly in treatment-resistant cases. These trends suggest that research on suicide and depression in minors is advancing toward an integrative, prevention-oriented paradigm, one that leverages diverse research methodologies and multidimensional data to enable earlier identification and more personalized interventions. Nonetheless, these methodologies remain largely investigational and require additional validation. At the same time, their application introduces new challenges related to the interpretability of results, ethical considerations, and real-world applicability. These challenges highlight the persistent gap between current research findings and routine clinical practice, underscoring the need for greater attention to translational research and practical implementation.

In the future, research on suicide and depression among children and adolescents may focus the following: (i) the prediction of long-term outcomes, EMA, predictive interventions, and online interventions; (ii) mindfulness-based therapies and conflict resolution, and caregiving; (iii) neuroimaging; (iv) validation effect and interpretability of advanced research methods, such as machine learning, artificial intelligence, or natural language processing; (v) special groups suffering from internet gaming disorder, or LGBTQ+ issues and disease subgroups, such as depressive symptom and major depressive disorder, suicide, suicidal attempt, suicidal thoughts, self-harm suicide, etc. Researchers will emphasis on well-being of children and adolescents and block the occurrence of suicide and depression, as well as adverse prognosis.

Our study is an overview regarding the entire period of first article publication to April 6, 2026, in the research field on suicide- and depression-related studies in children and adolescents. Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, bibliometric analysis itself depends on structural metadata rather than full-text evaluation, some terminological variability may introduce fragmentation and lead to bias. Second, the analysis relies on the WoSCC dataset; the single- source study may not cover the entire body of literature, especially those published in non-indexed journals. Third, the article types are limited to original articles and review articles, which may lose some literature in other types, including non-English, meeting papers, book chapters, etc., and lead to some trend bias. Fourth, the present study relied on a limited set of search terms. As a result, some relevant publications using alternative terminology, such as “self-harm,” “self-injury,” “internalizing symptoms,” “youth,” “young people,” and “pediatric,” may not have been captured introducing selection bias into the analysis. Fifth, based on the primary research design, including clustering resolution parameters and threshold settings, some low-frequency terms may be excluded from further analysis. For example, terms such as “functional connectivity” and “resting-state fMRI” may be underrepresented. In addition, the use of non-standardized or non-harmonized synonyms may further reduce term capture and affect the completeness of the analysis. Finally, we used several bibliometric indicators, including local and global citation counts, JIF, h-index, and m-index, to evaluate the scholar impact and quality of scientific publications. However, each indicator value is based on a special calculation, and may be influenced by factors such as publication age, journal visibility, disciplinary differences, self-citation, citation cartels, and regional research inequalities in research activity [26]. Furthermore, these indicators did not capture other important dimensions of research influence, including scientific collaboration, interdisciplinary work, knowledge translation, and broader societal impact [61].

Conclusions

By extracting and analyzing data from the WoSCC database, this bibliometric study provides valuable insights of global research on suicide and depression among children and adolescents. A total of 8454 publications were analyzed from 2005 to April 6, 2026. The findings demonstrate a sustained increase in scientific output, particularly in recent years, reflecting the growing recognition of these conditions as critical public health concerns. Research activity is highly concentrated in high-impact journals and is predominantly led by countries such as the United States and China, which also play central roles in international collaboration networks and research funding.

Thematic trends reveal a clear shift from clinical disorder-focused research toward a more integrative and preventive framework. Increasing attention is being given to risk factor identification, early intervention strategies, and the broader context of mental health. Notably, emerging research hotspots highlight the rapid incorporation of advanced methodologies, including machine learning, artificial intelligence, latent profiles analysis, and cross-lagged panel network analysis, as well as the use novel data sources such as social media, digital phenotyping, and even imaging, to improve prediction and intervention outcomes.

Despite these advances, the study also suggests potential imbalances in global research contributions and collaboration, with underrepresentation from low- and middle-income countries. Addressing these disparities and strengthening international partnerships will be essential for developing more inclusive and globally relevant evidence.

Overall, the field is moving toward a more data-driven, interdisciplinary, and prevention-oriented and personalized paradigm, leveraging multidisciplinary approaches and artificial intelligence to better understand, predict, and mitigate suicide risk and depressive disorders in youth. Future research should prioritize longitudinal and real-time data integration, strengthen cross-national collaborations, and emphasize translational impact to bridge the gap between research findings and clinical practice. Through these efforts, the scientific community can contribute more effectively to reducing the global burden of mental health disorders and suicide among children and adolescents.
