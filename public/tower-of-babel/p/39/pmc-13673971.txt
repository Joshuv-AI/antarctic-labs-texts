Strategic temporary bronchial division for safe central pulmonary artery control during complex left upper lobectomy
Authors: Shin-nosuke Watanabe, Daisuke Kimura, Takahiro Sasaki, Shuta Kimura, Chisaki Ichinohe, Tsubasa Kato, Masahito Minakawa
Published: 2026

Pulmonary arterial branch exposure in left upper lobectomy by strategic bronchial division.

Central Message

Strategic bronchial division enhances central pulmonary artery exposure enabling safe vascular control in left upper lobectomy, particularly in complex hilar conditions and intraoperative bleeding.

Secure management of the central pulmonary artery (PA) is a critical step in left upper lobectomy (LUL), particularly around the A3 branch, where injury can cause life-threatening hemorrhage.1 Restricted visualization and maneuverability in minimally invasive surgeries further amplify these challenges.1,2 In complex hilar conditions (eg, bulky lymphadenopathy, central tumors, dense adhesions), the bronchus is often a mechanical obstacle, obscuring vascular structures and hindering safe dissection. This report describes the strategic temporary bronchial division technique, wherein the segmental bronchus is intentionally divided to optimize PA exposure, facilitating secure vascular control across diverse scenarios, including as a bailout strategy during robot-assisted surgeries and in cases requiring PA reconstruction.

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 74-year-old man presented with a 32- × 23-mm solid tumor in the left upper lobe (S1+2), with hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Station 5, 12u), diagnosed as squamous cell carcinoma (cT2aN2M0, stage IIIA; Figure 1, A). Video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) LUL with lymph node dissection was performed.

A, Three-dimensional computed tomography reconstruction showing the tumor (purple) and bulky lymph nodes (yellow). B, Intraoperative view showing division of the segmental bronchi (divided bronchus) exposing the A3 branch.

The peripheral pulmonary arterial branches (A5, A4, A1+2c, A1+2b, A1+2a) were divided sequentially; however, the upper lobe was poorly mobilized and bulky hilar lymph nodes meant the central A3 branch could not be identified. The upper lobe bronchus was dissected distal to the segmental level, and the segmental bronchi (B1-3 and B4+5) were sharply divided (Figure 1, B). This maneuver exposed the main PA, facilitating the identification and safe division of A3 using a stapler. The bronchial stump was then closed proximally with a stapler (Video 1).

Case 2

A 15-year-old boy presented with a 35- × 30-mm central tumor in the left upper lobe diagnosed as an inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (Figure 2, A). Robot-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) LUL was chosen as the first surgical treatment approach.

A, Three-dimensional computed tomography reconstruction showing the central tumor (purple). B, Intraoperative view. Division of the lingular bronchus (divided bronchus) facilitated complete division of the upper lobe bronchus, exposing the A1+2a+b+A3 and mediastinal-type A4+5 branches.

After we divided the peripheral branches (A5 and A1+2c), we discovered dissection of the central branches was hindered by the tumor. During PA dissection, bleeding occurred from a branch of A1+2a+b and was controlled using a fibrin sealant patch. Conversion to thoracotomy was considered; however, instead, the lingular bronchus (B4+5) was dissected and divided sharply, thus exposing the mediastinal-type A4+5 branch. We followed this proximally, establishing a safe plane between the PA and superior segmental bronchus (B1-3). The bronchus was closed proximally with a stapler, and the common trunk with A1+2a + b, A3, and mediastinal-type A4+5 branches were safely divided en bloc (Figure 2, B, Video 2). Lobectomy was finally completed without conversion.

Case 3

A 58-year-old man presented with a 46- × 31-mm tumor invading the main PA, diagnosed as squamous cell carcinoma (cT4N2M0, stage IIIB; Figure 3, A). After a course of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, thoracotomy with PA reconstruction was planned.

A, Three-dimensional computed tomography reconstruction showing the tumor (purple) invading the pulmonary artery. B, Intraoperative view. Division of the segmental bronchi (divided bronchus) exposing the pulmonary artery trunk and A3 branch.

After division of the peripheral PA branches (A5, A4, and A1+2c), the central pulmonary branches were not identifiable because of tumor invasion. The segmental bronchi (B1-3 and B4+5) were sharply divided after distal dissection, facilitating direct exposure of the PA trunk and identification of the A1+2a, A1+2b, and A3 (Figure 3, B) branches, which were safely ligated and divided. The bronchus was closed proximally with a stapler (Video 3), after which the involved PA segment was resected and repaired with double-layer suturing after specimen extraction.

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians. The requirement for institutional review board approval was waived, in accordance with the institution's policy.

Discussion

Controlling the central PA, particularly around A3, represents a key technical challenge in LUL, particularly in cases with complex hilar anatomy. In such cases, the bronchus can hinder safe exposure and dissection. The strategic temporary bronchial division technique aims not simply to achieve a bronchus-first sequence3 but rather to ensure reliable and safe control of the PA when lung mobilization is difficult. The central PA can be directly visualized by dividing the segmental bronchus, thereby improving exposure and facilitating safer vascular control (Figure 4). However, as demonstrated in case 2, complete division of the upper lobe bronchus is not always required if safe PA control can be achieved.

Schematic illustration of the strategic temporary bronchial division technique. Temporary division of the segmental bronchi (B1-3 and/or B4+5) improves exposure of the central pulmonary artery (PA), facilitating safer vascular control.

Previous reports have described bronchial incision or bronchus-first approaches to facilitate difficult hilar dissections. Tsunezuka and colleagues4 reported a temporary segmental bronchus incision technique during fissureless thoracoscopic lobectomy, whereas Nomori and colleagues5 described preceding bronchial cutting for PA exposure in severe hilar scarring after chemoradiotherapy. However, our approach differs in that it strategically applies temporary segmental bronchial division for central PA control during complex left upper lobectomy. To our knowledge, such applications in RATS and cases requiring PA reconstruction have rarely been specifically described. This technique may additionally serve as an effective bailout maneuver in situations of difficult hilar exposure or unexpected PA bleeding. Temporary hemostasis was achieved in case 2, and bronchial division clarified the course of the PA to facilitate safe continuation without requiring conversion to thoracotomy. This is particularly relevant in RATS, where vascular control may be challenging because of limited tactile feedback. This technique is also applicable in cases that require PA reconstruction, because it improves both proximal and distal control.

From a technical perspective, bronchial division should be performed as distally as possible at the segmental level to maximize the distance from the PA while preserving the closure margins. The bronchus can be securely closed using a stapler at the proximal end. In our institution, this maneuver is consistently applied in complex LUL cases with difficult hilar exposure and has been performed safely without major intraoperative or postoperative complications, including bronchial stump fistula. When the tumor extends into the segmental bronchus, sufficient stapler closure margins may not be achievable. However, the concept of this technique may still be applicable by performing bronchial division at the lobar level with subsequent suture closure instead of stapling. In such cases with more proximal involvement, performing bronchial division at the lobar level—consistent with the maneuvers for sleeve lobectomy or deep wedge bronchoplasty—enables direct central PA exposure without exposing the tumor itself. This ensures safe vascular control while maintaining oncologic integrity by avoiding tumor manipulation. Potential concerns associated with this approach include bacterial contamination of the thoracic cavity after bronchial opening, which can be mitigated through predivision bronchoscopic suction and intraoperative irrigation. No infection-related complications were observed in our cases. Oncologic integrity is maintained by adherence to standard principles, including adequate margins, minimal tumor manipulation, and systematic lymph node dissection. However, this technique should be avoided in cases in which the tumor extends to the segmental or more proximal bronchus on preoperative imaging or bronchoscopy, because the bronchial opening may increase the risk of tumor dissemination.

This technique is applicable across thoracotomy, VATS, and RATS and may be particularly beneficial in minimally invasive surgery. The limitations of this study include the small sample size and retrospective design. Further validation is therefore warranted.

Conclusions

Strategic temporary bronchial division is a versatile technique that ensures safe PA control in complex hilar situations. Its use as a bailout for intraoperative bleeding and as an aid in vascular reconstruction facilitates challenging resections across VATS, RATS, and open platforms, thereby reducing unnecessary conversion.
