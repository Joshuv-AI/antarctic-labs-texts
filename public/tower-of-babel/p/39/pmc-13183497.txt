A feline case of intestinal small T-cell lymphoma with prominent lymphoid follicle formation and concurrent intestinal large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Kosei SAKAI, Tamiko FUMIMOTO, Masaru FURUYA, Shunsuke SHIMAMURA, Takeshi IZAWA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An 11-year-old castrated male mixed-breed cat presented with a 2-week history of lethargy and anorexia. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed jejunal thickening and adjacent masses. As fine-needle aspiration cytology and PCR for antigen receptor gene rearrangement of the masses suggested small T-cell lymphoma, resection biopsy was performed. Based on histopathology and immunohistochemistry, a diagnosis of small T-cell lymphoma with prominent lymphoid follicle formation and concurrent large B-cell lymphoma was made. Postoperative chemotherapy with chlorambucil and prednisolone was performed. The cat survived for 728 days since initial presentation, with no recurrence of clinical signs or ultrasonographic abnormalities. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing a feline case of intestinal small T-cell lymphoma with prominent lymphoid follicle formation and concurrent intestinal large B-cell lymphoma.

Lymphoma is the most common hematopoietic neoplasm in cats, presenting in varying anatomic forms including extra-nodal, mediastinal, and multicentric types. Among these, gastrointestinal lymphoma is the most frequently observed form, accounting for 50–75% of all feline lymphoma cases [10]. This form involves the gastrointestinal tract and often extends to extra-intestinal organs such as the lymph nodes, liver, and spleen.

The National Cancer Institute Working Formulation and the Revised European-American Lymphoma/World Health Organization (REAL/WHO) schemes are used for histological classification of feline lymphomas [11, 16,17,18]. In addition, several subtypes can be defined based on cell morphology (small or large cell) and immunophenotype (T or B cell) of the neoplastic lymphocytes [1]. This report presents a rare case of feline intestinal small T-cell lymphoma with extensive lymphoid follicle formation and concurrent intestinal large-B cell lymphoma.

An 11-year-old castrated male mixed-breed cat was referred to the Veterinary Medical Center of Osaka Metropolitan University, with a 2-week history of lethargy and anorexia. On presentation (day 0), the cat was in generally good condition, with mildly decreased activity and appetite and occasional vomiting (approximately once a week). On physical examination, the cat weighed 5.9 kg and had a body condition score of 4/5. No enlargement of peripheral lymph nodes was palpated.

Histologically, there was a diffuse proliferative lesion consisting of small lymphocytes in the submucosa of the jejunum, accompanied with numerous lymphoid follicles with enlarged germinal centers. Mild infiltration of small lymphocytes into the lamina propria was observed. The mucosal epithelium was well-preserved and had no observable lymphoepithelial lesion. The small lymphocytes had small amount of cytoplasm and small- to intermediate-sized round nuclei with indistinct nucleoli, with their nuclei being less than 1.5 times the size of a red blood cell. Cellular atypia was mild, and the mitotic count was 0–1 per 10 high-power fields, which was equal to 2.37 mm2. These lymphocytes did not compress lymphoid follicles in the lesion. Based on the above findings, a diagnosis of intestinal small T-cell lymphoma with lymphoid follicle formation and concurrent intestinal large-B cell lymphoma was made. In accordance with the standard treatment of feline gastrointestinal small T-cell lymphoma [2, 5, 7, 12, 15], postoperative chemotherapy with chlorambucil (starting dose: 2 mg/head, once every 2 days) and prednisolone (starting dose: 2 mg/kg, once a day) was performed. At manuscript preparation, the cat has survived for 728 days since initial presentation, with no recurrence of clinical signs or ultrasonographic abnormalities.

Thickened jejunum and adjacent mesenteric masses were observed in the present case. Histopathological examination revealed lymphocytic proliferation with prominent formation of lymphoid follicles within the lesions. The infiltrating lymphocytes were predominantly small T cells, and clonal rearrangement of the T-cell receptor genes was confirmed by PARR analysis, thereby supporting a diagnosis of intestinal small T-cell lymphoma rather than follicular lymphoma or reactive lymphoid hyperplasia. Using the REAL/WHO classification scheme, feline intestinal small T-cell lymphoma has been most commonly categorized as epitheliotropic small T-cell lymphoma, epitheliotropic T-cell lymphoma, intestinal T-cell lymphoma, or enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma [1]. In the present case, the tumor localization and histological features differed from these types, and the lesion was classified as peripheral T-cell lymphoma, not otherwise specified. However, this classification does not adequately capture the histological architecture observed in the case. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of feline peripheral T-cell lymphoma composed predominantly of small T cells with prominent lymphoid follicle formation in the intestine.

In the WHO classification of tumors in domestic animals, intestinal T-cell lymphoma is characterized by small neoplastic lymphocytes predominantly located in the lamina propria of the villi showing epitheliotropism [9]. These tumors are considered to originate from intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs) and lamina propria lymphocytes (LPLs). In contrast, the neoplastic lymphocytes in the present case were predominantly located in the submucosa, with minimal infiltration into the mucosal epithelium and lamina propria, and were accompanied by prominent lymphoid follicle formation. These features clearly distinguish this case from intestinal T-cell lymphoma described in the WHO classification. Instead, the histological features observed in this case suggest that the neoplastic lymphocytes may originate from lymphocyte populations associated with Peyer’s patches and isolated lymphoid follicles.

This case also had focal proliferation of large B cells within the lesion of the small T-cell lymphoma. Because these cells exhibited histological features consistent with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma according to the REAL/WHO scheme [17], they were interpreted as neoplastic in nature. Although clonal rearrangement of the immunoglobulin heavy- or light-chain genes was not detected in the B-cell foci, this was most likely attributed to the insufficient amount of DNA obtained from the small lesion, as indicated by the weak amplification of the control gene in this sample. In the present case, the differing immunophenotypes of the small and large neoplastic lymphocytes indicate that these cells may represent two independent lymphomas. Although information on immunophenotype is limited, previous studies have reported that 5.4–9.9% of cats diagnosed with gastrointestinal small-cell lymphoma subsequently develop gastrointestinal large-cell lymphoma [12, 19]. Given the limited available evidence, no definitive conclusion regarding the histogenesis of the large B-cell lymphoma can be drawn. Therefore, this case is best interpreted as intestinal small T-cell lymphoma with prominent lymphoid follicle formation and concurrent intestinal large B-cell lymphoma.

Cytological evaluation of the mesenteric masses in this case revealed numerous small lymphocytes, raising suspicion of small-cell lymphoma. However, cytology alone is insufficient for a definitive diagnosis of small-cell lymphoma, and tissue biopsy remains the current gold standard [8]. In fact, cytology failed to demonstrate key histological features such as lymphoid follicle formation in this case. This underscores the importance of combining cytology with tissue biopsy even when cytological findings suggest small-cell lymphoma.

As the efficacy of surgical resection and adjuvant chemotherapy has been reported in cats with discrete gastrointestinal large-cell lymphoma [3], this strategy was also adopted in the present case. Chemotherapy protocols for feline gastrointestinal lymphoma differ between small- and large-cell types. For gastrointestinal small-cell lymphoma, the most common first-line regimen consists of oral chlorambucil combined with a glucocorticoid (often prednisolone) [2, 5, 7, 12, 15]. In contrast, different first-line regimens have been reported for gastrointestinal large-cell lymphoma, including COP (cyclophosphamide, vincristine, and prednisolone), CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisolone), CMOP (cyclophosphamide, mitoxantrone, vincristine, and prednisolone), CCNU (lomustine), ACNU (nimustine), and l-asparaginase have been reported [4, 6, 13, 14]. In the present case, the tumor tissue contained a mixture of small- and large-cell lymphomas. The proliferative area of large B cells was limited and well-demarcated, whereas neoplastic small T cells were identified near the surgical margins. Therefore, chlorambucil combined with prednisolone was selected as the chemotherapy regimen. Approximately two years after surgery, the present case continues to show a favorable clinical course; however, further studies with more cases are needed to determine the optimal therapeutic approach for this disease.

In summary, this case illustrates a rare form of feline intestinal small T-cell lymphoma with lymphoid follicle formation and concurrent large B-cell lymphoma. Accurate diagnosis requires an integrated approach combining histopathological examination with molecular analyses, including immunohistochemistry and PARR analysis. Surgical resection followed by postoperative chemotherapy resulted in a favorable outcome. Although this report proposes a novel pathological entity in feline gastrointestinal lymphoma, further studies with additional cases are needed to improve understanding of this disease.
