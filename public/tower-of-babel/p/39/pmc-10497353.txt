Prevalence of Loneliness and Its Association With Suicidality in the General Population: Results From a Nationwide Survey in Korea
Authors: Jimin Lee, Sung Man Chang, Bong-Jin Hahm, Jee Eun Park, Su Jeong Seong, Jin Pyo Hong, Hong Jin Jeon, Hyonggin An, Byung-Soo Kim
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough it is known that a substantial proportion of the population experience loneliness, the consequence of loneliness remains unclear by countries and ages. Accordingly, this study aimed to assess the association between loneliness and suicidality in the general population of Korea.MethodA total of 5,511 Koreans aged 18–79 completed a tablet-assisted personal interview using the Korean version of the Composite International Diagnostic Interview and responded to questions about loneliness and lifetime suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts. A logistic regression analysis was used to examine the association between loneliness and suicidality.ResultsApproximately one-third of the Korean general population reported loneliness. Being older, never married, widowed, separated, or divorced, unemployed, and having a part-time job were all significantly related to loneliness. After adjusting for sociodemographic factors, individuals with loneliness were significantly associated with increased suicidal ideation (adjusted odd ratio [aOR], 4.05; 95% confidence interval [CI], 3.36–4.88), suicidal plans (aOR, 4.91; 95% CI, 3.34–7.21), and suicidal attempts (aOR, 4.82; 95% CI, 3.03–7.66). Even after adjusting for sociodemographic factors and mental disorders, suicidality remained statistically significant. Moreover, frequent, moderate-to-severe, and long-term loneliness were all associated with increased ORs for suicidality, regardless of sociodemographic factors and mental disorders.ConclusionLoneliness was associated with suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts. This study lays the foundation for public health policymakers to establish early intervention and mental health care support for lonely people.

INTRODUCTION

Loneliness is usually subjectively defined as an unpleasant feeling resulting from people perceiving their network of social relationships to be deficient in qualitative or quantitative ways.1 Although the extent of loneliness varies between countries and age groups, the problematic levels of loneliness are experienced by a substantial proportion of the population.2 Furthermore, loneliness has attracted particular attention in the context of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Public health interventions such as stay-at-home recommendations and other social distancing measures have exacerbated loneliness, particularly among vulnerable groups.3

In recent years, the detrimental effects of loneliness have gained increasing attention in the field of mental health.4 Loneliness has been associated with negative mental health outcomes such as anxiety, depression, low resilience, and low life satisfaction.5 Specifically, previous studies have demonstrated the associations of loneliness with suicidal ideation and behavior, including large, population-representative studies.6 The unfulfilled need for belonging results in tremendous psychological pain from which suicide offers an escape.7 More recently, the interpersonal theory of suicide (IPTS) has postulated that thwarted belongingness is an important factor in triggering the desire to die by suicide.8 Although suicidal behaviors and ideation have complex biopsychological etiologies, from a public health perspective, raising awareness regarding modifiable risk factors is particularly useful because they may be used as targets for prevention.9

However, most previous studies on the association between loneliness and suicidality have conducted only on specific age groups, including adolescents, youth, or older adults. Moreover, there has been relatively little research on Asian populations.10 As cultures differ in the meaning and form of social behavior and attribute different meanings and values to interpersonal relationships, the extent of loneliness in the population varies between cultures,1112 meaning that an understanding of loneliness and its association with suicidality is insufficient in the general population. Thus, we aimed to investigate the prevalence of loneliness among the general population of Korea, associated sociodemographic factors, and the association between loneliness and suicidality using data from the National Mental Health Survey of Korea 2021 (NMHSK 2021). We also aimed to determine whether the magnitude of the aforementioned association differed according to the intensity, frequency, and duration of loneliness.

METHODS

Participants

The NMHSK 2021 is a community-based comprehensive household survey of mental disorders assessing a nationwide sample of adults aged ≥ 18 years. The data of this study was extracted from NMHSK 2021, which was conducted from June 2021 to August 2021. Participants were selected using a multi-step stratified extraction method based on data from the 2019 Population and Housing Census and household list in South Korea (Fig. 1). A total of 17 survey areas were selected considering the number of each region’s population relative to the total population of South Korea. The first stratification was stratified by state(si) or province(do), and the second stratification was stratified by dong or eup/myeon considering the characteristics of the survey area. In the case of the dong part, it was classified according to household type. The primary sampling units, enumeration districts, were sampled using the probability proportional to size sampling method. A secondary sampling unit, households, was selected through systematic random sampling. Finally, one adult aged 18–79 years was selected in each household based on the priority sampling method. The interviewers visited 13,540 households and 5,511 participants completed tablet-assisted personal interviews for NMHSK 2021 (response rate: 40.7%).

Flow chart of multi-step stratified extraction method in participant selection.

Assessment of sociodemographic factors

The following sociodemographic variables were collected from all participants: gender, age (18–29/30–44/45–59/≥ 60 years), years of education (< 10/10–12/≥ 12), area of residence (rural/urban), employment status (unemployed/part-time job/full-time job), and marital status (married/ widowed, separated, or divorced/never married).

Assessment of loneliness

We assessed loneliness using a single-item question, “How often do you feel lonely?”, by referring to the direct measure of loneliness used in the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing.13 The question had three possible responses: “hardly or never lonely,” “lonely some of the time,” and “often lonely.” Those who reported hardly lonely were considered not lonely, whereas those who responded otherwise were considered lonely.

Moreover, we assessed the intensity and duration of loneliness by asking “Are you having a hard time due to loneliness?” for intensity and “How long has it been since you started feeling lonely?” for duration. Participants were able to answer “not at all,” “somewhat,” “moderately,” or “extremely” for intensity and “less than 6 months,” “from 6 months to 1 year,” “from 1 year to 3 years,” “from 4 years to 10 years,” and “more than 10 years” for duration.

To assess the effect of the frequency of loneliness on the odds ratios (ORs) for suicidality, we grouped participants who answered “lonely some of the time” into the “occasionally group,” and those who answered “often lonely” into the “frequently group.” Additionally, to assess the effect of the intensity of loneliness on ORs for suicidality, we grouped participants who answered “not” or “slightly” together into the “mild group” and those who answered “moderately” or “extremely” together into the category of “moderate to severe group.” To assess the effect of the duration of loneliness on ORs for suicidality, we grouped participants who answered “less than 6 months” into the “short-term group” and those who answered otherwise into the “long-term group.”

Assessment of mental disorders

Mental disorders were assessed using the Korean version of the Composite International Diagnostic Interview 2.1 (K-CIDI),14 which is a comprehensively standardized diagnostic interview based on the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders.15 K-CIDI was developed and validated for Koreans according to the CIDI translation guidelines provided by the World Health Organization.16

Assessment of suicidality

We evaluated lifetime suicidality using the modified Suicide Prevention Multisite Intervention Study on Suicidal Behaviors developed by the World Health Organization.17 To assess suicidality, all participants were asked about suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts. The questions were “Have you ever seriously thought about committing suicide?” for suicidal ideation, “Have you ever made a plan for committing suicide?” for suicidal plans, and “Have you ever attempted suicide?” for suicidal attempts.

Statistical analysis

Prior to the statistical analyses, we calculated and assigned weighted values for each sample respondent to approximate the national population in terms of age and gender by each catchment area as defined in the 2020 census of the Korean National Statistical Office.

We performed χ2 tests to compare gender, age, years of education, area of residence, employment status, and marital status between lonely and non-lonely people, as well as logistic regression analyses with a backward stepwise method to estimate the ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the sociodemographic factors pertaining to loneliness. To determine the ORs and 95% CIs, logistic regression analyses were performed using suicidality as the main outcome variable and lonely people as the principal predictor, with non-lonely people as the reference group. Adjustments were made for gender, age, years of education, area of residence, employment status, and marital status. We performed additional analyses with adjustments for gender, age years of education, area of residence, employment status, marital status, mental disorders (nicotine withdrawal, nicotine dependence, alcohol abuse, alcohol dependence, dysthymic disorder, major depressive disorder [MDD], specific phobia, social phobia, panic disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, agoraphobia, post-traumatic stress disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder). We calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) to evaluate the co-linearity between independent variables. Goodness-of-fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics for Windows version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All statistical values were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Seoul National University approved the protocol of NMHSK (approval number: 2008-176-1152). All participants included in the NMHSK were fully informed about the objectives and methods of the study and provided written informed consent. Additionally, this study was approved by the IRB of the Kyungpook National University Hospital (approval number: 2022-11-023).

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics

The study sample comprised 5,511 respondents who completed the psychiatric diagnostic interview, including questions on loneliness and suicidality. Of these respondents, 32.2% reported that they were lonely. Sociodemographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. The prevalence of loneliness was similar regardless of gender and area of residence (Table 2). However, reports of loneliness were higher among participants aged ≥ 60 years than among those aged 18–29 years (43.4% vs. 17.5%). Moreover, reports of loneliness were also higher in widowed, separated, or divorced participants than in married participants (66.0% vs. 26.7%). Older age, lower educational attainment, non-full-time employment (part-time employment or unemployment), and widowed, separated, or divorced status were associated with increased ORs for loneliness in the univariate logistic regression analysis. Moreover, in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, never married status as well as older age, non-full-time employment, widowed, separated, or divorced status were also associated with increased OR for loneliness.

Association of loneliness with suicidality

Table 3 shows the association between loneliness and suicidality. Loneliness was associated with an increase in suicidal ideations (adjusted OR [aOR], 4.05; 95% CI, 3.36–4.88), suicidal plans (aOR, 4.91; 95% CI, 3.34–7.21), and suicidal attempts (aOR, 4.82; 95% CI, 3.03–7.66) after adjusting for sociodemographic factors. After adjusting for sociodemographic factors and mental disorders, the associations between loneliness with suicidal plans (aOR, 2.47; 95% CI, 2.01–3.03), suicidal plans (aOR, 1.87; 95% CI, 1.20–2.92), and suicidal attempts (aOR, 2.26; 95% CI, 1.35–3.79) remained significant, though the corresponding ORs decreasing.

In terms of intensity, moderate-to-severe loneliness (aOR 8.49, 95% CI 6.29–11.47 for suicidal ideation; aOR 10.64, 95% CI 6.48–17.48 for suicidal plans; aOR 12.89, 95% CI 7.18–23.14 for suicidal attempts) was associated with larger ORs for suicidality than mild loneliness (aOR 3.56, 95% CI 2.93–4.34 for suicidal ideation; aOR 3.26, 95% CI 2.16–4.92 for suicidal plans; aOR 3.62, 95% CI 2.18–6.00 for suicidal attempts) after adjusting for sociodemographic factors. This pattern was maintained after adjusting for mental disorders as well as sociodemographic factors.

The results of the analysis based on duration were similar to those of the analyses based on intensity and frequency. After adjusting for sociodemographic factors, long-term loneliness (aOR 4.45, 95% CI 3.66–5.41 for suicidal ideation; aOR 4.89, 95% CI 3.26–7.33 for suicidal plans; aOR 5.44, 95% CI 3.37–8.78 for suicidal attempts) was associated with larger ORs for suicidality than short-term loneliness (aOR 2.68, 95% CI 1.90–3.79 for suicidal ideation; aOR 5.42, 95% CI 3.07–9.59 for suicidal plans; aOR 3.10, 95% CI 1.35–7.12 for suicidal attempts). Furthermore, long-term loneliness was significantly associated with suicidal attempts (aOR, 2.43; 95% CI, 1.43–4.16; P = 0.001), even after adjusting for sociodemographic factors and mental disorders, whereas short-term loneliness did not show any significant results (aOR, 1.93; 95% CI, 0.80–4.67; P = 0.143).

There was no co-linearity between the independent variables in any of the analyses (VIF ≤ 2).

DISCUSSION

We investigated the prevalence of loneliness, sociodemographic risk factors, and the association between loneliness and suicidality using data from a nationally representative sample. The main findings of this study were that approximately one-third of participants reported being lonely; the sociodemographic factors associated with loneliness were age, employment, and marital status; and loneliness was associated with suicidality, regardless of sociodemographic factors and mental disorders. Particularly, frequent, moderate-to-severe, and long-term loneliness was associated with greater ORs for suicidality.

The prevalence of loneliness in studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic ranged from 24.8% to 43.0%,181920 which was similar to that reported in our study (32.2%). The variability of the prevalence of loneliness in previous studies may be attributed to differences in data collection methods (e.g., online survey, and face-to-face interview), measurement tools (e.g., scale), and sample characteristics (e.g., age distribution of subjects). However, the prevalence reported in this study was higher than that reported in studies conducted before COVID-19.1820 With the increased incidence of COVID-19, almost all countries have implemented restriction measures that include school closures, prohibition of unnecessary public or private gatherings, business hour restrictions on multi-use facilities, and travel restrictions. These measures have increased loneliness and may be the reason behind the increased prevalence in this study.21

In this study, respondents who were older, unemployed, never married, widowed, separated, or divorced were more likely to experience loneliness. Although sociodemographic factors such as age, employment status, and marital status are the known risk factors for loneliness, there have been discrepancies between studies regarding this matter.22 Some previous studies on loneliness over the life span concluded that loneliness was the highest in late adolescence, gradually decreased during middle adulthood, and subsequently increased in late adulthood.2324 Others found that loneliness increases linearly with age,25 while others found it decreasing.2627 Older people are more likely to experience many risk factors that can exacerbate or cause loneliness, including changes in income, retirement, death of loved ones, chronic illness, new sensory impairment, or worsening health,28 possibly supporting our findings. Future studies should provide more evidence regarding the causality between age and loneliness. Moreover, individuals who were unemployed and those who have never been married or married previously (widowed, separated, or divorced) were at a higher risk of loneliness, as these individuals might have poor social and family support and might be vulnerable to mental health and stress.2930

In accordance with previous studies,30 loneliness was not significantly associated with gender in our study. However, some studies have shown that females are lonelier than males,2631 whereas others have shown that loneliness is more prevalent among males.32 It is possible that the association between loneliness and gender varies by age group. A meta-analysis revealed there was no gender-based difference in loneliness among middle-aged or older adults, but loneliness among young adults was greater in men than in women.33 Such varying results make us consider that gender is not a definite determinant of loneliness and that the results may have been influenced by various sociodemographic factors and sociocultural circumstances.

Our findings supported the association between loneliness and various suicide risks, which is consistent with previous studies.34 According to IPTS, which is the most relevant theory to this study in terms of loneliness as a risk factor, intention of suicide is initiated through risk factors, including thwarted belongingness, which refers to the feeling and belief that a person of a low level of belongingness is isolated from others and does not belong to any family, group of friends, or specific groups. Thwarted belongingness emerges from two reasons: loneliness and lack of reciprocal care.35 As such, disconnectedness from others is a critical component of suicide;8 people who feel alone and socially isolated from others are at increased risk of suicide in general as they may come to believe that their existence is of little concern to others.

Cognitive factors might also contribute to explaining the connection between loneliness and suicide risks. Previous studies have suggested that lonely people are more likely to have low self-esteem, and several studies have shown a strong relationship between low self-esteem and suicidal ideation or behaviors that are independent of depression.536 Additionally, lonely people may experience more chronic stressors and also perceive everyday life as more stressful. In people who experience stressful life events, high levels of chronic stress might interact with other biological, psychological, and psychiatric factors to increase the risk of suicidal ideation or behaviors.37

Consistent with previous studies,34 we observed a dose-response association showing that frequently lonely people were more likely to have suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts. This association highlights the importance of early detection and management of loneliness. Moreover, although the addition of mental disorders including MDD to the models attenuated the ORs, the dose-response relationship between loneliness and suicidality remained significant. Similarly, long-term loneliness showed a more robust relationship with suicidality than short-term loneliness. Moreover, moderate-to-severe loneliness was more strongly associated with suicidality than mild loneliness. Therefore, these results demonstrated the importance of evaluating suicidality of lonely people. Lonely people require sensitivity from healthcare providers so that they can be assessed for their suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts early on. Furthermore, it is necessary to recognize loneliness as a social disease and intervene at a national level.

This study has some limitations. First, this study was a cross-sectional survey that obtained information on mental disorders and suicidal ideation or behaviors through the participants’ retrospective reports. Therefore, recall bias may have influenced participants’ responses. Second, although our study showed associations between loneliness and suicidality, the causal relationship between loneliness and suicidality was unknown. In order to overcome these limitations, a longitudinal cohort study, in addition to the current cross-sectional survey, is needed. Additionally, the use of a single-item measure for loneliness might be considered a weakness of our study. There is a concern that the direct question using the term loneliness may result in underreporting due to the social stigma attached to loneliness. Nevertheless, some evidence suggests that there may be a high degree of concordance between a single-item measure and the overall scores of multiple-item scales.38 The single-item measure is also the most commonly used measure in academic research.39 Nevertheless, since this study comprised a large, nationally representative sample including a diverse range of age groups, the results offer useful resources for public health policymakers to establish governmental early intervention and mental health care support for lonely people.

In conclusion, this study has shown that a large number of Korean individuals have felt lonely during the COVID-19 pandemic, and that individuals with loneliness are more likely to have a higher risk of suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts. Moreover, individuals who have frequent, moderate-to-severe, and long-term loneliness are more likely to have a much higher risk of suicidal ideation, plans, or attempts. Therefore, individuals with loneliness, whether in the community or clinical settings, require early detection and intervention because they may be at a greater risk for suicidal ideation and behaviors.
