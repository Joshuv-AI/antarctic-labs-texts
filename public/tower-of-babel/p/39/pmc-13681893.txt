Extracellular vesicles in osteoarthritis: pathological mechanisms, regenerative therapies and clinical translation
Authors: Zi-Hao Ying, Xiao-Na Xiang, Jiang-Yin Zhang, Cheng-Qi He, Hong-Chen He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a common chronic degenerative joint disease that has severe impacts on quality of life and creates a substantial socioeconomic burden. Conventional therapeutic strategies primarily focused on alleviating symptoms such as pain and functional impairment, with limited efficacy and an inability to reverse the disease process. Over the past two decades, regenerative medicine has been developed rapidly. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have garnered widespread attention and there has been an upsurge of research interest in their immunomodulatory and regenerative potential in OA. However, there remains a lack of up-to-date systematic elaboration on EVs in the pathogenesis, regenerative therapy, and clinical translation of OA. Therefore, this paper focuses on a search of the relevant literature in the past five years, revealing that EVs of multiple tissue origins in individuals work together to drive OA pathological process and various sources-derived EVs demonstrate obvious effects in ameliorating OA, with the related molecular mechanisms becoming increasingly in-depth and comprehensive. Although naturally sourced EVs have limitations in yield, cargo loading, bioavailability and so on, the application of engineering technology is expected to improve these defects, positioning minimally invasive, cell-free, targeted EV-based therapies as a promising approach. In this regard, breaking through the technical bottlenecks in EV manufacturing and establishing unified industry standards have raised stringent requirements for the further clinical translation of EVs.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05194-z.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), the most common joint disorder, is one of the troublesome health issues for elderly people. According to the recent data of The Global Burden of Disease 2021, OA had already affected nearly 600 million individuals globally up to 2020 [1]. With the population ages, it has been resulted in increasing healthcare and socioeconomic burdens. The disease is characterized by joint pain, stiffness, swelling, and functional impairment owing to pathological alterations in cartilage, bone, synovium, ligaments, muscles, and periarticular adipose tissue [2–4]. The recognized major contributing factors to OA include increasing age, obesity, and joint injury, along with other determinants such as female gender, genetic predispositions, biological and biomechanical influences [5–8]. At present, no clinically approved disease-modifying osteoarthritis agents can arrest or reverse joint pathological damage; available regimens are limited to risk factor management, oral symptomatic medication, intra-articular injection, physical rehabilitation, and total joint replacement for end-stage patients to alleviate disability rather than eradicate underlying OA lesions [2, 3, 9, 10].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are a distinct class of lipid bilayer-enclosed structures secreted by most cells and they are incapable of self-replication [11]. EVs exist in biological fluids and tissues, and carry multiple contents, mainly including proteins, nucleic acids and lipids that are involved in intercellular communication and complete material exchanges [12]. Obviously, EVs carry specific and complex biological information that produces multiple effects on receptor cells to maintain normal physiology or advance pathological changes [13], which include cancers [14, 15], neurodegenerative diseases [16], autoimmune diseases [14, 17], cardiovascular diseases [18], metabolic diseases [19] and infectious diseases [20, 21], and musculoskeletal disorders [22]. The potential of EVs in cartilage repair and regeneration is gradually appreciated, and studies on EVs for the treatment of OA are increasingly emerging. However, the heterogeneous origins and classification of EVs necessitate rigorous investigation into the consistency and efficacy of their functional outcomes.

This is a narrative review, with the search strategy presented in supplementary 1. To deepen the fundamental understanding of the role of EVs in OA development and advance the clinical translation of EV-based regenerative therapy, this review systematically elaborate the pathological roles of heterogeneous EVs as intercellular messengers during multi-tissue OA deterioration and extract actionable therapeutic targets from pathological mechanisms; comparatively summarize pros, cons and translational value of natural EVs from divergent biological origins and multiple engineered EV modification technologies for OA regenerative treatment; and finally comprehensively sort out unmet translational obstacles and propose practical future research directions to bridge preclinical findings and clinical practice.

Extracellular vesicles classification, origin, composition

Classification and origin

EVs are generally divided into three subtypes based on their biogenesis: exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies (Table 1). Exosomes, ranging in diameter from 30 to 200 nm (average 100 nm), originate from the invagination of the plasma membrane, which leads to the formation of early sorting endosomes (ESEs). ESEs can develop into late sorting endosomes (LSEs) and eventually evolve into multivesicular bodies (MVBs), containing several intraluminal vesicles (ILVs). Finally, a part of mature MVBs fuse with the plasma membrane to release the contained ILVs as exosomes [23]. Microvesicles (MVs), diameter between 100 and 1000 nm [24], are produced through outward budding of the plasma membrane. Apoptotic bodies (ApoBDs), a relatively large subtype of EVs in size (500 to 2000 nm) [25], are formed during cellular apoptosis by undergoing membrane blebbing, apoptotic membrane protrusion formation and nuclear blebs fragmentation [26].

In practice, researchers have difficulty in extracting certain subtype of EVs owing to limitations of available technology, overlap in size between subtypes, and lack of consensus on biomarkers. In this regard, the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) suggests that scholars define the subtypes to be used in research based on the specific physical and biochemical characteristics of the vesicles, for example, ISEV recommends the use of “small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) (< 100 nm or < 200 nm in diameter)” instead of “exosomes" [27]. Therefore, in this paper, the terms “sEVs” and “exosomes” may be used interchangeably to ensure consistency with the original literature.

Composition

EVs contain a variety of cargoes including proteins, nucleic acid, lipids, metabolites and amino acids [28]. However, there are differences in components between subtypes of EVs.

For exosome proteins encompassing, they contain membrane transport, fusion and cellular adhesion related proteins and molecular chaperones. Membrane transport, fusion and cellular adhesion related proteins, such as tetraspanins (including CD9, CD63, CD81, CD82, CD106), along with syntenin-1, TSG101 and ALIX—participating in exosome biogenesis and signalinng—are regarded as exosomal biomarkers [25, 29, 30]. Also, Rabs involved in vesicular transport, specific class of integrins (mainly integrin-α2 regulating cell adhesion) are present [31]. Molecular chaperones mainly include heat shock proteins (Hsp60 and Hsp70) [32]. In particular, Jeppesena et al. [31] found that the highly enriched cytoplasmic proteins including Hsp90 and cytoskeleton-associated proteins that had been assumed to be present were not detected in the CD9/CD63/CD81-positive classical exosomes. This further suggests that the uptake of cytosolic constituents during exosome biogenesis is not random, while the loading must be a highly regulated process. Besides proteins, exosomes are rich in multiple species of RNA, covering mRNA and abundant non-coding RNA (including miRNA, lncRNA, circRNA), and carry both nuclear and mitochondria DNA [19, 32]. They collaborate with each other to generate functional proteins that affect the physiopathological process of the recipient cell. Lipids are another important component of exosomes that play an integral role in the biology of these vesicles. Exosomes are rich in cholesterol (CHOL), sphingomyelin (SM), glycosphingolipids and phosphatidylserine (PS), and low in phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylinositol, having higher lipid order and membrane stability than other subtypes [33].

The composition of MVs is similar to that of exosomes. MVs likewise carry tetraspanins (e.g. CD40), while the marker protein of MVs is Annexin A1, which is not present in exosomes [31, 34]. Probably due to the origin from plasma membrane budding, MVs contain more cytoskeletal proteins, cytoplasmic components (e.g. metabolic enzymes) and plasma membrane-related proteins, such as integrins (especially integrin-β, which is virtually absent from exosomes), selectin, cell surface receptors (e.g. PD-L1, TGF) and other cell adhesion molecules [31, 35]. In terms of nucleic acid, the RNA composition of MVs is relatively random and the amount is small compared to the highly enriched miRNA containing specific sequences that come from active sorting mechanisms in exosomes [30, 35]. However, it has also been suggested that intracellular RNA can target to the plasma membrane and release as MVs [36]. The process of sorting or loading of RNA in MVs deserves further exploration. About the lipids, the distribution of MVs is closer to the plasma membrane, with lower levels of CHOL and SM, looser lipid arrangement and better fluidity [33].

As for ApoBDs, their contents include nucleosomes composed of DNA fragments and histones, residual organelle components like mitochondria DNA, GM130, apoptosis-related proteins like Annexin V, and externalized PS as their only biomarker found to date [26, 37]. The PS flipped on the surface of ApoBDs acts as a “eat me” signal that induces endocytosis by macrophage [26].

EVs in the pathological phenotypes of OA

As defined by Osteoarthritis Research Society International, OA initiates with intracellular metabolic disturbance in articular resident cells, sequentially triggering cartilage degradation, aberrant subchondral bone remodeling and low-grade synovial inflammation, ultimately resulting in irreversible joint dysfunction [38, 39]. Articular cartilage tissue resides within a complex microenvironment comprising not only chondrocytes but also diverse non-chondrocytic types, including adipocytes, synovial cells, mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), endothelial cells, and immune cells [7, 40, 41]. Intra-articular heterogeneous cell populations maintain joint homeostasis via EV-mediated paracrine/endocrine cross-talk, and pathological EV release under disease state breaks microenvironmental balance to accelerate OA deterioration [40] (Figure. 1).

EVs in abnormal remodelling of subchondral bone

Early-stage excessive osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and late-stage osteoblast overactivation-induced subchondral sclerosis are core pathological signatures of OA [42]. In the early stage, when subchondral bone is under abnormal mechanical compression, there is an elevated ratio of receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL) to osteoprotegerin (OPG) expression in bone cells, driving excessive osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption activity [43, 44]. As the disease progresses to advanced stages, increased mechanical loading prompts osteoblasts to upregulate their mineralization activity through activation of the Wnt signaling pathway [45].

Existing researches have provided preliminary insights into the role of EVs in abnormal remodelling and mineralization of subchondral bone in OA. It is confirmed that pathological chondrocyte/adipocyte -derived EVs carry pathogenic miRNAs (miR-125, miR-3074-5p) to disturb osteoblast-osteoclast homeostasis and induce subchondral bone loss by targeting sirtuin-6 or SMAD4 [46, 47]. Therefore, although therapeutic EVs fail to directly target RANKL or OPG for bone protection owing to poor cartilage penetration at the early stage of OA, they hold promise for alleviating bone loss via modulating the global expression of endogenous EVs within the joint microenvironment. Gu et al. [48] found that EVs derived from osteoblast subtypes exacerbate subchondral bone remodeling in OA progression. Regulated by Wnt pathway, osteoblasts are heterogeneous in osteogenic differentiation and the activation of mitophagy will provide calcium phosphate for further mineralization. EVs from three OB subtypes (endothelial, stromal, and mineralizing osteoblasts) inheriting the heterogeneity can promote osteogenic with functional miRNAs and calcium phosphate, resulting in bone sclerosis. This also provides partial evidence for conservative treatment of non-early-stage OA via modulation of the Wnt signaling pathway.

Generally, studies that focus on the impact of EVs on subchondral bone destruction in osteoarthritis are inadequate. The precise mechanisms of how subchondral bone crosstalk with other articular tissues, and in particular how it affects subchondral bone sclerosis and bone loss through EVs during the initiation and development of OA remain incompletely elucidated, necessitating further investigations.

EVs in synovial inflammation

The synovium represents a critical tissue involved in early OA pathogenesis, which undergoes extensive histological alterations during this stage, including synovial hypertrophy, hyperplasia, angiogenesis, inflammation, and fibrosis [49, 50]. OA synovitis manifests as chronic low-grade inflammation driven by crosstalk between the innate immune system and multiple inflammatory mediators. The innate immune system recognizes pathogens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby activating innate immune cells, transcription factors, and downstream inflammatory mediators. These components act through intricate signaling cascades to initiate and sustain inflammatory responses [51]. DAMPs identified in OA primarily consist of cartilage debris, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation products, secreted intracellular and plasma proteins, and microcrystals [52, 53]. Multiple functional molecules are tightly associated with OA synovitis, encompassing cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, as well as adipokines, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and carboxypeptidase B [51, 54]. Stimulation of synovial cells by the aforementioned DAMPs and inflammatory mediators modulates the biosynthesis and secretion of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors [55].

EVs are pivotal regulators of synovitis-associated inflammatory signaling in OA. Compared with those derived from normal synovial fluid, exosomes isolated from OA synovial fluid markedly induce M1 macrophage polarization and the subsequent secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), CCL-8, CCL-1, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [56]. Mechanistically, synovium-derived EVs carry miR-182, which targets and binds to FOXO3, thereby facilitating the secretion of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α and triggering inflammatory responses in synovial mesenchymal cells, particularly in early-stage OA [57]. Additionally, elevated exosome secretion is observed in fibroblast-like synoviocytes and chondrocytes under OA pathological conditions. Exosomes derived from inflamed FLSs enhance glycolysis and drive M1 macrophage polarization, ultimately inducing synovitis and chondrocyte dysfunction characteristic of OA, with HIF1A serving as a core transcription factor governing this process [58]. Moreover, OA chondrocyte-secreted EVs can infiltrate synovial tissues and macrophages to amplify mature IL-1β production in macrophages. Specifically, these EVs deliver miR-449a-5p to suppress ATG4B expression, thereby inhibiting macrophage autophagy. Reduced autophagy further accumulates mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, potentiates inflammasome activation, and accelerates IL-1β maturation, ultimately exacerbating synovial inflammation [59]. It is evident that the inflammatory process of OA is regulated by multiple EV subtypes via diverse signaling pathways. Investigations into M1 macrophage polarization inspire the development of anti-inflammatory EV candidates (such as M2 macrophage-derived EV) to reverse macrophage subtype shift, suppress FLS hyperactivity and improve synovial lymphatic drainage, representing a mainstream therapeutic strategy for inflammatory OA subtype. However, in future research, identifying one or several core signaling pathways governing inflammation, and loading corresponding therapeutic molecules in a targeted manner will improve the anti-inflammatory efficacy of EV preparations.

EVs in cartilage degeneration

The degeneration of articular cartilage is mainly characterized by the dysfunction of chondrocytes and ECM degradation. Dysfunctional chondrocytes undergo programmed cell death or senescence [39, 60–63]. Dysregulated chondrocytes release excessive matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS) disrupting the homeostasis between ECM anabolism and catabolism [64]. ECM degradation products are released into the synovial fluid as DAMPs that trigger the synovial inflammation, then various cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), chemokines, and adipokines) converge to further promote the activities of MMPs and ADAMTSs, creating a vicious cycle of cartilage damage and tissue inflammation [40]. Cartilage calcification, another symbol of OA is formed by chondrocytes undergoing physiopathologic processes (e.g., mitosis, hypertrophy, apoptosis), leading to inflammation, cell death and cartilage catabolism [65].

Based on the pathological manifestations of cartilage degeneration in OA, we summarize several mechanistic axes underlying the involvement of EVs in this process, laying a theoretical foundation for research exploring the efficacy of EVs in alleviating cartilage degeneration.

EVs in inflammatory regulatory axis

EVs secreted from inflamed chondrocytes, activated synoviocytes and peripheral adipose tissue deliver non-coding RNAs to regulate pro-inflammatory signaling pathway, triggering chondrocyte inflammatory response. For example, exosomal PCGEM1 (a kind of lncRNA) from OA-FLSs facilitated IL-1β-induced apoptosis and cartilage matrix degradation in chondrocytes by sequestering miR-142-5p and upregulating RUNX2 [66]. Synovial tissue-derived EVs (STEVs) significantly promoted the activation of NF-κB signaling pathway, with the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α and COX-2) upregulated [67]. Notably, synovial fluid-derived EVs (SFEVs) exert stage-dependent dual effects. The chondrocyte transcriptome induced by SFEVs from individuals with mild/moderate OA is associated with the upstream activation of corticosterone, a potent anti-inflammatory mediator, while in individuals with severe OA, SFEVs promote the expression and release of chondrocyte catabolic mediators and pro-inflammatory mediators with upregulation of CRTAC1, COL6A3, TNC, RUNX2 and CXCL5, increase secretion of IL-6, MMP1, MMP3 and MMP13, and pro-nociceptive mediators (NGF and Substance P) [68]. This finding provides implications for staged clinical management of OA. At the early disease phase with subtle cartilage inflammation, cartilage-targeting/anti-inflammatory-phenotype EVs should not be administrated indiscriminately; instead, intra-articular homeostasis can be preserved via the modulation of extrinsic risk factors. For patients with prominent inflammatory manifestations, bioengineered EVs with pro-inflammatory gene knockdown or anti-inflammatory gene overexpression can be exploited to restrain inflammatory responses.

EVs in chondrocyte apoptotic/pyroptotic regulatory axis

Pathological EVs from intra- and extra-articular sources deliver proapoptotic signals across joint compartments. Inflammatory macrophage-derived EVs initiate pyroptosis via the activation of caspase 11–GSDMD signaling in chondrocytes, which contribute to the impairment of chondrocyte function and the release of catabolic factors and DAMPs, leading to the development of cartilage regeneration [69]. MiR-182 in STEVs induces chondrocyte apoptosis by regulating FOXO3, and its expression increases with disease progression. In individuals with severe OA, the apoptosis signal is strongest [57]. Serum EVs deliver miR-133a-3p to modulated the downstream SIRT1/NF-κB pathway-mediated pyroptotic cell death in OA.

EVs in chondrocyte senescence regulatory axis

Compared with healthy donors, the levels of connexin43 (Cx43) protein in EVs released by OA individual chondrocytes were significantly elevated. Chondrocytes-derived EVs containing high levels of Cx43 (hCx43-Cho-sEVs) markedly induced chondrocyte senescence and proinflammatory expression. Cx43 increased ß-galactosidase activity (a marker of senescence), promoted NF-kß nuclear translocation, and stimulated the secretion of IL-6, IL-1β, COX-2, and MMP-3. Cx43-Cho-sEVs could also spread cellular senescence in different bone and synovial cells, and may be involved in the spread of OA from one joint to another [70]. Infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP)-derived exosomes are closely associated with the development and progression of knee OA, as IPFP-Exos significantly promoted chondrocyte ECM catabolism and induced cellular senescence, where the highly enriched let-7b-5p and let-7c-5p in IPFP-Exos are essential to mediate detrimental effects by directly decreasing senescence negative regulator, lamin B receptor (LBR) [71].

EVs in anabolic-catabolic metabolic axis

EVs can regulate anabolic/catabolic pathways by delivering non-coding RNA. Inflammatory chondrocytes-derived EVs secrete circRNA “circ_0001846” to sponge miR-149-5p that targeting Wnt5B, promoting ECM degradation [72]. The abnormality of mechanical stress in OA induces osteocytes in subchondral bone to secrete EVs that accelerate cartilage metabolic dysregulation, where the miR-23b-3p promotes cartilage catabolism and inhibits anabolism by targeting OTUD4 [73]. Furthermore, it is reported that damaged articular chondrocytes deliver pathogenic lncRNA LOC102546541 packaged EVs to normal articular chondrocytes, amplifying the degradation of the ECM [74].

EVs in cartilage calcification regulatory axis

Abnormal biomechanical stimulation leads to increased exosomes secretion by chondrocytes and decreases the level of MGP (inhibits mineralization) in exosomes and increases NPP1, TNAP (both of them act to control the presence of substrate during the mineralization stage) expression to accelerate abnormal cartilage calcification [75]; in the early stage of OA, blockage of the autophagy flux due to histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6)-mediated microtubule destabilization results in the release of autophagy-derived microtubule-associated proteins 1 A/1B light chain 3B (LC3)-positive EVs, initiating pathological cartilage calcification in OA [76]. It is therefore evident that pathological cartilage calcification in early-stage OA deserves close attention. Developing engineered EVs that target and modify relevant enzymes and proteins to inhibit cartilage calcification is of great clinical significance.

Natural EVs from different sources for OA therapy

In existing research, the sources of EVs mainly include human or murine stem cells, differentiated cells, body fluids, and tissues. To overcome limitations in EV acquisition, researchers have also attempted to obtain therapeutic EVs from other organisms. Based on the characteristics of OA phenotype, therapeutic EVs primarily exert anti-inflammatory and chondroprotective effects through a series of molecular mechanisms; in addition, EVs improve lymphatic function, alleviate synovial fibrosis, promote the balance of bone remodeling, and so on (Figure. 2).

Mesenchymal stem/stromal cell-derived EVs

In recent years, mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) have been the focus of research in OA treatment due to their regenerative, immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory capacities [77]. MSCs-derived EVs are thought to inherit the therapeutic potential of parent cells while reduce the risks of cell transplantation. It’s worth mentioning that, in the existing literature, mesenchymal stem cells are often confused with mesenchymal stromal cells. Researchers basically follow the minimal criteria issued by ISCT’s MSC committee in their study, which is for mesenchymal stromal cells [78]. Therefore, to avoid terminological confusion, “MSC” is used in this paper to represent the common abbreviation for both types of cells, and we maintain consistency with the original documents in its entirety. From a certain perspective, mesenchymal stem cells may have a stronger differentiation potential [79], while mesenchymal stromal cells could play an important role in regulating the microenvironment by secreting cytokines, such as immunomodulation [80], and together they hold therapeutic promise.

Bone marrow derived mesenchymal stem/stromal cells

Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (BMSCs) are the most widely adopted source for generating therapeutic EVs. Their secreted EVs (BMSC-EVs) inherit the core chondroprotective and immunomodulatory activities of parental cells yet reduce safety concerns linked to live-cell transplantation [81], with key preclinical advances consolidated in Table 2. As summarized from recent in vitro chondrocyte assays and rodent OA models, BMSC-EVs rely on encapsulated miRNAs and lncRNAs to modulate PI3K-AKT, NF-κB, m6A and glutamine metabolism pathways, suppressing inflammation, aberrant cell death and extracellular matrix breakdown.

Nevertheless, the compiled preclinical evidence reveals notable translational bottlenecks. Most available findings originate from small rodent-based experiments, with validations in large animals and human tissues still sparse. Heterogeneous EV quantification, purification workflows and administered dosages across publications also hinder unified standardization, while existing mechanistic analyses predominantly focus on individual RNA-signaling pairs without accounting for intricate crosstalk among diverse EV cargos within complex articular microenvironments. Even so, these researches offer actionable clues for translational development. Verified functional RNAs can act as potency biomarkers to screen qualified GMP-grade EV products, and characterized beneficial molecular targets facilitate targeted EV engineering to enhance therapeutic potency.

Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem/stromal cells

Compared with BMSCs, adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ASCs) are more economical [79]. In inflamed conditions, ASCs release vesicles carrying chondroprotective mRNAs that readily penetrate cartilage [92], making ASC-derived EVs (ASCs-EVs) promising therapeutic carriers for OA. A panel of RNAs within ASCs-EVs exert anti-inflammatory and chondro-regenerative effects through multiple pathways. MiR-376c-3p inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin pathway via Wnt3/Wnt9a, miR-574-3p modulates the cysteine-rich motor neuron protein 1/BMP pathway to limit chondrocyte hypertrophy and inflammation, miR-338-3p represses RUNX2, and tsRNA-12,391 promotes mitophagy by maintaining PINK1 stability [93–96]. In addition, ASC-derived EVs protect mitochondrial function by activating mitophagy-related signaling and trigger metabolic reprogramming of dendritic cells in OA [97, 98]. Among the fat tissues throughout the body, the infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) is the most common source of ASCs-EVs, which is probably related to its rich ASC content and stronger chondrogenic ability [97]. In OA mice, IPFP MSC-derived EVs can alleviate pain, and inhibit cartilage degeneration, osteophyte formation and synovial inflammation [98]. Moreover, IPFP MSC-derived EVs can reduce proliferation of synoviocytes [99], which means inhibiting synovial fibrosis may be a potential advantage of EVs over cell therapies.

Synovial mesenchymal stem/stromal cells

Synovial mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (SMSCs)-derived EVs are characterized by relatively convenient acquisition and considerable production, and not easily affected by donor age [100]. Evidence suggests that SMSC-EVs are enriched with mitochondria and they could traverse the dense interstitial layer and migrate towards the deeper cartilage [101]. The author believes that these properties render SMSC-EVs an ideal natural carrier for engineered EV research.

Mechanically, mRNAs carried by SMSC-EVs can alleviate cartilage damage via canonical signaling pathways (Wnt and AKT pathways) [102]. Meanwhile, they target phosphatase and tensin homologue (PTEN) and HMGB1 to suppress chondrocyte apoptosis and inflammation [103, 104]. Studies have revealed that SMSC-EVs exerted dual effects in the treatment of OA. On the one hand, the Wnt5a and Wnt5b carried by exosomes activated YAP by replacing the Wnt signaling pathway, promoting the proliferation and migration of chondrocytes; on the other hand, the activation of YAP significantly inhibited the expression of SOX9 and led to the inhibition of ECM formation. To eliminate this side effect, researchers attempted to produce SMSC-Exos overexpressing miR-140-5p or miR-155-5p and achieved success in animal experiments. The overexpression of miR-140-5p rescued SOX9 and ECM synthesis while retaining the advantages of SMSC-Exos [105], and miR-155-5p promote ECM secretion by targeting RUNX2 [106].

Overall, although SMSC-EVs have notable limitations in intrinsic therapeutic potency, their multiple biological advantages endow them with potential for engineering and clinical translation.

Umbilical cord mesenchymal stem/stromal cells

Among the various sources of MSCs, the human umbilical cord is the most widely used in preclinical and clinical trials due to the richness and availability, noninvasive collection method, and strong proliferative ability of human umbilical cord MSCs (hUCMSCs) in vitro [107]. Current studies have yielded a more comprehensive understanding of the therapeutic phenotypes of hUCMSC-derived EVs (hUCMSC-EVs) compared with other MSC-EVs. These vesicles exert immunomodulatory effects, facilitate cartilage repair and protect subchondral bone, with their functions supported by well-characterized canonical signaling pathways (including Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K-AKT, STAT1, METTL3-NLRP3) [108–112]. Meanwhile, relevant emerging mechanisms have also been actively explored. For example, UCMSC-EVs significantly reduce osteoclast (OC) activity by transferring let-7a-5p, further suppressing the expression of osteoclast integrin β3 (ITGβ3), thereby protecting both subchondral bone and cartilage [113]. In addition, Tang et al. [114] treated chondrocytes with 5%, 10%, 15%µg/mL hUCMSC-sEVs and hUCMSCs respectively, and the 15%µg/mL sEVs group showed the greatest proliferative and migratory capacities, and they also found that many proteins connected to cartilage repair were more abundant in sEVs, and compared to hUCMSCs, the upregulated proteins in hUCMSC-sEVs were mostly involved in the regulation of immune effector process, ECM organization, PI3K-AKT signaling pathways, and Rap1 signaling pathway. Studies on dosage, concentration and proteomics have to some extent promoted the clinical translation of hUCMSCs-EVs. Following a series of preclinical studies, Wang et al. [115] confirmed that hUCMSC-EVs injection treatment of OA individuals did not cause any adverse consequences, and a certain degree of effectiveness was found in the comparison of clinical scores and MRI examinations before and after treatment. Figueroa-Valdés et al. [109] developed clinical-grade EVs based on preclinical experiments and the initial assessment of the safety of the clinical-grade product via IA administration in a first-in-human study showed no adverse effects after a 12-month follow-up period. Although these clinical trials have demonstrated preliminary safety profiles, they lack supporting evidence from large animal studies. This renders current clinical explorations somewhat empirical, and it remains challenging to verify the efficacy and long-term safety of EV therapies via large-sample randomized controlled trials.

Dental pulp stem cells and urine-derived stem/stromal cells

Compared to BMSCs, dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) are considered to possess greater proliferative capacity [116]. In Lin et al. [117] cellular experiments, DPSC‑derived exosomes (DPSC-Exos) promoted the mRNA expression of ACAN, COL2α1 and SOX9, in IL‑1β‑treated human chondrocytes and reduced their apoptosis. Fu et al. [118] isolated DPSC-Exos and determined the therapeutic effects in mice knee OA models. The results indicated that DPSC-Exos effectively improved abnormal remodeling of subchondral bone, inhibited the occurrence of bone sclerosis and osteophytosis, and alleviated cartilage degradation and synovial inflammation. As for the mechanism, DPSC-derived exosomes inhibited osteoclast activation in vivo by suppressing the transient receptor potential vanilloid 4, which is activated during OA progression.

Urine-derived stem cells (USCs) have gained attention in recent years due to their non-invasive acquisition. USC-Exos provides partial anti-swelling and chondroprotective effects [119, 120]. Besides, USC-Exos suppressed osteoclastogenic activity in the subchondral bone layer of the condylar cartilage and reduced apoptosis in the rat TMJ. Nevertheless, current research on USC-EVs for OA treatment is scarce. Further evidence is needed to support its therapeutic outcomes, particularly via parallel comparisons with conventional MSC-EVs.

In general, MSC-EVs from different sources exert chondroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects via several canonical signaling pathways (PI3K-AKT, Wnt/β-catenin) and each type of them have their own advantages and disadvantages. BMSC-EVs are considered to possess stronger chondrogenic potential. However, BMSC-EVs suffer from difficulties in parent cell acquisition and low production yields. Meanwhile, the limited availability of dental pulp poses a challenge to its emergence as a mainstream source of EVs. In contrast, ADSC-EVs and SMSC-EVs are relatively easier to obtain and exhibit higher yields. Besides, USC-EVs feature abundant raw materials and can be harvested non-invasively, but their immunomodulatory and cartilage regenerative capacities require further validation. Additionally, the age the donor is an important factor, as the therapeutic efficacies of both BMSC-EVs and ADSC-EVs derived from aged individuals are significantly diminished, while SMSC-EVs will not be easily affected [100]. As such, UCMSC-EVs, for which relevant clinical studies have already been conducted, combine the aforementioned advantages while avoiding major drawbacks, making them a relatively promising option for MSC-EV therapy in OA.

Mechanistically, DPSCs- and USCs-derived EVs have relatively few validated therapeutic mechanisms. In contrast, MSC-EVs from other sources have been extensively investigated, with studies covering canonical signaling pathways and extending to the gene expression level. Discrepancies in reported therapeutic mechanisms across literature are largely attributed to experimental heterogeneity. Findings from different studies can be mutually referenced to compensate for the limitations of current evidence.

Embryonic stem cells and induced pluripotent stem cells

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) have the infinite proliferation and self-renewal ability, contributing to the production of a large quantity of standard and functional exosomes that meet the criteria of clinical applications [121]. Feng et al. [122] demonstrated that ESCs-derived sEVs (ESC-sEVs) ameliorated senescent phenotype and cartilage destruction in both non-early-stage posttraumatic OA and naturally aged OA mice, as well as human end-stage OA cartilage explants, where FOXO1A-autophagy axis played an important role in the anti-aging effects, revealing the high translational value of ESC-sEVs-based therapy in non-early-stage OA treatment. ESCs are also considered a promising source of MSCs, providing a stable supply of MSCs. Wang et al. [123] proved that exosomes from human ESC-induced MSCs maintained the chondrocyte phenotype by increasing COL2 synthesis and decreasing ADAMTS5 expression.

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) can be induced from patient-specific adult somatic cells, and are similar to ESCs in terms of morphology, self-renewal, and differentiation capacity [124, 125]. Therefore, they also have the ability to form MSCs. Compared with adult MSCs, iPSCs exhibit superior characteristics in terms of cell proliferation, immune regulation, cytokine profiles, production of exosomes, and bioactive paracrine factors secretion [126, 127]. In the comparison of exosomes secreted by iPSC-derived MSCs (iMSC-Exos) and SMSC-Exos for OA treatment, iMSC-Exos showed better therapeutic effects. Histological and immunohistochemical analysis results indicated that OA mice in iMSC-Exos group had smoother cartilage surfaces, more regular cell organization, proteinoglycan content approaching normal levels, and typical transparency characteristics similar to normal cartilage; In cellular experiments, iMSC-Exos treatment resulted in more significant proliferation and migration of chondrocytes [128]. It should be noted that, despite cellular reprogramming, iMSC-sEVs from OA individuals retain the OA-specific protein signatures of the donor, and the presence of proteins associated with cartilage degradation in iMSC-sEVs may negatively impact their regenerative and immunomodulatory potential, thus limiting their therapeutic efficacy [129]. Therefore, the therapeutic potential of autologous iPSCs (even iMSCs)-derived EVs for OA needs to be further evaluated.

EVs from differentiated cells

Immune cells

Macrophages play an important role in the pathogenesis and progression of OA [55]. They can be polarized into proinflammatory M1 or anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes in response to different stimuli. M2 macrophage-derived exosomes (M2-Exos) can suppress inflammation, thus helping to alleviate joint inflammation and promote cartilage repair [130]. M2-Exos reprogram M1 macrophages into the M2 phenotype in OA models via NOD-like receptors (NLR), TNF, NF-κB and TLR pathways [131, 132]. Meanwhile, they exert chondroprotective effects by targeting PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade or COL10A1 [131, 133]. In addition, as impaired drainage function of the synovial lymphatic system contributes to OA advancement, Song et al. [134] proposed that polarized macrophages modulate lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) activity via exosome release in synovial tissue, with M2 macrophage-derived exosomes promoting LECs proliferation, migration, and lymphangiogenesis, thus alleviating OA progression.

Neutrophils are the earliest immune cells to enter the synovial tissue following joint injury and may play a vital role in the homeostatic regulation of early synovitis [135, 136]. Research indicated that neutrophils-derived microvesicles can be internalized by FLS to inhibit the secretion of a broad spectrum of proinflammatory cytokines (including IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1, IFNγ, MIP-1β, but excluding IL-2, IL-4) following TNFα stimulation [137]. Results from Kitahara et al. [138] showed that TGF-β-stimulated neutrophils-derived EVs exerted anabolic and chondroprotective effects by promoting secreted frizzled-related protein 5 expression.

Collectively, M2-Exos serve as a classic paradigm for developing therapeutics based on the inflammatory mechanisms underlying osteoarthritis. With substantial preclinical pharmacological validation, they are regarded as a viable candidate with great translational potential. Existing research reveals that neutrophils-derived EVs are activated largely by pro-inflammatory cytokines. Given the regulatory role of neutrophils in maintaining joint homeostasis during early OA, these findings provide valuable implications for stratified treatment research of OA. Despite insufficient supporting data and ongoing exploratory investigations into their therapeutic use, neutrophil-derived EVs are expected to exert favorable effects against early OA inflammation in future studies.

Osteochondral origin

Cartilage progenitor cells (CPCs) can be harvested from the superficial layer of articular cartilage and possess chondrogenic potential [139]. Research by Feng et al. [140] demonstrated that cartilage progenitor cells derived EVs (CPC-EVs) exhibited chondro-protective effects, and promoted matrix anabolism as well as inhibited inflammatory response at least partially via blocking STAT3 activation. Lee et al. [141] isolated exosomes from human fetal cartilage progenitor cells (hFCPCs). They found that hFCPC-Exos delivery miR-125b-5p, which down-regulates the IL-1β/TRAF6/NF-κB pathway, to exhibit anti-inflammatory and cartilage regeneration effects.

MVs released by osteoclasts displayed potent pro-chondrogenic, pro-osteogenic, and anti-inflammatory activities that were unique to osteoclastic MVs and were not shared by osteoclastic ApoBDs, exosomes, or MVs from other cell types [142].

Both CPCs and osteoclasts represent relatively underexplored sources of EVs with unique characteristics. CPC-derived EVs may possess strong cartilage-targeting capacity, while osteoclast-derived MVs exhibit osteogenic effects. Nevertheless, relevant experimental evidence is still lacking, and no studies have compared their performance with EVs from other cell types.

Body fluid-derived and tissue-isolated EVs

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous blood product obtained by centrifugation of autologous blood, containing a high concentration of platelets, a large number of growth factors and cytokines. PRP-derived Exos (PRP-Exos) are considered to have great potential in the field of tissue repair and regeneration, with anti-inflammatory effect and positive regulation of cell biological activity [143]. Liu et al. [144] compared PRP with PRP-Exos, and the results showed that PRP and PRP-Exos both inhibited the release of TNF-α and there was no statistically significant difference between the two. However, PRP-Exos are superior to PRP in chondrocyte protection; Although both can reverse the increased expression of β-catenin, RUNX2, and Wnt5a in IL-1β-treated chondrocytes, PRP-Exos are more effective. In animal models, the effect of PRP-Exos was obviously better than PRP by chondrocyte count and Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scoring system. Besides, Liao et al. [145] revealed the specific role and mechanism of PRP-Exos in synovial inflammation. PRP-Exos alleviated pain behaviors and synovial inflammation improved synovial lymphatic function and promoted lymphangiogenesis by enhancing the proliferation, migration, and tube formation ability of LECs via regulating the PI3K/Akt pathway.

Currently, PRP has been widely adopted in clinical regenerative therapy. However, it contains a variety of growth factors including fibroblast growth factors, vascular endothelial growth factors and nerve growth factors, leading to unpredictable tissue regeneration outcomes. For instance, previous studies have confirmed that PRP induces synovial hyperplasia and fibrosis [146]. Accordingly, PRP-Exos are expected to serve as a next-generation regenerative therapeutic alternative to PRP. Notably, autologous blood can be easily obtained in large quantities compared with other cell sources. Additionally, EVs derived from other blood products such as young human plasma, platelet-poor plasma and hyperacute serum, remains in the exploratory stage [147–149].

The chorion is tissue originating from the early embryo that encapsulates multiple growth factors, aiding in tissue healing, regeneration, and repair [150]. Fecskeova et al. [151] compared the biological effects of EVs from chorionic villus tissue (CV-Ti) with chorionic villus mesenchymal stem cells (CV-MSC) and platelet poor-plasma (PPP), and found that CV-Ti-EVs and PPP-EVs showed particularly beneficial effect on the inflammatory process, as their biological impact surpasses that of CV-MSC-EVs.

EVs derived from other organisms

Pieters et al. [152] were the first to utilize milk-derived EVs (mEVs) for OA therapy. They incubated human cartilage explants and human chondrocytes with mEVs, discovering that mEVs could reduce sulfated glycosaminoglycan release and inhibit metalloproteinase-1 expression in human cartilage explants. These alterations are probably associated with TGF-β and miR-148a. To date, mEVs represent the sole EV formulation investigated for oral administration against OA. Researches indicated that oral administration of mEVs ameliorated OA via modulating gut microbiota [153].

Velvet antler is currently the only known mammalian organ capable of complete annual regeneration, which is initiated by antler stem cells. Compared with other types of mammalian stem cells, antler stem cells are much easier to acquire and exhibit higher proliferative and regenerative capacity [154]. In vitro and in vivo studies by Lei et al. [155] demonstrated that antler stem cell-EVs attenuate senescence in human MSCs and improve post-traumatic OA in mice.

Although differences may exist in biogenesis, plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PELNs) have been confirmed to exhibit structural and functional similarities to mammalian-derived exosomes and PELNs are more readily available and scalable for mass production compared to animal exosomes [156, 157]. Yıldırım et al. [158] isolated PELVs from tomatoes and lemons, and demonstrated their cartilage-regeneration capacity. Rashidi et al. [159] investigated the safety and efficacy of grapefruit juice-derived PELNs (GEVs), showing an enhancement in human chondrocyte migratory activity by more than 13% and reduction in inflammation and oxidative stress.

Taken together, antler stem cell-EVs shows stable in vivo repair effect, but high acquisition costs constitute a major obstacle to its clinical translation. Interspecies biological equivalence and human long-term safety of mEVs remain unvalidated. plant-derived grapefruit/lemon nanovesicles only complete in vitro and small rodent verification, lacking standardized preparation specification and corresponding drug regulatory classification, which means these plant vesicles are far from clinical transformation and stay as exploratory research objects.

Engineered EVs for OA therapy

Although EVs represent a promising therapeutic approach, current limitations in yield, purity, circulatory stability, targeting capabilities and the retention following in vivo administration significantly constrain their efficacy for treating OA [160, 161]. The engineering of EVs can be categorized into exogenous and endogenous approaches. Endogenous EV engineering refers to methods for obtaining modified EVs through processing of parental cells, while exogenous EV engineering involves modifying or altering isolated EVs directly. Moreover, biomedical materials are increasingly being applied to assist EVs in their therapeutic role; artificially assembled EVs are also emerged in this field (Figure. 3).

Endogenous EV engineering

Endogenous EV engineering can be broadly understood as directly “manipulating” the parent cells, hence it is also referred to as indirect EV engineering. Endogenous engineering strategies can be divided into two major categories: modifying the culture conditions of parent cells and cell transfection. Among them, approaches for adjusting cell culture conditions include optimizing medium components, altering culture models (such as 3D culture and co-culture), as well as physical and biochemical stimulation. These culture-modulating approaches essentially mimic the intrinsic self-regulatory and reparative programs activated in native cells in response to adverse microenvironments. Such interventions frequently boost vesicle secretion by parent cells and enrich therapeutic molecules associated with immunomodulation and tissue repair within EV cargo. In contrast, transfection strategies upregulate the expression of specific molecules in secreted EVs, conferring superior target specificity and intentional modulation, yet few studies have reported elevated EV yields following transfection. Endogenous engineering techniques feature simple operational procedures and favorable preservation of intact vesicle architecture. Nevertheless, they are hampered by limited regulatory precision, incomplete understanding of EV biogenesis under intervention, and insufficient mechanistic interpretation of their enhanced therapeutic potency. To advance their clinical translation, future research should not only perform head-to-head comparisons of diverse stimuli and transfection protocols, as well as evaluate the practical utility of endogenous versus exogenous engineering strategies, but also thoroughly dissect EV biogenesis under distinct culture manipulations, identify molecules with drastically altered cargo loading, and characterize signaling pathways that are markedly activated or suppressed. Exogenous EV engineering

There are two major bottlenecks restricting the application of natural EVs in OA treatment. First, natural EVs contain only limited abundances of functional molecules, resulting in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes for certain OA phenotypes. Second, the dense cartilage extracellular matrix creates substantial steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion, which prevent EVs from penetrating deep cartilage layers and trap them merely at the superficial region [189, 190]. Meanwhile, abundant capillaries and lymphatic vessels within the synovium rapidly eliminate free intra-articular EVs, drastically shortening their local residence time [191].

Loading small-molecule drugs, nucleic acids, peptides and other bioactive agents into the lumens of EVs via physical approaches such as co-incubation, sonication and electroporation compensates for the insufficient endogenous bioactive factors in native EVs, enabling precise regulation of chondrocyte metabolism, inflammatory response and apoptotic phenotypes. Li et al. [192] encapsulated chitosan into ASC-EVs by mixing chitosan with EVs in an equimolar ratio, yielding EVs-COS and proved that EVs-COS could elevate chondrocyte viability, suppress apoptosis, cellular senescence and matrix degradation, facilitating comprehensive cartilage repair. Zhang et al. [193] mixed puerarin with BMSC-Exos and subjected the mixture to ultrasonic treatment, obtaining puerarin-loaded exosomes (Pue-Exos), which could increase the solubility of Pue and enhance drug uptake by chondrocytes to further promoted cartilage repair. Beyond small-molecule drugs, loading nucleic acids enables pathological regulation at the genetic level. Multiple studies have encapsulated MMP13-targeted nucleic acid sequences into chondrocyte-affinity peptide (CAP)-modified EVs, which not only prolong the intra-articular retention of vesicles but also stably suppress MMP13 expression to facilitate cartilage matrix remodeling [199, 200]. Liu et al. [194] delivered NLRP3-targeted miR-223 into hUCMSC-EVs via electroporation, achieving potent suppression of inflammatory responses. Feng’s [195] research team loaded siMDM2 into MSC-sEVs and exerted anti-chondrocyte senescence effects by modulating the MDM2-p53 signaling pathway.

Nevertheless, cargo loading alone fails to address the poor cartilage penetration and rapid in vivo clearance of unmodified EVs. Modulating the physicochemical properties of EV membranes and conjugating targeting peptides through lipid insertion, copper-free click chemistry, genetically engineered membrane protein modification and other strategies can improve the bioavailability of EVs by optimizing their spatial distribution inside joints. To overcome the electrostatic barrier posed by the cartilage matrix, researchers modified EV surfaces with the cationic amphiphilic macromolecule PPD to reverse the negative surface charge of vesicles, generating PPD-EVs. This modification strategy largely preserves the intact membrane structure and internal bioactive cargos of EVs, conferring superior stability within the anionic in vivo microenvironment [140, 196]. For specific chondrocyte-targeted delivery, modification with cartilage-affinity peptides (CAP and the COL2-targeting peptide WYRGRL) represents the predominant approach [194, 195]. Sun et al. [196] conjugated CAP onto hUCMSC-derived small EVs (sEVs) via copper-free click chemistry. Compared with unmodified vesicles, CAP-sEVs exhibit enhanced chondrocyte-specific accumulation, markedly prolonged intra-articular retention time, and correspondingly improved in vivo chondroprotective efficacy.

Monodimensional modification only addresses defects related to either delivery or therapeutic efficacy alone. In contrast, the dual engineering strategy combining surface modification and cargo loading simultaneously achieves targeted retention, improved cartilage penetration, and precise intervention against pathological signaling pathways, rendering it a modification approach with certain translational potential at present.

Zhang et al. [197] reported for the first time the anchoring of cationic arginine, lysine rich short length cartilage peptide carriers and avidin to the lipid bilayer membrane via simple lipid insertion, enabling exosomes to permeate cartilage at high concentrations through the entire layer. And the encapsulation of eGFP mRNA demonstrated that these exosomes can be utilized for mRNA delivery, which provided a theoretical basis for the combined application of targeted surface modification and miRNA loading. Kim et al. [198] attached positively charged insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) to the surface of the UCMSC-derived EVs carrying siMMP13 to give IGF-si-EVs, which possess chondroprotective effect, cartilage adhesion ability and also successfully inhibit MMP13.

Accumulating literature has clearly validated the value of exogenous EV engineering in overcoming the clinical delivery and therapeutic limitations of native EVs. Nevertheless, prominent drawbacks persist in current research. Most modification strategies have only been validated in small animal models, and standardized head-to-head comparisons between different modifying molecules and cargo combinations are lacking, making it difficult to screen out optimal modification systems. Furthermore, there is insufficient quantitative research regarding how various fabrication procedures affect EV yield, membrane integrity, and the loss of internal bioactive cargos.

Biomaterials-assisted EVs

Low local retention of EVs within joint lesions stands as a major clinical problem restricting the therapeutic efficacy of EVs. To address this challenge, injectable hydrogels have emerged as promising delivery platforms, as they enable prolonged, controlled EV release at lesion sites and extend the duration of therapeutic action [199–201]. A diverse array of hydrogel systems has been developed for EV encapsulation, and these formulations differ substantially in material composition, release kinetics, functional characteristics and applicable therapeutic mechanisms, each with distinct advantages and inherent limitations.

Hyaluronic acid (HA)-based hydrogels are among the most widely investigated carriers for OA-related EV delivery. The Diels-Alder crosslinked HA/PEG (DAHP) hydrogel relies on material degradation to achieve sustained release of MSC-sEVs, while effectively maintaining the biological activity of encapsulated vesicles [202]. As a classic natural joint component, HA possesses excellent biocompatibility and intrinsic lubricating properties for articular cartilage; yet its structural stability is relatively weak in complex inflammatory joint microenvironments. Thermosensitive hydrogels formulated with Poloxamer and Pluronic F-127 represent another mainstream category. These materials feature convenient in-situ gelation after intra-articular injection, which simplifies clinical administration. The Poloxamer 407/188 composite thermogel loaded with PRP-Exos can sustain EV release for up to 28 days, facilitating the proliferation, migration and chondrogenic differentiation of BMSCs, and meanwhile suppressing chondrocyte apoptosis, hypertrophy and degeneration [203]. Similarly, Pluronic F-127/HA blended thermogel carrying chondrocyte-derived exosomes not only realizes long-term EV retention, but also exerts immunomodulatory effects by driving M1 macrophage polarization toward the reparative M2 phenotype [204]. Nevertheless, most poloxamer-based thermogels tend to degrade rapidly under physiological conditions, which may shorten the effective treatment window.

ECM-mimicking hydrogels are designed to better recapitulate the native cartilage microenvironment. The oxidized chondroitin sulfate/methacrylamide gelatin (GMOCS) hydrogel loaded with BMSCs-Exos regulates the TGFB1/Nrf2 signaling axis to promote cartilage regeneration and inhibit neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation [205]. This biomimetic design provides a favorable microenvironment for cell growth, but its preparation process is relatively complicated, limiting large-scale clinical translation to some extent. Microfluidic electrospray technology has been adopted to fabricate HAMA/GelMA hydrogel microcarriers integrated with both hUCMSCs-Exos and stem cell-recruiting peptides [206]. This dual-functional system combines EV therapy with endogenous stem cell recruitment, achieving synergistic cartilage repair; however, the advanced fabrication technique raises the cost and technical threshold for practical application.

Apart from the above systems, herbal ingredient-based hydrogels have opened up a novel direction for EV delivery. The rhein hydrogel (Rh Gel) encapsulating Spirulina platensis-derived EVs (SP-EVs) targets the mitochondrial dysfunction and energy metabolism disorders typical of OA chondrocytes. It alleviates inflammation-induced oxidative damage, restores mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP synthesis, and inhibits the JAK/STAT3 inflammatory pathway [207]. This combination of natural herbal materials and microalgae-derived EVs shows unique advantages in regulating cellular energy homeostasis, and the self-assembled rhein hydrogel is suitable for mass production. Even so, the pharmacological stability of herbal components in the inflammatory joint cavity still requires further long-term verification.

Artificial vesicles

Artificial cell-derived nanovesicles (ACNVs) are engineered to mimic the characteristics and functions of natural extracellular vesicles. ACNVs exhibit EV-like membrane architecture and comparable particle size, while delivering significantly higher yields and protein content compared to their naturally derived counterparts [208, 209]. Current preparation techniques include ultrasonic technology, pH gradient method, microfluidic technology, electroporation and stepwise extrusion [210, 211]. Among these, stepwise extrusion has garnered attention due to its simplicity and convenience. For OA therapy, scholars have successfully extracted ACNVs from BMSCs and hUCMSC using stepwise extrusion in a mini-extruder with polycarbonate membrane filters with various pore sizes; In addition to higher yields, ACNVs have demonstrated equivalent or superior efficacy in cartilage regeneration and anti-inflammatory effects [212, 213]. The acquisition of artificial vesicles also encompasses the hybrid formation of exosome-liposome conjugates by incubating a mixture of the two, creating hybrid nanovesicles capable of loading larger molecules than exosomes while retaining the surface characteristics of exosomes [214, 215]. Liang et al. [216] developed a hybrid nanoscale delivery vehicle composed of engineered exosomes and liposomes that can encapsulate CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids and selectively deliver them to chondrocytes embedded in articular cartilage, thereby alleviating cartilage damage. Furthermore, Kim et al. [217] incorporated ethanol to mediate hybridization of EVs and functionalized lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) with a fusogenic lipid component, demonstrating excellent chondroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects with targeting and long-lasting properties in OA lesions.

Overall, the four engineering technologies have their respective advantages and disadvantages. Endogenous engineering is technically mature and relatively simple to operate, without requiring complex in vitro modification of EVs. It enables multi-dimensional optimization of EV functions by regulating parental cells, and the biocompatibility and safety of EVs are closer to those of natural EVs, reducing the risk of immune rejection. Meanwhile, this method can simultaneously improve the yield and function of EVs with relatively low cost, making it relatively suitable for large-scale research and application. In comparison, exogenous engineering offers higher regulation precision and allows direct customized modification of EVs, which specifically enhances their targeting, bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. However, this technique involves complex procedures that may easily damage EV membrane structure and reduce their activity. It also has high cost and great difficulty in large-scale preparation, which hinders clinical translation. The application of biomaterials effectively addresses the critical limitations of EVs, including rapid clearance and short duration of action in vivo. Theoretically, biomaterials can be combined with any other engineering strategy. Yet the process of preparation may affect EV activity. Balancing material degradation rate with EV release rate, as well as high production costs, remain major obstacles to clinical application. In addition, beyond injectable hydrogels, the exploration of other theoretically viable biomaterials holds great promise. For instance, progress in metal-organic framework (MOF) technology is expected to overcome traditional pore encapsulation, utilizing strategies such as biomimetic mineralization or surface anchoring to combine MOFs with EVs, thereby creating advanced “super delivery systems”[230]. Artificial vesicles exhibit significantly higher yield and cargo loading capacity, and can encapsulate larger therapeutic molecules with strong functional expandability. However, artificial fusion/hybridization and organic solvent-mediated preparation pose potential biosafety risks. In addition, their biodegradation, pharmacokinetics and in vivo distribution remain unclear.

Endogenous EV engineering, exogenous EV engineering, biomaterial-assisted EV delivery and artificial vesicles have different focuses and pros and cons in the treatment of OA, all having achieved phased progress. The combined application of two or more engineering technologies can complement each other to a certain extent and further enhance the therapeutic effect of EVs. However, all current studies on various EV engineering strategies are limited to validation in small animal models. Most investigations only assess short-term therapeutic effects, lacking systematic evaluations of the in vivo biosafety, long-term immunogenicity and degradation metabolism of engineered EVs. Additionally, nearly all these techniques face challenges in standardization and large-scale production. Future research should focus on process optimization, standardized comparative studies and long-term in vivo safety assessments, to accelerate the clinical translation of engineered EVs for OA treatment.

Clinical translation considerations for EVs in OA

Route of administration and pharmacokinetics

As carriers for precision therapy, EVs impose high requirements on bioavailability and targeting during treatment. Studies have shown that in other diseases, conventional administration routes of EVs (oral, intravenous, subcutaneous injection, etc.) lead to their predominant accumulation in the liver, lung, kidney and spleen [231]. Whether and how EVs can target joints, as well as degenerated cartilage and inflammatory synovium, is the major focus of EV application in OA.

Intra-articular injection

Intra-articular (IA) injection is the most commonly used administration route in current preclinical and clinical studies of OA. After injection, EVs are mainly retained locally in the joint and barely migrate to other tissues and organs. EVs enriched in the joint cavity are internalized by synoviocytes and superficial chondrocytes via dynamin-mediated endocytosis [232]. However, in early OA, when cartilage structural damage is mild, EVs are difficult to penetrate into deep cartilage. The retention time of EVs in the joint is usually short, and most vesicles are rapidly cleared within a few days after injection. A study by Feng et al. [233] demonstrated that the fluorescence signal of non-engineered EVs was significantly reduced 3 days after injection into the knee joint cavity of OA mice and completely disappeared after 14 days. The clearance of EVs in the joint cavity includes phagocytosis by macrophages, as internalized EVs are eventually targeted to lysosomes for degradation [12]. In addition, EVs that are not taken up by cells are cleared by subsynovial capillaries and lymphatic vessels in the joint [190].

To address the limitations of rapid clearance and poor deep-tissue penetration, engineered modifications (surface charge reversal, cartilage-targeting peptide conjugation) and biomaterial composite delivery (injectable hydrogels) have become mainstream optimization strategies. Hydrogel carriers can achieve sustained release of EVs for 28 days or longer in animal joints, effectively extending local exposure time, and this technical direction has gradually been validated for clinical transformation.

Oral administration

Oral administration is another route investigated for EV-based therapy of OA. Some scholars hold that gut microbiota is closely associated with OA progression and affects the joint microenvironment through the gut-joint axis. When intestinal permeability fluctuates, bacterial toxins, inflammatory factors and other substances enter the submucosa. Gut bacteria-derived EVs can also be readily taken up by intestinal epithelial cells, enter the systemic circulation, and ultimately influence the joint microenvironment through cartilage fissures or via neovascularization at the osteochondral junction [234]. Studies have shown that oral administration of milk-derived EVs can simultaneously modulate gut microbiota and alleviate cartilage degeneration in OA mice [153]. Therefore, the concept of the gut-joint axis and research on oral delivery of EVs provide novel insights for the treatment of OA.

Dosage criteria and injection frequency

EV quantification in OA research universally adopts two unified metrics: particle concentration (particle count) and total protein mass. There are prominent differences in joint volume, tissue tolerance and physiological metabolism between animal models and human patients, resulting in non-negligible gaps in dosage and administration frequency, which are core obstacles for preclinical result translation.

In preclinical animal models, the parameters for intra-articular injection vary across species and are largely determined by the maximum volume of the joint cavity. For small rodents (mice C57BL/6, rats Sprague-Dawley), the single IA dosage is 6–10 µL per mouse [112, 235], 30–80 µL per rat [173, 236]. The conventional therapeutic concentration of EV preparations for small rodents is 1 × 10⁹ ~ 1 × 1011 particles/mL [112, 173] (2–4 mg/mL protein content) [98]. And the classic regimen is once a week for 4 consecutive weeks. For New Zealand white rabbit models, the volume of IA into the knee joint reached 300–500µL. The commonly used concentration in New Zealand white rabbit models ranges from 100 to 300 µg/mL, and the weekly treatment adopted during experiments lasts 3 to 4 weeks [144, 237, 238].

Large animals are transitional bridge for clinical research. However, studies on EV-based therapy for OA in large animal models remain extremely scarce. In the limited available literature, the dosages of EVs administered to large animals are partially referenced from medium and small animal models. For instance, one study investigating OA in miniature dogs adopted an intra-articular EV dose of 1 × 10¹⁰ particles, with weekly injections over a 2-week period [239]. Another canine study only implemented a single injection [240]. By contrast, relevant EV dosages and injection frequencies were not reported in experiments focusing on porcine KOA [241].

In clinical trials, the total number of EVs for single injection ranges from 1 × 10¹⁰ to 5 × 10¹¹ particles, with an injection volume of 2–5 mL per knee joint. Limited by safety and patient compliance, the total number of treatments is generally no more than 3 times, with an interval of 2–3 weeks between each injection [115, 242].

Dosage conversion dilemma and frequency mismatch have hindered the clinical research of EVs. There is no recognized conversion formula for EV dosage from rodents to humans. Simply amplifying dosage according to joint volume will lead to excessive local concentration in human joints, increasing the risk of mild pain and inflammatory reactions; while blind reduction results in insufficient therapeutic concentration. The frequent administration (weekly injection) applicable to rodent models is difficult to implement in clinical practice due to patient pain, joint infection risk and compliance issues. Clinical dosing regimens rely on sufficient data from large animal studies, which are currently lacking. Existing clinical trials have adopted relatively conservative dosages, yielding only preliminary safety outcomes without remarkable therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, large animal experiments are critical for determining the optimal dose and administration schedule of EVs in subsequent clinical trials.

Potency detection and efficacy evaluation protocols

Reliable potency detection is the core link to guarantee the consistency of EV therapeutic effects, and it is also a mandatory item for clinical trial application per drug regulatory requirements. Combined with ISEV recommendations and OA research characteristics, the detection system is divided into in vitro potency testing, in vivo efficacy evaluation and clinical indicator monitoring [243].

The in vitro evidences include chondrocyte viability, proliferation, and apoptosis [174,180]; ECM synthesis and degradation (the expression of ACAN, COL2, MMPs, ADAMTS; RUNX2) [163, 174, 182, 192]; chondrocyte inflammatory response (regulation of NF-κB pathway, the level of COX-2/TNF-α/IL-6/IL-1β, macrophage polarization regulation) [164, 182, 192, 244, 245]. Furthermore, strictly speaking, it is necessary to verify that EVs can be internalized by target cells prior to presenting the results of in vitro experiments [246]. Basic validation of in vivo animal models includes macroscopic evaluation, histological assessment (cartilage structure and synovial fibrosis), immunohistochemistry (expression of molecules associated with cartilage repair and immunomodulation), micro-computed tomography (subchondral bone analysis), and evaluation of the intra-articular inflammatory microenvironment (e.g., enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) [108]. Routine clinical indicators include joint pain score (VAS, WOMAC scale), joint mobility assessment, and imaging examination (musculoskeletal ultrasound and MRI for cartilage and synovium changes) [242]. At present, clinical trials lack EV-specific molecular efficacy markers, and there is a gap between in vitro potency indicators and human clinical outcomes, which is one of the key reasons for the unstable efficacy of clinical EV products.

GMP manufacturing and donor variability control

GMP-compliant manufacturing specifications

As cell-derived biological products, therapeutic EVs for OA must comply with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) for cellular medicines. Combined with ISEV and international biopharmaceutical industry standards, the following GMP requirements are summarized in this paper for reference.

The upstream processing is dependent upon the EV source. In case of natural sources such as human plasma/blood, plants, and bovine milk no upstream processing is required. Primary/immortal cells such as MSCs, ESCs and iPSCs require upstream processing. Adopt xeno-free and serum-free 3D culture using CDM as the preferred protocol, strictly control cell passage number, culture temperature, pH and oxygen concentration, and complete mycoplasma, bacteria, fungi and virus detection before culture, to avoid exogenous pathogen contamination, enhance purity and reproducibility [247, 248].

About isolation and purification, priority is given to scalable purification technologies (size-exclusion chromatography, tangential flow filtration) instead of traditional ultracentrifugation or differential centrifugation, which is difficult for large-scale production because multiple purification steps are required to remove impurities such as lipoproteins and cell debris. In downstream sterilization, preservation and packaging, adopt 0.22 μm sterile filtration. The finished products should be stored at − 65 °C ~ − 85 °C to maintain EV membrane stability [249]. Completing batch record retention and traceability management throughout the whole process is necessary. Products require relative quality control testing. Real-time detection of EV particle size, purity, protein content and biological activity in each production link to ensure batch consistency [250].

At present, only a small number of hUCMSC-derived EVs have completed GMP production and entered early-phase clinical trials. Most laboratory-prepared EVs use non-GMP processes, which cannot be directly converted to industrial production.

Donor variability and its impacts

Donor-related heterogeneity is an inherent bottleneck for EV standardization, and its impacts run through production, efficacy and safety.

In terms of age factor, BMSC-EV and ADSC-EV show significantly decreased chondroprotective and immunomodulatory potency when derived from aged donors; SMSC-EVs are less affected by donor age, which makes synovial MSCs a superior cell source for elderly OA patients [100]. Individual physical state is of vital importance. Donors with obesity, diabetes, chronic inflammation will secrete EVs carrying pathological cargo (such as pro-inflammatory miRNAs), directly reducing therapeutic efficacy and even aggravating lesions [251, 252].

Accordingly, we offer the following control strategies to reduce the impact of donor variability. In experiments using non-autologous sources of EVs, establish strict donor screening criteria (exclude elderly, metabolic and inflammatory patients). Strengthen preclinical and clinical studies on autologous EVs to reduce allogeneic immune risks and individual differences. Adopt standardized cell culture and engineering modification to offset donor-derived EV heterogeneity.

EV characterization criteria and nomenclature

EV identification and characterization follow the MISEV2018/MISEV2023 guidelines issued by the ISEV [11, 253]. Three mandatory core detection items must be completed to define qualified therapeutic EVs.

The morphological standard identified by Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) should be typical bilayer membrane structure and cup-shaped/saucer-shaped morphology without microbial contamination or massive aggregation. It is noticeable that several OA treatment studies using EVs failed to perform strict morphological characterization. Protein particles comparable to sEVs in diameter were misjudged as sEVs via TEM, and exosomes, microvesicles and apoptotic bodies were not clearly distinguished, resulting in impure and poorly defined samples.

In Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA), the single-peak particle size distribution is standard, with PDI < 0.2, effective particle concentration ≥ 10⁸ particles/mL (It is also the core means for EV quantitative dosing). Among these, the particle size distribution graph obtained via NTA serves as a critical reference for the dimensional information of EVs. According to the recommended classification criteria for EVs size by the latest MISEV guidelines. sEVs have a diameter ranging from 30 to 200 nm, medium EVs range from 200 to 800 nm, and large EVs exhibit diameters exceeding 1000 nm. sEVs are universally recognized as the primary subtype that mediates molecular signal transmission and exerts therapeutic effects. Therefore, an NTA particle size distribution peak below 150 nm is generally regarded as an ideal characteristic. And the particle size peak values determined by NTA reported in published literature commonly range from 120 to 150 nm, which substantially satisfy the specified criteria.

The mandatory panel of biomarker includes positive markers (tetraspanin family CD9, CD63, CD81; EV biogenesis-related TSG101, ALIX) and negative marker (Calnexin, GAPDH). According to MISEV2018 and MISEV2023 guidelines, the characterization of EV biomarker proteins follows the “three positive, one negative” criterion, which means three arbitrarily selected positive marker proteins and one negative marker protein are required to demonstrate significant differential expression between EV samples and control samples, and such results are generally presented via Western Blot (WB). In current studies, nanoflow cytometry is frequently used to detect 2–3 positive proteins as a substitute for the “three positive, one negative” WB identification standard. Nevertheless, most experiments adopting nanoflow cytometry fail to test negative marker proteins. Future studies should improve the rigor of EV biomarker detection.

Current EV research is plagued by inconsistent nomenclature. A large number of published studies collectively refer to all tested vesicles as “exosomes”, yet they fail to verify the biogenesis of these so-called exosomes. The chaos of nomenclature impairs experimental reproducibility and comparability across different studies. The MISEV 2023 guidelines have released the latest specifications for EV nomenclature. Practically descriptive terms are recommended, which can be categorized based on physical properties (e.g., small or large extracellular vesicles), biochemical profiles (e.g., CD9-positive or CD63-positive extracellular vesicles), cellular origin and production conditions (e.g., MSC-derived EVs, hypoxia-induced EVs), along with other recommended descriptors. By contrast, terms associated with biogenetic mechanisms such as exosomes, ectosomes and microvesicles are not recommended for use.

Safety assessment and long-term follow-up

Safety is the primary prerequisite for clinical transformation of EV therapy. Combined with existing preclinical large-animal data and human early-phase trials, we divide safety evaluation into short-term adverse reactions and long-term potential risks, and sort out standardized follow-up schemes.

Short-term safety

Existing clinical data of IA injection of EVs for OA demonstrate preliminary safety. In terms of local reactions, most patients have no discomfort; a small number report mild transient joint pain, and occasional moderate pain accompanied by low-grade fever, which can be relieved spontaneously without intervention [242]. As for systemic reactions, Routine blood, liver and kidney function tests of patients remain normal within several months to 1 year after treatment with no obvious allergic reactions or organ damage observed. In large-animal models (pigs, dogs), No local inflammation, tissue hyperplasia or tumor formation was found after intervention [242, 243].

Long-term potential risks and follow-up requirements

Current clinical follow-up is mostly limited to 12 months, and long-term (3–10 years) safety data are severely insufficient, which is a major hidden danger for clinical promotion. Potential long-term risks include immune sensitization, ectopic tissue hyperplasia/calcification and tumorigenic risk. Repeated IA injection of allogeneic EVs may induce delayed immune responses; EVs regulate cell proliferation and mineralization, which may induce heterotopic ossification in joints in the long term; Although most sources-derived EVs have low tumorigenicity, long-term in vivo retention still needs verification.

The following are recommendations for a standardized long-term follow-up protocol.

(1) Laboratory indicators. Regular re-examination of blood routine, liver and kidney function, and inflammatory cytokine levels (every 6 months); (2) Imaging indicators. Annual joint MRI and X-ray to monitor cartilage, synovium and subchondral bone changes; (3) Special follow-up. Focus on recording delayed pain, swelling and other adverse reactions, and track tumor-related indicators for a long time.

Conclusion and perspective

Overall, in-depth investigations into the roles of EVs across diverse OA pathological phenotypes have advanced our understanding of this disease and provided guidance for the development of EV-based therapies. A wealth of preclinical data has preliminarily validated the therapeutic potential of EVs for OA treatment. Selecting optimal cellular sources and adopting targeted engineering strategies represent promising directions for EV therapeutics; nevertheless, their implementation in standardized clinical practice remains a distant prospect. Future research should prioritize unified standards for EV characterization and potency assessment, as well as the establishment of clinically relevant animal models and next-generation in vitro organoid platforms. On this basis, phenotype-specific therapeutic regimens need to be developed. Researchers should further compare the strengths and limitations of EVs from different origins and various engineering strategies, identify key translational parameters, and collect long-term safety data. Well-designed randomized controlled trials are also essential to accelerate the clinical translation of EV therapies for OA.
