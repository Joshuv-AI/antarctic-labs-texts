Enhancing neural signal quality: a spike-denoising model with BiLSTM and attention mechanism
Authors: Xinyu Xu, Nian Chen, Yuhao Deng, Tianhua Shen, Nannan Wei, Shicang Yu, Limin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeural signal spikes are recorded by microelectrode technology for specific applications. Nevertheless, spikes frequently encounter significant contamination from several noise sources, making efficient denoising quite challenging. Hence, this study presents a spike-denoising model with a bidirectional long short-term memory and attention mechanism combined with a shallow autoencoder to enhance the signal quality. To assess the effectiveness of the proposed method, various types of synthetic data, such as simulated white noise, correlated noise, colored noise and integrated noise, are used to show the performance. At very high noise levels, the proposed method maintains a high signal-to-noise ratio above 27 dB and the average Pearson 0.91. And the performance metrics of spike detection outperforms the traditional signal processing methods and the partial deep learning methods. Ultimately, the proposed method is used to process the real-world C57 fetal rat neural signals, which can recover a substantial amount of spikes from the obscured noise, showing the proposed method can effectively enhance the quality of neural signals damaged by noise.Keywords: Neural signal, Spike denoising, BiLSTM, Attention mechanism, Spike detection

Introduction

The brain’s structure is incredibly complex, with billions of neurons tightly linked to form a massive neural network system [1]. These neurons are basic building blocks for information processing and transmission. By working together, they can innervate different actions and behaviors in the brain [2]. Electrophysiologists have found that neurons communicate by emitting weak electrical impulses that resemble knife blades. They are known as action potentials or spikes [3]. By encoding and decoding neural signals, it is possible to understand the mechanism of several neurological disorders and develop therapeutic approaches [4, 5].

Microelectrode technology is currently a prevalent method for independently collecting signals from neurons in a biological brain. Like the patch clamp [6], this technique captures spikes emitted by neurons. It involves placing an electrode conductor near the neurons to measure the voltage difference. If many active neurons are located near the electrode, recording their firing activity and current transmission is possible [7].

The study of neural signals usually include four steps : denoising, spike detection, feature extraction, and spike clustering. The first stage is applying a band-pass filter to the raw signal. However, the filtered signal is susceptible to severe distortion or even obscuration from various external disturbances [8]. When noise level is high, the target signal can be completely overwhelmed, rendering traditional threshold-based detection methods ineffective. An effective denoising technique is essential to preserving the signal’s original shape and separating it from the surrounding noise.

Numerous denoising techniques have been published in various papers. Among these, Principal Component Analysis (PCA)-based methods have been proven to remove correlated noise from multichannel signals more efficiently than other techniques [9]. Similarly, a wavelet threshold denoising method can remove white noise from signals [10], and combining this with PCA (PCAW) can achieve even better results in preserving neural information [11]. Researchers have also quantitatively assessed the effects of various parameter combinations on denoising and preserving signal shape using two well-known wavelet approaches: stationary wavelet transform (SWT) and discrete wavelet transform (DWT) [12]. Another traditional method, the Kalman Filter, provides recursive estimation based on a state-space model, which can be effective for reconstructing signals when an accurate system model is available [13]. However, these methods have significant drawbacks. For instance, relying solely on these techniques is often insufficient when the noise components are complicated.

Independent Component Analysis (ICA) is a well-established method used to decompose multichannel electroencephalogram (EEG) signals for the purpose of removing electromyogram (EMG) artifacts [14]. Conversely, canonical correlation analysis (CCA) can utilize second-order statistical data for better decomposition and better EMG interference elimination [15]. Some researchers have attempted to pair these with ensemble empirical mode decomposition (EEMD) and PCA (PCAE) to enhance signal clarity [16]. Despite their utility, these methods are hindered by fundamental assumptions that constrain their practical application. Specifically, the efficacy of ICA is compromised by its reliance on the strong assumption of source statistical independence, which is often not met in biological signals [17]. Similarly, CCA’s dependence on second-order statistics makes it less reliable when the number of channels is limited [15].

To overcome these challenges in denoising biological signals, the field has increasingly turned to deep learning methods. Deep learning is data-driven, requiring no prior assumptions. It can autonomously learn deeper-level features to better separate signals from noise, thus demonstrating stronger robustness. Kechris et al. [18] used a fully convolutional denoising autoencoder (FC-DAE) with five pairs of autoencoder structures to denoise neural signal. Fabietti et al. [19, 20] developed the SANTIA and ABOT toolboxes, which utilize various neural networks (Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), 1-Dimensional Convolutional Neural Network (1D-CNN), etc.) to classify and label corrupted segments in Local Field Potential (LFP) recordings, effectively identifying low-quality data. In Frusque’s research [21], the learnable wavelet packet transform (L-WPT) was proposed, which combines deep learning capabilities with the resilience of traditional approaches. Wang et al. [22] proposed a novel GAN with a BiLSTM-LSTM generator that effectively removes specific EEG artifacts, such as EOG and EMG, even in high-noise environments. Gabardi et al. [23] proposed a dynamic evaluation convolutional filter network with four embedded one-dimensional convolutional (1D CNN) layers that can successfully extract electro-oculogram (EOG) and EMG from EEG. Zaliga [24] et al. proposed a 1D Residual Network (DRN) model. The model estimates and subtracts noise from corrupted ECG signals to achieve effective denoising while maintaining signal integrity. Wang et al. [25] proposed MrSeNet, a multi-resolution residual attention network for ECG signal denoising, which effectively remove different types of ECG noise and improve signal quality. Most deep learning methods have been applied to denoising human physiological signals, with limited application to neural signals. Furthermore, the data-dependent nature of deep learning presents a significant challenge in acquiring the large-scale, high-quality datasets necessary for effective training.

Confronted with these challenges, this paper proposes a denoising method based on attention mechanism and bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM). A shallow 1D CNN autoencoder is utilized to extract the spike’s shape from a noisy, complex environment. The proposed method is a prediction technique that goes from noisy signals to clean signals. The BiLSTM component [26] captures dynamic and contextual changes in time series, which is a critical function for neural processes exhibiting strong temporal association. On the other hand, by weightedly focusing on significant aspects, attention mechanism makes the model more responsive to relevant information [27] and prevents substantial changes in the spikes’ amplitude or shape [28]. Simulated synthetic data with varying signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) and noise components, are employed to verify the method’s performance. Additionally, an efficient training method is used, as explained in Sect. 2. The performance of the proposed method is then compared with that of PCAW [10], PCWE [29], SWT [12], FC-DAE [18], DRN [24] and MrSeNet [25]. Methods

Figure 1 illustrates the proposed method’s detailed flow, which comprises three unique steps: signal synthesis, feature extraction, and output prediction. First, four well-formed spikes are extracted from a real-world neural signal. These four spikes are averaged to obtain a spike template and randomly inserted into a blank signal to simulate a clean signal. Then, white noise, correlated noise, colored noise, and integrated noise are simulated and overlayed on the clean signal to obtain the simulated noise signal. Next, the clean signal and simulated noise signal are respectively separated using a window of width 200, achieved by splitting the signal into an equal number of spikes and non-spikes samples. These segments are then fed into the model. The input dataset comes from the simulated noise signal, and the target dataset comes from the clean signal.

General framework of the proposed method

Real-world neural signal acquisition

In a sterile setting, fetuses are extracted from C57 female mice, and their fetal brains are rapidly removed. Subsequently, the cerebral cortex is isolated, and the neurons intended for use are purified. A custom-designed non-contact microelectrode array is employed to detect extracellular potentials; this array features eight channels and a sampling rate of 39,062 Hz.

Network architecture

As shown in Fig. 4, the proposed model consists of an encoder, a BiLSTM layer, a LSTM layer, an attention mechanism layer, and a decoder. The encoder contains two convolutional layers, which are used to extract local features from the input signal window. These features are then batch normalised by a batch normalisation (BN) layer and processed by a rectified linear unit(ReLU) layer to introduce non-linear capabilities. Subsequently, the BiLSTM layer captures the long-range correlations of the signal in both directions, and the LSTM layer further integrates the temporal information. While the attention layer dynamically enhances key features and suppresses noise. The decoder maps the high-level features back to the original signal dimension through three transposed convolutional layers, ultimately outputting the denoised result.

Overall structure of the proposed model

Autoencoder (collective term for encoders and decoders)

Encoder: The encoder consists of two 1D convolutional blocks [30]. Each block comprises a convolutional layer, followed by a BN layer and a ReLU activation function. To balance feature extraction capability against computational complexity, a concise two-layer design is used. The first convolutional layer employs a kernel of size 3 with a stride of 1, while the second uses a kernel of size 3 with a stride of 2 to downsample the feature maps.

Decoder: The decoder utilizes three transposed convolutional layers to reconstruct the signal. Each transposed convolution is also followed by BN and a ReLU activation. This symmetric design ensures the signal is progressively upsampled back to its original dimension.

Since neural signals are, by nature, temporal sequences, dependence interactions between them cannot be captured by traditional convolutional networks. A RNN is a network that can learn the dependencies between input and output data to process time series. Expanding upon this, BiLSTM based on bidirectional recurrent neural networks [31] is used for the efficient extraction of high-frequency features from spikes. In this study, a single-layer BiLSTM is concatenated with three layers of LSTM to learn the features of sequence data more deeply. The model sets the hidden units to 150. By stacking both forward and backward LSTM layers, the BiLSTM enables the network to analyze temporal data in both forward and reverse directions, thereby enhancing data utilization and prediction accuracy.

Model training and implementation

An equal number of spike and non-spike samples are divided, each with a length of 200. During the noise dataset generation procedure, the randomly inserted spike index as is recorded. A full spike randomly appears in the sample to improve the model’s post-training generalization capacity. The simulated signals superposed with integrated noise are chosen as the dataset to maximize the model’s sensitivity to high noise level. Ultimately, only the integrated noise signal from the first 30 of the 60 samples is used, so a dataset with a size of is obtained–specifically containing 2400 pairs of spike samples and 2400 pairs of non-spike samples. Each pair consists of a clean sample and a noise sample.

The first 80% of the dataset is designated as the training set, and the remaining 20% as the validation set. The last 30 sets of samples are then chosen as the test set. Adam optimizer is used, the batch size is set to 32, and the mean squared error (MSE) between the clean signals and the predicted outcomes is seen as the loss function. There are 20 training epochs in all. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the training procedure reveals a sharp drop in loss for both the training and validation sets after ten epochs.

Methods comparison

PCAW, PCWE, SWT, FC-DAE, DRN and MrSeNet are adopted, using the last 30 sets of samples as test set, to further demonstrate effectiveness of the proposed method. First, the test set is treated as 30 channels. For the PCAW method, segmented processing is performed using a window length of 1000 with a 50% overlap. A principal component variance explanation threshold is set to 80%, and the “joint” mode is utilized to effectively leverage inter-channel correlations. In the subsequent wavelet processing stage, the “coif2” wavelet basis is employed with 4 decomposition levels, and soft thresholding is applied using the “heuristic” rule. For the PCWE method, which builds upon wavelet processing, a noise intensity coefficient of 0.2, 100 EEMD integration iterations, and 4 IMF components are configured. This process is executed with a window length of 6000 and a 50% overlap. For the SWT method, an exhaustive search of all parameter combinations is conducted, with the SNR and SD pre-calculated for each outcome. Ultimately, optimal results are achieved through the application of soft thresholding with the “minimax” rule, the “coif2” wavelet basis, and “scale” normalization.

For the FC-DAE and MrSeNet deep learning methods, the open-source models provided by their original authors [18, 25] are directly employed. To ensure compatibility with the training process of this study, adjustments are made to the models’ internal input and output dimensions. Specifically for the DRN architecture, the feature extraction layer, residual blocks, and stacked convolutional layers are replicated in accordance with the descriptions and diagrams from the source paper [24], alongside the necessary dimensional modifications. The training strategy remains consistent with the approach outlined in the proposed method, and all models are trained until convergence of their respective error metrics is achieved.

Performance evaluation metrics

In this paper, the performance metrics of the proposed method is validated by signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), standard deviation (SD) [29], root mean square error (RMSE), Pearson [12], as well as precision, false positive rate (FPR), specificity, recall and Youden’s index after spike detection. SNR is used to assess the denoising strength of the algorithm, while SD and Pearson are utilized to measure the distortion of individual spikes and the entire signal segment. RMSE evaluates the integrity of the whole denoised signal.

Performance comparison on simulated datasets

To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, a comprehensive comparison against six existing methods is conducted on the simulated datasets.

Figures 7 and 8 present the denoising performance of seven different methods at the 15 dB noise level. To illustrate the details of the denoising process, a 0.125-second segment is extracted from the denoising results, and its vertical axis is plotted within a consistent range to facilitate direct comparison. This visualization is presented in Fig. 9. The results indicate that the PCAW and PCWE methods’ denoising capabilities significantly deteriorate under this high noise level, as they fail to recover any spikes. The SWT method, on the other hand, introduces substantial signal distortion and amplitude attenuation, which leads to the appearance of numerous spurious spikes.

Validation on real-world datasets

After verifying the effectiveness of the proposed method on simulated data, its generalization ability to real-world neural signals is further validated. The developed method is applied to denoise the signal from channel 4 of chip 5, with the results presented in Fig. 11. As shown in the first dashed box, the proposed method further enhances the visibility of spikes with a high SNR. Additionally, it significantly improves the shape of spikes that are previously distorted by noise. Furthermore, the method effectively amplifies spikes with an initially low SNR or those submerged in noise but retaining a specific shape. These spikes are fully revealed in the signal, as indicated in the second dashed box.

DS2 denoising results

Tetrodotoxin (TTX) is a neuroinhibitor that theoretically suppresses ion transport through neural sodium channels [33], thereby blocking neural discharge. Neural signals are acquired before and after TTX addition using Chip 22. Subsequently, the method employs the same training dataset as DS2 (with slightly adjusted spike amplitudes) to perform denoising on signals before and after TTX addition. Two sets of TTX experiments are conducted. Figure 12 presents the spike detection results from the first set: (a) displays the raw signals before and after TTX addition, while (b) shows spike detection from the denoised results. The findings reveal a significant disparity in the number of detected spikes before and after TTX administration. The distribution of spikes for both sets is shown in Fig. 13. An observation of the temporal dimension reveals that the spike distribution density is more concentrated, and the number of spikes detected is greater before the addition of TTX. These phenomena are consistent with established theoretical frameworks.

Discussion

In this study, a deep learning method integrating a BiLSTM network and an attention mechanism is developed. Compared with traditional denoising methods and several state-of-the-art deep learning-based counterparts, the proposed method exhibits superior performance in neural signal denoising. Experimental results demonstrate that the method not only achieves a higher SNR and a lower SD for denoised signals on simulated datasets, but also enables effective recovery of spikes that are submerged in noise in real-world neural recordings.

The performance of the proposed method is assessed using metrics such as SNR, SD, Pearson, RMSE, comparing it against six methods: PCAW, PCWE, SWT, FC-DAE, DRN and MrSeNet. Under the 15 dB noise level, SNR obtained by the proposed method exceeds that of other methods, consistently remaining above 27 dB, with the Pearson of 0.91, indicating effective suppression of spike distortion.

To validate the universality of the proposed method in real-world applications, spike detection is performed on the denoising results of seven methods using a single-threshold approach, with their precision, recall, specificity, FPR, and Youden’s index calculated for comparison. As presented in Table 3, under 15 dB integrated noise, the proposed method achieves an average precision of 0.69, an average recall of 0.9869, an average specificity of 0.5925, a FPR below 0.5, and an average Youden’s index of 0.5794—outperforming the other six methods. Furthermore, when applied to real-world scenarios, the method successfully extracts spikes submerged in heavy noise and improves the smoothness of spike shapes, as illustrated in Figs. 11 and 12.

Although the proposed method demonstrates remarkable effectiveness, it remains subject to certain limitations. As illustrated in Table 4, it exhibits a relatively large number of parameters compared to most approaches, resulting in extended training and running durations. Consequently, its application incurs significant computational costs under low-noise conditions. Moreover, the proposed method has not yet been extended to applications beyond neural signals, and its transferability to other types of biological signals remains unclear. For future research, more effective training strategies should be explored - for instance, expanding the temporal span of individual samples instead of relying solely on synaptic fragments. The ultimate goal is to realize end - to - end automatic noise reduction where technically feasible, thereby eliminating the costs associated with manual design and parameter tuning.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a novel neural signal denoising method based on a BiLSTM and an attention mechanism. The denoising effectiveness of the proposed method is evaluated on both simulated datasets (especially those with high noise levels) and real-world datasets characterized by complex background noise. The results demonstrate that, leveraging its feature abstraction capability and temporal sensitivity, the method can effectively recover spikes in neural signals that are severely corrupted or even submerged by intense noise.
