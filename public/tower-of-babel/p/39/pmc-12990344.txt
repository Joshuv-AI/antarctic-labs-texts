Advances in detecting category A biothreat agents: Toward symptom-driven sensing strategies
Authors: Ruilin Li, Yongzhong Zhang, Qiuyu Fu, Chao Zhao, Shaotong Ren, Rui Zhong, Song Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rise of emerging technologies like synthetic biology and artificial intelligence has raised alarms about new forms of weaponized diseases, given their threat to life and societal economic burden. This underscores the critical role of developing sensor technologies for countermeasures. This review focuses on the latest biosensing platforms for Category A biothreat agents, summarizing their diagnostic targets and innovative methods for process optimization and signal amplification. We also examine the clinical progression and symptomatic characteristics associated with these agents. Our goal is to establish a systematic link between sensor technology and clinical application, ultimately supporting research on medical resource allocation for bioterrorism, epidemics, and public health.

Introduction

From the earliest documented biological warfare in 1346 to modern iterations with selective agents, the profound impact of such weapons has always underscored their terrifying potential [1]. While the Biological Weapons Convention (BWC) emerged to restrict state-sponsored biological warfare, the threat has largely shifted to bioterrorism [2]. Now, modern scientific and technological progress, particularly in microbiology and genetic engineering—evidenced by the published smallpox genome and the widespread accessibility of expertise—threatens to enable novel bioterrorist attacks and complicates the nature of their sources [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) classifies bioterrorism agents into three categories: A, B, and C. Complex infectious sources and early non-specific clinical symptoms can trigger a surge of casualties and even 'the worried well' [8] that rapidly overwhelms hospitals, posing an immense challenge to conventional healthcare infrastructure. The Ebola outbreak in West Africa starkly revealed such vulnerabilities: critical shortages of specialized diagnostic equipment, trained personnel, hospital beds, and the medical staff required to manage a massive influx of high-risk patients [9]. Detecting an infectious agent is pivotal to successfully isolating and managing a biothreat event. This capability is especially vital when confronting large-scale, non-specific symptoms, as detection is fundamental to environmental monitoring, quarantine initiation, prophylactic treatments, and patient prognosis management [10,11]. Across these applications, conventional technologies still show significant room for improvement. On the one hand, we aim to optimize biosensor performance parameters (such as sensitivity, dynamic range, and specificity) by analyzing factors including biomarker complexity, capture element affinity, and matrix type. Furthermore, we assess the sensor's practical utility from the perspective of stage-specific disease management, considering the challenges posed by non-specific clinical signs and rapid disease progression.

This article begins by overviewing molecular detection technologies based on their analytical mechanisms and discussing sensor optimization strategies from a perspective that balances sensitivity with practical applicability, thereby fostering a deeper understanding of biosensor principles and research progress. We then explore developing sensor technologies for each Category A biothreat agent, classified by the type of biorecognition element used. Our review, drawing from searches of PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus, covers nearly 100 research articles published predominantly within the last five years (Fig. A1). Finally, we explored symptom-driven rapid detection strategies for responding to large-scale bioterror attacks. In summary, by discussing the evolution of existing technologies and referencing specialized diagnostic practices, this review aims to provide a strategic framework for the effective allocation of medical resources in response to bioterrorism.

Developing sensor technologies

Biosensors translate the specific interaction between a biorecognition element (like DNA, an aptamer, or a monoclonal antibody) and its target (such as a bacterium, virus, or toxin) [12,13] into a measurable signal through various technical principles, including electrochemical, optical, and other methods [14]. This signal, in turn, yields valuable information about the target's presence, concentration, or activity (Fig. 2) [15]. A simple classification of detection technologies is challenging, as they often employ hybrid methods that fit into multiple categories. Nevertheless, based on their analytical mechanisms or workflows, we can broadly group them as follows [8,16]. First are technologies based on physical detection methods, such as mass spectrometry (MS), chromatography, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), and quartz crystal microbalance (QCM), which provide correspondingly high precision and accuracy but typically depend on complex, non-portable equipment and skilled personnel [17]. Second are those based on biochemical detection methods. This category includes antibody-based assays and nucleic acid tests (e.g., PCR). Among these, Lateral Flow Immunoassay (LFI) and Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR)-based technologies are preferred for point-of-care (POC) or field testing [[18], [19], [20]]. Finally, emerging transformative sensors—including electrochemical, optical, and photoelectrochemical (PEC) platforms—have surpassed traditional detection methods in terms of convenience and sensitivity. These technologies are particularly well-suited for rapid diagnostics, outdoor on-site detection, and resource-limited settings [21,22]; however, they still face challenges regarding stability and accuracy. Comparison of detection technologies across different categories.

Since technologies vary in their focus and advantages, evaluating key analytical parameters—such as sensitivity, specificity, selectivity, limit of detection (LOD), dynamic range, and repeatability—is crucial for assessing their suitability for practical applications [26]. Different optimization strategies are employed across various detection platforms to meet specific performance metrics [[21], [22], [26], [27], [28], [29]].

For instance, high-affinity recognition elements with superior properties, such as monoclonal antibodies and aptamers, are being used to replace conventional antibodies [30]. Compared to antibodies, aptamers exhibit greater stability and more straightforward synthesis pathways; furthermore, they can be easily modified with specific functional groups as required [31]. Furthermore, their single-binding mode prevents redundant target binding, which is crucial for eliminating spurious signals in competitive assays (e.g., in Raman spectroscopy) [32]. Significant efforts have been made to enhance signals, with optimizations spanning from sample processing to device materials and detection mechanisms. To optimize sample handling, isothermal amplification techniques [33] are employed to overcome the reliance on thermal cyclers. Magnetic beads with a three-dimensional hybridization pattern can significantly increase target loading density and overcome the diffusion limitations of 2D surfaces [34]. Additionally, integrating technologies like CRISPR and microfluidics ultimately improves the efficiency and quality of sample processing [35]. Nanotechnology has driven immense progress in this area. Nanomaterials such as gold, carbon, or their nanocomposites possess high specific surface areas and abundant active sites, which expand the sensor surface and enable ultra-low-level detection. These materials also shorten electron diffusion pathways and provide catalytic sites, thereby accelerating electron transfer kinetics, amplifying electrochemical signals, and improving biocompatibility [36]. For optical sensors, nanoparticles (NPs) like carbon dots (CDs) can enhance the optical response within an optical framework (laser source and collector). CDs are advantageous due to their multiple excitation/emission wavelengths, high quantum yield, chemical stability, ease of surface functionalization, and low photobleaching [28]. Furthermore, flexible materials are employed to enhance sensitivity, biocompatibility, and practical applicability. These include carbon nanomaterials (e.g., graphene), liquid metals (structurally optimized to balance electrical conductivity and flexibility), and hydrogels, which serve as flexible substrates that mimic the mechanical properties and biocompatibility of natural soft tissues [37,38]. Definitely, conventional methods remain important, such as improving the immobilization techniques for biorecognition elements and optimizing functional group design to enhance properties like proton conductivity [27,39]. Additionally, combining the strengths of different detection technologies is another effective strategy. For instance, the integration of CRISPR's high-sensitivity cleavage capability with the molecular sensitivity of SPR imaging [40], as well as the combination of analyte-triggered nucleic acid signal amplification with PEC biosensors [41], can significantly enhance detection sensitivity. To improve convenience and operability, systems are being integrated with microfluidics and sample preparation techniques that do not require nucleic acid extraction [42]. The incorporation of visualization systems further reduces the need for extensive operator training and facilitates automated reactions.

Biological threat agent sensor platform of category A

Anthrax sensor platforms

Bacillus anthracis exists in two forms: vegetative cells and spores. The anthrax spore exhibits exceptional resistance to extreme environmental conditions, including high temperatures, UV radiation, freezing, desiccation, and chemical disinfectants [43]. In fact, viable spores can still be detected in soil samples from World War II-era sites. Inhalation of more than 104 spores is typically fatal if not effectively treated within 24-48 h [44,45]. Furthermore, methods for inducing multidrug resistance in this microorganism have been published in peer-reviewed scientific literature [46].

Anthrax gene

The pathogenicity of Bacillus anthracis is primarily conferred by two plasmids. Plasmid pXO1 encodes the lethal toxin (from pagA and lef gene products) and the edema toxin (from pagA and cya gene products) [47]. Plasmid pXO2 carries capsule genes that create a poly-γ-d-glutamic acid capsule, enabling the pathogen to evade the host immune response. The expression of these toxin and capsule genes is controlled by the major virulence regulator, AtxA [48]. Consequently, these genes are critical for virulence and serve as primary detection targets. However, differentiating B. anthracis from genetically similar strains is challenging due to the presence of similar virulence plasmids in other Bacillus species and the occurrence of horizontal gene transfer [49]. To address this, species-specific, multi-copy chromosomal markers have been developed. These include specific sequences (e.g., BA813), single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in genes like rpoB and plcR, and ribosomal RNA genes (like 16S rRNA). Nevertheless, the reliability of these non-plasmid targets requires further validation across more isolates [47,50]. To enhance diagnostic performance, studies have shown that combining markers is highly effective; for example, the Ba-1 + BAPA + lef gene combination has achieved 100% specificity, sensitivity, and accuracy, demonstrating a powerful strategy for precise detection [51].

Although RT-qPCR is the gold standard for quantifying Bacillus anthracis target genes, its long thermal cycling times are a significant drawback. Wu et al. developed a SERS-PCR sensing platform based on hollow gold nanoparticles (HAuNPs) and magnetic beads (MBs), in which MB-conjugated capture DNA and HAuNP-SERS nanoprobe-conjugated detection DNA are designed to hybridize with a bridge probe in a sandwich configuration. The aggregation of SERS nanotags onto the MB surfaces via DNA hybridization generates a multitude of "hotspots," leading to a significant enhancement of the Raman signal under laser irradiation due to the localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) effect. Upon the presence and amplification of the target pagA gene, the exonuclease activity of Taq polymerase hydrolyzes the unlabeled bridge probe, causing the disassembly of the complex and a subsequent attenuation of the Raman signal intensity as the cycle number increases. Leveraging the robust enrichment capacity of the 3D MB surfaces, accelerated surface diffusion kinetics, and the potent electromagnetic enhancement of HAuNPs, this assay achieves effective detection after only five thermal cycles (with an LOD of 960 pM), addressing the sensitivity limitations of traditional RT-qPCR during early cycling. However, this "signal-off" mode necessitates meticulous optimization of probe concentrations and imposes stricter requirements for anti-interference capability [52].

To advance on-site detection in resource-limited settings, a visual, high-throughput, and low-cost system was developed. Li et al. combines Recombinase Polymerase Amplification (RPA) with CRISPR/Cas12a and a G4-DNAzyme-based visual readout, targeting the novel CR5 site. The target gene is first isothermally amplified by RPA, which in turn activates the Cas12a protein. The activated Cas12a specifically degrades the single-stranded blocking DNA (CatG4R) hybridized with the G-quadruplex sequence, thereby relieving its conformational constraint and releasing its antisense sequence, CatG4. CatG4 then binds with hemin to form a G-quadruplex (G4) structure with peroxidase-like activity. This G4-DNAzyme catalyzes a colorimetric reaction with substrates (ABTS and H2O2), causing the solution to turn green. This enables point-of-care detection in the field with an impressive LOD as low as 1 copy/reaction [53].

Xiao et al. pioneered an electrochemiluminescence (ECL) platform targeting atxA mRNA, utilizing a synergistic design that integrates the competitive consumption of dissolved oxygen with a dual-signal ratiometric output. In this system, K2S2O8 serves as the cathodic emitter—facilitated by the catalytic enhancement of silver metal-organic gels (AgMOG)—while luminol encapsulated within liposomes acts as the anodic emitter. Both emitters compete for dissolved oxygen in the solution, resulting in reciprocal signal variations. This ratiometric detection mode, combined with a dual-amplification mechanism—driven by the high loading capacity of hollow-structured liposomes and the catalytic prowess of AgMOG—significantly improves both analytical accuracy and sensitivity. Nevertheless, the thermodynamic stability of liposomes in complex biological matrices, as well as the assembly stability and reproducibility of the multi-component nano-interfaces, remain critical challenges to be addressed for the clinical translation of this technology [54].

Anthrax spores

Nucleic acid detection of Bacillus anthracis typically requires the disruption of its spores, which poses a risk of aerosolization. Consequently, developing methods for detecting intact, non-damaged spores presents a significant challenge [49]. Dipicolinic acid (DPA, or pyridine-2,6-dicarboxylic acid), a key component of B. anthracis spores, has been confirmed as a unique biomarker for anthrax identification [55,56]. Recent research has focused on fluorescent techniques for DPA detection, which quantify its concentration by modulating fluorescence intensity. A variety of fluorescent donor compounds have been employed for this purpose, including lanthanide metal-organic frameworks (LMOFs) [57], lanthanide-functionalized hybrid materials [58], layered rare-earth hydroxides (LRHs) [59], and CDs [60].

Lanthanide elements such as erbium (Er), europium (Eu), neodymium (Nd), and terbium (Tb) have garnered significant attention due to their advantageous properties, including high surface area, active interaction sites, large Stokes shifts, high quantum yields, and long fluorescence lifetimes [61]. Notably, they can form stable complexes with DPA, which acts as a tridentate molecule [62]. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are porous network materials composed of inorganic metal ions and organic linkers. They include MOFs containing d-block metals (primarily Zn2+ and Cd2+) and f-block metals (primarily Eu3+ and Tb3+) [57]. The combination of different metal ions and organic linkers endows MOFs with excellent tunability. The photoluminescent properties of MOFs, which are modulated by interactions with guest molecules, can be harnessed for the selective recognition of target analytes, with the energy transfer mechanism being influenced by the framework-analyte interaction. The luminescence centers can originate from various components, such as the organic linkers, metal ions, or loaded guest molecules [63,64]. Typically, the emission originates from an energy-conduction mechanism characterized by the 'antenna effect'. In this process, the organic ligands act as light-harvesting units that relay the captured excitation energy to the central metal ion through non-radiative energy transfer. This populates the excited state of the luminescent center, which subsequently returns to its ground state via radiative decay, yielding characteristic fluorescence. The introduction of DPA modulates this system by either truncating or facilitating the energy transfer pathways, thereby enabling a 'signal-on' (enhancement) or 'signal-off' (quenching) response [57]. Consequently, MOFs are frequently used to construct ratiometric fluorescent sensors, which can be broadly classified into three main types.

First, dual-lanthanide or dual-linker emitting MOF materials. Recent studies have ventured beyond the commonly explored Tb (III) and Eu (III) ions to investigate other lanthanide MOF structures. For instance, a dual-lanthanide ion (Ce3+, Eu3+) MOF, designated Ce@Eu@MOF-808, was designed to achieve detection through a dual-response "off-on" mode driven by the antenna effect, expanding the diversity of sensor designs [65]. A study on heterolanthanide MOFs introduced an innovative mechanism to modulate detection sensitivity by controlling the intrinsic energy transfer efficiency between Eu3+ and Tb3+. Specifically, under UV excitation, the BTC ligands sensitize Tb3+, which subsequently transfers energy to Eu3+, resulting in a dominant red emission over Tb3+. The introduction of DPA interrupts this inter-metal energy transfer; the carboxyl groups of DPA displace the water molecules coordinated to Tb3+, thereby restoring the antenna effect of Tb3+. This leads to an enhancement of Tb3+ luminescence and a visible color transition from red to green [66].

Due to the stringent requirement for energy level matching, it is often challenging to effectively sensitize dual-lanthanide centers using a single ligand. H. Zhang et al. employed a dual-ligand synergistic strategy—utilizing HGTDA to sensitize Eu3+ and the self-emission of HADHTP for ratiometric detection. Since the former exhibits higher energy transfer efficiency, the system initially emits red light. Upon DPA addition, Eu3+ emission is enhanced through coordination, while ligand emission is simultaneously boosted via hydrogen bonding with the ligand's hydroxyl groups. The significantly larger enhancement of green emission relative to red enables a visualized, self-calibrating ratiometric response [67]. However, these traditional Ln3+-MOFs face challenges such as complex synthesis, low atom utilization, linker leakage, and energy level mismatch between the excited and ground states of the two linkers [68,69]. Therefore, single-linker, single-lanthanide MOFs with dual-emission characteristics, particularly those using a single aromatic linker for optimal atom economy, represent a more cost-effective approach for DPA detection.

Second, single-linker, single-metal dual-emission Ln-MOFs, which can simultaneously display the characteristic emissions of both the linker and the rare-earth metal.

In contrast to previous strategies relying on extensive pre-experimental screening for Ln-MOF design, Yu et al. developed a theoretical prediction model comprising three computational modules—intramolecular hydrogen bonding, excited-state energy levels, and coordination stability between ligands and Ln3+. This allowed for the rapid and rational synthesis of the dual-emitting Tb-PTA-OH MOF. Specifically, the intramolecular hydrogen bonds of PTA-OH trigger an ESIPT process to generate stable blue light. When DPA is introduced, it displaces the PTA-OH ligand; since DPA's triplet state (T1) energy level matches the 5D4 level of Tb3+ and is higher than that of PTA-OH, it enables a dual-path (direct and indirect) sensitization of Tb3+ luminescence. Although this system was successfully integrated into paper chips and smartphone-based micro-devices for portable visual sensing, the quantitative accuracy of the smartphone platform remains notably inferior to that of traditional spectroscopic techniques [70]. A pioneering Eu-MOF built from redox-active ligands achieves ratiometric DPA sensing through a mechanism involving PET (photoinduced electron transfer) inhibition and competitive absorption, where ligand emission increases as Eu3+ emission decreases. By undergoing oxidation, the resulting Eu-MOF-Ox achieves a reduced HOMO-LUMO gap for intensified ligand fluorescence and an improved electron-accepting ability to promote charge transfer with DPA. These modifications result in enhanced emission performance and a lower LOD relative to the original Eu-MOF. However, further studies are required to verify the platform's performance in practical samples, including blood and soil [71]. Wang et al. reported the construction of a 3D coordination polymer, [Ln2(L)4Cl2(H2O)4]n, which serves as a structural motif providing new insights into the assembly of 3D cluster-based polymers. In this system, DPA detection is achieved through fluorescence quenching, a process driven by DPA competing for the energy transfer from the ligand to Eu3+. Although this platform was validated in fetal calf serum (FCS)—a complex biological matrix—the inherent nature of this quenching mechanism makes it inevitably susceptible to environmental fluctuations, which may lead to false-positive signals [72].

By integrating the multifunctionality of crystalline MOFs and the distinctiveness of amorphous substances, aMOFs—with their homogeneous metal distribution and synergistic properties—provide an ideal platform for high-performance optical materials. By tuning the ratios of metal ions to organic ligands, Dang et al. synthesized two new amorphous MOFs (amEu-NH2BDC and amTb-NH2BDC) and resolved the issue of Ln3+ luminescence quenching in water. In this system, the characteristic Ln3+ emission is initially shielded by coordinated water molecules. The introduction of DPA triggers a displacement of these water molecules, leading to the direct sensitization of Ln3+ while maintaining the ligand's blue emission, thus enabling ratiometric sensing with an LOD at the nM level. Nevertheless, the inherent structural disorder of amorphous MOFs makes it difficult to ensure and validate consistency across different synthetic batches [73].

In addition, transition metals have been introduced into rare-earth-doped MOFs (RDMOFs) to serve as structural scaffolds that protect active sites, achieving efficient one-step synthesis and a broader variety of MOF architectures. An experimental framework was constructed using Zr4+ nodes and 4,4′-BPDC ligands, where the ligand and Tb3+ act as luminophores. The addition of DPA triggers the sensitization of Tb3+ emission while the ligand's fluorescence remains constant, allowing for ratiometric sensing. Despite its effectiveness, the platform's applicability is restricted to the acidic pH levels typical of certain herbal medicine samples, which hinders its direct utility in physiological settings [74]. Xie et al. reported a Cd-based framework, with the TTCA ligand and Tb3+ serving as the primary luminophores. The system relies on efficient energy transfer between the ligand and the metal ion. When DPA is introduced, it competitively binds to Tb3+. Compared to TTCA, the energy level mismatch between DPA and Tb3+ is more pronounced, making DPA less effective at promoting Tb3+ to its excited state. Consequently, the energy transfer route is severed, causing the ligand's blue emission to rise while Tb3+ emission declines, enabling ratiometric detection with nM-level sensitivity. Nevertheless, the potential toxicity of the cadmium-based framework remains a drawback regarding environmental and biological safety [75]. By incorporating Eu3+ into a Zn-MOF, S. Li et al. pioneered the use of second-order scattering (SOS) signals to replace traditional dual-fluorophore systems, streamlining the probe architecture. The integrated nanostructure provides a robust initial SOS signal. Upon adding DPA, the competitive binding of DPA with Zn2+ and Eu3+ causes the nanostructure to disintegrate, resulting in the attenuation of SOS. Concurrently, the sensitization of Eu3+ by DPA increases the fluorescence output, achieving a ratiometric response with a low detection limit. Despite these advantages, the platform's reusability is hindered by the irreversible nature of the structural damage. Additionally, the vulnerability of scattering signals to complex sample matrices, especially those with high Zn2+ concentrations, restricts its broader application [76].

Third, MOF composite materials are combined with other luminescent materials. Although rare earth elements offer exceptional performance, their scarcity remains a major hurdle. To address this, integrating multi-fluorophores into lanthanide-free MOFs provides a viable strategy. In one research, red-emissive carbon dots (r-CDs) and Eosin Y (EY) were incorporated into a cobalt-based ZIF-67 framework, offering a more cost-effective and eco-friendly approach compared to lanthanide-based systems. The sensing mechanism is based on the ligand displacement effect of DPA, leading to the release of EY and a concomitant increase in its fluorescence, while the r-CDs serve as a constant internal standard. To facilitate practical application, a hydrogel film sensor was fabricated for quick visual detection using smartphone-based RGB analysis—a feature highly beneficial for field testing. However, the system's robustness and sensitivity still necessitate further optimization [77].

Beyond MOFs, functional hybrid materials like Layered Rare-earth Hydroxide (LRH) nanosheets serve as another platform for sensing. LRHs consist of positively charged layers and interlayer anions, where the intercalation of anions not only stabilizes organic emitters but also creates unique luminescent signatures through host-guest synergistic effects. This architecture effectively combines the structural advantages of layered hydroxides with the optical properties of lanthanides. For example, LEuH-based probes have been synthesized by intercalating BOP/SDS [78] or BDC-NH2/SDS [79], yielding nanosheets with monolayer structures. Upon interaction with DPA, the Eu3+ luminescence is dramatically enhanced through the antenna effect, while the emission from BOP (green) or BDC-NH2 (blue) acts as a constant internal standard. By eliminating diffusion resistance through fully exposed surface active sites, these probes achieve exceptional sensitivity. Nevertheless, their reliance on pure water environments for validation remains a limitation for practical, complex-sample applications。

Yang et al. reported a Eu(III) nano-molecular sensor utilizing a novel flexible Schiff base ligand L2−. The Eu3+ centers are fully encapsulated by L2− ligands without any coordinated solvent molecules. Initially, back-energy transfer from the Eu3+ ions to the ligands drains the excited-state energy, leaving the system in a quenched state. The introduction of DPA triggers a coordination displacement of L2−, which not only halts the back-energy transfer but also sensitizes Eu3+ luminescence. This mechanism allows for an exceptionally low LOD of 0.47 nM (FCS) and a fast response within 10 s. However, the low synthetic yield of these nanoclusters leads to relatively high production costs [80]. Cao et al. reported a ratiometric nanoprobe utilizing the confinement-induced emission enhancement of a cationic styrylpyridinium derivative (L) incorporated into MCM-22 zeolite. By leveraging the electrostatic attraction of the negatively charged AlO4 tetrahedral units, both dye L and Tb3+ ions are encapsulated within the zeolite pores. This spatial confinement effectively stabilizes the fluorescence of dye L by reducing non-radiative decay. The sensing mechanism relies on the DPA-triggered antenna effect, which activates the green emission of Tb3+ against the stable red reference signal of L, achieving high sensitivity (nM LOD) even in complex matrices. Admittedly, the complexity of the synthesis and the necessity for precise pore-size matching present challenges for the broader scalability of this strategy [81].

To mitigate the interference from various luminescent complexes formed by the coordination of free lanthanide ions with other ligands (such as hydroxyl ions and water molecules), functionalized iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) based on Tb3+ or Eu3+ were developed. By employing magnetic separation technology, DPA competitively displaces the Tb3+/Eu3+ ions initially bound to the IONP surface. Subsequently, the IONPs and unbound ions are magnetically removed, leaving only the formed Ln3+-DPA complexes in the solution. This approach significantly minimizes background noise, leading to a remarkably low LOD of 0.236 nM. However, their insufficient photostability under intense light may hinder long-term or repeated applications [82].

In an effort to utilize the excellent photoluminescence of carbon dots, Wang et al. constructed a FRET-based ratiometric nanoprobe using Zn-doped carbon dots (Zn-CDs) and EDTA-Eu. The sensing mechanism relies on the coordination between Eu3+ and DPA, which triggers an energy transfer that diminishes the blue fluorescence of the Zn-CDs while sensitizing the red luminescence of Eu3+. This probe exhibits a dual response to both DPA and uric acid (UA), demonstrating promising applications in biodefense as well as in vitro bioimaging [83]. In a recent breakthrough, H. Wu et al. synthesized π→π∗ transition-rich carbon dots (R-CDs) that utilize the inner filter effect (IFE) rather than the common strategy of modulating heteroatom unsaturation. The enhanced π→π∗ transitions strengthen the R-CDs' absorption in the short-wave UV range, creating a competitive absorption scenario with DPA that quenches the R-CDs' fluorescence. Meanwhile, Eu3+ emission is triggered by the DPA-mediated antenna effect and further boosted by R-CDs, which act as a shield to prevent water molecules from coordinating with and quenching Eu3+. This dual-signal probe facilitates real-time field analysis of lake water and bacterial growth monitoring, providing a robust tool for ensuring water safety [23].

Paper-based analytical platforms are highly anticipated for DPA detection in remote and impoverished areas because they are easy to recycle, offer flexible fabrication, and facilitate POCT when coupled with smartphones for quantification. Lv et al. introduced a portable ratiometric probe utilizing hydroxyapatite-supported Tb-EDTA (HAp:Tb-EDTA). In this system, hydrogen bonding ensures the homogeneous distribution and tight integration of Tb-EDTA within the paper, where the complex prevents water quenching to maintain a stable signal. The sensing mechanism involves DPA competitively replacing water molecules to coordinate with Tb3+ on the HAp surface, thereby sensitizing terbium luminescence through the antenna effect [24]. Another dual-functional paper probe incorporates YVO4:Eu as a red fluorescent reference and a Tb\β-diketone complex that responds to DPA with green emission. This probe achieves a LOD as low as 4.5 nM and further enables the cascade detection of Cu2+ and cysteine [84].

Beyond the development of various fluorescence detection technologies, an integrated POC SERS sensor has been engineered to provide a unified "enrichment-and-detection" solution. The platform features multifunctional CoFe2O4/APTES/Ag core-shell hybrids, combining magnetic properties with high SERS activity. Once DPA molecules are captured by the APTES layer on the nanomaterial surface, the magnetic composites are isolated from the sample matrix via an external magnetic field. Upon excitation, the SERS signal is significantly amplified through a dual mechanism: charge transfer (CT) between the semiconductor CoFe2O4 and plasmonic silver (Ag), coupled with the electromagnetic enhancement provided by AgNPs. The synergistic integration of CT enhancement, molecular capture, and magnetic separation addresses primary hurdles in practical SERS applications—namely, by reducing analysis time, minimizing matrix effects, and boosting sensitivity. Anthrax toxin

The serum concentration of toxin proteins directly reflects the viability and pathogenic capacity of Bacillus anthracis. Notably, the Protective Antigen (PA) of the anthrax toxin is a key biomarker for early diagnosis and real-time patient monitoring due to its high abundance [85]. A competitive Surface-Enhanced Raman Scattering (SERS) assay was developed using gold nanorod arrays (AuNRAs) conjugated with PA-aptamers as the substrate, and AuNPs modified with 1,2-bis(4-pyridyl)ethylene (BPE) and PA as SERS labels. In the presence of free PA, it competes with the PA on the SERS labels for the aptamer binding sites on the substrate; consequently, a higher concentration of free PA results in fewer SERS labels being anchored, leading to a diminished Raman signal. The signal is significantly amplified by a "triple hotspot" effect—comprising the self-enhancement of AuNPs, plasmon resonance between AuNPs and AuNRAs, and the gaps between AuNP aggregates and the AuNRA surface. This platform achieved a LOD as low as 1 pg/mL, though its performance has yet to be validated in real-world complex matrices [86]. A recent study developed an electrochemical immunosensor utilizing a polypyrrole-gold nanoparticle/multi-walled carbon nanotube (PPy-AuNPs/MWCNTs) hybrid to synergistically enhance electrode conductivity and antibody immobilization efficiency. The detection is based on potentiometric stripping analysis (PSA) of Cd2+ ions released from cadmium sulfide nanocrystals (CdSNCs) via acid dissolution. However, the high dilution requirement for serum matrices compromises its sensitivity advantage, and the necessity of an acid digestion step, along with the use of heavy metals, limits its direct application in rapid POCT [87].

Plague sensor platforms

Plague infections remain a global concern. For instance, Madagascar reports 200 to 700 cases annually, with a peak of 2417 cases in 2017. Despite this, no diagnostic products for plague have yet received U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval [88]. To improve the binding and enrichment of bacterial components, thereby potentially enhancing downstream assays like molecular (e.g., PCR) and protein-based methods, Ii et al. characterized the binding capacity of CN3080-type nanotrap particles with Yersinia pestis bacteria, proteins, and nucleic acids in whole human blood. The study found that these nanotraps could increase nucleic acid yield fourfold and demonstrated a high affinity for multiple components of the Yersinia Type III Secretion System (T3SS) [89].

Genetic detection

In 2021, the WHO revised its case definition for plague, recommending that laboratory confirmation via PCR-based methods should target at least two of the following four genetic markers: pla, caf1, ypo2088, and ypo2486, with both targets testing positive [90]. Based on this guidance, a triplex droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) assay was developed, targeting ypo2088, caf1, and pla, located on the chromosome, plasmid pMT1, and plasmid pPCP1, respectively. This method offers the advantage of comprehensive target and sample coverage [91]. Another triplex real-time PCR (qPCR) assay targets caf1, pla, and yopM, located on plasmids pMT1, pPCP1, and pCD1, respectively. It achieved a low LOD of 1 CFU/mL and demonstrated 100% sensitivity compared to culture methods [92].

Advances have also been made in CRISPR-based diagnostics suitable for field use. Mao et al. targeted a novel specific gene sequence (CH57_3927) by integrating RAA-CRISPR/Cas12a with a G-quadruplex DNAzyme-based colorimetric assay. Unlike the colorimetric mechanism used in anthrax CRISPR detection systems, this platform triggers Cas12a-mediated cleavage of the G-quadruplexes upon target recognition. The cleaved G-quadruplexes fail to bind with hemin and thus lose their peroxidase-like activity, which inhibits the catalysis of the colorimetric reaction, resulting in a colorless output for positive samples. This ELISA-like visual assay effectively distinguishes Yersinia pestis from its closely related species, making it highly suitable for resource-limited settings. Although a low LOD was achieved in simulated clinical samples, further validation with authentic clinical specimens is still required [93].

Protein-based detection

Detection of the Yersinia capsular protein F1 antigen has been widely used to diagnose plague. However, mutant strains lacking this antigen have been identified, necessitating the inclusion of additional targets to improve the performance of rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs). Promising candidates include components of the T3SS, such as chaperone proteins, Yop effector proteins, and the virulence factor LcrV (V antigen). The precise concentration of LcrV in human serum remains to be established [94,95]. To broaden the detection coverage across various genotypic strains, Hau et al. developed an ELISA and a LFI targeting dual antigens, LcrV and the F1 protein. Compared to ELISA, which requires prolonged incubation to reach binding equilibrium and specialized laboratory conditions, LFI systematically delivers reagents via capillary flow, eliminates intermediate washing steps, and utilizes AuNPs instead of enzyme labels for signal generation. Consequently, LFI is better suited for visual detection in resource-limited settings [95]. Devadhasan et al. focused on LcrV by developing a Vertical Flow Immunoassay (VFI) platform optimized through flow rate analysis, nitrocellulose (NC) membrane selection, and AuNP concentration adjustment, utilizing automated image analysis software for quantitative array intensity measurements. In this system, the complexes formed by incubating antigens with gold-labeled antibodies are enriched by capture antibodies via pressure-driven flow to generate signals. Although its miniaturization and active vertical flow offer enhanced sensitivity compared to LFI, the platform entails more complex operations and higher sample requirements. Further integration of passive pump systems and smartphone-based miniaturized quantification could facilitate its deployment in resource-limited environments [96].

Two other variants of immunochromatographic assays have been developed. One is a colorimetric lateral flow immunoassay (cLFIA) incorporating bimetallic silver-coated gold nanostars (BGNS) with superior optical properties. This platform achieved a sensitivity approximately 50 times higher than traditional AuNP-based LFIA in simulated clinical samples without pretreatment and exhibited excellent stability over six months, albeit with increased cost and synthetic complexity [97]. The other is an electro-driven immunochromatographic assay (EICA) that utilizes electro-osmotic flow (EOF) as an additional driving force to overcome the speed limitations of paper-based capillary action. By employing upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) as signal labels, it achieved a LOD of 1.2 × 103 CFU/mL, significantly surpassing the 104 CFU/mL LOD of the most sensitive commercial kits currently available. Despite improvements in speed and sensitivity, its reliance on external equipment and electrode stability remain challenges for application in resource-scarce and off-grid scenarios [98].

To enhance the sensitivity and specificity of label-free detection, research has been directed toward optical sensing technologies. Based on reflectance spectroscopy, Chen et al. utilized an AuNP-polymer composite double-striped nanowire array (APDW). In this system, double-striped PDMAEMA brushes utilize an alternating phase transition process to attract AuNPs synthesized via a two-phase system. The resulting periodic grating structure directionally immobilizes capture antibodies; upon antigen binding, the filling of hydrophobic gaps within the double stripes triggers nanostructural deformation and alters the grating diffraction effect. This leads to a shift in the reflection peak wavelength, enabling high-sensitivity quantitative detection with an LOD of 14.2 pg/mL [99]. Another approach, based on diffractive optics, leverages the properties of one-dimensional (1D) diffraction gratings. Specifically, a laser beam generates a sharp, high-intensity reflection diffraction pattern from regularly arranged polymer brush gratings. Antigen-antibody binding disrupts the regularity and periodicity of the grating structure, thereby modulating the reflection diffraction intensity to achieve a high-sensitivity LOD of 100 CFU/mL. However, these label-free methods are often characterized by time-consuming polymerization or pretreatment procedures and more sophisticated instrumentation requirements [100].

Tularemia sensor platforms

The bacterium Francisella tularensis comprises four subspecies, of which F. tularensis subsp. tularensis (Type A) and F. tularensis subsp. holarctica (Type B) are the primary causes of human tularemia [101]. Currently, there are no FDA-approved rapid, point-of-care diagnostic tests for the detection of F. tularensis antigens [102].

Genetic detection

Two recent PCR studies have expanded the diagnostic range and specificity of current detection technologies. One qPCR assay can accurately detect all members of the Francisella genus, including atypical Francisella species that cause tularemia-like symptoms [103]. Another qPCR assay has extended the capability to differentiate between F. tularensis subspecies tularensis and mediasiatica [104]. Recognizing that the Francisella gene TUL4 is highly conserved in both Type A and Type B strains, a two-pronged study was conducted using both RT-RPA and RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a, with TUL4 as the target gene. The RT-RPA component delivered results consistent with RT-PCR within 10 min, making it suitable for rapid screening. The RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a component provided a more accurate diagnosis within 40 min, achieving impressive LOD of 0.5 copies per reaction [105].

Protein-based detection

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a major component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, is often considered a potential diagnostic antigen for tularemia due to its abundance on the bacterial surface and its potential to be shed at detectable levels [106]. A study designed a highly sensitive near-infrared (NIR) photothermal Lateral Flow Immunoassay (LFIA) platform based on gold nanostars (GNS). This approach integrates photothermal technology into LFIA, improving sensitivity by several orders of magnitude compared to colorimetric LFIA. The study used GNS-3 probes, which have numerous branches and superior photothermal conversion efficiency, to replace traditional gold nanospheres. The GNS convert absorbed light energy into heat, and the signal is captured by a smartphone-integrated thermal imaging camera, achieving an outstanding LOD of 3.5 pg/mL [102]. However, a defined clinical range for LPS concentration in patient samples for diagnostic purposes has not yet been established. Further research is needed to characterize LPS shedding during infection to determine how this biomarker can be most effectively detected [107]. A different detection is based on an antibody targeting the outer membrane protein A (OmpA), a member of the OmpA family, to distinguish it from pathogens like Brucella spp., Yersinia enterocolitica, Vibrio cholerae, and Escherichia coli. The sandwich ELISA was established to investigate the feasibility of the proposed assay for target detection [108].

VHF sensor platforms

Two families of viruses that cause Viral Hemorrhagic Fever (VHF) are classified as Class A bioweapon agents: Filoviruses (Ebola and Marburg viruses) and Arenaviruses (Lassa virus). The most recent major outbreak was the West African Ebola epidemic. Large-scale Ebola Virus Disease (EVD) outbreaks are typically caused by three subtypes: EBOV, SUDV, and BDBV. Their single-stranded RNA genome encodes seven viral proteins, including the glycoprotein (GP), the polymerase (L), VP40, and others [109].

Genetic detection

Barnes et al. developed an RPA-CRISPR/Cas13a assay targeting the L gene, integrated with HUDSON (Heating Unextracted Diagnostic Samples to Obliterate Nucleases), which inactivates pathogens and releases nucleic acids through combined thermal and chemical denaturation. Utilizing the SHERLOCK (Specific High-sensitivity Enzymatic Reporter UnLOCKing) platform, the assay couples RPA with crRNA-guided Cas13a specific binding. Upon target recognition, the collateral cleavage activity of Cas13a severs single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) reporter probes, separating the fluorophore from the quencher to generate a fluorescent signal. The results are finally read out via lateral flow strips and fluorescence detection. This approach enables POCT using only a low-energy, single-temperature hot plate and basic pipetting supplies, achieving high safety at a cost of less than $1 per sample [110].

Generally, CRISPR diagnostics still rely on nucleic acid pre-amplification, multiple crRNA selection, or specialized probes and equipment to achieve higher sensitivity. To address this, Hang et al. introduced a simple and accurate follow-up amplification strategy into a batch CRISPR/Cas13a system to enhance sensitivity without compromising accuracy. This system utilizes two types of functionalized DNA-Au nanoparticles, modified with hairpin DNA H0 (which generates the roller starter primer L-Au upon cleavage) and FAM-H1 (the 3D DNA track), respectively. Once the crRNA-Cas13a complex recognizes and binds to the target RNA, the activated Cas13a enzyme cleaves the rArU sites in the loop of H0, exposing L-Au. This L-Au primer then acts as a "roller" moving along the 3D DNA tracks based on a Catalytic Hairpin Assembly (CHA) strategy to amplify the detection signal. Specifically, the L-Au roller anchors to the toeholds on FAM-H1 via its "legs," which are subsequently displaced by free hairpin DNA (H2) through the CHA reaction to form FAM-H1:H2 duplexes. Successive CHA reactions enable the roller to walk autonomously along the 3D track, generating a vast amount of FAM-H1:H2 duplexes on the microparticle surface through continuous binding and release. This process separates the FAM fluorophores from the AuNPs (quenchers), resulting in a high fluorescence signal and achieving a LOD of 291 aM [111].

Furthermore, Shu et al. developed Lift-CM, an integrated lift-heating centrifugal microfluidic platform. This platform addresses the inherent risk of aerosol contamination in traditional CRISPR/Cas assays while fulfilling the requirements for integration and automation in POCT. The platform utilizes a spatially encoded centrifugal disc (SEC-disc) to physically segregate the isothermal amplification process from CRISPR/Cas12a detection. A two-stage centrifugal injection mechanism enables closed reagent delivery, which effectively prevents aerosol contamination and expands the capacity for multi-target detection. Its innovative dual-temperature "lift-and-heat" mechanism automatically switches between heating and centrifugation; the dual-zone design ensures compatibility with various isothermal amplification methods and enables a fully automated, manual-intervention-free workflow. Additionally, an optical detection system with a full field-of-view (FOV) allows for the efficient monitoring of multiple reaction channels, ensuring data consistency. A dedicated smartphone application is used to configure key parameters and monitor the experimental process in real time. This platform offers an integrated, efficient, portable, and cost-effective solution for diagnostics in resource-limited settings and emergency scenarios [112].

Additionally, Gao et al. developed a simplified click reaction (SCR) platform leveraging Cu(I)-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition to achieve the one-step assembly of multilayer graphene oxide (GO) nanosheets onto magnetic beads (MBs). This approach addresses the issues of tedious procedures and time-consuming washing steps inherent in traditional layer-by-layer (LbL) self-assembly techniques. Specifically, the first layer of GO is immobilized on the MB surface via target DNA hybridization. Subsequently, these MBs are introduced into the SCR platform, where the self-assembly of multilayer GO serves as a potent signal amplification strategy. Finally, the resulting GO-MB complexes are collected and incubated with hemin to exhibit peroxidase-like activity, allowing for detection through the colorimetric oxidation of TMB. This method achieved a highly sensitive LOD of 4.89 pM and demonstrated broad universality. However, while the assembly process is a single step, the complexity of the preliminary material preparation poses significant challenges for controlling batch-to-batch consistency of the reagents [113].

Protein-based detection

The most abundant viral protein, VP40, has been selected as the diagnostic marker for several commercial RDTs that have received FDA Emergency Use Authorization (EUA). However, these RDTs are incapable of differentiating between virus species or quantifying viral load [109,114]. An immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) assay developed for the detection and quantification of the VP40 antigen can be performed using only a miniature mass spectrometer, offering high safety and the ability to differentiate between various Ebola virus subtypes. However, its sensitivity is insufficient for detecting low viral loads. Consequently, the assay is better suited for optimizing biosafety practices—such as validating virus inactivation methods—and for testing alternative specimen types (e.g., cerebrospinal fluid or semen) to monitor viral persistence in Ebola survivors [115].

The EBOV secreted glycoprotein (sGP) is the first and primary product of the GP gene's transcription and translation. It acts as a decoy antigen and appears in the serum earlier than other viral proteins, offering an opportunity to diagnose infection before it becomes contagious. Furthermore, non-human primate (NHP) studies suggest a potential prognostic value for sGP at a threshold of approximately 1000 ng/mL [116]. A single-step sandwich immunoassay was developed using single-chain capture antibodies identified through M13 phage display and high-throughput antibody pairing strategies. These capture antibodies are inkjet-printed as microdots onto glass chips coated with a cell- and protein-resistant polymer brush. Fluorescently labeled detection antibodies are printed on a soluble trehalose pad surrounding the capture antibody dots. When the sample solution dissolves the trehalose pad, it releases the detection antibodies to bind with sGP, forming a sandwich structure with the capture antibodies. To read the fluorescent signals, a low-cost, portable wide-field fluorescence reader (D4Scope) was developed. The antifouling properties of the polymer brush minimize background noise and enhance sensitivity, achieving a LOD of 20 pg/mL—1000 times more sensitive than FDA-approved RDTs—while maintaining robust performance in complex sample matrices. Furthermore, the platform has been evolved into D4 detection chips as self-contained passive microfluidic channel units to enable one-step operation, thereby providing enhanced biosafety and biocontainment capabilities [117]. Qavi et al. combined LFA with plasmonic fluorophores (PFs) as reporter molecules. The brightness of the PF fluorescent probes was enhanced by silver-gold nanorods, enabling an ultra-sensitive, quantitative, and rapid test, although it was noted to be insufficient for predicting or monitoring sGP level trends in infected patients [118]. Nano2RED, a nanobody-functionalized nanoparticle assay, utilizes antibodies selected through rapid phage-based affinity reagent platforms and conjugated to AuNPs. Upon antigen binding, centrifugation and vortex mixing are employed to accelerate the aggregation and sedimentation of the AuNPs, which are then analyzed via spectrophotometry or portable electronic circuitry. This method exhibits exceptional sensitivity and specificity, achieving a LOD for sGP of 10 pg/mL, though further validation using real-world specimens is required. Notably, this platform enables the swift establishment of rapid and accessible antigen diagnostic tools within just a few weeks of initial pathogen identification [119].

Orthopoxvirus sensor platforms

To our knowledge, no new RDTs have been recently developed for smallpox. However, the recent pandemic of mpox, which belongs to the same Orthopoxvirus genus as smallpox, has highlighted the inherent high transmissibility of Orthopoxvirus members [120]. The development of rapid detection methods for these viruses aims to address their global re-emergence against a backdrop of waning smallpox immunity [121]. Furthermore, these technological advancements serve as a benchmark and reference for developing detection platforms for other potential bioterrorism agents.

Genetic detection

The primary target genes for Mpox virus (MPXV) nucleic acid amplification include B6R and ITR (recommended by the U.S. CDC), F3L (recommended by the China CDC), and the commonly targeted ATI and D14L [122]. CRISPR-based detection methods have been widely adopted, especially in one-pot self-amplifying systems that combine CRISPR with various isothermal amplification techniques (such as RPA [123], MIRA [124], HAD [125], and RCA [126]). These systems enhance sensitivity while reducing the risk of aerosol contamination from amplification products, achieving LODs in the attomolar range. Researchers have also expanded signal reporting modalities, favoring "signal-on" systems (e.g., G-quadruplex DNAzyme catalysis) [127] and incorporating instrument-free platforms like LFAs [123] and microfluidic colorimetric readouts [35] to enable visual detection without specialized equipment.

Specifically, A universal, single-tube RPA-CRISPR fluorescence assay has been developed, which precisely modulates the cis-cleavage activity of Cas12 by using low-cost heparin sodium to interfere with Cas12a-crRNA binding. Specifically, the negatively charged heparin sodium binds to the positively charged Cas12a protein, inhibiting its cleavage activity during the early stages of amplification, thereby enhancing overall amplification efficiency. Driven by the accumulation of isothermal amplification products, the assay generates a significant fluorescent signal within 15–20 min, achieving a LOD as low as 0.6 copies/μL. Furthermore, this platform is compatible with both canonical and sub-optimal PAM sequences, as well as various Cas12a subtypes [128].

Another study utilized dual signal enhancement from both CRISPR and AuNPs to further improve the sensitivity of Surface Plasmon Resonance imaging (SPRi). By combining CRISPR-Cas12a with phase-interrogative SPRi for label-free, amplification-free, and real-time multiplexed nucleic acid detection, the activated Cas12a cleaves AuNP-ssDNA probes. This causes the AuNPs to detach from the chip surface, inducing a significant change in the surface refractive index. This change is captured by the phase-sensitive SPRi system and converted into a quantifiable electrical signal, achieving an ultra-low LOD of 1 aM. This amplification-free assay achieves exceptional sensitivity, delivering attomolar-level detection limits and single-nucleotide specificity while simultaneously mitigating the risks of contamination and variability. By further integrating microfluidic systems, optimizing optical architectures, and implementing accelerated signal processing algorithms, the platform enhances operational simplicity and response efficacy. These advancements collectively address the stringent requirements for resolution consistency and time-sensitive diagnostics [129].

To rapidly differentiate multiple dermotropic pathogens, a portable POCT device was developed. It combines a simple, power-free nucleic acid extraction and purification method based on magnetic beads with lyophilized colorimetric LAMP technology. This allows for the simultaneous detection and differentiation of pathogens like Orthopoxvirus, mpox (Clade I and II), and Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) without requiring a cold chain. The system is paired with an Android app for integration with medical databases. This approach minimizes operational turnaround time and the reliance on sophisticated instrumentation, ensuring robustness across diverse environmental conditions. Furthermore, this easily manufacturable and versatile system can be rapidly repurposed for various sample matrices and molecular targets to meet emerging diagnostic needs. However, the platform remains constrained by the non-quantitative nature of LAMP technology and its inherent susceptibility to instability caused by pH fluctuations [121].

Protein-based detection

The A29 protein's location on the exterior of the intracellular mature virus (IMV), its abundance, and its highly conserved amino acid sequence make it an ideal antigen for detection. An electrochemical sensor has been developed using differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) with a ferricyanide/ferrocyanide solution as the redox probe. The formation of an immunocomplex, resulting from the binding of the A29 protein to antibodies immobilized on the electrode surface, hinders the electron transfer process of the redox probe. This leads to a reduction in the redox peak current, which serves as the analytical signal. While this label-free platform demonstrates excellent sensitivity in a non-invasive simulated saliva matrix, its convenience for POC testing is constrained by the lengthy incubation and probe stabilization periods, as well as its limited storage stability [130].

Based on interferometric optics, an automated Interferometric Reflective Imaging Sensor (IRIS) platform enables digital detection by capturing light signals following antigen binding. The platform employs a novel imaging technique featuring multi-parameter particle excitation and specialized imaging sensors; when particles are excited by spatially and spectrally uniform light, encoded particle signals are extracted via a conventional color CMOS sensor, allowing for the reconstruction of target nanoparticles from a single image. Through the integration of microfluidics and a robust signal transduction mechanism—which remains resilient against environmental factors such as humidity and temperature—this automated and compact benchtop device achieves faster and more sensitive detection than conventional ELISA, with a LOD as low as 3.3 aM. However, it currently lacks validation using real-world clinical samples [131].

To further advance the development of portable devices integrating extraction and diagnosis, a microneedle (MN) sensor has been designed for the direct extraction of MPXV from skin lesions. This platform integrates electrochemical microneedles with a colorimetric hydrogel, utilizing multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) as the counter electrode (CE) and Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode (RE). Upon antibody-antigen binding, the oxidation of TMB catalyzed by MnO2–Au nanozymes is inhibited; due to steric hindrance, the charge transfer resistance at the electrode surface increases, leading to a progressive decrease in the TMB oxidation peak current as measured via square wave voltammetry (SWV). Simultaneously, the suppressed oxidation of TMB results in a visible color fading of the hydrogel, enabling dual-mode colorimetric detection. The LODs for the electrochemical and colorimetric modes are 0.1632 ng/mL and 0.3017 ng/mL, respectively. Although the design includes a control region for normal samples to provide a quality control (QC) function, it is important to note that microneedle-based extraction remains susceptible to contamination from unclean skin or lesion exudates [132].

Botulism sensor platforms

Clostridium botulinum serotypes A, B, E, and F are commonly associated with human foodborne botulism [133]. Among these, Botulinum Neurotoxin serotype A (BoNT/A) is the most potent neurotoxin, while the diagnosis of BoNT/B is often complicated by high false-positive and low false-negative rates [134]. Detection of BoNT serotypes can be achieved by assessing the toxin's biological activity or enzymatic activity, for which the mouse lethality assay (MLA) serves as the current gold standard. Alternatively, non-function-dependent methods, such as direct protein detection or antibody-based assays, can also be used [135].

Toxin's biological activity detection

An optimized EGFP-based FRET-pair molecular sensor was developed to analyze the endopeptidase activity of BoNT/A without requiring UV excitation. This sensor exhibits high sensitivity at the picomolar (pM) level and is well-suited for in vitro BoNT/A bioactivity assays. A cell-based sensor, also based on FRET, was constructed to enable real-time monitoring of the entire toxin action process. However, the relatively complex design and specialized materials associated with this platform currently limit its practical application primarily to laboratory settings [134].

Inspired by the cost-effectiveness and user-friendliness of commercial pregnancy test strips, a sensing platform was developed utilizing magnetic nanoparticles modified with hCG-functionalized peptides. The mechanism relies on the specific hydrolysis of the SNAP-25 peptide substrate by BoNT/A, which cleaves the MN-peptide-hCG complex and releases hCG-peptide probes into the solution. Following magnetic separation, the liberated hCG-peptide probes are recognized and detected by the pregnancy test strip. This platform achieves a visual LOD of 12.5 pg/mL for the BoNT/A light chain (LcA); however, its sensitivity toward the holotoxin requires further enhancement to meet the rigorous demands of clinical diagnostics [136].

Additionally, to simultaneously reduce costs and eliminate antibody dependency, the first antibody-free paper-based sensor was developed for the detection of BoNT/A and BoNT/C. This platform utilizes the specific cleavage of methylene blue (MB)-labeled synthetic peptides immobilized on AuNP-modified paper electrodes. The enzymatic action of the toxins leads to a decrease in the electrochemical signal, the magnitude of which is directly proportional to the neurotoxin concentration in the sample. While the sensor exhibits high sensitivity with a LOD of 10 pM, its short storage stability (7 days) poses a significant challenge for logistics distribution and stockpiling as a commercialized diagnostic kit [137].

Immunophenotyping detection

Although MS has demanding equipment requirements, its capabilities in serotyping and high-throughput analysis make it valuable for certain applications. To enhance the comprehensiveness of serotype identification, both antibody-independent and antibody-dependent high-resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS) assays have been developed, catering to rapid screening and complex sample analysis, respectively. These detection methods utilize in silico analysis to identify relevant peptides and have been successfully applied to all eight BoNT/A subtypes [138]. Another benchtop method, BoNT Endopep-MS, utilizes magnetic beads coated with serotype-specific antibodies to extract toxins, enabling the detection of various BoNT serotypes [139].

To address challenges regarding sensitivity thresholds, assay duration, and field-readiness, a Fabry-Pérot interferometer based on a SiO2/Si platform was developed to identify toxin types and evaluate activity through competitive immunoassay and endopeptidase activity analysis, respectively. In the competitive immunoassay, target antigens first react with antibodies in solution, after which the remaining free antibodies bind to mimetic antigens immobilized on the chip surface. HRP-labeled secondary antibodies then catalyze an oxidation reaction to form an insoluble precipitate, altering the refractive index of the SiO2/Si surface and generating a negatively correlated signal—where higher toxin concentrations result in weaker signals. For activity detection, substrate cleavage causes peptide fragments to detach from the chip; the remaining intact substrates are then bound to secondary antibodies, producing optical signals via oxidation. This platform demonstrates robust adaptability for complex matrix analysis and can be integrated into portable handheld spectrometers for on-site evaluation. It offers superior performance in sensitivity, portability, and turnaround time compared to traditional or other advanced rapid detection technologies, achieving LODs of 0.14 pg/mL for BoNT/C and 0.47 pg/mL for BoNT/B within a 2 h detection window [140].

Additionally, a handheld breath analyzer has been developed to provide real-time, non-invasive early warning by identifying five potential volatile biomarkers of BoNT intoxication—namely acetone, styrene, ethanol, 2-pentanone, and n-butane. By integrating wearable temperature sensors, the device enables the simultaneous monitoring of variations in breath signals and fluctuations in body temperature. Although primarily suited for medical aesthetic contexts and lacking high pathological specificity, its non-invasive and instantaneous nature facilitates self-monitoring and early alert capabilities for health-conscious individuals [141].

Summary and outlook of detection technologies for category A biothreat agents

Overall, our specific analysis of the development of biosensing technologies for Category A biothreat agents is structured across several dimensions (Table 2). On one hand, from the perspective of technical advancement, we focus on innovations in detection methodologies and improvements in signal amplification strategies to overcome bottlenecks in critical metrics such as sensitivity, LOD, and specificity, thereby enhancing overall analytical performance. On the other hand, we consider analytical parameters related to practical utility, including portability, cost-effectiveness, and robustness against sample matrix effects. Sensing technologies for category A biological threat agents.

Genetic detection

Our review analysis indicates that gene-targeted detection for various threat agents aims, on one hand, to achieve maximum coverage across entire genera or genetically similar groups; simultaneously, the development of multi-target assays for the same pathogen is intended to provide high-precision identification of species and quantification of pathogen loads. In this context, highly stable and comprehensive techniques such as PCR and MS are commonly employed. However, technologies offering superior sensitivity, specificity, precision, and throughput—namely Single-Molecule Array (SIMOA) and Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) [142] —warrant further research investment. Single-molecule methods enable the detection of low-concentration rare biomarkers and mutations, ensure precise quantification, and allow for the study of molecular heterogeneity within individual samples, thereby enhancing sensitivity, eliminating variability, and facilitating efficient re-testing. Furthermore, these methods provide real-time analytical capabilities to observe dynamic processes and interactions as they occur, offering higher specificity to distinguish between closely related biomarkers or variants [143]. On the other hand, CRISPR-based technologies have been increasingly adapted for POCT, particularly in response to recent infectious disease outbreaks such as Ebola and Mpox. Nevertheless, the majority of current CRISPR assays rely on nucleic acid pre-amplification, which entails several limitations, including prolonged assay duration, cumbersome operational steps, susceptibility to aerosol contamination, and the risk of false-positive results. To circumvent these issues, signal amplification can be further enhanced by coupling CRISPR systems with novel materials (e.g., nanomaterials and DNAzymes) and advanced methodologies, such as post-amplification strategies [144], hyper-localized reactors, and ultrasensitive all-in-one detection systems [145]. These advancements facilitate the development of amplification-free CRISPR biosensors to meet the urgent demand for rapid and highly specific detection. Furthermore, through integration with complementary technologies, the analytical scope of these platforms can be expanded from nucleic acids to a diverse range of non-nucleic acid targets [146].

Protein-based detection

For protein-targeted detection, research remains primarily focused on the development of portable flow-based platforms (e.g., ELISA, LFI, and VFI). In particular, the LFI is favored for its low cost and operational simplicity; however, the development and application of this technology remain relatively nascent. Given that sample complexity frequently results in diminished sensitivity and fluctuating signal intensities, sample pre-concentration schemes—such as ion concentration polarization [147] and ultrasonic enrichment [148]—can be implemented to facilitate the detection of low-abundance analytes within complex matrices like blood, saliva, or urine. The further integration of advanced optical modalities—including UCNPs [149], QDs [150], surface plasmon-enhanced fluorescence [151], FRET [152], and SERS—can significantly enhance target signals while suppressing background interference. This not only bolsters sensitivity but also enables rapid, user-friendly visual or instrumental optical readouts. Complementarily, low-power and rapid-response electrochemical techniques—such as amperometry, voltammetry, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)—can be incorporated into LFA platforms to facilitate the construction of miniaturized POCT systems with streamlined signal readout interfaces [153]. Notably, while electrochemical and optical sensor research is relatively extensive for Mpox and Anthrax, there is a notable scarcity of such studies for other biothreat agents. From a preventive standpoint, it is imperative to prioritize these gaps and strategically invest in next-generation sensor technologies, such as PEC sensors [154]—noted for their high sensitivity and ease of miniaturization—as well as the application of MOF/COF-based composite materials [155].

Finally, to realize fully integrated POCT devices, research platforms must further incorporate state-of-the-art microfluidic or end-to-end automated systems characterized by high integration, compact portability, and minimal reagent consumption. Notable examples include programmable gravity-driven microfluidic chips [156], computer-vision-based multiplexed microfluidic digital immunoassays [157], and microfiber interferometer-based multimode optofluidic chips [158]. These systems enable the execution of a comprehensive analytical suite—including sample pretreatment, multi-step sequential reactions [159], separation, timed fluidic control, and quantification—within a single miniaturized unit. Such integration leads to more precise sample handling, superior reproducibility, enhanced biomarker sensitivity, and accurate quantification [160]. For advanced disease diagnosis and monitoring, wearable biosensors can be further integrated. By leveraging non-invasive or minimally invasive approaches—combined with flexible materials, nanoparticle-based signal amplification, and wireless communication—these devices enhance the precision and sustainability of mobile monitoring. Examples of such technologies include microfluidic-integrated patches, microneedle-based patches [161], and wearable skin patches [162]. Furthermore, the synergy of AI and Machine Learning (ML) [163] can bolster detection automation, accuracy, and speed, ultimately providing predictive and personalized health solutions. In the following section, to enhance clinical feasibility, we further explore the development of rapid detection strategies from the perspective of clinical symptomatology.

Disease symptoms caused by biothreat agents

The medical diagnosis of a disease typically relies on a combination of patient-reported symptoms and signs, their medical and travel history, and a physical examination, supplemented by various medical tests [8]. However, many biothreat agents manifest as non-specific symptoms, such as fever, pain, myalgia and/or rashes. In situations involving a large-scale outbreak of patients with similar symptoms, or where medical resources are scarce, rapid diagnosis and the immediate initiation of effective management become critical first steps. Therefore, we have analyzed the clinical manifestations of Category A biothreat agents [45,[163], [164], [165], [166], [167], [168], [169], [170], [171], [172]], and categorized the symptomatic features into cutaneous, non-specific influenza-like, gastrointestinal, and severe (sepsis, meningitis) (Fig. 4), aiming to provide both a reference for medical specialists and a conceptual basis for adopting highly resource-efficient response strategies.Fig. 4Nonspecific Symptoms Caused by Biothreat Agents. a: Symptom classification categories. b: Pathogens reach high concentrations shortly after the onset of symptoms; conversely, antibodies are typically produced during the later stages of the disease and can persist for several years post-infection.Fig. 4

Exploring rapid detection and diagnostic strategies

Detection strategies for different disease stages

Incubation period

Diagnosing diseases during the incubation period is challenging due to the extremely low pathogen load before symptom onset. Furthermore, the complexity and hazardous nature of biothreat agents, combined with the complicated and widespread geographical distribution of cases resulting from potential multi-site attacks, can impede the assessment of an attack's scale and scope, leading to delays in deploying appropriate countermeasures [46]. Early-Stage Surveillance by employing sensors for environmental, animal population, and vector samples. These sensors should offer broad coverage of genetically similar pathogenic strains and feature multiplex targeting for single pathogens, alongside high-precision SIMOA and NGS to analyze species identification and mutation trends and achieve effective monitoring and precise screening. Following this, population monitoring should be conducted using sensors with high specificity, extremely low LODs, and quantitative capabilities. This allows for an understanding of the correlation between pathogen load and infection [173], which, when combined with event characterization tools [46,174] and coordinated with national and international early warning systems [175], can enhance the efficiency and appropriateness of response management.

Once symptoms appear, pathogens typically reach high concentrations in the bloodstream within the first few days [109], making antigen and molecular tests highly effective for acute diagnosis. At this stage, the focus of sensor design shifts toward accuracy and reliability within clinically defined ranges to secure the critical window for patient survival.

Skin symptoms

Our symptom analysis reveals that cutaneous symptoms, particularly papules, can occur in several Class A biothreat diseases, including anthrax (cutaneous, injectional), tularemia (ulceroglandular, glandular, oculoglandular), smallpox, bubonic plague, and Ebola. Cutaneous manifestations are often the first presenting sign of a covert bioterrorism event, making their early recognition crucial for epidemic control.

POCT capable of detecting multiple dermotropic pathogens are a vital tool for differentiation [77]. Microneedle sensors that can directly extract pathogens from skin lesions significantly enhance the efficiency and portability of detecting diseases with cutaneous symptoms [132]. Flexible wearable electronic sensors for wound detection and monitoring have been developed, enabling the comprehensive assessment of wound status and the prediction of healing outcomes [176]. Furthermore, a novel MOF-based nanozyme with intrinsic antibacterial properties has been designed. Through its finely-tuned catalytic centers and substrate channel structures, it effectively blocks bacterial energy metabolism pathways, thereby demonstrating significant potential for promoting the healing of infected wounds [177]. Additionally, for skin symptoms with distinct morphological features, AI-powered image recognition and intelligent diagnostics, linked with distributed computing and the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT), could potentially enable patient self-assessment and self-management. Connected to smart devices via Bluetooth or USB, these readers upload collected data to a cloud platform for automated AI analysis. The verified results are then delivered back to the patient's smart device [142].

Non-specific symptoms

Non-specific influenza-like symptoms occur during the prodromal phase of nearly all Class A biothreat agents (with the exception of botulism). Subsequently, many of these diseases can also present with gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms (e.g., GI anthrax, septicemic plague, Ebola, foodborne botulism). Both of them may progress to severe sepsis. Given the difficulty of differential diagnosis based on these early symptoms, multiplex sensors can serve as a first-line defense.

A high-throughput, multi-target fluorescent array platform combining Multi-Probe Amplification (MPA) with melt curve analysis can simultaneously detect eight potential bacterial biowarfare agents from complex samples, including the Class A agents for anthrax, plague, and tularemia. This platform overcomes the fluorescent channel limitations of traditional multiplex PCR by using melt curve technology to create a two-dimensional lock-in relationship between temperature and fluorescence for specific targets, achieving a 1.5 h detection time without nucleic acid extraction. However, it is better suited for fixed or mobile laboratories [178]. For portable, on-site monitoring, a study developed a method combining a paper-based dipstick for nucleic acid extraction with a microfluidic chip and LAMP. This portable system can simultaneously detect eight highly pathogenic viruses, including those causing hemorrhagic fevers, mpox, and smallpox, providing results in about 1 h [179]. Another study developed a VFI prototype using a portable syringe pump and a novel membrane holder. It detects antigens for plague (LcrV and F1) and tularemia (LPS) with LODs of 0.05 ng/mL for LcrV/F1 and 0.10 ng/mL for LPS, supporting its use in remote and resource-limited settings [180]. Integrated platforms can further reduce sample consumption, shorten processing time, and enhance detection efficiency. It also lowers the cost of acquiring multiple sensing platforms, diminishes the reliance on bulky and non-portable equipment, and allows for simplified training, enabling a rapid and safe response across community, government, and healthcare settings [10]. Furthermore, pathogen load monitoring facilitates patient risk stratification. For patients at risk of progressing to severe disease, the development of wearable patches, flexible electrodes, and soft microfluidic devices enables early monitoring and preventive intervention to improve clinical prognosis.

Severe symptoms

As progression to sepsis is a major cause of mortality, its early identification and prognostic prediction can significantly mitigate the life-threatening impact of bioterrorism. One sensing system uses deep learning-based hyperspectral imaging (HSI) analysis to monitor microcirculatory changes, enabling sepsis diagnosis and mortality prediction from a single HSI cube acquired in seconds [181]. Another approach employs a colorimetric gas sensor array to detect Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs) produced by bacteria, combined with a Rapid Sepsis Boost (RSBoost) algorithm to achieve high diagnostic accuracy (96.2%) in blood samples [182].

Anthrax and plague can progress to meningitis, which has a high mortality rate and thus requires special attention during triage. A screening tool for anthrax meningitis (designated 2A/2B and 2A/3) has been developed, which pairs algorithms with different combinations of symptoms and signs to rapidly and reliably assess patients, minimizing the proportion of patients requiring additional diagnostics [183]. Real-time quantitative detection in patients with neurological symptoms could enable accurate and effective monitoring. The use of AI algorithms for real-time spectral interpretation can facilitate precise biomarker detection and quantification through advanced data extraction, dimensionality reduction, and automated decision-making from complex samples [184]. Technologies for real-time monitoring of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) are emerging. For instance, micro-fabricated electrochemical and spectroscopic sensing technologies have been developed for precise monitoring of brain neurochemicals [185]. A multiplexed fiber optic sensor, modulated by a regression algorithm, can continuously detect the levels of multiple biomarkers in CSF [186]. Therefore, monitoring a panel of metabolites associated with bacterial meningitis has the potential to significantly improve diagnostic accuracy [187].

Challenges and opportunities for integrated detection systems

The future development of POCT systems can be categorized into several directions: 1) the miniaturization and automation of traditional laboratory instrumentation; 2) the development of single-use portable devices that integrate complex modules—including sample processing, reagent storage, and detection—leveraging advancements in microfabrication [188]; and 3) the integration of AI and ML into modern healthcare information systems. Nevertheless, several factors pose significant challenges to the advancement of POCT systems, namely the heterogeneity of sample preparation, the inherent complexity of integrated systems, and regulatory hurdles regarding clinical implementation.

Sample preparation

Sample preparation is a critical stage as it directly dictates the accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of an assay, while also representing a primary bottleneck in the POCT field [153]. Variability in the quality of biological sample collection can stem from extraction protocols, operator proficiency, specimen types, and storage conditions. Diverse matrices—including blood, urine, saliva, and tears—should be handled using strategic methods such as chemical stabilizers, lyophilization, and thermal regulation to preserve bio-activity and extend shelf life; meanwhile, rigorous precautions are necessary to prevent contamination from exogenous sources like sweat, saliva, or urine. Furthermore, appropriate personal protective equipment must be utilized based on the nature of the test and the hazardous materials involved [189].

In complex biological samples, interfering molecules and high-viscosity components can trigger non-specific adsorption, inhibit downstream nucleic acid amplification, or impede lateral flow kinetics, thereby severely compromising sensing efficiency. Measures such as cell lysis, target isolation, or enrichment are typically required to mitigate these matrix effects. Specifically, matrix effects can be alleviated through technical updates and strategies including: optimizing whole-blood separation and standardizing sample volumes to minimize target protein loss [153]; introducing blocking agents or non-ionic surfactants to reduce non-specific adsorption; and employing label-free detection to eliminate the complexity and background noise associated with chemical modifications [190].

Sample loading and pretreatment strategies must be miniaturized, automated, and cost-effective, integrating seamlessly with molecular detection technologies tailored to the sample type and target concentration. To achieve automated sample extraction, fluid transport, target concentration, and detection, a promising strategy is the integration of microfluidic chips—incorporating pretreatment modules such as silica-based solid-phase extraction, magnetic separation, or porous and paper-based materials [191]—into smartphone-based POC devices. However, the high cost and large-scale manufacturing complexity of microfluidic platforms remain significant barriers to their widespread application that remain to be addressed.

Clinical application

Integrating detection into broader extended care information systems is essential for significantly enhancing clinical productivity [192]. However, a lack of interoperability between systems can lead to ineffective care delivery, thereby creating additional operational burdens. While the integration of AI has substantially advanced POCT systems, it faces significant challenges regarding training data [193] and interpretability. Training data is vital for model evaluation and parameter tuning; however, hardware variations—such as changes in materials, sensing units, or signal readers—along with environmental fluctuations during testing, can compromise AI-based prediction and classification. This often results in imprecise and inconsistent outcomes. Furthermore, the "black-box" nature of AI decision-making remains a concern; more explicit and explainable decisions are required to reduce errors and facilitate accurate diagnosis [194].

POCT devices encounter hurdles at every stage of clinical implementation. Particularly as they expand into diverse environments for use by non-professionals, they must adhere to stringent mandatory regulations to ensure safety, efficacy, and market compliance. Regulatory bodies, such as the FDA and the European Union's In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR), enforce rigorous standards to safeguard patient safety and product efficacy. Nevertheless, various issues—ranging from pricing, availability in low- and middle-income countries, and a lack of quantitative data, to regulatory barriers (including inconsistent cross-regional approvals) and the constant need to update regulatory frameworks in response to evolving technologies [195]—hinder the global adoption and seamless integration of POCT into existing healthcare infrastructures [153].

Conclusion and perspective

In summary, the key is not only to pursue technological advancements in detection but also to implement appropriate, stage-specific strategies that correspond to the different phases of disease progression. Integrating emerging technologies like AI and the IoT can enhance the efficiency of epidemic control and optimize resource allocation when responding to a bioterrorism event. However, many unresolved concerns and needs remain. Furthermore, research in the field of biodefense has the potential to impact many aspects of infectious disease control and public health. It is therefore worthy of further funding and support to strengthen both global and local biodefense capabilities [175].
