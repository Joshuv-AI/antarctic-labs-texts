Dual regulation of HEPN RNase in fused MNT-HEPN toxin-antitoxin systems via protein OligoAMPylation and oligomerization
Authors: Jianyun Yao, Zhe Chen, Xiangkai Zhen, Pingjing Zhang, Ran Chen, Xingyi Fei, Cong Liu, Xinyu Liu, Xiaoxue Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnzymatic toxins targeting nucleic acids are a widespread strategy for resolving biological conflicts. Ubiquitous HEPN domain-containing proteins harbor a conserved RNase motif and are found in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes. In prokaryotes, HEPN domain toxins frequently pair with adjacent MNT domain antitoxins to form the prevalent type VII HepT/MntA toxin-antitoxin (TA) system. Here, we identified a group of fused MNT-HEPN proteins (renamed FhepTA) carried by mobile genetic elements in the Shewanella genus; these proteins show high similarity to the HepT/MntA TA systems. When encoded as separate genes, the MntA antitoxin oligoAMPylates the HEPN domain toxin HepT to block its toxicity. While FhepTA toxicity is also blocked by oligoAMPylation, it is additionally regulated by a redox switch involving two unique cysteines within its HEPN domain. Furthermore, the N-terminal MNT domain intrinsically inhibits toxicity by preventing dimerization of the C-terminal HEPN domain. Thus, the fused MNT-HEPN architecture represents a new family of TA modules potentially regulated in both ATP-dependent (via oligoAMPylation) and redox-state-dependent oligomerization.

Introduction

Biological conflicts at various levels, from intercellular competition to host–pathogen interactions, often employ enzymatic toxins as a key strategy [1, 2]. Among these toxins, nucleic acid-targeting toxins play crucial roles in shaping the evolutionary dynamics of both attackers and defenders [3]. One such class of toxins is defined by the HEPN (Higher Eukaryotes and Prokaryotes Nucleotide-binding) domain, a conserved structural motif that encompasses a short RNase consensus sequence. The HEPN domain was initially identified at the C-terminus of human Sacsin, a chaperonin linked to early-onset neurodegenerative disease, and subsequently found to be conserved across all three domains of life [4]. These domains typically form α-helical bundles that facilitate binding to RNA or DNA, enabling diverse biological functions involved in nucleotide interactions [5, 6]. In prokaryotes, HEPN domains are integral to defense mechanisms, such as those within the Cas13 and Csm6 proteins in the CRISPR-Cas systems, which function to cleave RNAs during viral immunity [7, 8]. In eukaryotes, the HEPN domain is also one of the domains in multiple-domain RNA processing and degradation proteins, such as Ire1, RNase L and RNase PNK [9]. They participate in stress responses and RNA interference pathways, safeguarding genomic integrity by targeting aberrant genetic material [10, 11]. Thus, HEPN domain proteins represent a critical evolutionary link of RNA processing between eukaryotes and prokaryotes.

Toxin-antitoxin (TA) systems are widespread in bacteria and archaea, playing crucial roles in stress responses and defense against phages [12–14]. These systems typically consist of a toxic protein (toxin) and its cognate inhibitory molecule, which can be a protein or RNA (antitoxin). Toxins often target essential cellular processes, such as DNA replication or translation, by damaging DNA or degrading RNAs, leading to growth arrest or cell death[3]. Consequently, TA systems are tightly regulated to maintain a neutralized state, primarily by ensuring a stoichiometric excess of antitoxin over toxin under steady-state conditions [15]. Originally, only four types of TA systems (I-IV) were defined based on the nature and mechanism of the antitoxin. Subsequent discoveries revealed additional types, including the type V antitoxin with endoRNase activity that specifically cleaves the toxin’s mRNA [16], the type VI antitoxin acts as a proteolytic adapter to promote degradation of the toxin by cellular proteases [17], the type VII antitoxin directly modifies the toxin protein post-translationally to neutralize it [18, 19] and the type VIII antitoxins are RNAs that repress the expression of their cognate RNA toxins either by acting as an antisense RNA or by recruiting a Cas protein acting as a transcriptional repressor [20, 21].

The most prevalent forms of HEPN domains in prokaryotes exist as single-domain proteins. These HEPN proteins are often genetically adjacent to genes encoding single-domain MNT (minimal nucleotidyltransferase) proteins [5, 22, 23]. Recent studies have established that these HEPN proteins function as toxin components (HepT) with RNase activity, while the neighboring MNT proteins function as their cognate antitoxins (MntA) possessing nucleotidyltransferase (NTase) activity. Together, they constitute a widespread family of type VII TA systems. Studies in Shewanella oneidensis, Aphanizomenon flosaquae, and Legionella pneumophila revealed that the MntA antitoxin inactivates HepT’s endoRNase activity by transferring two or more AMP moieties onto a conserved tyrosine residue adjacent to the RϕxxxH (where ϕ is the polar residue and x is any amino acid) motif within the HepT toxin [18, 24, 25]. This oligoAMPylation mechanism suggests that these type VII TA systems may function as cellular ATP sensors, potentially contributing to antiviral defense or growth control under stress conditions.

While type VII and type II TA systems share the commonality of protein-based antitoxins, they fundamentally differ in their neutralization mechanisms [26]. Type II antitoxins directly bind their cognate toxins via protein–protein interactions, whereas type VII antitoxins are enzymes that post-translationally modify their cognate toxins. Intriguingly, MNT antitoxins exhibit features reminiscent of type II antitoxins, including the ability to form complexes with their cognate toxins [18]. Furthermore, significant variability exists in the binding stoichiometry between MntA antitoxin and HepT toxin across species, observed as ratios of 2:6 in S. oneidensis, 2:2 in A. flosaquae, and 1:4 in L. pneumophila. Interestingly, two-domain proteins where the HEPN and MNT domains are fused within a single gene also exist in prokaryotes and eukaryotes. This unique configuration, forming a monocistronic unit, represents a notable departure from the typical bicistronic operon structure of most TA systems. Such fused arrangements are also observed in type II TA systems, including HicAB, VbhTA and FaRel2/ATfaRel2, and the antitoxin domain inhibits the biochemical activity of the toxin or blocks substrate binding via physical interaction with the toxin domain [27–29]. Recent work on the fusion form CapRelSJ46 has elucidated a mechanism in which the C-terminal domain sterically blocks the N-terminal toxin domain, with inhibition relieved upon phage protein binding [29, 30]. Despite these insights, the neutralizing mechanism within HEPN-MNT fusions remains enigmatic. In particular, it is unclear how the MNT domain effectively suppresses the HEPN domain within the same polypeptide chain under conditions of invariable 1:1 stoichiometry.

Through bioinformatic analysis, we identified a group of fused MNT-HEPN proteins (designated FhepTA) within the Shewanella genus. FhepTA exhibits high similarity to the canonical HepT/MntA TA systems and is predominantly encoded on mobile genetic elements. Comparative functional studies between FhepTA and homologous, non-fused HepT/MntA TA pairs in marine Shewanella species revealed that, in the fused form, the C-terminal HEPN endoRNase activity control is not only mediated by the N-terminal MNT domain via oligoAMPylation but also modulated by the intracellular redox state, likely through modification of conserved cysteine residues within the HEPN domain. Therefore, FhepTA represents a novel fused TA system featuring a complex regulatory network, where toxin activation is tightly coupled to the bacterial metabolic state, integrating signals from the cellular ATP and the redox levels.

Materials & methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids and growth conditions

The strains of S. oneidensis MR-1, S. putrefaciens W3-18-1, Shewanella sp. MR-7 and E. coli K-12, as well as the plasmids used in this study, are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The experiments were conducted at 37 °C for E. coli and at 30 °C for Shewanella strains in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium. DAP (2,6-diamino-pimelic acid) was added at a final concentration of 0.3 mM when E. coli WM3064 was cultured. Chloramphenicol (30 µg/ml), kanamycin (50 µg/ml), gentamycin (30 µg/ml) or spectinomycin (100 µg/ml) was added when the cells harbored the plasmids with the indicated resistance genes listed in Supplementary Table S1 or were used to block translation. When needed, 10 mM L-arabinose (L-Ara) or 0.05–1 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added as an inducer. For growth and cell viability (CFU) tests, the cells were cultured in LB supplemented with Cm or ampicillin, and L-Ara or IPTG was added at OD600 ∼ 0.1 as inducers. The cells induced for 2 h were serially diluted, dropped onto LB plates and incubated at 37°C for 12 h.

Construction of plasmids and deletion strains

For plasmid construction, the full coding region of fhepTA, its MNT domain (1–132 aa) or HEPN domain (136–276 aa) was cloned and individually inserted into pHGE for overexpression in E. coli K-12. A stop codon (TAG) was introduced after the MNT domain, and a start codon (ATG) was added before the HEPN domain to enable their expression. For expression of the split-form fhepTAS, the MNT and HEPN domains were cloned and inserted into pHGE and pBAD, respectively, and co-expressed in a single E. coli host using a two-plasmid system. Similarly, the coding regions of the mntAW3-18-1, hepTW3-18-1 and mntA-hepTW3-18-1 operons were cloned and inserted into pHGE to overexpress them in E. coli K-12. For protein purification, pET28b-based plasmids were constructed and introduced into E. coli BL21(DE3) hosts, and pCA24N-based plasmids were introduced into E. coli BW25113. During cloning into the pCA24N vector via the StuI site, ten extra residues (MRGSHHHHHHTDPALRT) were introduced into the N-terminus of the gene’s coding sequence. The lon gene and clpP-clpX operon were deleted from the S. oneidensis genome via homologous recombination using the pHGM01 suicide plasmid following a previously described procedure [31]. All primers used in this study are listed in Supporting Information Supplementary Table S2.

Mutagenesis

The mutations in the MNT domain of FhepTA were introduced by using a modified two-step PCR method via two internal primers and two external primers [20]. The PCR products generated via one of the primers containing the mutated sites were combined with one of the outside primers covering the whole gene or operon to generate a template for the second round of PCR, and the pair of outside primers was used for the second round of PCR to fuse the two PCR fragments from the first round of PCR (Supplementary Table S2). The two insider primers both have mutations in opposite directions. All the mutants were confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Protein stability assessment by western blot analysis

To detect the stability of the MNT domain and its variants in S. oneidensis, a pHGE-based plasmid with an N-terminal Flag tag was constructed. The cells harboring the indicated plasmids were cultured to OD600 ∼ 0.8, and then 0.05 mM IPTG was added for induction for another 30 min. Then, the cells were centrifuged thoroughly and refreshed in LB medium supplemented with Cm (50 µg/ml) to stop protein induction and cellular translation. Cell samples were collected at different time points ranging from 0 min to 60 min, and equal amounts of cells were used for western blot analysis. For the detection of in vivo forms of FhepTA in S. oneidensis, the upstream 300 bp region and the full coding region of fhepTA were cloned and inserted into the medium-copy plasmid pACYC184, which was tagged with an N-terminal Flag and C-terminal His tag. The same number of cells harboring the indicated plasmid was collected at different time points for western blotting.

HPLC-MS and ESI-MS analysis

The phosphodiesterase (PDEs) cleavage assay was conducted as previously described to characterize the small-nucleotide molecule products [18]. The modified FhepTA-CHis and MntA/HepTW3-18-1-CHis complexes were treated with PDEs at 37°C for 1 h to ensure that the modifications were fully cleaved. The small-molecule products collected were analyzed via LC‒MS. Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI‒MS) was performed in positive/negative mode via an integrated high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)/ESI‒MS system (1260 Infinity, Agilent Technologies/amaZon SL, Bruker Technologies) equipped with a Luna 5u C18 column (100A, 250*4.60 mm, 5 μm). Elution was performed with a linear gradient following the method described in a previous study [18]. The molecular weights of FhepTA-CHis and MntA/HepTW3-18-1-CHis before and after PDEs cleavage were determined via ESI/MS mass spectrometry, and the data presented by Origin Pro software are shown in Supplementary Table S5.

Structural analysis

The main FhepTA forms of structure in solution (monomer and dimer form) and homologues TA pairs HepT/MntAW3-18-1 (tetramer in solution, most likely with a ratio at 2:2) were predicted using AlphaFold3 via the AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com) [32]. AlphaFold3 generated five models without providing any additional constraints or templates. The model with the highest predicted template modeling score (pTM) for monomer and interface predicted template modeling score (ipTM) for multimer was selected for subsequent analysis. Molecular graphics and structural analyses were performed with PyMOL (version 2.6) [33].

Bacterial two-hybrid (BACTH) assay

For BACTH assays, the coding region of FhepTA, FhepTAT or FhepTA83-276 was both inserted into the pKT25 and pUT18C plasmid. The corresponding recombinant plasmids were co-transformed into E. coli BTH101(cya-99) competent cells. Co-transformed cells were plated on LB plates supplemented with ampicillin, kanamycin, IPTG and X-gal at final concentrations of 100 μg/ml, 50 μg/ml, 0.5 mM, and 40 μg/ml, respectively. The corrected cells were cultivated at 30°C for 12–24 h. The pKT25-zip and pUT18C-zip plasmids were used as positive controls (Euromedex), and the pKT25 (without insert) and pUT18C-zip (fused with a leucine zipper protein) plasmids were used as negative controls [34].

Protein purification and crosslinking

Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) strains carrying pET28b-based plasmids were grown in LB supplemented with kanamycin and induced with 0.2 mM IPTG for 12 h at 25°C. Specifically, to purify toxin HepTW3-18-1 and FhepTAT, which are toxic, stationary cells (OD600 ∼ 1.0) were induced with 0.5 mM IPTG for only 2 h at 37°C as previously described [35]. Cells expressing HepTW3-18-1, FhepTA or FhepTAT were collected, and the His-tagged proteins were purified under both reducing and non-reducing conditions. The reducing protocol included 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol in the lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0) throughout purification, and the final protein was desalinated with lysis buffer containing 1 mM DTT. For the non-reducing protocol, both the lysis buffer and the desalination buffer contain no reducing agent. Purification utilized Ni-NTA agarose beads as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Correspondingly, samples for SDS-PAGE were prepared using non-reducing (without DTT) or reducing (50 mM DTT) loading buffers.

To protect cysteine residues from artificial oxidation, FhepTA and FhepTAT proteins (in 1 × PBS, pH 7.4) purified under non-reducing conditions were treated with a 10- or 20-fold molar excess of N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) at 25 °C for 2 h before electrophoresis. The 10 µl reaction contained 0.1 mM protein and 1 or 2 mM NEM. Treated samples were then combined with non-reducing loading buffer for SDS-PAGE analysis.

For crosslinking of HepT, FhepTA or FhepTAT, proteins were purified using a 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5) buffer in the absence of reducing agents. Then, 40 μg of protein was incubated with a disuccinimidyl suberate (DSS) crosslinker (Pierce, 0–1000 µM) for 30 min at room temperature in a reaction volume of 40 μl. The reactions were stopped by adding 70 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), and then 10 μl samples was analyzed by 4–15% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis with reducing loading dye (50 mM DTT).

In vitro enzymatic assays

For the in vitro RNA cleavage assay, the ssRNA probe was synthesized by AZENTA (China) with 5′-FAM labeled (5′-AGUUUACAGCAUGGCUUCUAACUUUACUCAGUU

CGUUACUCGUCGACAA-3′). The assays were performed at 37°C for the indicated time periods. To compare the enzymatic activity of FhepTA, FhepTAT and HepTW3-18-1 under oxidized and reduced conditions, these proteins were purified from reducing and non-reducing conditions as described above. The reaction mixture for the RNase cleavage assay (5 μl) contained 100 nM RNA probe, 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2 and 500 nM protein. The reaction products were then detected by Urea-PAGE Gel (18%). When using bacteriophage MS2 RNA (Roche) as the substrate, a mixture of 1.0 μg MS2 RNA (∼160 nM) and 0.1 μg protein (600 ∼1200 nM) was incubated at 37°C for 20 min and then subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis.

The in vitro oligoAMPylation assay for unmodified FhepTAD41E, D43E and FhepTAY240F was performed using ATP as a substrate as previously described [18]. Briefly, purified FhepTAY240F (10 μg) was incubated in solution buffer (containing 20 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM DTT) supplemented with 10 μM ATP and incubated at 25°C for 30 min. Then, FhepTAD41E, D43E (20 μg) was added to the reaction mixture and incubated at 37°C for another 30 min. The products were further measured by MALDI-TOF to calculate the molecular weights.

Analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC)

Sedimentation velocity experiments were performed at 20°C in a Beckman Optima XLA ultracentrifuge equipped with absorbance optics and an An60 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA). Radial scans of absorbance were taken at 280 nm for FhepTA proteins (WT and variants), and sedimentation velocity experiments were performed at 30 000 rpm. The sedimentation coefficient was obtained via the c(s) method with the Sedfit program[36] and presented by Origin Pro software using the raw data in Supplementary Table S6.

Results

Two organizations of genes encoding MNT and HEPN protein domains

In the Shewanella oneidensis MR-1 genome, the adjacent genes mntA (SO_3165) and hepT (SO_3166), encoding MNT-domain antitoxin and HEPN-domain toxin, respectively, form a type VII TA pair. The 36-kb prophage CP4So is integrated into the tmRNA gene of MR-1. Cold-induced excision of this prophage via site-specific homologous recombination disrupts tmRNA function [37, 38]. Within CP4So, the gene SO_1440 encodes a 276-amino acid protein featuring an N-terminal MNT domain and a C-terminal HEPN domain (Fig. 1A). We propose naming this protein FhepTA (Fused HepT-MntA Toxin-Antitoxin) to show that it is a chimeric protein that merges the MNT and HEPN domains into one polypeptide. The N-terminal FhepTA contains the conserved nucleotidyltransferase (NTase) motif, although it shares only 28% sequence identity with the MntA antitoxin of MR-1. The C-terminal FhepTA retains the conserved RNase-active RϕxxxH motif and an adjacent Tyr residue and exhibits limited homology (34% identity) to the HepT toxin of MR-1.

Further bioinformatics analysis revealed that the MNT and HEPN domains in Shewanella species exist in two different configurations: as a two-gene TA system or as a single gene fused via a short linker (Fig. 1A). These genes, arranged as either single genes or two-gene operons, are predominantly located in prophages or mobile genetic islands. The two forms could share > 90% protein sequence similarity, but differ primarily at the junction where the fused form is connected by a short linker composed of 3–5 hydrophobic amino acids (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1).

For comparative studies, we selected two highly similar systems as representatives: the fused FhepTA (SO_1440) from S. oneidensis MR-1 and the non-fused homologous TA pair (HepTAW3-18-1) from S. putrefaciens W3-18-1, sharing 92% sequence similarity at the amino acid level and 87% at the nucleotide level (Fig. 1B). First, the hepTW3-18-1 gene and mntAW3-18-1 gene, along with their operon, were cloned and inserted into the IPTG-inducible pHGE plasmid to test their functionality as a TA pair in E. coli K-12. Cell viability assays showed that hepTW3-18-1 encodes a toxin, whereas mntAW3-18-1 encodes its cognate antitoxin, which fully neutralizes the toxin’s lethality when coexpressed (Fig. 1C). As expected, no toxicity was observed when the mntA-hepT operon was expressed via a pHGE-based plasmid, which is consistent with canonical TA system behavior. Similarly, the full-length fused FhepTA protein exhibited no toxicity (Fig. 1D). To determine whether the individual domains of FhepTA retain functionality, we individually cloned each of the two domains into pHGE. The N-terminal MNT domain (residues 1–132 aa) showed no toxicity, whereas the C-terminal HEPN domain (residues 136–276 aa) displayed strong toxicity. This finding confirms that the HEPN domain retains its cytotoxic activity, highlighting the functional conservation of its active RNase motif (Fig. 1D).

Next, we made specific modifications in the region connecting the two domains to transform the TA form to the fused form, and vice versa. In the TA form, the 15 bp intergenic region contains an RBS for the translation of HepT immediately following the stop codon of MntA (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Removing the stop codon of MntA and the start codon of HepT leads to a gene encoding a fused MntA-HepT protein (MntA-HepTF, 276 aa), with five residues added in the middle without altering the rest of the open reading frame of the hepT gene. Similar to the TA form, the fused protein did not demonstrate toxicity (Fig. 1C). Owing to the lack of a ribosomal binding site (RBS) and start codon ATG for the translation of hepn in the fhepTA gene, we employed a two-plasmid system (marked as FhepTAS with S standing for split-form) by cloning the MNT domain into pHGE to generate the fhepTAA gene and the HEPN domain (136–276 aa) into pBAD to generate the fhepTAT gene (Fig. 1D). Both proteins were produced using the RBS and start codon of the plasmids via the addition of inducers in E. coli. Unexpectedly, in the FhepTAS form, FhepTAA demonstrated a limited ability to neutralize the toxic effect of FhepTAT, despite efforts to increase the induction level of the MNT domain (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, when expressed in S. oneidensis, both FhepTAT and HepTW3-18-1 were toxic, with HepTW3–18–1 exhibiting greater toxicity than FhepTAT as measured by growth and cell viability assays (Supplementary Fig. S2). Collectively, these results suggest that the different organizations of MNT- and HEPN domain-containing proteins differ in the way HEPN toxicity is regulated.

MNT transfers three AMPs to Tyr adjacent to the active motif of HEPN in both forms

Previous studies have demonstrated that HepT toxins are polyadenylated by their cognate MntA antitoxins. However, the degree of polyadenylation varies across TA systems in different bacterial hosts. For example, HepT in MR-1 is modified with three AMP moieties, whereas the HepT homolog in Aeromonas flos-aquae incorporates only two AMPs [18, 24]. Since the covalent tethering of its N-terminal MNT and C-terminal HEPN domains could sterically hinder the conformational dynamics essential for MntA-mediated polyadenylation, it remains to be determined whether the fused FhepTA protein retains polyadenylation activity and what type of modification could occur.

To test this, the nucleotide modification of the FhepTA protein and two types of mutant proteins were determined by phosphodiesterase (PDEs) digestion followed by mass analysis as described previously [18, 24]. The two types of mutants include FhepTAD41E, D43E variant with the key NTase motif GSx10DxD mutated to GSx10ExE, and the FhepTAY240F variant with the Tyr (Y in RϕxxxHxY) mutated to Phe. The C-terminal His-tagged FhepTA protein, as well as these two mutated proteins, were purified via pET28b-based vectors in the E. coli host. These proteins were subsequently subjected to PDEs digestion, which removes the modified nucleotides attached to amino acid residues, and the resulting cleaved products were analyzed via high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) (Fig. 2A). HPLC-MS analysis of the cleaved products from PDEs treated FhepTA revealed that the major product had a molecular weight (MW) of 347.6 Da, which was the same as that of adenosine 5′ monophosphate (5′-AMP) (Fig. 2A). Site mutagenesis of the NTase motif in MNT or the residue receiving AMPs in HEPN resulted in proteins being smaller on SDS-PAGE. Accordingly, no AMP molecules were detected after PDEs digestion (Fig. 2A), confirming that the nucleotide modification of FhepTA at the tyrosine residue is mediated by the NTase domain. Compared with that of the FhepTAD41E, D43E mutant without modification, the MW of FhepTA with modification increased by 987.58 Da, matching the MWs of three AMP molecules (Fig. 2B).

Parallel assays were conducted for the HepT/MntA TA pair in the W3-18-1 host, and the results clearly showed that three AMP molecules were attached to HepTW3-18-1 (Fig. 2C and D, Supplementary Table S4). Thus, we concluded that the increase in the MWs of FhepTA and the HepTW3-18-1 toxin both resulted from triple-AMPylation of the HEPN domain mediated by the NTase activity of the MNT domain.

The N-terminal MNT is less stable in the fused form than the standalone antitoxin

In many TA systems, the antitoxin is inherently less stable than its cognate toxin. Given that splitting the FhepTA construct into separate genes affects the ability of the MNT domain to neutralize the HEPN toxin domain, we investigated whether the reduced stability of MNT contributes to its decreased ability to suppress toxins (Fig. 1D). We first employed SDS-PAGE gel using a pET28b-based expression system to assess the protein production of the original form and modified form of FhepTA and HepT/MntAW3-18-1 pair (Fig. 3A). For the native mntA-hepT operon, both the original TA pair and the fused form produced protein bands corresponding to their predicted size. This aligns with functional assays confirming that the antitoxin effectively neutralizes toxin activity in both configurations. In contrast, analysis of the split FhepTAs system revealed a key difference: while the original fused construct (FhepTA) showed a stable, full-length protein band, only the toxin-containing fragment (FhepTAT) was detectable in the FhepTAS form. The absence of the antitoxin fragment (FhepTAA) suggests that its instability in the dissociated state compromises its ability to counteract the toxin’s activity.

We then directly assessed protein stability in their native host via western blotting using N-terminally Flag-tagged MntAW3-18-1 or FhepTA. Cells in mid-logarithmic phase (OD ∼ 0.8) were induced with a low concentration of inducer (0.05 mM IPTG) for 30 min to express the target proteins. After induction, cells were washed, transferred to fresh LB medium, and treated with the translation inhibitor chloramphenicol to monitor protein degradation over time (Fig. 3B). Both the full-length FhepTA and the MntAW3-18-1 antitoxin remained relatively stable, exhibiting less than 10% degradation over 60 min. In contrast, the separately expressed MNT domain (FhepTAA) derived from FhepTA was significantly more labile than the standalone antitoxin, with approximately 50% degradation occurring within 40 min. To identify the host proteases responsible for degrading the unstable MNT domain, we analyzed its stability in MR-1 strains lacking the Lon or ClpXP proteases. FhepTAA was stabilized in the MR-1Δlon strain, indicating that Lon protease mediates its degradation (Fig. 3B).

Next, we focused on the eight residues differing between FhepTAA and MntAW3-18-1 (Fig. 3C). Site-directed mutagenesis revealed that substitutions at only three N-terminal residues (Asp13, Pro19, and Arg23) increased the stability of the MNT domain (Fig. 3D). Specifically, replacing Pro19 in FhepTAA with the corresponding residues in MntA3-18-1 (His19) significantly increased its stability, and the N13R mutant exhibited intermediate stability compared to the other two mutants in vivo (Fig. 3E). As shown earlier, the isolated MNT domain failed to fully neutralize the toxicity of FhepTAT (Fig. 1D), suggesting that antitoxin stability determines its functional efficacy. To test this hypothesis, we replaced the unstable FhepTAA with the stable MntAW3-18-1 in a two-plasmid toxicity assay. Stable MntAW3-18-1 fully neutralized HEPN domain toxicity, whereas unstable FhepTAA did not. Consistent with this, the stabilized mutant P19H exhibited toxicity-neutralizing capacity equivalent to that of MntAW3-18-1, whereas the unstable A23E mutant largely failed to suppress FhepTAT toxicity. As expected, mutant N13R exhibited intermediate toxicity-neutralizing capacity (Fig. 3F). These results demonstrate that fusion protein formation stabilizes the MNT domain, enabling effective control of HEPN RNase activity.

To determine whether N-terminal sequence differences broadly impact MNT stability, we analyzed additional MNT homologs within the Shewanella genus. Sequence alignment revealed extensive variation in the N-terminal region of MNT domains, both in fused FhepTA proteins and in the standalone MntA antitoxins (Fig. 3G). To validate this observation, we selected a fused MNT-HEPN protein from Shewanella sp. MR-7 and assessed the stability of its MNT domain in its native host. Similar to previous results, the isolated MNT domain was rapidly degraded, while its stability markedly improved either when fused to the HEPN domain or when its N-terminal residues were mutated to match those of the stable MntAW3-18-1 antitoxin (Fig. 3H).

The linker is critical for self-oligoAMPylation of FhepTA

NTases are catalytic enzymes that dynamically regulate cellular processes by adding nucleotides to diverse substrates. Their activity depends on precise interactions with NTPs, metal ions, and target molecules. Sequence alignment of the structural homologs revealed that the MNT domain of FhepTA possesses the highly conserved GS-x10-DxD motif and a shorter а4 helix compared with its homologues MntA from S. oneidensis and A. flos-aquae (Fig. 4A and B). Given that the stoichiometric ratio of HepT and MntA in the TA form was either 2:6 or 2:2 in the complex, we first determined the oligomerization of FhepTA via sedimentation velocity experiments. The primary MW of FhepTA in solution was 32.9 kDa, corresponding to the monomer, and a small fraction of dimers was also present (65.4 kDa) (Fig. 4C). In contrast, the primary forms of HepT/MntAW3-18-1 TA are tetramers (60.0 kDa), with a T/A stoichiometric ratio of 2:2 or 1:3 (Fig. 4D).

To determine whether FhepTA undergoes auto-oligoAMPylation in a dimer-dependent manner, we tested whether FhepTAY240F could intermolecularly modify FhepTAD41E, D43E when these two mutated proteins were co-produced by a pETDuet-1 dual expression vector in E. coli. Although FhepTAY240F retained the GS-x10-DxD motif and FhepTAD41E, D43E retained the receptor Y, no detectable modification was found for FhepTAD41E, D43E (Fig. 4E). Equally important, using in vitro AMPylation assays with purified FhepTAD41E, D43E and FhepTAY240F, no modification was found for FhepTAD41E, D43E (Fig. 4F). Thus, both assays suggest that FhepTA likely catalyzes self-oligoAMPylation through an intramolecular mechanism within a monomer.

We employed AlphaFold3 to predict the monomeric structure of FhepTA. The model revealed that a linker (SAQNHFII) connects the catalytic GS-x10-DxD motif in the MNT domain to the recipient Y residue in the HEPN domain (Fig. 4C). A separate prediction of the HepT/MntAW3-18-1 TA complex using a 2:2 stoichiometry positioned the receiving Y240 of HepT W3-18-1 in close proximity to the GS-x10-DxD motif of MntA W3-18-1 (Fig. 4D). Based on these predictions, we reasoned that the linker might serve a critical role in mediating self-oligoAMPylation within the fusion protein by potentially positioning the catalytic site near its substrate. We therefore investigated the impact of the linker region on FhepTA oligoAMPylation through targeted mutagenesis (Fig. 4G). Deletion of the 8-aa linker (M1: deleting AQNHFIIL) completely abolished its oligoAMPylation. Furthermore, altering three key residues within the full linker (SAQNHFIIL) to match the TA form sequence (M3: FAQSLFIIL) also abrogated the modification, confirming the functional importance of this region. Mutating five residues to FGQIHNGIL (M2) to alter side chain polarity did not impair oligoAMPylation (Fig. 4G; Supplementary Fig. S3A). Furthermore, serving as controls, deleting the adjacent α2 helix (56–74 aa) of the linker or mutating Q31 in the GSQx9-DxD motif of the MNT domain also blocked the reaction (Supplementary Fig. S3B, C). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the linker region plays an essential role in mediating the self-oligoAMPylation of FhepTA.

The MNT domain interferes with the functional dimerization of the HEPN domain in FhepTA

For the previously reported HepT/MntA pairs, oligoAMPylation on Tyr near the RNase motif of HepT plays a key role in neutralizing its toxicity [18, 24]. However, the loss of modification did not lead to the activation of the toxin domain in the fused FhepTA mutants constructed above (Fig. 4G; Supplementary Fig. S3D). To further investigate the activation of the HEPN domain in FhepTA, we then employed another two mutants, FhepTAD41E, D43E and FhepTAY240F, to test whether the disruption of oligoAMPylation would reactivate the toxicity exerted by the HEPN domain. Unexpectedly, the growth curve showed no obvious toxicity when the modifications were absent, suggesting that there are alternative ways to silence HEPN toxicity (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S4A). For HepT toxins or Csm6 proteins in the CRISPR-Cas system, dimerization of the HEPN domain is a prerequisite for its RNase activity [39]. Considering that full-length FhepTA exists mainly as a monomer, we then analyzed the oligomeric states of FhepTAD41E, D43E and FhepTAY240F. Importantly, both mutants were mostly present as monomers (Fig. 5B), and we speculate that the MNT domain interferes with the dimerization of HEPN and blocks its toxicity. Thus, we constructed a series of N-terminal FhepTA truncations to explore when the toxicity of HEPN can be released (Fig. 5C). Based on growth assessments, deleting the α1, β1, β2 region, including the NTase motif GS-x10-DxD (the N-terminal 1–53 aa), was still non-toxic, indicating that the retaining region (α2, β3, α3, β4, α4) of MNT can also play a role in blocking the toxicity of the HEPN domain. Further removing 30 aa from the N-terminus (1–82 aa) had a very limited toxicity, whereas truncations of 113 aa resulted in a pronounced toxicity (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S4B). The slight toxicity of FhepTA83-276 may result from degradation of the remaining MNT domain due to structure instability (Supplementary Fig. S4C).

The analysis of the oligomer states revealed that the primary MW of FhepTAT in solution was 27 kDa, matching the size of a dimer (Fig. 5D). In contrast, the primary MW of FhepTA83-276 was 27.8 kDa, which corresponds to the monomer (Fig. 5D). In addition, a cya-based bacterial two-hybrid (BACTH) assay confirmed that the HEPN domain (140–276 aa) forms dimers in vivo, whereas its dimerization is disrupted by fusion to the MNT fragment as short as 50 aa (83–276 aa) (Fig. 5C and E). These results are consistent with the toxicity test results, demonstrating that MNT interferes with dimerization of the HEPN domain, thereby affecting its toxicity.

Based on these findings, we reasoned that HEPN activation in vivo likely requires degradation of the N-terminal MNT domain. To investigate this, proteins were expressed in E. coli using pET28b-based plasmids and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. The purified HEPN domain FhepTAT alone was largely intact (Fig. 5F, lane 3), whereas the full-length protein (lane 2) and various truncation mutants (lanes 4–7) showed signs of degradation over time at 4°C (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Subsequent mass spectrometry analysis revealed that degradation of FhepTA is initiated primarily at the N-terminus and proceeds through gradual hydrolysis (MS1 in Fig. 5F; Supplementary Fig. S4D). To assess in vivo degradation in the native host, we employed a plasmid-encoded FhepTA with an N-terminal Flag tag and a C-terminal His tag under the control of its native promoter and performed western blot analysis on cells harvested at exponential, stationary, and overnight growth phases. The results demonstrated that FhepTA was hydrolyzed from the N-terminus and has the possibility to release the HEPN domain in the cellular (Fig. 5G). Since FhepTA or its N-terminal truncation mutants do not exhibit cytotoxicity under normal conditions, we speculate that the C-terminal products may have been oligoAMPylated prior to degradation of the N-terminal MNT domain.

Disulfide bond formation between cysteine residues inhibits the HEPN RNase activity of FhepTA

To determine the nucleic acid cleavage activity, we conducted an in vitro RNA cleavage assay with purified FhepTAT, FhepTA and HepTW3-18-1 proteins using a short ssRNA (49 nt) probe and a long MS2 phage ssRNA (3 569 nt) as substrates. For the short ssRNA probe, HepTW3-18-1 showed efficient cleavage activity, while FhepTA showed none, consistent with the in vivo toxicity results in E. coli (Fig. 6A; Fig. 1CD). However, a discrepancy in FhepTAT activity was observed between the in vitro and in vivo assays. When FhepTAT was purified with or without the reducing agent DTT in E. coli, we observed that the activity of the HEPN domain increased in the presence of 1 mM DTT, suggesting that a certain reducing environment is required for its activity. Moreover, FhepTAT and HepTW3-18-1 both cleaved the long MS2 phage RNA substrate more effectively than the 49 nt RNA probe. This activity, which suggests the availability of more cleavage sites, further confirms their mRNA cleavage function (Fig. 6A, bottom panel; Supplementary Fig. S5A).

To investigate the differences in the activity of FhepTAT in oxidative and reducing environments, we compared the protein states of FhepTAT, HepTW3-18-1 and FhepTA under conditions with and without a reducing agent by SDS-PAGE and AUC. In a reducing environment (1 mM DTT), AUC revealed that both HepTW3-18-1 and FhepTAT existed primarily as dimers in solution (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Fig. S5B). Under the denaturing conditions of SDS-PAGE, which disrupts non-covalent interactions, the monomeric form became predominant for all proteins (Fig. 6B and C). In contrast, in an oxidative state (without DTT), a significant fraction of FhepTA and FhepTAT formed multimeric species, whereas almost no polymerization was observed for HepTW3-18-1. This difference was further confirmed by cross-linking assays (Supplementary Fig. S5C). The addition of 1 mM DTT completely eliminated this polymerization. Interestingly, SDS-PAGE analysis indicated that oxidized monomeric forms of both FhepTA and FhepTAT persisted in 1 mM DTT but were fully reduced by the stronger agent TCEP, suggesting the presence of relatively stable intramolecular disulfide bonds (Fig. 6B). In contrast, no such oxidized monomer was detected for HepTW3-18-1, and its overall level of polymerization was significantly lower than that of FhepTAT (Fig. 6B).

Structural modeling with AlphaFold3 placed all four cysteines in monomeric FhepTA and dimeric FhepTAT on alpha-helices, with substantial distances between them (Supplementary Fig. S6A). This suggested that the observed intramolecular disulfide bonds in FhepTA might form artificially after denaturation. To test this, we treated purified FhepTA and FhepTAT with a molar excess of N-ethylmaleimide (NEM), which alkylates free thiols and blocks disulfide bond formation. Subsequent SDS-PAGE showed that protein oligomers persisted even when intramolecular disulfides were prevented. This confirms that the oligomers are pre-formed in solution, while the intramolecular disulfide bonds are likely artifacts generated during the denaturation process for electrophoresis (Fig. 6C).

To elucidate the differences between FhepTAT and HepTW3-18-1 in terms of activity and protein oligomeric state, we analyzed the cysteine residues within them and other homologous HEPN domain proteins in Shewanella species (Fig. 6D). Analysis revealed that two of these (C186 and C213) residues are highly conserved in all homologs, whereas FhepTA uniquely possesses two additional residues (C163 and C270). To assess the role of each cysteine, we performed growth assays with S. oneidensis expressing FhepTAT or its cysteine-to-serine mutants. Mutation of these residues did not significantly impair toxicity (Supplementary Fig. S6B), consistent with structural predictions placing them distal to the active site and substrate interface (Supplementary Fig. S6A). However, biochemical analysis by SDS-PAGE revealed that the unique cysteines C163 and C270 mediate disulfide bond formation. Under oxidizing conditions, FhepTAT forms intermolecular disulfide bonds, a property dependent on these two residues (Fig. 6E). This finding correlates with the observed reduction of toxicity in wild-type FhepTAT in vivo under oxidative stress (H₂O₂ treatment). In contrast, a C163T/C270W double mutant retained significant toxicity under the same conditions, confirming that the toxicity decrease in wild-type FhepTAT results from an oxidative stress-induced change in the oligomeric state (Fig. 6F). The strategic placement of these cysteines may enable a functional switch between intra- and intermolecular disulfide bond formation (Supplementary Fig. S6C and D). Overall, the high prevalence of cysteine residues in this HEPN branch appears crucial for regulating RNase activity and structural conformation, both of which are essential for precise activity control.

Discussion

Canonical TA systems consist of two adjacent genes organized within an operon. Transcriptional, translational, or post-translational regulation of the TA operon tightly represses toxin activity under normal growth conditions while enabling toxin activation when required under specific conditions [40]. Through comparative studies of a fused protein (FhepTA) containing both HEPN RNase and MNT domains with its highly homologous TA form in Shewanella, we reveal that the HEPN RNase activity in fused FhepTA is dually regulated by oligoAMPylation and the oligomeric state (Fig. 7). They represent a new family of NTases that catalyze auto-oligoAMPylation. Specifically, the MNT domain within FhepTA mediates self-oligoAMPylation on a conserved tyrosine residue near the RNase active motif, a mechanism analogous to how the standalone MntA antitoxin inhibits its cognate HepT toxin in type VII TA systems [19]. Beyond oligoAMPylation, FhepTA activity is also controlled by its oligomeric state: the N-terminal MNT domain prevents dimerization of the C-terminal HEPN domain, and RNase activity is further modulated by shifts between active dimers and inactive oligomers. Importantly, the HEPN RNase domain is also regulated by cysteine-mediated disulfide bonding, which influences oligomer configuration. Thus, FhepTA employs a multilayered regulatory cascade that integrates diverse signals, including ATP levels, redox state, proteolytic degradation (e.g. by Lon protease), and the presence of AMP-removing enzymes (e.g. phosphodiesterases), to control RNase activation under varying conditions (Fig. 7). Given that dimer formation is required for the catalytic activity of HEPN-containing RNases such as CRISPR-associated nucleases and eukaryotic HEPN RNase-containing proteins [6], this study provides a valuable insight for the control of RNA processing activity.

Proposed model for HEPN RNase regulation in fused FhepTA and canonical HepT/MntA TA systems. In fused FhepTA, HEPN domain activity is controlled by oligoAMPylation and the redox state of cysteine residues. The HEPN domain dimer represents the functional unit required for RNase activity, while the formation of higher-order HEPN polymers leads to a reduction or loss of RNase activity.

The oligomeric shift between a two-gene TA module and a single-gene fused system observed in this study is also reported in the toxSAS family type II TA pair FaRel2/ATfaRel2 (2:2) and its fused form CapRelSJ46 (monomer) [41]. In canonical two-gene TA modules, the freely expressed antitoxin often adopts an optimal conformation for efficient toxin neutralization by the formation of a stable complex (e.g. 2:2 or 2:4) that locks the toxin in an inactive state [3]. In contrast, the covalent linkage within fused systems constrains the conformational change of the antitoxin domain, a process that may enable a faster and more targeted response to specific cellular cues. The mechanistic elegance of this streamlined regulation is exemplified by CapRelSJ46 [29]. This monomeric fusion protein CapRelSJ46, exists in two conformational states: a “closed” conformation where intramolecular interactions repress the toxin’s translation-inhibition activity and an “open” active conformation that is directly triggered by the binding of the major capsid protein of an invading lytic phage. This ligand-induced conformational switch unleashes the toxin domain, executing an altruistic cell death program that halts viral propagation [29, 30]. Thus, the transition from a two-gene TA module to a single-gene fused system may represent a shift of the antitoxin’s role, from a stoichiometric inhibitor in a complex to an integral regulatory domain.

AMPylation has been documented as a widespread post-translational regulatory mechanism, especially for host–pathogen interaction [42], typically involving the transfer of a single AMP molecule [43]. The MntA antitoxin family represent a unique exception that is capable of catalyzing the transfer of two or more AMP molecules to their cognate toxin targets [18, 24]. Despite sharing low sequence similarity with the previously characterized HepT/MntA pair in the surface-water isolate S. oneidensis [18], the fused FhepTA from S. oneidensis and the HepT/MntAW3-18-1 TA from the deep-sea strain S. putrefaciens W3-18-1 also precisely mediate the transfer of three AMP molecules to their cognate HEPN proteins. This conservation of tri-AMPylation activity across highly divergent sequences and ecological niches (surface water vs. deep-sea) in Shewanella underscores the functional importance of this multi-AMP transfer mechanism within the MntA family. Therefore, deciphering the structural and catalytic determinants that precisely control the number of AMP units added will be essential to understand how oligoAMPylation is tuned to achieve specific biological outcomes.
