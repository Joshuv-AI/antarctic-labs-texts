The effect of the patient rotation and movement system (PRMS) on breastfeeding success, pain, and comfort in puerperants who gave birth by caesarean delivery
Authors: Merve Lazoğlu, Serap Ejder Apay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study was conducted to determine the effect of the patient rotation and movement system (PRMS) on breastfeeding success, pain, and comfort in puerperants who gave birth by caesarean delivery.MethodsThis is a randomised controlled trial. Data were collected in the caesarean unit of Kars Harakani State Hospital between 01.12.2020 and 30.06.2021. The study was completed with 146 puerperants (experimental group n = 74; control group n = 72). Data were collected using the LATCH Breastfeeding Diagnosis and Evaluation Scale, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Postpartum Comfort Scale (PCS), and Lactogenesis Symptoms Follow-up Form. The trial was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov.ResultsLATCH scores were significantly higher in the experimental group at all breastfeeding assessments (p < 0.001). VAS pain scores were significantly lower in the experimental group during the first three breastfeeding sessions and the first breastfeeding before discharge (p < 0.05). Physical comfort and total PCS scores were significantly higher in the experimental group (p < 0.05). Most lactogenesis breast symptoms were more prevalent in the experimental group from postpartum day 2 onward. No adverse events related to PRMS use were observed during the study period.ConclusionsThe PRMS improved breastfeeding success, reduced early postpartum pain, and enhanced physical comfort after caesarean delivery. No significant effect was observed on psychospiritual or sociocultural comfort. These findings suggest the PRMS may be a promising non-pharmacological supportive strategy in post-caesarean care. Future studies with formal safety endpoints and standardised protocols are warranted.

Introduction

Caesarean section, one of the most common obstetric surgeries, is a delivery method with an increasing incidence worldwide and in Turkey [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that the rate of caesarean section must be between 10 and 15% worldwide and there is no scientific evidence that higher rates of caesarean delivery prevent mortality and morbidity-related birth complications [3]. The rate of caesarean section is 32% in the United States of America, while it is 29.6% in Germany, 29.5% in the UK, and 55% in Mexico [1]. The Ministry of Health of Turkey reported that the rate of caesarean section was 54.4% in 2019 and would continue to increase unless awareness was raised [4].

Women usually experience physical and psychological exhaustion in the postpartum period, which doubles or even triples in caesarean section cases [5, 6]. Women have to cope with many physical and psychological problems including pain, constipation, perineal pain, urinary retention, fatigue, weakness, pain originating from the incision site, sleep disturbances, activity limitation, gastrointestinal disorders, and anaesthesia complications in the post-caesarean period [7]. The delayed start of breastfeeding due to the late secretion of the oxytocin hormone or the insufficient milk supply is another problem that the mother has to cope with after caesarean Sect [8]. All these negative conditions (pain originating from the incision site, fatigue, and the difficulty of holding the infant on the lap) make it difficult for the mother to display correct breastfeeding behaviour [9].

The pain that occurs after caesarean section makes recovery difficult and delays the mother’s contact with her newborn [10]. Pereira, De Souza, and Beleza [11] revealed that puerperants who gave birth by caesarean delivery experienced more severe pain during a position change. Numerous non-pharmacological methods have been employed to relieve post-caesarean pain [12, 13]. Optimal pain management increases breastfeeding success and facilitates daily activities such as neonatal care, personal care and hygiene [6], preparing an environment for early attachment with the infant [14].

Postpartum comfort is another requirement that should be addressed in physical, social, psychological, and environmental integrity in the post-caesarean period. Conditions such as post-caesarean incision pain, bleeding, dizziness, breastfeeding problems, and abdominal tension decrease the comfort of women [15]. Providing comfort during this period is extremely important for the mother’s physical and psychological health and the infant [16, 17].

The patient rotation and movement system (PRMS) enables midwives and other healthcare personnel to reposition the patient and determine the lying position sensitively and accurately [16]. This system also reduces the physical load on midwives and family members who assist patients in positioning [16]. While a study was conducted on long-term bedridden patients with regard to the PRMS [16], no scientific study examining its effects in the obstetric field had been identified prior to this research. The aim of this study was therefore to investigate the effects of the PRMS on breastfeeding success, postpartum pain, and comfort of puerperants who gave birth by caesarean delivery.

Methods

Study design and participants

The current research is an experimental, randomised, and controlled trial. The study was conducted in the caesarean unit of Kars Harakani State Hospital, affiliated with the Ministry of Health and located in Kars city centre, between 01.12.2020 and 30.06.2021. The sample was calculated as a minimum of 120 (60 per group) by power analysis; this was augmented by 25% to account for potential data loss, yielding 150 participants at enrolment. The study was completed with 146 puerperants (experimental group n = 74; control group n = 72).

The trial was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT05499208; registration date: 28 September 2022). Data collection occurred between December 2020 and June 2021, prior to formal trial registration. This retrospective registration occurred because the PRMS had not previously been evaluated in an obstetric setting, and the study was initially conceived as a service evaluation. The primary outcomes (breastfeeding success, pain, postpartum comfort, and lactogenesis symptoms) and the study design were pre-specified before participant enrolment commenced and are documented in the ethics committee application (approval number 2020/27). No modifications to outcomes or primary analyses were made after data collection began.

Inclusion criteria: being aged 18–35 years; primiparity; transverse caesarean incision; at least primary school education; no high-risk pregnancy; no complications in the woman or newborn during childbirth or the postpartum period.

Randomisation

Using the Random Integer Generator at Random.org, single-group columns between 1 and 150 were generated. By drawing lots at the beginning of the study, it was determined which number corresponded to the experimental or control group. The pregnant woman corresponding to number 1 was assigned to the experimental group and number 2 to the control group.

Intervention

The PRMS is a system designed for individuals weighing 40–200 kg that can be integrated into the patient bed, enabling bed rotation and repositioning via a remote control system to assist with hygiene, dressing, and pressure injury prevention [18].

The PRMS was installed in a dedicated room in the caesarean unit prior to commencement of data collection. After installation, necessary checks were made and the system was tested by the researcher. Puerperants in the experimental group were transferred to the bed equipped with the PRMS on arrival from the operating room. The device was used to place the mother in a lateral decubitus (side-lying) position, achieving approximately 30–45 degrees of lateral tilt. The newborn was positioned in a side-lying or ‘laid-back’ orientation at the level of the maternal breast, with the infant’s body aligned with the mother’s. No additional pillows or positioning aids were routinely used beyond what the mother requested for comfort. Each positioning episode lasted for the duration of the breastfeeding session (approximately 15–20 min). The mother was repositioned using the PRMS at the start of each of the six assessed breastfeeding sessions. The primary researcher was present throughout each breastfeeding session in the experimental group to operate the device and, if requested, to provide verbal guidance; however, no hands-on breastfeeding assistance was provided by the researcher beyond PRMS operation. Mothers in the experimental group could request repositioning at any time using the remote control, which was explained during device introduction. Whether mothers could independently reposition between assessed sessions was not specifically recorded and represents a limitation acknowledged below.

Puerperants in the control group were transferred to a standard hospital bed on arrival from the operating room and received the hospital’s routine postpartum care.

Analgesic administration was managed according to the hospital’s standard post-caesarean protocol for both groups. The analgesic regimen was not standardised as part of the study intervention and was not recorded as a study variable. This represents a potential confounding variable that is acknowledged in the limitations section. Similarly, the timing of first breastfeeding, skin-to-skin contact, and rooming-in practices were subject to the hospital’s routine protocols, which were applied uniformly to all eligible patients regardless of group allocation. No supplementary formula feeding or nursing/family breastfeeding assistance beyond routine care was provided to either group outside the study protocol.

Outcome assessment

The researcher and a second, independently trained observer simultaneously and independently completed the LATCH Breastfeeding Diagnosis and Evaluation Scale during the first three breastfeeding sessions and the last three breastfeeding sessions before discharge. The second observer was trained by the primary researcher prior to data collection. Neither the primary researcher nor the second observer was blinded to group assignment, as the PRMS was visible in the experimental group room. This limitation is discussed further below.

The VAS was completed by participants during the same six assessment time points. The PCS was completed 1–2 h before discharge. The Lactogenesis Symptoms Follow-up Form was completed face-to-face on postpartum days 1 and 2 and by telephone on days 3 and 4 after discharge.

Data collection tools

LATCH breastfeeding diagnosis and evaluation scale

Developed by Jensen and Wallace (1993); Turkish validity study by Yenal and Okumuş [19] (Cronbach’s α = 0.95). In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.75 (first observer) and 0.78 (second observer). Scores range from 0 to 10; higher scores indicate greater breastfeeding success [19, 20].

Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)

A 10 cm unidimensional pain scale Bond & Pilowsky [21]. The patient marks their pain intensity between 0 (no pain) and 10 (worst imaginable pain). First used to evaluate post-surgical pain in Turkey by Eti Aslan [22].

Postpartum Comfort Scale (PCS)

Developed by Karakaplan and Yıldız [23] from Kolcaba’s General Comfort Scale. Comprises 34 items across three subscales (physical, psychospiritual, and sociocultural comfort). Scores range from 34 to 170; higher scores indicate greater comfort. Cronbach’s α: physical subscale = 0.63; psychospiritual = 0.78; sociocultural = 0.61 in the current study.

Lactogenesis symptoms follow-up form

A researcher-developed form documenting breast changes indicative of lactogenesis onset, including breast fullness, tingling, leakage, heaviness, temperature increase, milk visible in infant’s mouth, and breast sensitivity.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM). Normal distribution was confirmed. Descriptive statistics, chi-square test, independent samples t-test, and Fisher’s exact test were applied. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. Kappa values are interpreted according to the Landis & Koch criteria: 0.41–0.60 = moderate; 0.61–0.80 = substantial; 0.81–1.00 = almost perfect (Table 1).

Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. In the experimental group, kappa values were 0.58, 0.73, and 0.74 for the first three breastfeeding sessions (moderate to substantial agreement) and 0.83, 0.73, and 0.78 for the last three sessions before discharge (substantial to almost perfect). In the control group, kappa values were 0.57, 0.55, and 0.50 for the first three sessions (moderate agreement by the Landis & Koch criteria) and 0.55, 0.58, and 0.51 for the last three sessions (moderate agreement). The lower kappa values in the control group during early assessments reflect greater variability in breastfeeding behaviours in this group and represent a limitation of the LATCH measurement in that context.

Pain (Table 4)

Pain levels (VAS) were significantly lower in the experimental group during the first three breastfeeding sessions and the first breastfeeding before discharge (p < 0.05). No significant difference was observed for the second and third breastfeeding sessions before discharge (p > 0.05), suggesting that the pain-reducing effect of the PRMS diminishes as maternal mobility increases with time from surgery.

Discussion

Preferring caesarean delivery at a high rate brings about numerous problems in terms of maternal and infant health in the postpartum period. Caesarean delivery causes negative effects on postpartum breastfeeding success, pain, and comfort levels in puerperants [11, 24–26].

Upon comparing women in terms of obstetric and descriptive characteristics in this study, no statistically significant difference was detected between the groups (Table 2), confirming homogeneity and the comparability of the groups.

Breastfeeding success

This study found that the mean LATCH scores of the experimental group were higher for both the first three breastfeeding sessions after caesarean section and the last three sessions before discharge compared to the control group. Several physiological and behavioural mechanisms may underlie this finding. First, the PRMS enables attainment of the lateral decubitus breastfeeding position without maternal effort or external physical support, thereby reducing abdominal wall tension at the caesarean incision site. Reduced incisional tension lessens pain during breastfeeding, which in turn is likely to reduce the sympathetic stress response and support oxytocin release — both of which are prerequisites for effective milk ejection [27–29]. Second, early and unimpeded maternal–infant positioning contact facilitated by the PRMS is likely to promote earlier and more effective latch, consistent with WHO recommendations for early initiation of breastfeeding [3]. Third, maternal confidence in handling the newborn is likely to be enhanced when the PRMS removes the need for external repositioning assistance, an effect that may amplify over successive breastfeeding sessions as the mother becomes familiar with device operation. These evaluations suggest that the PRMS, which enables independent positioning without requiring external support, facilitates early breastfeeding initiation and, accordingly, breastfeeding success.

The findings of this study should also be considered in the context of the Enhanced Recovery After Caesarean Surgery (ERACS) framework, which advocates for multimodal analgesia, early mobilisation, skin-to-skin contact, and early breastfeeding support as components of optimised post-caesarean recovery [30]. The PRMS directly supports the early mobilisation and early breastfeeding components of this framework, suggesting that its incorporation into an ERACS-aligned care pathway may amplify its individual benefits. Future studies should evaluate the PRMS as a component of a structured ERACS protocol rather than as an isolated intervention.

Lactogenesis symptoms

Upon comparing lactogenesis breast symptoms between the groups, the symptoms of breast fullness, heaviness, tingling, milk leakage, and breast milk visible in the infant’s mouth were found to be higher in the experimental group from postpartum day 2 onward. These symptoms are subjective indicators associated with maternal perceptions of lactogenesis and do not constitute objective confirmation of lactogenesis II or of increased milk volume. No quantitative breastfeeding outcomes - such as milk volume, infant weight gain, exclusive breastfeeding rates, or breastfeeding duration - were measured in this study. The discussion and conclusions should therefore be understood as reflecting that the PRMS may support maternal perceptions associated with lactogenesis, rather than directly improving lactogenesis itself. Future studies should incorporate objective measures of milk production to confirm this association. The finding that breast symptoms were more prevalent and appeared earlier in the experimental group is consistent with the hypothesis that effective early breastfeeding supported by the PRMS promotes the subjective experience of milk let-down.

Pain

This study found that the pain level of the experimental group was lower during the first three breastfeeding sessions and the first breastfeeding before discharge. The mean VAS pain reduction in the experimental group at the first breastfeeding was approximately 0.6 points (7.13 vs. 6.51). While statistically significant (p = 0.018), the clinical significance of a reduction of less than 1 VAS point is modest. A reduction of 2 or more VAS points is generally considered the minimum clinically important difference for post-operative pain [31]. The more clinically meaningful reductions were observed at the second and third breastfeeding sessions (differences of 1.29 and 1.36 points, respectively; both p < 0.001), where the group difference exceeds the threshold for clinical significance. These findings suggest that the analgesic effect of the PRMS on breastfeeding-related pain is most pronounced in the early postoperative period when incisional pain is greatest. It is thought that a decrease in pain may have originated from the fact that the pain caused by tension of muscle fibres at the operation site due to position change is minimised by the PRMS.

Postpartum comfort

This study revealed that physical comfort and total comfort levels were higher in the experimental group. The difference in total PCS score between groups was 3.44 points (128.62 vs. 125.18, p = 0.028). The clinical significance of this difference should be interpreted with caution, as the minimum clinically important difference for the PCS has not been formally established. The significant effect was limited to the physical comfort subscale; psychospiritual and sociocultural comfort subscales showed no significant group difference. This is consistent with the nature of the PRMS intervention, which directly addresses physical positioning and mobility but does not target the psychological or social dimensions of comfort. The hypothesis regarding postpartum comfort was therefore only partially confirmed. It is suggested that future interventions combining the PRMS with psychosocial support strategies may produce broader comfort benefits.

When integrated into the ERACS framework, the physical comfort gains observed with PRMS use may be synergistically enhanced by multimodal analgesia and early ambulation, which together reduce the need for mechanical repositioning over time — consistent with the observation that group differences in VAS scores were no longer significant by the second and third breastfeeding sessions before discharge.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that should be considered in interpreting the findings. First and most importantly, the study was not blinded at any level. The primary researcher (M.L.) performed the PRMS intervention, participated as one of the two LATCH observers, and coordinated data collection for all outcomes. The second observer was trained by the primary researcher and was aware of group allocations throughout all assessment points. Participants could not be blinded to group assignment given the visible presence of the PRMS. Absence of blinding introduces the risk of performance bias (differential behaviour by the researcher toward participants in each group) and detection bias (differential assessment of LATCH scores and subjective outcomes). Because the primary researcher has a professional interest in a positive result, even well-intentioned differences in attentiveness, encouragement, or time spent with participants in each group may have differentially influenced subjective outcomes such as VAS pain scores and PCS comfort ratings. The inclusion of a second observer partially mitigates detection bias for LATCH scoring, but not for VAS or PCS outcomes, neither of which involved independent dual assessment. Future studies should include blinded outcome assessors for subjective measures and, where possible, an independent researcher to deliver the intervention. Second, the intervention description as published is insufficient for fully independent replication. Although we have expanded the description of the PRMS protocol above, several parameters — including the exact degree of lateral tilt achieved in individual patients, the precise duration of each positioning episode, and the extent to which the researcher’s presence during breastfeeding sessions constituted a form of support independent of the device — could not be fully standardised or reconstructed from study records. Future studies using the PRMS or similar devices should include a standardised step-by-step positioning protocol, preferably with an accompanying figure or video supplement, and should clearly delineate device-related effects from associated human support effects. Third, the trial was retrospectively registered (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05499208; registration date 28 September 2022), as data collection was completed between December 2020 and June 2021. Although the primary outcomes and study design were pre-specified before participant enrolment and no outcome modifications were made, retrospective registration reduces the protection against selective outcome reporting and limits methodological transparency. This represents a limitation that cannot be corrected post hoc. Fourth, several potential confounding variables were not recorded or controlled, including analgesic type and dosage, timing of first breastfeeding, skin-to-skin contact, rooming-in practices, maternal fatigue, neonatal supplementation, and the level of nursing and family breastfeeding support received outside the study protocol. Although the hospital’s standard postoperative protocols were applied uniformly to all eligible patients, undocumented variation in these factors between groups cannot be excluded and may have influenced outcomes. Fifth, no objective measures of lactogenesis or milk production (milk volume, infant weight gain at discharge, exclusive breastfeeding rates) were included. The lactogenesis outcomes reported in this study reflect maternal self-report of subjective breast symptoms and should not be interpreted as confirmation of improved milk production or lactogenesis II onset. Sixth, the study was not designed or powered to detect rare adverse events. While no adverse events related to PRMS use were observed, the safety profile of the PRMS in the immediate postoperative context — including risks of mechanical repositioning pain, incision tension, haemodynamic changes, and neonatal safety during side-lying facilitated by a motorised device — warrants formal evaluation in future studies with pre-specified safety endpoints. Seventh, the study was conducted at a single centre in northeastern Turkey (Kars Harakani State Hospital) and involved an exclusively primiparous population. The results cannot be assumed to generalise to multiparous women, to other healthcare settings, or to populations with different sociodemographic, cultural, or healthcare infrastructure characteristics. The PRMS required installation in a dedicated room, which represents an infrastructural requirement that may not be replicable in lower-resource or smaller clinical settings. Finally, the PRMS requires device acquisition, dedicated space, staff training, and compatibility with post-operative monitoring equipment (intravenous lines, urinary catheters, pulse oximetry). These practical and financial barriers to implementation are not discussed in the practice implications section but must be acknowledged in any realistic assessment of widespread clinical adoption.

Conclusions

In postpartum women who underwent caesarean section, the PRMS resulted in significantly higher breastfeeding success scores across all assessment time points, reduced pain levels during early breastfeeding sessions, and improved physical comfort. No significant effect was observed on psychospiritual or sociocultural comfort. The intervention may support maternal perceptions associated with lactogenesis rather than directly improving lactogenesis itself, and future studies incorporating objective milk production measures are needed to confirm this association. It is recommended that the research be repeated in different sample groups and that implementation feasibility, including device cost, staff training, and infrastructure requirements, be prospectively evaluated.

Practice implications

As a result of this study, the PRMS demonstrates potential as a safe and effective non-pharmacological supportive intervention for puerperants following caesarean delivery. Integration of the PRMS into ERACS protocols, in combination with multimodal analgesia, early mobilisation, and structured breastfeeding support, may optimise post-caesarean recovery outcomes and warrants evaluation in future multicentre trials. Before recommending widespread hospital adoption, the acquisition cost, room requirements, staff training needs, and compatibility with post-operative monitoring equipment must be assessed. Midwives and institutions considering PRMS adoption should ensure that implementation feasibility, including resource requirements and infrastructure, is evaluated prospectively in their specific clinical context.
