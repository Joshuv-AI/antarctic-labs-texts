Comparative biology and morphometrics of pink bollworm, Pectinophora gossypiella (Saunders) on Bt cotton and alternate malvaceous hosts
Authors: S. Rakhesh, S. G. Hanchinal, M. Bheemanna, A. C. Hosamani, J. M. Nidagundi, S. Deshmukh Sharanabasappa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The comparative biology of pink bollworm (PBW) on Bt cotton bolls and fruit pods of malvaceous plants viz., Abutilon indicum, Abutilon hirtum and Abelmoschous ficulneus was investigated under controlled conditions. The incubation time of PBW eggs from different hosts ranged from 2.0 to 4.5 days, which was not differ significantly among treatments. The mean larval duration of all four instars as well as average larval duration of pink bollworm reared on Bt cotton was more as compared to the larvae reared on A. indicum, A. hirtum and A. ficulneus. The average male and female pupal period were longer on Bt cotton. Both adult male and female from Bt cotton lived longer as compared to adults from A. indicum, A. hirtum and A. ficulneus. The weight of the different life stages of the pink bollworm was also more significant when reared on Bt cotton. Similarly, morphometric parameters, including larval body length and width, as well as pupal size and adult body dimensions (including wing expanse), were significantly greater in PBW reared on Bt cotton than on other hosts. Larvae feeding on Bt cotton undergo physiological stress that delays development but allows extended feeding and compensatory growth, resulting in higher weight and longevity. Thus, Bt cotton induces delayed development in Bt tolerant populations, while non-cotton malvaceous hosts support faster but nutritionally inferior development. Thus, the results clearly indicate that the pink bollworm completes its life cycle on malvaceous hosts and this also might be a reason for the off seasonal survival of pink bollworm in cotton growing regions. This study could have implications for crop management strategies for controlling the pink bollworm, that can cause significant damage to cotton crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40601-8.

Introduction

Global cotton area and production is 32.6 million ha and 116.10 million bales1. India is an important cotton grower among all the cotton producing countries in the world and ranks first in area (12.96 million ha) with production of 37.1 million bales2 of 170 kg each with productivity of 487 kg lint ha− 1. The insect pest spectrum of cotton is quite complex, more than 1326 species of insect pests have been reported on this crop across the world3. However, 130 different species of insects and mites are reported to cause damage to cotton crop in India. Among these, the bollworms viz., American bollworm, Helicoverpa armigera (Hubner); spiny bollworm, Earias insulana (Boisdual); spotted bollworm, E. vitella (Fabricius) and pink bollworm, Pectinophora gossypiella (Saunders) pose greater threat to cotton production4. Among the bollworm complex, the pink bollworm (Pectinophora gossypiella) stands out as one of the most damaging insect pests of Bt cotton, exhibiting an extensive range across India and causing severe losses to cotton production5,6. Beyond cotton, it infests plants from seven families, 24 genera, and 70 species including okra, hibiscus, deccan hemp, roselle, Indian mallow, country mallow, hollyhocks, rose mallows, kenaf, Jamaica sorrel, milo, lucerne, and other malvaceous species worldwide7,8. The persistent status of pink bollworm as a major pest of Bt cotton in India signals the development of resistance to key Cry toxins, particularly Cry1Ac and Cry2Ab. In this context, alternate malvaceous hosts may serve as critical natural reservoirs or bridge hosts, sustaining Bt resistant populations during the cotton-free period. Such survival and development on these hosts could enable population carryover, thereby facilitating the persistence and spread of resistant individuals into subsequent cotton seasons. We conducted comparative biology of pink bollworm by measuring key life-history parameters including incubation period, larval and pupal duration, survival rate, adult longevity, fecundity, body weight, and morphometric traits across different malvaceous hosts. That in turn gives an insight to off seasonal survival, propagation and spread of the pink bollworm in major cotton growing areas. Here, comparative biology studies can help how pink bollworm adapt to different host plants and provide a better understanding of their life history and behaviour. This knowledge can help farmers and agricultural researchers make informed decisions about crop management practices and improve the sustainability of crop production.

Materials and methods

Comparative biology and morphometry of pink bollworm was studied at Department of Agricultural Entomology, University of Agricultural Sciences, Raichur under controlled condition of 27 ± 2 °C temperature, 65 ± 5 per cent RH and photoperiod of 14: 10 h (light: dark) during 2020-21. Pink bollworm larvae were collected from the Bt cotton field (resistant pink bollworm strains) and reared in laboratory on artificial diet9 and then sexed based on pupal characters. The distance between genital and anal pore helps in separating the sexes wherein distance is more than double in female as compared to male10. Sexed pupae kept in emergence chamber (40 × 40 × 60 cm) for adult eclosion. Ten pair of emerged adults were released to ovipositional chamber (18” diameter x 20” height) for mating and egg laying. Small cotton twigs bearing squares, tender leaves and bolls was placed in a small conical flask/plastic vial filled with water and this flask was kept in the ovipositional chamber. The cotton swab dipped in 10 per cent honey solution and was hung by using thread in ovipositional chamber and fresh food was given every day by changing honey solution dipped cotton swab. Fresh twig was given every day and old twig on which egg laid by gravid PBW female was removed and kept for incubation. After egg hatching, ten neonates were transferred using moist camel hair brush to individual plastic vials containing individual host food materials viz., cotton bolls and fruit pods of Abutilon indicum Link, Abutilon hirtum (Lam.) and Abelmoschous ficulneus (L.) Wight & Arnott ex Wight and were kept in separate tray, then placed in the growth chamber. Observations on each of the biological stages and morphometric measurements are detailed here under.

The adults obtained by rearing on Bt cotton, A. indicum, A. hirtum and A. ficulneus were released at a ratio of 1: 1 (male: female) into individual mating chambers containing individual host plants twigs as a source for oviposition. The old twigs of individual host containing eggs of pink bollworm were removed and replaced with new twig daily. The fresh Bt cotton, A. indicum, A. hirtum and A. ficulneus twig bearing fruit pod, flowers and leaves placed in vial containing sucrose solution, which was kept inside plastic jars (18” diameter x 20” height). The total eggs laid by individual gravid female on old twigs were counted and for each host ten replications were maintained.

The incubation period of PBW eggs laid on Bt cotton, A. indicum, A. hirtum and A. ficulneus twig were studied by keeping the egg laid twigs in plastic jars under room temperature (27 ± 2 °C). The duration between eggs laid and emergence of neonate was documented as the incubation period.

The PBW neonates were transferred to small plastic vials (2.5” diameter x 3” height) containing different food hosts viz., cotton bolls and fruit pods of A. indicum, A. hirtum and A. ficulneus. The period from egg hatching to pupation was noted as larval period. The duration of individual instars was recorded by cut opening the fruits and fruit pods, where we observed for moulted skin and head capsule. Concurrently, the larval weight of individual instars was recorded.

The pink bollworm pupae obtained from feeding on different food hosts were placed in different cages for adult emergence. The pupal period, male and female pupal weight was recorded.

The pre-oviposition period was studied by using a pair of freshly emerged adults of both the sexes, which were released into oviposition chamber containing fresh Bt cotton twigs, A. indicum, A. hirtum and A. ficulneus twig bearing fruit pod, flowers and leaves. Ten per cent honey solution served as a food source. The oviposition chamber containing the twigs of different hosts were observed for egg laying for every 12 h interval. This was continued till the first egg mass was noticed and duration was recorded as pre-oviposition period. The period from first egg to last egg laid by the gravid PBW female was taken as ovipositional period. However, gravid female longevity from the last egg laid to its death was recorded as post-oviposition period. Ten replications were maintained for these studies.

After the emergence of pink bollworm, weight of both the sexes were recorded and the period from adult eclosion till their death was documented as adult longevity. Ten pair freshly emerged moths of both sexes from different hosts were maintained separately for the mating and longevity studies.

The morphometrics of different stages of PBW i.e., egg, larvae, pupae and adults on four hosts were recorded by using Stereo-zoom binocular microscope (NIKON: SMZ 25,

Melville, NY 11747 − 3064, U.S.A) enabled with NIS Elements D 4.00.00 software. The ten specimens of each biological stages were observed under microscope to obtain an average length and width of each individual stage of the insect. At each instar, width of the head capsule, length (cephalic end to caudal end) and breadth (thoracic region) of the larva was measured. Similarly, the body length and wing span of moths were measured by taking observation from 10 specimens.

The data obtained in the experiment under current investigation for various parameters were subjected to ANOVA for a completely randomized design with suitable statistical transformation (percentage values with arcsine and counts with square root) in R software11.

Results

Pink bollworm eggs were white immediately after laid, then turned yellow and finally orange red at the time of hatching. Eggs were sculptured, oval, flat with longitudinal lines on it. On Bt cotton, PBW eggs were laid on axils of petioles, under the young and old leaves at the junction of veins, on the squares and flowers, structure near the boll tip or under the bracteoles at the base of bolls. However, in the malvaceous plants viz., A. ficulneus, A. indicum and A. hirtum eggs were laid on the axils of petioles, underside of young leaves, under old leaves or on the fruit pods or tip of the fruit pod or on the sepals (Fig. 1).

The pupae look light brown colour initially later turned into dark brown or black at the time of adult emergence and the pupae were oval shaped with pointed tip. Male pupae were smaller in size with less distance between the segments on ventral side. Whereas, female pupae were larger than male with more distance between the segments on ventral side. The average male pupal period was 8.26 ± 0.50, 7.92 ± 0.43, 7.78 ± 0.60 and 7.58 ± 0.52 days. However, the average female pupal period was 8.54 ± 0.67, 8.10 ± 0.52, 8.03 ± 0.40 and 7.99 ± 0.39 days.

Pink bollworm adult is dark brown coloured small moth with black markings irregularly on the forewing. Whereas, the hind wing is silver grey without any distinct markings and both the wings are fringed with long hairs posterior and are elongated. However, the hind wing tip was sharply pointed. The upcurved labial palpi’s are the prominent characters of the adults with siphoning type of mouthparts. Average adult male PBW longevity was 8.93 ± 0.40, 7.86 ± 0.39, 8.02 ± 0.49 and 8.00 ± 0.50 days and average adult female PBW longevity was 9.83 ± 0.70, 8.81 ± 0.54, 8.64 ± 0.48 and 8.62 ± 0.50 days on Bt cotton, A. ficulneus, A. indicum and A. hirtum, respectively. Both adult male and female from Bt cotton lived long as compared to adults from A. indicum, A. hirtum and A. ficulneus plants.

Adults from Bt cotton recorded significantly high pre and post oviposition period, which was ranged from 2.50 to 3.50 and 3.00-4.50 days with an average of 3.17 ± 0.33 and 4.21 ± 0.37, respectively. However, on all other alternate hosts pre and post oviposition period was less with range of 2.50-3.00 and 2.50-4.00 days, respectively. No significant differences were observed in the ovipositional period among the host plants, with mean egg-laying durations of 2.48 ± 0.56, 2.26 ± 0.41, 2.21 ± 0.41 and 2.26 ± 0.37 days on Bt cotton, A. ficulneus, A. indicum and A. hirtum, respectively.

The average percent adult emergence of pink bollworm was 88.70, 83.10, 81.30 and 80.20 days, when reared on Bt cotton, A. ficulneus, A. indicum and A. hirtum, respectively (Fig. 5). Similarly, fecundity (Fig. 4) and average per cent egg hatching (Fig. 5) was highest when reared on Bt cotton as compared to A. ficulneus, A. indicum and A. hirtum.

Discussion

The present findings are in concurrence with Sapna12, Shrinivas et al.13 and Pradhan14 where they found that the average incubation period of 3.77 ± 0.78, 3.81 ± 0.10 and 3.50 ± 0.82 days with a range of 2.50–4.50, 2.50–4.50 and 2.00-4.50 days, respectively on cotton. Similarly, Cacayorin15 and Mushtaq et al.16 observed an average incubation period of 3.68 ± 0.09 and 3.83 ± 0.57 days on cotton. Whereas, an average incubation period of 4.00 ± 0.00 days with a range of 4.00–4.00 days on both cotton and abutilon17. The data on total larval period of PBW is in line with Fand et al.18 and Shah et al.19 where they found 21.50 ± 1.04 and 21.50 ± 1.04 days on cotton. Similarly, Zinzuvadiya et al.20 found that a range of 15–21 and 15–23 days in male and female, respectively.

The total larval and pupal period of PBW on malvaceous plants was less than the Bt cotton, these results are in clear agreement with the results obtained by Shrinivas et al.13 who could record that larval and pupal period of PBW was shorter on okra (23.5 ± 1.45 and 8.14 ± 0.29 days) than cotton (26.10 ± 0.66 and 8.43 ± 0.18 days) and Syed et al.21, who could record that larval period of E. vittella was shorter on okra (10.80 ± 2.50 days) than cotton (11.50 ± 1.10 days). However, Ramya Sri & Uma Maheshwari17 noticed an average larval duration of 18.70 ± 1.40 and 20.85 ± 1.45 days with pupal duration of 7.50 ± 1.00 and 7.25 ± 0.25 days on okra and abutilon, respectively.

The mean duration of adult pink bollworm on malvaceous plants was less than the Bt cotton. According to Ramya Sri & Uma Maheshwari17, male and female longevity was longer than on Bt cotton but shorter than on cotton, with no statistically significant differences among treatments. Our results are alike with research findings of Mushtaq et al.16, as they noticed pink bollworm adult emergence of 87.54 ± 2.84 and 84.25 ± 4.79 per cent when reared on Bt cotton and okra. Similarly, Shrinivas et al.13 observed 88.32 ± 10.55 and 91.66 ± 8.47 per cent of adult emergence in Bt cotton and okra.

On Bt cotton, pre oviposition period of 2.50 ± 0.00 (also on okra and abutilon), 2.91 ± 0.70 and 3.38 ± 0.34 day with a range of 2.50–2.50 (also on okra and abutilon), 1.00–3.00 and 3.00-3.50 days were recorded17,19,20. However, Pradhan14 and Sapna12 reported the oviposition period ranged from 1.50 to 2.50 and 2.00-2.50 days with a mean of 2.20 ± 0.25 and 2.05 ± 0.53 days on Bt cotton. Whereas, Zinzuvadiya et al.20 documented the post oviposition period of pink bollworm ranged from 3.00 to 6.00 days with a mean of 4.30 ± 0.82 on Bt cotton and these results are in line with our experimental results.

The present experiment morphometric results are comparable with the findings of Mushtaq et al.16 and Zinzuvadiya et al.20 as they reported an average egg length of 0.43 ± 0.02 and 0.49 ± 0.04 mm, and the width of 0.18 ± 0.01 and 0.27 ± 0.04 mm, respectively. Similarly, Mushtaq et al.16 recorded that the length and breadth of fourth instar larvae as 9.91 ± 0.36 and 1.09 ± 0.37 mm, respectively on okra. Ramya Sri & Uma Maheshwari17 reported an average of 9.53 ± 0.12, 9.60 ± 0.30 and 9.50 ± 0.30 mm of length with a range of 9.32–9.69, 9.02–9.96 and 9.10–9.90 mm, and the average pupal length was 7.20 ± 0.26, 7.20 ± 0.34 and 6.97 ± 0.12 mm, with an average pupal breadth of 2.32 ± 0.14, 2.32 ± 0.16 and 2.32 ± 0.06 mm on Bt cotton, okra and abutilon, respectively. Likewise, Shah et al.19 recorded an average of 7.00–10.00 mm length of both male and female pink bollworm moth on cotton. The Fand et al.18, Jothi et al.22, Sapna12 and Dharajothi et al.10 as they reported weight of full-grown larvae on Bt cotton was 25.00 ± 3.63, 21.40 ± 3.63, 30.12 ± 1.11 and 21.40 ± 3.63 mg, respectively.

Present results are in contrary with Zinzuvadiya et al.20, where the average egg incubation period was 4.90 ± 0.99 days on artificial diet; Mushtaq et al.16 reported the average larval period was 24.90 and 26.38 days. This variation might be due to the effect of abiotic factors such as temperature, photoperiod and relative humidity at which the experiment was conducted. Contrary results with Shrinivas et al.13 on the mean fecundity of 118.65 ± 16.81 with a range of 100–185 eggs per female in okra. This variation might be due to the change in nutritional content or biochemicals compounds in the different malvaceous plants.

The pink bollworm average life cycle was less on all the malvaceous plants except Bt cotton. The life cycle of the insect mainly depends on the nutrient content and toxicity of the host, as the Bt cotton containing cry toxins might have influenced on increased life cycle period of pink bollworm. The reduced feeding rate and nutrient absorption in pink bollworm feeding on the cry toxin containing seeds and also, insect spend more time in detoxifying those toxins. This results in the slower larval development and longer life cycle of the insect. However, the average life cycle of the insect on other malvaceous (alternate) hosts was shorter, as these plants serve primarily for feeding, dispersal, or off-season survival rather than full development (to which the insects are not evolutionarily adapted). This reduction likely stems from differences in resources or environmental conditions, where alternate hosts fail to provide the necessary nutrients or shelter for optimal insect development. While these hosts enable faster development, their lower nutritional quality results in smaller, less fecund adults compared to those reared on “resource-rich” (though toxic) Bt cotton. Further, research is needed to know the nutritional and biochemical variation in different malvaceous hosts plants in comparison with pink bollworm biology.

Conclusion

This study reveals that Bt cotton fosters larger, longer-lived pink bollworm individuals despite extended development times, while alternate malvaceous hosts like A. indicum, A. hirtum, and A. ficulneus enable quicker generational turnover but yield smaller, less robust adults. These host-specific differences highlight the role of non-cotton malvaceous plants as off-season refuges sustaining Bt resistant populations, complicating resistance evolution in cotton systems. Integrating host eradication, refuge strategies, and toxin rotation into IPM frameworks could disrupt this cycle, enhancing Bt cotton durability and sustainable pest control in India.
