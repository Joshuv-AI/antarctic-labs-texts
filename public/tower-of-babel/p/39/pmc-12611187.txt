Lipoxin A4 Regulates M2 Macrophage‐Derived Exosomal miR‐25‐5p to Protect Cell Pyroptosis in Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia
Authors: Tianyu Chen, Yun Yang, Yuhan Zhang, Mingyu You, Yufeng Zhou, Tao Ning, Han Lu, Jin Wang, Song Peng, Xiaoqing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) involves macrophage‐driven immunoinflammation, while macrophage‐derived exosomes act as key extracellular vehicles linking macrophage‐driven inflammation with tissue injury. Emerging evidence suggests that Lipoxin A4 (LXA4) modulates macrophage immune responses. However, the precise role of LXA4 in regulating macrophage function in BPD and its underlying mechanisms remains unclear. Neonatal rats were exposed to 85% hyperoxia to establish a BPD model. LXA4 or M2‐polarized macrophage‐derived exosomes (M2‐exo) were injected intraperitoneally, and pulmonary inflammation, macrophage polarization and pyroptosis were examined. M2‐exo were co‐cultured with type II alveolar epithelial cells (AT2) to further validate the animal experimental findings and explore underlying mechanisms. Here, we find that monocyte‐derived macrophages were activated in BPD. LXA4 significantly ameliorated lung tissue injury and pyroptosis by promoting M2‐exos release. Single‐cell transcriptomic profiling revealed altered macrophage‐epithelial cell interactions in BPD lungs. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that M2‐exo‐derived miR‐25‐5p alleviated AT2 pyroptosis, which targeted nuclear receptor binding protein 2 (NRBP2) and downregulated the PI3K/AKT pathway. Additionally, PI3K inhibition (LY294002) or miR‐25‐5p knockdown reversed these protective effects. This study proved that LXA4 promotes M2 macrophage polarization and enhances exosomal miR‐25‐5p release, thereby inhibiting pyroptosis via the NRBP2/PI3K/AKT axis and ameliorating BPD lung injury. These findings elucidate the lung‐protective mechanism of LXA4 and highlight a novel macrophage–AT2 cross‐talk in BPD, providing new therapeutic strategies for BPD‐related lung injury.

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) remains a common and serious respiratory complication in preterm infants, associated with high mortality and long‐term impacts on patient well‐being [1]. Persistent lung function impairment and structural damage to pulmonary airways and vessels may continue into adulthood, with no effective therapies currently available [2, 3]. Thus, seeking effective treatments for BPD has significant clinical and socioeconomic importance.

The pathogenesis of BPD involves various prenatal and postnatal factors. Prenatal influences include intrauterine growth restriction and maternal smoking, while postnatal factors comprise mechanical ventilation, oxygen toxicity, and infections [4, 5]. These insults trigger inflammation in the delicate, developing lungs of preterm infants, exacerbating alveolar simplification and impairing pulmonary microvascular development, thereby establishing the pathological basis of BPD [1, 6].

As a potent endogenous anti‐inflammatory mediator, lipoxin A4 (LXA4) has therapeutic potential in inflammatory disorders, including asthma, myocardial infarction, and rheumatoid arthritis. Current research has highlighted the emerging therapeutic potential of LXA4 in BPD. Experimental studies have demonstrated that LXA4 effectively attenuates hyperoxia‐induced airway inflammation, alveolar simplification, and restores alveolar septal thickness in preclinical models [7, 8, 9], suggesting its capacity to mitigate pathological structural remodeling of BPD progression. In‐depth investigation of LXA4's mechanisms not only advances BPD treatment but also optimizes its anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties from a small‐molecule drug perspective, laying a foundation for clinical application transformation. Research indicates that alveolar macrophages are the primary target cells of LXA4 [10], which exhibit well‐developed immune function during the early postnatal period in preterm infants [11]. Recent studies establish that macrophages play pivotal roles in BPD pathogenesis and are essential for pulmonary immune homeostasis [12, 13, 14].

Alveolar macrophages display remarkable plasticity and are influenced by epigenetic and immunometabolic microenvironmental factors. Upon environmental stimulation, resting alveolar macrophages (M0‐Mφs) differentiate into pro‐inflammatory M1 (M1‐Mφs) or reparative M2 (M2‐Mφs) subtypes, which can interconvert under specific conditions [15]. Exposures such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and hyperoxia promote circulating monocyte recruitment to the lungs and their reprogramming into M1‐Mφs, exacerbating lung injury and impairing alveolar and microvascular development [16, 17]. Conversely, cytokines like interleukin‐4 (IL‐4), interleukin‐13 (IL‐13) and glucocorticoids induce M0‐Mφs transformation into M2‐Mφs, attenuating alveolar epithelial injury and enhancing barrier function [18, 19]. Maintaining an appropriate balance between M1/M2 enables effective clearance of inflammatory mediators and promotes lung repair [15, 17], indicating that modulating macrophage polarization and inhibiting the cascading inflammatory response is crucial for treating BPD‐related lung injury.

Exosomes, nanosized extracellular vesicles with a size from 40 to 160 nm, have emerged as key mediators of intercellular communication. They transport bioactive molecules (e.g., nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and metabolites) to recipient cells, facilitating intercellular communication across both short and long distances. Served as a pleiotropic intercellular transport system in the human body, they affect diverse processes of cytobiology in health and disease. Exosomes influence gene expression and signaling pathways to regulate immune responses primarily through miRNA transfer, with their bilayer membrane protecting bioactive molecules from degradation in the extracellular environment, ensuring long‐term stability [20]. On the one hand, due to the correct recognition of target cells by surface receptors and ligands, these active molecules are successfully and completely absorbed by recipient cells [21]. On the other hand, exosomes exhibit low immunogenicity, high specificity, high tolerance, and good biocompatibility, making them ideal endogenous nanocarriers for drug delivery for various diseases.

In recent years, successive reports on pyroptosis have sparked new explorations into the mechanisms behind acute and chronic lung injury [22, 23, 24]. Pyroptosis is a type of programmed cell death distinct from apoptosis, characterized by the activation of caspase‐1, NOD‐like receptor thermal protein domain–associated protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, and key execution protein Gasdermin D (GSDMD), with the release of interleukin‐18 (IL‐18) and interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β). When cells are exposed to external stimuli, NLRP3 associates with speck‐like proteins to form complexes, which possess the ability to sense extracellular pathogens or damage signals. Subsequently, pro‐caspase‐1 undergoes autoproteolytic cleavage to generate caspases‐1. Caspase‐1 functions to cleave the GSDMD protein, producing an active N‐terminal fragment (GSDMD‐N). GSDMD‐N perforates the cell membrane by binding to phosphatidylserine and phosphoinositides. This disrupts cellular osmotic equilibrium, leading to membrane rupture and cell death. Additionally, caspase‐1 facilitates the maturation of IL‐1β and IL‐18 from their inactive precursors into active forms. Following cell death, IL‐1β and IL‐18 are released extracellularly to amplify the inflammatory response [25]. On stimulation in the lungs by pathogen‐associated molecular patterns or damage‐associated molecular patterns, a dual‐phase immunopathological cascade is initiated. On the one hand, innate immune responses are rapidly activated, characterized by neutrophils and macrophages infiltration. These infiltrating leukocytes release substantial quantities of reactive oxygen species that not only trigger the expression of pyroptotic executors GSDMD but also function as secondary messengers to amplify inflammatory signaling [26, 27]. On the other hand, signaling pathways such as NF‐κB are activated, driving the transcriptional upregulation and post‐translational assembly of NLRP3 inflammasome components [28, 29, 30]. Notably, pyroptosis itself massively releases pro‐inflammatory mediators (IL‐1β, IL‐18), which exacerbate alveolar epithelial cell damage and disrupt alveolar‐capillary barrier integrity [23, 31, 32]. Pyroptosis plays a pivotal role in lung injury caused by various stimuli, while inhibiting excessive pyroptosis can effectively alleviate acute and chronic lung injury [17, 33]. Hyperoxia activates the inflammasome to trigger pyroptotic execution, whereas pharmacological inhibition of caspase‐1 or genetic knockout of GSDMD ameliorates cell pyroptosis and restores impairment in alveolar and vascular development in BPD [24, 34]. Therefore, further exploration of the regulatory mechanisms of the BPD pyroptosis signaling pathway is essential for overcoming previous inflammation regulation challenges. Recent literature suggests that LXA4 inhibits the expression of key pyroptosis targets such as NLRP3 and caspase‐1 through multiple signaling pathways [35, 36, 37], however, the specific role and mechanism of LXA4 in BPD pyroptosis remain unexplored.

In this study, we aimed to demonstrate the role of LXA4 in BPD by inducing M2‐Mφs polarization and reversing pyroptosis in vivo. Bioinformatics analysis and further mechanistic studies revealed that M2‐exo transferred miR‐25‐5p to AT2, where it suppressed nuclear receptor binding protein 2 (NRBP2) to downregulate the PI3K/AKT pathway. These findings reveal that LXA4 protects AT2 from pyroptosis through a novel macrophage‐AT2 crosstalk pathway, thereby mitigating inflammation and protecting against BPD lung injury.

Materials and Methods

Animal Model's Construction of BPD

Clean‐grade Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from the Animal Experimental Center at Nanjing Medical University. All animal research was carried out following the laboratory animal use guidelines set forth by the National Institutes of Health and received approval from the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (Approval Number: IACUC‐2210014).

Following timed mating, gestational day (GD) 0.5 was defined by the identification of a vaginal plug in SD rats. Pregnant dams were individually housed under controlled photoperiod (every 12 h light/dark cycle) until parturition. To establish BPD, neonatal pups were exposed to 85% oxygen in a sealed hyperoxic chamber from postnatal day (PND) 0 to PND 9, with continuous oxygen concentration monitoring via a calibrated gas analyzer (Servomex 5800C). Normoxic controls were maintained in ambient air (21% O2) under identical temperature and humidity conditions. All litters remained with nursing dams, with cross‐fostering protocols implemented daily: dams were systematically rotated every 24 h between hyperoxic and normoxic groups to mitigate oxygen toxicity‐induced maternal behavioral confounders, while ensuring uninterrupted ad libitum feeding and pup grooming.

Cell Culture and Differentiation

Type II alveolar epithelial cells (A549) and human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS‐2B) were grown in DMEM medium (KeyGEN BioTECH, Nanjing, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cells were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (THP‐1) were grown in RPMI‐1640 medium (Gibco, Suzhou, China). THP‐1 cells were differentiated into resting‐state M0‐Mφs by treatment with 75 ng/mL phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) for 48 h. M0‐Mφs were polarized into M1‐Mφs using 20 ng/mL LPS and interferon‐γ for 48 h, or into M2‐Mφs by treatment with 20 ng/mL IL‐4 and IL‐13 for the same duration.

Isolation and Characterization of Exosomes

The adherent M1‐Mφs and M2‐Mφs obtained from the polarization of Thp‐1 had a slower proliferation rate. The medium was replaced with fresh Opti‐MEM serum‐free medium (Gibco, Suzhou, China) and cultured for another 48 h before collecting the supernatant. To isolate extracellular vesicles (EVs) from the cell culture supernatant, the supernatant was first centrifuged at 3000 × g for 20 min to remove cell debris. Then, the supernatant was mixed with exosome isolation reagent (Vazyme #R601) at a ratio of 3:1 and incubated vertically at 4°C for 16 h to separate the exosomes. Subsequently, the solution underwent ultracentrifugation (10 000 × g, 30 min, 4°C) for complete exosome isolation. The resultant pellet was subsequently resuspended in PBS through vortex mixing before cryopreservation at −80°C. Temperature control was maintained at 4°C throughout all centrifugation procedures.

The morphology of the exosomes was examined by a JEM‐2100 transmission electron microscope (TEM, Hitachi, Japan). The particle dimensions were measured by a nanoparticle tracking analyzer (Nano FCM, China). The protein markers (CD81 and CD9) of purified exosomes were validated by Western blotting.

It should be added that exosomes obtained from M1‐Mφs and M2‐Mφs were, respectively, designated as M1‐exo and M2‐exo. LXA4 + M2‐exo was obtained from M2‐Mφs treated with 200 ng/mL LXA4, while LXA4 + M1‐exo was acquired from M1‐Mφs after co‐incubation with 200 ng/mL LXA4 as the control.

In Vivo Administration

Neonatal pups were divided into three groups (n = 10/group): (1) Hyperoxia (HOX) group, (2) Normoxia (NOX) control, and (3) HOX + LXA4 intervention group (intraperitoneal injection of 5 μg/kg LXA4 on PND 3, 5, and 7). Phosphate buffer (PBS) was used as a negative control in an equal volume. On PND 9, lung tissue was harvested after saline perfusion and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde.

To investigate M2‐exo roles, neonatal rats were divided into: (1) NOX (21% O2), (2) HOX (85% O2), and (3) HOX + M2‐exo (1 × 106 particles/rat via i.p. injection on PND 3, 5, 7). All pups were euthanized on PND 9, and lung tissue was collected for further analysis.

H&E and Immunofluorescence Staining

The lung tissue was embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate lung inflammation and injury. The size of the alveolar space, the thickness of the alveolar septum, and the infiltration of the alveolar space were observed to assess lung injury.

For immunofluorescence staining, the paraffin‐embedded lung tissue sections were dewaxed with xylene, repaired antigen with 0.01 M citric acid buffer, blocked with 1% goat serum and 3% BSA in PBS, incubated with Arg‐1 and iNOS antibodies at 4°C for 12 h, then conjugated with species‐specific secondary antibodies and Alexa Fluor dyes. Nuclear DNA was stained by DAPI and the degree of staining was observed under an inverted optical microscope (cells with brown cytoplasm were judged as positive cells).

Data Sources and Processing

The raw expression matrix of single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data from 6 normal and 6 BPD lung tissues was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, with the accession number GSE209664. The dataset includes scRNA‐seq profiles of PND 14 C57BL/6 mouse lungs exposed to room air or hyperoxia (male: female = 1:1).

Cellular Aggregation and Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) Analysis

Raw scRNA‐seq data were processed using Cell Ranger v3.0.2 to generate count matrices, which were subsequently loaded into Seurat v4.1.1 (https://satijalab.org/seurat) for downstream analysis [38]. To minimize technical variability, the “sctransform” function in Seurat was applied for normalization, scaling, and variance stabilization. Quality control metrics were assessed to exclude low‐quality cells, with thresholds set for mitochondrial gene content, total unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), and detected genes. After filtering, variable genes were identified, and dimensionality was reduced by principal component analysis (PCA). Clustering was conducted using the Louvain algorithm, and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) [39] was applied for the visualization of cell populations.

DEGs for each cluster were identified by the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat, with thresholds set at a Log2 fold‐change > 1 and an adjusted p‐value < 0.05. Identified marker genes were used to annotate cell types based on canonical markers reported in the original data literature [40]. Subpopulations of interest were further subsetted into new Seurat objects for detailed reanalysis.

Pseudotime Trajectory and Cell Developmental Pathway

Utilizing the Monocle 3 suite (https://cole‐trapnell‐lab.github.io/monocle3), the dynamic changes of cells along the pseudotime trajectory were analyzed [41]. Following clustering and dimensionality reduction, and visualizing with UMAP, the developmental trends of cells were observed. On this basis, cells were precisely classified, and single‐cell trajectory analyses for Type II alveolar epithelial cells and monocyte–macrophages were conducted.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

To deeply understand the biological functions of Type II alveolar epithelial cells and macrophages, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were employed. Utilizing the clusterProfiler package [42], GO and KEGG pathway functional enrichment analyses were conducted based on the unique marker genes of each cell subset. Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the background set “c2.cp.kegg.v7.0.symbols.gmt” from the MSigDB database and the clusterProfiler package [43, 44]. A p value threshold of less than 0.05 was established to denote statistical significance.

Cell‐to‐Cell Communication Analysis

To investigate the interaction mechanisms between the 20 clustered cell subsets, the CellChat R package was used to perform cell–cell communication analysis [45]. DEGs within each cell cluster were identified and integrated with known receptor‐ligand interaction databases provided by CellChat. The analysis leveraged curated signaling pathways and receptor‐ligand pair information to infer intercellular communication networks. This approach provided insights into the roles of specific cell types in the overall signaling network and their functional contributions to the biological processes of interest.

Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) Cell Proliferation Assay

PBS was added to the perimeter wells of a 96‐well plate to reduce medium evaporation. Cells were devolved into the 96‐well plate at a density of approximately 4 × 103 cells/well. The plate was incubated overnight at a 37°C incubator containing 5% CO2 to allow cell attachment and stabilization. After replacing fresh medium, 1 1 μg/mL LPS was added and incubated for 5 h. Acidic ATP was neutralized with sodium hydroxide to a pH of 7. Then, ATP of different concentrations was added and incubated for 2 h. Subsequently, the fresh culture medium was replaced. After 48 h, 100 μL of 10% CCK‐8 detection solution (Vazyme, China) was added to each well, incubating for 1 h. Then, the optical density (OD) at 450 nm of different treatment groups was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA). The cell viability was calculated as follows: Cell viability (%) = [(As‐Ab)/(Ac – Ab)] × 100% (As: OD value of the experimental wells containing cells, medium, drug, and CCK‐8 solution; Ab: OD value of the blank wells containing only medium and CCK‐8 solution, without cells and drug; Ac: OD value of the control wells containing cells, medium, and CCK‐8 solution, without drug).

Cell Transfection

Cells were seeded in six‐well plates and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 until 60%–70% confluent. Following the guidelines of LipofectamineTM 3000 (Invitrogen, USA), solution A (125 μL Opti‐MEM + 5 μL miR‐25‐5p mimic/inhibitor/nc) and solution B (125 μL Opti‐MEM + 5 μL Lipofectamine3000) were prepared and incubated for 5 min. Solutions A and B were mixed, incubated for 20 min for lipoplex formation, and gently added to the culture medium. The miR‐25‐5p mimic/inhibitor/nc were synthesized by GenePharma(Shanghai). The sequences are shown in the Supporting Information Table S1.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Cells were collected when they grew to over 80% confluency, and resuspended in 1 × binding buffer. Apoptosis was assessed following the guidelines provided by the flow cytometric apoptosis detection kit (Lianke Biotechnology, Hangzhou, China). Cells were treated with 5 μL of propidium iodide (PI) and 3 μL of Annexin V working solution, then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. A blank control and single‐reagent controls were set up to adjust the parameters. Respectively, the Annexin V‐FITC fluorescence and PI fluorescence were detected via the FITC channel (Ex = 488 nm/Em = 530 nm) and PI channel (Ex = 535 nm/Em = 615 nm) of a flow cytometer. Data were analyzed with Flowjo software.

PI/Hoechst 33342 Double Fluorescent Staining

When the cells in the six‐well plate grew to 70%–80% confluency, the cell supernatant was discarded. After washing with PBS, all cells were re‐suspended in 1 mL Cell Stain Buffer and stained with 5 μL Hoechst 33342 and 5 μL PI working solution following the guidelines of the cell apoptosis/necrosis detection kit (MCE, USA). The mixture was placed on ice for 20–30 min; then, fluorescence intensity was assessed under a fluorescence microscope.

Dual‐Luciferase Gene Reporter

The 3′‐UTR sequence of NRBP2, which includes the anticipated miR‐25‐5p binding site along with its mutant version, was inserted into a plasmid vector and subsequently introduced into A549 cells. In the described plasmid vector, firefly luciferase was cloned as an experimental reporter gene, and the Renilla luciferase gene was cloned to monitor transfection efficiency. Following the guidelines of the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (E1910, Promega), the cells were lysed, and Luciferase Assay Reagent II was added to measure the activity of firefly luciferase. Subsequently, we added Stop & Glo Reagent to assess Renilla luciferase activity. Target gene luciferase activity was expressed as relative light units, calculated by dividing the average firefly luciferase activity by the average Renilla luciferase activity.

Real‐Time Quantitative PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was isolated with TRIZOL reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse‐transcribed into cDNA using the mRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qRT‐PCR was conducted with SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a LightCycler 96 real‐time PCR instrument (Roche, Switzerland). Gene expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences can be found in Supporting Information Table S2.

miRNA Quantification

After total RNA extraction using TRIZOL reagent (Invitrogen, USA), miRNA was reverse‐transcribed into cDNA according to the instructions of the riboSCRIPT Reverse Transcription Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China, C11027‐2). qRT‐PCR was performed using the Bulge‐LOOPTM miRNA qRT‐PCR Starter Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). U6 was used as an internal reference, and the relative expression was calculated by the comparative cycle threshold method (Ct = 2−ΔΔCT). The stem‐loop reverse transcription primer and qPCR primer for has‐miR‐25‐5p were synthesized by Guangzhou RiboBio corporation.

Western Blot Analysis

Protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein samples were loaded and run on a 10%–12% SDS‐PAGE gel (Vazyme, China). Then the proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). The membrane was washed in TBST buffer containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 10 min × 3 times, blocked with 5% skim milk (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies against GAPDH (GB15004‐100, Servicebio), NLRP3 (ab263899, abcam), caspase‐1 (ab207802, abcam), GSDMD (ab209845, abcam), GSDMD‐N (ab215203, abcam), CD9 (ab26301, abcam), CD81 (ab109201, abcam), Arg‐1 (ab315110, abcam), iNOS (ab178945, abcam), PI3K (HA601206, Proteintech), AKT (HA721870, Proteintech), P‐PI3K (AP0427, ABclonal), P‐AKT (AP1453, ABclonal), NRBP2 (ER1914‐13, Proteintech) at 4°C overnight. After that, the membrane was washed with TBST buffer for 10 min × 3 times and incubated with HRP‐conjugated anti‐rabbit or anti‐mouse secondary antibody (SA00001‐2 or SA00001‐1, Proteintech) at room temperature for 2 h. The protein strips were observed using ECL chemiluminescence reagent (Biosharp, China) on a Tanon—5200 image analyzer (Tanon) and quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Differences between two groups were assessed by Student's t‐test, while multiple group comparisons (≥ 3 groups) were performed by one‐way ANOVA. Survival curves were compared using the Kaplan–Meier method. Data are presented as mean ± standard error, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

LXA4 Protects Against Neonatal Rat BPD Lung Injury and Modulates Macrophage Polarization

Newborn pups were subjected to 85% oxygen to establish a BPD model. To assess LXA4's impact on the lung inflammation in BPD, LXA4 was injected intraperitoneally in SD rats. To rule out the toxicity of LXA4 to organisms, we conducted a toxicity test of LXA4 in lung epithelial cells, and the results showed that LXA4 had minimal effect on cell activity (Figure S1A). Survival rates and body weights were monitored every other day from PND 1 to 9. There were significant differences in survival rates among the three groups from PND 4, with the HOX group showing lower survival than the NOX group, but improving after LXA4 intervention (Figure 1A). Body weight gain in the HOX group slowed from the 5th day, which was statistically different from the NOX group, and this difference became more notable as the rat age increased. While there was no statistical difference existing between the HOX + LXA4 group and the NOX group from PND 1 to 9, this indicated that LXA4 mitigated hyperoxia‐induced growth restriction (Figure 1B).

(D) The mRNA expression levels of pyroptosis key molecules (NLRP3, caspase‐1, GSDMD, IL‐18) and macrophage markers (iNOS, Arg‐1) in lung tissues detected by qRT‐PCR. On PND 9, rats underwent saline perfusion for lung tissue collection. The findings indicated that the HOX lung tissues displayed alveolar wall disruption and thickening, erythrocyte infiltration, and inflammatory cell exudation, which were alleviated by LXA4 (Figure 1C). Hyperoxia increased the levels of mRNA and proteins associated with pyroptosis (including NLRP3, caspase‐1, GSDMD, IL‐18), while these markers were significantly reduced by LXA4 (Figure 1D,E), indicating that LXA4 suppresses pyroptosis in lung tissue of SD newborn rats that were subjected to hyperoxia. M1‐Mφs and M2‐Mφs polarization was assessed by markers for inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and arginase‐1 (Arg‐1). As confirmed by mRNA and protein levels, M1‐Mφs were significantly increased after hyperoxia challenge, while LXA4 promoted a shift toward M2‐Mφs (Figure 1D,E). Immunohistochemical staining was performed on the lung tissues and confirmed these findings (Figure 1F).

Single‐Cell Landscape of Normal and Hyperoxic Lung Tissues

Through scRNA‐seq analysis of lung tissues derived from 6 normal and 6 hyperoxia‐exposed mice (GSE209664 dataset), we characterized the multicellular ecosystem of the lung at single‐cell resolution and explored the cellular composition changes and potential differentiation trajectories. Following quality control, dimensionality reduction, and clustering analysis, a total of 20 distinct cell types were identified based on canonical marker gene expression, including type I alveolar epithelial cells (AT1), type II alveolar epithelial cells (AT2), alveolar macrophages (AM), interstitial macrophages–monocytes (IM‐monocytes), monocytes–dendritic cells (Monocytes‐DC), T cells, and others (Figure 2A). Cell proportion analysis revealed significant shifts in pulmonary cellular composition induced by hyperoxia exposure. Compared to normoxic controls, hyperoxia‐exposed lungs exhibited increased proportions of AT1 cells, proliferating AT2 cells, and AM, while the proportions of AT2 cells and IM‐monocytes were decreased (Figure 2B,C).

To investigate potential cellular differentiation processes, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle 3. The trajectory reconstruction suggested potential differentiation pathways from AT2 to AT1 cells, and from IM‐monocytes toward AM (Figure 2D,E). These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that under inflammatory conditions, circulating monocytes can be recruited to the lung and differentiate into various myeloid cell types, rather than solely relying on resident macrophage proliferation [16, 46, 47]. Our analysis revealed two potential key cellular responses to hyperoxia: (1) enhanced AT2 cell proliferation concurrent with accelerated AT1 cell differentiation; (2) polarization of IM‐monocytes toward the AM lineage under oxygen stress conditions.

Cell–cell interaction analysis using CellChat revealed extensive and coordinated intercellular communications under hyperoxia conditions, with AM showing direct signaling interactions with AT2 cells (Figure 2F). Quantitative analysis of interaction strength and frequency demonstrated that AT2 cells exhibited more robust signaling connections with macrophage populations compared to monocytes (Figure 2G,H). Cell clustering was according to acknowledged cell marker genes (Figure 2I). Notably, hyperoxia appeared to enhance the crosstalk between these cell types, suggesting a critical role for macrophage‐epithelial interactions in alveolar remodeling and repair under oxygen stress. These findings provide valuable insights into the dynamic changes within the lung multicellular ecosystem under oxygen stress and highlight potential mechanisms underlying lung injury and repair.

Macrophage Phenotypes and Exosome Characterization

We further explored the signaling and biological functions involved in AM and AT2 under hyperoxia exposure by functional enrichment analysis. AM was enriched in responses to LPS and injury stimuli, chemotaxis, endocytosis, and T cell immune regulation (Figure 3A), while AT2 showed enrichment in biological tissue development, transforming growth factor‐β signaling, response to stimuli, and redox homeostasis (Figure 3B). Previous studies have found that circulating monocytes are the main players in immune action during lung injury; accordingly, suspended human monocytes (THP‐1) were differentiated into adherent macrophages in vitro to further investigate macrophage‐epithelial interactions. M1‐Mφs are characterized by round blunt polygons, while M2‐Mφs are more biased toward an elongated spindle shape (Figure 3C). qRT‐PCR and Western blot confirmed successful polarization: M1‐Mφs expressed high levels of CD80, CD86, and iNOS, while M2‐Mφs upregulated CD163, CD209, and Arg‐1 (Figure S1B,C). Exosomes were isolated from macrophage‐conditioned media using an exosome extraction kit. M1‐exo and M2‐exo exhibited a disc‐shaped lipid bilayer structure (Figure 3D), with diameters ranging from 30 to 150 nm (Figure 3E). Western blot verified that M1‐exo and M2‐exo specifically expressed their surface markers CD81 and CD9, which were absent in parental macrophages (Figure S1D).

Induction of Pyroptosis in AT2 Cells

As key inducers of lung injury inflammation, LPS is a Gram‐negative bacterial cell wall component, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is a high‐energy compound. Pyroptosis of AT2 in vitro was induced by stimulating them with LPS (1 μg/mL, 5 h) and ATP (0–5 mM, 2 h). CCK‐8 showed that cell viability gradually decreased with increasing ATP concentrations, with the lowest survival observed at 5 mM ATP (Figure 3F). Interestingly, the cell survival rate increased when induced with LPS alone, indicating that LPS at a low concentration could stimulate cell proliferation (Figure 3F). However, LPS triggers only an inflammatory reaction in the cells, while the occurrence of pyroptosis also requires ATP to initiate inflammasome assembly. The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome led to the cleavage of procaspase‐1 into caspase‐1, which in turn cleaved GSDMD to form pores in the cell membrane and secreted mature IL‐1β and IL‐18 extracellularly. qRT‐PCR confirmed that the mRNA expressions of GSDMD and IL‐18 were up‐regulated with the increase of ATP concentration (Figure 3G), and LPS + ATP (5 mM) maximized the protein expression of pyroptosis markers (NLRP3, caspase‐1, and GSDMD‐N) (Figure 3H). Therefore, LPS + ATP (5 mM) was used to induce pyroptosis (pyro+) in subsequent experiments. These data indicate that LPS provides the initial signal to trigger the inflammatory response, while ATP is the second essential signal for the final induction of pyroptosis in vitro.

M2‐Exo Ameliorates Pyroptosis in AT2

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of M2‐exo, AT2 cells were co‐cultured with M2‐exo (100 μg/mL) for 24 h before pyroptosis induction. Microscopic analysis revealed that pyroptotic cells exhibited characteristic swelling and blebbing, which were markedly reduced by M2‐exo intervention (Figure 4A). Compared to the Pyro+ group, M2‐exo significantly downregulated mRNA expression of NLRP3, GSDMD, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β (Figure 4B). Correspondingly, protein levels of these pyroptosis‐related mediators were reduced after M2‐exo treatment (Figure 4C). Flow cytometry showed that M2‐exo significantly reduced the occurrence of cell death (Figure 4D). PI/Hoechst 33342 dual staining further demonstrated a marked reduction in red‐stained pyroptotic cells with compromised membrane integrity following M2‐exo treatment (Figure 4E). These findings collectively demonstrate that M2‐exo effectively mitigates pyroptosis in AT2 cells by suppressing key inflammatory and cell death pathways.

LXA4 Enhances Exosome‐Mediated Inhibition of Pyroptosis

AT2 cells were incubated with LXA4 (200 ng/mL) for 24 h before pyroptosis induction to evaluate the role of LXA4 in pyroptosis. Compared with the Pyro+ group, qRT‐PCR revealed that LXA4 significantly downregulated mRNA levels of NLRP3, caspase‐1, GSDMD, and IL‐1β (Figure S2A). Additionally, THP‐1 cells were co‐incubated with LXA4 for 24 h before differentiation into M1‐Mφs and M2‐Mφs, and their exosomes (M1‐exo, M2‐exo, LXA4 + M1‐exo, LXA4 + M2‐exo) were extracted. AT2 cells were co‐cultured with these exosomes for 24 h before pyroptosis induction. Compared with the Pyro+ group, M1‐exo increased the levels of caspase‐1, GSDMD, and inflammatory cytokines IL‐18 and IL‐1β, thereby exacerbating pyroptosis, while M2‐exo suppressed these markers (Figure S2B,C), consistent with their pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory roles, respectively. Notably, LXA4 intervention altered M1‐exo function. LXA4 + M1‐exo reduced IL‐1β and IL‐18 levels compared to M1‐exo (Figure S2B), suggesting partial reversal of pyroptosis. LXA4 + M2‐exo downregulated caspase‐1, GSDMD, IL‐1β, and IL‐18 more effectively than M2‐exo alone, exhibiting enhanced protective effects (Figure S2C). These findings suggest that LXA4 reprograms macrophage‐derived exosomes, enhancing their anti‐inflammatory and anti‐pyroptotic properties. Combined with prior evidence that LXA4 promotes M2 polarization, we propose that LXA4 induces functional changes in THP‐1‐derived macrophages, enhancing the anti‐inflammatory capacity of M2‐exo and enriching exosomal cargo with pyroptosis‐inhibiting factors.

M2‐Exo Attenuates Lung Injury and Pyroptosis in Neonatal Rats

Next, we verified the rescue effect of M2‐exo on BPD‐induced lung injury and pyroptosis in neonatal rats in vivo. The results showed that M2‐exo administration alleviated the hyperoxia‐induced decrease in survival rate (Figure 5A). Although the survival rates could not be completely restored to normoxic levels, M2‐exo normalized body weights to NOX group levels (Figure 5B). Compared to NOX controls, hyperoxia‐exposed rats showed disordered alveolar structure with varying sizes, septal rupture, alveolar fusion, reduced alveolar count, erythrocyte infiltration, inflammatory cell exudation, and interstitial proliferation, which were ameliorated by M2‐exo treatment (Figure 5C). M2‐exo improved alveolar structure, increased alveolar number, and enhanced secondary septal development, showing reduced alveolar fusion phenomenon and more normalized alveolar structure. qRT‐PCR and Western blot revealed that hyperoxia significantly upregulated pyroptosis markers (NLRP3, caspase‐1, GSDMD, IL‐1β) at both mRNA and protein levels, while M2‐exo suppressed these markers (Figure 5D,E). These results indicate that M2‐exo mitigates hyperoxia‐induced lung injury and pyroptosis in BPD rats.

M2‐Exo Delivers miR‐25‐5p to Protect Against Pyroptosis in AT2

We conducted high‐throughput sequencing to map the miRNA expression profiles of M1‐exo and M2‐exo, identifying up to 501 differentially expressed miRNAs (Figure 6A). Among the top 10 upregulated miRNAs in M2‐exo, miR‐25‐5p was the most abundant miRNA (Supporting Information Table S3). qRT‐PCR confirmed high miR‐25‐5p expression in M2 macrophages and its increase in AT2 cells after M2‐exo coculture (Figure 6B). To investigate its role, AT2 cells were transfected with miR‐25‐5p nc (miR‐nc) as a negative control, miR‐25‐5p mimic (miR‐mimic) to overexpress miR‐25‐5p and miR‐25‐5p inhibitor (miR‐inhibit) to knock down miR‐25‐5p before pyroptosis induction (Figure 6C). Overexpression of miR‐25‐5p significantly reduced the mRNA and protein levels of pyroptosis‐related molecules (NLRP3, caspase‐1, GSDMD) and inflammatory cytokines (IL‐1β, IL‐18), while knockdown had the opposite effect (Figure 6D–F). Flow cytometry confirmed that miR‐mimic reduced the occurrence of cell death, whereas miR‐inhibit increased it (Figure 6G). Similarly, PI/Hoechst 33342 double staining showed that the red‐stained cells with broken cell membranes decreased after miR‐mimic intervention, which was significantly increased under the action of miR‐inhibit (Figure 6H). These findings demonstrate that M2‐exo delivers miR‐25‐5p to AT2 to suppress pyroptosis and inflammation.

miR‐25‐5p Protects AT2 and BPD Lung From Pyroptosis by Inhibiting PI3K/AKT Signaling

KEGG pathway indicated that the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway ranked first among the enriched pathways for upregulated differentially expressed microRNAs (Figure 7A). The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is closely related to growth, development, and cell apoptosis. Our experiments found that the mRNA levels of PI3K and AKT were upregulated during pyroptosis, while miR‐25‐5p overexpression reversed this (Figure 7B). Results by Western blot showed that the protein levels of phosphorylated PI3K and AKT increased in pyroptotic cells, inhibited by miR‐25‐5p mimic (Figure 7C,D). LY294002, an inhibitor of the PI3K signaling, was used to incubate cells for 0.5 h. qRT‐PCR and Western blot indicated a notable reduction in PI3K expression (Figure 7E,F). Before inducing pyroptosis in AT2, they were transfected with miR‐25‐5p inhibitor to knock down miR‐25‐5p. qRT‐PCR showed that the mRNA levels of PI3K and AKT were partially up‐regulated, while LY294002 reversed this situation (Figure 7G). Moreover, Western blot results showed that compared with the pyroptotic AT2 transfected with miR‐25‐5p inhibitor, the intervention of LY294002 reduced the protein expression level of the key pyroptotic molecule GSDMD‐N (Figure 7H), demonstrating that LY294002 could reverse the aggravation of cell pyroptosis induced by miR‐25‐5p inhibitor. These results indicate that miR‐25‐5p protects AT2 from pyroptosis by suppressing the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

By quantifying miR‐25‐5p in BPD lung tissue, we found that LXA4 intervention increased the level of miR‐25‐5p in vivo (Figure 8A). We next investigated the functional role of miR‐25‐5p in vivo. Rats were exposed to 85% hyperoxia from PND 1 to 9, then miR‐25‐5p mimic (miR‐mimic) or negative control (miR‐nc) was administered to each rat via tail vein injection on 1 PND 3, 5, and 7. miR‐25‐5p/PI3K/AKT Pathway Protects Bronchial Epithelium From Pyroptosis

To further verify the role of the miR‐25‐5p/PI3K/AKT pathway in pyroptosis, human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS‐2B) were treated with LPS (1 μg/mL, 5 h) and ATP (5 mM, 2 h). qRT‐PCR revealed significant upregulation of pyroptosis‐related genes (NLRP3, GSDMD, IL‐1β, and IL‐18) in pyroptotic cells compared to controls (Figure S3A). Western blot confirmed elevated protein levels of NLRP3, caspase‐1, and GSDMD‐N (Figure S3B). PI/Hoechst dual staining further demonstrated increased membrane rupture in pyroptotic groups, which was evidenced by a higher proportion of red‐stained cells (Figure S3C). Next, before pyroptosis induction, cells were transfected with miR‐25‐5p nc, miR‐25‐5p mimic, or miR‐25‐5p inhibitor. RT‐qPCR and Western blot analysis revealed that compared with miR‐25‐5p nc transfection, overexpression of miR‐25‐5p significantly reduced IL‐1β and IL‐18 mRNA levels and NLRP3 protein expression (Figure S4A,B). Conversely, miR‐25‐5p inhibitor exacerbated mRNA and protein levels of pyroptosis markers in BEAS‐2B cells (Figure S4A,B). PI/Hoechst staining showed that miR‐25‐5p mimic reduced the number of red‐stained cells, while miR‐25‐5p inhibitor amplified pyroptotic cell populations (Figure S4E). These results indicate that miR‐25‐5p can protect BEAS‐2B cells from pyroptosis to a certain extent.

To further dissect the involvement of the PI3K/AKT pathway, cells were pre‐treated with the PI3K inhibitor LY294002. This intervention suppressed PI3K/AKT phosphorylation in BEAS‐2B cells (Figure S4C,D) and reversed miR‐25‐5p inhibitor‐induced pyroptosis scaling effect by reducing NLRP3, caspase‐1, and GSDMD‐N expression (Figure S4A,B). PI/Hoechst staining also showed that LY294002 reduced red‐stained dead cells caused by the miR‐25‐5p inhibitor (Figure S4E). These results confirm that miR‐25‐5p mitigates pyroptosis in bronchial epithelial cells by attenuating PI3K/AKT signaling.

Exosomal miR‐25‐5p Attenuates Pyroptosis by Targeting NRBP2 in AT2

TargetScan, mirbase, and miRDB were used to predict the target genes of miR‐25‐5p by bioinformatic analyses. Cross‐referencing Targetscan and miRDB databases revealed 13 overlapping candidate genes (Figure 8E), with NRBP2 ranking among the top six predicted targets (Supporting Information Table S4). NRBP2 mRNA was significantly elevated in pyroptotic AT2 cells, but reduced by M2‐exo intervention (Figure 8F). Compared to miR‐25‐5p nc, qRT‐PCR results showed that miR‐25‐5p mimic transfection downregulated NRBP2, CYP17A1, and SP8 mRNA levels (Figure 8G).

NRBP2, a known activator of the PI3K/AKT pathway, is involved in cell autophagy, inhibition of inflammatory response, and tissue repair. As predicted by TargetScan in our study, miR‐25‐5p retains a conserved binding site within the 3′ UTR of NRBP2 (Figure 8H,I). To verify this bioinformatic prediction, we performed a dual luciferase reporter gene assay. The plasmids containing the wild‐type and mutant 3′ UTR of NRBP2 were constructed. Results showed that miR‐25‐5p mimic reduced luciferase activity specifically in the wild‐type NRBP2 3′ UTR plasmid (Figure 8J), indicating NRBP2 as a direct target negatively regulated by miR‐25‐5p. In addition, Western blot showed decreased NRBP2 protein levels after miR‐25‐5p mimic transfection (Figure 8K). These findings demonstrate that M2‐exo delivers miR‐25‐5p to AT2 cells, where it binds NRBP2's 3′ UTR to suppress its expression, ultimately alleviating pyroptosis.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that LXA4 mitigates hyperoxia‐induced lung injury in neonatal rats by promoting M2‐Mφs polarization and suppressing pyroptosis. Since the main pathological basis of BPD is lung epithelial cell injury, and M2‐Mφs have anti‐inflammatory and repair‐promoting effects, we propose the hypothesis that LXA4 protects lung epithelial cells from pyroptosis in BPD through M2‐Mφs. scRNA‐seq revealed dynamic macrophage‐AT2 interactions in BPD, with monocyte‐derived macrophages playing a central role in tissue repair, enriched in chemotaxis, immune response, and wound repair. We also found that LXA4 has the ability to promote macrophage to M2 differentiation in vitro. Given exosomes as crucial intercellular communication tools, we isolated M2‐exo in vitro and demonstrated that it ameliorates pyroptosis in lung epithelial cells and BPD‐induced lung injury. This study confirmed that the interaction between M2‐Mφs and lung epithelial cells is mediated by exosomes. As miRNAs serve as key signaling molecules in exosomes, high‐throughput sequencing revealed the high expression of mir‐25‐5p in M2‐exo. Mechanistically, M2‐exo delivered miR‐25‐5p to AT2 cells, targeting NRBP2 to inhibit PI3K/AKT signaling and reduce pyroptosis (Figure 9). This pathway was further validated in bronchial epithelial cells, where miR‐25‐5p overexpression or PI3K inhibition attenuated inflammatory cell death. These findings conclusively establish mir‐25‐5p as a crucial mediator of anti‐pyroptosis in M2 macrophages.

Schematic representation of the mechanism by which LXA4 induces M2 macrophage polarization and rescues cell pyroptosis by exosomal miR‐25‐5p in BPD.

AS a chronic lung disease predominantly affecting preterm infants, BPD remains a significant clinical challenge despite advancements in neonatal care. The 2018 National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) diagnostic criteria emphasize persistent parenchymal lung disease and oxygen reliance at a postmenstrual age of 36 weeks, highlighting the need for innovative therapies to improve outcomes [48]. Including early respiratory support and surfactant administration, postnatal steroid therapy, and inhaled bronchodilators to improve airway hyperreactivity, current strategies offer limited efficacy [49], prompting exploration of novel approaches like mesenchymal stem cell‐derived extracellular vesicles and lipid mediators such as LXA4.

LXA4, a specific lipoxin with anti‐inflammatory and pro‐resolving dual properties, is produced from arachidonic acid by lipoxygenase and acts during inflammation resolution [50]. Known as the “braking signals” of inflammatory responses, lipoxins are widely used in asthma, myocardial infarction, arthritis, and other diseases [51, 52, 53]. It has been found that LXA4 regulates macrophage M1/M2 polarization, which underlies its anti‐inflammatory effects [54]. In an inflammatory environment, the state transformation of macrophages is the front line for the body to initiate an inflammatory response, which is also a key step for downstream cells to receive anti‐inflammatory signals [55]. Although a prior study discovered that LXA4 decreased lung damage in newborn mice suffering from BPD caused by hyperoxia [56], its mechanism remains unclear. Our study further demonstrates that hyperoxia promotes lung macrophages to tend to M1‐Mφs, while LXA4 drives M2‐Mφs differentiation. These findings reveal a new anti‐inflammatory mechanism of LXA4, providing insights into its drive for the polarization of resting macrophages to M2‐Mφs, which could act as a possible treatment for preterm infants suffering from BPD.

In vitro induction of pyroptosis typically necessitates a dual‐signal process: an initial priming signal and a subsequent trigger. LPS primes NLRP3 inflammasome and pro‐IL‐1β expression, while a secondary stimulus (such as ATP or nigericin) triggers the assembly of multiprotein complexes comprising NLRP3, apoptosis‐associated speck‐like protein (ASC), and pro‐caspase‐1, which further facilitates the formation of pyroptosome and caspase‐1 activation [17]. This LPS‐ATP interplay in pyroptosis underscores the complexity of inflammatory cascades [57]. Almost all types of mammalian cells can release ATP upon stimulation [58], hyperoxia may provoke lung cells to release massive ATP. According to our results, while LPS alone failed to induce pyroptosis, ATP via NLRP3 inflammasome drove cell death, suggesting hyperoxia‐induced ATP release as a key contributor to lung injury.

Cellular communication between structural cells and immune cells is essential for preserving homeostasis of the internal environment [59]. Macrophages–AT2 reciprocity affects the occurrence and development of BPD. AT2 not only serve as gas‐exchange surfaces, but also as natural physical barriers between the lung and blood, protecting the body from the invasion of exogenous pathogens [60]. In addition, AT2 are also involved in immune responses, activating macrophage‐mediated pathogen elimination. Lung macrophages are categorized into two types: tissue resident macrophages and monocyte‐derived macrophages. Inflammatory stimulation causes the reprogramming of circulating monocytes rather than tissue‐resident macrophages for macrophage differentiation [16]. In this study, our scRNA‐seq atlas revealed upregulated AM and AT2 in BPD lung with close mutual connections, providing fresh perspectives into macrophage–epithelial crosstalk. In particular, the IM‐monocytes subset rather than AM dominated AT2 interactions, indicating monocyte‐macrophages as key immune effectors in lung repair.

During the disease process, macrophages‐AT2 crosstalk involves multiple mechanisms, including cytokine communication, extracellular vesicle signal transduction, pulmonary surfactant‐associated protein signal transmission, and cell gap junction channels [61]. In recent years, extracellular vesicles, particularly exosomes, have emerged as central mediators of intercellular signaling, transferring bioactive substances including miRNAs, proteins, and lipids [61, 62] Research emphasizes the important function of exosomes in coordinating lung development and injury resolution, with their cargo dynamically reflecting the functional state of parent cells [63, 64]. Therefore, we investigated macrophage‐derived exosomes in hyperoxia‐induced BPD. Our study found that administration of M2‐exo restored alveolar architecture and suppressed pyroptosis in BPD rats. Complementary in vitro experiments confirmed that M2‐exo attenuated AT2 cell pyroptosis, underscoring its therapeutic potential. These findings align with emerging paradigms that position exosomes as versatile tools for modulating immune responses and epithelial repair in lung diseases.

Emerging evidence highlights exosomal miRNAs as potent modulators of intercellular communication, capable of regulating gene expression in distal cells and serving as therapeutic agents [65]. For instance, Li et al. designed nanomolecules containing 7 key miRNAs to mimic exosomes, which demonstrated efficacy in improving mortality and organ damage in sepsis models as the same as biological exosomes [66]. In this study, we identified miR‐25‐5p as a differentially expressed miRNA in macrophage‐derived exosomes, with notably elevated levels in M2‐exo in comparison to M1‐exo. This expression disparity aligns with the distinct function of M1‐Mφs and M2‐Mφs. Notably, we further verified that M2‐exo transmitted miR‐25‐5p to AT2, suppressing pyroptosis and attenuating lung injury. These findings position macrophage‐derived exosomes not only as carriers of miR‐25‐5p, but also as an ideal target for exploring BPD pathogenesis.

Studies have shown that miRNA sequences are divided into extracellular vesicle–released sequences and cell‐retention sequences, which participate in miRNA retention or secretion. The exosome miRNA profiles contain a common G + C sequence and manipulating their introduction or knockout can change the distribution of miRNAs [67]. In our experiment, the selective enrichment of miR‐25‐5p in M2‐exo was attributed to its rich G + C sequence, which complete sequence is 3′‐GUUAACGGGUUCAGAGGCGGA‐5′. We proposed that LXA4 enhanced miR‐25‐5p packaging into M2‐exo by upregulating exosomal G + C sorting mechanisms, though this requires further investigation.

NRBP2 plays a role in inhibiting autophagy in macrophages and is related to cell proliferation, pyroptosis, and immune evasion. It is found that NRBP2 is downregulated in macrophages with specific miRNAs, protecting against LPS‐induced cell damage [68]. NRBP2 has protein serine/threonine kinase activity and is capable of acting on the PI3K/AKT pathway [69]. miRNAs regulate cellular biological functions by acting on target genes in three ways: (1) directly cleaving target mRNA molecules; (2) inhibiting post‐transcriptional translation; (3) destroying mRNA stability [70, 71]. In this study, miR‐25‐5p binds to the 3′UTR of NRBP2 mRNA, inhibiting its translation and destabilizing the transcript. In pyroptotic cells, NRBP2 expression was elevated, while miR‐25‐5p overexpression or M2‐exo intervention suppressed its levels. Mechanistically, miR‐25‐5p notably suppressed the phosphorylation of PI3K/AKT, thereby reversing cell pyroptosis. In addition, in pyroptotic cells with knocked‐down miR‐25‐5p, the expression of PI3K/AKT was upregulated and pyroptosis was further aggravated. Notably, the centrality of the PI3K/AKT pathway on pyroptosis was further confirmed using LY294002, a PI3K inhibition. In miR‐25‐5p‐deficient cells, LY294002 reversed exacerbated pyroptosis by blocking PI3K phosphorylation. These results establish PI3K/AKT as a critical checkpoint in pyroptosis regulation and position NRBP2 as a linchpin connecting miR‐25‐5p to inflammatory cell death.

A recent study by Lin et al. characterized hyperoxia‐induced senescent lung macrophages in neonatal mice using scRNA‐seq, revealing that the majority of senescent cells were M1‐polarized and alveolar macrophages, exhibiting enhanced expression of genes related to innate immunity, phagocytosis, and glycolytic metabolism [72]. These findings align with our observation of predominant M1 macrophage activation in BPD lungs and underscore the importance of macrophage polarization in disease pathogenesis. Our results extend these insights by demonstrating that LXA4 promotes M2 macrophage polarization and exosomal transfer of miR‐25‐5p, which attenuates pyroptosis via inhibition of NRBP2/PI3K/AKT signaling—a pathway potentially linked to macrophage metabolic reprogramming.

While this study provides critical insights into the role of LXA4 and exosomal miR‐25‐5p in BPD, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our experiments relied on a BPD model in newborn rats caused by hyperoxia but lacked a complementary in vitro BPD cell model under hyperoxic conditions. However, pyroptosis was confirmed in BPD lung tissues, and a separate pyroptotic cell model was employed to validate LXA4 and M2‐exo effects. Second, while miR‐25‐5p was identified as a key mediator in M2‐exo, the potential synergistic contributions of other exosomal cargo (e.g., proteins, lipids, or additional miRNAs) remain unexplored. Future studies should dissect the synergistic action of exosomal content in pyroptosis regulation. Furthermore, exosomes derived from other cell types, such as endothelial cells or platelets, may also influence BPD progression, highlighting the need to map cross‐cellular exosomal communication in BPD. Additionally, this study employed A549 cells as a model for pulmonary epithelium, based on their widespread application and practicality for investigating epithelial pyroptosis and related signaling pathways. However, it is important to note that A549 cells are derived from lung cancer, and their biological behavior may differ from that of pulmonary epithelial cells under physiological conditions, particularly those in the developing lung. Therefore, our findings warrant further validation in models that more closely resemble the physiological context, such as primary pulmonary epithelial cells or organoids, as well as in vivo models. Notably, the key mechanistic insights obtained from A549 cells were consistently recapitulated in both BEAS‐2B normal bronchial epithelial cells and in our rat model of BPD. Future studies should aim to employ more disease‐relevant models to consolidate these conclusions. Finally, NRBP2 knockout or silencing in pyroptosis models was not performed, leaving its precise mechanistic role partially unresolved. Subsequent study should employ genetic or pharmacological NRBP2 inhibition to confirm its centrality in the miR‐25‐5p/PI3K/AKT axis and evaluate its potential as a biomarker for BPD severity. These limitations, however, do not diminish the study's key finding: exosomal miR‐25‐5p serves as a critical anti‐pyroptotic agent in BPD. Addressing these gaps will refine therapeutic strategies targeting macrophage‐epithelial crosstalk in neonatal lung injury.

Conclusions

This study identifies LXA4 as a potent modulator of macrophage polarization and exosomal signaling in BPD. By delivering miR‐25‐5p via M2‐exo, LXA4 suppresses NRBP2‐dependent PI3K/AKT activation, thereby attenuating pyroptosis and preserving alveolar integrity. These findings unveil a novel macrophage–AT2 interaction axis and propose exosome‐based therapies as a promising strategy for neonatal lung injury. Targeting this pathway is expected to provide new targets and strategies for the precise clinical treatment of BPD in preterm infants.
