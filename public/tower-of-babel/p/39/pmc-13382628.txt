Integrin-linked kinase promotes hepatic fat accumulation via F-actin stabilization-dependent CD36 plasma membrane localization
Authors: Kakali Ghoshal, Fabian Bock, Nathan C Winn, Richard L Printz, Deanna P Bracy, David H Wasserman, Roy Zent, Ambra Pozzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIncreased hepatic lipid accumulation occurs in high-fat diet (HFD)-induced insulin resistance. Integrin-linked kinase (ILK) contributes to HFD-induced hepatic insulin resistance by increasing hepatic triglyceride content. How ILK promotes HFD-induced hepatic fatty acid accumulation is underexplored. ILK favors the formation of F-actin bundling and is upregulated following HFD. Moreover, the fatty acid transporter CD36 localizes to membrane ruffles rich in F-actin following HFD. Thus, we investigated whether ILK-mediated F-actin stabilization and liver fatty acid uptake are mechanistically linked. HFD-fed mice lacking ILK in hepatocytes have reduced hepatic F-actin abundance, CD36 plasma membrane localization, and intracellular lipid accumulation. ILK-null cells treated with the F-actin stabilizer jasplakinolide increased cortical F-actin polymerization, plasma membrane-associated CD36, and intracellular lipid uptake. CD36 inhibition reduced lipid uptake in jasplakinolide-treated ILK-null cells, confirming that F-actin-induced CD36 plasma membrane localization promotes lipid accumulation. Thus, ILK regulates intracellular lipid transport by linking CD36 to an F-actin-rich cytoskeleton.Keywords: free fatty acids, insulin resistance, high-fat diet, cytoskeleton, transmembrane transporters, hepatocytes

Introduction

Integrin-linked kinase (ILK) is a pseudokinase that serves as an intracellular scaffolding protein that links the cytoplasmic domains of the extracellular matrix receptors, integrins, with cytoskeletal components.1 ILK regulates several cell functions by forming a complex with two other intracellular scaffolding proteins, namely PINCH (particularly interesting new cysteine-histidine-rich protein) and Parvin, thus forming the IPP (ILK-PINCH-Parvin) complex.1 This tightly regulated complex governs key cellular functions such as integrin-mediated cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis.1 In addition to these classical cellular functions, ILK regulates pathological processes such as hepatic insulin resistance. We previously reported that ILK contributes to high-fat diet (HFD)-induced hepatic insulin resistance.2 Selective deletion of ILK in hepatocytes improves insulin action in HFD-fed mice with increased hepatic mitochondrial glucose oxidation and decreased hepatic triglyceride content.2 However, how ILK promotes hepatic fatty acid accumulation in a setting of HFD is not known.

ILK controls cytoskeletal dynamics by regulating the balance between filamentous (F) and globular (G) actin. It promotes the formation of F-actin bundling by interacting with PINCH and Parvin.3 Consistent with this finding, deletion or pharmacological inhibition of ILK in cells leads to less organized F-actin fibers4 and reduced cytoskeletal rearrangement.5

Diet-induced obesity disrupts the cytoskeleton, increases F-actin polymerization, and alters the remodeling of liver and adipose tissues.6,7 HFD also leads to increased cholesterol plasma membrane levels, with concomitant decreased cortical F-actin and impaired insulin-mediated glucose transport in skeletal muscle.8 The mechanistic links between F-actin disruption and liver fatty acid uptake are undefined.

F-actin regulates the localization and activity of plasma membrane-associated transporters. In skeletal muscle, the F-actin network is required to promote the recruitment of the glucose transporter GLUT4 to the plasma membrane, thus facilitating insulin-mediated glucose intracellular transport.9 Two major fatty acid transmembrane transporters in the liver are CD36 and FATP2, and their expression, plasma membrane localization, and activity are upregulated in the setting of an HFD and insulin resistance.10,11 Based on the findings that ILK expression is upregulated in the setting of HFD,12 that it controls F-actin assembly, and that CD36 localizes to membrane ruffles rich in polymerized F-actin,13 we investigated whether ILK promotes HFD-mediated hepatic fatty acid accumulation by promoting F-actin polymerization and in turn CD36 plasma membrane localization.

Results

Reduced hepatic lipid accumulation and fibrosis in hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice

We showed that mice lacking ILK in hepatocytes are protected against hepatic insulin resistance in a model of diet-induced obesity.2 Because insulin resistance leads to fatty liver14 and hepatic fibrosis,15 we evaluated the morphology and collagen content of livers of wild-type (WT) and hepILK-knockout (KO) HFD-fed mice (12 weeks on HFD starting at 6 weeks of age). H&E and oil red O staining revealed a significant increase in hepatic lipid vacuoles and lipid accumulation in WT HFD-fed mice compared to WT standard chow diet (SCD)-fed mice (Figures 1A–1C). In contrast, hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice showed reduced hepatic lipid accumulation with lipid content similar to that of hepILK-KO SCD-fed mice (Figures 1A–1C). Consistent with this result, picrosirius red staining revealed a significant reduction in collagen deposition in the livers of hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice compared to control mice, suggesting reduced fibrosis in the former group (Figures 1D and 1E). Hepatic lipid influx was measured by C1-BODIPY uptake by isolated primary hepatocytes and found to be decreased in hepILK-KO vs. control hepatocytes isolated from HFD-fed mice (Figure 1F).

To validate these histological findings, we performed a biochemical analysis of hepatic glycerol-phospholipids (Figure 2). Total hepatic triglyceride and diglyceride concentrations were significantly increased in control HFD-fed mice compared to control SCD-fed mice or hepILK-KO HFD mice (Figures 2A and 2B). Total hepatic diglyceride levels were similar between hepILK-KO SCD-fed and HFD-fed mice (Figure 2B). By contrast, hepatic triglycerides were increased in hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice compared to hepILK-KO SCD-fed mice, although this increase was less than that seen in control HFD-fed mice (Figure 2A). Total phospholipid levels were decreased in hepILK-KO SCD-fed compared to control SCD-fed mice (Figure 2C). By contrast, the reduction in phospholipids in control HFD-fed mice was greater than that detected in hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice (Figure 2C).

We next determined indices of fatty acid elongation (18:0/16:0) and saturation (18:1ω9/18:0) within glyceride and phospholipid species. The fatty acid elongase index decreased in triglyceride and diglyceride fractions and increased in phospholipids of HFD-fed control mice (Figures 2D–2F). There were no differences in the fatty acid elongation index in the triglyceride fraction of livers from SCD-fed hepILK-KO mice when compared to control mice (Figure 2D); however, it was still significantly reduced in the triglyceride fraction of HFD-fed control mice (Figure 2D). The diglyceride fraction in the control SCD-fed mice was higher than that of hepILK-KO mice on the same diet (Figure 2D). The diglyceride fraction decreased in both HFD-fed control and hepILK-KO mice; however, it was significantly decreased in the control HFD-fed mice (Figure 2E). Although the elongase index in the phospholipid fraction was improved in hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice, it was significantly lower than that detected in control HFD-fed mice (Figure 2F).

We found that the desaturation index was increased in HFD-fed control mice and was attenuated in hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice in the triglyceride fractions, with no differences between SCD groups (Figure 2G). In the diglycerides, the desaturation index was also increased by HFD with a non-significant reduction in HFD-fed hepILK-KO mice (Figure 2H). A small, although significant, increase in the desaturation index in phospholipids manifested in SCD hepILK-KO mice compared to control mice (Figure 2I). The reduction in the desaturation index caused by HFD was improved in hepILK-KO mice, but this did not reach statistical significance compared to control HFD-fed mice (Figure 2I). Collectively, these data show that ILK worsens the hepatic lipid profile primarily in diet-induced obesity.

Reduced CD36 plasma membrane localization in the livers of hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice

CD36 and FATP2 regulate intracellular transport of long-chain fatty acids into hepatocytes.10,11 Western blot analysis of hepatic FATP2 revealed comparable levels of this transporter in liver lysates and plasma membrane-rich fractions of control and hepILK-KO SCD-fed mice and remained unchanged following 12 weeks of HFD (Figures 3A, 3C, 3D, and 3F). By contrast, CD36 was not detected in liver lysates or plasma membrane-rich fractions of control and hepILK-KO SCD-fed mice (Figures 3A and 3B). HFD equally increased CD36 levels in liver lysates of control and hepILK-KO mice (Figures 3A and 3B). However, plasma membrane localization of CD36 was detected only in livers of control but not hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice (Figures 3D and 3E). To further corroborate this finding, we co-stained liver sections of HFD-fed mice with anti-ILK and anti-CD36 antibodies or anti-CD36 and anti-Na/K ATPase (a plasma membrane-associated protein) antibodies. Cell junction colocalization of ILK and CD36 and plasma membrane colocalization of CD36 and Na/K ATPase were detected in livers of control HFD-fed mice (Figures 3G and 3H). By contrast, intracellular staining of CD36 was detected in the livers of hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice (Figures 3G and 3H), confirming that loss of hepatic ILK results in reduced plasma membrane-localized CD36.

Reduced hepatic F-actin abundance in hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice

To determine how loss of ILK in hepatocytes leads to reduced CD36 plasma membrane localization following HFD, we investigated the actin cytoskeleton of livers from SCD- and HFD-fed mice. The rationale for this is that (1) HFD triggers F-actin remodeling at the cell cortex in liver and adipose tissues, underscoring a critical role for cytoskeletal reorganization in metabolic adaptation7,16; (2) the actin cytoskeleton regulates membrane protein dynamics17; (3) ILK triggers F-actin filament bundling18; and (4) in macrophages, CD36 membrane motion and clustering are dependent on the cortical actomyosin meshwork.19

Hepatic cortical F-actin intensity, visualized by staining liver sections with phalloidin, was significantly lower in hepILK-KO SCD-fed mice compared to control mice on the same diet (Figures 4A and 4B). HFD resulted in a significant increase in hepatic cortical F-actin in control mice, while it was blunted in hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice (Figures 4A and 4B).

Next, we investigated whether CD36 colocalized with the F-actin network in livers of HFD-fed mice. Double staining of liver sections with phalloidin and anti-CD36 antibody showed that CD36 colocalized with the F-actin network at the plasma membrane junctions in livers of HFD-fed control mice (Figure 4C). In contrast, the F-actin network was disrupted, leading to a loss of CD36 colocalization and intracellular accumulation of this transporter in the livers of HFD-fed hepILK-KO mice (Figure 4C). Moreover, the absolute intensity of plasma membrane junctions, indicative of F-actin and CD36 colocalization, was significantly higher in livers of HFD-fed control compared to hepILK-KO mice (Figures 4D and 4E).

Based on these findings, we assessed the G-to-F-actin ratio in the livers of these mice by super-resolution confocal ratiometric imaging. In both normal and HFD-fed mice, the loss of ILK increased the G/F-ratio, which was accompanied by decreased cortical F-actin intensity (Figures 5A–5C). Thus, these results confirm that ILK is required to stabilize cortical F-actin. To further confirm the microscopy results, we quantified the G- and F-actin content in liver homogenates using the G-actin/F-actin in vivo assay kit. A similar G-to-F-actin ratio was detected in liver homogenates from WT and hepILK-KO SCD-fed mice (Figures 5C and 5D). In contrast, the G-to-F-actin ratio increased significantly in hepILK-KO HFD-fed mice compared to their WT counterparts, indicating attenuated G-to-F-actin polymerization and remodeling in response to HFD in the absence of ILK (Figures 5C and 5D). In summary, these data indicate that ILK promotes hepatic cortical F-actin stability.

Increased F-actin facilitates CD36 plasma membrane localization

CD36-dependent lipid transport is linked to the actin cytoskeleton.19 Thus, we investigated whether the loss of cortical F-actin contributes to reduced CD36-mediated lipid uptake in ILK-deficient cells. To do this, we first analyzed basal CD36 levels in mouse kidney collecting duct (CD) epithelial cells that express (ILKfl/fl) or lack (ILK-KO) ILK expression (Figures 6A and 6B).20 The rationale for selecting these cells is that livers from SCD-fed mice do not express detectable total or plasma membrane-associated CD36 levels (Figures 3A, 3D, and 3G). Western blot analysis of ILKfl/fl (WT) and ILK-KO CD cells showed comparable total CD36 levels (Figures 6A–6C). However, plasma membrane-rich fractions showed detectable CD36 levels only in WT but not ILK-KO CD cells (Figures 6D and 6E).

To further confirm the role of F-actin in CD36 plasma membrane localization, cortical F-actin was hyperstabilized in ILK-KO CD cells using jasplakinolide. Treatment with jasplakinolide (100 nM for 1 h) significantly increased plasma membrane-associated CD36 in ILK-KO CD cells compared to vehicle-treated ILK-KO CD cells (Figures 6E and 6F).

Immunofluorescence analysis of CD cells co-stained with anti-ILK and anti-CD36 antibodies and phalloidin revealed loss of F-actin polymerization and plasma membrane-associated CD36 in ILK-KO CD cells compared to WT cells (Figure 6G). Treatment with jasplakinolide increased both cortical F-actin polymerization and plasma-membrane distribution of CD36 in ILK-KO CD cells (Figure 6G), further elucidating the role of ILK in F-actin distribution and subsequent CD36 localization to the plasma membrane. Consistent with this finding, the absolute intensity of plasma membrane junctions evaluated for ILK/CD36, ILK/F-actin, or CD36/F-actin colocalization was significantly higher in WT and jasplakinolide-treated ILK-KO cells compared to vehicle-treated ILK-KO cells (Figures 6H–6J).

CD36 inhibition leads to reduced lipid uptake in vitro

We next performed BODIPY assays to determine whether jasplakinolide-mediated increases in CD36 plasma membrane localization led to increased lipid uptake in ILK-KO CD cells. Lipid uptake was significantly lower in vehicle-treated ILK-KO CD cells compared to WT cells (Figure 6K). However, treatment with jasplakinolide significantly enhanced lipid uptake in ILK-KO CD cells compared to vehicle-treated ILK-KO cells (Figure 6K). Notably, lipid uptake in jasplakinolide-treated ILK-KO cells was comparable to that of vehicle-treated WT cells (Figure 6K). The finding that the CD36 inhibitor SMS121 significantly reduced lipid uptake in jasplakinolide-treated ILK-KO cells to levels seen in ILK-KO cells treated with vehicle only (Figure 6K) further suggests that F-actin promotes intracellular lipid accumulation by enhancing CD36 plasma membrane localization.

Discussion

We show in male mice that ILK promotes HFD-induced hepatic lipid accumulation and development of fatty liver, two processes linked to insulin resistance. These effects are mediated by increased hepatic F-actin abundance and plasma membrane localization of the free fatty acid transporter CD36.

The cytoskeleton has emerged as a key regulator of insulin sensitivity. In adipocytes, the development of the F-actin structure potentiates GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane in response to insulin, thus facilitating insulin-mediated intracellular glucose transport.21 HFD or treatment of adipocytes with cytochalasin D (which inhibits actin polymerization) results in an overall increase in the G/F-actin ratio and decreased insulin-mediated GLUT4 plasma membrane location.21 This is likely because reduced F-actin leads to defective trafficking of GLUT4 storage vesicles to the plasma membrane.22 HFD also leads to increased accumulation of plasma membrane cholesterol with consequent reduction in the F-actin structure and GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane in skeletal muscle.8 Insulin resistance stimulates the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway, which promotes cholesterol synthesis.23 Increased levels of intracellular cholesterol lead to membrane cholesterol toxicity and cortical filamentous actin derangement.24 Interestingly, mice exposed to HFD and undergoing exercise showed overall decreased cholesterol levels with preserved skeletal muscle cortical F-actin and insulin-induced GLUT4 plasma membrane localization.8 Thus, these studies suggest that impairment of F-actin formation in adipose tissue or skeletal muscle induces insulin resistance.

In contrast to these studies, we provide evidence that the levels of hepatic cortical F-actin significantly increased in 12-week HFD-fed mice compared to SCD-fed mice. Increased F-actin levels correlate with increased CD36 plasma membrane localization and enhanced intracellular fatty acid accumulation. Our data are consistent with the finding that primary adipocytes isolated from 2-week HFD-fed mice are associated with an increase in F-actin polymerization and impaired insulin response.6 Our results are also in agreement with the observation that cortical F-actin mediates the plasma membrane localization of transporters, including GLUT4.25

We found that F-actin polymerization primarily affects CD36 but not FATP2 or Na/K ATPase plasma membrane localization. This selectivity could be due to how these proteins are intracellularly stored and delivered to the plasma membrane. FATP2 is primarily localized to the endoplasmic reticulum/peroxisomes,11 and Na/K ATPase reaches the membrane mainly through constitutive secretory pathways that are relatively actin independent.26 Interestingly, CD36 is stored in intracellular endosomal-type vesicles (a distinct recycling/endosome pool) and in caveolae-derived/vesicular carriers (VAMP2-positive transport vesicles) that move to the plasma membrane upon stimuli such as insulin, cell contraction, and fatty acid overload.27,28,29 Thus, it is conceivable that an F-actin-rich milieu primarily facilitates the transport of these specialized CD36 vesicles to the plasma membrane, facilitating its delivery to the cell surface. This hypothesis is supported by our evidence that treatment of cells with the F-actin stabilizer jasplakinolide increases CD36 plasma membrane localization and fatty acid uptake.

ILK, a key regulator of the actin network, can also contribute to insulin resistance. However, its effects on metabolic regulation are tissue and cell specific. Global depletion of ILK in adult mice shows baseline increased glycemia and insulinemia due to reduced GLUT4 expression in insulin-sensitive peripheral tissue.30 In addition, short-term administration of HFD to mice with a global transgenic downregulation of ILK expression exacerbated the expression of markers related to imbalanced metabolism such as white adipose tissue lipolysis (HSL), hepatic gluconeogenesis (PCK-1), and glycerol transport (AQP9).31 Moreover, anti-coagulant medications have the potential to restore the function of white adipose tissue by increasing ILK expression and decreasing FABP4 and CD36 expression.32 In contrast to these studies, we reported that ILK is necessary for the development of HFD-mediated insulin resistance both in liver and skeletal muscle.2,33 In muscle, loss of ILK resulted in increased insulin-mediated AKT phosphorylation and increased capillarization due to decreased activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK).33 In the liver, loss of ILK prevented HFD-mediated insulin resistance by enhancing mitochondrial glucose oxidation, and decreasing hepatic lipid accumulation.2 In the present study, we show that ILK contributes to HFD-mediated hepatic lipid accumulation by enhancing CD36 plasma membrane localization via F-actin stabilization. Consistent with a detrimental role of ILK in insulin resistance, deletion of ILK in adipocytes improved metabolic regulation in obese, insulin-resistant mice by enhancing the anti-lipolytic action of insulin and increasing insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in brown adipose tissue.12 Interestingly, loss of ILK in adipocytes reduced fat mass in HFD-fed mice, most likely due to overall decreased free fatty acid uptake into adipocytes via the downregulation of CD36 gene expression.12

ILK-mediated gene regulation and/or localization of transporters is tissue and cell type dependent. To this end, ILK promotes translocation of TRPV4 to the plasma membrane by activating AKT signaling and, in turn, clathrin- and dynamin-mediated exocytosis.34 ILK regulates both expression and localization of AQP2, and inhibition of ILK in porcine kidney epithelial cells increased F-actin polymerization, thus leading to perinuclear localization of AQP2 and overall decreased plasma membrane-associated AQP2.35 Interestingly, we show that deletion of ILK in hepatocytes or kidney CD cells also prevents the membrane translocation of CD36 by reducing, rather than increasing, F-actin polymerization.

Our hepatocyte findings show that ILK primarily governs CD36 plasma membrane trafficking without altering total CD36 expression in HFD-fed mice, which contrasts with the evidence that ILK affects both the transcription and trafficking of several transporters in non-hepatic cells and short-term HFD models.31,32,36 A possible explanation for these divergent results likely stems from the tissue-specific action of ILK and/or experimental models (e.g., HFD duration or composition, as well as in vitro vs. in vivo studies).

In addition to the cytoskeleton, another key regulator of CD36 expression or localization is AMPK.37 AMPK activation in Chinese hamster ovary cells directly increases CD36 translocation to the plasma membrane,38 while in cardiac myocytes prolonged AMPK activation leads to increased CD36 expression and intracellular fatty acid transport.39 Increased fatty acid accumulation, observed in the setting of insulin resistance, promotes the phosphorylation/activation of AMPKα at Thr172 to enhance lipid oxidation,40 thus enhancing CD36 plasma membrane translocation and paradoxically enhancing intracellular lipid accumulation. Interestingly, we found increased phosphorylation of AMPKα at Thr172 in the liver of control HFD-fed mice compared to hepILK-KO mice, consistent with increased hepatic lipid accumulation and impaired insulin signaling in mice expressing ILK fed an HFD (Figure S1). Thus, ILK deletion may prevent intracellular lipid accumulation by reducing CD36 plasma membrane localization through decreasing the pool of F-actin and AMPKα Thr172 phosphorylation.

In conclusion, we provide evidence that ILK promotes HFD-mediated lipid accumulation by primarily regulating F-actin levels and, in turn, CD36 plasma membrane localization in the liver. Thus, we suggest that selective inhibition of hepatic ILK can be viewed as a potentially beneficial maneuver to reduce unwanted lipid accumulation.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we used primarily adult male mice. The rationale for this choice is that estrogens are linked to insulin sensitivity41 and female mice require ovariectomy to manifest insulin resistance.42 In addition, estrogens induce cytoskeleton reorganization43 and downregulate CD36 expression.44 Although we recognize this is a limitation as only one sex was analyzed, we sought to analyze the role of ILK in hepatic lipid accumulation in the most robust and reliable model.

Although ILK deletion in hepatocytes showed protection from the development of fatty liver following HFD, a major challenge is to target ILK in the setting of HFD-induced insulin resistance. Given the ubiquitous distribution of ILK, hepatocyte-specific targeting of this scaffolding protein needs to be achieved. In addition, the generation of selective ILK inhibitors could represent a challenge. The first generation of ILK inhibitors included ATP-competitive inhibitors, based on the assumption that ILK was thought to be a serine/threonine kinase. Since the realization that ILK is a pseudokinase, small molecules that target its pseudo-active site, thus affecting its scaffolding properties, have been generated. However, their use has limitations including selectivity, toxicity, and insufficient information regarding the specific molecular signatures that predict sensitivity to available ILK inhibitors. Thus, more effort is required before this pseudokinase can be selectively targeted in humans.

Experimental models and study participants details

Mouse models

We deleted ILK in hepatocytes by crossing C57BL/6J ILKfl/fl mice with Albumin (Alb) Cre mice (both available at our institution). F1 generation was used to generate Alb-Cre;ILKfl/fl and control littermates (e.g., ILKfl/fl). The animal protocol for this study (M2000076-00) was approved by the Vanderbilt University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and were conducted in facilities accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. Male mice were initially fed a standard chow diet (SCD, ∼14% fat, Purina Laboratory Rodent 5001) until they reached 6 weeks of age. At this point, the mice were divided into two groups. One group was transitioned to a high-fat diet (HFD, 60% calories from fat, BioServ F3282) while the other group remained on SCD. Twelve weeks later, the mice were sacrificed. Male mice were used because estrogens are linked to insulin sensitivity,41 and female mice require ovariectomy to manifest insulin resistance.42

Cell lines and cell culture

Mouse kidney collecting duct (CD) cells were isolated from females and/or male ILKfl/fl mice, and ILK deletion was achieved by infecting the cells with an adenocre virus. ILKfl/fl (WT) and ILK depleted (ILK-KO) CD cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin (Corning®, 30-002-CI) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, R&D Systems, S11150). Mouse renal proximal tubular secondary cell line TKPTS (ATCC, CRL-3361) and human HepG2 cells (ATCC, HB-8065) were cultured under the same conditions. All cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2 incubators. Sex of TKPTS and HepG2 cells is not available. All cells have been authenticated for expression of key makers such as aquaporin-2 (for CD cells), aquaporin-1 (for TKPTS cells) and albumin production (for HepG2 cells). Cells were not tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

Histopathological examination and lipid accumulation analysis

Livers were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E, Epredia, 7211 & 22-110-637) for evaluation of tissue structure. To visualize neutral lipid droplets, freshly isolated livers were fixed in 10% formalin (Sigma-Aldrich, HT501128-4L) and then incubated with 30% sucrose in PBS (MP Biomedicals, 821713). Ten-micrometer liver sections were stained with Oil Red O (Sigma-Aldrich, O0625) according to the manufacturer's instructions (Sigma-Aldrich, GHS316). Fibrillar collagen deposition was assessed by staining paraffin sections with picrosirius red (Sigma-Aldrich, 365548). For microscopic analysis, bright-field images (40x magnification) of Oil Red O and Picrosirius red-stained liver sections were captured using an Olympus DP72 microscope equipped with Cellsens Standard software. For quantitative analysis, five images per liver were analyzed, and the percentage of liver area stained red in the microscopic field was quantified using ImageJ software.

Characterization of hepatic lipids

Hepatic lipids were extracted by homogenization (50 mg liver) in 2:1 chloroform-methanol mixture. Individual lipid classes were separated by thin layer chromatography using Silica Gel 60 A plates developed in petroleum ether, ethyl ether, acetic acid (80:20:1) and visualized by rhodamine 6G. Phospholipids, diglycerides, and triglycerides were scraped from the plates and methylated using boron trifluoride-methanol. Gas chromatographic analyses were performed on an Agilent 7890A gas chromatograph equipped with flame ionization detectors and a capillary column (SP2380, 0.25 mm x 30 m, 0.20 μm film, Supelco, Bellefonte, PA). Helium was used as the carrier gas. The oven temperature was programmed from 160°C to 230 °C at 4 °C/min. Fatty acid methyl esters were identified by comparing the retention times to those of known standards. Inclusion of lipid standards with odd chain fatty acids permits quantitation of the amount of lipid in the sample. Dipentadecanoyl phosphatidylcholine (C15:0), diheptadecanoin (C17:0), trieicosenoin (C20:1), and cholesteryl eicosenoate (C20:1) are used as standards. Fatty acyl chain desaturation indexes were determined from the palmitoleate (C16:1)-to-palmitate (C16:0) ratio and the oleate (C18:1)-to-stearate (C18:0) ratio. The fatty acyl chain elongation index was calculated as [(C18:0 + C18:1)/C16:0].

BODIPY assay

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from 5h-fasted SCD and HFD anesthetized mice as follows. A 24 Gauge catheter, connected to a peristaltic pump, was inserted into the inferior vena cava which was then infused with warm PBS. The portal vein was cut thus allowing PBS to properly flush the liver. After 5 min, the inferior vena cava was infused with digestion buffer consisting of 50 ml PBS, 100 μl 3.38M CaCl2, 20 mg collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, # C5138). After 10 min, the liver was removed, chopped, and cells were released into cold DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and Penicillin-Streptomycin. Cells were counted and aliquoted at 50,000 cells in suspension per tube. Fifty microliters of 20 μM Bodipy-BSA (C1-BODIPY 500/510 C1, C12; Thermo Fisher, D3823) were added per tube, and the cells were incubated for 2 min in the dark. The cells were then washed with ice-cold stop solution (0.1% BSA in PBS containing Ca2+ and Mg2+, Sigma 14040182), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and resuspended in PBS. The cells were loaded into black 96-well plates (Corning, 3603) and analyzed using a SpectraMax iD3 fluorimeter (excitation = 500-650 nm, emission = 510-665 nm). Cell-free wells served as blanks. After analysis, the cells were lysed for protein concentration, and lipid uptake was expressed as RFU/μg of protein.

In some experiments, 12-h serum starved adherent murine CD cells were treated with either vehicle (0.01% DMSO, Sigma Aldrich, D2650), the actin stabilizer Jasplakinolide (100 nM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, J7473) with or without the CD36 inhibitor SMS121 (200 μM; MedChemExpress, HY-163541).45 After 1 hr, lipid uptake was evaluated as described above.

Evaluation of hepatic ILK, CD36 and FATP2 expression and plasma membrane localization

Lysates from liver tissues (50 mg) or cells were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer (Sigma Aldrich, R0278-50ML) supplemented with cOmplete EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor (Roche, 04693159001), phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Sigma-Aldrich, P5726, P0044), and 2 mmol/L Na3VO4 (Sigma-Aldrich, S6508). For Western blot analysis, 50 μg of the lysates (tissue) and 75 μg (cell lysates) were separated by 8% (v/v) SDS-PAGE and transferred to 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes. β-actin served as the loading control.

Plasma membrane-rich fractions were prepared and validated as follows. Liver tissues or cells were incubated in Buffer A (50 mmol/L Tris·HCl [pH 8.0], 0.5 mmol/L dithiothreitol, 10 mmol/L NaF, 2 mmol/L Na3VO4, 0.1% [v/v] NP-40, and protease inhibitors), gently homogenized, passed through a 25-gauge needle, and centrifuged for 1 min at 200g. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged for 10 min at 800g and supernatant (A) and pellet (B) were collected. Supernatant A was labeled as “cytosol-rich fraction.” Pellet B was resuspended in buffer B (50 mmol/L Tris·HCl [pH 8.0], 0.5 mmol/L dithiothreitol, 10 mmol/L NaF, 2 mmol/L Na3VO4, 1% [v/v] NP-40, and protease inhibitors) and then centrifuged for 20 min at 12,000g, and the new supernatant was saved as “plasma membrane-rich fraction.” For Western blot analyses, 50 μg of plasma-membrane-rich fractions were separated by 8% (v/v) SDS-PAGE and transferred to 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes. Na/K-ATPase (a plasma membrane associated protein) and α-tubulin (a cytoplasmic associated protein) were used to verify fraction purity.

Immunofluorescence

For ILK, CD36, F-actin, and Na/K ATPase localization, liver tissues were fixed by perfusing euthanized mice with 20 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 15714) via the inferior vena mice cava, followed by immersion in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 12 h. The fixed tissues were then immersed in 30% sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich, S5016) in PBS at 4°C for 24 h. Subsequently, the tissues were embedded in OCT compound (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23-730-571) and frozen. Serum-starved WT and ILK-KO CD cells (2 x 105 cells per well), plated in multiwell 4 chamber slides (Millipore; PEZG S0416), were fixed and permeabilized for 1 h with 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.3% Tween-20 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, BP337-500) in PBS.

Liver frozen sections (15 μm) from HFD fed mice or permeabilized cells were co-incubated overnight with anti-ILK (Mouse monoclonal, Sigma-Aldrich, #I0783) and anti-CD36 (Rabbit monoclonal, Abcam, #ab255331) followed by incubation with anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 555 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, # A-21422), anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, # A-11008) secondary antibodies, 647-Phalloidin to visualize F-actin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A22287) and DAPI diluted in blocking buffer. In some experiments, liver frozen sections were incubated overnight with anti-CD36 and anti-Na/K ATPase (Mouse monoclonal, DSHB, #a5), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 594 (CD36) and Alexa Fluor 488 (Na/K ATPase) secondary antibodies and DAPI (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #4083). The sections or cells were washed twice with PBS, airdried and mounted (ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant, Invitrogen, # P36930) for subsequent imaging. To reduce endogenous mouse IgG staining, the M.O.M. (Mouse on Mouse) Immunodetection Kit (Vectorlabs, #FMK-2201) was used. Images were collected using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope with an inverted Axio Observer 7 and Airyscan 2 detector at super-resolution. A 20x/0.80 NA Plan Apochromat objective was used. Experimental groups were imaged using identical protocols to ensure comparability and technical consistency. Images were subsequently exported to Fiji/ImageJ for adjustments of brightness and contrast, as well as background correction. Regions of interest (ROIs) of uniform size were drawn over the cell-cell plasma membrane junctions for all groups and images to specifically focus on the area of cortical actin or plasma membrane-associated proteins (e.g., ILK and CD36, ILK and F-actin, or CDK36 and Factin) and a plot profile was generated. The mean gray value was then collected for analysis. The absolute intensity at the peak area served as a quantitative measure of the intensity of membrane proteins or cortical actin at the cell junctional area. A total of five images per liver with a total of 3 livers per genotype or 4 images of cells from five independent experiments were analyzed, and the mean intensity was plotted as mean ± SD.

F actin and G/F-actin staining

30 μm thick liver frozen sections were incubated in blocking buffer (2% BSA, 0.2% Triton X-100, 0.2% Tween 20 in PBS) at room temperature. After 30 min, the sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with Rhodamine-Phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #R415) and DAPI, both diluted 1:100 in blocking buffer. The sections were washed twice with PBS, airdried and mounted for subsequent imaging. Images were collected with confocal microscopy at super-resolution using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope equipped with an inverted Axio Observer 7 and Airyscan 2 detector. The objective used was a 63x/1.4 numerical aperture (NA) Plan Apochromat oil. Airyscan super-resolution images were acquired under identical settings for all groups and images. Images were then exported into Fiji/ImageJ for brightness/contrast adjustments and background correction. Regions of interest (ROIs) of identical size for all groups and images were then drawn over the F-actin of cell-cell junctions (“cortical actin’’) and the mean gray value was collected. To evaluate G-actin labeling and ratiometric G/F-actin, formaldehyde fixed frozen sections were permeabilized using ice-cold acetone and subsequently incubated with the antibody JLA-20 (Sigma CP01, 1:50). This antibody recognizes all forms for actin, including a preference for G-actin when acetone-based permeabilization on fresh tissue is used.46,47 Fluorescence imaging was performed on thin frozen tissue sections at the surface layer, where signal intensity was highest, rather than acquiring a 3D imaging stack or performing optical sectioning. Both experimental groups were imaged using identical procedures to ensure comparability and technical consistency. Images were acquired as described above and processed with ImageJ’s image calculator. G-actin images were divided by F-actin images and the ratio was visualized on a rainbow look up table. Ratios were visualized as a color-coded map using ImageJ's rainbow color scale feature. Average ratios were quantified for the ROI.

F-actin stabilization

12 h serum starved WT and ILK-KO CD cells (6x106 cells/10cm dish) were treated either with vehicle (0.01% DMSO) or the actin stabilizer Jasplakinolide (100nM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, J7473). After 1h, plasma membrane rich fractions (75 μg) were analyzed by Western blot for CD36 localization.

Evaluation of hepatic G and F-actin content

The G- and F-actin content in liver samples was assessed using the G-actin/F-actin In Vivo Assay Kit (Cytoskeleton Inc., BK037). Briefly, 100 mg of frozen liver tissue was homogenized and lysed in a detergent-based lysis buffer (provided with the kit), which stabilizes and maintains the G- and F-actin conformation. The buffer solubilizes G-actin while leaving F-actin insoluble. Following ultracentrifugation (100,000 x g, 1 h, 37°C, Optima TL with TLA 100 rotor), F-actin was pelleted, whereas G-actin remained in the supernatant. F-actin depolymerization buffer (100 μl) was added to the pellets and incubated on ice. After 1 h, equal volumes of supernatant and pellet fractions were analyzed by Western blot to quantify actin levels. Actin signal was normalized to the total protein content, as quantified by Ponceau S staining (Sigma-Aldrich, P7170).

Insulin-mediated signaling

To assess insulin sensitivity in vivo, liver tissues collected from 5-hour–fasted mice fed HFD were lysed and 50 μg of total liver lysates were analyzed by Western blot for levels of total and activated Insulin Receptor-β, AKT, FOX1 and AMPK.

Western blot quantification

Protein bands were visualized using the Odyssey CLx imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences), and their intensities were quantified through densitometric analysis with Image StuHFD Lite software (LI-COR Biosciences). The results are expressed as ratios of total protein to loading control.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed unpaired t-test. Statistical comparisons for three or more groups were performed using one-way and/or two-way (two factors diet x genotype) analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by multiple comparison tests. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis and graph preparation were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Statistical details of the experiments (including the statistical tests used, exact number of samples analyzed, and experimental repeats) can be found in the figure legends.
