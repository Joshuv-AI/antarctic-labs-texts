Synergistic effect of cattle bone and rice husk ash on mechanical and microstructural properties of cement mortar
Authors: Bahiru Bewket Mitikie, Teshome Anteneh Wubetie, Walied A Elsaigh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe production of cement is growing every year due to its higher consumption in the construction industries. Several studies have been carried out that focus on the possibility of alternative cementing materials such as Industrial and agricultural wastes. Rice husk is one of such agricultural residues and cattle bone is animal waste that are available but didn’t get much attention as alternative cementing material. This study aimed to investigate the effect of partial replacement of cement by combining cattle bone ash and rice husk ash mix in cement mortar at 0%, 5% 10% 15% and 20% by volume of cement. The workability test, compressive strength, Ultrasonic Pulse Velocity, water absorption, and sulfate attack tests were conducted for different curing ages (3, 7, 28, 56, and 90 day). In addition, the morphology scanning electron microscopy (SEM), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), differential thermal analysis (DTA), fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area test, complete silicate analysis test was conducted. Compressive strength increases with an increment of from 0 to 10% replacement at 28 days of curing from 31.5 to 45.9 MPa and slightly decreased beyond 15–20% replacement with 39.9 MPa and 30.6 MPa. The result revealed that the mechanical performance of mortar indicated enhancement of calcium silicate hydrate gel, thus reduce the porosity of cement matric. Under scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) image, mortar sample possessed a dense matrix with a widespread presence of calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gels.Keywords: Mechanical properties, Bone ash, Rice husk ash, Durability, MicrostructureSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Cement production continues to rise each year due to its extensive use in the construction industry. It is one of the most widely utilized materials in the sector1. The manufacturing process of cement is highly energy-intensive and significantly contributes to CO2 emissions. It is estimated that cement production accounts for approximately 5–7% of the total carbon dioxide emissions generated by human activities2. Studies indicate that producing one ton of cement results in nearly one ton of CO2 emissions3, with nearly half of these emissions originating from the chemical process involved in clinker production. The overall impact of cement manufacturing leads to the release of millions of tons of CO2 into the environment4,5. Cement, is frequently excessively expensive, in part because of the extremely high energy consumption involved in its production6,7.

To mitigate the economic and environmental challenges associated with clinker production, it is essential to explore alternative and locally available materials, such as red clay, ceramic waste, coffee husk, cattle bone, and rice husk, as potential cement replacements. Additionally, to address the increasing demand for new construction, certain countries may adopt concrete as a primary building material8. Bone powder ash is produced by burning cattle bones at a temperature of 900 °C. Prior to burning, the bones are thoroughly cleaned, sun-dried, and then oven-dried to reduce their oil content. After combustion, the burnt bones are allowed to cool before being finely ground using a hammer mill9. When bones are burned at 600 °C in a furnace, they lose their carbon content. However, at 900 °C, the ash undergoes complete carbon loss and acquires calcined properties10.

Compared to conventional cement, both rice husk ash and cattle bone ash can be transformed into cementitious materials with lower energy consumption and reduced CO2 emissions. Additionally, these materials have been shown to enhance the properties of concrete10. This study explores the combined effects of rice husk ash and cattle bone ash as partial replacements for cement in both mortars. Due to their high silica and calcium content when subjected to specific heating conditions, these materials exhibit promising pozzolanic properties. Given the abundant availability of cattle bone and rice husk in the region, this research focuses on evaluating the potential of cattle bone ash as a supplementary cementitious material and assessing its performance when combined with rice husk ash to develop a more cost-effective and sustainable alternative to conventional cement.

The particle size distribution (PSD) of rice husk ash (RHA) plays a critical role in determining the mechanical strength and durability of mortar. Finer RHA particles, with a well-graded and narrow PSD, enhance the pozzolanic reaction due to their higher surface area, leading to more efficient consumption of calcium hydroxide and improved formation of calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H). This results in denser microstructure, increased compressive strength, and reduced permeability. Conversely, coarser RHA particles with broader PSD may act as fillers but contribute less to pozzolanic activity, potentially weakening the mortar matrix and lowering durability. Therefore, optimizing PSD through controlled grinding or processing of RHA is essential for achieving desirable concrete performance11. Both the pozzolanic activity and particle grading of RHA-cement blends significantly affect blending efficiency of mortar12. The grain size distribution of cattle bone ash (CBA) greatly influences mortar performance. Finer particles provide a higher surface area, which enhances pozzolanic activity. This leads to better formation of calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H), improving compressive strength. Fine CBA also fills micro-voids between cement grains, reducing porosity and increasing durability. This contributes to better resistance against water penetration and chemical attack10,13.

Pozzolanic action plays an important role in influencing the rate of strength gain over the long-term curing period of cementitious materials14,15. RHA is a highly reactive pozzolanic substance that is produced by burning nice at temperatures below 700 °C in a regulated marine environment16–18. Cow bone ash has been reported to mitigate the corrosion of steel in reinforced concrete structure owing to its ability to reduce or suppress the thermodynamics fluctuations of films around the reinforcement. This research aimed at investigating the influence of CBA when used as partial replacement of cement on the properties of concrete in the fresh and hardened states13. ASTM C 12519 in their definition of pozzolan called it a siliceous or an aluminous and siliceous material which in its natural state possesses little or no cementitious nature, but will in the presence of moisture and a finely divided form, which on reaction with calcium hydroxide at room temperature will form compounds that cementitious in nature20. A pozzolan material has a content of siliceous or siliceous and aluminous materials; it holds little or no property of cement21.

This study is positioned within the broader global agenda of sustainable construction and green materials research, responding to the urgent need to decarbonize the cement industry. As traditional cement manufacturing remains one of the largest industrial contributors to global CO2 emissions, the shift toward alternative cementitious materials is critical for achieving sustainability goals. The use of agricultural and animal waste materials such as rice husk ash (RHA) and cattle bone ash (CBA) as supplementary cementitious materials represents a circular economy approach, transforming waste into valuable construction inputs.

Cattle Bone Ash (CBA) is used as a partial replacement for cement in cementitious materials due to its beneficial chemical composition, particularly its high calcium oxide (CaO) content22. This makes CBA a reactive material that contributes to the strength development in concrete. At an optimum replacement level of around 10%, CBA has been shown to significantly improve the compressive strength of concrete. Additionally, its use leads to a reduction in the amount of calcium hydroxide Ca(OH)2 in the hardened matrix, which can enhance durability. These properties make CBA a promising alternative material for sustainable construction23. Even, it can be observed that bone powder ash have a high calcium oxide more than cement, the silica oxide of bone powder ash is very low when compare to that of rice husk ash9.

Ca(OH)2, a byproduct of cement hydration, reacts with Cow bone ash. Excess Ca(OH)2 can leak out and react with aggressive substances like chlorides or carbon dioxide, lowering the pH of the concrete and jeopardizing the passive layer that shields the steel reinforcement24. Cow bone ash contributes to the maintenance of high alkalinity, which is essential for steel corrosion resistance, by lowering Ca(OH)2. Fine particles found in CBA contribute to a denser microstructure by filling in gaps in the concrete matrix. By reducing the concrete’s permeability, this finely tuned pore structure inhibits the entry of water, oxygen, chlorides, and other corrosive agents that cause and hasten steel25.

Previous studies have highlighted the pozzolanic potential of both RHA and CBA due to their high silica and calcium content, respectively, making them suitable for partial cement replacement. By exploring the synergistic effect of these two waste-derived ashes, this research contributes to the development of low-carbon binders and provides a viable solution for resource-efficient, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly mortar production, especially in regions with high availability of these by-products.

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the impact of blending cattle bone ash with rice husk ash as a partial substitute for conventional cement in mortar production. This research aims to assess how this combination influences the overall properties of mortar, particularly in terms of workability, strength, durability, and microstructural characteristics.

Materials and sample preparations

Materials and properties

Materials

One of the most significant hydraulic binders which are inorganic, non-metallic materials is cement. It forms a solid working material by a chemical reaction with water. When there is water present, this reaction occurs. Compounds such as limestone, chalk, clay, sand, or iron ore are burned at extremely high temperatures, between 1400 and 1600 °C, to create cement26. Cement clinkers are grayish-black pellets produced by chemical processes. The main differences between cement types are their fineness and C3A concentration27,28.

The amount of Calcium, Silicon, Alumina, Iron, Sulfate and the total quantity of Alkalis, including Na and K, are the most significant factors influencing cement minerals29. The chemical reaction that occurs when Portland cement comes into contact with water is known as hydration. Cement pastes harden as a result of a sequence of chemical reactions involving its constituent compounds. Hydration is the term used to describe reactions with water, and the new solids that result from hydration are all referred to as hydration products30.

Bone is a strong, hard, and fibrous material forming the structural framework of the mammalian endoskeleton. It is a rigid tissue composed of cells embedded within a dense intercellular matrix that provides mechanical support for soft tissues and plays a crucial role in regulating calcium and phosphate levels within the body. Bone minerals primarily consist of calcium, phosphate, and hydroxyl ions, with small amounts of cationic elements such as magnesium and strontium replacing calcium, while bicarbonate and fluoride may substitute hydroxyl anions31. The dominant minerals in the intercellular matrix are calcium and phosphate, which contribute to the bone’s structural integrity. The crystalline mineral components provide high hardness, rigidity, and exceptional compressive strength, while the collagen fibers offer elasticity and intrinsic tensile properties32.

Bone powder is an inorganic material derived from animal bones, which are first dried and then subjected to high-temperature treatment through furnace heating or open-air burning. This process eliminates organic matter, including fats and other tissues, leaving behind a mineral-rich residue. After cooling, the burned bone is crushed into small fragments before being finely ground using a milling machine22. During high-temperature processing, calcium phosphate in bone reacts with calcium hydroxide (Ca (OH)2) to form hydroxyapatite (Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2). As the hydroxyapatite crystals develop, they incorporate other inorganic elements, including calcium carbonate, sodium, magnesium, and fluoride33.

The extraction of inorganic minerals, particularly calcium ions, occurs through calcination at temperatures exceeding 900 °C10,34. The degree of crystallinity and crystal size progressively increase with higher calcination temperatures, whereas porosity significantly decreases. Structural and mineralogical transformations occur upon heating; however, these changes remain consistent across bone specimens.

In the calcination process, bone fragments are placed in an open alumina crucible and heated in a furnace to eliminate organic constituents, leaving behind pure hydroxyapatite. The samples are subjected to various temperatures (700 °C, 900 °C, and 1100 °C) for three hours at a controlled heating rate of 10 °C/min before being gradually cooled to room temperature35. For this study, cattle bones were first broken into smaller pieces and sun-dried. They were then incinerated in a muffle furnace under ambient temperature, and the resulting ash was pulverized using a disk mill until it reached a particle size of less than 75 μm. The fine ash passing through a 75 μm sieve was subsequently used.

Rice husk is an agricultural byproduct generated during rice milling, constituting approximately 20% of the total rice paddy. In Ethiopia, rice husk is commonly found in certain regions and has traditionally been used as cattle feed and as fuel for cooking in rural areas. However, extensive research has demonstrated that rice husk is rich in silica content, making it a valuable pozzolanic material when subjected to controlled combustion. It occupies a significant portion of agricultural waste, accounting for 78.5% of the total area coverage and 85.5% by volume36. The quality of rice husk is influenced by various factors, including the rice variety, soil chemistry, climatic conditions, and geographical location. These factors contribute to variations in silica content and reactivity.

The transformation of rice husk into reactive silica is largely dependent on the calcination temperature, burning duration, and combustion conditions. Research findings indicate that controlled burning within a temperature range of 500–700 °C for 1–6 h effectively converts most of the crystalline siliceous structures into reactive amorphous SiO2, which is essential for its pozzolanic activity37. In response to the growing demand for environmentally friendly materials, rice husk-based composites have been developed by combining rice husk with various polymers. These composites are utilized in industries such as automobile manufacturing, construction (building profiles), decking, and railing products38,39. Blending rice husk ash (RHA) with cattle bone ash (CBA) could lead to the formation of a novel chemical matrix with enhanced properties, making it a more effective pozzolanic material for concrete applications.

The synergy between these materials may contribute to improved mechanical performance, durability, and sustainability in cementitious systems. For this study, rice husk was ground to the desired fineness using an ultra-centrifugal mill. The resulting ash was then sieved through a 75 μm ASTM sieve to remove larger particles. The fine RHA that passed through the 75-µm sieve was subsequently utilized in the experimental investigation as shown in Fig. 1.

Materials preparation procedures.

Cleaned and dried rice husks were calcined in a muffle furnace under air atmosphere at temperatures typically ranging from 500 °C to 700 °C conditions optimized to preserve the amorphous silica content. A heating rate of 10 °C/min was maintained, and the samples were held at the final temperature for 2 to 3 h, followed by natural cooling within the furnace.

According to ASTM C33, aggregates with a particle size of less than 4.75 mm are classified as sand or fine aggregate. In this study, locally sourced, impurity-free river sand was selected as the fine aggregate. To ensure its suitability for use, a series of standardized tests were conducted to assess its quality and compliance with ASTM specifications.

Water is the least expensive yet most essential component in concrete production. According to standards, potable water which is safe for drinking is also suitable for mixing mortar40. In general, any natural water free from unusual taste or odor can be used for mortar preparation. Therefore, in this study, potable water was utilized for all experimental procedures to ensure consistency and quality.

Testing methods and procedures

Fresh mortar property test

Workability is one of the most crucial properties of fresh mortar, as it significantly influences the ease of mixing, placing, and finishing, ultimately affecting the overall quality of the hardened material. In this study, the workability of fresh mortar was evaluated under both static and vibrational conditions to assess its consistency and performance. To determine the consistency of the fresh mortar mix, a slump test was conducted using the flow table method, following the ASTM C1437-07 standard. This test provides a quantitative measure of the mortar’s ability to flow and spread under controlled conditions, reflecting its cohesiveness and fluidity.

Additionally, a setting time test was performed to examine the time-dependent changes in the mortar’s properties, which are critical for ensuring proper handling and application. ASTM C80745 which stands for “Standard Test Method for Time of Setting of Mortar. Modified Vicat Needle apparatus was used. This test involves placing a sample of mortar in a mold and periodically measuring the Vicat plunger’s penetration depth. When the needle stops penetrating past a predetermined depth, the initial setting time is established.

The workability assessment of fresh mortar is essential for producing a high-quality mixture with desirable characteristics, including appropriate stickiness and a balanced combination of ingredients. The slump test serves as a practical and widely accepted method for determining the consistency of a mortar mix, as variations in slump values can indicate significant changes in the water content or mix proportions.

Hardened mortar properties test

Testing the properties of hardened mortar is one of the important properties of the hardened mixes. The properties of hardened mortar were examined in the laboratory using the standards. The mortar cubes measuring 50 × 50 × 50 mm were cast for each mortar mix to track the development of compressive strength at 3, 7, 28, 56, and 90 days of age. The mixing and casting procedure were conducted according to the ASTM standard specified in ASTM C109. All the samples were demolded after 24 h of casting and constantly cured underwater until the age of testing. An automatic compression machine was used to perform the compressive strength tests of the mortar.

The ultrasonic pulse velocity test is also one of the most promising methods for evaluating mortar structures as it allows to study homogeneity of the material. This process is based on the propagation of high-frequency sound waves through the material. The speed of the wave varies with material density, allowing porosity estimation and discontinuity detection46. Pulses of compressional waves are generated by an electroacoustic transducer which is held in contact with a surface of the concrete to be tested. After passing through the mortar, the pulses are received by a second transducer and converted into electrical energy, which is located at a distance L from the transmitting transducer. Transit time t is measured electronically, and pulse velocity v is calculated by dividing L by t and result are in Table 5. Durability of mortar properties test

The sulphate attack of mortar is tested using sodium sulphate. It was prepared by immersing the specimens in a sodium sulfite Anhydrous (Na2SO3) of 5% solution prepared by mixing a weight of sodium sulfate with water for 24 h after mixing until being subjected to compressive strength tests at different ages (3, 7, 28, 56, and 90 days) to determine the compressive strength loss due to Sulfate attack.

50 mm × 50 mm × 50 mm specimens are used in the test, ensuring a consistent sample size for accurate and consistent results47. Five samples are prepared at 3, 7, 28, 56, and 90 days for each curing period in order to account for material property variability and allow for statistical significance. A total of 25 samples are prepared for the assessment of water absorption properties, and another 25 samples are cast and assigned to the sulfate resistance evaluation. This extensive sampling approach makes it easier to comprehend how well the material performs over a range of curing times, allowing for a thorough examination of permeability and durability in various environmental settings. Microstructure and morphological structure

The scanning electron microscope (SEM) is one of the most versatile instruments for the study and analysis of the microstructural characteristics of solid objects49,50. In this study, the hydrated cement mortar samples depending on their compressive strength were taken for SEM analysis. The scale range used in SEM analysis was 10 μm with a resolution of 3000×.

Microstructure analysis was performed at 28 and 56 days of hydration, revealing that the samples were fully dried by these stages. This indicates that hydration had effectively ceased, and no further reaction kinetics were anticipated beyond this point. The sample preparation was done manually by reducing the sample size to a small piece. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) is a thermo-analytical technique that measures the weight changes of a sample at a given time and temperature51. It is just a continuation of mass measurement when research goes beyond room temperature. TGA is primarily used to study thermal stability, although the weight gain/loss of the samples can also be related to a variety of parameters. Weight loss is usually attributed to decomposition, adsorption, and dehydration52. TGA is the major method for quantifying the amount of material and has been established to allow its measurement with relatively high accuracy on a routine basis.

After curing for 28 and 56 days, the paste sample pieces were placed in an alumina crucible container of a thermal gravimetric analyzer. Samples were heated in a thermal gravimetric analyzer at a temperature range of 50 to 800 °C, and the rate of heat increment was set at 20 °C/min using nitrogen as a medium under static conditions. While the samples are exposed to a temperature program, the difference in temperature between the sample and a reference sample is checked against time53. The instrument is a differential thermal analyzer, and the record is the differential thermal or DTA curve.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy is a valuable device in the atomic characterization of organic and inorganic species54. Vibrational techniques, which are common in many analytical laboratories, have certain advantages over the other X-ray diffraction techniques, and thermal analysis techniques, such as the small amount of sample required, quick and easy sample preparation, and short analysis time. It also allows simultaneous study of organic and inorganic species and crystalline or amorphous compounds. The crystalline or amorphous content can be examined using the background absorbance underling the crystalline band in the 750–920 cm−1 region55,56. The main product of Portland cement hydration (specifically, the hydration of alite (C3S) and belite (C2S)) is C–S–H gel. The infrared active and absorbed infrared wavelengths of functional groups such as Si–O, SO4, H2O, OH, Al–O, and CaCO3 are found in cement57. The optical frequency between 400 cm− 1 and 4000 cm− 1 was used to set the resolution of the mid-IR spectrum.

The speed at which sound travels through mortar is directly correlated with its compressive strength, according to the Ultrasonic Pulse Velocity (UPV) test61. When 10% pozzolanic material is added to the mixture, this relationship becomes especially noticeable. A denser and more consistent internal structure is indicated by the higher UPV at this level. This supports the beneficial effects of pozzolanic addition and is consistent with an observed increase in compressive strength62. These results are further supported by Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) analysis, which reveals a more refined microstructure with fewer voids, indicating better packing and hydration products. All of these findings show that adding 10% pozzolanic replacement improves the mortar’s mechanical and microstructural qualities.

The result reveals that the concrete’s quality in terms of uniformity, lack of internal defects, cracks, and segregation was medium concrete quality up to 15% replacement at 28 days, and later (56 and 90 days) it shows improvement with good quality of concrete. This trend was also observed in the results of the compressive strength tests. The bone ash and rice husk ash blends showed higher UPV with higher compressive strength. The provided Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) micrographs offer a glimpse into the microstructural characteristics of cementitious materials where Cement is partially replaced by Rice Husk Ash and cattle bone ash at varying percentages (0%, 10%, and 20%). In the control sample with 0% replacement, a moderately dense matrix with visible Calcium Silicate Hydrate (C–S–H) formation, the primary binding phase, is observed alongside some voids, which are potential weaknesses within the structure, suggesting an intermediate level of compressive strength. Introducing 10% appears to refine the microstructure, leading to a more distributed and potentially denser C–S–H network with a noticeable reduction in the size and number of visible voids; this microstructural improvement strongly suggests that the (CBA-RHA)10 replacement sample is likely to exhibit the highest compressive strength among the three, likely due to the pozzolanic reaction forming additional C–S–H and enhancing particle packing.

With a further increase to 20% of replacement, the micrograph still indicates a relatively dense matrix and the presence of ettringite, a phase associated with early strength development. Thermogravimetric and differential thermal analysis (TGA/DTA)

The TGA analysis was conducted on mortar samples containing 0%, 10%, and 20% cattle bone ash and rice husk ash (mixed in equal proportions) as a partial cement replacement at 28 and 56 days of hydration. The first weight loss, observed in the temperature range of 110–300 °C, is primarily attributed to the dehydration of C–S–H. The second weight loss, occurring between 350 and 500 °C, is associated with the decomposition of portlandite. The third weight loss, recorded within the 600–800 °C range, corresponds to the decomposition of carbonates.

Corresponding peak temperature is related to the decarbonization of calcium carbonate66. The TGA/DTA result displays three endothermic peaks located at 70–300 °C, 400–500 °C and 600–800 °C for (CBA- RHA)0, (CBA- RHA)10, and (CBA-RHA)20 samples at a selected time of 28 and 56 days as shown in Figs. 7 and 8 below. The peaks implanted at 70–300 °C mainly associated with the deportation of uncombined water and disintegration of amorphous parts of C–S–H gels and calcium aluminate hydrates. The peaks that appeared at 400–500 °C is correlated to the dehydration of CH (portlandite)67. The peaks formed at 600–800 °C is described to the degradation of calcium carbonate with different crystallinity handling68.

TGA/DTA graph for 28 day of curing.

TGA/DTA graph for 56 day of curing.

Looking at the Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) curves (the black lines) observed as a gradual mass loss as the temperature increases for all samples, both at 28 and 56 days of curing. The initial mass loss below 200 °C is primarily due to the evaporation of free and bound water within the cementitious matrix. Noticeably, the samples with Rice Husk Ash (RHA–CBA )10, and CBA- RHA)20 generally exhibit a slightly lower initial mass loss compared to the control sample (CBA-RHA) 0 at both curing ages. This suggests that the incorporation of (RHA-CBA) might lead to a denser microstructure with fewer pores holding water. The significant mass loss occurring between approximately 400 °C and 600 °C is mainly attributed to the dehydroxylation of calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2​), a by-product of cement hydration. Comparing the DTA curves (the red lines), the endothermic peak associated with this decomposition appears less pronounced in the RHA-CBA amended samples, particularly at 56 days of curing. This indicates a lower content of Ca(OH)2​ in these samples. Since Ca(OH)2​ is a weaker phase compared to the calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel which is the main binder in cement, a reduction in its content, as suggested by the TGA/DTA results for the RHA samples, often correlates with an increase in strength.

Furthermore, the mass loss observed between 600 °C and 800 °C is primarily due to the decomposition of calcium carbonate, which could be present as a result of carbonation. The slight variations in this region might be influenced by the pozzolanic reaction of RHA-CBA, where the silica in RHA-CBA reacts with Ca(OH)2​ to form more C–S–H gel, potentially leading to a denser matrix and influencing the carbonation process. Considering the correlation with strength, the TGA/DTA results suggest that the incorporation of RHA, particularly at 56 days of curing, leads to a reduction in the less desirable Ca(OH)2​ content and potentially a denser microstructure. Conclusion

Even though rice husk ash (RHA) and cattle bone ash (CBA) show promise as partial cement substitutes, a number of obstacles must be overcome before their full potential can be realized. Their overall performance is significantly influenced by the variability in their chemical composition and the crucial requirement for controlled processing conditions. The results of this study highlight the advantages of employing RHA and CBA, offering insightful information for future sustainable cementitious material.

The mechanical properties of mortar containing 5% and 10% CBA and RHA as cement replacements showed notable improvements in strength compared to control mixes. The compressive strength increases from 31.5 to 45.9 MPa when the percentage replacement of the rice husk ash and cattle bone ash mix increases from 0 to 10% at 28 days of curing.

The mechanical properties of mortar containing 5% and 10% CBA and RHA as cement replacements showed notable improvements in strength compared to control mixes. The compressive strength increases from 31.5 to 45.9 MPa when the percentage replacement of the rice husk ash and cattle bone ash mix increases from 0 to 10% at 28 days of curing.

The combined effect of Cow bone ash and Rice husk ash is densification of the mortar microstructure by the clogging of micro-sized capillary voids due to the formation of pozzolanic hydrates.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) results show a reduction in weight loss with when the temperature is going up. Differential thermal analysis (DTA) reveals the formation of additional structures through an exothermic process at 10% replacement level, correlating well with improvements observed in compressive strength, ultrasonic pulse velocity, and resistance to sulfate attack. Furthermore, FTIR analysis confirms the presence of amorphous silica, which facilitates the formation of a secondary, denser calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel through the reaction of silicates with calcium hydroxide produced during cement hydration.
