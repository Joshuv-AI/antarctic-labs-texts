An Illumina-based amplicon sequencing approach designed to determine grapevine fanleaf virus isolates
Authors: Isabelle R. Martin, Pierre Mustin, Wassim Rhalloussi, Shahinez Garcia, Myriam Hagege, Julie Kubina, Emmanuelle Vigne, Jean-Michel Hily
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditionally, to comprehensively assess the genetic diversity of a viral isolate, i.e. the population of variants, many molecular steps are necessary, mostly involving PCR amplification, cloning and Sanger sequencing. Lately, with its advent, high throughput sequencing (HTS) has become the method of choice. Both approaches, while extremely informative, may not be economically advantageous to use on a large scale, especially when dealing with highly complex viral populations. Here, we describe an Illumina-based amplicon sequencing (AmpSeq) approach, associated with an analytical workflow for rapid diagnostic, combining HTS quality screening at a more affordable cost. Our multi-primers assay was designed to identify a large diversity of both genomic RNAs (RNA1 and RNA2) of Nepovirus foliumflabelli (also known as Grapevine fanleaf virus, GFLV), compatible with the known genetic diversity of this bipartite virus down to detecting rare variants (i.e. present at less than 2% in an isolate). Beyond diagnostics, our approach can be used and adapted to characterize GFLV populations across vineyards as well as to support the development of GFLV cross-protected vines in France.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-53623-z.

Introduction

Disease management in crop production remains a critical issue, especially when current solutions do not align with sustainable practices with low environmental impact requirements or meet technical and regulatory deadlocks. Thus, exploring alternative protection strategies to fight plant pathogens is a requirement. Cross-protection has been studied as a biocontrol anti-viral approach in crops for some time1. The ability of a mild viral isolate to confer protection to the plant it infects against other related viruses is multifactorial, with the interplay between viral fitness and genetic relatedness being major determinants, even though the underlying mechanisms remain unresolved. Cross-protection has also been tested in grapevine against Nepovirus foliumflabelli (grapevine fanleaf virus, GFLV)2, the causing agent of fanleaf disease, being a major threat to the viticulture industry worldwide3, for which no effective solution currently exits. Field assays, complementing laboratory experiments on both natural and herbaceous hosts, are conducted to develop an effective cross-protection in multiple French vineyards4. In this aim, the exhaustive diagnostic of the primary-infected vines with GFLV candidate isolate for cross-protection is of the most importance. Current methods such as serological assays (DAS-ELISA), molecular-based technics (RFLP, cloning and Sanger sequencing) and biological indexing have limitations in sensitivity and specificity and are not informative enough to evaluate the sanitary status at the viral variant level (Table1). However, high throughput sequencing (HTS) could surpass them, as shown to assess diversity5. Combining both PCR and HTS may help in the detection of low ratios of viral to host RNA, and in the identification of low variants titer6,7. Early HTS study on the GFLV-population composition4,8,9 allowed us to synthesize cocktails of primers recognizing a large GFLV diversity and specifically variants found in specific area in France dedicated to cross-protection assays. From this, we developed molecular biology tools, coupled with HTS protocols complemented with bioinformatics pipelines to analyze and establish the infectious status [i.e. confirming the presence of the expected primary-infecting GFLV variant(s) while identifying any other (super-infecting) GFLV sequences in the sample, when present]. Here, we detail our experimental approach using Illumina-based amplicon sequencing (AmpSeq) and its evaluation as a tool for characterizing primary-infected vines and analyzing them once they have been subjected to secondary infections in cross-protection experimental trials. To do so (Fig. 1), (1) we confirmed the identification power and quantification capacity of the sequencing methodology, (2) we confronted our primers’ cocktails with a controlled and known genetic diversity of our templates and finally (3) we tested different enrichment methods and validated our approach onto field samples. Materials and methods

Nucleic acids preparation

Three GFLV strains (F13, GHu and B844: GenBank numbers MG418839, MG418840, JN391442, EF426852, MF804979 and MF182354) were selected to mimic the genetic diversity of the virus8. Two plasmids per strain (namely GFLV-RNA1 and -RNA2 molecules) derived from the corresponding infectious clones were amplified in E. coli. DNA was extracted using NucleoSpin Plasmid kit (Macherey–Nagel, Hoerdt, France) following manufacturer’s recommendations.

For natural sources, six grapevines from the greenhouse (with known virome) and eight from a French commercial vineyard were sampled and processed as previously described9 to preserve nucleic acid content. Briefly, leaves were crushed in liquid nitrogen. From the same homogenized leaf powder, either total RNA was obtained using RNeasy Plant mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), or encapsidated viral RNA was recovered after an immunocapture (IC) assay using GFLV polyclonal antibodies (Bioreba AG, Reinach, Switzerland)9. RNAs were then either used individually (N = 8), or pool of 3 extracts were mixed (N = 2). Total RNA and encapsidated viral RNA were then reverse transcribed with either specific reverse primer or oligo(dT) (OdT) using Superscript IV Reverse Transcriptase kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts) as per manufacturer’s recommendations.

PCR assay

Close to 100 complete coding sequences of GFLV-RNA1 and -RNA2 obtained from previous HTS studies and/or gathered from NCBI, were used to design two degenerated primer cocktails recognizing a large diversity of GFLV variants. All primers contained Illumina adapters to assist libraries preparation. Primer cocktail #1 and #2 amplified 450 bp and 420 bp products from GFLV-RNA1 (amplicon1) and -RNA2 (amplicon2) respectively. PCRs were carried out with cDNA (see above) or plasmid DNA either alone or in mix at different percentage combinations (Table S1 and S2), using GoTaq DNA polymerase kit (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin) under the following cycling parameters: initial denaturation at 95 C for 2 min, followed by 36 cycles of 30 s at 95 C, 30 s at 52 C and 45 s at 72 °C, ended with a final elongation for 5 min at 72 C. As recommended for Illumina sequencing, all amplicons were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA) following adapted instructions (unpublished). Plasmids and purified amplicons were quantified for DNA concentration by Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen Waltham, Massachusetts), and fragment quality was assessed using Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California).

Libraries preparation and sequencing

Equimolar preparation of both amplicons from the same sample (derived from RNA1 and RNA2), indexing, and library sequencing was performed by the sequencing company, GenoScreen (Lille, France), and sequencing was completed on an Illumina MiSeq (2 × 250 bp). Two runs of sequencing were performed: the first one consisted of 102 samples and the second consisted of a confirmation run with 91 of these samples. Only results from the 1st run are shown in the main text. The results of the second run are available in the supplemental Data.

Ten total RNA extracts (coming from the fourteen selected greenhouse and field grapevines) were submitted to RNASeq to compare with AmpSeq results (Table S3 and S4). Briefly, a poly(A) selection was done prior to library preparation using TrueSeq Stranded mRNA kit (Illumina, San Diego, California), and sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 (2 × 150 bp) was performed by Get-PlaGe Genotoul platform facility (INRAE Castanet-Tolosan, France).

Bioinformatic analyses and protocol

AmpSeq and RNAseq analyses

After the demultiplexing steps being done by each sequencing platform, all dataset analyses were performed and finalized using CLC Genomics Workbench v12.0 software (Qiagen Bioinformatics, Aarhus, Denmark). Genome assembly and near-complete GFLV consensus sequences were obtained following previously described RNAseq protocols10. For RNASeq downstream analyses, 5’- and 3’-UTR were not considered. Briefly, after quality check procedures and trimming, only reads above 70 nucleotides were kept. GFLV-variant detection was performed by de novo assembly. Contig list was then mapped against GFLV-RNA1 and -RNA2 consensus sequences with low stringency parameters (length fraction of 0.2 with similarity of 0.5) to detect a maximum of a putative diversity of our viral population. For AmpSeq analysis, similar steps were performed as in10. Final sequences considered in the study, consisted of the 450 pb and 420 pb for RNA1 and RNA2 respectively. Percentages were obtained following a direct mapping (length fraction of 0.5 with similarity of 0.7) against all sequences from a sample and completed for each sample separately.

Sequence analyses

Codon-based multiple sequence alignments and maximum likelihood (ML)-based phylogenetic trees were prepared using MUSCLE, implemented in MEGA-X software11, excluding the viral untranslated regions (UTRs). The best ML-fitted model for each sequence alignment was used, and nodes in phylogenetic trees were validated by bootstrap analysis (100 replicates). For visualization effects, the graphical viewer FigTree v.1.3.1 was used (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/). To analyze polymorphism, the diversity index (π), corresponding to the average number of nucleotide substitutions per site between any two sequences in a multi-sequence alignment was calculated using DnaSP v.6.12.0312 and MEGAX software.

Bioinformatic pipeline

To maximize the automation process, reducing human hour while increasing quality control and reproducibility, a bioinformatics pipeline was developed and is available (https://forge.inrae.fr/jean-michel.hily1/ampseq_nextflow_pipeline.git ). To investigate viral diversity and estimate the number of distinct variants present as well as their abundance in grapevine amplicon-seq datasets, we designed a Nextflow pipeline to cluster highly similar viral sequences and infer variant abundance from cluster composition. The workflow begins with read trimming and quality filtering using fastp : v.0.24.1 to remove low-quality bases, adapter contamination, and sequences too short to contain full information. Reads corresponding to PhiX control sequences are identified and discarded following alignment with BWA-MEM2 : v.2.2.1 - and filtering with Samtools. The remaining reads are then aligned to the Grapevine fanleaf virus (GFLV) reference genome using BWA-MEM and reads mapping to RNA1 and RNA2 segments are separated for independent processing. Since all data is paired-end, reads belonging to the same pair are merged using fastq-pair to facilitate downstream clustering. Subsequently, merged sequences are clustered at 90% identity using Vsearch : v.2.30.0, grouping reads that represent closely related viral variants. For each cluster, a consensus sequence is generated, and the merged reads are realigned to their respective consensus using Minimap2 to estimate their abundance in the sample. The resulting cluster statistics provide an estimate of the number of distinct viral variants present in each sample and their relative abundance.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were computed using the R software (R version 4.4.2, http://www.r-project.org/). We consider for each genomic segment (RNA1 and RNA2) the proportion of each consensus sequence based on the number of reads. As the proportion of GFLV-RNA1 and -RNA2 may not always segregate continuously, correlations between observed and expected RNA proportions were determined using the Spearman’s rank correlation method. To compute the detection thresholds of GFLV RNAs, we used the LL.2 function of the ‘drc’ R package (version 3.0–1)13. It provides a two parameters log-logistic function:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f(x) = \frac{1}{{1 + \exp \left( {b\left( {\log (x) - \log (e)} \right)} \right)}}$$\end{document}where x is the proportion of reads corresponding to each GFLV sequence, b is the slope of the curve and e the inflexion point (i.e. the detection threshold). In this model, the lower and upper limits are set at 0 and 1, representing the unexpected and expected detection of GFLV sequence in each sample (binomial response). The Area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve (AUC) was selected as the most suitable algorithm performance metric, to account for the misclassification in the dataset using the ROC function of the ‘pROC’ R package (version 1.18.5)14. When needed, after fitting the data, recalls (sensitivity), precisions and accuracies were calculated from the data to analyze and compare the performance of the models15.

Results and discussion

Assessment of amplicon sequencing technic (AmpSeq)

Various combinations of known quantities of three variants of GFLV-RNA1 and -RNA2 amplicons were submitted to AmpSeq (Fig. 1). This step was performed to ascertain the identification power (accuracy) and quantification capability (precision) of the HTS-based technology being used. Composition of all 32 mixes tested are shown in Table S1.

First, the amplicons specific to the genomic segments (GFLV-RNA1 and -RNA2) of each of the three strains were detected in all mixes as expected. In addition, the sequencing error rate was low, with a maximum of 2 mutations out of the 450 pb RNA1 amplicon product and a single mutation out of the 420 pb RNA2 amplicon. The error rate of 0.45% is well below our sequence identity criterion or our clade definition of 95% identity. Such an error could have most probably occurred during the sequencing process itself since each of the 32 prepared mixes came from the same source of amplification before being blended. Second, the theoretical and post-sequencing ratios for each amplicon mixes were confronted. Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) between theoretical and observed percentages assigned to each variant was higher than 0.98 for either RNA1 or RNA2 (Fig. 2, Table S1). Some discrepancies in the results will be discussed in the troubleshooting section. As described in the first result section, the sequencing was quite powerful since each variant was detected where it was expected, and the sequencing error rate was observed at a maximum of 3 mutations for either targeted RNA (error rate max of 0.71%). This higher mutation rate compared to the first analysis could be explained by the fact that here, the amplicons from each mix were the results of independent amplification from each other, adding the potential polymerase error rate to the above-described sequencing error rate. Post-sequencing, we confronted ratios for each GFLV-RNA1 and -RNA2 amplicon versus theoretical ratios. As before, the Spearman’s ρ between theoretical and observed percentages assigned to each variant was higher than 0.98 for either RNA1 or RNA2 (Fig. 3A and B). At this stage, we can confirm that our methodology presents satisfactory accuracy and precision and that our multi-primers’ cocktails are not biased toward a specific variant out of our selection. From these results, using a two parameters log-logistic function, we can calculate cut-offs at which we are confident in the identification of the presence of a GFLV variant, which is set at 0.21% and 0.10% for RNA1 and RNA2 respectively (Fig. 3C). For each model, the computation of the ROC curves showed that the classification was almost perfect with areas under the curves close to 1 (0.988 for RNA1 and 0.997 for RNA2, Fig. 3D).

Assessment using field samples

For the subsequent phase, we dedicated our effort to (1) confirm the detection power of our multi-primers’ cocktails using a wider range of genetic diversity of the virus and (2) confront the AmpSeq methodology with real viral targets which are RNAs, and not DNA-based clones used as template in the above-described analyses.

To this purpose, 14 isolates from GFLV-infected grapevines originating from either the greenhouse (with known virome) and the field (with unknown status) were evaluated. Single grapevine samples (N = 8) were used as well as a mix of three grapevine extracts (N = 2) to confront the robustness of the AmpSeq methodology to these highly complex sources of GFLV (especially for the samples corresponding to mixtures of three plants).

In order to characterize the genetic diversity of these complex GFLV-RNAs mixtures, ten RNA extracts and mixes were submitted to RNASeq to identify the GFLV molecular variants composition within each sample (Table S3). In total 65 near-complete sequences were obtained after de novo sequence assembly, 34 GFLV-RNA1 and 31 -RNA2 molecules. Isolates ranged from a very simple composition in GFLV variant, with a single RNA1 and/or RNA2 molecule (sample 23.24), to very complex ones with up to seven molecules of each RNA within a single sample (samples 23.25 for GFLV-RNA1 and 23.26 for GFLV-RNA2). The read proportion corresponding to each GFLV consensus sequence within a sample was quite variable, ranging from 2.12% (sample 23.26, sequence 23.26.1–5) to 100% (sample 23.24, RNA1 and RNA2). More than just the number of consensus sequences being detected in our test samples, genetic diversity was also quite important as confirmed by the alignment followed by phylogenetic analyses from the complete ORF1 and ORF2 (Fig. 4 and S1, respectively). Up to 27 and 25 clades (defined as 95% identity within a clade) were identified, with an overall nucleotide diversity of π = 0.108 ± 0.002 and π = 0.118 ± 0.004 for GFLV-ORF1 and -ORF2 respectively. Such genetic diversity is quite representative of the, so far known and previously described, genetic diversity for GFLV8 and new clades, that were not present in the alignment of about 100 sequences used to design the primers ‘cocktails, were detected. While focusing on the theoretical region amplified by PCR, 22 and 20 clades were defined and the overall nucleotide diversity was π = 0.101 ± 0.001 and π = 0.115 ± 0.004 for amplicon1 and amplicon2 respectively (Fig. 4 and S1). Comparison of different enrichment methods vs RNAseq

The last step was dedicated to comparing different extraction protocols coupled with different enrichment methods [total RNA extraction, immunocapture (IC), using OdT or gene specific primers (GSP)], making a total of four distinct trials. For this, we used the same ten samples described above (Tables S3 and S4). All sequences detected by RNASeq were also detected following our four different AmpSeq protocols. The only variation found between trials lies in the accumulation percentage of each molecular variant, reflecting more on the different RNA sources and enrichment methods, being used prior amplification, rather than an accuracy problem of the AmpSeq method itself. Indeed, results obtained for amplicon2 after total RNA extraction were slightly more alike to RNASeq data, rather than the one resulting from the IC-based experiments: ρ for RT-PCR versus IC-RT-PCR at 0,67 versus 0,59 when using oligo dT and at 0,60 versus 0,54 using GSP (Fig. 5). Such difference could probably be due to the differences in the RNA sources, i.e. total RNA versus encapsidated RNA. Nevertheless, this tendency was not observed for amplicon1 with similar ρ (0,87 to 0,89) for the four methods used.

Finally, comparing the four ROC curves plots (Fig. 6), modelling false-positive rate against true-positive rate, methodologies based on cDNA obtained from GSP seem to be more performant than the ones using OdT. Due to the higher number of false positives with the protocols using OdT, probably due to intra-lane contamination, the cut-offs (at which we are confident in the identification of the presence of a sequence) are effectively higher for these protocols. Potential hitches and troubleshooting

To maximize the accuracy (i.e. identifying accurately the lowest percentage of presence of a variant within an isolate) of the AmpSeq methodology, we push forward several key elements. (i) The first one is directly associated with the sensitivity of the quantification tools and subsequently the handling and mixing of each PCR product, and/or the mixing of libraries preparation prior to the sequencing itself. According to our provider, mixing 192 samples on a MiSeq should yield over 25 K reads per sample. Such depth is more than adequate to estimate the presence of and at each percentage both molecules by mixing at a 1:1 ratio both RNA1 and RNA2 amplicons obtained for each sample. However, post-sequencing, we can confirm that it was rarely the case. Indeed, average reads were close to 90 K per sample and the 1:1 ratio was rarely attained but much rather closer to a 3:2 ratio in favor of amplicon2 (Fig S3). In our analysis, average coverage was 27 K/nt and 62K/nt for RNA1 and RNA2 respectively. In addition, the number of reads per sample was quite variable, ranging from 63 to 119 K (Figure S4). Altogether, these metrics suggest that the quantification and handling of the matrix prior to any sequencing is of prime importance. However, even with the lowest number of reads being delivered, set at 13K/nt for RNA1, provides 12 000 times more information than the cloning/Sanger sequencing protocol at a lower price (considering a minimum number of 10 clones to be sequenced, Table 1). Also, even with very different metrics for each sample, the AmpSeq methodology produced enough data to draw conclusions regarding the composition in GFLV molecules, with sufficient accuracy and precision compared to RNAseq. (ii) The second element would be minimizing intra-lane contamination, also known as ‘index hoping’, i.e. false-positive in which the presence or proportion of specific variants was not expected in a sample. We were able to identify a few samples for which ‘index hopping’ occurred (Tables S1, S2, Figs. 6, S2, S12, S13). Indeed, none of the GFLV variants used in the ‘Assessment of AmpSeq technic’ (section I of the paper) and in the ‘Evaluation of the primers’ cocktail efficacy’ (section II of the paper), were identified in any of the field study samples. However, some of those variants were detected in field samples following the Ampseq methodology. Such discrepancy could either be due to contamination in the lab, or most probably due to index hopping. The first hypothesis was put aside since these steps were performed by two different persons separated in time and space. Thus, to minimize or limit ‘index hopping’, problematic indexes can be removed from the pool of indexes for future runs, ultimately reducing the multiplexing power of the methodology, or closely monitored. Another possibility would be the use of dual-indexing, which is now nowadays becoming standard practice and recommended by sequencing companies. (iii) The third component is dedicated to lowering discrepancies regarding RNA2 data compared to the one obtained for RNA1 (i.e. lower Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ), p-value with higher cut-offs at which we are confident in the identification of the presence of a GFLV variant associated with lower AUC). Those metrics may be due to detection saturation, occurring at different steps of the process, either the reverse transcription, mainly occurring while using OdT, or during the amplification phase. Indeed, RNA2 molecules are generally present in higher quantity than RNA1 in a sample and adjustments in the time of reverse step and/or the number of cycles during PCR may be needed prior to using AmpSeq for a better precision. (iv) The fourth and last element would be data analysis. We added a ‘read cleaning’ step to the protocol, to see if that could improve quality results. Comparing both protocols, with and without ‘read cleaning’ step, we did not see major differences in the final results (data not shown). Knowing such step can be time-consuming, we recommend not doing so. All the above-analyzed and data being presented were manually obtained using CLC Genomics Workbench software. While the results are very satisfactory, data management is time-consuming and error prone. The analysis of a run of 102 samples takes close to fifty human work hours, from the reception of the raw-reads down to the creation of tables with final percentage of accumulation of each variant per sample. To minimize human input, time of analyses, resources and potential errors while maximizing the automation process, increasing quality control and reproducibility, a bioinformatics pipeline was developed and is available (https://forge.inrae.fr/jean-michel.hily1/ampseq_nextflow_pipeline.git).

Overall, for the first run of 102 samples, a similar output (i.e. a list of consensus amplicon sequences per sample and percentage summary tables) obtained via the pipeline is produced in less than an hour. To ensure the robustness of the pipeline, data obtained manually and automatically were compared. Only a few minor differences are observed, corresponding to less than 3.5% of the consensus sequences being produced (involving 22 sequences out of the 644 produced, from 19 samples out of 102). Discrepancies were only linked to the production of two or more sequences within a clade when using one sequencing methodology versus the other. However, 100% of the clades were well identified using both methodologies. When consolidating the data, i.e. regrouping sequences and percentages from the same clade, correlation was almost perfect with slope close to 1 and R2 = 0.99 (Supp Fig. 5 A).

Following this, all metrics were re-analyzed following the results produced by the pipeline. Outcomes were comparable to the ones produced manually (Supp Fig. S5 A, S6, S8, S10, S12).

Reproducibility of our approach was tested by performing the same experiment a second time, with 91 samples, asking the sequencing provider to be careful mixing 1:1 RNA1 and RNA2 amplicons, and by changing indexes, when ‘index hopping’ was identified. The outcomes were highly similar to the first experiments and are detailed in Supp Fig. S5B, S7, S9, S11, S13, comforting our procedure and methodology analysis using our pipeline.

Conclusion

While RNASeq is highly informative it remains expensive for large scale study of viral populations. Other molecular methodologies can also be employed however, while quite cheap, they are low throughput with limited output information, rendering them almost obsolete (Table 1). The Illumina-based amplicon sequencing approach, AmpSeq, presented in this article is an effective and relatively inexpensive alternative method which allows to determine the composition of GFLV variants present in one sample. It is a PCR-based approach using two primers cocktails designed for the broad-spectrum detection of both GFLV genomic RNAs. We were able to demonstrate that this methodology could accurately identify different proportions of RNA1 and RNA2 of three GFLV strains in various mixtures. AmpSeq was able to confidently detect and identify a variant present at 1% in proportion in a mixture, regardless of the variant considered. Furthermore, this method has proven to be equally effective on complex samples originating from vineyard and has enabled the detection of up to seven genetically distinct variants in a sample. In addition, we developed a pipeline, proving to be as sensitive and accurate as the manual approach, while taking a fraction of the time. Altogether, more than 150 unique pools of amplicons from experimental samples can be submitted to Illumina sequencing in a single batch and treated in a way that is accurate and scalable.

This methodology provides a cost-effective and robust tool for studying GFLV populations at multiple levels: (i) the plot level by studying the dynamic of the propagation of mixed infections, that are the norm in vineyards, and spatial distribution of the variants, (ii) the plant level by examining intra-host colonization and (iii) the nematode-vector level by investigating potential bottleneck during virus transmission step. Finally, this AmpSeq methodology will also be very useful in studying cross-protection against GFLV. Indeed, one of the major difficulties in developing this approach is coupled with the need for precise techniques that can distinguish between different viral variants. Now, with this AmpSeq approach, it will be possible to validate the presence of specific variant(s) within a primary-infected plant and then demonstrate the effectiveness of protection by the absence of secondary superinfecting variants.
