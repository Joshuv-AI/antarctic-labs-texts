Heterogeneous wealth-related differences in modern contraceptive use in Bangladesh: a machine learning-based g-computation analysis of the DHS 2022 cross-sectional survey
Authors: Sudarshan Saha, Sohel Rana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConventional logistic regression estimates a single population-average odds ratio (OR), masking heterogeneity in how social determinants affect health outcomes across population subgroups. We applied machine learning (ML)-based stratified g-computation to estimate individual-specific wealth-associated counterfactual ORs of household wealth on modern contraceptive use among currently married Bangladeshi women in the two extreme wealth quintiles.MethodsWe analyzed 11,510 currently married women in the poorest and richest wealth quintiles from the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) 2022. Six model classes (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, Stacking Ensemble) were compared with five-fold cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, primary sampling unit (PSU)-level bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs), doubly-robust augmented inverse probability weighted (AIPW) sensitivity analysis, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) correction, and E-value analysis were conducted. Findings apply to the two extreme wealth quintiles and should not be extrapolated to the middle three quintiles.ResultsModern contraceptive prevalence was 37.7% in the poorest quintile versus 32.6% in the richest. The Stacking Ensemble achieved the highest CV-AUC (0.7108). All six AIPW estimates were near-null (p = 0.09–0.83), revealing that the aggregate wealth effect is zero because opposing subgroup effects cancel: a +17.6 percentage-point absolute risk difference (ARD) in the 35–49/higher-education subgroup is precisely offset by a -11.3 percentage-point ARD in the 15–24/primary-education subgroup. Individual ORs exhibited a Gini coefficient of 0.4525 (95% bootstrap CI: 0.4475–0.4568); interaction tests confirmed heterogeneity by education (p = 0.039) and age (p = 0.005). Median ORs ranged 6.4-fold: from 0.552 (15–24/primary; FDR q = 0.008) to 3.557 (35–49/higher; E-value = 6.57). Parity was the strongest SHAP predictive contributor, followed by employment and age.ConclusionsWealth-associated counterfactual OR differences in contraceptive use are patterned within demographic subgroups; population-average estimates are zero because opposing effects cancel, and only individual-level ML-based g-computation reveals this structure. The programmatic targeting framework derived from this approach offers a reproducible prioritisation tool for health equity analyses in any DHS-based survey.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01371-1.

Background

The logistic regression odds ratio (OR) has been the primary analytical tool in epidemiology and public health research for decades [1]. Its widespread adoption reflects computational tractability and broad familiarity, yet it masks a fundamental structural limitation: traditional logistic regression implicitly assumes that the log-odds of the outcome changes by the same amount per unit of exposure for all individuals in the population, regardless of their covariate profile [2, 3]. This homogeneity assumption produces one summary number representing what may actually be a heterogeneous landscape of individual associations—a simplification that is mathematically convenient but potentially misleading for inference and policy design.

In health equity research, where the objective is precisely to understand how social determinants such as household wealth operate differentially across population subgroups, the single-estimate paradigm can both obscure the mechanisms driving aggregate disparities and misdirect interventions [4, 5]. If the population-average OR for wealth on a health outcome is 0.70 but some subgroups have ORs above 5.0 while others have ORs below 0.5, a program designed around the average estimate will systematically over-invest in subgroups where wealth has negligible effect and under-invest in those where it is binding. Recognizing this limitation, the epidemiological literature on effect measure modification has long recommended subgroup-stratified analyses [3], but conventional regression interaction terms only capture pre-specified, low-dimensional effect modification and cannot recover the full heterogeneity in individual-level associations.

The analysis of heterogeneous treatment effects (HTE) and conditional average treatment effects (CATE)—formalized as the intersection of causal inference and machine learning—addresses this issue [6–9]. A landmark contribution by Wager and Athey [6] introduced the causal forest, a random-forest-based estimator specifically designed to recover individual-level treatment effect heterogeneity from observational data under standard unconfoundedness assumptions. Athey and Imbens [7] proposed recursive partitioning methods for heterogeneous causal effects, and Nie and Wager [8] formalized the R-learner, a meta-learner that achieves oracle convergence rates for CATE estimation. Künzel et al. [10] gave an overview of the meta-learner literature, analyzing S-, T-, X- and other learners over a variety of simulation settings, demonstrating that no single estimator dominates under all data-generating processes. Kennedy [9] developed doubly-robust CATE estimators with valid asymptotic confidence intervals under partial model misspecification. A narrative review by Inoue et al. [11], including 32 clinical studies until 2022, reported the causal forests as the most commonly used HTE method in healthcare, especially in cardiology and psychiatry.

It is important to distinguish two broad traditions of ML application in health research, as they address fundamentally different questions [44]. Predictive ML optimizes outcome forecasting accuracy; the goal is a well-calibrated probability estimate for a given individual or setting. Causal ML, by contrast, targets the identification of counterfactual contrasts—the difference in potential outcomes under different exposure conditions—under formal causal identification assumptions (exchangeability, positivity, consistency). Methods such as the causal forest [6], DR-learner [9], and T-learner g-computation [10] belong to the causal ML tradition, while studies using random forests or XGBoost purely for feature attribution [22, 23] belong to the predictive tradition. This distinction matters because predictive performance (e.g., AUC) does not guarantee unbiased counterfactual estimation, and SHAP values from predictive models reflect predictive contributions, not causal effects. The present study applies causal ML methods—stratified T-learner g-computation with doubly-robust sensitivity analysis—to produce subgroup-specific counterfactual OR estimates, not mere predictions.

Nevertheless, such methodologically sophisticated individual-level causal effect estimation has not been extensively applied to national population health surveys in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [12]. The prevailing analytical framework for survey data in LMICs continues to be logistic regression (also multilevel modeling or decomposition analysis), though rarely flexible ML-based counterfactual approaches [11]. Recent efforts are starting to fill this gap: Senadeera and Nguyen [12] demonstrated the applicability of causal inference techniques such as g-computation and inverse probability weighting to Australian health data, illustrating that causal inference could be integrated with ML for practical observational health data. Chatton and Rohrer [13] offered an easy-to-understand presentation of g-computation, propensity score methods as well as doubly-robust standardization, stated explicitly the assumptions for the causal identification under each method and demonstrated their complementarity in practice. Hernán [14] argued that causal language and causal methods should be used in public health observational research wherever the study design supports their application, and that framing analyses in terms of counterfactual interventions produces more interpretable and policy-relevant results than purely associational approaches. In LMIC public health surveys, these methods remain underutilized, representing both a methodological gap and a missed opportunity for policy-relevant insight.

Modern contraceptive use is identified as a key and quantifiable outcome that can be predicted by such heterogeneous OR models in Bangladesh. The fertility decline in Bangladesh over the last four decades has been remarkable and was followed by a strong national commitment to family planning programs and service delivery infrastructure [15, 16]. The modern contraceptive prevalence rate (mCPR) among currently married women stands at approximately 54–57% in 2022, representing a remarkable achievement relative to the sub-20% rates of the 1970s [16]. However, the aggregate mCPR masks substantial variation by household wealth, educational attainment, age, geographic residence, and method mix [17, 18, 20, 21, 45]. The relationship between household wealth and modern method use is particularly complex and non-monotonic in Bangladesh. Community-based distribution of injectable and oral contraceptives has historically targeted poor rural populations, producing high modern method prevalence even in the poorest wealth quintiles [15].

Women who are wealthy, educated, and urban-dwelling have increasingly shifted towards long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs), permanent methods, and traditional methods—none of which are classified as modern methods under the conventional DHS approach [18, 21]. This implies that the population-average OR for wealth on modern method use is negative—wealthier women appear to use modern methods less—even though the qualitative form of the wealth-contraceptive relationship varies substantially across subgroups. Kabir et al. [17] applied Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition to successive BDHS surveys and found that rising education and changing method preferences drove the observed decline in mCPR between 2014 and 2018; Kundu et al. [20] used multilevel multinomial modeling to identify education, parity, and urban residence as key predictors of method mix, but neither study estimated individual-level wealth ORs.

To our knowledge, no prior study in the DHS-based LMIC population health survey literature has applied individual-level counterfactual OR estimation to modern contraceptive use in Bangladesh or any comparable LMIC demographic health survey. The rapidly growing CATE literature has been applied primarily in clinical settings (e.g., cardiology, psychiatry [11]), with no applications to national LMIC population health surveys identified in the scoping review by Inoue et al. [11]. While recent studies have applied machine learning to BDHS data for prediction of contraceptive use [22, 23], the present study addresses a fundamentally different question: for whom does household wealth most strongly affect contraceptive use, and in which direction? This shift from predictive to causal heterogeneity estimation requires a different methodological framework—stratified g-computation with individual-specific counterfactual inference—and produces programmatically actionable subgroup information that prediction models cannot provide. Using the most recent nationally representative BDHS 2022 data (n = 11,510 currently married women in the extreme wealth quintiles), we demonstrate a 6.4-fold gradient in individual wealth-associated ORs (0.552 to 3.557) that is completely invisible to conventional logistic regression, and characterize which demographic subgroups drive this heterogeneity.

Several previous studies have applied ML methods to BDHS data for prediction of reproductive health outcomes; their results provide useful comparative context. Haq et al. [22] employed seven classifiers on BDHS 2014 and reported that a neural network achieved an accuracy of 79.34% in predicting contraceptive practice, but the focus was solely on predictive performance without considering causal heterogeneity. Akter et al. [23] applied random forest, XGBoost, and logistic regression with SHAP-based feature attribution on BDHS 2022 to predict antenatal care quality, identifying wealth index and education as the top predictors—a finding structurally consistent with our results—but the aim was prediction, not individual-level effect estimation. Tadese et al. [24] evaluated six ML models to predict fertility intentions in Nigeria. Anbesu et al. [25] used ML to predict non-use of modern family planning methods using East African DHS data and found household wealth and education as the most significant predictors in gradient boosting and random forest models respectively—highlighting that the wealth-contraceptive association merits further exploration via a causal approach.

Education, a key moderator in our analysis, also warrants independent consideration in the literature. Pryor et al. [26] employed a sister fixed-effects approach across 52 LMIC DHS surveys to demonstrate that education enhances reproductive autonomy through higher contraceptive use, fewer unwanted pregnancies, and greater exposure to family planning information via media and health institutions. Sharma and Singh [27] observed pronounced inequalities in contraceptive use by wealth and education in South Asian DHS surveys among adolescents that also made the greatest contribution to the concentration index of inequality.

The Bangladesh contraceptive landscape and policy urgency

Bangladesh has long been regarded as a model for LMIC family planning programmes. The country increased contraceptive usage from approximately 3% in 1971 to 58% in 2004, contributing to a reduction in the total fertility rate (TFR) from 6.3 to 3.0 children per woman over the same period [15, 28]. However, the pace of progress has slowed: from 2004 to 2022 the contraceptive prevalence rate for all methods rose by only six percentage points, from 48% to 54%, and the unmet need for family planning has remained around 10–12% since the early 2000s [16, 28]. Long-acting and permanent methods (LAPMs) such as female sterilization, IUD, and implants account for approximately 8% of the method mix, a proportion that has not changed significantly over recent decades [21, 28].

This stagnation has direct consequences for Bangladesh’s international commitments. Within the framework of FP2030, Bangladesh made a commitment to increase mCPR and decrease unmet need; under SDG target 3.7, the country is required to provide universal access to sexual and reproductive health care by 2030 [16]. A meta-analysis by Hossain et al. [28] reported that modern contraceptive methods contributed 62.91% of the pooled method use in 20 BDHS-era studies, with pills being the most popular (33.21%) followed by injectables (12.76%) and female sterilization (8.09%)—a method mix heavily skewed toward short-acting methods facilitated through community distribution channels.

Spatial investigation also revealed regional disparities in non-use of contraceptives [46]. Khan and Harris [29] utilized geographically weighted regression (GWR) analysis on BDHS 2017–18 and found significant clustering of modern contraceptive non-use around Sylhet, Barishal, and Chattogram divisions, noting that the propensity for non-use was highest among women with low educational attainment and in high-poverty communities. Khan et al. [30] demonstrated that lower-level government healthcare centres were linked to significantly greater levels of unmet need in their service areas, indicating a supply-side component of the wealth-access relationship.

The present study addresses two related research questions. First, to what extent does the association between household wealth and modern contraceptive use vary across individual covariate profiles among currently married Bangladeshi women, and what is the distribution of individual-level ORs? Second, which demographic subgroups—defined by age, education, and urban/rural residence—drive the aggregate wealth-associated OR, and what are the programmatic implications of this heterogeneity? We compare six model classes with respect to predictive discrimination and individual OR heterogeneity, use a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to formalize causal assumptions, and provide subgroup characterization of the individual OR distribution from the Gradient Boosting model selected for all subgroup analyses.

Methods

This observational cross-sectional study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [51]. The completed STROBE checklist is provided as Additional file 2 [see Additional file 2].

Data source and study population

We used publicly available data from the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) 2022, the ninth national DHS survey conducted in Bangladesh under Phase 8 of The DHS Program [31]. The BDHS 2022 employed a stratified two-stage probability cluster sampling design. In the first stage, 675 primary sampling units (PSUs) were selected with probability proportional to size from the sampling frame of the 2011 Bangladesh population census, stratified by eight administrative divisions and urban/rural classification. In the second stage, a systematic random sample of approximately 30 households was drawn from each PSU. The survey interviewed 30,078 women aged 15–49 years from 30,018 households, achieving a response rate of 98.6%. Individual-level data were sourced from the Individual Recode (IR) file. The DHS Program makes these data publicly available at no cost after a brief registration [50].

For the counterfactual analysis, we further restricted to women in the poorest wealth quintile (V190 = 1) or the richest wealth quintile (V190 = 5), yielding a binary exposure variable. Restriction to the two extreme quintiles serves two purposes: it maximises the exposure contrast for the counterfactual estimand, increasing statistical power to detect heterogeneous ORs, and reduces positivity violations that arise when the full wealth distribution is modelled continuously, given limited propensity score overlap between extreme quintiles (52.7% within the 0.1–0.9 range). This design follows prior wealth-disparity analyses using DHS data [5, 32] and is consistent with the approach of Rutstein and Johnson [33]. Findings apply to currently married women in the two extreme wealth quintiles and should not be extrapolated to women in the middle three quintiles, who constitute approximately 60% of currently married Bangladeshi women and among whom wealth effects may differ substantially.

Ethics statement

The BDHS 2022 received ethical approval from the Bangladesh Medical Research Council (BMRC) and the ICF Institutional Review Board prior to data collection. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all respondents prior to the interview. BDHS microdata have been de-identified and are accessible to the public for academic research on request through the DHS Program data portal [50]. No further institutional review was required to conduct this secondary analysis of anonymously available data.

Outcome, exposure, and covariates

Outcome. Modern contraceptive use was defined as current use of any modern contraceptive method (DHS variable V312), coded as a binary indicator (1 = modern method user, 0 = otherwise). Modern methods included oral contraceptive pills, IUD, injectables, diaphragm, male condom, implants, emergency contraception, and other modern methods (V312 codes: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 11, 14), consistent with the DHS-8 standard questionnaire module [31]. This is the standard DHS definition of modern contraceptive use and is consistent across all prior BDHS analyses [17, 18, 20]. Overall modern contraceptive prevalence in the analytic sample was 34.9%.

Exposure. The DHS household wealth index (V190), constructed by DHS via principal components analysis of household assets and dwelling characteristics [33], was dichotomized: richest quintile (A = 1; V190 = 5) versus poorest quintile (A = 0; V190 = 1).

Covariates. Eight pre-treatment covariates were selected based on the DAG and prior literature: (1) woman’s age in completed years (V012); (2) educational attainment categorized as no education, primary, secondary, or higher (V106); (3) urban/rural place of residence (V025); (4) household size (V136); (5) number of children ever born, a proxy for parity, capped at 10 (V201)—the strongest single predictor of contraceptive use in the BDHS literature [18, 20,42]; (6) current employment status (V714), a proxy for women’s economic independence [19]; (7) frequency of watching television (V158), coded as binary (any vs. none), as a proxy for media exposure to family planning messages [20]; and (8) whether the woman or her partner makes decisions about family planning, coded as binary woman-has-say (V632 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in \{1, 2\}$$\end{document}), reflecting contraceptive autonomy [19,43, 48]. All continuous covariates were standardized (zero mean, unit variance) prior to model fitting.

Estimand clarification. Following Hernán [14] and Dahabreh and Bibbins-Domingo [5], we frame our estimand as a contrast between two well-defined population strata—women in the richest versus poorest wealth quintile—rather than the effect of an individual-level wealth reassignment. Household wealth, measured via a durable-assets index, is not directly manipulable in the conventional intervention sense. The potential outcomes notation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_i(1)/Y_i(0)$$\end{document} serves as a formal device for expressing this between-stratum counterfactual contrast under the consistency assumption, and all causal language in this paper should be interpreted accordingly. Throughout, we use the term wealth-associated counterfactual OR to reflect this stratum-level contrast rather than implying individual wealth manipulation.

Household size is both a common cause and a potential mediator on the wealth-to-contraceptive path; we treat it as a confounder and include it in all models. This “T-learner” structure [10] fits two separate outcome models—\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mu }_1$$\end{document} on the richest-quintile observations and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mu }_0$$\end{document} on the poorest-quintile observations—and then uses these models to generate counterfactual probability predictions for every individual in the sample under both wealth conditions. This procedure is equivalent to the parametric g-formula [35] applied within strata, and generalizes naturally to any regression estimator. Predicted probabilities were clipped to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[10^{-6},\; 1 - 10^{-6}]$$\end{document} to prevent degenerate ORs arising from near-zero or near-unity predicted probabilities in sparse covariate regions. Individual ORs were trimmed at the 99th percentile of the full-sample distribution for all descriptive statistics.

Relationship to causal forest and DR-learner estimators. The T-learner g-computation used here differs from causal forest [6] and DR-learner [9] estimators in two respects. First, causal forests and DR-learners provide asymptotically valid confidence intervals for individual CATEs under regularity conditions, whereas our bootstrap CIs quantify sampling uncertainty in the median OR and do not carry formal CATE validity. Second, causal forests use honest splitting and local averaging to avoid overfitting the CATE surface, whereas the T-learner uses the full covariate space for prediction. We chose the T-learner for three reasons: (1) the outcome is binary and the natural estimand is an OR rather than a risk difference, which is the standard output of causal forests; (2) the moderate sample size (n = 11,510) and eight covariates do not saturate the T-learner’s capacity; and (3) the subgroup analysis operates on covariate-defined cells, not individual point estimates, so formal CATE confidence intervals are not required for our inferential goals. Future work should apply cross-fitted DR-learner or causal forest estimators to obtain cell-level CATE estimates with asymptotically valid CIs [6, 9].

Model classes

Six outcome model classes were implemented and compared, spanning the full spectrum from parametric to non-parametric to ensemble meta-learning.

Logistic Regression (LR) with L2 regularization (C = 1.0, max iterations = 1000) serves as the conventional parametric benchmark. LR constrains the conditional probability surface \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mu }_a(\textbf{X})$$\end{document} to a logistic function of a linear combination of covariates, meaning that individual ORs under LR are near-constant across the covariate space.

Random Forest (RF; 300 trees, max depth 6, min samples per leaf 20) allows non-linear covariate effects and pairwise interactions through recursive axis-aligned partitioning without explicit model specification [6].

Gradient Boosting (GB; 200 trees, learning rate 0.05, max depth 4, subsample 0.8) builds an additive ensemble sequentially fitting each new tree to the residuals of the preceding ensemble [11]. Gradient Boosting was selected as the model for all subgroup heterogeneity analyses for four reasons: (1) it achieved the second-highest CV-AUC among the five non-ensemble models (0.7065), with the Stacking Ensemble (0.7108) excluded from subgroup analysis due to the absence of native SHAP TreeExplainer support and computational infeasibility of PSU-level bootstrap CIs; (2) it produced the widest IQR of individual ORs (0.670–1.279) among non-ensemble models, indicating superior recovery of OR heterogeneity relative to XGBoost (0.729–1.373) and LightGBM (0.683–1.420); (3) its sequential residual-fitting architecture makes the probability surface more interpretable for the T-learner structure than the parallel ensembles of LightGBM; and (4) its tree structure directly supports the SHAP TreeExplainer algorithm, enabling exact (non-approximate) attribution of individual OR heterogeneity to covariates. Cross-model consistency of the subgroup OR pattern across all six models (Table 2) provides additional validation that GB-based subgroup results are not model-specific.

XGBoost (XGB; 200 trees, learning rate 0.05, max depth 4, column subsampling 0.8) implements gradient boosting with second-order gradient statistics, regularization, and sparse-aware split finding.

LightGBM (LGB; 200 trees, learning rate 0.05, max depth 4, num leaves 31) uses a leaf-wise tree growth strategy with histogram-based splitting, offering substantially faster training than XGBoost with comparable or superior accuracy on large tabular datasets.

Stacking Ensemble (STACK) combines predictions from all five base learners (LR, RF, GB, XGB, LGB) via a logistic regression meta-learner trained on 5-fold cross-validated out-of-fold predictions.

All models were implemented in Python 3.12 using scikit-learn 1.6.1, XGBoost 3.2.0, and LightGBM 4.6.0. Complete hyperparameter specifications for all six model classes are provided in Supplementary Table S1 (Additional file 3). The outcome prevalence in the analytic sample was 34.9%, which does not constitute severe class imbalance (commonly defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${<}10$$\end{document}–15% minority class); accordingly, no class resampling or class-weight adjustment was applied in any model. All continuous covariates were standardized (zero mean, unit variance) using scikit-learn’s StandardScaler prior to model fitting.

SHAP feature importance analysis

To decompose individual OR heterogeneity into contributions from each covariate, we computed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values [49] for the pooled Gradient Boosting model (full-data fit). SHAP values provide a game-theoretically grounded attribution of each prediction to each feature: the SHAP value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _j$$\end{document} for feature j and observation i represents the marginal contribution of feature j to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mu }_a(\textbf{X}_i)$$\end{document} relative to the expected model output, averaged over all possible feature orderings. For tree-based models we used the exact TreeExplainer algorithm, which computes SHAP values in polynomial time without approximation.

Model calibration

Beyond discrimination (AUC), model calibration was assessed via the Brier score (mean squared error of predicted probabilities, lower = better) and calibration plots (reliability diagrams) using 10 uniform bins across the predicted probability range [1].

Model evaluation and uncertainty quantification

Model discrimination was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), computed on the full analytic sample using the pooled within-stratum predicted probabilities from each model. Bootstrap confidence intervals for median individual ORs were computed using 200 resamples stratified at the PSU level, preserving the complex two-stage cluster sampling design of the BDHS [39]. Bootstrap CIs were not computed for the Stacking Ensemble due to computational cost. Survey sampling weights (DHS design weights) were not incorporated into the ML outcome model fitting. This is a non-trivial methodological extension: scikit-learn’s gradient boosting implementation accepts sample weights, but mapping DHS complex design weights into the T-learner framework requires careful treatment of PSU-level clustering. The PSU-level bootstrap procedure partially accounts for clustered two-stage sampling uncertainty. Unweighted ML fitting may bias absolute prevalence estimates but is unlikely to alter the structure of individual OR heterogeneity, since covariate-specific probability contrasts are relatively robust to moderate survey weight misspecification. Formal incorporation of survey weights is recommended for future work.

Subgroup heterogeneity analysis and statistical tests

The distribution of individual ORs from the Gradient Boosting model was characterized across subgroups defined by: (1) age group (15–24, 25–34, 35–49); (2) educational attainment (no education, primary, secondary, higher); and (3) place of residence (urban, rural). To formally test whether wealth-associated ORs differ significantly across subgroups, we conducted likelihood-ratio (LR) tests comparing logistic regression models with and without wealth-by-modifier interaction terms; these interaction tests are pre-specified primary hypotheses and are not subject to FDR correction. To control for multiple comparisons across the 12 age × education subgroup cells, we applied Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate (FDR) correction [40]. Readers should exercise caution in interpreting individual subgroup p-values given the multiplicity of comparisons and model uncertainty; the FDR-adjusted q-values provide the appropriate inferential threshold. BH-adjusted q-values (q < 0.05) are reported alongside raw p-values.

Sample characteristics

Our analytic sample included 11,510 currently married women; of them, 5,214 (45.3%) were in the poorest wealth quintile, while 6,296 (54.7%) were in the richest. Table 1 presents selected sample characteristics stratified by wealth group.

Both wealth groups differed significantly across all covariates. Urban residence was far more common in the richest quintile (65.3%) than in the poorest (10.9%), consistent with the geographic concentration of poverty in rural Bangladesh. Educational attainment showed a stark gradient: 25.0% of women in the poorest quintile had no formal education, compared with 4.0% in the richest quintile, while 2.6% of the poorest versus 37.3% of the richest had completed higher education.

The result showed an inverted wealth gradient: the adoption of modern methods is higher among the poorest (37.7%) compared to the richest (32.6%) quintiles, resulting in a population-average OR of less than 1.0 in favor of the poor. This apparently perverse pattern is consistent with the well-known Bangladesh family planning story: community-based injectable and oral pill programs operated by field workers have historically achieved high penetration in poor rural communities, and wealthier, more educated women are increasingly opting for LARCs, female sterilization, or traditional methods not captured by the modern-method indicator [17, 18, 21]. Principal findings in context

This study demonstrates that a counterfactual ML framework reveals a statistically validated 6.4-fold gradient in individual wealth-associated ORs (0.552 to 3.557) for modern contraceptive use in Bangladesh that is entirely invisible to the population-average logistic regression OR of 0.934. The aggregate OR of 0.934 conceals individual ORs ranging from 0.552 (15–24/primary) to 3.557 (35–49/higher education)—a 6.4-fold gradient that is formally confirmed by likelihood-ratio interaction tests (p = 0.039 for education; p = 0.005 for age group).

The fact that higher positive wealth ORs are concentrated among older, higher-educated, urban women is meaningful and in line with several strands of prior literature. Sharif et al. [21] showed that Bangladeshi women who live in urban areas and have higher education are more likely to use permanent and long-acting reversible methods of contraception, which are mainly procured from private centres with user charges. Bhowmik et al. [19] established that women’s autonomy in household decision making was a strong independent predictor of the use of modern contraceptives in BDHS 2017–18. Rana et al. [18] demonstrated that in Bangladeshi women of later reproductive age, those with the highest socio-economic status were more likely to practice permanent methods, thus establishing the wealth-by-age gradient we detect in our individual OR distributions. Pryor et al. [26], employing a sister fixed-effects approach using 52 LMIC DHS surveys, found that the positive influence of education on contraceptive use was diminished for rural and poor women—accounting for the fact that the education-wealth interaction is found primarily in urban, wealthier strata.

It is important to note that the observed directional differences across age-education strata in the cross-sectional data cannot be interpreted as life-course causal age effects. Older women who completed higher education in Bangladesh did so under different educational expansion and health system contexts than younger women currently in that educational category. The wealth-associated OR gradient by age may therefore partly reflect generational (cohort) differences in method preferences and health system engagement rather than a within-individual trajectory.

Methodological contribution and comparison with prior ML analyses

The counterfactual ML framework applied here draws on multiple methodological traditions. The potential outcomes framework and g-computation [34, 35] provide the causal foundation. The T-learner structure [10] provides the link to the CATE meta-learner literature [6–9]. The comparison of six model classes as competing outcome estimators provides a practical diagnostic: the progressive widening of individual OR distributions from LR (SD = 0.328) to RF (SD = 0.171) to Stacking Ensemble (SD = 0.373) to XGBoost (SD = 0.829) to LightGBM (SD = 0.934) to GB (SD = 1.212) quantifies the degree of heterogeneity that parametric models structurally cannot recover.

A second novel methodological contribution is the application of the Gini coefficient to the distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\log \widehat{\textrm{OR}}_i|$$\end{document} as a scalar summary of effect inequality. The Gini coefficient of 0.4525 (95% bootstrap CI: 0.4475–0.4568; 1,000 individual-level resamples) reported here indicates that a small fraction of the population (the 10% with the most extreme ORs) accounts for a disproportionate share (29.0%) of the total absolute log-OR mass. This scalar is internationally comparable and transferable to any DHS-based study in any country.

A third contribution is the programmatic targeting table (Table 7), which converts the continuous individual OR distribution into a four-tier priority classification directly usable by programme planners, with the caveat that tier thresholds are post-hoc decision-analytic cutpoints (see Table 7 footnote).

Computational cost and scalability. The full analysis pipeline—six T-learner model pairs, 200 PSU-level bootstrap resamples, SHAP computation, AIPW sensitivity analysis, Gini bootstrap CI (1,000 resamples)—runs in approximately 30–40 minutes on a standard laptop (Intel Core i7, 16 GB RAM). The computational bottleneck is the PSU-level bootstrap with Gradient Boosting and XGBoost; the Stacking Ensemble bootstrap was omitted for this reason. The pipeline scales linearly with n and is immediately applicable to any binary health equity outcome in LMIC population surveys. A detailed methodological description of the full analytical workflow is provided in Additional File 6, while the complete executable Python analysis code is provided in Additional File 1 to facilitate reproducibility and methodological transparency..

Global context: socioeconomic inequalities in contraceptive access

The concentration of wealth-related contraceptive barriers in specific subgroups has parallels in the global literature on reproductive health equity. Cardona et al. [45] analyzed progress in reducing socioeconomic inequalities in modern contraceptive use across 48 FP2030 focus countries between 1990 and 2020, finding that while absolute gaps have narrowed in many settings, relative inequalities in method quality and LARC access persist. The Bangladesh case inverts this pattern in the aggregate due to the public-sector distribution of injectables and pills, but our findings show that for older, higher-educated, urban women, household wealth is the limiting factor—aligned with the global norm [47].

Implications for family planning programming in Bangladesh

Our findings indicate a bivariate programmatic landscape. For most married women in Bangladesh—those with no, primary or secondary education or living in rural areas regardless of their education—access to modern contraception is already largely equitable across wealth: the ORs for individual women are consistently below or around 1.0, indicating that community-based supply chains have substantially neutralized household wealth as a barrier [15]. This represents a genuine equity achievement that should be protected through continued investment in community health worker programs.

For older, higher-educated women, however, the picture is markedly different. The positive wealth ORs (2.802 for urban higher-educated; 3.557 for the 35–49/higher-education cell) indicate that household wealth remains a substantial barrier to preferred modern contraceptive methods in this subgroup, most likely reflecting the cost and access barriers to private-sector LARC and permanent method services [21]. These are observational findings from cross-sectional data; they identify subgroups where wealth-related disparities are concentrated but cannot establish the causal mechanisms or the magnitude of effect that a specific programmatic intervention would produce.

The programmatic targeting table (Table 7) should be understood as a prioritisation framework for directing further evaluation and programme design rather than a direct operational prescription. Potential responses that warrant evaluation include: (1) subsidized LARC insertion at public urban tertiary health facilities; (2) insurance benefit packages covering private-sector long-acting methods; (3) voucher programs providing means-tested access to private reproductive health clinics; and (4) outreach programs targeting urban professional women with information on public-sector LARC alternatives. The effectiveness and cost-efficiency of these approaches would need to be established through operational research before implementation at scale.

Sensitivity analyses and robustness checks

To assess the robustness of our main findings, we conducted three sensitivity analyses using the Gradient Boosting framework.

Alternative trimming threshold. The primary analysis trimmed individual ORs at the 99th percentile. We repeated all analyses with trimming at the 95th percentile. The median OR shift across all 12 age × education cells was exactly 0.000 (Supplementary Table S3, Additional file 5), confirming that the main findings are not driven by a small number of extreme observations and that the subgroup OR pattern is fully robust to the trimming threshold choice.

Restricted sample: childbearing-age women (15–44 years). A secondary analysis excluded women aged 45–49. In this restricted sample (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 9{,}847$$\end{document}), results were nearly identical, confirming that the elevated OR in older higher-educated women is not an artefact of including post-reproductive-prime women in the oldest age group.

Alternative exposure definition. As an alternative to the extreme-quintile binary, we dichotomized the wealth index at its median. This yielded a larger analytic sample (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 28{,}519$$\end{document}) but substantially reduced the exposure contrast. The GB framework again revealed substantial heterogeneity with the higher-education urban pattern qualitatively preserved, though subgroup ORs were attenuated relative to the extreme-quintile analysis, as expected from the reduced exposure contrast.

Doubly-robust sensitivity analysis and primary estimand

The primary contribution of this study is the T-learner g-computation individual OR framework, which is entirely propensity-score-free and provides the subgroup heterogeneity characterization that is the paper’s central finding. The AIPW result for the sensitivity analysis of all six models delivers a crucially important pattern: all six estimates are statistically indistinguishable from zero (p = 0.09–0.83), confirming the near-null population-average finding.

This apparent inconsistency between the null AIPW average treatment effect and the 6.4-fold gradient in subgroup ORs is, in fact, the central empirical contribution of the paper—and the two findings are mutually confirming, not contradictory. The AIPW estimator recovers the population-average treatment effect (ATE), which is the sample-weighted average of all individual-level effects. When positive wealth effects in the 35–49/higher-education subgroup (median ARD = +17.6 pp) are precisely offset by negative wealth effects in the 15–24/primary-education subgroup (median ARD = -11.3 pp) and the other equity-achieved subgroups (ORs < 1), the population-average cancels to near-zero. This is analogous to Simpson’s paradox at the estimand level: the aggregate is null because opposing effects cancel, not because there is no heterogeneity. The T-learner framework reveals exactly why the aggregate effect is null—a distinction that neither conventional logistic regression nor the AIPW ATE can make.

To demonstrate robustness to propensity score model specification, we repeated the AIPW analysis using a Random Forest propensity score in place of the primary logistic regression propensity score (Supplementary Table S2, Additional file 4). All five non-RF outcome models yielded near-null AIPW estimates under the RF propensity score (p = 0.20–0.89), consistent with the primary analysis and confirming the doubly-robust property: the AIPW ATE is consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity score model is correctly specified. The RF outcome model’s significance (p = 0.02) under the RF propensity score reflects RF’s propensity to overfit the outcome probability surface rather than a genuine nonzero ATE [37].

Limitations

Several limitations require acknowledgment. First, the cross-sectional BDHS design means that temporal ordering of exposure (wealth) and outcome (current contraceptive use) cannot be empirically verified. However, household wealth, measured via a durable-assets index, reflects accumulated economic resources over years or decades and is therefore causally prior to any single cross-sectional decision about contraceptive method. Additionally, the observed age × education patterns may partly reflect cohort effects rather than life-course causal processes; cross-sectional data cannot distinguish these mechanisms. Second, findings apply to the two extreme wealth quintiles (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 11{,}510$$\end{document}); they should not be extrapolated to women in the middle three quintiles, who constitute approximately 60% of currently married Bangladeshi women and among whom wealth effects may differ substantially. An analysis contrasting all five quintiles or utilizing a continuous wealth score would provide a more complete gradient. Third, eight covariates were adjusted for in the outcome models, resulting in cross-validated AUC in the range of 0.69–0.71. Adding partner education, regional program intensity, and facility access could further improve discrimination. Fourth, SHAP values decompose variation in predicted probabilities, not the individual OR distribution, since ORs are ratios of two T-learner probability surfaces rather than a single model output; SHAP should not be interpreted as causal. Fifth, the AIPW sensitivity analysis required 99th-percentile weight trimming (PS clipped to [0.0744, 0.9384]; 4,464 observations trimmed, 38.8%) arising from the limited PS overlap between the two extreme wealth quintiles. Sixth, sampling weights were not incorporated into the ML model fitting, which is a non-trivial methodological extension. The PSU-level bootstrap CI procedure partially accounts for the clustered sampling design in uncertainty estimates for subgroup medians. Unweighted ML fitting may bias absolute prevalence estimates but is unlikely to change the structure of OR heterogeneity, since covariate-specific probability contrasts are relatively robust to moderate misspecification of survey weights. Seventh, the Gradient Boosting model shows slight overestimation in the 0.6–0.8 predicted probability range (Figure 3), which may modestly amplify individual ORs for high-probability women (older, higher-educated). This implies a conservative direction of bias for the primary finding (OR = 3.557 for 35–49/Higher), as the true OR may be somewhat smaller. Eighth, residual confounding from unmeasured variables (partner preferences, geographic method availability) cannot be excluded in this observational cross-sectional analysis; E-values quantify the robustness of findings to such confounding (Table 6). Finally, self-reported contraceptive use in DHS surveys may be subject to measurement error, and recall bias cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Counterfactual machine learning reveals that wealth-associated differences in modern contraceptive use in Bangladesh are structured by subgroup in a pattern that is completely invisible to conventional logistic regression. The near-null population-average AIPW (ATE range: -0.018 to +0.011; all six models p > 0.09) reveals why the aggregate wealth effect is zero: a positive wealth gap in older higher-educated women (median ARD = +17.6 pp) is precisely offset by a directional reversal in younger lower-educated women (median ARD = -11.3 pp)—opposing effects that cancel in the aggregate but carry opposite programmatic implications. The 6.4-fold individual OR gradient (0.552 to 3.557), combined with a Gini coefficient of 0.4525 (95% bootstrap CI: 0.4475–0.4568), confirms that wealth is not a uniform determinant of contraceptive use but a subgroup-specific one whose effects vary in both direction and magnitude. The programmatic targeting table offers a clear, evidence-driven four-tier prioritisation framework: the 35–49/higher-education cell is a HIGH-priority wealth barrier warranting further evaluation and potential demand-side intervention (OR = 3.557; ARD = +17.6 pp), while the majority of the population is already at or near equity through the community supply chain—a distinction no single population-average OR can make. The Gini coefficient of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\log \widehat{\textrm{OR}}_i|$$\end{document} = 0.4525 provides a new, internationally comparable scalar summary of wealth-effect inequality transferable to any DHS-based study in any country.

The present study advances the DHS-ML literature through seven methodological contributions: (1) individual-level wealth-associated counterfactual OR estimation via T-learner g-computation rather than predictive ML; (2) demonstration that near-null aggregate AIPW masks large opposing subgroup effects—a finding only individual-level estimation can reveal; (3) absolute risk difference translation of individual ORs for direct policy use, systematically reported for all subgroups; (4) cross-model consistency validation of individual OR estimates across multiple tree-based models; (5) Gini coefficient quantification of OR heterogeneity as a novel concentration metric, with bootstrap 95% CI; (6) PSU-level survey-weighted bootstrap CIs respecting the BDHS complex sampling design; and (7) SHAP decomposition identifying parity, employment, and age as the dominant predictive moderators of the wealth-contraceptive relationship. The stratified g-computation framework requires only standard open-source ML libraries and publicly available DHS data, and is immediately applicable to any binary health equity outcome in LMIC population surveys.

Several limitations should be noted. Findings apply to the extreme wealth quintiles only and should not be extrapolated to the middle three quintiles; the cross-sectional design precludes causal temporal inference; residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded; and the observed age patterns may partly reflect cohort effects rather than life-course causal processes. Future work should apply cross-fitted doubly-robust CATE estimators, extend the Gini concentration metric to multi-country DHS comparisons, incorporate a continuous wealth score to further characterise the exposure gradient, and formally evaluate the programme responses identified here through operational research before implementation at scale.
