Rethinking Readmissions in Advanced Heart Failure: Insights From Left Ventricular Assist Device and Heart Transplant Care
Authors: Nicholas R. Hess, Xander Jacquemyn, David J. Kaczorowski
Published: 2026

Heart transplantation remains the definitive therapy for select patients with end‐stage heart failure, offering substantial improvements in survival and quality of life. 1 Over the past several decades, advances in surgical techniques, perioperative management, immunosuppressive therapy, and mechanical circulatory support, including left ventricular assist devices (LVADs), have transformed outcomes for patients with end‐stage heart failure, including those ineligible for transplant. 2 , 3 Despite these advances, patients with advanced heart failure continue to experience a substantial burden of illness, complex health care needs, and frequent hospital readmissions. These findings reflect the intricate demands of advanced heart failure management and the social, economic, and psychosocial vulnerabilities inherent to this population. 4

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Mogga et al. critically evaluated one of these key components of the burden of illness—hospital readmissions—among patients with advanced heart failure, including those supported with LVADs and those post heart transplantation, in the context of the post‐2018 heart allocation policy. 5 Using a large, contemporary cohort from the Nationwide Readmissions Database, the authors examine 30‐ and 90‐day readmissions, stratifying outcomes by therapeutic strategy and assessing patient‐, device‐ and transplant‐related factors that contribute to early rehospitalization. This focus is particularly timely, as the current allocation system has reduced transplant priority for durable LVAD recipients, coinciding with declining LVAD use and increasing reliance on temporary mechanical circulatory support as a bridge to transplantation—developments that have reshaped clinical decision‐making and revived debate regarding the optimal timing and role of LVAD versus heart transplantation. 3 The authors report several important and clinically intuitive findings (Figure). Compared with heart transplantation, patients receiving an LVAD experienced significantly higher in‐hospital mortality (12.7% versus 5.5%) and a greater burden of complications related to anticoagulation, including gastrointestinal bleeding and thromboembolic events, as well as a higher incidence of acute myocardial infarction. These findings are consistent with existing registry data. 6 , 7 Discharge disposition also differed meaningfully between groups, with LVAD recipients substantially less likely to be discharged home and more likely to require postacute institutional care. From a resource use standpoint, LVAD implantation was associated with a higher index hospitalization cost ($237 443), representing an excess of $22 093 compared with heart transplantation. After discharge, when patients return to the realities of outpatient management and chronic care, distinct patterns emerged. LVAD recipients were more likely to experience rehospitalization within 30 days (27.9% versus 25%) and, when readmitted, had longer hospital stays, averaging one additional day compared with transplant recipients. These differences persisted at 90 days, with LVAD patients again demonstrating higher rehospitalization rates (45% versus 39.3%) and longer lengths of stay. Importantly, the drivers of readmission diverged by therapy. Among LVAD recipients, readmissions were most commonly attributable to heart failure exacerbations, device‐related complications, and bleeding events, whereas heart transplant recipients were more frequently rehospitalized for transplant‐related complications. These patterns are well documented in the literature, 8 , 9 although it remains uncertain whether these arise from intrinsic differences between treatment strategies, differences in patient selection, or a combination of both factors.

Despite higher readmission frequency, LVAD‐associated readmissions were less costly on a per‐episode basis, with lower median costs at both the first ($11 736, [$4870 less than transplantation]) and second ($11 680, [$3551 less than transplantation]) readmissions. Nevertheless, the higher upfront cost of LVAD implantation meant that cumulative costs continued to favor heart transplantation, 10 even after multiple rehospitalizations, such that LVAD recipients would require ~5 readmissions to approach cost equivalence. Notably, these findings were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses, including propensity score‐matched models. It is important to recognize that LVAD therapy and heart transplantation serve distinct therapeutic roles, with fundamentally different goals, patient selection criteria, and care pathways. Accordingly, although these findings provide valuable insights for patient counseling, health system planning, and policy discussions, they should not be used to guide individual clinical decision‐making. 11

From a clinical standpoint, these findings emphasize that readmissions following advanced heart failure therapies are not uniform events but instead reflect fundamentally different care trajectories. 11 Patients supported with LVADs experience frequent rehospitalizations driven by chronic device management, anticoagulation‐related complications, and the persistent physiology of advanced heart failure. In contrast, heart transplant recipients are more commonly rehospitalized for immunologic, infectious, or graft‐related complications that are often episodic but high acuity. 8 , 12 These divergent patterns underscore that readmission frequency alone is an incomplete, and potentially misleading, marker of quality. Without accounting for clinical context, severity, and underlying pathophysiology, readmission metrics risk oversimplifying complex posttherapy care and conflating expected, manageable events with preventable adverse outcomes. 13 Beyond clinical and biologic considerations, readmissions serve as a powerful lens through which to examine social vulnerability and the adequacy of the current landscape of care infrastructure. LVAD therapy, in particular, shifts a substantial portion of disease management from the inpatient setting to patients, caregivers, and outpatient systems. Discharge to home, although often viewed as a favorable outcome, does not necessarily equate to readiness, particularly for patients requiring meticulous device care, anticoagulation management, and frequent monitoring. Readmissions in this context frequently reflect gaps in caregiver support, health literacy, access to specialized heart failure and LVAD centers, and the capacity of outpatient systems to absorb the complexity of care. 14 These challenges are further amplified by racial, socioeconomic, and geographic disparities. 15 , 16 Framed this way, readmissions should be viewed not as patient failure, but as a systems‐level stress test, exposing areas existing models of care fall short in supporting some of the most medically and socially vulnerable patients.

As mentioned earlier, these findings are representative of the post‐2018 allocation era. It may be that readmission metrics that fail to distinguish between expected, manageable rehospitalizations and high‐acuity complications and risk mischaracterizing both quality and value in advanced heart failure care. 13 This concern is especially salient as policy and reimbursement frameworks increasingly equate readmission reduction with quality of care. In heart failure more broadly, hospital readmissions are already deeply embedded within national performance metrics, most notably through the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program, which imposes financial penalties on hospitals for excess 30‐day risk‐standardized unplanned readmissions for conditions including heart failure. As articulated by Goodhart's law “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” Moving forward, the goal should not be fewer readmissions at any cost, but better metrics that reflect the realities of advanced heart failure care. 17 Future efforts should prioritize differentiating readmissions by type, acuity, and preventability, while integrating patient‐centered outcomes that more meaningfully reflect quality of life and functional recovery. Metrics such as “Days Alive and Out of the Hospital” may offer a more nuanced assessment of patient well‐being and care effectiveness. 18 , 19 Moreover, investments in transitional care programs, caregiver education, home‐based support, and remote monitoring will be essential to reducing avoidable rehospitalizations while preserving access to life‐saving therapies. 20 Just as important, policy frameworks must align with the biology of disease and the lived experiences of patients and caregivers. Ultimately, improving outcomes after LVAD implantation and heart transplantation will require moving beyond readmission counts toward care models and policies that recognize biologic complexity, social vulnerability, and the realities of life after advanced heart failure therapy. 20
