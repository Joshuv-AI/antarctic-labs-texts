Alkaline phosphatase-activated prodrug system based on a bifunctional CuO NP tandem nanoenzyme for on-demand bacterial inactivation and wound disinfection
Authors: Quan-Quan Zhuang, Zhi-Shan Zhang, Ting-Jin Zheng, Lin-Yan Lu, Meng-Ting Lin, Jia-Lin Yang, Hao-Hua Deng, Ying-Ying Xu, Wei Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanozymes are promising antimicrobials, as they produce reactive oxygen species (ROS). However, the intrinsic lack of selectivity of ROS in distinguishing normal flora from pathogenic bacteria deprives nanozymes of the necessary selectivities of ideal antimicrobials. Herein, we exploit the physiological conditions of bacteria (high alkaline phosphatase (ALP) expression) using a novel CuO nanoparticle (NP) nanoenzyme system to initiate an ALP-activated ROS prodrug system for use in the on-demand precision killing of bacteria. The prodrug strategy involves using 2-phospho-L-ascorbic acid trisodium salt (AAP) that catalyzes the ALP in pathogenic bacteria to generate ascorbic acid (AA), which is converted by the CuO NPs, with intrinsic ascorbate oxidase- and peroxidase-like activities, to produce ROS. Notably, the prodrug system selectively kills Escherichia coli (pathogenic bacteria), with minimal influence on Staphylococcus hominis (non-pathogenic bacteria) due to their different levels of ALP expression. Compared to the CuO NPs/AA system, which generally depletes ROS during storage, CuO NPs/AAP exhibits a significantly higher stability without affecting its antibacterial activity. Furthermore, a rat model is used to indicate the applicability of the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel in wound disinfection in vivo with negligible side effects. Introduction

Bacterial infection is a major global threat to public health and continues to worsen due to the rapid evolution of pathogenic bacteria, which may generate antibiotic resistance. However, the numbers of novel antibiotic types and antibiotics approved by regulatory agencies have been decreasing since the 1980s [1]. The dwindling number of novel antibacterial agents reaching the clinic is projected to result in antibiotic-resistant pathogens killing 10 million people annually by 2050 [2]. Therefore, developing alternative novel antibacterial agents is critical.

Instead of modifying currently marketed antibiotics or synthesizing novel antibiotics, which may result in new resistances, researchers are exploring various nanomaterials, such as Ag, Au, or ZnO nanoparticles (NPs) [3–5] or graphene [6], as antibacterial alternatives with the development of nanotechnology. Novel approach using hybrid nanostructure material had also been studied [7]. These nanomaterial-based antibacterial agents, with large surface areas, unique electronic properties, and high stabilities, are distinct from antibiotics and other bactericides [8]. However, the antibacterial applications of these nanomaterials are considerably limited because of their potential cytotoxicities, complicated processing, and high costs [9]. In the human immune system, after the phagocytosis of bacteria by neutrophils, myeloperoxidase from azurophil granules may catalyze the conversion of H2O2 to highly reactive oxygen species (ROS) to attack the membranes of microbes. Inspired by natural self-defense systems, considerable attention has been focused on employing enzymes to irreversibly damage bacteria [10]. However, the practical utilization of natural enzymes presents constant challenges in terms of high preparation cost, purification, and long-term stability. Therefore, materials that mimic enzyme functionalities, such as V2O5 [11], single-atom nanozymes [12], NSP-co-doped carbon quantum dots [13], and ferromagnetic NPs [14], could be employed as sustainable, green, cost-effective agents to aid H2O2 in generating ROS and killing bacteria. Using nanozymes may be a promising strategy to produce ROS with higher antibacterial activities. However, the use of H2O2 presents several disadvantages, including tissue damage and chemical instability [15]. Previously, we designed a green substrate that did not require toxic H2O2 to produce ROS [16]. Furthermore, despite their advantages, nanozymes are generally toxic to pathogenic and non-pathogenic bacteria, lacking the selectivities necessary for ideal antimicrobials due to the intrinsic lack of selectivity of ROS toward pathogenic bacteria, normal flora, or healthy cells. Clearly, rendering nanozymes preferentially active against pathogenic bacteria over non-pathogenic bacteria or healthy cells is highly attractive but remains a significant challenge.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is expressed in the periplasmic membrane, which is external to the cell membrane of a pathogenic bacterium (e.g., Escherichia coli (E. coli)) [17]. ALP hydrolyzes the phosphomonoesters of numerous organic molecules, such as phosphate esters and anhydrides of phosphoric acid. The identification of alkaline phosphatase produced by bacteria is typically used to rapidly differentiate these organisms [18]. In this study, we fabricated an ALP-activated prodrug system based on a bifunctional CuO NP tandem nanozyme using the greener substrate 2-phospho-L-ascorbic acid trisodium salt (AAP) for the on-demand treatment of bacterial infection. The stable AAP can be catalyzed in the presence of ALP to produce ascorbic acid (AA), which is further converted by the CuO NPs with intrinsic ascorbate oxidase- and peroxidase-like activities to ·OH (Scheme 1). Consequently, the effective radius of action of the toxic ·OH is limited (< 200 nm) [19], suggesting that the nanozyme may preferentially kill ALP-expressing bacteria over other cells if it generates ·OH in situ. Herein, the therapeutic precisions of the bifunctional tandem nanozyme against different levels of ALP-expressing bacteria were evaluated. Additionally, we examined the stability of the CuO NPs/AAP system and investigated a fibrin gel based on this system for use in wound healing with minimal adverse effects. AAP combined with CuO NPs should be an ideal antimicrobial prodrug system for combating bacterial infection.

Method

In vitro antimicrobial performance

E. coli cells (ATCC 25922) were grown in a Luria-Bertani (LB) medium at 35 ℃ to the mid-exponential phase. Thereafter, 400 µL of the bacterial suspension (1.0 × 108 cfu mL–1, cfu = colony forming units) was incubated in LB broth, then the bacterial solution was mixed with CuO NPs (0.3 µg mL–1), AAP (1.7 mg mL–1), or CuO NPs (0.3 µg mL–1)/AAP (1.7 mg mL–1) in a tube, with water used as the control group. The bacteria were incubated at 35 °C for 20 h, following which the solution was diluted 1.0 × 105-fold with sterile NaCl (0.9%). The bacterial dilution (100 µL) was spread onto a blood agar plate, and the number of cfu was counted after incubation at 35 °C for 16 h. Bacterial morphological characterization

E. coli cells were collected via centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 2 min after the antimicrobial study and then washed three times with regular saline. Thereafter, bacterial samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 h. Subsequently, the fixed samples were dehydrated with ethanol solutions with sequential concentrations of 20%, 50%, 80%, and 100% for 10 min each. Finally, the dried samples were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) after coating with gold (~ 2.5 nm). SEM was conducted using a Tecnai G2 F20 instrument (FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA) operating at 200 kV [21].

Live/dead fluorescent staining

A bacterial culture in the logarithmic phase was diluted using sterile NaCl (0.9%) to yield a value of 0.5 on the McFarland scale, with a bacterial concentration of 1.0 × 108 cfu mL− 1. The bacteria were then allotted into two groups, namely, water and CuO NPs/AAP groups. After 20 h, 5 µL of Syto 9 fluorescent dye (10 µmol L–1)/propidium iodide (PI, 0.3 mg mL–1) was added to the bacterial suspension and incubated in darkness for 15 min. Finally, the bacterial suspensions were harvested via centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 2 min and then resuspended in sterile NaCl (0.9%), followed by imaging using fluorescence microscopy (DMi8, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). In the fluorescence images, bacteria stained with Syto 9 display green fluorescence (excitation/emission wavelengths: 539/570–620 nm), whereas those stained with PI exhibit red fluorescence (excitation/emission wavelengths: 470/490–540 nm) [22].

Determination of DNA degradation

After 20 h of water or CuO NPs/AAP treatment, genomic DNA from E. coli was first collected and processed using a Tiangen Bacterial Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotechnology, Beijing, China). The extracted DNA was then identified using ethidium bromide-stained agarose gel electrophoresis (1%) [23].

In vivo anti-infective therapy

All animal studies were in accordance with the guidelines of the research ethics committee of Quanzhou Medical College (2020012). Eighteen Sprague Dawley rats (male, 180–200 g) were anesthetized with 2% pentobarbital sodium and their backs were shaved, where 1.5 × 1.5 cm full-thickness skin wounds were created on their dorsum skin via excision. The wounded rats were then randomly divided into three groups: control, CuO NPs/AAP@fibrin gel, and ceftriaxone (cef) groups. The wounds were treated with 200 µL of E. coli suspension containing ~ 108 cfu to establish the infection models. After incubation on the defined skin wounds for 24 h, 200 µL of water, CuO NPs/AAP@fibrin gel (containing 3.0 µg mL–1 of CuO NPs and 17 mg mL–1 of AAP), or cef (20 µg mL–1) was smeared evenly on the wounds daily. At Day 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14, photographs of the wounds of the rats from three different groups were collected, and the wound conditions were recorded perpendicularly (d1 ≤ d2) using a digital caliper. The residual wound area was calculated by the following formula: residual wound area (%) = πd1d2/4. The rate of variation in the wound area was calculated to evaluate wound healing ((current wound area/initial wound area) × 100%) [24]. The infected wounds were homogenized with 100 µL water, following which the plate counting method was employed to calculate the number of bacteria after 20 h of culturing at 35 ℃ (bacteria survival rate = NE/NC × 100%, where NC and NE respectively represent the numbers of colonies of the control and treated groups, respectively) [25].

Histopathologic analysis

The wounds with adjacent regular rat skins were collected at Day 14 and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for the staining of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson trichrome (MT). Moreover, the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were also stained with H&E to investigate the biocompatibility of the CuO NPs/AAP@fibrin gel in vivo. In addition, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and CD68 were used in immunohistochemical staining, and CD31 was assessed using immunofluorescence staining. The results of histochemical analysis were further quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) [26].

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the bifunctional CuO NP nanozyme.

The ultraviolet-visible absorption peak of the synthesized CuO NP sample, as shown in Fig. S1A, is observed at ~ 280 nm, which is identical to that previously reported [27]. The inset shows an image of the brown CuO NP solution under daylight. Figure 1A shows the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM images of the CuO NPs, and the lattice fringes with d spacings of 0.23 nm correspond to the crystal planes of monoclinic CuO (002). Meanwhile, the average diameter of the CuO NPs is 6.21 ± 1.87 nm (Fig. 1B). The X-ray photoelectron (XP) spectrum is shown in Fig. 1C, with the high-resolution peaks representing Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 located at binding energies of 933.7 and 959.7 eV, respectively. The shake-up satellite peaks at 943.4 and 940.7 eV assigned to Cu2+ species are also observed, and the peak at 962.3 eV further confirms the presence of the CuO NPs [28]. In our previous research, we report that CuO NPs can catalyze the oxidation of AA in the presence of O2 to produce H2O2 and ·OH via their intrinsic ascorbate oxidase mimetic and peroxidase-like activities [27, 29]. However, AA is unstable, which may hinder the application of the NPs in the fields of biosensing and -therapy [30]. Hence, AAP is used as a more stable substrate to facilitate the practical applications of CuO NPs. AA may be produced via the ALP-mediated hydrolysis of AAP, and we further estimated the effect of the dual enzyme-like properties of the CuO NPs based on the enzymatic hydrolysis of the green substrate AAP by ALP. The ROS generation capacity of the system was determined when CuO NPs were mixed with AAP and ALP, verified by electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy. Using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) as an ·OH scavenger, the generation of a peak in the ESR spectrum confirms that the CuO NP-AAP-ALP system-induced ROS is ·OH [31]. In the absence of ALP, the signal representing DMPO-OH is not observed in the CuO NP-AAP group (Fig. 1D). As shown in Fig. 1E, the generated ROS were further identified using terephthalic acid (TA), which could be used as a specific fluorescence dosimeter for ·OH. The resulting ·OH could react with TA, a non-fluorescent molecule, to form highly fluorescent hydroxyterephthalate and produce strong yellow-green fluorescence with two emission maxima at approximately 440 and 550 nm (λex = 321 nm) [32]. In addition, L-phenylalanine was employed as an ALP inhibitor to confirm the mechanism of the CuO NP-AAP-ALP system [33]. The fluorescence intensity clearly decreases due to the presence of L-phenylalanine in the CuO NP-AAP-ALP system compared to that observed without L-phenylalanine (Fig. 1F). Inspired by prodrug chemistry, we prepared an ALP-activated targeted system for use in the on-demand treatment of bacterial infection.

Antibacterial activity of the CuO NPs/AAP system

The excellent one-pot cascade catalysis effects of the CuO NP material inspired us to explore its antimicrobial performance, which is consistent with the very highly expressed ALP of pathogenic bacteria (e.g., E. coli) [34]. Hence, we used E. coli as a model microorganism and highlighted the functionality of CuO NPs/AAP as a prodrug system in bacterial inactivation. AAP (0.1–2.3 mg mL–1) and CuO NPs (0.08–0.7 µg mL–1) both had weak antibacterial effects on E. coli (Fig. S2A and S2B). In the presence of AAP, CuO NPs significantly reduced the VRs of E. coli and exhibited AAP-dependent antibacterial ability (Fig. S2B). As shown in Fig. 2A, numerous colonies of E. coli are observed after incubation with water, CuO NP, and AAP. Remarkably, the number of colonies on the blood agar plate clearly decreases following CuO NP/AAP treatment. As displayed in Fig. 2B, when the concentrations of CuO NPs and AAP are 0.3 µg mL–1 and 1.7 mg mL–1, respectively, the respective calculated VRs of E. coli are 89.8% and 81.3%. After combination of the CuO NPs with AAP, the survival rate of the bacteria decreases to approximately 10%. The morphology of E. coli after incubation with CuO NPs/AAP was further examined by SEM. Untreated E. coli cells show a rod-like morphology with a smooth and intact membrane structure, whereas the surfaces of the bacteria collapse and fuse after incubation with CuO NPs/AAP (Fig. 2C and D). In addition, Syto 9 and PI staining confirms the destructive effect of the CuO NPs/AAP system on the bacterial membrane, where Syto 9 may enter living and dead cells to produce green emission, whereas PI may only enter the dead cells to produce red emission [35]. Compared to those of the control group, the red PI signals of the treated groups are clear, indicating that membrane permeability increases significantly (Fig. 3A and B). A quantification of the dead bacteria is shown in Fig. 3C, obtained by analysis of the red-field and fluorescence microscopy images acquired during different treatments. The PI-stained death rates of E. coli after incubation with water and CuO NPs/AAP are 2.3% and 79.1%, respectively. The results indicate that the bacterial cell membrane was severely damaged after exposure to CuO NPs/AAP. Protein leakage assays were also conducted to further analyze these mechanisms. As shown in Fig. 3D, the Bradford assay indicates an increased protein level in the cell supernatant treated with CuO NPs/AAP. This signifies the damage to the cell membranes of E. coli, resulting in the release of protein into the supernatant. The data is consistent with the results of SEM and fluorescence analysis, further suggesting bacterial cell death due to membrane disruption. Additionally, DNA degradation of E. coli is initiated following treatment with CuO NPs/AAP, whereas the genomic DNA remains stable in the control group (Fig. 3E).

ALP-responsive, stable CuO NPs/AAP system for use in the selective treatment of bacteria in vitro

With unique ALP-responsive properties, we constructed an ALP-activated ROS prodrug system based on a CuO NP nanoenzyme platform and investigated its targeted antibacterial application in vitro. Regular healthy skin is colonized by normal flora, such as Staphylococcus hominis (S. hominis), which compete for nutrients and space, thereby preventing the colonization of opportunistic pathogens. However, traditional broad-spectrum antibacterial agents generally nonspecifically kill these bacteria. As a proof-of-concept study, S. hominis was used as a low-ALP-expressing bacterium to verify the selective targeting of the CuO NPs/AAP system [36]. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, upon incubation of CuO NPs/AAP with E. coli, a notable bactericidal activity (~ 90%) is observed, whereas no bacterial killing of S. hominis is observed. The results suggest that the effective targeting capacity of the CuO NPs/AAP system towards high-ALP-expressing bacteria may significantly enhance its antibacterial efficacy by increasing the concentration of ·OH around the bacterial surface and reducing the damage to low-ALP-expressing bacteria or cells. To highlight the remarkable advantages of the CuO NPs/AAP system, the antimicrobial stabilities of the CuO NP mixture with AAP and AA were compared. Figure 4C reveals that the antimicrobial property of the CuO NPs/AA system gradually decreases from 100 to 71.9% from Day 1 to 7. Conversely, the antibacterial property of the CuO NPs/AAP system changes negligibly during the same period. This indicates the poor stability of the CuO NPs/AA mixture, which generally results in the depletion of ROS during storage [37].

Cytotoxicity and hemolysis assays of the CuO NPs/AAP system in vitro

If CuO NPs/AAP is considered as a potential targeting system, the evaluation of its potential toxicity against normal cells is critical. Thus, we first assessed the appropriate cytotoxicities of CuO NPs/AAP in cultures of normal human immortal keratinocyte (HaCaT) and rat myocardial (H9c2) cell lines using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide viability assay. Our in vitro cytotoxicity studies indicate that the cell viabilities of HaCaT and H9c2 are significantly reduced by 34.2% and 30.6%, respectively (Fig. S3A and 3B), after 20 h of exposure to CuO NPs/AA, which is attributed to ROS-mediated nonselective cytotoxicity towards non-targeted cells. However, no clear toxic effects in HaCaT or H9c2 cells (cell viabilities of > 70%) are exhibited by CuO NPs/AAP [38], confirming its potential in vivo applications. An in vitro hemolytic assay was also conducted to explore the interactions between CuO NPs/AAP and erythrocytes to estimate the safety of this system. After contact with blood for 3 h, no clear hemolytic phenomena are observed in the treated group (Fig. S3C). These results indicate the good biocompatibility of the CuO NPs/AAP system.

In situ formation of the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel and its antibacterial activity in vitro.

Fibrin gels are of increasing interest due to their unique merits, including excellent biocompatibilities and bacterial adhesion capacities. Therefore, a convenient spray method was established to promote wound healing by creating a temporary shield to connect and protect injured tissue. According to the method used in a previous study [39], fibrin gel is rapidly formed by simultaneously spraying and mixing equal volumes of solutions containing fibrinogen with CuO NPs or thrombin with AAP (Fig. S4A). The SEM image shown in Fig. S4B reveals that the fibrin gel containing the CuO NPs/AAP composites displays an irregular, porous network structure. To assess the release of CuO NPs and AAP from this fibrinogen platform, the UV-vis absorption spectrum was employed to quantify the collective CuO NPs and AAP diffusion rate, revealing that 51% of CuO NPs and 66% of AAP were released within 180 min. (Fig. S5). Two different methods were employed to evaluate the bacterial inhibition activity of the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel. A contact inhibition method was initially used. After pathogen contact with the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel for 8 h, no colonies form on the LB agar plate (Fig. S4C). Another method of evaluating the antibacterial activity was the co-incubation of the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel with pathogens in LB media. The CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel exhibits a superior bactericidal effect (90%), which is significantly higher than that in the control group, illustrating the considerable antibacterial activity of CuO NPs/AAP fibrin against E. coli (Fig. S4D).

In vivo wound healing using the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel

To evaluate the antibacterial efficiency of CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel in wound sterilization, we established the bacterial infection model and performed treatment as shown in Fig. 5A. An overall view of wound healing after treatment with the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel for 3, 7, 10, and 14 d is displayed. The wounds of rats treated with the antibacterial fibrin gel exhibit significantly accelerated closure after 3 d. After 14 d of treatment, the areas of the wounds of the rats treated with CuO NPs/AAP and cef are much smaller than those of the control group, indicating the efficiency of CuO NPs/AAP in accelerating wound regeneration (Fig. 5B). The CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel and cef groups display 63.1% and 61.3% wound closure, respectively, on Day 5, which are distinctly higher than that of the control group (48.0%, Fig. 5D). The survival of E. coli in the wounded tissues after the different treatments was evaluated at Days 0, 7, and 14. As shown in Fig. 5C and E, the survival rates of E. coli are clearly reduced in the CuO NPs/AAP- and cef-treated groups and far lower than that of the control group. Trauma hemorrhages are common clinical emergencies worldwide, and fibrin gels exhibit good blood-clotting properties [40]. Therefore, we also evaluated the hemostatic properties of the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel in vivo. First, we evaluated hemostasis using a rat-tail amputation model. The hemostatic process is shown in Fig. 5F (inset), and blood loss is reduced after application of the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel. Notably, the blood loss in the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel group (3.2 ± 2.1 mg) is approximately 1/7 of that in the control group (22.7 ± 0.9 mg, Fig. 5F). This result suggests that the remarkable hemostatic capacity of CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel renders it suitable for wound dressing applications.

Histopathologic analysis and in vivo toxicity evaluation

The healing of a skin wound is a continuous, complicated process. To further elucidate the effect of the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel on the microenvironment of a skin wound, typical pathological sections of each group were obtained after therapy and stained by H&E as well as Masson trichrome. As shown in Fig. 6A, the control group exhibits more inflammatory (orange arrow) and neutrophil (yellow arrow) infiltrations: red cells (red arrow) can also be observed in the wound area. In contrast, the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel and cef groups display minimal neutrophil infiltrations and increased numbers of new blood vessels (green arrow) with higher proportions of collagen fibers (blue arrow). In addition, the skin tissue in the control group only regenerates immature granulation tissues without epithelization or with disrupted epithelization, whereas continuous epithelization and skin appendage (black arrow) regeneration are observed in the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel and cef groups (Fig. 6A and C). In addition, MT staining was used on tissue sections to inspect the deposition of collagen during healing. Compared with the control group, the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel and cef groups display more collagen fibers with deeper colors and denser arrangements (Fig. 6B). Moreover, the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel and cef groups exhibit collagen depositions of 66.9% and 66.2%, respectively, which are 1.39- and 1.38-fold higher than that of the control group (48.1%), respectively, indicating their high therapeutic efficiencies in wound healing (Fig. 6D) [41]. Meanwhile, no clear inflammation, abnormality, or damage is observed in the H&E staining of the main organs of the rats, further indicating the good biosafety of the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel in vivo (Fig. S6).

We then investigated the mechanism of the promotion of wound healing and confirmed the favorable immune regulation of the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel. The pro-inflammation factor TNF-α and respective tissue macrophage surface and vascular endothelial-specific markers CD68 and CD31 were used as indicators in evaluating wound healing mediated by the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel. Compared to those of the control group, the results of immunohistochemical staining reveal that the expressions of CD68 and TNF-α in wounds treated using the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel or cef are significantly reduced (Fig. 7A − D). Therefore, the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel may inhibit excessive inflammation during wound healing. Finally, immunohistochemical staining of CD31 was performed to study the neovascularization of wound tissue. Relative to that in the control group, increased neovascularization is observed using the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel or cef, according to fluorescent microscopy, which confirms that the formation of new blood vessels is accelerated by the CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel (Fig. 7E and F). In combination, the results indicate that our novel CuO NPs/AAP fibrin gel may inhibit inflammation, stimulate new blood vessel formation, and enhance collagen deposition in vivo, thereby exhibiting an excellent therapeutic efficacy during wound healing.

Conclusion

In this study, an ALP-activated prodrug system based on a bifunctional cupric oxide nanoenzyme platform was prepared for use in the on-demand precision killing of bacteria. This highly effective antimicrbial system was demonstrated to degrade bacterial membranes and destroy DNA structures. In addition, compared to the CuO NPs/AA system, the CuO NPs/AAP system was superior, as demonstrated by its constant ·OH generation and subsequent antimicrobial property. In contrast to ROS-based traditional system, AAP was highly tolerant to CuO NPs, which could not directly catalyze the substrate to produce ·OH, as they did with AA. Meanwhile, CuO NPs/AAP remained harmless toward low-ALP-expressing bacteria and healthy cells. In addition, the fibrin gel fabricated using the CuO NP nanoenzyme, AAP, fibrinogen, and thrombin was biocompatible and could regulate anti-inflammatory and hemostatic cytokines and growth factors. It could also further accelerate infected wound healing in vivo, with negligible side effects towards major organs. In the future, continuous study should be conducted on improving the activity of bacterial ALP to enhance the antibacterial efficiency. In conclusion, our proposed prodrug strategy provides a suitable method of resolving the contradiction of instability and premature activation of the conventional CuO NPs/AA system, and reveals the potential of utilizing ALP as an initiator in developing on-demand antibacterial agents.
