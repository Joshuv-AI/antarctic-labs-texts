Comparative evaluation of feed intake and blood metabolites in goats fed different vetch varieties
Authors: NH Msiza, KE Ravhuhali, HK Mokoboki, S Mavengahama, GM Chelopo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
One way to evaluate the value of a feed is by comparing the actual intake based on its nutritional value. Annual legumes are considered significant protein supplements to ruminants. The objective of this study was to assess the feed intake, and blood parameters of goats offered different vetch varieties. Each of the five male Boer goats was provided with four feeding troughs, containing four different feeds (Cenchrus ciliaris grass hay) and three legumes (Vicia sativa - Common vetch, Volga vetch, and Vicia villosa Roth - Haymaker plus vetch). Vetch varieties were also analysed for chemical composition. The feeds were used as treatments, with goats as replicates in a completely randomised design (CRD). For blood metabolites, blood samples were collected from each of the five male Boer goats at the commencement and the end of the experiment. Haymaker plus vetch had the highest (P < 0.05) CP, K, Cu, Zn, Mn, total phenols and condensed tannins with the lowest (P < 0.05) ADL. Common and Volga vetch exhibited the highest (P < 0.05) intake. Cholesterol, lymphocytes, neutrophils, globulin, and phosphorus levels were higher (P < 0.05) in goats exposed to vetch varieties. Given the high crude protein observed in all vetch varieties, these forages may serve as a protein supplement for low roughage grasses, potentially enhancing the productivity of these ruminants. Due to lower intake observed on the tanniferous Haymaker plus vetch, an amelioration strategy can be introduced to enhance its feeding value. Further metabolic experiments can be conducted to determine the optimal inclusion level of vetch forages.

Introduction

Goats, as part of the Capra aegagrus hircus family, are primarily utilised for a range of needs (milk, meat, and skin) by various cultural groups. This positively contributes to improving livelihoods, as it provides food security and socio-economic relief to resource-poor households that rely on rearing goats (Hawu et al., 2022). These ruminants can thrive well under the most extreme conditions, which also endows them with desirable traits such as hardiness and adaptability. Goats can flourish in ecological zones that are typically unsuitable for cattle grazing, as feed resources in tropical, subtropical, and temperate environments are highly limited in both quality and quantity. Sheridan et al. (2003) found that the differences among ruminants in terms of energy requirements and digestive efficiencies are a significant criterion for selecting the most appropriate type of animal to be kept in a particular environment.

The primary hurdle in livestock production within sub-Saharan Africa stems from the insufficient supply of forage, particularly during the dry season (Anele et al., 2011). Variations in weather patterns, inadequate management of available feed, and the impact of climate change collectively led to significant fluctuations in the quantity and quality of forage between seasons and years (Sultan et al., 2008). During the dry season, farmers face the challenge of either migrating in search of pasture or purchasing feeds, primarily hay, from commercial farms (Lawal- Adebowale et al., 2018; Bontsa et al., 2024). However, the hay available in tropical regions is typically of poor quality and does not fulfil the basic nutritional needs of animals when used as the sole feed source (Kubkomawa et al., 2015). Among the lacking nutrients during this period, protein and energy are the most critical, with protein often being the limiting factor. Thus, there is a pressing need to explore alternative sources to supplement the inadequate pasture quality in the dry season. Leguminous plants emerge as a promising option.

According to Cook et al. (2005), Vetch species are grown in all climatic conditions (tropical, subtropical and temperate). Vetch species are known for tolerating any climatic condition, and they can survive well at temperatures below 0 °C (Sanchez-Gutierrez et al., 2020). It has been proven that this species can improve the productivity of small ruminants in most developed countries when used as a supplement to low-quality roughages (Berhane & Eik, 2006; Rihawi et al., 2010). These legume species are not only known for providing crude protein to livestock; they may also enhance soil qualities, which can contribute to improving resources for livestock efficiency.

However, ruminants tend to develop preferences for feed that satisfies their hunger quickly (Ginane et al., 2015). Literature has highlighted several factors influencing dietary choices. Among these, forage palatability, diversity, availability, nutrient composition, and antinutritional properties are key determinants of dietary choice and intake (Jarrige et al., 1995; Hawu et al., 2022). Mueller-Harvey (2006) stressed that the high concentration of CTs in the diet can reduce DM intake through binding proteins, reducing their availability, and inhibiting digestive enzymes; and this can have a negative effect on the health of ruminants (McAllister et al., 2020). Goats can tolerate and benefit from moderate levels of bioactive compounds in the diets, and this is normally influenced by their adaptation, through the production of saliva with tannin-binding proteins and having gut microflora that can handle tannins (Min & Solaiman, 2018; Schmitt et al., 2020; Nascimento et al., 2021). However, Besharati et al. (2022) emphasised that the CT above 50 g/kg DM can lead to decreased DM intake. Apart from bioactive compounds, Sebata and Ndlovu (2010) alluded that the morphological structure (leaf size and accessibility) of the foliage can influence its acceptability by goats, with clustered-leaf plants being favoured over large-leafed plants. McDowell (1972) highlighted that a high leaf-to-stem ratio can contribute to forage quality, and most ruminants prefer plants with a high proportion of leaves, which may indicate high-quality forages that lead to improved nutrient intake and digestibility. This may warrant the need to assess the morphological attributes of the vetch variety leaves. The amount and balance of nutrients in ruminant diets have been known to significantly influence animal health and blood profiles. Research demonstrates that balanced dietary nutrient efficiency can positively impact blood parameters (Uemura et al., 2014; Mohammed et al., 2016; Tiwari et al., 2021). As indicated by Shingfield et al. (2002), forage is known to influence blood profile due to its effect on rumen fermentation, which may alter blood metabolites including glucose, minerals and protein levels. Chase (2011) concluded that forage can positively influence animal health by providing essential nutrients, which have health benefits such as improving digestive health and enhancing livestock production potential.

The impact of hedonic feeding behaviour, which is related to pleasure and enjoyment, may be especially significant in situations where animals have choices or for those with low productivity. One way to evaluate the hedonic value of a feed is by comparing the actual intake with the predicted intake based on its nutritional value (Distel et al., 1994). Therefore, the study aimed to determine the intake and blood metabolites of vetch varieties (Common and Volga vetch, and Haymaker plus vetch) offered to goats. The study hypothesised that significant nutritive value and secondary metabolites would influence the DM intake and blood metabolites in goats fed vetch varieties.

Materials and methods

Study site

The field study was carried out at the North-West University Research Experimental Farm (Molelwane) in Mafikeng, North West province, South Africa (S 25 88′’ 00′, E 25 63′’ 33′, at an altitude of 1500 m above sea level). The prevailing temperatures vary from 7 °C to 11 °C during the winter season and 22 °C to 34 °C throughout the summer season. The area receives an average annual rainfall ranging from 200 to 571 mm annually, with most of the rainfall occuring during the summer season. Planting took place during the summer 2021/2 planting season.

Planting, harvesting, and processing

Three legume varieties, Vicia sativa (Common vetch, Volga vetch) and Vicia villosa Roth (Haymaker plus vetch), were used in this study. The seeds were sourced from Barenbrug (Pretoria, South Africa) and cultivated under uniform agronomic practices. The experimental site featured soil profiles characteristic of Clovelly and Hutton forms. Each variety was grown in a monoculture system in three plots, which serve as replicates, making a total of nine (9) plots. The vetch leaves were harvested prior to flowering, cut at 10 cm above ground level, and shade-dried for approximately three weeks until dry. Cenchrus ciliaris grass hay was outsourced from the North-West University experiential Farm and was chopped into small pieces of 20–30 mm.

Animals, housing, experimental design, and feeding

A total of five weaner goats (weight 16 ± 2 kg) were used in this experiment. The weaner goats were housed individually in 1.7 m2 pens with a roof and half-walled shed with individual feeders, and each animal had access to a mineral block ad libitum. For each goat, four feed troughs were placed in each pen, with each trough containing C. ciliaris, Common vetch, Volga vetch, and Haymaker plus vetch leaves. Each animal was offered all the feeds simultaneously. These feeds were used as treatments, and the goats were used as replicates in a completely randomised design (CRD). Before the commencement of the feeding experiment, all goats were treated against internal parasites. The animals underwent 14 days of adaptation to confinement feeding before the start of the experiment, of which the data collection period lasted seven days, totalling 21 days. Each of the animals was offered 300 g of grass and an additional 300 g of each vetch hay (four troughs) as outlined by Mokoboki et al. (2010), Ravhuhali (2010), and Brown et al. (2016). The amounts for each feed were adjusted daily to maintain a 10 - 15 % refusal rate (Kaitho et al., 1996). The order of placement of feeds in the troughs was randomly changed daily to avoid habit reflex. Water was provided ad libitum using a rubber bucket. Chemical composition of the forages

About 1 g of each sample from 9 plots was placed into pre-weighed crucibles and then placed in an oven set at 105°C for 12 to estimate the dry matter (DM). The loss in weight was measured as moisture content, and DM was calculated as the difference between the initial sample weight and moisture content. Organic matter (OM) content was determined by ashing the dried samples in a muffle furnace set at 600 °C for 6 h, and the loss in weight was measured as the OM content. Total nitrogen content was determined following the standard macro Kjeldahl method (AOAC, 2005, method no 984.13) and was converted to crude protein (CP) by multiplying the percentage of the N content by a factor of 6.25 and expressed in g/kg DM. Neutral detergent fibre (NDF) and acid detergent fibre (ADF) were determined using ANKOM2000Fibre Analyser (M/s ANKOM Technology, New York), according to van Soest et al. (1991). A heat-stable bacterial α-amylase was used for the NDF analysis. Acid detergent lignin (ADL) was determined by treating ADF residue in ANKOM F57 bags with 72 % sulphuric acid. The chemical composition of C. ciliaris hay grass was as follows: DM (915.94 g/kg), Ash (145.65 g/kg DM), CP (20.50 g/kg DM), NDF (774.82 g/kg DM), ADF (425.88 g/kg DM) and ADL (50.89 g/kg DM).

The vetch and grass samples were assessed for mineral composition at the Animal Health Center laboratory using dry ashing techniques for both macro and trace minerals, in accordance with the method outlined by the Agri-Laboratory Association of Southern Africa (AgriLasa, 1998). Samples previously used for dry matter (DM) determination were subjected to incineration in a muffle furnace for 12 h. The resulting ash was weighed and digested in a Microwave Reaction System Model 3000 by adding 1 mL of 55 % nitric acid and 10 mL of 32 % hydrochloric acid into each sample containing vessel. Following a 45-minute digestion period, the samples were cooled, transferred into 100 mL volumetric flasks, diluted with distilled water, and left undisturbed for 24 h to allow sedimentation. After settling, the supernatant was carefully decanted into McCartney bottles, avoiding disturbance of the sediment. Concentrations of phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), and manganese (Mn) were determined using an Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometer (PerkinElmer, NexION 300Q). The mineral content of grass (Cenchrus ciliaris) hay offered were as follows: P (0.152 % DM), K (2.198 % DM), Ca (0.446 % DM), Mg (0.16 % DM), Na (356.2 mg/kg DM), Fe (491.64 mg/kg DM), Cu (91.94 mg/kg DM), Zn (20.32 mg/kg DM), Mn (41.056 mg/kg DM).

Condensed tannins and total phenols

As described by Makkar (2003), to determine the condensed tannins, the Acetone extract was transferred into a test tube, with added butanol-HCl (3 mL) reagent. The heat block was used to heat the covered test tube to 100 °C and later left to cool at room temperature. The spectrophotometer at 750 nm wavelength was used to measure the absorbance. To determine total phenols, the Folin-Ciocalteau method, as defined by Makkar (2003), was used. The aliquot of tannin (0.01 mL) with extracts containing an aliquot of tannin (0.01 mL) was pipetted into a test tube (0.49 mL) of distilled water. Thereafter, a 0.25 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 1.25 mL of sodium carbonate solution were added. The tubes were vortexed and left to stand at room temperature for at least 40 min. The spectrophotometer at 750 nm wavelength was used to measure the absorbance. In order to predict the concentration of total phenols (Tannic acid in dry matter %), the standard curve was used.

Blood parameters analysis

At the beginning (day 0) and end of the experiment (day 21), blood samples from each goat were withdrawn from the jugular vein via puncture using hypodermic syringes. A 5 mL blood sample was collected into labelled sterile bottles containing ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) as an anticoagulant for determining haematological parameters. Tubes containing blood samples were sealed and stored at a temperature of -20 °C before being taken to the laboratory for immediate analysis using an IDEXX Laser Cyte Haematology analyser (IDEXX Laboratories, South Africa). Parameters measured included white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), Haematocrite (HCT), haemoglobin (HGB), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), and red blood distribution width (RDW). Additional blood samples (2 mL) were collected into anticoagulant-free bottles, allowed to coagulate at room temperature, and then centrifuged at 1500 rotations per minute (rpm) for 10 min. This process allowed for the separation of serum from other blood components. Serum biochemical parameters were analysed for urea, glucose, creatinine, cholesterol, total proteins, albumin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), phosphorus, and calcium concentrations using an automated spectrophotometer.

Statistical analysis

A one-way ANOVA in general linear model (GLM) procedure of SAS (2010) was used to test the chemical composition, feed intake and blood metabolites. The following model was used:Yij=μ+Si+Eij

Where;

Yij = response variable; µ = population mean; Si = the effect of vetch varieties; Eij = random error associated with observation ij, statistically significant differences were declared at P < 0.05.

Results

Chemical composition and feed intake of forages

Dry matter, NDF, ADF, Ca and S contents did not differ significantly among the vetch varieties. However, significant differences were observed for ash, ADL and CP values, where the lowest (P = 0.0004) ADL and highest (P = 0.0022) CP were observed in Haymaker plus vetch (Table 1). Volga vetch had the highest (P = 0.0178) P content when compared to the Common vetch and Haymaker plus vetch. Haymaker plus vetch was significantly higher in Cu and Mn concentrations when compared to other vetches, which also differ significantly from each other. The Mg and Fe values were lower (P < 0.05) in the Haymaker plus vetch with no significant differences observed for Common vetch and Volga vetch varieties. Haymaker plus vetch was significantly higher on condensed tannins (63.23 g/kg DM) when compared to Volga vetch (36.29 g/kg DM) and Common vetch (41.49 g/kg DM). Haymaker plus vetch had higher (P < 0.0001) concentrations of total phenols (2.50 g/kg DM) when compared to Volga vetch (1.72 g/kg DM) and Common vetch (1.42 g/kg DM). Discussion

Chemical composition

The current study highlights significant variations in the nutritive value parameters among vetch varieties, corroborating previous statements that adaptability, agroecology, species, and agronomic practices significantly influence forage quality (Kassahun & Wasihun, 2015; Msiza et al., 2021). Crude protein (CP) concentrations ranged from 226.53 to 273.93 g/kg DM, exceeding the minimum (110 g/kg DM) requirement for optimal rumen microbial activity (Pathak, 2008). These values were marginally lower than Akdeniz et al. (2018) yet higher than those documented by Georgieva et al. (2016) confirming vetch as a valuable protein source. Haymaker plus vetch, showed slightly higher CP content (> 200 g/kg DM), aligning with reports that hairy vetches generally offer better quality forage (Vasiljević et al., 2009). In this study, ash content was the lowest in Common vetch (122.43 - 134.35 g/kg DM), but higher than values reported by Semman et al. (2018). These differences among the vetch plants are possibly due to morphological fractions (Zemene & Sisay, 2025), genetic composition and mineral absorption capacity (Sokupa et al., 2024).

Low fiber fractions (NDF and ADF) were expected due to the high leaf: stem proportion, as leaves generally contain fewer cell wall fractions compared to stems. None of the studied varieties (353.59 – 381.29 g/kg DM) exceeded the 600 g/kg DM NDF threshold at which ruminant intake and digestibility are severely affected (Brown et al., 2018). Acid detergent fibre values (227.45 - 246.97 g/kg DM) were lower than those reported by Jabessa et al. (2020), thus indicating a desirable low value because it gives more chance for ruminants to have better feed conversion efficiency to induce higher digestibility of forages (Abdullah et al., 2017; Aung et al., 2019). Lower fiber concentrations among vetch varieties may promote microbial colonization and fermentation in the rumen, thereby improving feed digestibility and intake. Acid detergent lignin (ADL) values (39.37 – 51.33 g/kg DM) were below the critical levels, indicating enhanced digestibility (Chelopo et al., 2023). These values were consistent with those reported by Tassew et al. (2022), who found the ADL content ranged from 46 to 77 g/kg DM in different V. sativa varieties. Importantly, all vetch varieties in the present study had ADL concentrations below the 310 g/kg DM threshold, associated with severe digestibility constraints, therefore highlighting their potential as a good-quality roughage source, as reported by Belete et al. (2024). Generally, vetch varieties are promising as high-quality protein supplements for ruminants due to their high crude protein content and relatively lower fiber content, making them a valuable nutritional resource to enhance livestock productivity in animals fed on low-quality roughage.

Macro and micro minerals

Phosphorus (P) concentrations ranged from 5.91 to 7.11 g/kg DM, with Volga vetch exhibiting the highest value, which supassed the minimum dietary requirement of 2 – 4 g/kg DM recommended for goats (MSD manual, 2025). This suggests that this forage can adequately support and even exceed phosphorus-dependent functions such as bone development, energy metabolism, and rumen microbial activity. Calcium concentrations in Vicia varieties ranged from 9.13 - 9.41 g/kg DM, which were above the critical level of 3 g/kg DM recommended for ruminants (Mbukwane, 2017). Calcium is essential for bone formation, muscle contraction, nerve transmission, and blood clotting in goats (MSD manual, 2025). Considering the Ca:P ratio (1.3:1 to 1.6:1) which was slightly below the ideal 2:1 ratio recommended for goats (Agustin et al., 2024). Although this falls within an acceptable range, the prolonged feeding of forages with a low Ca:P ratio may increase the risk of urinary calculi and Ca imbalance, particularly in bucks. Therefore, maintaining a balanced diet mineral profile is essential to prevent metabolic disorders. Magnesium concentrations (3.44 - 4.25 g/kg DM) were below the recommended amount of 40–100 g/kg DM required for goats (NRC, 1981), indicating a potential risk of deficiency. Magnesium is vital for neuromuscular function, enzyme activation and energy metabolism. Low dietary Mg, especially when coupled with high potassium intake, can impair absorption and lead to hypomagnesaemic tetany, a potentially fatal condition in ruminants (Schonewille, 2013).

Potassium levels in the vetch varieties ranged between 5.85 – 7.11 g/kg DM, with Volga vetch recording the highest concentration. These values are slightly higher than those reported by Öten et al. (2024) for V. sativa (4.2 g/kg DM). While potassium is essential for osmotic regulation, nerve function, and enzyme activation, high K levels can interfere with Mg absorption, potentially leading to hypomagnesaemia tetany in ruminants (Römheld & Kirkby, 2010). The copper concentrations (5.99–7.53 mg/kg DM) were within the recommended range of 4–10 mg/kg DM for goats (NRC, 1981), indicating an adequate dietary supply. This mineral supports red blood cell formation, immune function, and antioxidant defence (López-Alonso & Miranda, 2020; Wang et al., 2025). The observed values suggest that the vetch can meet the goats' copper requirements without posing any toxicity risks. Zinc (Zn) concentrations (43.18 – 67.25 mg/kg DM) exceeded the upper limit (17.43 – 50.0 mg/kg DM) for ruminants (Das et al., 2018). While zinc is important for enzyme activity, immune function, and tissue repair, excessive intake may lead to lameness and joint issues (Kincaid et al., 1976; Fagari-nobijari et al., 2012). Manganese (Mn) concentrations (37.81 –54.47 mg/kg DM) were within the recommended range (20.0–77.7 mg/kg DM) (Singh et al., 2020; Tenikecier et al., 2020). Thus, the observed levels depict that vetch can adequately meet the Mn requirements of goats, with minimal risk of deficiency. The Fe concentrations (377.45 - 610.67 mg/kg DM) were within the recommended range of 50 – 1000 mg/kg DM for small ruminants (NRC, 2007). Iron is essential for oxygen transport, enzyme function and immune response, primarily through its role in haemoglobin, myoglobin, and cytochromes (Gowda et al., 2024).

Vetch varieties from the current study exhibited varying levels of essential minerals, with K and Zn exceeding recommended levels that require monitoring to prevent potential toxicity, especially with prolonged feeding. Minerals such as Ca, Cu, Mn and Fe were present at levels that adequately meet the nutritional requirements of goats. In contrast, Mg concentrations were below recommended thresholds, indicating a potential risk of deficiency and the need for dietary supplementation.

Dry matter intake of vetch varieties fed to goats

The dry matter intake reflects the actual amount of feedstuff consumed by animals. This information is essential as it provides a measure of the specific diet level taken by an animal for rumen fill, regardless of forage nutritive value. In the current study, DM intake ranged between 67.47 and 240.64 g/day for different forages fed to goats, with Common vetch and Volga vetch being the highest. This is normally influenced by several factors, including nutritional value, bioactive compounds, morphological traits, and succulence (Mokoboki et al., 2010; Raufirad et al., 2016). Indeed, forage with high fiber and lower CP, as grass, will have lower DM intake (Hawu et al., 2022). According to Mueller-Harvey (2006) and McSweeney et al. (1999), the CTs can reduce DM intake through binding proteins and reducing their availability for utilization, while Waterman and Mole (1994) stressed that tannins' ability to reduce the substrates' digestibility may also depend on the structure and chemical nature of the tannins. Indeed, Common and Volga vetch had CT levels below the threshold (41.49 g/kg DM and 36.29 g/kg DM, respectively), resulting in higher DM intake. Ribeiro da Silva et al. (2021) and Besharati et al. (2022) emphasized that the CT levels above 50 g/kg DM can lead to decreased DM intake, as observed in this trial, where Haymaker plus vetch, despite its high CP and lower ADL content, was less preferred when compared to other varieties. This supports previous findings that high CT concentrations negatively impact feed acceptance by animals. Although Common vetch and Volga vetch had lower CP content compared to Haymaker plus vetch, the CP content of these varieties was still above the recommended level for maintenance and for the rumen microbes to function effectively, promoting better nutrient intake and digestibility. Pathak (2008) stressed that a crude protein level of 11 % in the diet is sufficient for optimal microbial growth. In general, the high voluntary intake of vetch by goats suggests that these forages have the potential to be used as a feed for ruminant animals

Blood profiles of goats fed vetch forages

Blood profiles provide a significant basis for assessing the health and nutritional status of animals. The levels of blood urea measured in this study were higher (53.60 - 55.20 mmol/L) and did not align with the reference range (5.6–7.69 mmol/L) provided by Merck, 2016. This is attributed to the excess dietary protein from vetch, which resulted in increased urea concentrations due to enhanced ruminal ammonia production and subsequent hepatic urea synthesis. The total blood protein (TP) levels (2.36 g/l - 2.32 g/l) were below the normal range (60 g/L to 75 g/L) (Kaneko et al., 2008; Merck, 2016; Adeduntan et al., 2020). Despite the high protein composition in the feed the findings indicate poor protein utilization (Anya et al., 2018; Okukpe et al., 2015). When an animal is fed a high-protein diet, its total blood protein may decrease because the excess protein is converted to ammonia and then to urea, which is excreted rather than used for protein synthesis (Desta, 2024). Globulin levels (22.00 – 27.00 g/L) were within the normal range (MSD manual, 2025), and this may reflect the animal’s ability to fight infections (Okpara et al., 2023).

The serum albumin concentrations and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activity recorded in goats for this study were outside the established ranges reported by Kaneko et al. (2008) and MSD manual (2025), respectively. Albumin levels were below expected norms, while ALT activity (0.60 and 0.52 IU/L) was substantially lower than the reference range of 6–19 IU/L. These deviations may be attributed to the nutritional profile of the vetch forage, which was characterised by both high CP and excessive Zn content. High CP levels, commonly associated with leguminous forages such as vetch, are generally beneficial for hepatic protein synthesis and metabolic function (Kern-Lunbery, 2024). The observed low albumin values suggest a possible interference with zinc-induced hepatotoxicity. Excess Zn intake has been associated with impaired liver function and suppressed enzyme activity, potentially explaining the reduced albumin and ALT values (Hill & Shannon, 2019). Wu et al. (2023) further demonstrated that forage type and mineral composition can modulate hepatic gene expression in ruminants. These findings underscore the need to balance protein and mineral content in forages to maintain optimal liver function and metabolic health in goats.

The blood glucose values (14.46 – 15.54 mmol/L) were significantly higher than the normal range for healthy goats (2.78–4.16 mmol/L) as reported by the MSD manual (2025). Similar findings were reported by Khan et al. (2020), who noted elevated glucose levels in goats fed organic forage in high-altitude pastures, suggesting that forage quality and composition significantly influence energy metabolism. Allaoua et al. (2021) noted that blood glucose and cholesterol concentrations are reliable indicators of energy supply in goats, with glucose playing a crucial role in nourishing body tissues (Wahyuni et al., 2011). Cholesterol results (10.20–14.20 mmol/L) were significantly higher compared to the recommended range (2.07–3.88 mmol/L) for healthy goats (MSD manual, 2025). Copper (Cu) is known to play a vital role in lipid metabolism, particularly in regulating cholesterol through enzymatic processes, such as ceruloplasmin activity (Zhang et al., 2023). However, the Cu concentrations assessed in this study did not show deficiency, and thus, it was not a contributing factor to the high cholesterol content. Creatinine is a widely recognised biochemical marker for assessing kidney function in ruminants. In the present study, creatinine contents ranged between 88.4 and 159 µmol/L, which is in line with the reported range (88–177 µmol/L) as mentioned in the MSD manual (2025). These findings suggest that the goats maintained a normal kidney function throughout the feeding trial.

Macro-minerals are essential for livestock growth, health, and reproduction, and are regarded as critical nutrients, since their deficiency can hinder the utilization of other nutrients and reduce overall performance (Ogunjemite et al., 2021). The P and Ca were within the recommended range (P: 1.7 mg/dL), (P: 1.7 mg/dL), and (Ca: 2.2 – 3.05 mmol/L) (MSD manual, 2025). These results may be linked with the concentration levels of the same macro minerals in the forages. In the present study, RBC counts in goats (11.04 and 14.66 × 106 µL) were within the normal range (8 -18 × 106 µL) as reported by the MSD manual (2025), thus confirming the absence of microcytic hypochromic anaemia (Sirois, 1995; Olafadehan, 2011). However, the haemoglobin concentrations (57.42 - 65.72 g/L) were below the reference range (80 – 160 g/L), suggesting a potential subclinical anaemia. Increased MCH (21.0 - 33.68 g/L) and MCV (54.82 - 61.54 fL) values signify altered erythrocyte characteristics, possibly reflecting physiological adaptations. The WBC counts are key to immune response, the neutrophil levels (7.75 % - 10.88 %) were below the range of 30 – 48 % as reported by the MSD manual (2025), indicating a reduced disease resistance (Anya et al., 2018). Higher levels of neutrophils generally indicate the animal’s capacity to withstand diseases, as noted by Fouad & Bynner (2008). Lymphocyte (1.09 % to 0.71 %) and eosinophils (0.12 – 0.11 %) levels were outside the normal range of 40 – 70 % and 1 – 8 % (Bhat et al., 2011; MSD, manual, 2025). On the other hand, monocytes (0.34 – 0.31 %) remained within the expected ranges (MSD, manual, 2025).

Conclusion

Goats showed a preference for Volga and Common vetch over Haymaker plus vetch and grass, with higher intake of these varieties. Haymaker plus vetch had higher CP and CT. The observed deviations in blood metabolites, as indicated by low total protein and albumin levels, with elevated urea, glucose, and cholesterol levels, imply an imbalance in protein metabolism. Nevertheless, these vetch varieties can be utilized as a protein supplement to enhance ruminant productivity. The amelioration strategies should be employed to improve their protein utilization, particularly for Haymaker plus vetch, with high condensed tannin content. Further metabolic experiments can be conducted to determine the optimal inclusion level of vetch forages.
