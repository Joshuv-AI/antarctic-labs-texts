Sequential Single-Conduit Antegrade Flow Reconstruction for Multivessel Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia After Failed Endovascular Therapy
Authors: Gökçen Özkan, Cihan Yücel, Helin El Kılıç, Abdullah Burak Balcı, Necdet Kılıçaslan, Zakir İbrahim Kılınç, Hakan Yırgın, Yasir Musa Kesgin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic mesenteric ischemia (CMI) with multivessel occlusive disease remains challenging when endovascular therapy fails. We describe sequential antegrade reconstruction of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) and celiac artery using a single 8-mm Dacron conduit originating from the supraceliac aorta. A 59-year-old man with severe malnutrition presented with progressive postprandial abdominal pain and substantial weight loss. Computed tomography angiography revealed flush occlusion of the celiac trunk, heavily calcified chronic total occlusion of the proximal SMA, patent distal target vessels, and limited collateralization through a diminutive inferior mesenteric artery. After two unsuccessful endovascular attempts, open revascularization was performed. The distal SMA was selected beyond the occluded segment, and the celiac trunk was reimplanted onto the same graft to preserve antegrade physiologic inflow while avoiding additional conduits. The patient recovered uneventfully and remained asymptomatic with patent graft at 3 months. This report outlines considerations for conduit configuration in CMI after failed endovascular therapy.Keywords: Mesenteric ischemia; Mesenteric artery, superior; Celiac artery; Vascular surgical procedures; Revascularization

INTRODUCTION

Chronic mesenteric ischemia (CMI) is an uncommon but clinically significant condition that classically occurs in patients with multivessel mesenteric arterial stenosis or occlusion, although isolated superior mesenteric artery (SMA) disease may also be clinically relevant [1-3]. Although endovascular therapy is currently considered the first-line treatment [2], open surgical revascularization remains necessary in selected patients with flush occlusions, heavily calcified origins, long-segment chronic total occlusions, or failed percutaneous interventions [1,2].

Antegrade aortomesenteric bypass through the supraceliac aorta is an established technique that provides physiologic inflow and durable revascularization in selected patients [4,5]. However, the optimal graft configuration for multivessel disease remains controversial. Options include separate grafts to each vessel, aortoceliac bypass with a jump graft to the SMA, and sequential single-conduit strategies [6-8].

Contemporary reports rarely detail the technical reasoning behind configuration selection. Here, we present a case of multivessel CMI managed with sequential single-conduit antegrade flow reconstruction, and discuss the operative considerations guiding this strategy. In particular, conduit design and the hemodynamic rationale for sequential single-conduit revascularization after failed endovascular therapy remain insufficiently detailed in the literature.

This report describes a single clinical case managed according to standard surgical indications. Institutional review board approval was not required according to institutional policy for single case reports. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this report and accompanying images.

TECHNIQUE

A 59-year-old man presented with progressive postprandial abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and unintentional weight loss of approximately 40 kg over two years. His body mass index at presentation had decreased to 16.5 kg/m2. His medical history included heavy smoking (40 pack-years), hypertension, and dyslipidemia.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed hemodynamically significant multivessel mesenteric arterial occlusive disease. The celiac trunk showed flush occlusion at its origin, and the proximal SMA contained a dense, heavily calcified, chronic total occlusion extending approximately 3 cm from the ostium. The distal SMA and celiac branches were patent and suitable as target vessels. The inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) was diminutive, and collateralization through the meandering mesenteric artery was poorly developed. Extensive clinical and laboratory evaluations did not suggest systemic vasculitis or connective tissue disorders, and severe atherosclerotic disease was considered the primary etiology.

These findings, together with severe postprandial symptoms and profound weight loss, supported the diagnosis of advanced multivessel CMI rather than incidental mesenteric arterial disease. Representative preoperative CTA images are shown in Fig. 1.

Preoperative three-dimensional computed tomography angiography demonstrating flush occlusion of the celiac artery ostium and chronic total occlusion of the proximal superior mesenteric artery, with patent distal celiac and distal superior mesenteric artery target vessels.

Two endovascular revascularization attempts—one by cardiology and one by interventional radiology—were unsuccessful because of an inability to traverse the heavily calcified chronic total occlusions. After multidisciplinary evaluation, open antegrade revascularization was recommended.

1) Proximal inflow and graft routing

The visceral vessels were exposed through a midline laparotomy. The supraceliac aorta was selected as the inflow source because it provided a relatively disease-free, high-flow segment and allowed a short antegrade graft course toward both visceral targets. Retrograde inflow from the infrarenal aorta or iliac arteries was considered less favorable due to diffuse aortoiliac calcification and the need for a longer C-loop configuration to reach the distal SMA target. In addition, antegrade supraceliac reconstruction avoids some potential geometric disadvantages of long retrograde grafts, including redundancy, compression beneath the mesentery, and graft angulation. The supraceliac aorta was accessed by careful dissection and partial diaphragmatic crural division to achieve optimal visualization. After systemic heparinization, a partial side-biting clamp (Satinsky type) was used to avoid complete aortic cross-clamping. An 8-mm Dacron graft was anastomosed end-to-side to the supraceliac abdominal aorta under partial side clamping (Fig. 2).

Proximal end-to-side anastomosis of an 8-mm Dacron graft to the supraceliac abdominal aorta under partial side clamping.

The conduit was then routed antegrade through the retropancreatic space toward the SMA (Fig. 3). Given the potential risk associated with the peripancreatic region, meticulous surgical technique was required to safely navigate this space. Careful blunt and sharp dissection was performed posterior to the pancreas. The peripancreatic bridging venous tributaries were systematically controlled and ligated to prevent retroperitoneal hemorrhage. The splanchnic nerve plexus was avoided, and extreme caution was exercised to prevent direct pancreatic trauma that could precipitate enzyme leakage or abscess formation.

Antegrade routing of the Dacron graft through the retropancreatic space toward the superior mesenteric artery, with the celiac trunk exposed for subsequent reimplantation.

2) Distal SMA target selection and anastomosis

The distal SMA was selected beyond the occluded origin based on healthy arterial wall quality, adequate luminal diameter, reduced calcification, and a favorable inflow angle to minimize turbulence. Although the proximal patent SMA segment did not appear to be severely diseased on CTA, intraoperative exposure of the proximal and mid-SMA was considered unfavorable because of dense perivascular fibrosis, proximity to the uncinate process, and the acute graft angle from the retropancreatic route. Therefore, a more distal SMA segment, beyond the transverse mesocolon, was selected. This target provided a soft, disease-free landing zone and allowed a smoother geometric curve for the graft as it exited the retropancreatic space. An end-to-side anastomosis was performed first to restore midgut perfusion, preserve potential collateral branches, and maintain physiological mesenteric flow dynamics (Fig. 4).

Intraoperative exposure of the retropancreatic Dacron graft and the distal superior mesenteric artery target before completion of the superior mesenteric artery anastomosis.

3) Celiac reimplantation strategy

Instead of performing a separate aortoceliac bypass or constructing a jump graft from the celiac conduit to the SMA, the celiac trunk was divided at the bifurcation and reimplanted end-to-side onto the posterolateral wall of the same Dacron conduit (Fig. 5). Reconstruction was performed in the following order: aorta, SMA, and celiac artery. Establishing the aorto-SMA bypass first rapidly restored crucial perfusion to the bulk of the intestine (midgut) and enabled precise assessment of tension in the primary conduit. The celiac artery was then anastomosed to the posterolateral wall of the pressurized graft. This orientation naturally aligns with the anatomical position of the mobilized celiac trunk during retropancreatic routing, thereby facilitating a direct tension-free connection without twisting the vessels.

End-to-side reimplantation of the celiac trunk onto the same Dacron conduit following superior mesenteric artery revascularization.

This configuration was chosen to reduce the number of grafts, minimize proximal inflow competition, avoid complex multi-graft geometry, and maintain antegrade physiologic flow. Sequential revascularization restored both territories while preserving streamlined hemodynamics. After completion of the anastomoses, the clamps were sequentially released, and intraoperative assessment demonstrated satisfactory pulsatile flow in both revascularized territories.

4) Postoperative course and follow-up

The postoperative course was uneventful. Bowel function resumed on postoperative day 2, and oral intake was initiated without symptom recurrence. The patient was discharged with dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel) to mitigate the risk of early prosthetic graft thrombosis. Given the use of a prosthetic conduit and multivessel reconstruction, intensified early antiplatelet therapy was considered appropriate, although standardized guidelines for mesenteric bypass surgery are lacking.

Postoperative contrast-enhanced CTA performed as part of the early surveillance demonstrated patency of the aorta-SMA-celiac bypass graft without evidence of stenosis or kinking (Fig. 6). At 3 months, the patient remained asymptomatic, with stable weight and no recurrence of abdominal pain. Long-term follow-up will include duplex ultrasonography at 6 months and annually thereafter.

Postoperative three-dimensional computed tomography angiography demonstrating patency of the sequential single-conduit aorto-superior mesenteric artery (SMA)-celiac bypass graft. Arrows indicate the supraceliac inflow/proximal aortic anastomosis, celiac artery reimplantation, and distal SMA anastomosis.

DISCUSSION

Open surgical revascularization remains an important treatment option for selected patients with CMI, particularly when endovascular therapy is not technically feasible or has failed [1,2]. Although current guidelines support an endovascular-first approach in anatomically suitable patients, open repair remains appropriate in younger or lower-risk patients and in those with flush occlusions, long-segment chronic total occlusions, severe calcification, or failed percutaneous interventions [1,2]. In this case, two prior endovascular attempts failed because the heavily calcified chronic total occlusions could not be crossed, making open reconstruction an appropriate definitive strategy in this specific anatomical setting.

Although antegrade bypass is well established, conduit configuration is often an intraoperative judgment that may influence both technical complexity and long-term durability [4,5]. The extent of revascularization in CMI remains individualized. The SMA is generally regarded as the primary target because it supplies the largest portion of the small intestine and proximal colon. Isolated SMA revascularization has shown satisfactory outcomes in appropriately selected patients [9-11], and no randomized evidence has proven the routine superiority of two-vessel reconstruction over SMA-only bypass. However, in the presence of multivessel occlusion and limited collateralization, complete revascularization of both the SMA and the celiac territories may offer a greater mesenteric perfusion reserve [1,2]. In this patient, preoperative CTA revealed occlusion of the celiac trunk and proximal SMA, patent distal target vessels, and limited IMA collateralization. Therefore, two-vessel reconstruction was selected to reduce the risk of recurrent ischemic symptoms in a relatively young patient with severe weight loss and advanced chronic symptoms.

Supraceliac aortic inflow was selected based on anatomical and hemodynamic considerations. The supraceliac aorta provided a relatively disease-free, high-flow inflow source and enabled antegrade reconstruction of both visceral targets [4,5]. This strategy avoided a diffusely calcified infrarenal aortoiliac segment and reduced the need for long retrograde C-loop grafts. Retrograde bypass from the infrarenal aorta or iliac arteries was considered less suitable due to diffuse aortoiliac calcification and the long graft course required to reach the target distal SMA. Similarly, retrograde open mesenteric stenting was considered less favorable because two previous endovascular attempts had already failed to cross the heavily calcified chronic total occlusions.

Distal SMA target selection was dictated by intraoperative anatomy rather than routine preference. Although the more proximal SMA segment did not appear to be severely diseased on CTA, intraoperative exposure of the proximal and mid-SMA was technically unfavorable because of dense perivascular fibrosis, proximity to the uncinate process, and an acute graft angle from the retropancreatic route. A distal SMA segment beyond the transverse mesocolon provided a soft, disease-free landing zone and allowed for a smoother graft trajectory with a lower risk of kinking or tension.

Reimplantation of the celiac trunk onto the same conduit was chosen over a separate aortoceliac bypass or jump graft configuration to reduce the number of proximal aortic anastomoses while maintaining antegrade inflow to both the SMA and celiac territories [7,8]. After completion of the aorto-SMA bypass, the celiac trunk was reimplanted onto the posterolateral aspect of the same conduit, thereby providing the shortest and least angulated route. This configuration avoided an additional separate graft, minimized graft redundancy, and preserved favorable graft geometry.

From a technical standpoint, this strategy should not be regarded as technically simple. It requires careful exposure of the supraceliac aorta, controlled retropancreatic routing, appropriate distal target selection, and meticulous assessment of graft orientation. The technical value of this case lies not in proposing a universally applicable configuration but in demonstrating how inflow selection, target choice, and graft orientation can be individualized according to disease distribution, collateral status, and intraoperative anatomy.

Sequential single-conduit antegrade flow reconstruction of the SMA and celiac artery may be considered in carefully selected patients with multivessel CMI after failed endovascular therapy. This strategy requires individualized assessment of inflow quality, distal target selection, graft routing, and anastomotic orientation. In selected cases, this configuration may provide effective multivessel mesenteric revascularization while avoiding additional conduits and complex multigraft geometry.
