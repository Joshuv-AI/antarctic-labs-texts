Background

Trauma remains a major global health concern, causing 4.3 million deaths annually and standing as the leading cause of mortality among individuals under 45 years of age [1]. Trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC), a formidable complication affecting approximately one-quarter of severely injured patients, is associated with a mortality rate of 35 to 50 percent [2, 3]. The complex, dynamic nature of TIC poses significant challenges in trauma management, necessitating a deeper understanding of its underlying mechanisms.

TIC evolves dynamically through the interplay of tissue injury and hemorrhagic shock (HS). In the early phase, patients often present with hypocoagulability and high hemorrhage risk, which may progress to a hypercoagulable state linked to thromboembolic events and organ failure [4, 5]. Acute hypocoagulability stems from fibrinogen depletion, impaired thrombin generation, platelet dysfunction, and dysregulated fibrinolysis, exacerbated by inappropriate fluid resuscitation and the lethal triad of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy [2, 6]. Early hyperfibrinolysis, observed in a minority of patients, results from increased tissue plasminogen activator release and crystalloid hemodilution, while severe tissue injury can trigger hypercoagulability via histone-mediated mechanisms [7]. Later stages may involve fibrinolytic shutdown, elevating thrombotic risk [4, 8, 9]. Additional contributors include endothelial glycocalyx degradation, platelet dysfunction, inflammatory pathway activation, and hypoperfusion in HS [10, 11]. Traumatic brain injury (TBI) further complicates TIC, as a distinct coagulopathy in severe TBI cases promotes intracranial hemorrhage expansion [12].

Despite advances in severe trauma management over the past decade, TIC understanding remains limited, with some patients experiencing irreversible outcomes. Key knowledge gaps persist regarding TIC’s dynamic evolution, stage-specific phenotypic characterization, and effective targeted interventions-rooted in incomplete grasp of core mechanisms. Integrating insights from translational and basic research is critical to address these gaps. A comprehensive summary of recent advances and remaining challenges is urgently needed to refine research priorities and improve clinical outcomes.

This review synthesizes foundational science advances in TIC, focusing on interactions between coagulation, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction. It highlights key drivers (tissue injury, HS, lethal triad) and progress in targeted resuscitation, antifibrinolytic therapies, and cellular interventions. By evaluating knowledge gaps, we aim to facilitate precision therapies reducing TIC-related morbidity and mortality.

Methods

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed for English-language basic research articles on TIC and HS across common animal models, published from January 2016 to July 2025. Initial identification yielded 236 records, which were screened by two independent reviewers (titles, abstracts, full texts), with disagreements resolved by consensus. Exclusions included studies outside the review scope, clinical nature, or lack of full-text availability. Ultimately, 103 eligible articles were included for data extraction and synthesis (Fig. 1). The multidimensional pathobiology of TIC and its regulatory networks

TIC arises from integrated, time-dependent pathophysiological processes extending beyond traditional coagulation disorders [13–15]. Triggered by traumatic tissue injury and HS, it initiates systemic responses including endothelial disruption, inflammatory activation, dysregulated hemostasis, and subsequent multi-organ dysfunction (Fig. 2). Inappropriate iatrogenic resuscitation further complicates these processes and is an indispensable factor in TIC development.

Endothelial dysfunction and glycocalyx degradation

Endothelial dysfunction and glycocalyx loss play pivotal roles in TIC pathogenesis. Trauma and HS disrupt vascular barriers, provoke inflammation, and perturb coagulation balance [16–18]. Reactive oxygen species accumulation in the microvasculature compromises endothelial junctions, fostering microvascular occlusion [11]. Release of glycocalyx fragments (e.g., versican) modulates platelet aggregation and hemostatic balance [18]. Acute, organ-specific increases in vascular permeability are hallmark features following HS [19].

Platelet dysfunction

Platelet dysfunction is central to TIC, characterized by impaired activation, reduced aggregation, and diminished contribution to clot strength, even when platelet counts remain within normal ranges. Severe trauma frequently exhibits platelet hyporesponsiveness to agonists (ADP, platelet-activating factor [PAF], collagen, arachidonic acid), impairing thrombin generation and clot stability [18, 20–22]. Platelet-leukocyte aggregation (especially with monocytes) results in reduction of hemostatic function [23]. Animal models recapitulate early platelet hypofunction post-HS and tissue injury, reducing clot firmness and increasing bleeding risk [24, 25]. Sex differences exist, with female platelets showing higher aggregation and activation potential, partly explaining sex-based outcome variations [20]. The lethal triad synergistically exacerbates platelet dysfunction, as hemodilution under flow conditions impairs both platelet adhesion and aggregation, with these impairments becoming even more pronounced in the presence of acidosis [22, 26].

Coagulation cascade, fibrinolytic balance, and regulation

TIC involves complex dysregulation of the coagulation cascade and fibrinolytic system, driven by tissue injury, hypoperfusion, and systemic inflammation. Post-trauma, rapid coagulation cascade activation occurs with consumption of factors and platelets, impairing thrombin generation and clot formation [5]. Dysregulated fibrinolysis presents as pathological hyperfibrinolysis (excessive tPA release, plasmin generation, accelerated clot breakdown, increased hemorrhage risk) or fibrinolytic shutdown (inhibited fibrinolytic activity, microvascular thrombosis, organ dysfunction) [7, 8, 27]. Conversely, some patients experience a profound inhibition of fibrinolytic activity, contributing to microvascular thrombosis and organ dysfunction. An LY30 (30-min fibrinolysis) value of 3% or greater is independently associated with both massive transfusion requirements and increased mortality in patients with uncontrolled HS [9]. Endothelial dysfunction, driven by glycocalyx degradation and inflammatory mediators, further exacerbates coagulation abnormalities via consumption coagulopathy, ongoing fibrinolysis, and barrier function impairment [18].

Immunothrombosis and inflammatory crosstalk

The immune-coagulant nexus is integral to TIC, where trauma induces a surge in IL-6, TNF-α, and other inflammatory mediators correlating with platelet and endothelial dysfunction [13] (Fig. 3). Bradykinin, derived during coagulopathy, links vascular leak to local cytokine upregulation [28], while leukocyte activation, especially via mitochondrial superoxide, exacerbates fibrinogen oxidation and aggregate coagulopathy [29]. NETosis and immunothrombosis destabilize endothelial and gut barriers; their inhibition by TXA or dekaparin reduces both systemic inflammation and thromboinflammation, preserving vascular function [30, 31]. HMGB1 and complement are crucial pathogenic mediators-elevated HMGB1 impairs platelet function, with inhibition via monoclonal antibodies or rTM improving hemostasis [32], and complement C3 blockade (CP40) in primate HS models ameliorates coagulopathy and preserves tissue integrity [33].

Metabolic and microenvironmental perturbations

The interplay of hypothermia, acidosis, and hemodilution forms a deleterious microenvironment, compounding coagulation defects and worsening clinical outcomes [22, 34]. Acidosis reduces thrombin generation and impairs platelet aggregation, whereas hypothermia enhances fibrinolytic activity and disrupts enzymatic reactions within the coagulation cascade. Metabolic stress manifests as disrupted redox and mitochondrial function and impaired energy homeostasis, with therapies such as Mdivi-1 and ALM providing organ protection via mitochondrial stabilization and microcirculatory optimization [35, 36]. Metabolic derangements exacerbate coagulopathy, promote tissue hypoxia, oxidative stress, and systemic inflammation, fueling a vicious cycle of organ dysfunction. Targeting the correction of these metabolic and microenvironmental imbalances is thus vital for the effective management and mitigation of TIC [37].

Models as translational platforms for TIC investigation

Diverse animal and experimental models replicate TIC complexity, each with unique translational advantages and limitations [38] (Table 1). Rodents are widely used for accessibility, low cost, and genetic modification ease, effectively modeling coagulopathy, endothelial/platelet dysfunction, and inflammation-hemostasis interactions [39]. Examples include rat HS models with IL-1β-primed MSC reducing hepatic permeability and syndecan-1 shedding, and murine models enabling high-throughput intervention evaluation [30, 40, 41]. Rabbit models balance physiological relevance and experimental accessibility, bridging rodent mechanistic studies and large-animal translational research [24, 42]. Canine models bridge veterinary and comparative research, with viscoelastic assays (VCM-Vet) revealing complex post-HS coagulopathies and aligning with human hemostatic profiles, aiding transfusion strategy refinement [43, 44]. Swine, with close physiological/anatomical resemblance to humans, robustly replicate TIC phenotypes, facilitating delineation of trauma-induced hypercoagulability vs. HS-associated hypofibrinolysis and evaluating interventions (e.g., vasopressin for prehospital stabilization, REBOA therapeutics) [45–48]. Nonhuman primates (NHPs, e.g., rhesus macaques) offer the closest parallel to human coagulation/immune responses, replicating TIC-related derangements and validating mechanisms, but are constrained by cost and ethics [25, 33, 49].

Integrated models illustrate polytrauma synergy in TIC. In mice, treatment of TBI combined with HS using hypertonic saline induces greater systemic proinflammatory cytokine responses compared to whole blood, while propranolol specifically suppresses cerebral inflammation [50]. In swine models, serum from TBI with HS impairs pulmonary endothelial function through the thrombin-PAR1 pathway [51]. Polytrauma platforms (chest, hepatic, limb trauma) replicate clinical TIC heterogeneity, with porcine studies supporting staged surgical management over early total care [52]. Controlled hemorrhage models allow for precise evaluation of resuscitation fluids, showing that hydroxocobalamin provides superior hemodynamic support compared with lactated Ringer and is less likely to exacerbate coagulopathy than colloids [53]. Uncontrolled hemorrhage models underscore hemorrhage pattern influence, with ongoing bleeding intensifying resuscitation fluid adverse effects (especially colloids) on coagulopathy, metabolism, and complement activation [54]. Prolonged field care models (military/austere environments) evaluate extended prehospital intervals, with therapies like ALM in swine enhancing hemodynamic resilience and attenuating inflammation [55, 56]. Environmental stressors (high-altitude hypoxia, 15 °C cold-water immersion) worsen TIC phenotypes via amplified acidosis, hypothermia, and coagulopathy (rat HS models) [57, 58].

In vitro and ex vivo systems complement in vivo approaches, offering precise, controlled platforms for TIC mechanism elucidation and intervention evaluation. Simulated TIC in healthy human blood reproduces key clinical coagulopathies, showing hyperfibrinolysis (more than dilution) destabilizes clots and combined fibrinogen/PCC/TXA therapy restores clot integrity better than monotherapy [26, 59]. Microfluidic models using trauma patient blood uncover broad defects in platelet deposition and aggregation, enhancing risk prediction capabilities [34]. Furthermore, advanced ex vivo assays such as thromboelastography (TEG) and rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) provide greater detail in coagulation assessment. Studies in canine HS have detected coagulopathies that are not identified by standard laboratory tests [43]. In addition, analysis of swine ROTEM data using principal component methods has improved the discrimination of phenotypes, which together enhance the preclinical evaluation of antifibrinolytics and procoagulants [46].

Despite advancing TIC research, animal models have inherent limitations [60]. Canine and NHP models more faithfully recapitulate human responses, but the former are limited by species differences and the latter by significant ethical and financial barriers. Rodent models are best suited for mechanistic and genetic studies, while swine and NHPs are indispensable for testing approaches with direct clinical applicability [46, 49]. Additionally, the swine fibrinolytic system demonstrates a limited response to tPA-induced activation, attributable to the inherently low activatability of porcine plasminogen by tPA [61]. In vitro platforms afford high precision in variable isolation, but lack systemic, multi-organ interactions. Model fidelity can be enhanced by replicating injury complexity, with polytrauma and environmental scenario models more accurately reflecting clinical TIC and supporting more robust translational evaluation of therapies.

Experimental interventions and therapeutics

Advances in the mechanistic understanding of TIC have driven significant innovation in experimental interventions and therapeutic strategies over the past decade. Diverse pharmacologic, biologic, and supportive approaches have been developed, with growing emphasis on precise agent selection, timing, and administration.

Fluid resuscitation strategies and physical interventions

When blood products are unavailable or cannot be administered promptly, non-blood fluid resuscitation remains the primary option for initial management of HS. Both small volume infusion of Ringer's lactate solution and normal saline may be safe regarding coagulation influence in early stage of HS [62]. Hypertonic saline (7.5%) in uncontrolled HS stabilizes hemodynamics better than 4.5% or normal saline, reducing blood loss and renal dysfunction via osmotic preservation of endothelial integrity [42]. However, it has been shown to exacerbate coagulopathy and fibrinolysis [63]. Balanced electrolyte solution, hydroxyethyl starch, and gelatine-polysuccinate have been associated with worsening coagulation and hemodilution [64]. The Low-Fouling Zwitterionic polymeric colloids demonstrated acceptable biocompatibility in HS, effectively restoring intravascular volume without disrupting coagulation [65]. Albumin and tailored colloids further promote basement membrane and microcirculatory integrity, surpassing crystalloid resuscitation in limiting vascular leak [66]. However, an earlier study demonstrated that albumin in a 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) product (Kcentra) has no effect on endothelial permeability [67].

The most direct way to intervene in TIC is to effectively control bleeding early, either externally or internally. Among patients who achieved hemostasis, each 15-min reduction in time to hemostasis correlated with a lower 30-day mortality rate [68]. REBOA performed in Zone 3 is associated with reduced organ dysfunction and coagulopathy compared to Zone 1 [69]. Physical interventions, such as mild therapeutic hypothermia during REBOA in swine, mitigate ischemia–reperfusion injury by improving pH, reducing lactate, and limiting distal organ damage [48]. Conversely, cold fluid resuscitation exacerbates hypothermia and acidosis in rat HS, decreasing survival time compared to warm fluids [70]. It is noteworthy that, in an extended prehospital simulation setting, swine subjected to HS did not exhibit significant alterations in coagulation or endothelial function, despite being maintained at permissive hypotension with substantial hemodilution induced by crystalloid infusion [71]. These findings suggest that targeting systolic blood pressure at the hemodilution threshold may safely preserve organ perfusion in resource-limited settings.

Plasma, cryoprecipitate, and blood products

Plasma and blood products address TIC’s multi-factorial coagulopathy by replenishing clotting factors and preserving endothelial integrity. More importantly, plasma resuscitation may support mitochondrial function by enhancing glutaminolysis/amino acid catabolism and purine salvage pathways [37]. Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) stabilizes vascular barriers, reducing pulmonary and intestinal permeability in murine HS, while lyophilized or 5-day post-thaw cryoprecipitate matches FFP’s organ protection with reduced volume requirements, making them ideal for prehospital settings [19, 72]. Whole blood (WB) outperforms red blood cells (RBCs) plus plasma in ex vivo models, preserving platelet function and clot strength by maintaining physiological ratios of coagulation factors and platelets, avoiding dilutional coagulopathy [73]. Prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) corrects vitamin K-dependent factor deficiencies but may induce consumptive coagulopathy in acidotic TIC, with 4-factor PCC safer than 3-factor formulations [74]. In a rabbit model of pulmonary contusion and HS, administration of PCC resulted in more rapid initial clot propagation compared to FFP [75]. Platelet transfusion with a 2:1 platelet-to-RBC ratio improves clot firmness in rat polytrauma models more effectively than 1:1 ratios, reducing transfusion volumes without increasing thromboembolic risk [76].

Vasopressors and novel hemodynamic agents

Vasopressin (deficiency in severe T/HS) and its analogues facilitate hemodynamic stabilization in TIC patients by inducing vasoconstriction and thereby enhancing perfusion of vital organs [77–79]. Mechanistically, vasopressin enhances vascular contractility via RhoA/ROCK-mediated connexin 43 phosphorylation, preserving mesenteric artery tone in HS [80]. Intramuscular vasopressin in swine with HS transiently elevates systolic pressure and cardiac index, with systemic uptake confirmed by increased serum levels, supporting its use for prehospital stabilization [47]. Meanwhile, non-pressor vasopressin analogues have recently been investigated for their hemostatic properties. In a rat model of T/HS, ddAVP significantly improved shock severity and ROTEM coagulation parameters, despite having no effect on organ injury indices [81]. However, caution is warranted with prolonged use, as vasopressin has been shown to elevate mesenteric vascular resistance and promote pulmonary capillary leakage in swine, underscoring the importance of carefully balanced dosing to prevent organ hypoperfusion [82, 83]. Our laboratory first demonstrated that intramuscular terlipressin rapidly increases mean arterial pressure, enhances systemic perfusion, and reduces tissue injury in rat models of uncontrolled HS [84]. Notably, terlipressin outperformed norepinephrine by better preserving microcirculatory flow. In addition, terlipressin combined with conservative fluid management in rats mitigates HS-induced acute kidney injury by downregulating proinflammatory cytokinesand normalizing vasopressin 1a receptor expression, linking neurohormonal modulation to renal protection [85].

Field devices and delivery options

Prehospital TIC management relies on adaptable delivery routes and point-of-care monitoring. Intramuscular and IO provide effective alternatives when intravenous (IV) access is challenging, as intramuscular vasopressin achieves hemodynamic stabilization comparable to IV administration in swine HS, and TXA through IO demonstrates similar pharmacokinetics and therapeutic concentrations within 10 min [47, 86]. Point-of-care devices, such as the VCM-Vet in canine HS, detect hemostatic disturbances (increased coagulability/fibrinolysis) not captured by conventional tests, guiding real-time resuscitation [43]. Advanced imaging, including parametric contrast-enhanced ultrasound in rat polytrauma, assesses persistent microvascular perfusion deficits post-resuscitation, linking microcirculatory dysfunction to TIC [87]. These tools bridge prehospital and definitive care, ensuring timely intervention in remote settings.

Antifibrinolytics and protease inhibitors

TXA plays a central role in the management of TIC, and its efficacy depends on the timing, route, and dose of administration. Early administration within one hour of injury is critical, as rodent models show that delayed TXA does not inhibit neutrophil extracellular trap formation or preserve intestinal tight junctions, while prompt delivery by intravenous, intramuscular, or enteral routes mitigates organ injury [30, 88, 89]. Route optimization studies in swine demonstrate that intramuscular TXA at 30 mg/kg achieves serum concentrations comparable to IV, with dual injection sites offering no advantage, supporting intramuscular route as a viable prehospital alternative [90, 91]. Intraosseous (IO) administration of TXA matches IV pharmacokinetics in HS, further expanding access in austere settings [86]. Beyond antifibrinolytic effects, TXA functions as a serine protease inhibitor, as enteral administration in rats reduces pancreatic protease leakage, preserves α1-adrenergic receptor expression, and restores vasopressor responsiveness, thereby blunting systemic proteolysis [92–94]. However, the hemodynamic benefits of TXA are context dependent. Its efficacy becomes evident only when combined with whole blood resuscitation rather than crystalloids. These findings highlight the importance of concurrent hemostatic support [95]. Combination with other agents, such as fibrinogen, rFVIIa or Factor XIII, enhances clot stability, with synergistic effects reducing fibrinolysis more effectively than single-agent therapy [96, 97].

Anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and protective agents

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant interventions can disrupt the inflammatory-coagulation axis in TIC. Valproic acid attenuates acute lung injury in rat HS by inhibiting tissue factor expression in THP-1 cells via the p-ERK1/2–Egr-1 pathway [98]. Complement inhibition with compstatin CP40 in nonhuman primates mitigates renal and intestinal barrier dysfunction induced by HS, while reducing neutrophil infiltration and restoring normal coagulation [33]. Targeting HMGB1 with monoclonal antibodies or recombinant thrombomodulin (rTM) in murine T/HS improves clot formation and reduces bleeding time, with synergistic effects of combined therapy [32]. Melanocortin fusion proteins (AQB-565) suppress leukocyte mitochondrial superoxide, protecting fibrinogen from oxidation and preserving coagulation in rat TIC [29]. Estradiol pretreatment enhances male platelet activation to female-like levels, while CeO2 nanoparticles reduce hepatic oxidative stress and inflammation in HS mice, expanding therapeutic options [20, 99]. Finally, TXA and Inhalation of high-concentration oxygen have been shown in T/HS through mitigating anti-inflammatory effect [100, 101].

Cellular and biologic therapy

Cell-based and cell-free therapeutic products, which are easily stored and rapidly deployable, offer significant potential in the emergency management of TIC [102]. Cellular therapies may target TIC’s endotheliopathy and inflammation via paracrine mechanisms. IL-1β-primed MSC in rat HS reduce hepatic vascular permeability and leukocyte adhesion, associated with lower syndecan-1 and ICAM-1 levels, while MSC extracellular vesicles (EVs) preserve lung and intestinal adherens junctions, limiting inflammatory biomarker release [16, 40]. Freeze-dried platelets retain functional EVs that incorporate into clots and attenuate pulmonary leakage, offering a shelf-stable alternative to fresh platelets [41]. However, platelet-leukocyte aggregation may diminish these benefits, as in vitro studies indicate that aggregation between platelets and monocytes impairs clot firmness, emphasizing the necessity of balancing prohemostatic and proinflammatory effects [23]. Additionally, fibrinogen γ-chain-coated, adenosine-diphosphate (ADP)-encapsulated liposomes (H12-[ADP]-liposomes) effectively controlled bleeding by enhancing platelet aggregation in a coagulopathic rabbit model of mesenteric vessel injury [24].

TIC in special scenarios

TIC manifests distinctively in complex injury states, mechanism of injury, vulnerable populations, and under comorbid or environmental stressors, necessitating tailored investigative and therapeutic approaches. Combined injuries, as seen in TBI plus HS or polytrauma, induce synergistic coagulopathic and inflammatory responses. For example, murine TBI plus HS models highlight how resuscitation choices and adjuncts like propranolol differentially modulate systemic and cerebral inflammation [50], while estrogen analogs improve survival in large animal TBI plus HS models with sex-dependent effects [103]. Polytrauma models help clarify distinct fibrinolytic responses. Traumatic injury leads to fibrinolysis suppression through increased PAI-1, while HS induces hyperfibrinolysis. Studies in nonhuman primates confirm these findings by showing delayed D-dimer peaks and preserved clot lysis [104]. Moreover, duration of interventions such as REBOA in swine profoundly affects distal organ injury and coagulopathy, emphasizing precise timing in complex trauma management [105].

Sex-based differences are evident, with female trauma patients demonstrating enhanced platelet aggregation and heightened receptor activation relative to males, effects that are modulated by sex hormones and associated with improved survival rates [20]. In pediatric populations, TIC is frequently characterized by a pronounced “cytokine storm,” particularly in cases of HS and traumatic encephalopathy syndromes, which correlates with unfavorable prognoses [106]. TIC in geriatric trauma patients has been found to be independently linked to greater ISS scores (> 16), heightened shock index (> 1), as well as to acidosis, hypocalcemia, and hypofibrinogenemia [107]. Elderly individuals often have a history of anticoagulant use, which complicates management [108]. Metabolic comorbidities such as obesity and metabolic syndrome potentiate the severity of TIC. In experimental models, obesity exacerbates endotheliopathy and coagulopathic responses following HS, effects that may be mitigated by targeted fibrinogen resuscitation [109]. Likewise, metabolic syndrome is associated with diminished vascular reactivity and suboptimal responses to vasoactive interventions [110]. Environmental extremes, including seawater immersion and high-altitude hypoxia, further aggravate coagulopathy and metabolic derangements, resulting in worse outcomes [57, 58]. Notably, interventions that target specific pathophysiological pathways, such as inhibition of endoplasmic reticulum stress [111], administration of N-acetyl-L-cysteine [112], or use of moderately hypotonic saline [113], have demonstrated potential in improving hemostatic function, organ protection, and survival in preclinical HS models. The selection of experimental models and patient cohorts is pivotal to ensure translational relevance. Species-specific differences in baseline coagulation, especially among rodents and swine, may limit the generalizability of preclinical findings [60]. Canine models utilizing viscoelastic coagulation monitoring closely recapitulate the clinical features of TIC [43, 44], while studies in nonhuman primates offer the highest fidelity for evaluating novel therapies, such as complement inhibition [49]. Each preclinical model presents unique advantages and limitations; therefore, integrative approaches across species are essential to advance clinical innovation in TIC management.

Future directions and enduring challenges

TIC remains a complex syndrome, with knowledge gaps limiting the translation of preclinical research to clinical care (Fig. 4). Accurate delineation of TIC phenotypes remains paramount. Traumatic injury and HS each influence coagulopathic trajectories in distinct ways. Injury often leads to hypercoagulability with suppressed fibrinolysis. In contrast, shock typically results in hypocoagulability accompanied by hyperfibrinolysis. The molecular mechanisms underlying these divergent responses, particularly in polytrauma, are still not fully understood. Comorbidities such as obesity, hypertension, and diabetes further aggravate endothelial dysfunction. However, optimized and individualized resuscitation strategies for these conditions are still lacking. Environmental challenges further compound metabolic and hemostatic derangements, mandating the development of contextually adapted interventions for extreme environments.

The incorporation of omics technologies and the challenge of interspecies translation present additional hurdles. Although proteomic and metabolomic investigations have illuminated critical pathways and candidate biomarkers, fundamental differences in coagulation physiology between animals and humans may distort the extrapolation of experimental data, with rodents and swine demonstrating more robust and rapid clotting profiles that confound preclinical interpretation. Emerging machine learning and systems biology tools offer promise in bridging species divides by uncovering conserved molecular mechanisms, yet their clinical integration and validation are nascent, perpetuating the gap from computational discovery to practical utility.

Therapeutically, the focus is shifting to target the interconnected axes of inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and coagulation. Selective targeting of inflammatory mediators, such as HMGB1 and complement factors, shows efficacy in preclinical models. Strategies that stabilize the endothelium, including the use of MSC vesicles and albumin, have also demonstrated beneficial effects. The imperative for personalized therapy, attentive to sex-based differences, platelet function, and trauma-specific dysregulation, demands further refinement of diagnostic modalities to facilitate accurate patient stratification.

Looking ahead, next-generation therapies will emphasize combination regimens that concurrently modulate multiple TIC pathways, exemplified by synergistic administration of antifibrinolytics with procoagulants or anti-inflammatories to fortify hemostasis while attenuating inflammation. Concurrently, systems-based monitoring that integrates viscoelastic assays, microfluidic platforms, and metabolomic profiling will enable timely phenotype identification and direct targeted, precision resuscitation (Fig. 5).

Conclusions

Recent advances have profoundly expanded our understanding of TIC pathophysiology, defining it as a dynamic and heterogeneous syndrome governed by the interplay of tissue injury, HS, comorbidities, and environmental exposures. Each of these factors necessitates tailored therapeutic approaches. Integrative studies across rodent, swine, and nonhuman primate models have delineated critical molecular pathways, advanced resuscitation paradigms, and substantiated emerging therapeutics. These include optimized protocols for TXA, innovative uses of plasma and blood products, targeted administration of vasopressors, and the potential of cellular therapies. Persistent challenges in cross-species translation highlight the need for model refinement and clinical validation. Looking ahead, the field’s priorities include precise TIC subtype classification using multi-omics and machine learning, robust validation of candidate biomarkers in diverse clinical settings, and the development of combinatorial treatment strategies tailored to individual patient profiles. Enhanced diagnostic accuracy, achievable through integration of viscoelastic testing and microcirculatory assessment at the point of care, will further individualize management. Through such multidisciplinary and translational efforts, TIC research is poised to close the gap between bench and bedside, ultimately advancing outcomes for trauma patients worldwide.
