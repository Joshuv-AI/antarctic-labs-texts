Clinical features and risk factors for severe pediatric scrub typhus in Liangshan Prefecture, China: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yuanyuan Hua, Yan Zhang, Jimin Zhou, Yan Shi, Feng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to analyze the clinical characteristics and identify risk factors for severe disease in pediatric scrub typhus in Liangshan Prefecture, a resource-limited region, to facilitate early diagnosis and intervention, thereby improving outcomes and reducing mortality.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of 103 children with confirmed scrub typhus admitted to a major hospital in Liangshan between July 2023 and December 2024. Patients were classified into severe and non-severe groups. Demographics, clinical features, and laboratory parameters were compared. Univariate logistic regression was performed to identify laboratory parameters associated with disease severity. Due to the limited number of severe cases (n = 12), multivariable analysis was not performed.ResultsA total of 103 children were enrolled, comprising 92 mild and 11 severe cases. The majority of children (53.4%) were aged 5–11 years. A distinct seasonal peak was observed from August to September (84.5%). Universal clinical manifestations included fever (100%) and eschar/ulcer (100%). Compared to the mild group, severe cases demonstrated significantly higher levels of CRP, AST, and LDH, and more pronounced reductions in hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count(PLT), fibrinogen, and albumin (all P < 0.05). Univariate analysis using categorized laboratory parameters identified decreased albumin (OR: 20.54, 95% CI: 4.07–376.76, P < 0.001), decreased hemoglobin (OR: 4.53, 95% CI: 1.38–15.87, P = 0.011), and decreased platelet count (OR: 3.20, 95% CI: 1.03–12.22, P = 0.037) as significant risk factors for severe disease.ConclusionIn resource-limited settings during scrub typhus season, the constellation of fever, eschar/ulcer, hepatomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and characteristic laboratory derangements supports the diagnosis. Among these, elevated CRP and LDH, coupled with reductions in PLT, fibrinogen, Hb, and particularly albumin, should alert clinicians to severe disease, enabling early intervention and improved prognosis.Keywords: Scrub typhus, Pediatrics, Disease severity, Risk factors, Hypoalbuminemia, Resource-limited settings

Introduction

Scrub typhus, also known as tsutsugamushi disease, is an acute febrile illness caused by Orientia tsutsugamushi, an obligate intracellular bacterium.

The disease is maintained primarily in chiggers (trombiculid mites) and transmitted to humans through the bite of infected larval mites of the genus Leptotrombidium [1]. Globally, scrub typhus is predominantly distributed within the Asia-Pacific “tsutsugamushi triangle,” extending from the Russian Far East and Japan in the north and east to northern Australia in the south and Pakistan in the west [2]. Recent epidemiological investigations have identified expanding geographic boundaries, with cases reported beyond this classical region in Africa, South America (particularly Chile), and adjacent areas of Central Asia and the Middle East [2]. In China, surveillance data from 2006 to 2016 revealed a 3.15-fold increase in affected counties and a 15.4-fold rise in case numbers, with notably high incidence rates among preschool children (0–6 years) and the elderly [3]. Subsequent data from Sichuan Province (2015–2021) demonstrated persistent annual increases, particularly in the Panzhihua and Liangshan Yi Autonomous Prefecture regions [4, 5]. After a 5–14 day incubation period, patients typically present with influenza-like symptoms including fever, rash, headache, myalgia, cough, and lymphadenopathy, often accompanied by gastrointestinal manifestations such as nausea, vomiting, and abdominal discomfort. The pathognomonic eschar or ulcer at the chigger bite site remains a crucial diagnostic feature [6, 7]. In adults, older age and comorbidities have been identified as major risk factors for severe scrub typhus [8–10]. In children, severe complications including hepatitis, pneumonia, acute kidney injury, meningitis, shock, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) can also occur, with reported case fatality rates reaching 1.1% [11]. However, the risk factors for severe disease in the pediatric population remain less well characterized, warranting further investigation.

This study aimed to characterize the clinical features of pediatric scrub typhus in Liangshan Prefecture and identify risk factors for severe disease in this resource-limited setting. Early recognition of severe cases through readily available clinical and laboratory parameters could facilitate timely intervention, improve outcomes, reduce family economic burden, and ultimately decrease mortality.

Methods

Study population and design

A retrospective study was conducted across four hospitals in Liangshan Prefecture (with the majority of cases from a single center). Medical records of pediatric scrub typhus cases diagnosed between January 2023 and December 2024 were reviewed. A total of 103 children were enrolled, comprising 91 non-severe and 12 severe cases. The majority of cases (84.47%) occurred during August to September. The cohort included 52 males and 51 females (ratio = 1:1), with a median age of 9 years (IQR: 5, 11).

Inclusion criteria

According to the diagnostic criteria issued by the guideline of the National Scrub Typhus Control and Prevention Guideline (2009) issued by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention [12], patients were enrolled if they met the criteria for clinical diagnosis:

Data collection

Demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, and laboratory parameters were systematically collected, including: white blood cell count, C-reactive protein, platelet count (PLT), hemoglobin (Hb), albumin, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, lactate dehydrogenase, creatine kinase, and fibrinogen. All laboratory tests were performed on blood samples collected on the day of admission, prior to the initiation of antibiotic therapy.

Statistical analysis

Laboratory parameters were classified as normal or abnormal based on institutional reference ranges. Univariate logistic regression was performed to assess the association between each categorical laboratory parameter and severe scrub typhus. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Due to the limited number of severe cases (n = 12), multivariable logistic regression was not performed to avoid model overfitting and unstable estimates. For parameters with near-universal abnormality (CRP and LDH), continuous values were also compared between groups. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First People’s Hospital of Liang Shan Prefecture (Approval No: 2023-008). Informed consent was obtained from all participants’ legal guardians.

Results

Clinical characteristics

All 103 cases were clinically diagnosed based on the national diagnostic criteria.All enrolled children presented with fever and had a history of environmental exposure (100%). Other common clinical manifestations included cough (31.1%), headache (26.2%), vomiting (6.8%), and abdominal pain (4.9%). Some cases were accompanied by anorexia, lethargy, fatigue, or tachypnea. Rash was observed in all patients (100%), predominantly presenting as macules and maculopapules. A characteristic eschar was identified in all patients (100%), with the most common sites being the axillae (16.5%) and scrotum (16.5%). Lymphadenopathy was observed in 34.0% of patients, followed by hepatosplenomegaly in 11.7% (Table 1).

Laboratory findings and risk factor analysis

In univariate analysis using categorical variables, decreased ALB (OR: 20.54, 95% CI: 4.07–376.76, P < 0.001), decreased Hb (OR: 4.53, 95% CI: 1.38–15.87, P = 0.011), and decreased PLT (OR: 3.20, 95% CI: 1.03–12.22, P = 0.037) were significantly associated with severe scrub typhus. Elevated ALT showed a trend toward significance (OR: 4.48, 95% CI: 0.96–41.33, P = 0.056). Elevated CRP and LDH were observed in nearly all patients in both groups (91.2%–100%), precluding reliable categorical analysis; however, their continuous levels were significantly higher in the severe group (Table 2). Decreased FIB and elevated AST were not significantly associated with severe disease in categorical analysis (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

The diagnosis of scrub typhus in the present cohort was established entirely on clinical grounds, based on the presence of an eschar (or ulcer) together with supporting features including environmental exposure history, fever, rash, and lymphadenopathy. This diagnostic approach reflects the clinical reality of primary care settings in endemic areas such as Liangshan Prefecture, where access to laboratory confirmation remains limited. A prior regional survey documented that merely 4.50% of clinically suspected scrub typhus cases underwent serological or molecular confirmation [3]. Left untreated, the disease carries a substantial mortality rate of up to 6%, which remains notable at 1.4% even with appropriate antibiotic therapy [3]. The characteristic eschar served as the critical diagnostic clue in our cohort, and was identified in all 103 patients (100%). The most common sites were the axillae (16.5%) and scrotum (16.5%), followed by the groin (10.7%), ears (8.7%), and thighs (8.7%). This distribution is broadly consistent with previous reports [15]. Another study reported that 79.5% of eschars were located on the anterior aspect of the body and highlighted notable sex differences in distribution [16]. In pediatric patients, the predilection for warm, moist, and concealed areas such as the axillae and genital region remains consistent with adult reports. However, eschars may also occur in less typical sites, emphasizing that thorough full-body examination remains essential for timely diagnosis in children, particularly in resource-limited settings where laboratory confirmation is unavailable.

Consistent with the known clinical phenotype, fever was universal (100%) in our pediatric cohort. Other common manifestations included headache, cough, abdominal pain, vomiting, lymphadenopathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. In our study, 26.2% of children presented with headache and 6.8% with vomiting—symptoms that may indicate mild or early central nervous system (CNS) involvement. Although none of the patients in our cohort underwent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, CNS involvement is a recognized complication of pediatric scrub typhus. Previous studies from endemic regions have identified Orientia tsutsugamushi as the causative agent in 1%–4.7% of childhood CNS infections [17–21], and one study reported it as the most common identifiable cause of childhood meningitis, accounting for 24.4% of cases [22]. The pathogenesis of neurological involvement involves vasculitis and microthrombosis secondary to endothelial infection [23–26]. Although CSF data are not available from our cohort, the observed frequency of headache and vomiting suggests that scrub typhus should be considered in the differential diagnosis of children presenting with fever and CNS symptoms during epidemic seasons in resource-limited settings. When clinically indicated, such as in the presence of altered consciousness, seizures, or persistent severe headache, CSF analysis should be pursued to exclude or confirm CNS involvement.

In the present study, the most frequently observed complications were elevated liver enzymes (80.58%), hyponatremia (41.75%), thrombocytopenia (37.86%), and hypoalbuminemia (18.45%). This complication profile is broadly consistent with that reported in a recent pediatric study from Nepal [27]. Notably, the proportion of patients with elevated liver enzymes in our cohort (80.58%) was higher than the pooled estimate of 30.68% for hepatitis reported in a recent meta-analysis of 3,329 pediatric scrub typhus cases [11]. While this difference may reflect geographic variation in disease presentation, differences in case ascertainment, or delayed healthcare-seeking behavior in our setting, the finding underscores the importance of monitoring hepatic function in pediatric scrub typhus patients in this region. Other clinical studies from various regions have also identified hepatic injury as a predominant complication [28, 29]. The pathogenesis of multi-organ involvement relates to the tropism of O. tsutsugamushi for macrophages and endothelial cells [30]. Thrombocytopenia may result from multiple mechanisms, including endothelial injury-mediated platelet consumption, direct bacterial effects on megakaryocytes, secondary hypersplenism, and bone marrow suppression [31]. Murine models have demonstrated that the liver, spleen, kidneys, and brain are target organs, with the lungs exhibiting the highest bacterial loads [32–34]. The lungs have also been recognized as a site for platelet biogenesis and a reservoir for hematopoietic stem cells, which may contribute to the pathophysiology of thrombocytopenia in scrub typhus [35].

In our univariate analysis using clinically categorized laboratory parameters, hypoalbuminemia (OR: 20.54), anemia (OR: 4.53), and thrombocytopenia (OR: 3.20) were significantly associated with severe scrub typhus. Elevated CRP and LDH were near-universal in both groups, limiting their discriminatory value as categorical variables; however, their continuous levels were significantly higher in severe cases. However, reliance on a single laboratory parameter is generally insufficient for robust risk stratification in clinical practice. Previous studies have identified a range of factors associated with severe pediatric scrub typhus, including young infancy, thrombocytopenia, anemia, elevated C-reactive protein, leukocytosis, hyperbilirubinemia, hypoalbuminemia, elevated blood urea nitrogen and creatinine, and coagulopathy [31, 36]. One predictive model for severe disease incorporated six indicators: hemoglobin, platelet count, lactate dehydrogenase, blood urea nitrogen, creatine kinase-MB, and hypoalbuminemia [37]. Another multicenter cohort study identified tachypnea (OR: 6.85, 95% CI: 2.69–9.87), altered consciousness (OR: 11.48, 95% CI: 3.43–10.19), leukocytosis (OR: 3.38, 95% CI: 1.12–10.16), hypoalbuminemia (OR: 10.78, 95% CI: 2.66–48.76), and hyponatremia (OR: 10.08, 95% CI: 2.11–23.42) as independent risk factors for severe disease [38]. In our cohort, hypoalbuminemia was among the factors significantly associated with severe disease in univariate analysis, a finding that aligns with its identification as an independent predictor in the aforementioned multicenter study [38]. In resource-limited settings such as Liangshan Prefecture, monitoring of readily available laboratory parameters—including serum albumin—may assist in identifying patients who warrant closer clinical attention. However, given that the small number of severe cases in our study precluded multivariable analysis, the independent predictive value of any single parameter in this population requires validation in larger prospective cohorts.

Our study has several limitations. First, all patients were clinically diagnosed according to the national consensus, without laboratory confirmation (e.g., specific IgM or PCR). This was due to resource limitations at the study hospital; confirmatory testing would have required out-of-pocket payment by families, which was not feasible given local economic constraints. Second, although this study included patients from four hospitals, the majority of cases were from a single center, and the number of severe cases was limited (n = 12). The small number of severe cases precluded multivariable regression analysis; therefore, we cannot determine whether the observed associations between laboratory parameters and disease severity are independent of each other or confounded. All findings from the univariate analysis should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, requiring validation in larger prospective cohorts. Third, no patient underwent CSF analysis because lumbar puncture was not clinically indicated in the absence of altered consciousness, seizures, or other overt neurological signs, and due to resource constraints in our setting. Therefore, the true prevalence of CNS involvement in our cohort cannot be determined. In addition, data on certain potential prognostic biomarkers, such as ferritin, cytokines, and O. tsutsugamushi genotypes, were not available for analysis. Fourth, the presence of an eschar was required for study inclusion, which may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalizability of our findings to scrub typhus patients without an identifiable eschar.

Nevertheless, it is worth noting that relatively inexpensive rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) are now available for scrub typhus, such as immunochromatographic assays for Orientia tsutsugamushi-specific IgM/IgG. These tests are simple to perform, provide results within 15–30 min, do not require complex equipment, and are less costly than molecular methods such as PCR. The introduction of such rapid tests in resource-limited settings like Liangshan Prefecture may help improve diagnostic accuracy and reduce sole reliance on clinical diagnosis in the future.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study of pediatric scrub typhus in Liangshan Prefecture, fever and eschar were universal, and hypoalbuminemia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia were significantly associated with severe disease. In resource-limited settings where laboratory confirmation is often unavailable, these readily accessible clinical and laboratory parameters may assist frontline clinicians in early identification of children at risk for severe scrub typhus, enabling timely intervention and potentially improving outcomes. Future multicenter, prospective studies with larger sample sizes and systematic laboratory confirmation are warranted to validate these findings and to establish more robust predictive models for severe pediatric scrub typhus.
