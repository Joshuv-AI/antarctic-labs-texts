Romosozumab for the treatment of thalassemia-associated osteoporosis in two female patients
Authors: Maria P Yavropoulou, Maria-Evangelia Koloutsou, Polyxeni Delaporta, Polyzois Makras, Athanasios D Anastasilakis, Eva Kassi, Antonis Kattamis, Paraskevi Xekouki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoporosis is a major complication of thalassemia bone disease, leading to increased fracture risk and impaired quality of life. Anti-resorptive agents, such as bisphosphonates and denosumab, have shown efficacy in randomized trials, but data are scarce, regarding the role of anabolic agents in severe cases with fragility fractures. We report 2 cases with severe osteoporosis associated to thalassemia with multiple vertebral and non-vertebral fractures, despite long term-treatment with anti-resorptive agents. Both patients were initiated on subcutaneous romosozumab (210 mg once monthly) for 12 mo. At 12 mo, BMD showed marked improvement at the LS by 6.8%-15.4%, TH by 14.6%, and FN by 8.7%-24.6%. Bone turnover markers changed in line with clinical trials of postmenopausal osteoporosis. No adverse events occurred during the treatment period. Following completion of romosozumab therapy, each patient received a single infusion of zoledronate to consolidate the bone mass gained. Over 24 mo follow-up, no new fragility fractures were observed. Romosozumab can be an effective and safe anabolic option for patients with thalassemia and high fracture risk. While anti-resorptive therapy remains the standard of care, romosozumab may provide additional benefit in thalassemia patients with severe osteoporosis and increased fracture risk.

Introduction

Beta thalassemia is an inherited blood disorder caused by reduced or absent production of the beta-globin chains of hemoglobin, leading to anemia that usually requires lifelong regular transfusions and subsequent iron overload.1 In recent years, conventional treatment with regular blood transfusions and iron chelation therapy, along with the introduction of newer treatment options, including allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, gene therapy, and pharmacological agents, such as luspatercept has significantly improved the life expectancy of patients with thalassemia.2 However, as survival increases, the burden of long-term complications also rises, necessitating comprehensive management strategies.

A common complication in these patients is thalassemia bone disease (TBD), manifesting clinically as osteoporosis, fragility fractures, spinal deformities, nerve compression, and chronic pain.3 The pathophysiology of TBD involves a multifactorial interplay of a variety of risk factors related to thalassemia per se, including chronic anemia with BM expansion (mainly affecting trabecular bone), iron overload, iron chelator-related toxicity and chelator-induced hypercalciuria, hypogonadism, GH/IGF-1 axis dysfunction, vitamin D deficiency inflammatory cytokine activity (elevated interleukin-1α [IL-1α], IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α]), poor nutritional status, and limited physical activity.4 In beta-thalassemia osteoporosis predominantly affects the LS (50.7%-74.1%) compared to the femur (10.8%-37.9%), with fragility fractures occurring in up to 44% of patients, especially after the age of 20 yr.5

Bisphosphonates (BPs) are the most widely used agents for the prevention and treatment of bone loss in TBD, followed by denosumab (Dmab), a human monoclonal antibody targeting RANKL.4,6,7 Limited evidence is available for the osteoanabolic agent teriparatide, mostly from case reports and a small case series, which suggest favorable efficacy and safety outcomes.8–10 Currently, no data exist regarding the use of romosozumab—a monoclonal antibody that inhibits sclerostin, which is approved for postmenopausal women with osteoporosis at high fracture risk—in patients with TBD.5

Herein, we present the treatment course of 2 patients with TBD with a history of multiple fragility fractures on long-term anti-osteoporotic treatment who initiated therapy with romosozumab.

Case 1

A 59-yr-old woman with transfusion-dependent beta thalassemia (genotype: homozygous for IVSI-6T>C), was referred from the Pediatric Hematology/Thalassemia Unit, where she had been followed since her initial diagnosis at the age of 2.5 yr.

She presented to our osteoporosis outpatient clinic with severe postmenopausal osteoporosis, a history of multiple rib fractures and an intertrochanteric fracture of the right FN. While on Dmab therapy, she sustained 2 new fragility fractures: a right femoral shaft fracture and a fracture of the 12th thoracic vertebra (T12). She had previously been treated with oral BPs (Risedronate) for over 7 yr, followed by 5 yr of Dmab.

Her gynecological history included menarche at the age of 12 and regular menstrual cycles until menopause at the age of 50. Regarding her hematologic history, she was under regular blood transfusions (every 3 wk) and received iron chelation therapy with deferasirox (14 mg/kg/d) without the evidence of cardiacand with mild liver iron overload as assessed by her most recent MRI [liver iron concentration: 2.2 mg/g Fe dry weight (d.w) − normal range <1.8 mg/g d.w. and cardiac T2*:39 ms − normal range >20 ms]. She underwent cholecystectomy at the age of 55. Due to iron chelation-induced hypercalciuria, she was also treated with hydrochlorothiazide.

Treatment

Due to persistent hypercalciuria despite high-dose hydrochlorothiazide and the potential exacerbation risk with teriparatide, romosozumab therapy (210 mg subcutaneous monthly for 12 mo) was initiated 6 mo after the last Dmab injection.

Outcome and follow-up

Bone turnover markers (BTMs), P1NP (procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide) and β-CTX (C-terminal telopeptide of type 1 collagen) were quantitatively measured on a Roche automated analyzer (Elecsys cobas e series) using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) at baseline, 3, 6, and 12 mo (Figure 1B). The intra- and inter-assay co-efficient variation (CV) were ≤1.7% and ≤4.4%, respectively, for P1NP and ≤2.6% and ≤4.1%, respectively for β-CTX. The bone formation marker P1NP, increased from baseline (63.2 ng/mL) to 3 mo of romosozumab therapy (144 ng/mL), remained elevated at 6 mo (139 ng/mL), and decreased toward baseline by 12 mo (70.6 ng/mL) (Figure 1B). The bone resorption marker β-CTX, declined from baseline (1.18 ng/mL) to 3 mo (0.596 ng/mL), rose slightly at 6 mo (0.955 ng/mL), and decreased again at 12 mo (0.572 ng/mL) (Figure 1B).

After completion of romosozumab therapy, significant BMD gains were observed at all sites: +6.8% at the LS, +24.6% at the FN, and +14.6% at the TH (Figure 1A). No adverse events or new fragility fractures occurred during treatment. One month after the final romosozumab dose, the patient received a single i.v. infusion of zoledronate (Zol) (5 mg), which was well tolerated without acute phase reactions or other adverse effects. A sustained decline in BTMs was observed two months post-infusion (Figure 1B).

Case 2

A 65-yr-old woman with non-transfusion dependent beta thalassemia, presented to our Osteoporosis outpatient clinic, with three recent low-energy vertebral fractures while on long-term treatment with anti-osteoporotic agents.

The patient had no transfusion requirements and maintained hemoglobin levels around 10 g/d. Due to extramedullary hematopoietic foci, she had received radiotherapy for paraspinal lesions. Her gynecological history included menarche at age 12, 2 pregnancies, and regular menstrual cycles until bilateral oophorectomy at the age of 30 due to suspicious ovarian cysts, with negative histology for malignancy and without subsequent hormone replacement therapy. The rest of the medical history included Factor V Leiden thrombophilia, chronic atrial fibrillation, and hyperuricemia. The patient underwent splenectomy at 20 yr of age, which was subsequently complicated by reactive thrombocytosis and leukocytosis and cholecystectomy at 28 yr of age.

With respect to bone disease, she had sustained thoracic vertebral fractures at T10-T12 at the age of 50, which were managed with kyphoplasty. Since then, she has received sequentially nearly all available anti-osteoporotic treatments, including 3.5 yr of Dmab, teriparatide (2 separate courses-totaling 2 yr), strontium ranelate for 1 yr, BPs for 1 yr, and an additional 3.5 yr of Dmab.

During her last course of Dmab, she developed severe low back pain. Imaging revealed a low-energy fracture of L3, requiring hospitalization for 15-d, followed by a 3-mo inpatient rehabilitation program. She was subsequently managed with a spinal brace and continued outpatient rehabilitation. Follow-up imaging identified additional low-energy fractures, at L4 and S1 vertebrae (Figure 2).

Comprehensive laboratory evaluation including calcium metabolism was unremarkable (Table 1).

While on Dmab therapy, her most recent DXA results were as follows: LS, 0.831 g/cm2 (T-score: −2.9); left FN, 0.713 g/cm2 (T-score:-2.2); and right FN, 0.692 g/cm2 (T: −2.4).

Treatment

Given the occurrence of multiple spontaneous fractures while on long-term treatment, the patient met the criteria of treatment failure.11 In accordance with national osteoporosis guidelines,11 romosozumab (subcutaneous 210 mcg monthly for 12 mo) was initiated as the only remaining pharmacological option.

Outcome and follow-up

After 12 mo of romosozumab therapy, substantial BMD improvements were observed: +15.4% at LS, +8.7% at left FN, and +10.4% at right FN (Figure 3). Bone turnover markers were not available in this patient. No new fragility fractures or other adverse events occurred, and the patient subsequently received a single dose of Zol 5 mg i.v.

Discussion

Our patients represent 2 documented cases with severe osteoporosis and multiple vertebral fractures related to TBD, who responded very well to romosozumab, after failure of previous anti-osteoporotic regimes, highlighting the drug’s potential as a safe, well tolerated and efficacious treatment in TBD with increased fracture risk. Prior studies in TBD have predominantly evaluated oral BPs,12–15 whereas evidence for subcutaneously administered denosumab remains limited to one randomized controlled trial and one prospective open-label study, both demonstrating increases in BMD.16,17 Zoledronate has also been extensively studied using a range of dosing regimens, all demonstrating clinically meaningful improvements in BMD, albeit with differences in intensity, biological effects, and tolerability. Early studies employing low-dose regimens (1 mg intravenously every 3 mo) showed significant increases in LS BMD accompanied by favorable modulation of BTMs, including reductions in N-terminal telopeptide (NTX) and osteocalcin and a delayed but significant increase in osteoprotegerin (OPG), suggesting suppression of osteoclast activity through the RANK/RANKL/OPG pathway.18–20 This regimen was well tolerated, with minimal adverse events, and appeared particularly suitable for young thalassemia patients with severe bone turnover abnormalities. Escalation to higher-dose regimens (4 mg intravenously every 3 mo)21,22 resulted in more pronounced and rapid BMD gains at the LS and hip, as well as improvements in pain scores and functional status, but at the cost of increased acute-phase reactions. Comparative studies later demonstrated that annual administration of zoledronic acid (4-5 mg once yearly)20,22 achieved significant improvements in LS BMD comparable—or in some analyses superior—to the quarterly regimen, while being associated with fewer adverse effects, improved adherence, and lower treatment burden.20 Importantly, cumulative annual exposure appeared sufficient to suppress the high bone resorption, characteristic of TBD, challenging earlier assumptions that more frequent dosing was necessary in this population.23

Data on the anti-fracture efficacy of anti-osteoporotic agents in TBD are relatively scarce.12–16 Notably, a 3-yr extended follow-up study with Zol, demonstrated persistent beneficial effect in bone mass and anti-fracture efficacy even after treatment discontinuation, a feature of particular relevance in patients with TBD who usually initiate anti-osteoporotic treatment at younger ages compared to non-thalassemia patients.24

Teriparatide, abaloparatide, and romosozumab are currently the only approved bone anabolic treatments for postmenopausal osteoporosis with established anti-fracture efficacy.25–27 Teriparatide exerts its anabolic effect by inducing differentiation of pre-osteoblasts into active osteoblasts, stimulating the function of bone lining cells and also attenuating apoptosis of osteoblasts and osteocytes.28 Additionally, it reduces sclerostin levels, relieving its inhibitory effect on Wnt signaling, which may be beneficial in TBD, where sclerostin levels appear to be elevated.28,29 Data on the use of teriparatide in TBD are limited to a retrospective observational study including 6 patients,9 and 2 case reports.8,10 Two years of treatment with teriparatide was associated with significant increases in BMD at the LS and FN, and protection against new fragility fractures, although considerable withdrawal (up to 50% of the patients that initiated treatment) due to side effects including fever and severe osteo-muscular pain during the s.c. injections was also reported.9 Up to date no data is available for abaloparatide in TBD.

Romosozumab, is a monoclonal antibody, which directly targets and inactivates the Wnt inhibitory protein sclerostin. Through activation of the Wnt signaling, it both stimulates osteoblast-mediated bone formation and inhibits osteoclast-mediated bone resorption.30 Although its action in TBD has not yet been examined, sclerostin could be a potential therapeutic target, as osteocyte apoptosis induced by iron overload leads to its increased expression and elevated serum levels of sclerostin have been reported in beta-thalassemia patients.29,31

In postmenopausal osteoporosis, 12 mo treatment with romosozumab, induces significant BMD increases of approximately 11.3% at the LS, 4.1% at the TH, and 3.7% at the FN, with established anti-fracture efficacy.32,33 In our TBD patients, LS BMD increased by 6.8%-15.4%, FN BMD by 8.7%-24.6%, and TH BMD by up to 14.6%, with no new fragility fractures during the study period.33 Changes in BTM were also similar to changes reported in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with postmenopausal osteoporosis, in line with the drug’s dual mechanism of action, which simultaneously increases bone formation and decreases bone resorption.25,33

Romosozumab has been associated with a numerical imbalance in serious adjudicated cardiovascular events in the ARCH trial when compared with alendronate (2.5% vs 1.9%), although this difference was not statistically significant.25 Importantly, no excess in cardiovascular events was observed when romosozumab was compared with placebo in the FRAME study.33 To date, a direct causal relationship between romosozumab and severe cardiovascular adverse events has not been definitively established.

Nevertheless, based largely on the ARCH trial findings, romosozumab is currently contraindicated in patients with a history of myocardial infarction or stroke within the preceding year in US or ever, according to European and some Asian regulatory approvals. Current guidance emphasizes careful patient selection rather than exclusion based solely on the presence of cardiovascular risk factors.34

Patients with thalassemia are known to have an increased baseline cardiovascular risk due to multiple, interrelated mechanisms.35 In well-transfused patients with beta thalassemia major, iron overload—particularly myocardial iron deposition—is a major contributor, whereas in patients with thalassemia intermedia who are not regularly transfused, chronic anemia, hemolysis, and pulmonary hypertension play more prominent roles.34 Additional contributors include splenectomy, chronic oxidative stress, nutritional deficiencies, and lung disease, all of which may lead to structural and functional cardiovascular impairment.

In this context, iron overload affecting the heart represents a relevant consideration but not an absolute contraindication to romosozumab therapy. Merely having cardiovascular risk factors should not automatically preclude the use of romosozumab in patients at very high fracture risk who may derive substantial skeletal benefit. In our patients, advanced age, chronic anemia, and iron overload (in one case) may have increased the baseline cardiovascular risk; however, neither had active cardiovascular disease nor recent major cardiovascular events. Consistent with current clinical practice, we would avoid romosozumab in patients with active cardiovascular disease (eg, angina, recent ischemic events) or poorly controlled risk factors, such as hypertension or diabetes, while considering its use on an individualized basis in carefully selected patients after thorough cardiovascular assessment.

In conclusion, these 2 cases highlight the potential role of romosozumab as a safe and effective anabolic therapy for TBD, particularly in patients who sustain fragility fractures despite long-term anti-resorptive treatment. Both transfusion-dependent and non-transfusion-dependent thalassemia patients demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements in BMD with no reported adverse events, and no new fractures observed during follow-up.

Currently, anti-resorptive agents, namely BPs and denosumab, constitute the standard of care for osteoporosis in beta-thalassemia major, and long-term treatment decisions must carefully balance efficacy against potential adverse effects, including cumulative BP exposure or rebound bone loss following denosumab discontinuation. Although data on anabolic agents in TBD are limited, anabolic therapy may be considered in selected patients with severe osteoporosis, persistent bone loss, or fragility fractures despite adequate anti-resorptive treatment. In this context, romosozumab may represent a promising therapeutic option, particularly in cases of treatment failure, given that experience with teriparatide, albeit limited, has been associated with a relatively high rate of adverse effects and may exacerbate hypercalciuria, a common finding in thalassemia patients receiving iron chelation therapy.

Nevertheless, the use of romosozumab should be individualized, acknowledging the current lack of disease-specific evidence, and larger prospective studies are warranted to confirm these preliminary observations and better define its long-term efficacy and safety in this unique population.
