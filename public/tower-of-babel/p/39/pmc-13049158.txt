EEG microstate alterations in Parkinson’s disease dementia
Authors: Yuqing Zhao, Jinfeng Xu, Xianling Xu, Jian Song, Jiayu Cai, Haoran Shi, Wei Wei, Xiehua Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate alterations in resting-state electroencephalogram (EEG) microstates across the cognitive spectrum of Parkinson’s disease (PD) and to evaluate their utility as electrophysiological biomarkers of cognitive impairment. Resting-state EEG was recorded using a 19-channel system during a 3-min eyes-closed session in 36 healthy controls (HC), 38 PD patients with normal cognition (PDNC), and 39 PD patients with dementia (PDD). Temporal parameters (duration, occurrence, coverage) of six canonical microstates (A–F) were computed and compared across groups. Correlation analyses were conducted between microstate metrics and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores. Significant group differences were found in microstate dynamics. The PDD group exhibited a longer mean duration of microstates A, C, and E, and a lower occurrence per second of microstates B and C compared to both the PDNC and HC groups (all P < 0.05). Critically, the duration of microstates A and C showed significant negative correlations with MoCA scores (P < 0.05), while the occurrence of microstates B and C demonstrated positive correlations with MoCA scores (P < 0.05). Specific EEG microstate abnormalities are associated with cognitive status in PD. The prolongation of microstates A and C and the reduced occurrence of microstates B and C are stage-sensitive biomarkers that reflect the severity of cognitive decline, providing novel insights into the neural mechanisms of PD-related cognitive dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40029-0.Keywords: EEG, Microstates, Parkinson’s disease, DementiaSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder, imposing a significant burden due to both motor disability and progressive cognitive decline1. Non-motor symptoms of PD can emerge several years before motor symptoms appear2. Cognitive impairment is a particularly significant non-motor symptom due to its substantial impact on patients’ quality of life. Studies report that 20% to 57% of PD patients develop mild cognitive impairment (MCI) within 3 to 5 years of diagnosis, with many progressing to dementia as the disease advances3. Current clinical assessments of cognitive function in PD relies mainly on neuropsychological scales such as the MoCA and MMSE4. The mechanisms underlying cognitive impairment in PD, however, remain poorly understood. Further research is therefore needed to elucidate the pathogenic mechanisms and identify biomarkers that can improve diagnostic accuracy5. Several studies suggest that neuroelectrophysiological testing may reflect the degree of cognitive dysfunction in PD6,7.

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a non-invasive technique with high temporal resolution that has proven advantageous in recent studies of neurodegenerative diseases. Previous research indicates that the occurrence, duration, and sequence of EEG microstates govern the quality of spontaneous thought, suggesting they may constitute fundamental elements of conscious mental processing8. EEG microstates are sub-second, quasi-stable scalp field potential topographies that represent synchronized activity of large-scale neural assemblies9,10. By examining spatiotemporal dynamics of whole-brain neural activity during the resting state, this method reveals rapid reorganization within cortical-subcortical networks10. The six canonical microstate classes (types A–F) correspond to activations in auditory, visual, default mode, and attention networks11. These microstates are linked to distinct stages of cognitive processing and reflect specific resting-state networks12. They have been shown to correlate significantly with cognitive impairment severity in disease such as Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia13. Furthermore, studies utilizing resting-state EEG connectivity and network analyses have consistently demonstrated disruptions within fronto-striato-thalamo-cortical circuits in cognitively impaired PD patients, particularly affecting the default mode and fronto-parietal networks14,15.Studies indicate6 that the likelihood of transitioning from microstate B (visual network) to microstate D (attention network) is diminished in patients with PD-MCI. This reduction correlates with the decline in the visuospatial score of MoCA and precedes structural imaging changes. The pathophysiology of cognitive dysfunction in PD involves frontostriatal circuit dysregulation from dopaminergic loss, along with aberrant cortico-limbic connectivity16.

In recent years, EEG microstate analysis has been applied to explore the neural network dynamics of Parkinson’s disease. A recent systematic review by Li and Zhu17 comprehensively synthesized the application of this technique in PD, highlighting its promise for identifying disease-specific brain activity patterns, assessing cognitive impairment, and evaluating therapeutic interventions. Costa and colleagues found that even under medication, the microstate C of PD patients was abnormal, and its dynamic parameters were significantly correlated with cognitive function and quality of life18. Furthermore, studies from event-related potentials have shown that compared with patients with Alzheimer’s disease dementia (ADD), Parkinson’s disease dementia (PDD) patients exhibit more significant frontal lobe delta/theta rhythm disorders when performing cognitive tasks 19, suggesting that the neural network damage in PDD may be specific. However, as highlighted in the recent systematic review by Li and Zhu, a major limitation persists in the field: most existing microstate studies in PD have either analyzed patients as a heterogeneous group or relied on broad comparisons (e.g., PD vs. HC, or PD-MCI vs. HC)17. This approach has left a critical gap in our understanding—namely, how spatiotemporal microstate characteristics specifically evolve across the full cognitive continuum, from healthy aging to PD with normal cognition (PD-NC) and ultimately to Parkinson’s disease dementia (PDD). Consequently, it remains unknown whether the progression to PDD is driven by the linear deterioration of a single core network or results from a nonlinear, synergistic disruption of multiple networks.

To address this, the present study investigates EEG microstate patterns in healthy controls (HC), PD patients with normal cognition (PD-NC), and PDD patients. We hypothesize that PDD is characterized by a specific "multi-network dysregulation" profile, distinct from earlier stages, particularly involving microstates associated with the default mode and salience networks. By delineating these microstate signatures, this work aims to provide novel insights into the large-scale brain network dynamics that underlie cognitive deterioration in PD and to identify potential electrophysiological biomarkers for early detection and progression monitoring of PDD.

Methods

Participants

A total of 113subjects of the Rehabilitation Hospital affiliated to Fujian University of Chinese Medicine from May 2024 to May 2025 were included. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Rehabilitation Hospital affiliated with Fujian University of Chinese Medicine and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All the subjects were from the Parkinson’s Disease study cohort(No.2023KY-056–002). All participants provided written informed consent before participating and were free to withdraw at any time. Our research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinkii.

The inclusion criteria for PD were: 1) meeting the 2015 diagnostic criteria for Parkinson’s disease as revised by the International Movement Disorder Society (MDS)20; 2) the ability to cooperate with EEG assessments and the behavioral scales used in this study. Exclusion criteria comprised: 1) a history of brain trauma, cerebrovascular disease, brain tumor, or severe dysfunction of other organs; 2) a diagnosis of secondary Parkinsonism or Parkinson-plus syndromes; 3) a history of mental illness; 4) cognitive impairment attributable to other central nervous system diseases.

Clinical and neuropsychological assessments

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment Scale (MoCA) was used to assess cognitive function. Based on their MoCA scores21, the 77 PD patients were divided into three groups: the PDD consisting of 39 subjects, and the PDNC comprising 38 subjects with MoCA scores between 26–30. Patients with PDD were screened in accordance with the clinical diagnostic criteria for PDD established by the International Movement Disorder Society (MDS)22. All available information was used including medical files, neuropsychological testing, cognitive decline detected either by the repeated neuropsychological evaluations and or by MoCA, and an interview by the study nurse to detect functional decline and information from family members. Thirty-six healthy controls (HC) matched for age and gender were also recruited. All the subjects were native Chinese speakers and right-handed. The inclusion criteria of the healthy control group were: (1) aged between 45 and 80 years; (2) The age and gender were matched with those in PD group; and (3) No history of neurological or mental illness.

Motor impairment of PD patients was assessed by means of H&Y stages and MDS-UPDRS23. Moreover, the motor subscores were calculated as the sum of the specific items of MDS-UPDRSIII (rigidity, 3.3; bradykinesia, 3.4–3.8, 3.14; tremor, 3.15–3.18; gait/postural stability, 3.9–3.13). Levodopa motor complications were excluded using MDS-UPDRS IV23.

EEG acquisition

EEG recordings were performed in a soundproof, dimly lit room equipped with a comfortable chair. All participants were instructed to wash their hair and ensure their scalp remained dry. Nineteen electrode caps (NVX52 Scalp EEG Acquisition Device (NeuroMed, China) were placed on the subject’s head, and the electrodes were located at Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, Fz, F7, F8, C3, C4, Cz, P3, P4, Pz, T3, T4, T5, T6, O1 and O2. Place a reference electrode (A1 and A2) on the earlobe.

The hardware amplifier utilized in this study is provided by Nanjing Neuromed Technology Co., Ltd, featuring a 52-channel direct current (DC) amplifier. The internal specifications include a 24-bit analog-to-digital (A/F) converter with a sampling rate of 500 Hz, a common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR) of no less than 110 dB, an input impedance of at least 100 MΩ, and a noise level not exceeding 1 μV. The alternating current is set to 220 V and 50 Hz. The sampling rate is configured at 2000 Hz, with a high-pass filter set to 0.5 Hz, a low-pass filter set to 30 Hz, and a notch filter set to 50 Hz. A fast Fourier transform algorithm with a step size of 0.25 Hz, Hanning windowing, and a window length of 4 s is employed.

Microstate analysis

Microstate analysis was then performed separately on the preprocessed data for each condition/epoch duration using the Microstate analysis toolbox in MATLAB. These template maps represent the canonical microstate classes commonly reported in the literature, corresponding to known resting-state networks. The procedure, illustrated in Fig. 1, is described as follows:

(A) The analytical pipeline for EEG microstate analysis. The process begins with preprocessed EEG data, followed by the calculation of Global Field Power (GFP). The topographies at GFP peaks are then clustered to derive canonical microstate templates. These templates are subsequently back-fitted to the continuous EEG signal to quantify temporal dynamics, including mean duration, occurrence, and coverage. (B) Standardized schematic of the 19-channel EEG cap layout based on the international 10–20 system.

1) Extraction of Microstate Topographies at Global Field Power (GFP) Peaks: The GFP, defined as the standard deviation of the electric potential across all electrodes, was calculated for each time point. The topographic maps at the local maxima of the GFP curve were extracted as candidate microstate maps.

2) Template Fitting and Time-Series Labeling: The six standard template maps were fitted to the preprocessed EEG data of each individual subject. At every time point, the topographic map of the EEG was spatially correlated with all six templates, and the label of the best-matching template was assigned, resulting in a discrete microstate time series for each subject. An improved K-means algorithm was used to derive six group-level prototypical microstate maps, which were labeled as Microstates A–F in accordance with standard microstate nomenclature10,24–26.

3) Temporal Parameter Extraction: From the labeled microstate time series, the following key temporal parameters were computed for each microstate class: mean duration (the average time a given microstate remains stable), occurrence per second (the mean frequency of appearance per second), and coverage (the total percentage of time covered by a given microstate). To filter out noise, microstate segments lasting less than 30 ms were excluded from the analysis.27 We already have six predefined map patterns: (Fig. 1A).

We processed 3 min of resting EEG recordings on all eligible patients within 48 h after enrollment. The participants were seated in a comfortable armchair, placed in a dimly lit, sound-damped, and electrically shielded room. Before collecting EEG data, the subjects were required to wash their hair to reduce resistance interference, all subjects were in a conscious and eyes-closed state throughout the data collection process. Throughout the 3-min eyes-closed resting-state recording, the experimenter continuously monitored the participants. Immediately after the recording, participants were queried about their subjective state to ensure they remained awake. Furthermore, during the data preprocessing stage, all data were meticulously inspected, and EEG segments exhibiting clear signs of drowsiness were excluded. The scalp locations corresponding to each electrode of the EEG are illustrated in Fig. 1B.

To define the microstate parameters, we measured the following parameters: (a)Mean Microstate Duration (MMD): Mean length of microstate segments (in time frame) as shown in Fig. 1; (b)Mean Frequency Occurrence per second (MFO): Frequency of occurrence of the same microstate class across all analysis epochs; (c)Mean Coverage (MC): Time coverage percentage of each microstate class across all analysis epochs; (d)Transition rate: likelihood of state changes such as microstate A tansfer to microstate B. These features quantify the temporal dynamics and prevalence of different microstates in the EEG in PD, allowing for group comparisons and investigation of potential associations with cognitive function.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Prior to conducting parametric tests, the normality of the data for all dependent variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. For data that slightly deviated from a normal distribution, ANOVA was still employed due to its robustness. For cases where the assumption of homogeneity of variances was severely violated, Welch’s ANOVA was used instead of the standard one-way ANOVA.

To ensure transparency, the specific statistical approach for each variable was determined as follows:

Age and Education Years: These variables were treated as normally distributed across groups (Shapiro–Wilk P > 0.05). Therefore, group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc correction.

MoCA Scores: The distribution of these cognitive scores across the combined sample deviated significantly from normality (Shapiro–Wilk P < 0.05) and showed a non-continuous profile. Consequently, they were treated as non-normally distributed. Group comparisons were conducted using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction.

EEG Microstate Parameters: Microstate temporal features such as MMD, MFO and MC were also extracted and compared among the groups using ANOVA test, post-hoc multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction method to evaluate the difference between HC, PDNC and PDD. For these comparisons, significance was determined at P < 0.05. Prior to correlation analysis, the normality of all continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, with the significance level set at α = 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was applied if the variables were normally distributed; otherwise, Spearman’s rank correlation was used. For all statistical analyses involving multiple comparisons (including post-hoc tests for inter-group ANOVA and all correlation analyses), the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied for false discovery rate (FDR) correction, with the significance level set at FDR q < 0.05. For Spearman rank correlation analyses, the strength of the relationship is indicated by reporting the correlation coefficient and its squared value (ρ2), the latter serving as an approximate measure of the proportion of variance explained by the ranked relationship.

Correlation between microstate parameters and MoCA scores

The correlations between microstate parameters and cognitive function in PD patients are summarized in Fig. 3. After FDR correction, significant associations were observed between specific microstate temporal features and global and domain-specific cognitive performance.

Regarding MMD, both microstate A and C exhibited significant negative correlations with total MoCA scores (A-MMD: ρ = –0.254, q = 0.039, ρ2 = 0.06; C-MMD: ρ = –0.308, q = 0.018, ρ2 = 0.09). This indicates that poorer cognitive performance was associated with longer durations of microstates A and C. Analysis of cognitive subdomains further revealed that both A-MMD and C-MMD were negatively correlated with performance in memory, visuospatial and attention (all q < 0.05), consistent with the overall MoCA trend. No significant correlations were found with language or executive function (Supplementary Table 5 and Fig. 1).

In terms of MFO, B-MFO showed a significant positive correlation with MoCA scores (ρ = 0.244, q = 0.048, ρ2 = 0.06), whereas C-MFO demonstrated a significant negative correlation (ρ = –0.285, q = 0.024, ρ2 = 0.08). At the subdomain level, both B-MFO and C-MFO were significantly negatively correlated with memory and attention (all q < 0.05). Additionally, B-MFO was positively correlated with visuospatial performance (q < 0.05). All reported correlations survived FDR correction, and detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table 5 and Fig. 1. The results were also consistent with the findings of Chu C et al6.

Discussion

This study employed EEG microstate analysis to investigate the neural correlates of cognitive impairment in PDD. Our results demonstrate distinct microstate alterations in PDD compared to HC and PDNC, and these alterations are correlated with cognitive performance across multiple domains. The findings underscore the utility of microstate analysis in uncovering the neuroelectrophysiological mechanisms underlying cognitive dysfunction in PD and complement insights gained from conventional spectral analyses6.

Altered EEG microstate dynamics in Parkinson’s disease dementia

EEG microstate analysis assesses the spatial and temporal configurations of whole-brain states, extending beyond a mere focus on the spectral content of EEG data28. Unlike traditional band power analysis, which primarily reflects local neural oscillatory synchrony, microstate analysis captures the rapid dynamic reconfiguration of whole-brain networks at millisecond temporal scales and is thought to reflect the basic building blocks of conscious thought processing29–31. It provides a spatiotemporal framework that corresponds well with resting-state networks identified by fMRI11,32. Therefore, microstates are not intended to explain more variance in clinical scores than spectral measures, but to provide a complementary and distinct perspective on the brain’s functional organization in space and time10. This perspective is especially pertinent for investigating the disintegration of large-scale network dynamics, a core mechanism increasingly implicated in the cognitive impairments of neurodegenerative diseases33,34. Our analysis reveals a nuanced picture, wherein overt group differences in microstate parameters were largely attenuated after FDR correction, yet the correlational evidence robustly supports their functional significance.

The Salience/DMN network and microstate C: A core alteration in PDD

The most robust group difference surviving FDR correction was a significantly lower C-MFO in the PDD group compared to healthy controls. Microstate C is linked to the salience network and a subnetwork of the default mode network (DMN)7,35. A reduced occurrence rate suggests that this particular brain network configuration is engaged less frequently in PDD. This aligns with theories of reduced brain network flexibility in neurodegenerative diseases and is consistent with prior EEG and fMRI studies in PD that have shown alterations within the salience and default mode networks15,36.

Furthermore, correlation analyses provided compelling evidence for the role of microstate C. We found that a longer C-MMD was significantly negatively correlated with lower MoCA scores after FDR correction. This indicates that patients who remained “trapped” in this microstate for longer periods exhibited poorer global cognitive function. This prolonged duration may reflect neural inefficiency within the salience/DMN, potentially impairing its ability to facilitate task-relevant network transitions37. Notably, while statistically significant, the effect size of this correlation (ρ2) was modest, a finding common in studies attempting to link complex neurophysiological measures to multifaceted cognitive scores in PD38,39. This underscores that cognitive decline in PDD is a multi-factorial process, and microstate dynamics likely capture one important dimension of the underlying large-scale network dysfunction40. The concurrence of reduced occurrence and prolonged duration paints a picture of a dysfunctional network that is both inflexible and inefficient when active, contributing critically to the cognitive profile of PDD6.

Microstates A and B: Correlational evidence for auditory and visual network involvement

Although group differences in microstates A and B did not survive FDR correction, their correlation with cognition remained robust. The mean duration of microstate A (auditory network7,11showed a significant negative correlation with MoCA scores. This is consistent with prior studies linking prolonged microstate A duration to cognitive impairment in other disorders, suggesting a common mechanism of temporal lobe network inefficiency7,9,27. Similar prolonged microstate A durations have been observed in Alzheimer’s disease, indicating a potential transdiagnostic signature of temporal network dysfunction in dementia10.

Similarly, the mean frequency of occurrence of microstate B (visual network10,11) was positively correlated with MoCA scores. A higher occurrence of this visual network-related microstate was associated with better cognitive performance, particularly in memory, visuospatial ability, and attention. This underscores the importance of preserved visual network integrity for supporting broader cognitive functions41.The absence of significant group differences in B-MFO after correction suggests that while the overall group mean may not drastically shift, individual differences in visual network dynamics are strongly tied to cognitive health within the PD spectrum. This aligns with research highlighting the role of posterior cortical networks, including visual processing areas, in PD-related cognitive impairment42.

Microstate D and the effect of medication

No significant differences or correlations were found for microstate D (attention network10,35,43) after FDR correction. This contrasts with some previous studies in unmedicated patients44,45but aligns with evidence that levodopa administration primarily modulates microstate D5. Our cohort of medicated PD patients likely had dopaminergic function partially restored, potentially normalizing microstate D parameters and masking underlying pathological differences. This highlights the importance of considering medication status in microstate research and is a critical factor emphasized in recent methodological reviews of EEG in PD.

Microstate dynamics: The Intersection of pathological inefficiency and potential compensatory mechanisms

A key challenge is interpreting the direction of microstate changes. Our findings, particularly the prolonged durations of microstates A and C associated with worse cognition, are most parsimoniously interpreted as signs of reduced neural efficiency and impaired network dynamics10,29,36. A longer duration indicates a neural assembly is less able to transition away from a given state, hindering the flexible information processing required for optimal cognition.

However, an alternative or complementary explanation posits that prolonged duration may reflect compensatory mechanisms in certain contexts. The brain could sustain activity in relatively preserved networks to maintain function and offset deficits in more affected systems. Therapeutic evidence supports this perspective, as interventions such as deep brain stimulation (DBS) and dopaminergic medication modulate aberrant microstate parameters in Parkinson’s disease patients—for example46, shortening the prolonged duration of microstate A to more closely resemble patterns in healthy individuals. These findings suggest that prolonged microstate duration represents a malleable state rather than a fixed deficit, potentially indicating the brain’s attempt to compensate for impaired network function. For instance, the trend-level prolongation of microstate E (also linked to the salience network) could represent an effortful but ultimately inefficient attempt to maintain attentional control in the face of progressing pathology47,48. The definitive disentanglement of pathological versus compensatory microstate changes requires future longitudinal studies tracking these parameters alongside disease progression and therapeutic interventions.

Correlation between motor symptoms and cognition

Our results reaffirm the established link between motor and cognitive symptoms in PD. Higher UPDRS-III total scores, particularly bradykinesia and rigidity subscores, were significantly correlated with poorer global and domain-specific cognitive function. The distinct patterns of association—bradykinesia with broad cognitive deficits and rigidity with visuospatial, language, and executive impairments—suggest potentially divergent pathophysiological pathways. These motor-cognitive relationships have been documented in prior large-scale clinical studies of PD49. These findings, while descriptive in this cohort due to sample size, underscore the intertwined nature of motor and cognitive decline in PD.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and a larger cohort might have produced more significant results. Second, we did not collect corresponding biomarker samples, which prevented a comprehensive two-factor validation of interactions between clinical factors and biomarker classifications when determining EEG microstates. Third, this study employed linear correlation models to analyze the relationships between cognitive scale scores and electrophysiological indices. It is noted that the distributions of some cognitive variables may not be perfectly continuous or normal. Although Spearman correlation offers some robustness in this context, such analysis inherently simplifies complex relationships. Future studies could consider employing group-based comparisons or more sophisticated non-linear models to more precisely characterize the relationship between different stages of cognitive decline and dynamic brain network changes.

Conclusion

Despite its limitations, our study provides robust evidence that EEG microstate dynamics are intimately linked to cognitive function in Parkinson’s disease. The most robust group finding was a reduced occurrence of microstate C in PDD. More broadly, through rigorous correlation analysis, we demonstrate that prolonged durations of microstates A and C are markers of global cognitive impairment, while the frequency of microstate B’s occurrence is a marker of cognitive preservation. The dysfunction in networks associated with salience processing (microstate C) appears to be particularly critical in PDD. Integrating microstate analysis into the biomarker toolkit for PD holds promise for tracking cognitive decline and understanding its underlying network pathophysiology.
