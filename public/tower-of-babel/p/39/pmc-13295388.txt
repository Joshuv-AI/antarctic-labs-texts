Absence of false negative RDT results due to pfhrp2 deletion among symptomatic malaria infections in Kenya: findings from the 2022 Kenya national longitudinal surveillance of pfhrp2/3 deletions

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApproximately 74% of malaria diagnoses worldwide rely on Plasmodium falciparum histidine-rich protein 2 (PfHRP2)-based rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), whose accuracy can be compromised by deletions in its encoding pfhrp2 gene and its paralogous pfhrp3 genes. Estimating the burden of false-negative RDT results due to pfhrp2 deletion among individuals with malaria symptoms is critical for guiding the continued use of PfHRP2 tests.MethodsThis cross-sectional survey obtained 5394 samples from individuals presenting with symptoms of malaria at selected health facilities in ten counties, Kenya. Each participant was tested using both PfHRP2-based and P. falciparum lactate dehydrogenase (PfLDH)-based RDT. Samples that tested positive by PfLDH and negative by PfHRP2 were scored as discordant. All discordant samples, plus a random subset of the Plasmodium positive samples, were sequenced to characterize HRP2/3 gene deletion as well as polymorphisms in the Plasmodium falciparum kelch 13 (pfk13) propeller gene.ResultsThe HRP2- and PfLDH-based RDTs showed similar positivity rates (44% vs. 45%), with 1.26% discordance. Discordance was highest in Nairobi, Trans Nzoia, and Kisumu, and minimal in Kwale and Tana River. None of the discordant samples carried pfhrp2 deletions, although 5.4% (3/56) had single pfhrp3 deletions, comparable to concordant infections. Among 598 samples that were sequenced, single pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 deletion prevalences were 4.5% and 12.4%, respectively, with no double-deletions detected. Nairobi exhibited the highest prevalence of pfhrp2-only deletions. Tana River, Garissa, Kirinyaga, and Kisii had the highest pfhrp3-only deletions. Among discordant samples, PfLDH-based RDTs identified P. ovale and P. malariae. Finally, sequencing of pfk13 in a subset of P. falciparum infections revealed 0.7% mutation prevalence, comprising A578S (n = 2) Kisumu and A675V (n = 2) each from Nairobi and Kirinyaga counties.ConclusionsAlthough single pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 deletions were observed across Kenya, they do not appear to be the primary cause of false-negative HRP2-based RDT results. Continued absence of parasites with double pfhrp2/pfhrp3 deletions supports the ongoing reliability of HRP2-based RDTs for P. falciparum detection. Routine molecular surveillance remains critical to monitor emerging deletion trends. The detection of the A675V mutation, a WHO-validated marker of partial artemisinin resistance in two counties, suggests limited spread of artemisinin resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05942-9.

Introduction

Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) offer great potential for the prompt diagnosis of malaria infections, enabling timely treatment especially in rural settings. These lateral-flow immunochromatographic tests detect Plasmodium parasite antigens in blood [27]. Current malaria RDTs target three antigens: histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2), Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH), and aldolase [39, 54]. HRP2 is an abundant protein expressed only by P. falciparum and is the antigen most commonly targeted by RDTs to detect this species. Although the antibodies on an HRP2-based RDT are designed to recognize the HRP2 antigen, they can also cross-react with the related HRP3 antigen due to strong similarities in amino acid sequence and shared epitopes [30]. HRP2-based RDTs tend to be more sensitive and heat-stable than RDTs that detect LDH or aldolase [51], World Health Organization, and [54] and indeed generally have the highest sensitivity for P. falciparum malaria [19]. However, parasite strains have been identified that have deletions in the genes encoding HRP2 and in some cases its paralogous gene encoding the HRP3 protein, rendering them undetectable by HRP2-based RDTs [19]. Strains with pfhrp2 and/or pfhrp3 gene deletions thus produce false-negative results on HRP2 RDTs [12, 21, 24, 51]. Notably, an infection with only a pfhrp2 gene deletion can sometimes still be detected by an HRP2 RDT if HRP3 is intact and the parasite density is high often > 1000 p.uL, due to antibody cross-reactivity with HRP3 epitopes [16]. There have been no reports of parasites failing to express pLDH or aldolase, as these target enzymes are essential for parasite metabolism and survival. Of these two antigens, pLDH-based RDTs have shown better performance than aldolase-based RDTs in detecting all Plasmodium species, making pLDH a better complement to HRP2-based RDTs in regions with overlapping endemicity of multiple malaria species. Furthermore, pLDH-based tests are not susceptible to the prozone effect, and the pLDH antigen clears rapidly from the bloodstream once parasites are eliminated, thereby reducing the occurrence of false-negative results at very high parasite densities as well as false-positive results due to persistent HRP2 antigenemia [35]

In 2010, P. falciparum parasites with pfhrp2/3 gene deletions were first reported in the Amazon Basin of Peru [21]. Retrospective analyses in Peru’s Loreto region showed that the prevalence of pfhrp2/3-deleted parasites increased from 20.7% in samples collected during 1998–2001 to 40.6% in 2003–2005 [21]. Since then, the prevalence of pfhrp2/3 deletions has shown substantial local variability. Studies in other countries such as India [15], Mali [29], Honduras [1], Ghana [7], Colombia [20, 36], Myanmar [31], Suriname [6], Guyana [1], and Senegal [55], generally found much lower prevalence estimates (often well under 5%) [4], although differences in study design make direct comparisons difficult. In contrast, some regions have recently documented alarmingly high deletion frequencies. For example, in Eritrea the prevalence of dual pfhrp2/pfhrp3 deletions was found to be extremely high (reaching ~ 80% in certain sites), prompting an urgent policy change away from an HRP2-only testing strategy [12]. Elsewhere in Africa, the frequency of the pfhrp2 deletion remained less than 1% in Tanzania [42] and Uganda [2, 3] as of 2024, while some regions of South Sudan show up to 15.6% [34].

While prompt diagnosis must be coupled with effective antimalarial [50, 52], the emergence and spread of artemisinin resistance present a major challenge to global malaria control efforts. This resistance was first identified in Southeast Asia and has been strongly associated with mutations in the P. falciparum kelch 13 (pfk13) propeller domain [8]. Of particular concern is the recent independent emergence of validated pfk13 resistance-associated mutations, such as A675V and R561H, in East Africa, including Uganda and Rwanda, where delayed parasite clearance following ACT treatment has been reported [10, 46] as well as sections of Kenya [5, 28, 40]. These findings underscore the importance of proactive molecular surveillance in African settings to detect early signals of resistance.

In Kenya, pfhrp2/3 deletions have been observed primarily in asymptomatic infections. An earlier survey reported ~ 10% pfhrp2 deletion prevalence among asymptomatic individuals in two endemic regions namely Mbita in western Kenya and Kilifi in coastal Kenya [14]. A follow-up study on cross-sectional samples collected between 2018 and 2020 from asymptomatic individuals in the Lake Victoria region detected parasites carrying double pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 gene deletions in 36/125 = 28.8% of 125 samples suspected pfhrp2/3 deletion samples with good quality of DNA [37]. This converts to 2.5% (36/1450) prevalence among PCR confirmed P. falciparum samples.

In settings where microscopy is either unavailable or not feasible due to time or resource constraints, it is imperative that malaria be diagnosed and treated based on RDT results. The Kenya Ministry of Health recognizes the disparity in resources across health facilities—some higher-level hospitals (levels 4–6) are well-equipped with microscopy and trained staff, whereas lower-level facilities (levels 1–3) have fewer personnel and rely on RDTs. Therefore, microscopy is recommended at well-resourced hospitals, while supporting RDT use at peripheral facilities. In such settings, monitoring the accuracy of RDT results is critical to validate continued reliance on this diagnostic method. The main causes of false-negative RDT results include product quality issues, improper transportation or storage conditions, operator error, or parasite densities below the limit of detection. However, deletions of the gene encoding the target antigen must also be considered as a contributor to false-negative results. To avert a crisis like the one that emerged in Eritrea and Djibouti, WHO has urged that countries with any reports of pfhrp2/3 deletions as well as neighboring countries to conduct surveillance for such deletions, particularly among symptomatic patients. Indeed, current WHO guidelines recommend switching to a non-HRP2-based diagnostic method if pfhrp2 gene deletions are found to cause ≥ 5% of P. falciparum RDT results to be false-negative [43, 49]. Because of the complexity of the procedures and the high stakes associated with misidentifying PfHRP2 deletions, it is critical that such results are accurate. This survey established the burden of clinically significant Pfhrp2/3 deletions in 120 health facilities within ten counties located in the five malaria transmission zones of Kenya.

Study design and methods

This survey was carried out at selected health facilities among individuals seeking treatment at the outpatient department with symptoms of malaria as recommended by the WHO protocol for surveillance of pfhrp2/3 deletions [53].

Between 01–January 2022 and 03 -December 2023, we conducted a cross-sectional survey to establish the prevalence of P. falciparum parasites carrying pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 gene deletions among symptomatic patients seeking care across 120 health facilities selected from the five malaria transmission zones defined in the Kenya Malaria Indicator Survey (2015). In line with WHO recommendations, surveillance prioritized areas in Kenya with reported discordance between HRP2 and PfLDH RDT results, regions lacking representative deletion data, and areas bordering countries with confirmed pfhrp2/3 deletions. Sixty facilities were drawn from six counties in high-transmission zones, including the lake endemic: Kisumu and Homabay, highland epidemic: Kisii and Trans Nzoia, and coastal endemic regions: Kwale and Kilifi. The remaining sixty facilities were selected from four counties representing the semi-arid seasonal: Garissa and Tana River as well as low-transmission zones: Nairobi and Kirinyaga, ensuring broad geographical coverage of P. falciparum endemic areas. Throughout this period, study staff sitting at the selected hospital sites situated within the outpatient department recruited individuals presenting at the healthcare facilities with symptoms of malaria and willing to participate in the study.

Male and female individuals, aged six months and above presenting with symptoms meeting the national case definition for suspected malaria were enrolled upon providing a written informed consent (or assent for minors) and agreeing to interact with the study team for approximately 15 min. Individuals were excluded if they had participated in this survey already, had received artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) within the past seven days, or declined consent. Additionally, patients with severe illness or severe malaria, those for whom venipuncture was contraindicated (e.g., severe anemia), prisoners or individuals in correctional facilities, children without a parent or guardian, and infants weighing less than 5 kg were excluded.

Enrolment into the study

Patients meeting the case definition for suspected malaria were tested using both the routine HRP2-based rapid diagnostic test (RDT) (Bioline™ Malaria Ag P.f test (Abbott Diagnostics Korea, Inc.) and a P. falciparum-specific PfLDH RDT (Bioline™ Malaria Ag P.f (HRP2/pLDH) (05FK90) from Abbott Diagnostics Korea Inc.) in order to identify suspected false-negative HRP2 RDT results at health facilities. For each participant, at least two dried blood spot (DBS) samples were collected on filter paper alongside the diagnosis sample. Diagnosis outcome was recorded as negative if no bands were returned by either of the test, positive if all or either of the test bands was seen, and discordant if the PfHRP2 band was negative when the Pf-LDH band was reactive. All Individuals testing positive by either of the tests were treated with Coartem® (Novartis, Basel, Switzerland), which contains artemether-lumefantrine (AL) in accordance with the Kenya Ministry of Health recommended case management guidelines for uncomplicated malaria. The attending clinician administered and observed the first dose of Coartem® 20 mg/120 mg tablets, with individuals weighing 5 to < 15 kg prescribed 1 tablet per dose, 15 to < 25 kg 2 tablets per dose, 25 to < 35 kg 3 tablets per dose and individuals greater than 35 kg 4 tablets per dose. Each patient was given the remaining 5 doses (i.e. 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 tablets for each weight range) and advised taking the next dose after eight hours, followed by the remaining doses at 12 hourly intervals till completion of treatment.

Discordant diagnosis

To identify suspected false-negative HRP2 RDT results, all enrolled patients were tested with both HRP2 and P. falciparum-specific PfLDH RDTs at health facilities. Molecular confirmation of infection and genotyping for pfhrp2/3 deletions were performed primarily on dried blood spot (DBS) samples collected on Whatman® 903 filter paper from individuals with discordant RDT results (samples gave negative results by HRP2 RDT but gave positive results by pf-pLDH RDT). This was a recommended screening method for pfhrp2/3 deletions by the WHO, as the discordant samples are most likely to contain samples with pfhrp2/3 deletions. Samples positive for HRP2, but negative for Pf-pLDH were not included in the discordant samples.

Molecular analyses

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from a subset of 978 DBS samples including discordant (n = 56), randomly selected concordant (n = 610) and negative RDT (n = 312) samples. Extraction was performed using a commercial silica-membrane–based extraction kit (QIAGEN, Redwood City, CA 94063, United States) and automated on a robotic workstation (QIAGEN, Redwood City, CA 94063, United States), following the manufacturer’s protocols to ensure consistency and minimize cross-contamination. DNA was eluted in a final volume of 100 µL of AE buffer and stored at−20 °C until further use.

The extracted DNA served as template material for downstream molecular analyses, including detection of pfhrp2/3 deletions and molecular markers associated with antimalarial resistance.

Characterization of pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 deletions

The pfhrp2/3 deletion status of all samples was determined using a multiplex quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay, as described earlier [26]. This assay simultaneously amplifies fragments of the pfhrp2, pfhrp3, pfldh, and human tubulin (htb) genes. Two internal controls were incorporated in each reaction: the pfldh gene, which serves as a marker for parasite DNA quality, and the human tubulin gene, which serves as a control for DNA extraction efficiency from DBS samples.

Each PCR run incorporated a comprehensive panel of controls to ensure assay reliability and interpretative accuracy. These included reference laboratory strains with well-characterized pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 deletion profiles, namely 3D7 (no gene deletions), Dd2 (pfhrp2-deleted), HB3 (pfhrp3-deleted), and 3BD5 (double pfhrp2/3-deleted). In addition, serial dilutions of cultured P. falciparum 3D7 DNA at concentrations of 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001 ng/μL were included to assess the analytical sensitivity of the assay. A positive extraction control consisting of normal human blood spiked with P. falciparum DNA was also included in each run to confirm the efficiency of the DNA extraction and amplification processes.

The classification of PCR results was based on quantification cycle (Cq) values and amplification profiles of target genes. Samples with Cq < sub > htb < /sub > values below 30 were considered to have undergone efficient DNA extraction. Samples that failed to amplify pfldh or exhibited Cq < sub > pfldh < /sub > values greater than 35 were categorized as invalid, indicating insufficient or degraded parasite DNA, possibly resulting from low parasitemia, the absence of P. falciparum, or infection with non-falciparum species. Conversely, samples with Cq < sub > pfldh < /sub > values ≤ 35 were deemed valid and included in the analysis of pfhrp2/3 gene status.

For valid samples, pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 deletion status was determined by calculating the difference in quantification cycles (ΔCq) between the target genes (pfhrp2 or pfhrp3) and the parasite housekeeping gene pfldh. Samples with ΔCq values (i.e., Cq < sub > pfhrp2 < /sub > – Cq < sub > pfldh < /sub > and/or Cq < sub > pfhrp3 < /sub > –Cq < sub > pfldh < /sub >) equal to or greater than 3 were classified as pfhrp2 and/or pfhrp3-deleted. This threshold corresponds to parasite populations comprising at least 90% gene-deleted strains, as established in previous studies [13, 23, 26].

Single nucleotide polymorphisms in the P. falciparum kelch 13 (pfk13) propeller gene.

A total of 306/680 (51%) of randomly selected P. falciparum–positive samples were sequenced for the pfk13 gene. Screening approximately 50% of the samples collected nationwide for the hrp2/3 deletion survey, while simultaneously assessing the presence of K13 mutations, was undertaken as an opportunistic approach to maximize data yield and overall study output. Briefly, the propeller region of the pfk13 gene (codons 435–680) was amplified from HRP2 +/pLDH + and HRP2-/pLDH + P. falciparum samples randomly selected from each collection site using published primers and PCR conditions [8] modified for use with Q5® high fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs, U.S.A.) and associated reagents. Amplified fragments were sequenced from both ends by Sanger sequencing using Bigdye reagents.

Results

Participant characteristics, malaria testing history and travel

A total of 5394 participants were enrolled in the survey from across 10 Counties located across the 5 malaria transmission zones of Kenya (Fig. 1). Of these, 56.9% (3,068) were female, while 43.1% (2326) were male, indicating slightly higher participation among women. Majority of participants (98.6%, n = 5310) reported not taking any malaria medication prior to their current visit to hospital while 1.4% (n = 74) had taken antimalarial treatment in the past two months. 147/5247 participants (2.7%) reported having been tested for malaria within the previous two months and 25.2% (n = 37) of this tested group indicated that they tested positive, 65.3% (n = 96) reported a negative result, and 9.5% (n = 14) were unsure or did not recall their results. An assessment of participants' travel history revealed that 10% (559/5394) had recently traveled prior to the survey, with Nairobi County having the greatest proportion of participants with travel experience (73%), reflecting its metropolitan nature and significant population mobility. Moderate travel rates were observed in Kirinyaga (10%), Kisumu (6%), and Trans Nzoia (6%), while the rest of the counties—Garissa, Kisii, Tana River, Kilifi, and Kwale—reported minimal travel (≤ 1%).Fig. 1Map of Kenya showing the counties selected across the malaria epidemiological zones

Suspected false negative HRP2 samples

The comparison of HRP2-based and PfLDH-based RDTs across ten counties in Kenya showed a generally comparable positivity rate of 44.2% (2386/5394) and 44.5% (2401/5394), respectively, suggesting close agreement between the two RDTs (Chi-squared test, p = 0.7538) (Table 1, 2). Discordant results between the two RDTs were 1.3% (68/5394), highest in Nairobi (3.0%), followed by Trans Nzoia (2.2%), and Kisumu (1.9%) while Kwale and Tana River recorded no or negligible discordant results. Plasmodium species and pfhrp2/3 status in the discordant sample set

Pfhrp2/3 status in discordant (HRP2-/PfLDH +) samples

Among 56 discordant samples, 17 (17.6%) samples were classified as valid by the multiplex qPCR as having good quality of P. falciparum DNA for assessing pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 status (Cqhtb < 30 cycles and Cqpfpldh ≤ 35 cycles). 14 of the 17 (82.4%) valid samples were determined not to have deletions of pfhrp2 or pfhrp3, while 3 of 17 (17.6%) samples had single pfhrp3 gene deletion (pfhrp2 + ve/pfhrp3-ve). No pfhrp2 deletions were detected in this subset of samples (Supplementary Table 1). The remaining 39 (67.9%) samples were deemed invalid, all but for one sample was due to pfldh not being detected or Cqpfldh > 35, indicating no or insufficient P. falciparum DNA in these samples.

Species composition of infections

Plasmodium species was determined for 56 discordant samples using a multiplex conventional PCR based on amplification of 18S rRNA gene [41]. PCR results showed that among the 17-qPCR valid (have sufficient P. falciparum DNA) samples, P. falciparum DNA alone was detected in 15 samples, while P. falciparum mixed with P. ovale or mixed with P. malariae was detected in one sample each. Among the 39-qPCR invalid (not sufficient P. falciparum DNA) samples P. falciparum DNA alone was detected in 8 samples (low density Pf), P. ovale DNA alone in 9 samples and P. malariae alone in 4 samples. Seven and 3 samples were determined to have mixed P. falciparum with P. ovale and mixed with P. malariae (Table 3). A further 8 samples were PCR negative which may have resulted from false positive detection by the PfLDH RDT.

The results demonstrated that the PfpLDH detecting RDT used for screening P. falciparum infections in the survey detected non-P. falciparum species. This finding has important implications for the utility of this new generation RDT and pfhrp2/3 deletion surveys globally.

Causes of false negative RDT results

PCR results showed that the combination of low P. falciparum parasite density (low density and mixed species infections of P. falciparum + non-P. falciparum species) and no P. falciparum parasites (non-P. falciparum species infections and negatives) attributed to 69.6% (39/56) of discordant RDT results. While parasites with pfhrp3 deletion accounted for 5.4% (3/56) discordant RDT results, these parasites were not significantly enriched in this subset of samples as compared to the concordant sample set.

Pfhrp2/3 status in concordant samples.

The RDT concordant samples included 610 samples that were positive by both HRP2 and pf-pLDH RDTs and 312 samples that were negative by both RDTs. Of the 610 RDT positive samples, 540 (88.5%) were classified as having valid P. falciparum DNA for assessing pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 status, while 70 (11.0%) samples as invalid. In contrast, the proportions of valid and invalid samples among 312 RDT negative samples were 13.1% (41/312) and 86.9% (271/312), respectively (Table 4, Supplementary Table 2). The results show that the majority of RDT negative samples gave invalid PCR results indicating low or no parasite DNA. Prevalence of pfhrp2/3 deletions

The prevalence of parasites with single pfhrp2 and single pfhrp3 deletions in valid RDT positive samples was 5.0% (27/540, 95% CI 3.30–7.28%) and 12.4% (67/540, 95% CI 9.62–15.8 %), respectively. No double pfhrp2/pfhrp3 deletions were detected (Table 5). The prevalence of parasites with single pfhrp3 deletions in the valid RDT negative samples was 9.8% (4/41, 95% CI 2.66–24.98%). The remaining 37 samples had no deletions of either pfhrp2 or pfhrp3 genes. There were no significant differences in the prevalence of single pfhrp3 deletion between the discordant, RDT positive and RDT negative sample groups (p=0.5245, p=0.4109, p=0.6244). Prevalence of pfk13 single nucleotide polymorphisms

From the 680 P. falciparum-positive samples, 306 (51.0%) were randomly selected and successfully sequenced for the pfk13 propeller gene. Two non-synonymous variants were found: A578S in two samples from Kisumu County and A675V (n=2) in one sample each from Kirinyaga and Nairobi counties. The cumulative prevalence of these mutations was 0.7% (2/306; 95% confidence interval: 0.08–2.34%). The bulk of the sequenced isolates (98.7%, 302/306; 95% CI 96.67–99.64%) contained wild type pfk13 sequences.

Discussion

This study estimated the burden and distribution of P. falciparum parasites harboring deletions in the pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 genes and explored their relationship with false-negative malaria diagnosis outcomes in HRP2-based RDTs among symptomatic individuals across multiple sites in Kenya. The participant population was predominantly female (56.9%), consistent with previously reported gender-based differences in healthcare-seeking behavior [17, 25]. The low proportion of individuals who reported recent anti-malarial use (1.4%) or prior malaria testing (2.7%) minimizes potential confounding factors such as residual antigenemia or treatment-related false positive results, thereby strengthening the reliability of the HRP2-based RDT outcomes. Moreover, the limited history of malaria testing highlights gaps in diagnostic access and utilization of malaria commodities, posing a significant barrier to malaria treatment efforts in Kenya.

Travel

Although recent travel was reported by only 10% of participants, the high mobility observed in Nairobi (73%) underscores the potential for importation and dissemination of P. falciparum strains with pfhrp2/3 deletions across diverse transmission settings [32, 47, 52] while revealing marked geographic heterogeneity in both malaria transmission and human movement across Kenyan counties. The coexistence of higher RDT positivity in western and coastal regions with elevated travel rates in urban centers such as Nairobi suggests that integrated genomic and epidemiologic surveillance is essential to address both local transmission dynamics and the risk of imported infections [52].

Concordance in diagnostic tests and causes of false negative tests

High HRP2 and PfLDH-based RDTs concordance with positivity rates of 44.2% and 44.5%, respectively, separated by only 15 extra positive diagnoses by RDT2 that RDT1, evocative of strong diagnostic agreement between the two assays as has been reported elsewhere [22]. The low proportion of discordant results (1.26%) indicates that both antigen-based tests remain reliable for detecting P. falciparum infections across most Kenyan regions. Nonetheless, several factors may contribute to performance discrepancies between the RDTs. Antigen persistence is a contributor, as PfHRP2 can remain detectable for several weeks following parasite clearance, leading to HRP2-positive but PfLDH-negative results due to residual circulating antigen [18]. Moreover, the study design could underestimate true deletion prevalence because low-density dual-negative infections, cross-reactivity of HRP2 tests with HRP3, and mixed infections containing HRP2-expressing clones could mask deletions. Similarly, low parasite densities may contribute to discordance, since PfLDH, an enzyme linked to active parasite metabolism, generally has a lower sensitivity relative to HRP2-based RDT [45]. Furthermore, deletions in the pfhrp2 gene can result in HRP2-negative but PfLDH-positive outcomes, directly impacting the diagnostic performance of HRP2-based RDTs [21, 43]. However, the potential influence of HRP2 persistence and relatively lower sensitivity of pf-LDH RDTs has been minimized by the design of the WHO survey protocol, which focusing on discordant samples that were HRP2 negatives/PfLDH positives, not the HRP2-positive/PfpLDH-negative samples.

Spatial variation in discordance that was most notable in Nairobi (3.0%), Trans Nzoia (2.2%), and Kisumu (1.9%) appear to suggest differences in malaria transmission intensity, treatment practices, and parasite genetic composition across counties. The elevated discordance in Kisumu, Nairobi and Trans Nzoia appears not to be related to prevalence of pfhrp2/3-deleted parasites because even though Nairobi had the highest prevalence of parasites with a single pfhrp2 deletion, the prevalence of gene deletions varied greatly between these sites.

In all except 3 counties, single pfhrp2 and single pfhrp3 deleted parasites were detected. Importantly, Counties with higher prevalence of single pfhrp2 or pfhrp3 deletions generally correspond to settings with low or unstable malaria transmission rather than areas with sustained endemicity. Nairobi, where single pfhrp2 deletions were predominantly detected, there is no malaria transmission, with most malaria cases attributable to travel-associated importation rather than ongoing community transmission [52]. Similarly, Garissa, Kirinyaga, Kisii and Tana River, where single pfhrp3 deletions were more frequently identified, are largely low- or seasonal-transmission settings, often semi-arid, with limited and unstable parasite circulation and populations with lower levels of acquired immunity. The prominence of single gene deletions in low- or unstable-transmission counties suggests that these deletions may arise or be detected in relatively small, transient parasite populations, including imported or sporadic infections, rather than reflecting widespread local expansion [16]. In contrast, counties with stable, moderate-to-high transmission maintain larger, more continuously circulating parasite populations, which can support greater genetic diversity and sustained propagation of parasite lineages over time [48]. This pattern highlights the importance of interpreting pfhrp2/3 deletion data within the broader ecological and transmission context, as the presence of deletions in low-transmission settings does not necessarily imply increased transmission intensity, but may have significant implications for diagnostic performance and surveillance strategies.

The absence of pfhrp2 deletions in the HRP2-/pLDH+ samples is an encouraging indicator that, at the time of the study, HRP2-based RDTs remained a reliable diagnostic tool for P. falciparum detection across Kenya. While pfhrp3 deletions alone rarely compromise HRP2-based diagnostic performance without pfhrp2-deletion, their presence warrants attention, as these may lead to combined deletions of pfhrp2 and pfhrp3 as increasingly reported in neighboring regions and can render HRP2-based tests ineffective [16, 52]

Findings from this survey show that HRP2-based RDTs remain dependable tools for malaria diagnosis in Kenya, despite the small proportion of discordant results. The findings also underscore the importance of sustained molecular surveillance. Importantly, although no parasites with double pfhrp2/3 deletions were identified at the time of this survey, a high prevalence of single pfhrp2 and single pfhrp3 deletions was identified in some counties such as Garissa and Tana River. With continued use of HRP2-based RDTs, these single gene deleted parasites may evolve into double pfhrp2/3 deletion in the near future causing false negative RDT results. To maintain diagnostic accuracy and direct efficient case treatment, the national malaria surveillance program should incorporate targeted monitoring of pfhrp2/3 deletions, especially in counties showing more discordance and where pfhrp3 deletion prevalence is high [6, 51].

Molecular characterization of discordant samples

Confirmatory molecular testing of DBS excluded 35 samples due to logistical discrepancies, highlighting the challenges of maintaining sample traceability in multi-site surveillance studies, an important consideration for national malaria molecular monitoring programs [6, 51]. Multiplex quantitative PCR (qPCR) effectively verified that 17 (30.4%) of the 56 discordant samples had enough P. falciparum parasite DNA for further analysis of the pfhrp2/3 gene deletion (Cq<30 for htb and ≤35 for pfldh). This percentage of legitimate DNA extracts is consistent with earlier field-based molecular investigations that frequently indicate significant attrition in number of analyzable samples from studies because of low parasitemia, low DNA yield, or degradation [9, 24, 43].

Samples that were negative by both RDTs exhibited significantly lower relative Pf DNA quantities compared to discordant and RDT-positive samples based on the ΔΔCq method, indicating that these are either true parasite negative samples or samples with parasite densities below the detection threshold of both HRP2 and PfpLDH RDTs. The lowest relative Pf DNA quantities median values were observed in Kwale, Nairobi, and Tana River which may relate to the different levels of malaria transmission intensity, vector ecology, and population immunity [11, 38]. This observation underscores the importance of integrating molecular surveillance with local epidemiological data to inform diagnostic and treatment strategies. Interestingly, median Pf DNA quantities among samples with pfhrp2 deletions, pfhrp3 deletions, and those without deletions were comparable suggesting that pfhrp2/3 gene deletions occur independently of parasite density, and intrinsic parasite fitness changes in the burden of malaria in sub-Saharan Africa [47]. Overall, the molecular results corroborate RDT performance findings, indicating that discordance between HRP2 and PfLDH RDTs is largely attributable to other factors rather than widespread pfhrp2 deletions.

Nonetheless, the detection of pfhrp3-deleted parasites, though infrequent, underscores the necessity of sustained molecular surveillance to detect early shifts in parasite population genetics that could compromise diagnostic reliability. These findings reinforce WHO’s recommendation for routine monitoring of pfhrp2/3 deletions, particularly in areas showing elevated RDT discordance, to inform adaptive diagnostic policies and maintain the accuracy of malaria case detection [44, 52]

Our study has several limitations. The absence of double pfhrp2/pfhrp3 deletions suggest the frequency of parasites with double pfhrp2/3 deletions in the population was very low in the 10 counties included in this survey. However, these mutant parasites may exist in some of the survey areas that we did not detect due to sample size limitations or local transmission dynamics. Additionally, gene deletions do not always equate to antigen loss—post-transcriptional mechanisms or HRP3 cross-reactivity may sustain HRP2-based detection despite underlying deletions [21]. Furthermore, we did not fully evaluate the impact of parasite density, which may independently influence RDT sensitivity, particularly in low-density infections (Houngbedji et al., 2022).

Performance of pfLDH RDT

Molecular species identification [41] among 56 discordant samples showed complex infection patterns, indicating that non-falciparum parasites were major contributors to negative HRP2/positive PfLDH RDT results. This cross-reactivity between pfLDH specific RDT with non-P. falciparum species was unexpected. In the WHO pfhrp2/3 deletion survey protocol, it is specially recommended that a PfLDH-specific RDT, not a pan-LDH RDT, should be used in parallel with a HRP2 RDT to screen suspected samples with pfhrp2/3 deletions. This design aimed to enrich samples with pfhrp2/3 deletions, causing false negative RDT results in the discordant sample set. The detection of non-falciparum species in discordant samples underscores a diagnostic limitation that could confound pfhrp2/3 deletion surveys globally, as RDT discordance might arise from mixed or non-falciparum infections rather than true gene deletions. This cross reactivity may be brand specific and warrants further investigation to determine if this will limit utility of this new generation RDT and pfhrp2/3 deletion surveys globally. A similar finding was also reported in South Sudan (Coldiron, 2024 ASTMH abstract). These findings highlight the importance of testing and validating the specificity of pfLDH RDTs against non-P. falciparum species and compare with that of Pan-LDH RDTs.

In addition, the pfLDH RDT detected 8 PCR negative samples. This is likely due to false positive results not due to degradation of DNA as all 8 samples have human tubulin gene detected with comparable Cq values as other samples.

Overall, single pfhrp2 and single pfhrp3 deleted parasites were detected in 7 of the 10 counties included in the survey. The prevalence was highly heterogeneous among these counties. Importantly, counties with higher prevalence of single pfhrp2 or pfhrp3 deletions generally correspond to settings with low or unstable malaria transmission rather than areas with sustained endemicity. Nairobi, where single pfhrp2 deletions were predominantly detected, is characterized by very low local transmission, with most malaria cases attributable to travel-associated importation rather than ongoing community transmission [52]. Similarly, Garissa, Tana River, and parts of Kilifi—where single pfhrp3 deletions were more frequently identified—are largely low- or seasonal-transmission settings, often semi-arid, with limited and unstable parasite circulation and populations with lower levels of acquired immunity.

In contrast, counties with stable, moderate-to-high transmission maintain larger, more continuously circulating parasite populations, which can support greater genetic diversity and sustained propagation of parasite lineages over time [48]. The prominence of single gene deletions in low- or unstable-transmission counties suggests that these deletions may arise or be detected in relatively small, transient parasite populations, including imported or sporadic infections, rather than reflecting widespread local expansion [16]. This pattern highlights the importance of interpreting pfhrp2/3 deletion data within the broader ecological and transmission context, as the presence of deletions in low-transmission settings does not necessarily imply increased transmission intensity, but may have significant implications for diagnostic performance and surveillance strategies.

Interpretation of findings

The absence of pfhrp2 deletions among false-negative HRP2-based RDT results suggests that these deletions are not a major driver of RDT diagnostic failure in the study area at the time of survey. Likewise, the comparable prevalence of pfhrp3 deletions across discordant and concordant samples indicates that pfhrp3 deletion alone does not substantially compromise HRP2-based RDT detection. These results suggest that rather than gene deletions per se, cross-reactivity with non-P. falciparum species and variables like low parasite density or technical issues (such incorrect RDT handling or storage) were the major contributing factors to the reported false negatives [22],WHO, 2021).

Finally, the overall single pfhrp2 deletion prevalence of 4.5% aligns with recent studies from East Africa reporting low frequencies (<5%) of pfhrp2 deletions [7, 14]. A systematic review by Molina-de la Fuente et al. (33) estimated the global prevalence of pfhrp2 deletions at approximately 21.3% and pfhrp3 deletions at 34.5%, with markedly lower prevalence across most African countries. The World Health Organization (2021) recommends considering a diagnostic policy review when the prevalence of pfhrp2 deletions causing false negative RDT results among symptomatic malaria cases exceeds 5%. Accordingly, while HRP2-based RDTs remain suitable for P. falciparum detection in most surveyed Kenyan sites, Nairobi’s elevated deletion prevalence warrants closer monitoring.

Policy and programmatic implications

These findings carry several implications for malaria diagnosis and surveillance in Kenya. First, the overall low frequency of pfhrp2 deletions and absence of double pfhrp2/pfhrp3 deletions suggest that HRP2-based RDTs remain suitable diagnostic tools for effective malaria case detection in the surveyed areas. However, continued routine surveillance is critical, particularly in counties showing elevated deletion rates, to detect emerging trends that may affect diagnostic accuracy (WHO, 2021).

Second, the detection of non-P. falciparum species by the PfLDH RDT poses a challenge to effective screening of pfhrp2/3 deletions using the two RDT screening strategies. The extent of cross-reactivity of PfLDH specific RDTs requires further investigation. The introduction of next-generation multispecies RDTs that detect both HRP2 and PfLDH antigens could improve diagnostic reliability while mitigating risks posed by pfhrp2/3 deletions [5, 40]. Third, the spatial heterogeneity observed underscores the need for subnational decision-making regarding diagnostic policies.

Prevalence of Pfk13 single-nucleotide polymorphisms

Sequencing of the pfk13 propeller gene revealed a predominance of wild-type Plasmodium falciparum isolates across the study sites, indicating sustained susceptibility to artemisinin-based therapies in Kenya. Of the two mutations identified, only A675V is classified by the World Health Organization as a validated marker of partial artemisinin resistance; this mutation has previously been reported as predominant in Uganda [2, 3] but has not been commonly observed in Kenya. In October 2023, a study by Jeang et al. reported the detection of the Pfk13 gene A675V mutation in three field isolates collected from Kakamega County in western Kenya in 2021, a year before the current survey. [28]. The A578S mutation, commonly reported in Africa and not associated with resistance, was detected at low frequency. Other studies have continued to report this mutation in Kenya [5, 40]. Although the WHO-validated resistance marker A675V was detected with low frequency and limited geographic distribution, its presence in these 2022 samples, combined with data from other investigations, appears to indicate growing artemisinin resistance.

Future directions

Future work should include longitudinal monitoring to track changes in deletion prevalence, molecular surveillance integration into routine malaria programs, and evaluation of alternative RDTs under field conditions. Cost-effectiveness studies comparing HRP2-based versus dual-target diagnostic strategies would also be valuable for evidence-based decision-making.
