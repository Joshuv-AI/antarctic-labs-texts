Quantitative Analysis of Early White Matter Damage in Cuprizone Mouse Model of Demyelination Using 7.0 T MRI Multiparametric Approach
Authors: Emma Friesen, Maxina Sheft, Kamya Hari, Vanessa Palmer, Shenghua Zhu, Sheryl Herrera, Richard Buist, Depeng Jiang, Xin-Min Li, Marc. R. Del Bigio, Jonathan D. Thiessen, Melanie Martin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMagnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is commonly used to follow the progression of neurodegenerative conditions, including multiple sclerosis (MS). MRI is limited by a lack of correlation between imaging results and clinical presentations, referred to as the clinico-radiological paradox. Animal models are commonly used to mimic the progression of human neurodegeneration and as a tool to help resolve the paradox. Most studies focus on later stages of white matter (WM) damage whereas few focus on early stages when oligodendrocyte apoptosis has just begun. The current project focused on these time points, namely weeks 2 and 3 of cuprizone (CPZ) administration, a toxin which induces pathophysiology similar to MS. In vivo T2-weighted (T2W) and Magnetization Transfer Ratio (MTR) maps and ex vivo Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI), Magnetization Transfer Imaging (MTI), and relaxometry (T1 and T2) values were obtained at 7 T. Significant changes in T2W signal intensity and non-significant changes in MTR were observed to correspond to early WM damage, whereas significant changes in both corresponded with full demyelination. Some DTI metrics decrease with simultaneous increase in others, indicating acute demyelination. MTI metrics T2A, T2B, f and R were observed to have contradictory changes across CPZ administration. T1 relaxation times were observed to have stronger correlations to disease states during later stages of CPZ treatment, whereas T2 had weak correlations to early WM damage. These results all suggest the need for multiple metrics and further studies at early and late time points of demyelination. Further research is required to continue investigating the interplay between various MR metrics during all weeks of CPZ administration.

Introduction

Multiple Sclerosis (MS) is a chronic auto-immune demyelinating disease which affects the central nervous system (CNS) with histological presentations of ß-amyloid plaques, also called lesions, in the brain and spinal cord of affected individuals (Enzinger & Fazekas, 2015; Filippi et al., 2019; Fjaer et al., 2013; Gudi et al., 2014; Guglielmetti et al., 2020; Hemond & Bakshi, 2018; Krämer et al., 2019; Kipp et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2021; Lipp et al., 2019; Matsushima & Morell, 2001; Nathoo et al., 2014; Petiet et al., 2016; Popescu et al., 2013; Schmierer et al., 2004; Stidworthy et al., 2003; Thompson et al., 2018; Varga et al., 2018; Walton et al., 2020; Wood et al., 2016). Traditionally, tissue damage was considered to be predominant in white matter (WM) tissues, however, recent studies have shown there is substantial damage observed in gray matter (GM) tissues as well (Enzinger & Fazekas, 2015; Klaver et al., 2013; Meyer et al., 2020; MacKenzie-Graham et al., 2009).

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is commonly used to follow the progression of neurodegenerative conditions, including MS (Filippi & Rocca, 2011; Thompson et al., 2018), following its addition to the McDonald criteria (McDonald et al., 2001) and subsequent revisions (Polman et al., 2005, 2011; Thompson et al., 2018). MR images are capable of detecting lesions prior to the onset of clinical symptoms; however, they are limited by a lack of correlation between conventional imaging results and clinical presentations of MS, referred to as the clinico-radiological paradox (Barkhof, 2002; Hemond & Bakshi, 2018). This paradox is most likely due to the inability of MRI to distinguish between different types of tissue damage (Gudi et al., 2014; Krämer et al., 2019; Kipp et al., 2009; Khodanovich et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2021; Lipp et al., 2019; Merkler et al., 2005; Thiessen et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2011; Wood et al., 2016; Xie et al., 2010). MRI is preferred over other imaging modalities due to its non-invasive nature and high sensitivity to differing pathological changes over the course of neurodegeneration (Filippi & Rocca, 2011). Recent developments in MRI pulse sequences, which probe microstructural tissues, have aided researchers in identifying various structural and functional information regarding underlying pathophysiological processes which govern these diseases (Filippi & Rocca, 2011; Krämer et al., 2019; Nathoo et al., 2014; Thompson et al., 2018).

Given the complex relationship between pathophysiology and histopathological presentations of neurodegeneration, we suspect a complex relationship between MR metrics, MR signal alterations, and EM metrics (Friesen et al., 2024; Hertanu et al., 2023; Krämer et al., 2019; Khodanovich et al., 2019; Kipp et al., 2009; Lipp et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2021; Merkler et al., 2005; Thiessen et al., 2013; Wood et al., 2016; Xie et al., 2010). In addition, no single MR metric has been proven to be effective at all stages of demyelination (Ding et al., 2021; Lipp et al., 2019; Thompson et al., 2018) and therefore we propose a multiparametric approach (Friesen et al., 2024). With early disease detection and characterization of the early damage, targeted treatment can begin earlier, which has the potential to lead to better outcomes. It is thus of the utmost importance to study the cuprizone model of demyelination at earlier time points to facilitate determining relationships between MR metrics and MR signal changes and pathophysiology during early WM damage. This may be a start to understanding MR images at earlier time points of MS prior to the onset of clinical symptoms.

Animal models which mimic the pathology of MS are commonly used due to the ability to perform both in vivo and ex vivo MRI, followed by validation via histopathological analysis. Various animal models include Experimental Autoimmune Encephalomyelitis (EAE), Theiler’s Murine Encephalomyelitis Virus (TMEV), or toxin-induced models, including cuprizone (CPZ), lysolecithin, and ethidium bromide. Although toxin-induced models of MS also have drawbacks, including lack of heterogeneity in pathological characteristics, their effects on the oligodendrocytes of the brain make them a commonly used model for studying MS in the mouse model (Procaccini et al., 2015). The CPZ model, described in detail in Friesen et al. (2024; Kipp et al., 2009; Matsushima & Morell, 2001; Torkildsen et al., 2008; Zhan et al., 2020), produces reproducible spatiotemporal reversible demyelination in WM and GM regions of the brain, mimicking the progression of human MS (Friesen et al., 2024). Extensive research has investigated the later stages of CPZ demyelination; however, little has focused on the earlier stages where demyelination is just beginning (Abakumova et al., 2015; Friesen et al., 2024; Herring & Konradi, 2011; Kipp et al., 2009; Skripuletz et al., 2011; Thiessen et al., 2013; Vega-Riquer et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2009; Zhan et al., 2020).

In this work, we chose to study the CPZ model during week 2 and week 3, during the time where demyelination is just beginning. We examine the correlations between MR metrics, as well as between MR metrics and EM findings at different time points and discuss our findings in comparison to later time points in the CPZ diet. This work will help to understand how various pathological changes affect MR metrics and is meant as a means to begin to categorize early WM damage for a means to provide detection and understanding of early WM disease.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Approach

The implemented experimental protocol was approved by the University of Manitoba and University of Winnipeg Animal Care Committees, groups which adhere to the guidelines and principles created by the Canadian Council on Animal Care. A total of thirty-six 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were housed at the University of Manitoba’s Animal Care Facility (sourced from Genetic Models Centre, Winnipeg, Canada). Mice were separated into two groups prior to experimentation, including those receiving chow with added CPZ (n = 18) and control (CTL) mice receiving unaltered chow (n = 18). Cuprizone (CPZ, Sigma Aldrich) was mixed with mouse chow (Teklad Global 16% Protein Rodent Diet) to yield a final concentration of 0.3% (w/w) and pressed into pellets. Due to the expected higher potency, a decrease in concentration to 0.3% w/w was chosen to match previous studies course of demyelination; study results indicate this reduction in dose was successful in producing desired course of acute demyelination. CPZ feeding began immediately following initial collection of in vivo imaging at week 0. Anesthesia and imaging procedures are identical to those used in previous experiments (Thiessen et al., 2013) and are summarized below.

In Vivo MRI

MRI was performed using a 7.0 Tesla 21 cm Horizontal Bore Bruker Avance III MRI Systems with ParaVision 5.0 (Bruker BioSpin) using a Bruker BGA12-S actively shielded gradient system with integrated shim coils (Bruker BioSpin). All in vivo images were acquired using angled coronal slices with resolution of 98x98x750 μm3 with field of view (FOV) of (2.5 cm)2 and matrix size of 256x256. Each mouse which had not yet been sacrificed was imaged in vivo weekly, beginning at week 0 prior to CPZ diet, until week 6. Six mice (CPZ n = 3, CTL n = 3) were imaged per day of week 0 for 6 days to image all 36 mice. The time course of the study is shown in Table 1, outlining mice imaged in vivo and ex vivo, as well as those sent for EM analysis at each week of the study.

Over the course of imaging, a consistent slice placement was obtained across each week and between each mouse using the following procedure. Based upon high resolution T2-weighted (T2W) sagittal image which was taken along the center line of the brain, a coronal slice was centered on the anterior commissure and moved 0.5 mm anterior, while intersecting the rostral region of the corpus callosum (CC) (Image slice 2S in Figure 1). Another slice was positioned perpendicular to the CC and intersected the initial slice through the CC (Image slice 1S in Figure 1). Image slice 1S was used for all MR imaging in the current study. Slice 1S was selected perpendicular to the rostral region of the CC to reduce partial volume averaging effects in the voxels of interest. The rostral portion of the CC was chosen for consistency with previous studies (Thiessen et al., 2013) and has also been shown to be less susceptible to CPZ-induced myelination as compared to other regions of the CC (Stidworthy et al., 2003; Wu et al., 2008).

For in vivo imaging, each mouse was anesthetized using 5% isoflurane in O2/N2O for induction and 1.5% isoflurane in O2/N2O for maintenance using a nose cone during image acquisition. Each mouse was placed in a 24 mm diameter radiofrequency (RF) volume coil with 300 Hz inductively coupled quadrature (NRC Institute for Bio-Diagnostics, Winnipeg, MB, Canada), on a custom-built mouse bed. Physiological monitoring was performed using an MR compatible respiration and temperature monitoring systems (SA Instruments, Stony Brook, NY, USA). A heating circulator bath (Thermo Scientific, HAAKE, Karlsruhe, Germany) was used to maintain external body temperature. External body temperature, and respiration rate were continuously monitored, where appropriate action taken to maintain stable values of 37 °C and 40–90 breathes per minutes, respectively.

Following set up for image acquisition using the procedures outlined above, T2W images were acquired using a Rapid Acquisition with Relaxation Enhancement (RARE) sequence with fat suppression (Hennig et al., 1986; Thiessen et al., 2013). The RARE imaging sequence was chosen for its relatively short acquisition time, limiting the stress placed on living mice undergoing multiple imaging procedures throughout the course of the study. Parameters used for the RARE sequence included effective echo time (TEff) = 80 ms, repetition time (TR) = 1640 ms, RARE factor = 8, and a 10-minute acquisition time.

Additionally, Magnetization Transfer Ratio (MTR) maps were created using two in vivo Fast Low Angle Shot (FLASH) images, with and without a magnetization transfer (MT) saturation pulse (Haase, 1990). Parameters used included echo time (TE) = 6 ms, TR = 70 ms, 48 averages, 10-degree flip angle, and 2x14 minute acquisition time. If used, the saturation pulse was Gaussian-shaped with a length of 10.25 ms, 10 μT saturation amplitude, and frequency offset of 6 kHz.

Ex Vivo MRI

Beginning at week 1, following in vivo imaging procedures described above, a total of six mice (CPZ n = 3, CTL n = 3) were euthanized to perform ex vivo imaging analysis (Table 1). Sacrifice of each mouse involved intracardial injection under deep anesthetic with 10 mL cold 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for approximately 2 minutes, followed by 10 mL cold fixative solution containing 0.5% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for approximately 10 minutes, and finally 10 mL cold PBS to flush out any remaining fixative (Thiessen et al., 2013). Following intracardial perfusion, tissue external to the skull was removed and each sample was stored in a separate tube submerged in PBS prior to overnight imaging. In preparation for ex vivo imaging, the brains (in skull) were removed from PBS and stored within a custom-built acrylic brain-holder and immersed in room temperature Fomblin Perfluoropolyether Y04 grade fluid (Solvay Solexis) to maintain sample hydration and eliminate external proton signals and susceptibility artifacts (Thiessen et al., 2013). Following each imaging session, the brain (in skull) was removed from the imaging tube, rinsed with PBS, and stored at 4 °C submerged in a solution of 2% glutaraldehyde and 2% PFA.

Each ex vivo image was acquired using the same angled coronal slice from Figure 1 with resolution of 98x98x750 μm3 with FOV (1.25 cm)2 and matrix size 128x128. During overnight imaging of approximately 10 hours in duration, the magnet bore was maintained at temperature of approximately 18 °C. T2W images were acquired using RARE sequence with fat suppression; parameters used include TEff = 46 ms, TR = 1640 ms, 4 averages, RARE factor = 4, FOV = (2.5 cm)2, and 7-minute acquisition time. Combined T1 and T2 relaxometry data was acquired using a series of RARE acquisitions with parameters TEff = 11, 33, 55, 77, 99 ms, TR = 5, 3, 1.5, 0.8, 0.4, 0.2 s, respectively and each with RARE factor = 2, 8 averages, and an acquisition time of 71 minutes. MT images were collected using an identical FLASH sequence compared to the in vivo 18 MT saturation pulse images acquired with RF amplitudes of 5, 10, and 20 μT and frequency power offsets of 1, 2, 4, 6, 10, and 30 kHz (64 averages and acquisition time of 19 x9.6 minutes) for each power and one with no saturation pulse (M0/PD image). Diffusion weighted imaged were collected using a Pulsed Gradient Spin Echo (PGSE) sequence with 7-direction tetra-orthogonal gradient-encoding scheme, with b-value = 1000 s/mm2 (δ = 6 ms, Δ = 14 ms), 6 averages, TE = 26 ms, TR = 5000 ms, and a 5-hour acquisition time (Neema et al., 2007).

MR Image Processing and Analysis

In vivo T2W and MT images with no saturation pulse (M0) were aligned to saturation (Msat) images using affine transformation matrix, predetermined by minimizing the 2-D correlation coefficient and mutual image information with a custom-built MATLAB® GUI code, following previous procedures (Thiessen et al., 2013). Ex vivo M0, Msat, T1/T2 relaxometry images were aligned to the smallest b-value diffusion weighted image using the same methods and custom MATLAB® GUI code as in vivo image analysis. For the Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) data, each b-matrix associated with an image was rotated to reflect any rotations applied during registration in order to maintain accurate orientational information (Leemans & Jones, 2009). Diffusion tensors were determined using non-linear least squares fit and a modified Cholesky decomposition in order to ensure positive definiteness (Thiessen et al., 2013). For in vivo measurements, T2W signal intensity was normalized to the CSF signal within the third ventricle (Thiessen et al., 2013). Small partial volume effects can be introduced with this normalization; however normalization allows comparison of signals between studies.

Accurate construction and comparison between different imaging acquisition methods via voxel-by-voxel basis involved image registration. For in vivo imaging sessions, small movements of the head could have potentially occurred between each image due to the lack of ear bars to fix the head in place, as well as a low anesthetic dose, which was used to reduce the chance of overdose (Thiessen et al., 2013). Image registration was also performed in order to account for slight differences in spatial encoding due to gradient field inhomogeneities between spin echo and gradient echo pulse sequences. Ex vivo imaging sessions were relatively long, and therefore potentially allowed for drift in the Larmor frequency of the magnet of approximately –14 Hz per day in the 7.0 T Bruker Magnet (Thiessen et al., 2013). Image registration was therefore used to allow for accurate voxel-by-voxel analysis (Thiessen et al., 2013). Ideal parameters for image registration were found by registering images with the highest signal to noise ratio (SNR), including the lowest b-value image using DTI, the M0 image from quantitative MTI (qMTI), and the first echo image from the relaxometry data set. These parameters, which were used in subsequent image registration, were found to ease the process of aligning images of lower quality in accordance with their lower SNR and high b-values from DTI data, higher RF-saturation from qMTI, and later echo times in relaxometry data (Thiessen et al., 2013).

Electron Microscopy

Following MRI data acquisition, each brain was rinsed with PBS to remove any excess Fomblin from image acquisition and submerged in 2% glutaraldehyde and 2% PFA solution for a minimum of seven months prior to electron microscopy (EM) (Thiessen et al., 2013). MR results indicated that week 2 and week 3 were ideal time frames for the study of pre- and early demyelination induced by treatment with CPZ. Therefore, of the mice sacrificed throughout the course of the study, five mice from week 2 (CTL n = 2, CPZ n = 3) and six mice from week 3 (CTL/CPZ n = 3) were selected for EM analysis (Table 1).

EM analysis followed the general procedure outlined below, adapted from our previous study to allow for ease of comparison between results (Thiessen et al., 2013). Following fixation, brains were removed from the skull and a 1 mm slice was removed, corresponding to the oblique coronal slice used for MR analysis (Slice 1S in Figure 1). A printed full-scale image of the T2W sagittal slice was overlaid with the oblique coronal slice from imaging software in order to correctly identify the slice; the excised brain was aligned to the printed image and sliced carefully using a razor blade. The acquired tissue slice was compared with the MR image to ensure the slices matched using visual methods. A dissecting microscope was used to excise the CC from the slice. Elongated cubes of the CC were fixed using phosphate-buffered 4% glutaraldehyde, followed by 1% osmium tetroxide overnight, dehydrated using alcohol followed by acetone, and embedded in Eponresin. Sections were cut perpendicular to the long axis of the axons within the CC to ensure the view was in cross section. Semithin sections, approximately 1 μm, were stained with toluidine blue. Ultrathin sections, approximately 90 nm, were contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citratethen and were viewed and photographed using a JEOL 1010 transmission electron microscope equipped with a digital camera. An experienced neuropathologist took representative photomicrographs, and differences between cuprizone-fed and control mice were obvious, and therefore blinding was not possible.

In order to account for the cellular features seen in the photomicrographs, a 2x2 μm2 grid was overlaid on two 5,000X EM images per mouse, with 180 points/image (360 points/mouse). At each point, tissue was classified as one of the following features: myelinated axon (a), myelin sheath (m), non-myelinated material (c), or extracellular space (e). Myelinated axons were characterized based upon a full ring of myelin surrounding the axon diameter. Non-myelinated material included demyelinated axons, those which should have myelin, and unmyelinated axons, small axons which do not normally have myelin, nuclei, glial processes, lipid droplets of macrophages, damaged axons, and apoptotic cells. Glial processes, lipid droplets (inside macrophages), damaged axons, and apoptotic cells were observed only in CPZ-fed mice. The fraction of each tissue characteristic (a, m, c, and e) were calculated for both CTL and CPZ groups. Myelin sheath fraction was not included in the myelinated axon fraction; myelinated fiber fraction was found by adding the myelin sheath and myelinated axon fractions. Using two of the 15,000X EM photomicrographs per mouse, the narrowest axon diameters and myelin sheath thickness were measured in all visibly myelinated axons, compensated for possible oblique sections. Axon diameters were grouped into three equally sized groups based upon distributions found in the measurements of the CTL group (<0.3 μm, 0.3–0.45 μm, >0.45 μm) and similarly for the myelin sheath thickness (<0.06 μm, 0.06–0.075 μm, and >0.075 μm). Based upon the distribution of myelinated axons per 100 μm2 was determined. Additionally, the M/A ratio, myelin sheath thickness compared to axon diameter, as well as the g-ratio (axon diameter/fiber diameter) were calculated for all ten mice.

Statistical Analysis

Independent sample t-tests were performed for each week of data collection, for both in vivo and ex vivo imaging parameters of the CC. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used to find significant week-to-week differences within both the CPZ and CTL groups for both in vivo and ex vivo data sets. Tukey’s post hoc test was used to account for multiple testing in accordance with type I errors. A series of mixed regression model procedures were performed on the in vivo metrics, including T2W and MTR, with the number of weeks post week 0 serving as the within-subject factor and the group, CPZ or CTL, serving as the between-subject factor. Such a mixed model allows for the within-subject factor to be treated as continuous and accounts for reduced data as mice were periodically sacrificed for collection of ex vivo images to measure the progression of acute demyelination. The chosen procedure therefore handles a gap in the data more effectively as compared to two-way repeated measures ANOVA (Leemans & Jones, 2009). Statistical analysis was performed using SAS Software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Additionally, Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated between T2W, DTI, and MTI parameters, as well as between each in vivo and ex vivo images using individual voxel values of the CC in all CPZ, CTL and combined mouse data sets. Voxel-based correlations of CC in CPZ versus CTL groups, as well as the combined data set, account for variations in individual mice and treats these correlations as another measure between diseased and control states (Thiessen et al., 2013). Although individual voxels may exhibit more noise as compared to larger sample sizes, such analysis is advantages over Region of Interest (ROI) based analysis due to the assumption that the signals remain constant over the entire CC or that averaging the signal to noise ratio across the CC accurately reflects the pathology of each individual voxel (Thiessen et al., 2013). Other studies investigating WM tract pathologies have used tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) in their analysis, however, Fieremans et al. (2013) found that ROI-based analysis was consistent with TBSS in their analysis, and we therefore chose to focus our analysis on ROI- and voxel-based analysis.

Results

Data collected from week 0 through week 3 are presented below, with additional data collected from week 4 through 6 presented in Supplemental Data.

In Vivo MRI

0.3% (w/w) CPZ diet was observed to cause a significant decrease in mouse body mass as compared to the control mice, as expected (Matsushima & Morell, 2001). Body mass measurements taken at week 6 indicated the CPZ group had an average mass of 20 ± 1 g and the CTL group has an average body mass of 24 ± 2 g; this is consistent with previous studies conducted using the cuprizone diet (CPZ 16.5 ± 1.0 g vs. CTL 21.1 ± 1.3 g) (Gudi et al., 2014; Matsushima & Morell, 2001; Skripuletz et al., 2011; Thiessen et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2010; Yano et al., 2018). Subsequent analysis focused on week 2 and 3 of cuprizone feeding to elucidate changes in MR metrics in early demyelination, although data analysis was performed for all 36 mice over all weeks of the study and is presented in detail in the thesis describing this work (Palmer, 2016). Ex Vivo MRI

Signal changes due to CPZ-induced acute demyelination were also observed in ex vivo image acquisition, using DTI, qMTI, and relaxometry metrics. Ex vivo measurements obtained from each week included n = 3 CTL mice and n = 3 CPZ mice, with the exception of week 1 CTL n = 1 qMTI measurements due to contamination of k-space with high frequency noise in Msat images which was unable to be removed.

DTI metrics, including mean diffusivity 〈D〉, axial diffusivity λ║, radial diffusivity λ┴, and fractional anisotropy FA all increased over the course of the CPZ diet, compared to the relatively constant values in the CTL mice (Figure 4, ROI based; Figure S3). 〈D〉 values of the CC in CPZ mice peaked at week 3, and values from the first two weeks were significantly lower than the values from the last four weeks of the study (p < 0.01); CTL group 〈D〉 remained relatively constant throughout the entirety of the study (Figure 4). λ║ increased significantly from week 1 to week 3 (p < 0.05) (Figure 4). CTL λ║ remained relatively constant over the course of the study, with differences between the CPZ group shrinking in the last few weeks (Figure 4). λ┴ was observed to increase significantly from week 1 to 3 in the CPZ mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 4). Weekly decreases in FA in the CC of CPZ mice were shown to be nonsignificant. Discussion

Determination of relationships between MR metrics and WM pathophysiological may provide a deeper understanding of underlying damage and its development over time during demyelination and neurodegeneration. Observations in agreement with predictions made according to biophysical models strengthen assumptions, while those in disagreement provide steps for model improvement in future studies (Skripuletz et al., 2011).

In Vivo MRI

In vivo T2W signal intensity and MTR values have been observed to have similar correlations to MS tissue pathologies, including alterations in brain microstructure and underlying histopathological changes induced by CPZ diet (Wang et al., 2019). At high field strengths, T2W images provide WM/GM contrast in vivo and changes to T2W signal intensity indicate non-specific microstructural alterations (Tagge et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2008). T2W signal intensity was observed to increase significantly in the CC between CPZ and CTL groups (p < 0.0001) at week 2 (Figures 2 and 3), with the largest week-to-week change occurring from week 2 to week 3 (Figure 2). Correlations between T2W signal intensity and EM metrics include extracellular space fraction (ρ =–0.93, p < 0.05), myelinated axons + myelin sheath fraction (ρ = –0.64, p < 0.05), non-myelinated content fraction (ρ = 0.63, p < 0.05), and myelinated axon fraction (ρ = –0.80, p < 0.05) in the mixed model condition (Table 6).

MTR is known to be sensitive towards WM microstructural properties including myelin loss, myelin debris, inflammation, remyelination, cortical demyelination, axonal density, and infiltration via astrocytes, microglia and macrophages (Friesen et al., 2024). MTR values were observed to decrease in WM regions at week 4 of CPZ diet (Figure 2), with the largest significant decrease occurring between week 3 and week 4 (p < 0.0001; Figure 3) showing strong correlation with non-myelinated content fraction (ρ = –0.89, p < 0.05; Table 6). Such changes in MTR are consistent with studies conducted by Thiessen et al. (2013), Merkler et al. (2005), and Zaaraoui et al. (2008). At week 2 and week 3, EM observations indicated the appearance and activation of microglia and astrocytes (increased cellularity), the beginnings of oligodendrocyte apoptosis, peak apoptotic oligodendrocyte numbers, and early demyelination, which is consistent with Wang et al. (2011). These factors all appear to have a decreasing effect on T2W signal intensity and competing effects on MTR, resulting in a less significant alterations in MTR in response to changes in pathology.

Alterations T2W signal intensity and MTR values over the course of CPZ administration were observed to correlate at all weeks, with the correlation strengthening at later time points (Table 2). While T2W and MTR provide similar information regarding tissue pathology in pre-clinical studies, in clinical settings, independent use of T2W images leads to the overdiagnosis of MS due to lack of pathological specificity, where increases in T2W signal intensity is defined as lesional tissue (Enzinger & Fazekas, 2015; Filippi et al., 2019; Klaver et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2008). The addition of MTR maps to clinical evaluation improves identification of additional MS pathologies, including edema, axonal density, and inflammation, with high specificity for indicating myelin integrity (Filippi et al., 2019; Hemond & Bakshi, 2018). We therefore strongly caution the elimination of one method as a replacement for both methods, as they are commonly used concurrently in clinical diagnosis of MS (Enzinger & Fazekas, 2015; Filippi et al., 2019; Hemond & Bakshi, 2018; Klaver et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2008). At early time points in CPZ demyelination, the Spearman correlation coefficient between T2W signal intensity and MTR values are closer to zero as compared to later time points, further suggesting the need for both metrics when studying weeks 1 through 4 of CPZ administration (early demyelination) (Tables 2 and S1). For instance, significant changes in T2W signal intensity and little changes in MTR corresponded to early WM damage and significant changes in both metrics corresponding to demyelination (Figures 3 and S2).

Ex Vivo DTI

DTI metric 〈D〉 is reflective of cellular status and correlates with myelinated and non-myelinated axon fractions (Yano et al., 2018). 〈D〉 has been observed to decrease significantly with WM demyelination at week 2 and week 3 of CPZ administration (Cerina et al., 2020; Guglielmetti et al., 2016; Yano et al., 2018). In the current study, however, values peaked at week 3 of the CPZ diet (p < 0.01; Figures 4 and S3; Tables 4 and S3), which has been observed in later weeks in previous studies (Cerina et al., 2020; Ding et al., 2021; Guglielmetti et al., 2016; Thiessen et al., 2013; Yano et al., 2018). These studies have found later increases in 〈D〉 indicate cell membrane damage and demyelination (Cerina et al., 2020; Ding et al., 2021; Guglielmetti et al., 2016; Thiessen et al., 2013; Yano et al., 2018), whereas the present study observed correlation with the myelinated axon fraction (ρ = –0.84, p < 0.05; Table 6). Decreases in 〈D〉 have been shown to be proportional to the extent of WM damage in MS lesions (Filippi & Agosta, 2016).

Both λ┴ and λ║ are more sensitive towards myelin status as compared to 〈D〉 (Yano et al., 2018).

λ║ is sensitive towards axonal integrity (Boretius et al., 2012; Ding et al., 2021; Nie et al., 2014; Song et al., 2005; Thiessen et al., 2013) and λ┴ is sensitive towards myelination status (Soustelle et al., 2019). Simultaneous significant increase in 〈D〉 and λ║ from week 1 to week 3 (p < 0.05) corresponds to initial stages of demyelination, most likely attributable to increased intra- and extracellular water (Matsushima & Morell, 2001; Salinas Tejedor et al., 2015; Skripuletz et al., 2011; Varga et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2012). Previous studies have observed significant decreases in λ║ from during week 2 and week 3 with correlations to acute demyelination (Guglielmetti et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2006) despite our agreement with our previous findings, where λ║ increases in response to edema effects (Thiessen et al., 2013). During later weeks of CPZ administration, λ║ has been observed to increase significantly in WM regions (Sun et al., 2006; Thiessen et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2019), which was not observed in the current experiment (Figures 4 and S3; Tables 4 and S3). Small changes in λ║ contradict previous findings by Zhang et al. (2012), who proposed such changes are manifestations of microglial protective functions, enclosing diseases areas, preventing diffusion. Other studies demonstrate, however, that λ║ increased as result of edema, corresponding with CPZ treatment effects (Sun et al., 2006; Thiessen et al., 2013). The large number of inconsistent findings between the current experiment and previous literature suggests the need for additional studies focusing on all weeks of CPZ administration with a multiparametric approach in attempts to fill gaps and solidify findings in current literature.

λ┴ has been observed to increase across all six weeks of CPZ treatment in previous studies (Ding et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2006; Thiessen et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2019; Yano et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2012) and is consistent with the current experiment (p < 0.05; Figures 4; Tables 4 and S12). λ┴ has been observed to correlate with increased cellularity, edema, axonal damage, and loss, which are all indicative of alterations in the internal geometry of white matter tracts (Thiessen et al., 2013) and accurately reflects the severity of CPZ induced demyelination in the CC (Wu et al., 2008). Increases in λ┴ during early weeks of CPZ administration have been correlated with myelin content and demyelination (Ding et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2019; Yano et al., 2018), however, the current study observed only weak correlations between both λ┴ (ρ= –0.40) and FA (ρ= –0.13) and myelinated axon fraction were observed (Table 6).

FA has been shown to be sensitive towards changes in myelin and axonal diameters, indicative of many neurodegenerative disorders (Boretius et al., 2012), however, the current study found no significant changes in FA over the course of the CPZ diet (Table 6). Research conducted by Yano et al. (2018) has observed decreases in FA corresponding to decreased myelin content and demyelination in both week 2 and week 3. Decreases in FA at later weeks (week 4–6) were confounded by λ┴, where demyelination and loss of myelin content were more severe (Ding et al., 2021; Nie et al., 2014; Thiessen et al., 2013; Yano et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2012). These previous findings suggest FA to be an important metric for studying earlier timepoints of CPZ administration and should be considered along with λ┴ at later points in the diet.

Ex Vivo MTI

T2A was observed to significantly decrease at week 3 (p < 0.05; Table 4) and found to have the strongest correlation with EM validated extracellular space fraction in both CTL/CPZ (ρ = 0.63, p < 0.05) and CPZ (ρ = 0.94, p < 0.05) groups and myelin sheath fraction (ρ = 0.99, p < 0.05) in the CPZ group (Table 6). Our previous study observed significant increases in T2A at later weeks of CPZ administration, suggesting the need for additional studies following this metric across all six weeks (Thiessen et al., 2013). T2B was not observed to alter significantly across CPZ administration (Tables 4 and S12), suggesting this qMTI provides information regarding tissue pathology at later stages of demyelination (Portnoy & Stanisz, 2007; Thiessen et al., 2013; Whittaker et al., 2018).

However, f and R have been observed to significantly decrease and increase at later weeks of CPZ administration, respectively (Thiessen et al., 2013), where the decrease in f at week 5 is reflective of myelin content loss (Soustelle et al., 2019; Turati et al., 2015). In the current study, both f and R did not alter significantly from week-to-week during CPZ administration (Tables 4 and S12) and were observed to have correlations to the extracellular space fraction (ρ = 0.72, p < 0.05) and (ρ = –0.62, p < 0.05) respectively (Table 6). f has been consistently shown to have strong correlations with CPZ-induced demyelination in small rodent models (Thiessen et al., 2013; Turati et al., 2015) and the lack of correlation between f and myelinated content may be due to our focus on early WM damage where competing effects, such as cellularity, are present and may mask the effects of demyelination on f. Previous studies have observed significant changes in f to occur with severe demyelination at week 6 (Boretius et al., 2012; Dula et al., 2010; Merkler et al., 2005; Tagge et al., 2016; Thiessen et al., 2013; Whittaker et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2012).

Ex Vivo Relaxometry

Changes in T1 and T2 relaxation times are related to myelination status and axonal damage, respectively (Petiet et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2008). T1 relaxation times were observed to increase in the CC with CPZ treatment, with the most significant increase between week 5 and week 6 (p < 0.01; Supplemental Data Table S12), consistent with previous studies (Boretius et al., 2012; Laule et al., 2007; Merkler et al., 2005; Thiessen et al., 2013; Varga et al., 2018). Lack of changes in T1 during week 2 and week 3 suggests insensitivity towards increased cellularity characteristics of early WM damage observed in EM (Table 6) (Haase, 1990; Whittaker et al., 2018). T1 has been observed to correlate with microscopic tissue damage associated with MS pathology, however it remains insensitive to specific pathologies (Neema et al., 2007; Thiessen et al., 2013) and is liable to confounding tissue alterations, such as myelin integrity (Turati et al., 2015).

T2 relaxation times were also observed to alter non-significantly across the CPZ diet, however a nonsignificant increase was observed from week 2 to week 3 (Table 4) and a correlation with myelinated axon fraction (ρ = –0.68, p < 0.05) was observed (Table 6). As a reflection of the change in cellularity, alterations in T2 are consistent with previous literature (Jelescu et al., 2016; Merkler et al., 2005; Wu et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2012). Previous studies have observed increases in T2 at week 4 with a strong correlation to myelin content (Thiessen et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2008) and decreased with inflammation and edema (Zhang et al., 2012), week 5 (Thiessen et al., 2013), and week 6 (Jelescu et al., 2016; Merkler et al., 2005; Thiessen et al., 2013).

Some studies have measured correlations between MR and EM metrics in the CC of the CPZ mouse. Table 7 summarizes the significant correlations found in this study and other studies which focused on one time point. Our study is the first to report significant correlations in the early stage of demyelination and these correlations are different from those found at the stages of more severe myelination when the multiple anatomical changes have differing influences on the MR metrics.

Limitations and Future Directions

The current study focused on the CPZ model of demyelination; however, there are many other mouse models and human studies which allow for quantification of MRI metrics in other WM disorders. These other models of neurodegeneration may provide alternative means of confirming metric correlations and investigate additional combinations of WM damage. Improvements which should be considered for future experimental designs include accounting for the timing between fixation and shortening the amount of time elapsed between sacrifice and EM measurement collection; a study conducted by Dyrby et al. (2011) found that proper fixation allowed for rodent brains to be stored in fixation buffer for up to 1,106 days without significant changes in DTI metrics (Dyrby et al., 2011).

The current experimental methods employed a b-value of 1000 s/mm2 so that smaller gradient strengths could be used. More recent advancements in MRI metrics have allowed for b-values of 2000 s/mm2 and above (Tang & Zhou, 2019). There are limitations of using smaller b-values, and we suggest that future studies implement the use of larger b-values, which are standard practice in more recent studies for their ability to detect microstructural WM damage. Additionally, improved characterization of increased cellularity could be accomplished by implementing diffusion basis spectrum imaging (DBSI), which has been proven to be sensitive to such pathologies (Wang et al., 2011). 3D imaging could also be implemented to provide whole brain images and improve tissue characterization.

Other limitations include the larger slices in MRI compared to EM, which create a partial volume effect. Differences in the macroscopic structures analyzed using MR images and microscopic structures using EM analysis introduced sampling bias into comparisons between the two methods. Correlations between MRI and EM photomicrographs were chosen, however, in part due to its common practice in other studies using the CPZ model of demyelination (Gudi et al., 2014; Stidworthy et al., 2003; Sun et al., 2006; Turati et al., 2015; Torkildsen et al., 2009; Varga et al., 2018), as well as direct comparison to our previous study (Thiessen et al., 2013).

Throughout the study, the sample size of in vivo mice decreased each week to allow for sacrifice, and the sample sizes of EM and ex vivo measurements per week were relatively low. A larger sample size in both in vivo and ex vivo mice groups would improve the reliability of the experimental conclusions.

Sex difference could not be studied because only female mice were used in these experiments. A previous study on SJL mice using found male mice were more severely demyelinated, whereas female mice were partially resistant. The study also found that microglia, astrocyte and oligodendrocyte precursor cell populations did not differ between male and female mice (Taylor et al., 2008). This study also concluded that the pattern of demyelination in the SJL mice was different from that seen in the C57BL/6. Another study found inhibiting microglia exacerbates the early effects of cuprizone in male rats, but not female rats (Xavier et al., 2023). In a study using C57BL/6 mice, cuprizone caused significant demyelination in the corpus callosum that was consistent across both sexes but led to different behavioral deficits between the sexes (Paton et al., 2022). Future studies should analyze both sexes and different strains.

Conclusion

MRI plays an important role in the diagnosis and treatment of neurodegenerative conditions (Filippi & Rocca, 2011; Thompson et al., 2018). Recent literature reviews have highlighted technological advancements in the field of MRI, which have shown correlations between many MR metrics and underlying tissue pathology (Friesen et al., 2024; Filippi & Rocca, 2011; Gudi et al., 2014; Kipp et al., 2009; Krämer et al., 2019; Khodanovich et al., 2019; Lipp et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2021; Merkler et al., 2005; Nathoo et al., 2014; Thompson et al., 2018; Thiessen et al., 2013; Wood et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2011; Xie et al., 2010). Previous studies have highlighted the speed of single-parameter analysis; however, they have elucidated the inability of distinguishing between various tissue pathophysiology’s (Ding et al., 2021; Lipp et al., 2019; Thompson et al., 2018), suggesting the need for a multiparametric approach (Friesen et al., 2024; Thiessen et al., 2013). Results from the current study show differences in a variety of metrics, including in vivo T2W and MTR maps and ex vivo DTI, MTI, and relaxometry metrics, across each week of CPZ administration. Simultaneous analysis of alterations in T2W signal intensity and MTR provided insight into the stage of demyelination, being either acute or severe. Ex vivo DTI analysis revealed decreases and increases in 〈D〉 and both λ║ and λ┴, respectively, are present at the early stages of demyelination. MTI metrics T2A and T1 showed alterations at each week of CPZ administration, suggesting their potential as biomarkers each week of the CPZ diet. Both f and R were observed to correlate with later stages of demyelination.

We therefore suggest the interrelationships between different MR metrics provide additional specificity towards underlying tissue pathophysiology and may help us to close the gap between clinical symptoms and MR findings. Further investigation of multiparametric MRI and underlying tissue pathophysiology is required to continue the attempt at solving the clinico-radiological paradox (Barkhof, 2002; Hemond & Bakshi, 2018).
