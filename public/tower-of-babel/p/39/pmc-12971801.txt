Allocation in field crop and livestock life cycle assessment
Authors: Nicole Bamber, Roland Kroebel, Nathan Pelletier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeField crops and livestock are significant contributors to a variety of environmental impacts, commonly assessed using life cycle assessment (LCA). In LCA, allocation is used to partition these impacts of production when processes produce multiple co-products, such as different parts of a field crop, or multiple animal products produced from a single livestock species. The choice of allocation method can have a large impact on estimated impact assessment results. However, the International Organization for Standardisation (ISO) standard for LCA is not applied consistently with respect to the choice of allocation methods and the differentiation between co-products, wastes, and recycled products in agri-food supply chains, which undermines their rigor and utility with respect to informing credible, evidence-based decision making. Therefore, the goals of this paper were (1) to assess current food sector allocation methods in published research against the ISO standard, and (2) to provide recommendations for decision-making regarding allocation methods that align with the ISO standard, and that can be consistently applied to model the different co-products and wastes associated with field crop and livestock production systems.MethodsPublished agricultural LCA studies from the last 5 years were reviewed to determine the allocation methods used. A total of 126 peer-reviewed agri-food LCA studies which reported on allocation methods between co-products were included in the review. Based on these studies, and other relevant journal articles and reports, recommendations were developed for allocation methods that align with the ISO hierarchy.ResultsTaken together, physical, natural science-based allocation approaches were the most common in the literature, and a large variety of such methods were applied. Economic allocation was also found to be commonly performed in agricultural LCAs (and equally as common as natural science for crop-specific LCAs), often justified because it represents the motivation behind producing each co-product. However, the ISO standard indicates a clear prioritization of natural science-based approaches. Therefore, we provide recommendations in line with this prioritization – namely, biophysical methods that represent pathways of causality in agri-food systems. The results of the literature review showed many options for natural science-based biophysical allocation for the co-products of crop and livestock production. Preferentially, these are based on internal causality – the real flows of material and energy within a system, particularly metabolic partitioning of energy within an animal, or construction cost partitioning within crops. Otherwise, external causality, such as the nitrogen content of the products, is recommended.ConclusionsIn this paper we provide detailed recommendations for a representative sample of multi-functionality scenarios in crop and livestock product systems. These can be used to enable consistent comparisons of the environmental impacts of different food production pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11367-026-02618-z.

Introduction

Food systems are one of the largest contributors to anthropogenic environmental impacts, including 70–80% of human freshwater withdrawals (Jägerskog, A., Jønch Clausen, 2012), 95% of nitrogen pollution (Bodirsky et al. 2014) and 30% of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions (Hallström et al. 2017). Agricultural land occupies 35–40% of terrestrial surface area with little room for expansion (FAO 2013). Given the current climate crisis, and the exceedance of several planetary boundaries for resource consumption and emissions globally (Richardson et al. 2023), it is imperative for food system policies and practices to be informed by evidence-based sustainability data. Therefore, principled and rigorous assessment methods must be consistently applied, in order to compare food production pathways and to provide evidence-based decision support for improved environmental performance.

Life cycle assessment (LCA) is commonly used to assess and compare the environmental impacts of different food production pathways throughout the entire life cycle of food products (Nemecek et al. 2016). In LCA, when multiple co-products are produced by a process (e.g., different parts of a crop destined for different uses), this is known as multi-functionality, and the impacts of the supply chain leading up to this process must be allocated between the co-products, unless allocation can be avoided by subdividing or expanding the system. As defined in the ISO hierarchy, allocation can be done, in order of preference, based on a physical or other (including economic) relationship between the co-products (ISO 2006a; Sect. 4.3.4.2). In agricultural systems, there are many different possible relationships between co-products. The choice of allocation method can have a large impact on the estimated environmental impacts of each co-product (Del Hierro et al. 2021), hence allocation choices should be principled and well-justified. However, the current ISO standards for addressing multi-functionality in LCA are often not applied in a principled and consistent manner by LCA practitioners, nor are they adhered to in derivative, sector-specific methodological guidance documents. For example, the Livestock Environmental Assessment and Performance (LEAP) guidelines for poultry ((FAO 2014a) and the European Dairy Association guidelines (EDA, 2018) mostly adhere to the ISO standard. However they sometimes categorize products as “residuals” (which is not a term defined by ISO 14044), and sometimes recommend economic allocation where physical allocation methods are clearly feasible. The distinction between wastes and co-products is also not made consistently in the literature, particularly with respect to products such as manure that may be a waste from one system, but valorized as an input to another.

Pelletier et al. (2015) proposed three separate hierarchies for multi-functionality, one for consequential LCAs, and one each for attributional LCAs that adhere to a natural science (as prioritized by the ISO 14040 standard for LCA, Sect. 4.1.8) or a social science framework. They argue that the goal and scope of the LCA, as well as the preferred framework (consequential LCA, natural science, or social science), must also be taken into account when selecting methods to solve multi-functionality problems (Pelletier et al. 2015). They note that each of the three philosophical approaches is internally consistent, but mutually exclusive. This paper starts with the ISO 14044 standard as the baseline, and hence aims to use the ISO multi-functionality hierarchy as a baseline to provide more concrete rationale and guidance for allocation in LCAs of crop and livestock systems.

Specifically, the goals of this paper were (1) to assess current practice with respect to allocation methods in field crop and livestock LCAs against the ISO standard, and (2) to provide structured tables of practical recommendations for allocation methods in field crop and livestock LCAs that align with the ISO standard. This information can be used to inform the collection of life cycle inventory (LCI) data for crop and livestock LCAs, and to appropriately differentiate between and model co-products and wastes. Methodologically consistent, transparently reported and justified LCAs can then be used to accurately compare the impacts associated with different crop and livestock systems, and to inform the identification of environmentally friendly food production pathways.

Methods

Literature review

In order to assess current methodological choices with respect to solving multi-functionality problems for co-products in field crop and livestock supply chains, a systematic review of the literature was performed. This was done by searching the Web of Science Core Collection database for keywords (“LCA” OR “life cycle assessment”) AND (“crop” OR “grain” OR “oilseed” OR the name of each specific field crop [i.e., wheat, canola, soy, corn, oats, barley, flax, peas, lentils, beans] OR “livestock” OR the names of key animal products [i.e., beef, dairy, pork, chicken, eggs]), AND “allocation”. Selected publications were limited to a 2017–2022 timeframe to enable current trends in allocation choices to be captured.

This search yielded a total of 174 papers. From those, 117 were selected for further review based on the inclusion criteria of being a peer-reviewed agri-food LCA study which reported on allocation methods, published in English, and accessible through institutional means. The other 57 studies were removed based on the exclusion criteria of not focussing on relevant products or not reporting allocation methods between co-products in an LCA. Nine additional papers (peer-reviewed and grey literature) were consulted as methodologies discussing allocation methods for agricultural systems (provided as references in papers included in the review). Thus, a total of 126 papers were included in the review, and the following pieces of information were collected and tabulated: (1) the product system studied, (2) the specific products, co-products and wastes to which allocation (or other multi-functionality) methods were applied, and (3) the allocation methods, or other methods employed to avoid allocation for multi-functionality.

The results of the review studies were only used in the discussion of the results, and in the development of recommendations. To avoid double-counting, they were not used when counting the number of studies that employed a certain methodology (i.e., type of allocation).

Based on the common types of product systems seen in the review, the results were sorted into the following categories: (1) production of field crops (whether for animal feed, human consumption, or other uses), or the feed production stage of livestock product systems, (2) dairy production (including livestock production and downstream processing), and (3) all other livestock production and processing systems. The distinction between dairy and other livestock systems was made due to the large volume of dairy studies, and relatively smaller volume of studies for each other livestock system.

Assessment of conformance with ISO standards

When provided, the justifications given for the chosen allocation methods were noted. The different allocation methods identified, and the justifications for using them, were assessed based on their adherence to the ISO 14044 standard for allocation in LCA (ISO 2006a; Sect. 4.3.4.2), which defines the following hierarchy to assign impacts to multiple co-products produced by the same process in LCA:“a) Step 1: Wherever possible, allocation should be avoided by1) dividing the unit process to be allocated into two or more sub-processes and collecting the input and output data related to these sub-processes, or2) expanding the product system to include the additional functions related to the co-products.b) Step 2: Where allocation cannot be avoided, the inputs and outputs of the system should be partitioned between its different products or functions in a way that reflects the underlying physical relationships between them; i.e. they should reflect the way in which the inputs and outputs are changed by quantitative changes in the products or functions delivered by the system.c) Step 3: Where physical relationship alone cannot be established or used as the basis for allocation, the inputs should be allocated between the products and functions in a way that reflects other relationships between them. For example, input and output data might be allocated between co-products in proportion to the economic value of the products.”

c) Step 3: Where physical relationship alone cannot be established or used as the basis for allocation, the inputs should be allocated between the products and functions in a way that reflects other relationships between them. For example, input and output data might be allocated between co-products in proportion to the economic value of the products.”

Studies were considered to adhere to this standard if they avoided allocation either by subdivision or system expansion. However, as argued by Pelletier et al. (2015), system expansion is generally not applicable for attributional LCAs (which make up the majority of published LCA studies). Subdivision is clearly preferred in the ISO hierarchy, but is often not possible, particularly in agri-food systems. Therefore, studies were also considered to adhere to the ISO standard if they followed step 2 (where allocation cannot be avoided), by allocating based on an underlying physical relationship between the inputs and outputs of the system.

A common interpretation of the phrase “underlying physical relationship” is the principle of causality, which has been argued to be the fundamental principle of allocation (Ekvall and Tillman 1997; Finnveden et al. 2009). Therefore, the application of mass-based allocation, simply because it is a commonly reported physical (or natural science-based) property, was not always considered to be in line with the second tier of the ISO hierarchy. Rather, only physical properties that were reflective of causal pathways were considered to adhere to the standard, which in some cases could include mass, as well as other physical properties such as energy. Azapagic and Clift (1999) described the principle of causality in allocation as based on “the real behaviour of the product system”. They state that if there are causal relationships between the functional units and the environmental burdens, then changing one output or system parameter (with others constant) will lead to changes in the associated burdens. These causal relationships should therefore be used as the basis for allocation (Azapagic and Clift 1999). In line with the prioritization in ISO 14040 (ISO 2006b; Sect. 4.1.8), a natural science-based approach was taken to assess the relevance of the allocation method to the principle of causality.

Finally, studies that used economic allocation were not considered to adhere to the ISO standard, unless they could clearly demonstrate that it was not possible to establish a physical causal relationship between co-products. As per the ISO standard, the only case in which “other relationships” (with economic value as an example) can be used for allocation is when a “physical relationship alone cannot be established or used as the basis for allocation”. However, no examples were found in the literature which demonstrated that no physical relationship could be established, especially in those using economic allocation. Since those publications are thus in direct contradiction with the ISO standard, they were not considered to adhere. As noted by Pelletier et al. (2015), the socioeconomic approach, which favours economic allocation, is internally consistent but mutually exclusive from the natural science-based approach which is favoured by the ISO standard.

Development of case-specific recommendations for allocation methods

Based on the results of the previous sections, and additional literature sources (i.e., relevant review papers and reports), a set of recommendations was developed for common allocation scenarios in field crop and livestock LCA. Allocation methods were the focus of these recommendations, rather than methods to avoid allocation, since the ISO hierarchy is quite clear that avoiding allocation is the preferred solution (ISO 2006a). In all cases, subdivision is an acceptable method to avoid allocation, when it is possible (Pelletier et al. 2015). However, system expansion may not be relevant for attributional LCA, and is therefore mainly recommended when performing consequential LCA. This has been clearly established and discussed by Pelletier et al. (2015), therefore this work will focus on the more contentious issue of allocation methods, in cases where allocation cannot be avoided.

Causal allocation methods based on physical properties are clearly prioritized in the ISO 14044 hierarchy (ISO 2006a). However, there are a wide variety of physical attributes of agri-food co-products that may potentially be relevant as a basis for allocation. Therefore, even if all studies followed the ISO standard, there would not necessarily be methodological consistency. Following Arulnathan et al. (2022), the distinction was made between external and internal causality, as defined in Ekvall and Tillman (1997). Arulnathan et al. (2022) noted that most methods of causal allocation performed in the literature are based on external causality (i.e., reflecting the motivation or the consequences of producing each co-product). They argue that internal causality is more relevant to represent the partitioning of flows within agri-food systems based on the actual flows of materials and energy within the systems – for example, the way the metabolism of a chicken uses energy from feed to support either egg production or muscle growth (for meat) (Arulnathan et al. 2022). This is also consistent with the ISO 14040 preference for methods based on natural science (ISO 2006b) since internal causality, particularly in agri-food systems, represents the biological processes occurring within the system or organism. Schau and Fet (2008) similarly describe the need for more detailed allocation guidance for food systems, since physical allocation methods can refer to a wide variety of physical properties, and proposed allocation based on biological causality. For consistency with Arulnathan et al. (2022), the term “internal causality” will be used throughout this manuscript to refer to this concept.

All methods of physical allocation are defined here as synonymous with natural science-based allocation. When possible, physical allocation methods based on internal causality (synonymous with biophysical methods) were sourced from the literature and recommended for each allocation scenario presented. Physical allocation methods based on external causality were also included in the recommendations, in instances where options for internal causal methods were not apparent. Multiple allocation methods (i.e., internal and external physical causality) were also recommended in order to provide LCA practitioners with a basis for sensitivity analysis. Since allocation is such an influential methodological choice in an LCA, it is important to perform a sensitivity analysis on this choice to determine its influence on the results of the study. This is also in line with the recommendation in the ISO standard for such a sensitivity analysis “whenever several alternative allocation procedures seem applicable” (ISO 2006a; Sect. 4.3.4.1). Throughout the allocation scenarios included in this work, the distinction was made between wastes and co-products, using both the internal and external causality frameworks. These frameworks can yield different classifications, for example for manure, which is a metabolic waste (internal causality), but may be used as an input to other activities (external causality). Generally, a product must be determined to be a co-product or a waste, according to the ISO definition of waste products as “substances or objects which the holder intends or is required to dispose of” (ISO 2006a; Sect. 3.35). The impacts of the treatment or recycling of waste products must be allocated to co-products, following the allocation hierarchy presented.

Figure 1 summarizes the guiding principles used to assess conformance with the ISO standard, and to provide case-specific recommendations for allocation methods (presented in Sect. 3.1 to 3.3). The hierarchy here is similar to, and consistent with, the ISO hierarchy, but includes more detail distinguishing between the different types of physical relationships discussed above. This decision tree can serve as general guidance for all multi-functionality scenarios, with specific recommendations for agri-food products presented in the following sections. These recommendations are not meant to be exhaustive, since each LCA will have case-specific multi-functionality problems, but they can serve as guidelines for many common scenarios in agri-food LCAs.

Results and discussion

Crop/feed

For the crop and/or feed product systems reviewed, natural science-based allocation methods and economic allocation were used equally frequently (50% − 18 studies each). The natural science-based methods used were mass (55%), energy (32%), animal nutrition (1%), and physiological construction cost of plant compounds (0.5%) (Supplementary Information [SI] Table S1 – note that many studies used multiple methods). Economic allocation does not align with the ISO hierarchy, since (ISO 2006a) requires, when allocation cannot be avoided, that allocation based on physical relationships be used (i.e. economic allocation, or some other method, should only be used if no physical allocation methods are possible). However, no evidence of such a case was found in the literature. Rather, the subset of those who used economic allocation who provide a justification argued that it represents the motivations behind the process (i.e., a social rather than physical causality) (Weinzettel et al. 2012). In addition, the application of mass-based allocation was not often in line with the second tier of the ISO hierarchy, except for select instances such as allocation of impacts associated with transportation of goods, since the amount and associated burdens of the product transported is directly related to its mass or volume.

Goglio et al. (2018) proposed a hierarchy of methods for attributing environmental burdens to individual crops within cropping systems, which we further elaborate on here, and include as part of our recommendations (Table 1). They propose that, first, impacts should be subdivided, when possible, to attribute specific activities, inputs, and outputs to a single crop in the system (e.g., seeding or harvesting a specific crop). This is often done by default when only one crop is studied in an LCA. However, some inputs and outputs, such as soil nutrient fluxes, cannot be clearly subdivided between crops in rotation. In these cases, Goglio et al. (2018) suggest to allocate impacts between crops based on relevant causal allocation methods, e.g., allocating nutrient inputs based on the nutrient requirements of the crops. For example, Laage (2022) allocated the impacts of green manures (leguminous crops), grown for the purpose of providing fixed nitrogen to organic cropping systems, between the subsequent crops in rotation based on their respective nitrogen contents.

If such causal allocation methods are not possible, then Goglio et al. (2018) recommend to allocate based on other physical attributes, such as mass, energy or cereal units. The cereal unit method is based on nutritional value to animals (Brankatschk and Finkbeiner 2017). This method was designed to include all crops in a rotation as co-products, and allocate impacts to each crop in the cropping system based on their nutritional value to animals, with barley as a benchmark. However, this may not be representative since some crops are used for purposes other than animal nutrition, hence the preference for internal causality when possible. One relevant example of when Goglio et al. (2018) recommend to use such generic physical allocation is for attributing soil carbon changes to individual crops within cropping systems, since the mechanisms of soil carbon sequestration are not well known, therefore identifying a causal method of allocation is challenging. This is due to the complexities of soil nutrient dynamics, with a lack of knowledge on a causal relationship between cropping activities and soil carbon changes. One alternative is to instead allocate based on the amount of time each crop is in rotation (Sevenster et al. 2020) (Table 1).

For legumes included in crop rotations, Costa et al. (2020) performed a review of the literature and found that no studies fully allocated the fertilizer credit due to nitrogen (N) fixed by the legumes to the legume crop, although this was recommended by the Australian guidelines for LCA (Grant et al. 2019). This is the method recommended herein (Table 1), and employed in the LCAs of Canadian pulses by (Bamber et al. 2022; 2024). They included N fixation as an additional function of the pulse production systems, and performed system expansion plus substitution to include a credit of N fertilizer that was avoided for the subsequent crop in rotation. This differs from consequential LCA, which also makes use of system expansion plus substitution based on markets (Pelletier et al. 2015), since they did not model a potential change, but instead modelled the current status quo, where N fixation by leguminous crops already allows for lower usage of N fertilizer in the same crop rotation. Rather than an external, market-mediated substitution of avoided inputs, it is simply an internal flow of N within the system which means that the system requires less external input of N (determined based on N content, not market equivalence). All flows included in the system boundary in this case still contribute materially to the system at hand.

For co-products from single crops, van der Werf and Nguyen (2015) proposed an alternative method of causal allocation, based on internal causality of crop growth, rather than external causality such as crop digestibility by animals. They proposed the methodology and equations to calculate the “physiological construction cost” of different plant compounds, which can be used as a method for allocation. It is relevant to note that this method of allocation closely resembles subdivision based on underlying biological processes, rather than the more arbitrary methods of allocation used to split burdens between co-products. Since it involves a model to understand the biological processes occurring within the crops, rather than actual processes that could be subdivided, it cannot be fully classified as subdivision. However, as such a close approximation of subdivision, this method of allocation is clearly preferrable within the ISO hierarchy.

This approach is broadly applicable to crop co-products used for any purpose, since it does not rely on any external valuation, only the growth within the plant. This method is therefore a good choice due to the varied uses of crop co-products (e.g., human consumption, animal feed, industrial uses, etc.). Internal causality is also the most in line with the ISO standard, since it is related to the actual processes occurring within the system (in this case within the crop as it grows). This is consistent with the ISO 14040 principle 4.1.8 that “decisions within an LCA are preferably based on natural science” (ISO 2006b) and the statement that “the inventory is based on material balances between inputs and outputs. Allocation procedures should therefore approximate as much as possible such fundamental input/output relationships and characteristics” (ISO 14044, Sect. 4.3.4.2). It also solves the contention in the LEAP guidelines for crop feed that there is no clear biophysical relationship between crop components, thus leading to the recommendation of economic allocation in both the LEAP guidelines, and the PEFCR for Feed for food producing animals (FEFAC, 2024; FAO, 2014b). For these reasons, the plant growth model from van der Werf and Nguyen (2015) is recommended here as the basis for allocation between crop co-products (Table 1).

One product from crop production that is often neglected in crop LCAs is crop residues (10 out of 23 primary articles). Crop residues from cash crops that are left on the field should be considered waste products, with the impacts (i.e., N emissions from the residues on agricultural soils) allocated to the harvested products. However, similar to N credits from leguminous crops, they have the potential to supply carbon and nutrients to the soil and thus provide fertility to subsequent crops, as well as additional soil moisture (snow trapping and increased albedo) (Liu and Lobb 2021). When such information is available, they may be included as co-functions as suggested for N credits for biological nitrogen fixation. Crop residues may also be harvested and used as inputs to other systems, such as livestock production systems. In this case, they should be considered co-products, and allocated to accordingly on a causal, physical basis (Table 1).

We also recommend a sensitivity analysis on allocation methods when possible, in line with ISO 14044 (2006a, Sect. 4.3.4.1). Such a sensitivity analysis is also recommended by the PEFCR for Feed for food producing animals (FEFAC, 2024). Despite the assertion that economic allocation is necessary due to the lack of common physical properties (FAO, 2014b), they recommend a sensitivity analysis “testing two physical alternatives for allocation in addition to the allocation methods recommended in this PEFCR” (FEFAC, 2024; Sect. 7.6.2).

Dairy

Dairy production systems were the most common livestock type assessed using LCA. The most common allocation scenario for dairy supply chains was between milk, meat, and other co-products produced from the dairy cows (in 28 different studies – 31%). Natural science-based allocation methods were used most commonly (32 times – 36%), followed by social science-based approaches (18 times – 20%), and “no allocation” by allocating all impacts to the main product (11 times – 12%) (SI Table S2). The most common natural science approach was allocation based on metabolic partitioning, often based on the equations from (Thoma et al. 2013) for metabolic energy use in dairy cows for different functions such as lactation and muscle growth (53%), followed by mass (27%), nutrient contents (such as protein and fat) (10%), and energy content (10%). For social science-based methods, economic allocation was by far the most common (94%), as well as one instance of allocation based on farmers’ perception (6%).

Allocation between different dairy products produced from milk (after the co-production of milk and other co-products from the animal) was performed in 21 different studies. For these allocation scenarios, natural science-based allocation methods were used more often (67%) than methods based on social science or other principles (33%) (SI Table S2). The most common natural science-based method was a causal/biophysical method based on the amount of dairy solids in each dairy product (63%), followed by nutrient content (as protein or fat) (21%), mass or volume (13%), and exergy content (0.5%). Economic allocation was the most common social sciences method (83%), followed by “no allocation”, with all impacts attributed to the main dairy product produced (17%). It should be noted that the latter option is clearly non-compliant with ISO 14044.

The biophysical allocation methods, that were the most common, are in line with the ISO 14044 standard (in cases where subdivision and system expansion were not possible). The metabolic partitioning within the cow, and the dairy solids present in each dairy co-product, each represent biophysical relationships on which to base allocation factors, in line with the second tier in the ISO hierarchy. The same can be said about the protein content for dairy co-products, however the mass of the dairy products may not be a representative biophysical metric for allocation of production impacts (other than transportation or other processes where impacts occur relative to the mass of the product), since there are varying levels of moisture content in different dairy products.

The International Dairy Federation (IDF 2015), and PEFCR for dairy (EDA, 2025) recommend a biophysical/causal method of allocation between multiple dairy products produced at the dairy processing plant, which we also recommend (Table 2). For these processes, they recommend allocation based on the amount of milk solids (dry weight) present in each dairy product produced. For the internal processes within the cow, before the milk enters the processing stage, IDF (2015) recommends to allocate between milk and meat, produced in the body of a dairy cow, based on the biophysical methods developed by Thoma et al. (2013), who proposed allocation factors based on the metabolic partitioning of feed consumed for the production of both milk and beef. They provide equations for allocation factors based on the amount of net energy per kg of dry feed available for specific metabolic activities in the animal that lead to the production of milk and beef. This method of allocation, based on internal causality, is also recommended herein (Table 2), as well as in the PEFCR for dairy (EDA, 2025). As with the crop construction costs, this method of allocation very closely approximates subdivision via a model to estimate the disaggregation of subprocesses occurring within the cow’s metabolism, and should therefore be prioritized in the ISO hierarchy.

In addition to milk and meat, manure and mortalities are produced on dairy farms. According to the PEFCR for dairy (EDA, 2025), manure and mortalities should be considered residues with no impacts allocated to them, except when manure has economic value, then economic allocation should be used for manure (but still biophysical for milk and live animals). However, this is not in line with the ISO hierarchy, or the natural sciences framework for LCA, since the economic value is not related to the biophysical flows in the system. We instead recommend that the economic value of a product should not be used in determining whether or not to assign impacts to a product, since this does not impact the real flows of materials and energy that are responsible for the impacts. Furthermore, there is no mention of “residues” in the ISO 14044 standard, instead all outputs from the system should be classified as either co-products, or wastes. Therefore, we recommend to allocate impacts to mortalities according to the same metabolic partitioning model as meat and milk (Thoma et al. 2013), as long as they are used as a product (i.e. destined for rendering). If they are disposed of, they should be considered a waste (Table 2).

Manure, however, presents a more complicated case. According to the metabolic partitioning of energy within the cow, manure is a waste product of metabolism, and must therefore be considered a waste, and the impacts of the treatment of the waste should be allocated to meat and milk. However, in practice, manure is often used as an input to other agricultural systems, therefore it becomes an input to other product systems, rather than being disposed of as a waste. Leip et al. (2019) propose that manure used as a nutrient source in a subsequent process should be considered as a co-product, up to the point that it has supplied adequate nutrients to its subsequent use (e.g., crop nutrient requirements). Any over-application of nutrients from manure is then no longer considered a co-product, and should be treated as a waste, since it is no longer providing a function to the crop production system (although there are long-term benefits of manure application that may make it difficult to precisely estimate the point of over-nutrition (Larney et al. 2011)). Leip et al. (2019) suggest to perform the allocation between manure providing usable nutrients and other livestock products using economic allocation, which does not adhere to the ISO standard.

We instead recommend that first, the practitioner should determine whether they will use internal or external causality principles in decision-making. These principles should be applied consistently, first to the classification of manure as a waste or a co-product, then subsequently to the choice of allocation factors. While the concepts are related, ISO defines wastes (Sect. 3.35) separately from the selection of allocation factors (Sect. 4.3.4.2), therefore it is important to do so in practice as well. This guidance also assumes that the manure, if used, is used in a system that is considered distinct from the livestock system in the system boundary definition. If, instead, the system boundaries include a larger integrated agriculture system where the manure is produced and used on the same farm, then it should just be considered an internal flow within the system. In this case it is neither a waste, nor a co-product.

If the practitioner intends to use internal causality as a basis for decision-making, then manure must be considered a waste since it is a waste product of metabolism. Even if the manure is used as an input into another process, such as crop production, in line with metabolic partitioning, it is considered a waste that is recycled in the cropping system. Therefore, the impacts of field application of the manure should be allocated to the co-products according to the metabolic partitioning model (Thoma et al. 2013) (Table 2). Additionally, when relevant, impacts must be allocated between the livestock system and the subsequent system (e.g., crop production) based on the requirements for recycling scenarios in Sect. 4.3.4.3 of ISO 14044, as either a closed- or open-loop recycling scenario. Special care must be taken to only allocate impacts of manure recycling to the livestock system once, to avoid double-counting (e.g., field application of manure should not be allocated to the livestock system for both the feed crops and the recycling of waste manure). This differs from the system expansion plus substitution method recommended for biological N fixation, which included a “credit” for avoided N fertilizer, since the manure is considered a waste rather than a co-product (or co-function) when using internal causality logic. Based on internal causality, manure is by definition a metabolic waste, unlike N fixed by legumes. Additionally, N fixed by legumes does not leave the system to receive this “credit” via the market for N fertilizers, it is remaining in the cropping system and reducing its own N fertilizer requirements.

If instead, the practitioner chooses to use external causality as the basis for decision-making, and the manure is used in another process (e.g., applied to cropping systems), then manure is a co-product, and it should be allocated to. In this case we recommend to allocate based on gross chemical energy content, since this is a good proxy for metabolic energy (Arulnathan et al. 2022), but does not dictate that manure is a metabolic waste. Alternatively, a relevant external causality metric may be used for allocation, depending on the functions supplied by the co-products, for example N content (Table 2). When using external causality, manure would still be a waste if it is disposed of, as per the ISO standard (ISO 2006a). Since the choice between internal and external causality metrics may greatly affect the estimated life cycle impact assessment (LCIA) results, we recommend a sensitivity analysis for each relevant allocation method.

Other livestock

For other livestock systems, including beef, poultry, swine, and fish, natural science-based allocation methods were the most commonly used, either using causal metrics (e.g., amount of metabolic energy required to grow and maintain different parts of the animal) (12 times – 52%), or mass (11 times – 48%) (SI Table S3). This is in line with the ISO standard, and these examples of causal biophysical allocation methods have informed our recommendations in this paper, as described in the rest of this section. Economic allocation was also commonly employed (17 times), which is not in line with the ISO standard.

For beef production systems, to allocate between co-products at the slaughterhouse, Hietala et al. (2021) and Le Féon et al. (2020) used allocation factors developed and used by Chen et al. (2017) based on physical causality, calculated as the total energy of the cow’s life cycle divided by energy of maintenance and activities (growth phases: calf, young heifer, pregnant heifer, dairy/suckler cow; activities: lactation, gestation, growth). In line with internal causality principles, and due to its close approximation of subdivision, we recommend this allocation method for co-products at the slaughterhouse, for all relevant livestock systems (Table 3). In contrast, the PEFCR Guidance document dictates that economic allocation must be used for slaughterhouse co-products (European Commission 2017; Sect. 7.11.5). As a demonstrative example, Bamber (2024) altered the economic allocation factor used by Aboagye et al. (2024) for beef co-products at the slaughterhouse to use the metabolic partitioning factors from Chen et al. (2017). This internal causality methodology gave an allocation factor of 50% to the human food portion of co-products at slaughter, whereas the economic allocation factor was 90%. Economic allocation factors are also subject to price volatility that would alter the estimated impacts of co-products with no change to the processes in the system.

In addition to livestock co-products at the slaughterhouse, there are co-products produced by the animals, such as eggs and spent hens (for meat) from laying hens, and wool and meat from sheep. In these cases, we also recommend allocation based on metabolic partitioning. Such metabolic partitioning models are available for common livestock species such as cattle (Chen et al. 2017), pigs (Strathe et al. 2012), laying hens (Arulnathan et al. 2022), and broiler chickens (Lopez and Leeson 2008). However, there may be some animal species where these models have not been developed. In cases where allocation based on metabolic partitioning is not possible, then we recommend allocation based on gross chemical energy as a proxy, or a relevant external causality metric such as N content. Since this choice may not be representative of the true partitioning of flows within the system, we recommend a sensitivity analysis if there are multiple relevant physical allocation metrics (Table 3).

For poultry production systems, (FAO 2014a) published the LEAP guidelines, which included recommendations for allocation procedures. For multiple animal species grown in the same place, they recommend subdivision when possible, then allocation based biophysical causality (proportion of total feed energy used by chickens). We recommend these same procedures, which can be applied to any livestock species, not only poultry (Table 3). For spent hens, FAO (2014a) recommends allocation based on biophysical causality, from the proportions of total energy required for growth and egg/hatchling production. Here, we recommend this allocation based on internal causality (metabolic partitioning) for spent hens as well as any co-products produced by livestock (Table 3).

According to the LEAP guidelines (FAO 2014), if spent hens or cull birds are disposed of, they are considered wastes rather than co-products, and thus no impacts are allocated to them, and the impacts of the waste treatment are allocated to the main products, with the potential for credits for substituted products when relevant. This is in line with the ISO definition of waste (ISO 2006a; definition 3.35), and is recommended here (Table 3). For meat and egg processing co-products, FAO (2014) recommends subdivision when possible, then economic allocation. They claim that if a product has no economic value, and the material has a subsequent use, then it should be considered a “residual” and receive no allocation. If instead the product has no economic value, and has no subsequent use, it should be considered a waste, and the impacts of waste treatment should be allocated to the remaining co-products (FAO 2014a). Most of the LEAP guidelines (FAO 2014a) are in line with the ISO hierarchy, except when they recommend economic allocation over biophysical allocation, and in the consideration of a co-product as a residual if it has no economic value, since this is akin to economic allocation. Furthermore, there is no mention of “residuals” in the ISO 14044 standard, therefore, products should be classified as either a co-product or a waste, based on the ISO definition that wastes are “substances or objects which the holder intends or is required to dispose of” (ISO 2006a; definition 3.35). When flows are considered wastes, they should not be allocated to and instead the impacts of waste treatment or recycling should be allocated to the other co-products. When flows are considered co-products, impacts should be allocated to them according to internal causality (e.g., metabolic partitioning within livestock animals) when possible, otherwise according to gross chemical energy or relevant external causality metrics (e.g., N content) (Table 3).

Similar to dairy systems, manure from other livestock is a particularly complex case, since it is a biological waste of metabolism, but it may be used as a product input to other supply chains, and is not always intended or required to be disposed of by the farmer. As with dairy manure, we recommend to first determine whether the practitioner intends to use internal or external causality principles in allocation. If internal causality is to be used as a basis for allocation, manure must be considered a waste since it is a waste product of metabolism. Then, manure waste treatment impacts should be allocated to the co-products, e.g., meat and milk, based on metabolic partitioning allocation (Thoma et al. 2013), with additional allocation considerations between the livestock system and the subsequent system, for closed- and open-loop recycling scenarios (as per ISO 14044 Sect. 4.3.4.3). If instead, the practitioner chooses to use external causality as the basis for allocation, and the manure is used in another process (e.g., applied to cropping systems), then manure is a co-product, and it should be allocated to. When using external causality, manure would still be a waste if it is disposed of, as per the ISO standard (ISO 2006a; definition 3.35). We recommend gross chemical energy or a relevant external causality metric for allocation, depending on the functions supplied by the co-products, for example N content (Table 3). Since the choice between internal and external causality metrics may greatly affect the estimated LCIA results, we recommend a sensitivity analysis for each relevant allocation method.

Conclusions

In LCA, when multiple products are produced by the same process, isolating impacts attributable to a single product requires partitioning the life cycle impacts of that process between the co-products. The (ISO 2006a) standard for LCA requires to first avoid allocation of impacts by subdividing the process into multiple processes that each produce a single product, or by system expansion to include all co-products. If this is not possible, (ISO 2006a) requires allocation of impacts between co-products based on a causal, physical relationship between flows (based on principles of natural science). Finally, the last option (when higher tier options are not possible) is to allocate impacts based on another kind of relationship, such as the economic value of the co-products. Taken together, natural science-based allocation methods are clearly prioritized by ISO 14040 (ISO 2006b; Sect. 4.1.8) and have become increasingly common in the literature. In fact, they are now used more often than economic allocation. However, there are numerous potential options for natural science-based allocation, with differing degrees of uptake in the literature. Despite its place at the bottom of the hierarchy, economic allocation is still a common form of allocation performed in agricultural LCAs, often justified because it represents the motivation behind producing each co-product. This is in line with a social sciences approach to causality in allocation, as identified by Pelletier et al. (2015). However, in this paper we provided recommendations for solving multi-functionality issues to be in line with the ISO standards (ISO 2006a, b), which indicate a preference for natural science-based causality.

The results of this literature review show many options for biophysical allocation to co-products of crop and livestock production, often based on the internal causality of energy partitioning within the plant or animal. Recommendations were made for a set of representative allocation scenarios from crop and livestock product systems to follow the ISO standard, using a natural sciences approach. In the common event that allocation cannot be avoided, we recommend using internal causality-based biophysical allocation for most co-products, providing specific guidance for each scenario (Tables 1, 2 and 3). However, there are some limitations in availability of metabolic partitioning models for livestock species outside the main ones presented in this review. In any situations where there are no relevant internal causality relationships between co-products, or no models to represent them, then we recommend gross chemical energy as a proxy, or other types of physical allocation such as N content, depending on the relevant external causal relationship between co-products.

In general, flows should be considered wastes rather than co-products if they are intended or required to be disposed of, as per the ISO (2006b) standard. In this case, production impacts should not be allocated to the waste flow, and instead the impacts of the waste treatment or recycling process should be allocated to the other co-products, according to the chosen allocation method. Manure represents a unique situation because, when using a metabolic partitioning model for allocation, manure is a waste product of metabolism, regardless of the farmer’s intention to either dispose of the manure or use it as an input to another activity. Therefore, manure can only be considered a co-product if using external causality for allocation, and if the manure is in fact used as an input to another activity, such as field application for crop production. Otherwise, manure is a waste and the associated waste treatment or recycling impacts must be allocated to the other co-products in line with the chosen allocation method. For all such cases where multiple allocation methods are possible and justifiable, we recommend performing a sensitivity analysis, since the choice of allocation methods may greatly influence the estimated results.

The results of this review have highlighted some gaps in the allocation literature, particularly the lack of comprehensive metabolic partitioning models, lack of standardization for allocation in crop rotations or integrated agriculture systems, and the lack of harmonization between ISO and PEFCR. Therefore, future research efforts should focus on developing metabolic partitioning allocation factors for more diverse livestock species. Standardized allocation methodologies for crop rotations should be developed, with a particular focus on complex soil dynamics such as carbon storage and nitrogen fixation. Additional allocation standardization should be developed for larger integrated agriculture systems, where potentially the proxy metric of gross chemical energy may be appropriate to apply a consistent allocation strategy for the variety of plant and livestock co-products (if desired – this is not an ISO requirement). Further, the emerging field of nutritional LCA may add nuance to biophysical drivers of allocation factors, and warrants further methodological development. We also recommend that further updates to both the ISO standard and PEFCR guidance should be done in a consistent manner, leading to greater harmonization between the multiple standards. This will allow for greater adherence to both standards, and to overall more comparable LCAs being conducted.

The allocation recommendations from this paper represent a consistent methodology for addressing multi-functionality in agri-food LCAs that is in line with the ISO 14044 standard for LCA (ISO 2006a, b). The results of such LCAs can be used to perform robust comparisons of the relative impacts of different food production pathways, in order to provide decision support for reducing the burdens of the food system while supplying adequate nutrition for the growing global population.
