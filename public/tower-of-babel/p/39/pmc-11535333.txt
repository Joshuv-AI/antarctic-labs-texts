Characteristics of Oak (Quercus petraea (Matt.) Liebl), hornbeam (Carpinus betulus L.) and ash (Fraxinus excelsior L.) in a plain-low hills-mountains-plateau sequence
Authors: Crișan Vlad Emil, Dincă Lucian, Vasile Diana, Murariu Gabriel, Drasovean Romana, Mocanu George Dănuț, Georgescu Lucian, Apăfăian Andrei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The climate has changed significantly over the past few decades around the globe. To make meaningful comparisons between changes occurring over time in the tree world, comprehensive databases on the most important species and their evolution must be created. It is essential to consider latitude, longitude, altitude and local relief when analyzing data. Given the extensive distribution of oak, hornbeam and ash, a sequence analysis was conducted to definitively examine the characteristics of these species in West Plain, West Hills, Apuseni Mountains and Transylvania's Tableland. The data for all the regions comprised a total of 708511 ha, which were subjected to rigorous analysis to determine characteristics such as yield class, volume, increment, structure and participation percentage. The data was analyzed using the most appropriate modern statistical programs, including correlation matrices, ANOVA, principal component analysis, matrix data analysis and the cluster method. In relation to the altitudinal variation, oak is present at average altitudes of 200–250 m in West Plain, 400–500 m in West Hills and Transylvania's Tableland, and 900–950 m in Apuseni Mountains. Hornbeam has a similar spreading with oak, but records its presence in slightly higher altitudes of West Hills (400–700 m) and lower in Apuseni Mountains (700–750 m). Volume is also an important indicator, as it is susceptible to climate change. Oak has the highest age-volume, over 95 years, while ash and hornbeam have it less than 90 years. The results are crucial for understanding the ecological requirements of oak, hornbeam and ash, as well as for effectively managing these forests.

Introduction

Forests are an important source of ecosystem services, providing goods and socio-cultural benefits [1,2]. They also contribute by absorbing carbon, improving biodiversity [[3], [4], [5], [6]], reducing soil erosion [7], while also contributing to recreational activities and bringing diversity in rural and peri-urban landscapes [8].

As forests are composed of a variety of species, they can easily recover after perturbations [9,10], are more productive as species differ in height, crown shape and root system [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]] and can easily adapt to current climatic changes [15,16].This is also valid for all plant and tree species that are affected by these changes [17]. However, many of these forests are losing this ability due to excessive biotic interferences such as anthropogenic perturbations and uncontrolled grazing [18,19]. Understanding qualitative and quantitative information in relation to structural and functional dynamics is essential for biodiversity conservation and sustainable management of fragile ecosystems [[20], [21], [22]]. Some of the most important broad-leaved species from Romanian forests are oak (Quercus petraea (Matt.) Liebl.), hornbeam (Carpinus betulus L.) and ash (Fraxinus excelsior L.). They are widespread from the plain and low hill areas up to plateaus and mountain area [23].

Oak is widespread in most parts of Europe, Asia while in Romania it forms pure or mixed stands with other broad-leaved species. It occupies over 8.3 % of the forest fund's surface [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]] and is widely known as having an extremely important ecologic, economic, and cultural value [29]. In this research all species of oak were taken for the statistical analysis [26] having resilient characteristics due to the fact that it reaches advance ages in comparison to other tree species [19] and their impact to biodiversity is plays an significant role [27]. It can reach advanced tree ages (800–900 years), heights of over 35 m and diameters of 3–4 m [[30], [31], [32]]. Oak is affected by extreme temperatures and late frosts if we consider the fact that leaves appear at the end of April, negative temperatures can destroy its young leaves [[33], [34], [35]], [36], [37], [38]]. Due to this aspect, some studies [39,40] have established some genetic variations based on altitude or latitude due to temperature gradients.

Hornbeam is a broad-leaved species, widespread in South and Central Europe, except for Spain and Portugal. It can grow from sea level up to altitudes of 700 m. In Romania, the species occupies approximately 3 % of the forest's surface, being very resistant to frosts, especially late ones. It can also install itself in areas with “frost hole” issues, places that are avoided by species such as common beech and fir [24,[41], [42], [43]]. Hornbeam can reach heights of 20–25 m, rarely 30 m [44]. The species grows especially in mixed stands, on fertile soils, together with different species of oak (Quercus robur, Quercus petraea), but also common beech (Fagus sylvatica), ash (Fraxinus excelsior), wild cherry (Prunus avium), field maple (Acer campestre) and hazel (Corylus avellana) [45].

Ash is a common species in all Europe, in the North and West parts of its natural areas, growing from plain forests up to mountain areas at altitudes of up to 1600–1800 m [46]. It is present in broad-leaved mixture forests, especially in forests dominated by common beech, as well as mixed with oak. In mountain areas it is mixed with Silver fir (Abies alba Mill.) and Norway spruce (Picea abies (L.) Karst) [47]. Soils that favor the development of ash originate from chalky parental material, clay, or sedimentary rocks, that have a pH between 5 and 7.5, are humid and well drained and are rich in sand [[48], [49], [50]], [51], [52]]. Ash is a species with a high economic value as it grows fast, forms a hard essence wood that is very good for high quality timber [53].

In the light of climate change it is essential to analyze as many characteristics of the existing forests as we can. Knowing existing patterns of the species and analyze their changes will provide us information needed for the future forest management plans.

The scope of this research article is strongly related to ecological and edaphic factors at the individual level that affect the development and growth of the three main species in the studied forest stands. Edaphic factors are of great importance influencing even forest management practices [22]. From the three relief types, the ecological requirements of the species are expressed through individual requirements that are related to a particular factor and do not take into account the way the factors combine or interact.

The objectives of this investigation are.i)to determine the site and stand characteristics for the three species (oak, hornbeam and ash) in four major geographic areas, namely field, hill, mountain and plateau;ii)to observe the growth differences (volume) of each species in the four geographic areas;iii)to observe growth differences (volume) between the three species for the same geographic area.

Materials and methods

The research was conducted in the West and central part of Romania (Fig. 1), on a surface of 708511 ha, by inventorying an extremely large number of stands, the data were extracted from the forest management plans of these areas (51 forest management plans) carried out during the period 1991–2008 [54]. These data were obtained by analyzing all the management plans from those areas. Although in some cases, data can be collected automated by using applications such as Sentinel – 1 [55,56], in this case all the data had to be manually extracted and computed following the key elements that were used for this study. As per region, the number of stands are in the West Plain (WP): 5272 oak stands, 5543 hornbeam stands, 3213 ash stands; 5272 oak stands, 5543 hornbeam stands, 3213 ash stands; in the West Hills (WH): 7097 oak stands, 7540 hornbeam stands, 770 ash stands; in Apuseni Mountains (AM): 7802 oak stands, 11043 hornbeam stands, 449 ash stands; and in Transilvania Tableland (TT): 28528 oak stands, 20914 hornbeam stands, 2315 ash stands.Fig. 1Studied area (http://www.geomil.ro/Produse/HartiScanate).Fig. 1

Studied area (http://www.geomil.ro/Produse/HartiScanate).

We have especially focused on the following elements: the species participation percentage in the composition (PRP), stand surface (SRF), age (AGE), average diameter (AVD), average height (AVH), yield class (YC), volume (VOL), increment (INC), slope (SLP), altitude (ALT), structure (STR), pruning (PR), and crown density (CND), relief (RLF).

Direct measurements were used for determining the stand's characteristic elements, while direct observations were used for environmental elements, following the methods and procedures mentioned in the current technical norms.

In this regard, sample surfaces were placed in the field where diameters and heights were measured on studied species, the results were used for determining the volume.

The field slope was measured with a clinometer in different points of the stand, after which an average value was determined. Yield classes were established based on each species, height, age, and origin. The altitude was determined by using plans with level curves, while the stand's volume designated for harvesting was established by statistical or integral inventories.

The factors were grouped in the following manner: Factor 1 (age + pruning + diameter + height), Factor 2 (proportion + volume + increment), and Factor 3 (slope + altitude). The cluster grouping is represented by diameter + height, slope + relief, and structure + crown density + increment.

Statistical methods

After the parameters considered for analysis for each species were established for the databases that contains over 12000 recordings, an evaluation of the correlation matrix between the 14 selected parameters was made. Consider the large set of recordings for each species, in most cases the level of the trust coefficient was p≪0.01.

Since ANOVA and PCA are generally known to identify factors that have a characteristic action on a wider process, they were chosen in the present study as well [57].

ANOVA was applied to assess the difference between the means of more than two groups and in particular to emphasize the factors that have a significant contribution over the volume. By using effect size values, we were able to identify and emphasize the parameters that made a significant contribution.

Complementary, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied due to the huge number of collected data. The method effectively simplifies a large data set into a smaller set while maintaining significant patterns and trends. For the investigation, dividing the parameters introduced in this study in groups will explain the resulted variation for each database.

To emphasize completeness in presenting the results, we have applied the Matrix Data Analysis (MDA) for the results and Cluster method for the analysis of the distance between data sets.

It is crucial to have a volume spread starting from 20 years of age until almost to the harvesting age. This provides forest specialists and researchers with an essential overview when elaborating management plans or modeling the evolution of such species.

Discussion

Vegetation composition and structure typically vary in space and time due to variations in topography, climate, weathering processes, soil physical chemical properties and sometimes also from microbial activities [58,59] but also to several other biotic and abiotic factors [ 22]. In highly fragmented landscapes, bioclimatic conditions change rapidly with altitude and can vary over short distances, resulting to considerable diversity in soil types [60], which in turn affects vegetation patterns [61].

Altitude

It can be seen that the three species are present in all four geographical areas. In the West Plain, ash is the only species that appears at lower altitudes, while oak and hornbeam stands reach up to 250 m. Ash stands are well developed on rich soils that are well drained at lower altitudes [54]. Furthermore, ash remains at the altitude of 250 m in West Hills, while oak and hornbeam can reach up to 500 m (oak) respectively 700 m (hornbeam). At lower altitudes, ash stands are developing very well, with an optimum of 100–300 m in the plain and hill areas.

In Apuseni Mountains, ash stands remain at a rather low altitude (700 m), similar with the ash from North Europe which is limited at an altitude of 600 m [52,62], while in central and South Europe it can reach 1600–1800 m in mountain areas, with even 2200 m in North Iran [53].

Oak is the species that goes the highest, reaching 900–950 m. The fact that oak can reach the highest altitudes was also observed in Pyrenees Mountains [63] where oak populations develop very well, even though they reduce their sizes, and have a high genetic diversity. In the Župa region from central Serbia, oak stands can reach up to 1100 m [64], while in South Turkey they reach altitudes of over 2000 m [31].

In Transylvania's Tableland, the altitude between 400 and 500 m (600 m) is where the largest number of stands is concentrated for all three species.

Slope exposition

It is a well-established fact that there is a significant relationship between slope aspects and plot vegetation [18]. Almost all four geographic areas, it resulted that in the plain, field and hill areas, ash stands are most concentrated, due to the fact that the species is sensitive towards harsh winters and late frosts [47]. Even though ash is as tolerant to shadow as hornbeam and common beech [65], according to Beck et al. [66], it prefers light once it reaches maturity. The same fact was observed in the case of the ash from Perridge Estate, Devon (United Kingdom), which prefers and grows better in plain and hill areas, at altitudes of maximum 200 m [67].

The oak stands are larger than the hornbeam ones in the plain and hill areas on sunny expositions (S, S-W) and partially sunny ones (S-E). On the other hand, hornbeam is dominant on shadowed and partially shadowed expositions, tending to dominate the oak. This situation was also observed in field areas from Poland and in mixed forests (oak-hornbeam) from Hungary, where the hornbeam seedling is larger [68].

In mountainous areas, the same situations occurs where oak dominates the sunny expositions, in the detriment of hornbeam, a situation also observed in the Belgian mountain area, where oak (that doesn't act as a shadow species) grows faster on sunny expositions than the hornbeam and common beech (species that prefer shadowed expositions) [69].

The link between volume and the other site and stand elements

By applying the ANOVA univariate method, the factors that have a significance over the volume are the specie's participation percentage, volume (in most cases), and sometimes only the height or the combination of participation percentage, diameter, and height. This fact is logical, as the volume is calculated based on the tree diameter and height; however, the participation percentage is a parameter that should be considered. Indeed, mixed stands show superior characteristics to pure stands [70,71]. By grouping all of the analyzed factors, using the PCA method, we can see that Factor 1 (which has an influence between 26 % and 34 %) is generally composed of the stand's parameters (AGE, VOL, PR, CND, AVD, AVH, ± STR, CND), Factor 2 (who has an influence between 18 % and 22 %) is composed of PRP, INC, ± RLF, YC, and STR, while Factor 3 (who has an influence between 10 % and 15 %) of SLP, ALT, ± CND. There is however a rather large variability between the species and the analyzed areas. In the upper mountain area [57], we have identified slope and altitude as main factors for spruce species. These factors are located in our third group. The explanation resides in the fact that in mountain areas, slope and altitude play a main role, unlike in field and hill areas.

Through the MDA method, the results are more homogenous: Factor 1 is represented by AGE, PR, AVD, and AVH, Factor 2 by PRP, VOL, and INC and Factor by SLP, and ALT; here we also have the RLF factor in mountains and hills. Indeed, the relief is more simplified in the plain area, while areas located on slopes appear in mountain and hill areas. We can see that the PCA method emphasizes more factors, when compared with the MDA method. However, the main factors remain mainly the same: AGE, VOL, PR, AVD, and AVH. In a study from the Southern Carpathians [57], the main factors for Silver fir also include, besides age and volume, structure and consistency. This fact reflects the fact that in that area, the stand characteristic factors play an important role.

By using the cluster method, we have noted the presence of a group of cluster factors: AVD with AVH, SLP with RLF, STR with CND and with INC. The relation between diameter and height is well known in the relevant published literature [72], but the strong link between structure, consistency and growth was not yet emphasized.

Species volume on geographic areas

In regard with oak, hornbeam and ash volumes from the four geographic studied areas, it can be observed that, according to the graphics, the annual increment is the same for all three species up to 50 m3/ha that have an age of up to 50–60 years. After that, the ash and hornbeam volumes increase with age, while oak volume remains around the value of 50 m3/ha, even at advanced ages.

Ash has the highest volumes in the field area, reaching 170 m3/ha at 75–85 years, while in the plateau area can reach 120–140 m3/ha at 55 and 85 years. Ash grows in height up to the age of 50 years, and then grows in diameter so that at 65 and 85 years has highest volumes in the optimal areas. The same growths were observed at ash trees from Belgium [73] and Slovenia [74].

Oak has the highest volumes in the hill and mountain areas, where it reaches 140–160 m3/ha at 95–100 years. In the forests from Bulqiza, Albania, similar higher oak volumes were also observed in the superior areas, in the hill and mountain areas [75].

In the last years, once climatic changes have appeared, it was observed that oak is a beneficiary of them due to its resistance towards drought [76,77]. In this context, in France for example, they have described a change in the growth relations between oak and common beech in the favor of oak [78], while Kölling and Zimmermann [78] suggest a change of climate conditions that will favor in the future the main sessile oak forests from Bavaria.

The fact that oak presents higher volumes in all four geographical areas comes to validates the classical theory [79,80] that a dominant oak equilibrium structure is the result of a succession process, being considered an adaptation to the local environment conditions [81,82]. After the apparition of a disturbance, hornbeam could reach higher volumes, but these changes would be temporary as the ecosystem comes back to its equilibrium through its specific self-regulation processes.

Conclusions

The research clearly demonstrates that oak hornbeam and ash, when situated on a wide geographical area from Romania, exhibit certain major traits that are indicative of their past and future development. We created comprehensive databases by analyzing relief characteristics such as altitude, slope, and exposition. A healthy forest ecosystem provides numerous benefits, including carbon sequestration and the maintenance of habitats for diverse plant, insect, and bird species. The results provide further understanding of tree species and their evolution in the context of future changes in nature. The results clearly show that ash has the lowest altitude, while hornbeam has the highest altitude distribution and oak falls between the two. From an exposition point of view, it is clear that each species has its preference. Ash is widespread on plain areas, oak appears in all areas, and hornbeam is mainly widespread on shadowy and partial shadowy expositions. Our study also looked at the volumes of different species aged 20–120 years old across all relief types. This confirmed that oak volume remains constant, while hornbeam and ash volumes increase slightly as trees age. The research results are of great interest and will undoubtedly inform future specialists in the face of climate change when elaborating management plans. They are also set to shape future studies on the evolution of certain forest species over time.
