Plasminogen Activation System and Fibroblasts: Impact on Tissue Remodeling, Disease, and Organ Homeostasis
Authors: Fathimath Muneesa Moideen, Yashodhar Prabhakar Bhandary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The interplay between fibroblasts and the plasminogen activation system is increasingly recognized as a central driver of fibrosis, thrombosis, and cancer progression. Fibroblasts, as key producers of extracellular matrix (ECM), regulate tissue integrity, repair, and remodeling. Through their interactions with urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA), tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA), plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), and plasmin, fibroblasts influence ECM degradation, cell migration, angiogenesis, and inflammation. Dysregulation of this crosstalk contributes to persistent fibrosis in organs such as lung, liver, kidney, and heart, and facilitates stromal remodeling in cancer. Notably, paradoxical effects are evident-for example, PAI-1 promotes fibroblast survival while inducing apoptosis in alveolar epithelial cells, highlighting context-dependent actions. This review summarizes recent findings across major organs and tissue-specific mechanisms. We also discuss the emerging therapeutic potential of targeting fibroblast–plasminogen interactions, including PAI-1 inhibitors, uPA/uPAR antagonists, and natural compounds, alongside challenges in clinical translation. This review critically integrates mechanistic and organ-specific evidence, including plasminogen receptors as essential mediators of pericellular plasmin generation with uPA/tPA/PAI-1 signaling, across lung, skin, kidney, liver, heart, and cancer contexts. These findings position fibroblasts as central coordinators of fibrinolysis-fibrosis crosstalk and highlight therapeutic opportunities targeting this integrated network.

Introduction

Fibroblasts are the most common type of cells present in the connective tissues throughout the body. Their principal function is tissue remodelling, i.e. they help in maintaining the structural integrity of tissues. They are the key sources of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins. They also help in maintaining ECM composition, wound healing, angiogenesis and fibrosis. Fibroblasts also secrete numerous cytokines, adipokines, and growth factors. They deliver positional information for other cells and thereby are essential during embryonic development.

Fibroblasts are usually spindle-shaped and have an oval-shaped nucleus. Fibroblasts have plenty of rough endoplasmic reticulum and a large Golgi apparatus in the cytoplasm. During chronic inflammation, the resident fibroblasts, transform into myofibroblasts which secrete ECM at an increasing rate and are highly contractile and proliferative, when compared to the fibroblasts. These activated forms of myofibroblasts express alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) as markers. The ECM deposited by them includes collagen, fibronectin, laminin, connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) and vitronectin. Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) is the most important mediator in the process of myofibroblast differentiation. The morphology of myofibroblasts is similar to that of both fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells. But myofibroblasts have nuclear membrane folds as well as long collections of microfilaments as a distinct feature. The transition from fibroblasts to myofibroblasts is required for wound contraction and closure [1].

Fibroblasts are not a homogeneous population but comprise multiple functionally and phenotypically distinct subpopulations that vary across tissues and disease states. Early studies demonstrated that fibroblasts can be segregated into functional subsets based on surface markers such as Thy-1, class II MHC, and C1q, with distinct regulatory properties [2]. Subsequent work established that this heterogeneity arises from both intrinsic transcriptional programs and extrinsic spatial cues within tissues [3, 4]. Recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics have provided an unrestricted, high-resolution view of fibroblast diversity across tissues and disease contexts, including cancer [5, 6]. Single-cell RNA sequencing studies have further revealed that fibroblast identity is defined by distinct molecular signatures, including a 90-gene profile enriched in ECM components and receptors such as Col1a1, Col1a2, Col5a1, Cd34, and Pdgfra, while also demonstrating the absence of a universal fibroblast marker [7]. These analyses show that ECM and matrisome gene expression are major drivers of fibroblast heterogeneity, reflecting organ- and niche-specific functional specialization. Moreover, distinct fibroblast subpopulations are anatomically localized to specific tissue niches, such as perimysial cells in skeletal muscle, valve interstitial cells in the heart, and zonated populations in the colon and bladder [7]. Importantly, this heterogeneity has significant disease relevance, particularly in cancer, where diverse cancer-associated fibroblast (CAFs) phenotypes are shaped by their underlying transcriptional states [5].

Fibroblasts are implicated in many diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and organ fibrosis [8]. In fibrotic tissues, the number and the phenotype of fibroblasts significantly affect the extent of fibrosis. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) contributes to fibroblast accumulation by generating mesenchymal-like cells from injured epithelia, expanding the profibroblastic pool and amplifying matrix deposition [9]. EMT is primarily triggered by tissue injury and inflammatory signaling, particularly through the activation of TGF-β and dysregulation of the plasminogen activation system (PAS). Plasminogen system components orchestrate EMT progression: uPA and plasmin degrade the basement membrane to facilitate epithelial cell migration and phenotypic switch, while tPA activates latent TGF-β1, the master EMT regulator, and PAI-1 stabilizes the mesenchymal phenotype by inhibiting ECM turnover [10–12]. Plasmin also liberates growth factors, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and fibroblasts growth factor (FGF), from the matrix that sustain fibroblast activation and myofibroblast differentiation [13, 14]. The interaction between fibroblasts and the PAS, including EMT-mediated fibroblast accumulation, is thus detrimental to disease pathophysiology [15–17].

Despite extensive research demonstrating the central roles of fibroblasts in tissue remodeling, wound healing, and fibrosis, there remain critical gaps in our understanding of the precise mechanisms by which fibroblasts interact with the PAS to influence disease progression. This is especially urgent given the high clinical burden of chronic fibrotic disorders such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, liver cirrhosis, and systemic sclerosis, which currently lack effective treatments. The paradoxical roles of key regulators like PAI-1, and the context-dependent functions of urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA) in different organ systems, complicate therapeutic targeting. Therefore, elucidating these complex molecular interactions holds promise not only for uncovering fundamental biology but also for guiding the development of novel, selective anti-fibrotic and anti-thrombotic therapies. In this review, we will be discussing the crosstalk between fibroblasts and PAS in different parts of human body.

The Plasminogen System

A critical mechanistic link between the PAS and fibroblast biology lies in the generation of pericellular plasmin and its downstream signaling effects. Upon activation by uPA or tPA, plasminogen is converted to plasmin on the fibroblast surface via plasminogen receptors, leading to localized proteolysis of ECM components [18]. This process not only facilitates matrix degradation but also activates latent growth factors such as TGF-β, releases matrix-bound cytokines, and triggers Matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) activation cascades [19–21]. These events collectively drive key fibroblast responses, including proliferation, migration, myofibroblast differentiation, and survival [22]. In parallel, PAS components such as PAI-1 and uPA/uPAR initiate intracellular signaling through integrins and LRP1, further modulating fibroblast phenotype in a context-dependent manner [23, 24]. Thus, plasminogen activation acts upstream of fibroblast functional reprogramming, forming a causal axis that links proteolytic activity to fibrotic remodeling.

The PAS involves proteolytic proteins involved in protein degradation mainly, fibrin. The system performs its functions like cell proliferation, migration, adhesion, and other functions by the proteolytic mechanism, chemokine activation and intracellular signal transduction. The key players in the PAS are tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA), uPA, PAI-1 and plasminogen.

Fibroblasts across multiple tissues broadly express plasminogen receptors (Plg-Rs) that localize plasminogen to the cell surface and enable its rapid activation to plasmin by uPA or tPA. These receptors drive pericellular proteolysis essential for ECM remodeling, wound healing, cancer invasion, lung and kidney fibrosis, and inflammation [25, 26]. Functionally, these receptors can be broadly classified into those that bind plasminogen activators and those that directly bind plasminogen.

Receptors for plasminogen activators play a central role in initiating plasmin generation by anchoring the enzymes responsible for converting plasminogen into plasmin. Among these, uPAR is the most extensively characterized and functions as a GPI-anchored glycoprotein that binds pro-uPA with high affinity, facilitating its activation and localizing proteolytic activity to the cell surface. In addition to its role in proteolysis, uPAR interacts with integrins and vitronectin to regulate cell adhesion and signalling [27]. In contrast, LRP1 primarily functions as a clearance receptor, binding complexes such as uPA: PAI-1 or tPA: PAI-1 and mediating their internalization and lysosomal degradation, thereby preventing excessive proteolysis [28]. Annexin A2 (ANXA2), although primarily recognized as a plasminogen receptor, also binds tPA on endothelial cells and acts as a co-receptor to enhance plasmin generation [25, 29].

These receptors all share a conserved mechanism of action. Surface-exposed C-terminal lysine residues on these receptors bind the kringle domains of plasminogen with high affinity, which in turn induces a conformational shift from the closed, less activatable Glu-plasminogen (Glu-Plg) to the Lys-plasminogen (Lys-Plg) form. However, certain receptors such as α-enolase can also utilize internal lysine residues to mediate plasminogen binding independently of the C-terminus [30–32].

Multiple plasminogen receptors are frequently co-expressed on the same cell type, resulting in a degree of functional redundancy within the plasminogen activation system. Several receptors can mediate similar processes, including plasminogen binding, plasmin generation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and cellular migration. However, this apparent redundancy may provide functional flexibility, as individual receptors can be differentially regulated depending on the cellular context, inflammatory stimuli, or stage of disease progression. In addition, different plasminogen receptors may cooperate to achieve sufficient localized plasmin activity or contribute to distinct signaling and migratory responses despite sharing overlapping functions [32].

Plg-Rs directly bind plasminogen, predominantly through interactions between kringle domain lysine-binding sites and C-terminal lysine residues exposed on the receptor surface. Plg-RKT, a transmembrane glycoprotein enriched on fibroblasts and macrophages, leverages multiple C-terminal lysines to promote plasmin-mediated inflammation, matrix degradation, and dysregulated repair in wound healing and fibrosis [33]. The ANXA2–S100A10 complex (AIIt) represents a major fibrinolytic regulator, in which ANXA2 anchors the complex to the membrane while S100A10 provides the C-terminal lysines necessary for ligand binding [34, 35]. Similarly, α-enolase (ENO1), a glycolytic enzyme with moonlighting receptor function, abounds on lung fibroblasts where it facilitates plasmin-driven TGF-β activation and migratory phenotypes in pulmonary fibrosis [31, 36]. Cytokeratin 8 (CK8), via its ectodomain lysines, binds uPA and plasminogen on tumor cells to enable pericellular proteolysis and invasive behaviour [37]. Fibroblasts do not typically express CK8 [38]. Histone H2B, when translocated to the cell surface, functions as a high-affinity plasminogen receptor that facilitates localized plasmin generation and drives inflammatory signaling through PAR1/PAR2 activation [39]. Plasminogen is cleaved to form active plasmin, which cleaves fibrin dissolution degradation products which, in turn, is then eliminated from the blood [40]. Plasmin can also cleave ECM proteins as well as activate pro-MMPs (zymogens) to active MMPs [41, 42]. It is also reported to activate numerous cytokines, chemokines and growth factors from ECM including VEGF, FGF and TGF-β.

There are two types of plasminogen activation factors; intrinsic factors, which are intrinsic to the plasma and extrinsic factors which are extrinsic to plasma [43]. Intrinsic plasminogen activators include kallikrein, active coagulation factor XII (FXIIa), and FXIa. These take part in both the intrinsic coagulation cascades as well as in fibrinolysis. Extrinsic plasminogen activators include tPA, a fibrin-dependent enzyme and uPA, a fibrin-independent enzyme. The tPA is largely involved in blood clot fibrinolysis, while uPA is involved in pericellular proteolysis [44].

Apart from fibrinolytic function of uPA, tPA and PAI-1, they are also involved in regulation of inflammation, senescence, and cell death [45–49]. PAI-1 emerges as a central mediator of cellular senescence, serving as both a biomarker and driver of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) that perpetuates fibrotic tissue remodelling [50]. Upregulated through p53/p21 signaling pathways in response to cellular stress and TGF-β stimulation, PAI-1 sustains senescent fibroblast persistence while amplifying SASP secretion of IL-6, IL-8, and TGF-β-key profibrotic mediators that reinforce myofibroblast activation and ECM deposition [51]. This PAI-1-senescence axis links replicative and stress-induced senescence to the chronic inflammatory microenvironment characteristic of pulmonary fibrosis, vascular aging, and organ fibrosis, providing mechanistic rationale for PAI-1’s prioritization as a fibroblast-selective therapeutic target within the plasminogen activation system [51].

The PAS is implicated in many diseases. For instance, elevated levels of PAI-1 have been found in cancer, cardiovascular disease, tissue fibrosis, musculoskeletal disorders, and renal disease [52–57]. In this review, we will be discussing the crosstalk between fibrinolytic system and fibroblasts in various organ physiology and diseases.

Lungs

Fibroblasts comprise of one third of total lung cells. They are located in the interstitial space in the parenchyma and in the subepithelium of the airway. Activation of lung fibroblasts is necessary to perform their functions. Stimulation from growth factors, interactions with ECM proteins, cell–cell communications or mechanical changes can lead to activation of fibroblasts. One of their main functions is to synthesise ECM proteins like collagen I, elastin, fibronectin, vimentin and platelet derived growth factor (PDGF). These ECM proteins help in alveologenesis, alveolarization, and endothelial cell adhesion. Fibroblasts also play a role in epithelial regeneration in lungs [58].

Fibroblasts are reported to play a critical role in various lung diseases. Most of the population of fibroblasts in the distal end of the airways from chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (COPD) patients are myofibroblasts and these fibroblasts exhibit amplified contractile properties [59]. The lamina propria sublayer thickness in COPD patients is associated with myofibroblasts in the small airway wall [60]. Myofibroblast population also influence the expression of ECM scar proteins, collagen-1 and fibronectin [60]. Though the large and small airways in the lungs of COPD patients are featured with hypocellularity, the prominent cell type present in both of the regions was mesenchymal or fibroblast-like population. This, probably, may be due to EMT [61, 62]. It is demonstrated that re-organisation of the ECM during the airway wall tissue remodelling causes the airflow limitation in COPD [63].

The PAS regulates lung fibrosis through control of EMT and matrix deposition, driven by the balance between PAI-1 and uPA [64, 65]. In alveolar type II epithelial cells, increased PAI-1 and decreased uPA during lung injury promote EMT via Src kinase signaling, leading to loss of epithelial markers and gain of mesenchymal markers, a process reversible upon Src inhibition [64]. Concurrently, plasmin-mediated proteolysis regulates matrix remodeling by releasing and activating hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), with increased HGF levels reducing collagen accumulation [65, 66]. Plasmin also degrades ECM components and activates growth factors and MMP cascades, contributing to tissue remodeling [65, 67, 68]. Fibroblasts mediate plasminogen-dependent HGF activation, enhanced in PAI-1 deficiency and reduced with uPA loss, while PAI-1 further promotes fibrosis by inhibiting plasminogen activation and regulating cell adhesion and motility [48, 51, 52].

Elevated expression of PAI-1 is seen in many fibrotic and thrombotic disorders [52, 69]. PAI-1 also contributes to fibrogenesis in a plasmin-independent fashion. The extent of resolution of fibrotic phenotype in a PAI-1 deleted model was shown to be much more evident than inhibition of PAI-1 binding to the plasminogen activators or in fibrinogen deficient mouse lung [70–72]. PAI-1 mediates intracellular signaling in fibroblasts through the uPA/tPA receptors and cell surface integrins [73, 74]. While PAI-1 overexpression or uPA/uPAR deficiency consistently exacerbate fibrosis in bleomycin models, therapeutic uPA administration reduces fibrosis. However, the paradox lies in PAI-1’s cell-specific effects: it promotes apoptosis in alveolar epithelial cells but enhances survival of fibroblasts. This duality suggests that broad PAI-1 inhibition may impair epithelial repair while attenuating fibroblast-driven scarring, underscoring the need for selective targeting strategies.

ANXA2, forming the plasminogen receptor S100A10/ANXA2 heterotetramer on fibroblasts, localizes to fibroblast-rich fibrotic lesions in IPF lungs. Extracellular ANXA2 from IPF fibroblasts binds factor Xa (FXa), augmenting PAR-1 activation, IL-6 production, and myofibroblast differentiation. IPF lung fibroblasts release elevated extracellular ANXA2 compared to controls, amplifying FXa-driven fibrogenesis-attenuated by ANXA2 antibodies, FXa inhibitors (e.g., apixaban), or PAR-1 antagonists. ANXA2 knockout mice show reduced bleomycin-induced lung collagen deposition, inflammation, and fibrosis [75, 76]. The S100A10/ANXA2 complex accelerates tPA-mediated plasmin generation essential for pericellular proteolysis in fibrotic remodelling [29]. ANXA2 regulates pulmonary endothelial barrier integrity during inflammation, preventing leakage that fuels fibrosis, though dysregulated surface ANXA2 promotes pathologic ECM turnover [77]. Bleomycin induces ANXA2 surface translocation in lung epithelial cells via exosomal p11 interaction, activating NF-κB/IL-6 signaling; ANXA2 blockade or knockout mitigates these profibrotic effects [76]. Thus, ANXA2 surface dynamics mediate coagulation-inflammation-fibrosis crosstalk in IPF.

In IPF and bleomycin models, EMT expands the fibroblast pool through alveolar type II (ATII) cell transdifferentiation, directly contributing to matrix deposition and myofibroblast accumulation [16, 78]. The PAS orchestrates EMT in IPF through a p53-mediated imbalance where elevated PAI-1 and suppressed uPA/uPAR activity trigger Src kinase activation, leading to basement membrane degradation and the transdifferentiation of alveolar type II cells into a mesenchymal phenotype [64, 79]. tPA, upregulated by ZEB1 in RAS-activated ATII cells, activates latent TGF-β1, the primary EMT inducer, and directly stimulates fibroblast α-SMA/collagen I expression [80, 81]. PAI-1, robustly induced during EMT, stabilizes the mesenchymal state by inhibiting ECM turnover while paradoxically promoting fibroblast survival through LRP1/integrin signalling [70, 82]. S100A10/ANXA2 on transdifferentiated fibroblasts amplifies pericellular plasmin generation, sustaining autocrine TGF-β activation and matrix contraction [76, 83, 84]. Ultimately, these localized shifts in the PAS create a self-sustaining feedback loop where epithelial dysfunction and fibroblast activation converge to drive progressive alveolar remodeling and irreversible lung fibrosis.

Thrombin, a protein involved in PAS, is said to augment fibroblast proliferation and thrombin inhibitors attenuates their proliferation in bronchoalveolar lavage (BALF) of IPF lung [85]. Thrombin stimulation induces α-SMA expression in lung fibroblasts. Thrombin contributes to the progression of pulmonary fibrosis by directly inducing myofibroblast differentiation [7]. Elevated expression of proteinase-activated receptor-1 (PAR-1) on lung fibroblasts is reported in human pulmonary fibrosis [86, 87]. Eye

Cornea is the transparent and curved structure mediates the focussing of image on to the retina in the eye. Because of its location and structure, it is very vulnerable to various kinds of injury. But unlike other organs there is no bleeding as it lacks blood vessels. However, there is deposition of fibrin at the site of injury to cover the exposed stromal surface and also to facilitate the migration of epithelial cells to the site. The deposition of fibrin is elevated during inflammation.

As discussed earlier, the fibrinolytic system not only degrades fibrin clots but also plays crucial role in wound healing and inflammatory response. In case of corneal epithelial cells, the fibrinolytic system components uPA, uPAR and PAI-1, facilitates the detachment of epithelial cells from provisional matrix and subsequently migration. MMP-9 also aids in this process. All of these events finally lead to alveolar reepithelialisation.

The transparency of cornea is maintained by the ECM, particularly fibrillar collagen and proteoglycans, secreted by the corneal fibroblasts and endothelial cells. In a normal cornea, the fibroblasts or keratocytes are mostly in quiescent stage [88]. Corneal fibroblasts are also required for maintaining the epithelial barrier and degradation of ECM. When there is a stimulus, disrupting the epithelial barrier of cornea, the fibroblasts differentiate into myofibroblasts. These activated keratocytes lead to collagen degradation, chemokine generation, phagocytosis and active proliferation [89].

Collagen degradation is mainly performed by the MMPs [90]. Fibroblasts release MMPs which can cleave fibrillar collagen. MMP-1 released by corneal fibroblasts helps in the process of destruction of cornea. Pro-MMP-1 is activated to active MMP-1 by plasmin. Plasmin is present in normal cornea only in small amounts. Its level is elevated in various corneal disorders. Plasmin, the active form of plasminogen, is generated by the action of uPA. uPAR, and is involved in the process of myofibroblast differentiation of corneal fibroblasts [42]. uPAR helps in the cleavage of uPA. uPA is bound to membrane bound uPAR and therefore localised on the membrane of corneal fibroblasts. But in case of myofibroblasts, uPA is released and is not localised on the membrane owing to the degradation of the membrane bound uPAR by a chymotrypsin-like protease [91]. uPA has also been shown to enhance collagen degradation in corneal fibroblasts [92].

S. aureus infection in cornea results in the upregulation of uPA and pro-MMPs. Staphylokinase, secreted by the bacteria, binds to plasmin and enhances its collagenolytic activity and it is also capable of activating plasminogen into plasmin [93]. Thus uPA-mediated collagen degradation by corneal fibroblasts is essential for stromal remodeling, but its excessive induction during S. aureus infection exacerbates tissue damage. Thereby, uPA acts as a double-edged sword: beneficial for epithelial migration in wound healing but detrimental in infection-induced corneal ulceration. This highlights the therapeutic challenge of selectively modulating uPA activity depending on the context.

In corneal fibrosis and limbal stem cell deficiency, EMT of corneal epithelial cells contributes to stromal fibroblast accumulation and subepithelial haze formation [94]. Plasminogen system components drive this process: uPA and plasmin, activated via corneal fibroblast uPAR, degrade fibrillar collagen and provisional fibrin matrices, facilitating epithelial cell invasion into stroma and acquisition of mesenchymal markers [95, 96]. Plasmin activates pro-MMP-1/2/9 released by keratocytes, amplifying basement membrane proteolysis and EMT progression while liberating TGF-β from ECM to sustain myofibroblast differentiation [97, 98]. PAI-1 upregulation during corneal EMT stabilizes myofibroblasts by inhibiting ECM turnover, promoting persistent haze [99]. In the retina, the ANXA2/S100A10 (AIIt) complex acts as a surface co-receptor for tPA and plasminogen, accelerating pericellular proteolysis to drive RPE cell migration and EMT-like transition into myofibroblasts, contributing to proliferative vitreoretinopathy and subretinal fibrosis in neovascular AMD [100]. These findings illustrate that the plasminogen activator system acts as a pivotal proteolytic switch across different ocular tissues, where its dysregulation, driven by the coordinated action of uPA/tPA, surface receptors like uPAR and AIIt, and the inhibitory control of PAI-1, transforms stable epithelial layers into invasive, myofibroblastic phenotypes that dictate the severity of vision-threatening fibrosis.

The PAS is also implicated in inflammatory pathways. uPA gene transcription is regulated by nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) [92, 101]. Plasminogen has been reported to be involved in interleukin 1-alpha (IL-1α) and IL-1β induced collagen degradation by corneal fibroblasts in a 3D model culture system. Collagen and IL-1β in turn induces the elevated expression of uPA. Also, IL-1β-induced collagen degradation by the corneal fibroblast cells is also reliant on the expression of uPA and is facilitated by uPA-plasmin-MMP pathway [11]. uPA induces the expression of chemokines, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) and macrophage inflammatory protein-2 (MIP-2), which in turn leads to infiltration of inflammatory cells into the corneal stroma (Fig. 3) [24]. Plasminogen promotes phagocytosis of foreign particles or injured tissue constituents in corneal fibroblasts by binding to the cell surface [102]. uPA and uPAR are also involved in the fibroblast cell motility during wound healing process in cornea. uPA-uPAR complex is colocalized with F-actin and might be functioning in cell signalling as well. Maspin, a non-inhibitory member of the serine proteinase inhibitor (serpin) family, is involved in the migration and adhesion of corneal stromal fibroblasts [103]. It enhances pro and active uPA and tPA and thereby resulting in plasmin activation and angiostatin generation by corneal fibroblasts and myofibroblasts [104]. Briefly, the uPA/uPAR complex is involved in inflammation, cell motility, myofibroblast differentiation and collagen degradation mediated by corneal keratocytes in the cornea and can be a potential therapeutic target in corneal disorders.

Skin

The predominant type of cells present in skin is keratinocyte. Keratinocytes undergo differentiation and form a barrier between the organism and environment called stratum corneum. The dermal-epidermal junction constitute of undifferentiated basal cells which are proliferative in nature. After gradual maturation, these basal cells migrate vertically and attain supra-basal position. During the differentiation of keratinocytes, changes occur in the cascade of PAS and influence epidermal differentiation and interaction between cell and matrix. uPA and uPAR are expressed by migrating keratinocytes during wound healing. Keratinocytes do not require uPA for migration over a substratum but for invasive migration through fibrin deposits to degrade ECM [105]. Plasminogen and fibrinogen are essential for quick wound healing by rendering migratory ability to the keratinocytes [106, 107]. The deficiency of plasminogen leads to delayed healing of the wound. The speed of wound healing is even less in mice deficient of both uPA and tPA [108]. PAS and the MMP share similar functions during wound healing and inhibition of both of the system leads to total aberrance in wound healing [109]. If either of the systems is dysfunctional there would be drastic change in the events in wound healing.

The uPA/tPA and plasmin play substantial roles in tissue remodelling and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis. α2‑antiplasmin (α2AP), the main circulating plasmin inhibitor, is a key regulator of fibrinolysis. It is implicated in the development and progression of fibrosis in systemic sclerosis. In dermal fibroblasts isolated from patients, there is increased expression of α2AP. MMP-3 is capable of degrading α2AP and lessens the protein expression of α-SMA and type 1 collagen, thereby reversing the fibrotic features of SSc fibroblasts [110]. Other players of PAS, Factor Xa, thrombin, plasmin and uPA are shown to induce myofibroblast differentiation [111].

PAI-1 is anti-fibrinolytic in nature and it maintains tissue homeostasis by modulating the actions of uPA/tPA plasminogen activator system and MMPs [52]. Normally, in the epidermis PAI-1 is not expressed by keratinocytes. But an injury or stimulation triggers the expression of PAI-1 in the hyperproliferative migrating zone of epithelial cells [112]. PAI-1 overexpression in keloid fibroblasts elevates collagen accumulation [113].

In hypertrophic scars, keloids, and scleroderma, EMT of keratinocytes contributes to dermal fibroblast accumulation and pathological matrix deposition [114]. Plasminogen system signaling drives this process: uPA/uPAR on keratinocytes and fibroblasts generate plasmin, which degrades provisional fibrin and interstitial collagen to facilitate keratinocyte migration into dermis and mesenchymal conversion characterized by decreased E-cadherin, increased N-cadherin and α-SMA expression, and elevated collagen I production [115]. Plasmin activates MMP-1/3/9, amplifying basement membrane proteolysis while liberating TGF-β1 from LAP complexes, which sustains myofibroblast differentiation and ECM stiffening [116]. PAI-1, hypersecreted by EMT-derived fibroblasts, inhibits ECM turnover to promote collagen-rich scar deposition; keloid keratinocytes exhibit fibroblast-like phenotypes with upregulated PAI-1 under hypoxia [117, 118]. Plg-RKT on keratinocytes modulates plasmin-dependent inflammation and reepithelialization, with knockout delaying wound closure but reducing scar thickness [33]. Elevated PAI-1 levels in diabetic patients contribute to antifibrinolytic states causing delayed wound healing and chronic ulceration, while PAI-1 deficiency or inhibition accelerates closure [119, 120]. This EMT-plasminogen axis underlies PAI-1’s antifibrinolytic role in delayed diabetic ulcers and excessive scarring.

Plg-Rs on fibroblasts and keratinocytes orchestrate pericellular plasmin activity during skin repair, ECM remodeling, and fibrosis regulation. Plg-RKT, upregulated on keratinocytes and fibroblasts in wounds, critically modulates burn wound healing phases in mice. Plg-RKT−/− mice show delayed proliferative healing with impaired macrophage recruitment, reduced cytokines, and defective fibrin clearance; partial fibrinogen reduction (50%) rescues closure [33]. Myeloid-Plg-RKT deletion disrupts inflammation, while keratinocyte-specific deletion accelerates reepithelialization but yields thinner epidermis via elevated filaggrin/caspase-14. Miles et al. reveal Plg-RKT’s profibrotic role, as knockouts exhibit reduced scarring ([121, 122].

The ANXA2/S100A10 heterotetramer (A2t) functions as a critical host factor for HPV16 infection in human keratinocytes by facilitating virus entry, trafficking, and nuclear delivery. Research shows that HPV16 induces Src-mediated phosphorylation of ANXA2, causing the A2t complex to translocate to the plasma membrane, where it facilitates viral binding and internalization [123]. ENO1 acts as a critical metabolic and structural regulator in skin tissues, with overactivity contributing to photoaging, barrier dysfunction, and melanoma progression. Key studies indicate that inhibiting ENO1 can prevent fibroblast senescence, maintain keratinocyte tight junctions, and mitigate psoriatic lesions [124–127]. CK8 is typically absent but becomes ectopically expressed in invasive cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC), where it acts as a critical driver of basement membrane invasion and metastasis by promoting cytoskeletal reprogramming and signaling through the integrin-FAK pathway [128, 129]. Collectively, these proteins demonstrate how the skin co-opts the PAS to drive pathology, where A2t and KRT8 act as surface receptors that localize proteolysis to facilitate viral entry and cancer invasion, while ENO1 integrates metabolic signaling with plasmin-mediated matrix remodeling to regulate skin aging and barrier integrity.

Kidney

ECM buildup in the kidney interstitium is a dynamic process that starts with an increase in microvascular permeability, an invasion of inflammatory cells, and activation/transformation of kidney cells, especially fibroblasts and tubular epithelial cells. If the initial renal damage persists, the molecular makeup of the interstitial “scar” will also alter significantly over time, both in terms of the matrix makeup and the interactions between different macromolecules. It has been found that numerous proteases attach to cellular receptors, and significantly alter cellular behaviour. The essential later phase of cellular death marked by peritubular capillary rarefaction and tubular atrophy as the interstitial matrix expands and consolidates may also be affected by these effects in addition to the early “inflammatory” phase of renal fibrogenesis. When functional nephron units are lost, the latter stage of chronic kidney disease signals the end of the road [130, 131].

Proximal and distal tubular epithelial cells produce a substantial amount of uPA, which is then released through the apical membrane and into the urine area. Monocytes/macrophages, fibroblasts, and myofibroblasts are further sources. Despite having high quantities, it is still unclear what uPA’s major physiological function in the kidney is. One hypothesis is that it might stop the development of renal stones [132]. uPAR is not expressed in normal kidneys; however, in human renal diseases such as diabetic nephropathy, allograft rejection, and pyelonephritis de novo expression of uPAR by glomerular and tubular epithelial cells and a variety of renal interstitial cells (fibroblasts, inflammatory cells, and endothelial cells) is reported [133]. In unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO)-induced chronic kidney disease model, enhanced uPAR expression in tubular and interstitial cells was associated with renoprotective effects such as lower numbers of myofibroblasts and reduced renal fibrosis when compared to uPAR-/- animals [134].

Plasmin(ogen) directly promotes EMT of tubular epithelial cells, contributing to myofibroblast accumulation in renal fibrosis. In cultured tubular epithelial cells, plasmin induces ERK phosphorylation followed by conversion to fibroblast-specific protein-1+, α-SMA+, and fibronectin-producing fibroblast-like cells. Plasminogen deficiency attenuates EMT markers, ERK phosphorylation, active TGF-β levels, and fibrosis severity in obstructed kidney models [135, 136]. During UUO, uPA expression and activity increase, potentially promoting fibrosis through TGF-β activation and PAR-1-dependent EMT signalling [137–140]. Endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) also contributes to fibroblast accumulation, with Tie2 + endothelial cells acquiring myofibroblast markers in fibrotic kidneys [141–144]. tPA promotes myofibroblast activation through LRP1-mediated β1 integrin signaling in the renal interstitium after obstructive injury [21]. PAI-1, acting independently of plasmin inhibition, contributes to ECM accumulation as myofibroblasts produce elevated plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 and TIMP-1 [56, 145, 146]. Notably, uPAR induction in chronic tubulointerstitial disease reduces fibrosis severity, indicating complex regulation beyond simple plasmin generation [137]. uPAR acts as a critical anti-fibrotic regulator by complexing with LRP1 to facilitate the scavenging and degradation of PAI-1, thereby preventing the hyperproliferation of fibroblasts and their differentiation into myofibroblasts. Recent studies also emphasize that uPAR-mediated signaling interacts with integrins to coordinate cell migration and maintains full-length receptor integrity to suppress the fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition [137, 147].

ANXA2, forming the plasminogen receptor complex with S100A10 on renal fibroblasts and macrophages, amplifies profibrotic signaling in kidney disease [148]. Macrophage-specific ANXA2 knockout markedly attenuates fibrosis in UUO, the canonical renal fibrosis model, by impairing macrophage plasminogen activation and reducing TGF-β1-driven fibroblast activation [149, 150]. ANXA2-deficient macrophages exhibit defective plasmin generation, blunted ROS production, and diminished inflammatory cytokine secretion (IL-1β, TNF-α), which collectively suppress myofibroblast differentiation and ECM deposition in UUO kidneys [151]. ANXA2 further emerges in renal fibroblast nicotinic receptor signaling, where it mediates ROS generation and ECM deposition downstream of uPA. Therapeutic ANXA2 blockade thus offers a convergent strategy targeting both macrophage-fibroblast crosstalk and fibroblast-intrinsic profibrotic pathways in UUO-like fibrosis [151].

PAI-1 alone causes considerable cell surface low density lipoprotein receptor-related protein (LRP) clustering in uPAR+/+ but not uPAR-null primary kidney fibroblast cultures, pointing to a potential direct interaction between PAI-1 and uPAR [152, 153]. LRP-1 is a multi-ligand receptor that serves a number of crucial biological purposes [89]. These ligands consist of tPA, uPA, and PAI-1. It has been demonstrated that tPA-activated LRP-1 promotes MMP-9 production, macrophage migration, and fibroblast survival through triggering intracellular signalling.

Urokinase promotes renal fibroblast activation via the MAPK pathway, driving proliferation and TGF-β1 production. In turn, TGF-β1 reinforces myofibroblast differentiation and ECM accumulation, creating a feed-forward loop that sustains fibrosis. This reciprocal signaling highlights why interrupting the urokinase-TGF-β1 axis may be a more effective therapeutic strategy than targeting either pathway alone. Because uPA can start mitogenesis in uPAR-/- fibroblasts, it appears that this regulation is at least partially mediated by a different urokinase receptor. An alternative urokinase cellular signalling receptor for renal fibroblasts is the muscle type nicotinic receptor alpha1 (nAChR 1) [154]. It regulates a collection of signalling proteins via tyrosine phosphorylation, including the calcium-binding proteins, ANXA2 and desmoyokin or ahnak, the cytoskeletal proteins actin, actinin, and vimentin, and the nucleoprotein histone 4. ANXA2 induces secretion of inflammatory mediators in immune cells, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, disrupted cell death pathways and ECM deposition; All of these processes start significant profibrotic consequences in the kidney [155, 156]. In short, PAS and the downstream signalling molecules interact intricately in the complex signalling pathways that underlie the development of kidney fibrosis.

Liver

Many studies have clearly demonstrated that adult stellate cells in the liver can respond to various types of injury by transforming into or promoting the generation of activated fibroblasts. In addition, other fibroblast cells, such as portal fibroblasts and vascular myofibroblasts, have also been recognized for their potential to promote fibrosis. The transition of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) into active myofibroblasts, the halt of hepatocyte growth, and the accumulation of ECM are all key features of liver fibrosis [157].

The decreased concentration of plasminogen activators (PAs) is one of the features responsible for the delayed liver regeneration after acute injury. This delay is due to the extended buildup of fibrin and the decreased activation of growth factors and MMPs [158]. It has been shown that mice lacking uPA contribute to the aberrant response in liver injury and the extent of liver fibrosis in uPA deficient mice was also reported to be remarkably severe [159]. Rats exposed to carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) were studied for the potential role of uPA. It was found that lack of uPA brought about a buildup of fibrin and fibronectin in the injured areas, insufficient clearance of dead cells, and a delay in tissue healing. Similarly, post liver injury, a plasminogen deficit also causes an abnormal buildup of connective tissue and a substantial stimulation of HSCs [160]. These findings collectively provide strong in vivo evidence supporting an antifibrotic role of uPA and plasminogen in liver injury models. However, most of these studies rely on genetic knockout models or chemically induced injury systems (such as CCl₄), which may not fully recapitulate the complexity and heterogeneity of human liver diseases.

It is also shown that cultured, activated HSCs had lower uPA expression, which resulted in decreased uPA activity and an inability to treat fibrotic scarring [161]. The antifibrotic role of uPA is also evidenced in a study which showed that uPA gene modified bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells treatment significantly reversed fibrosis by inhibiting Wnt signalling pathway [162]. Wnt signalling pathway activates the HSCs which aids in the progression of liver fibrosis. When HSCs were stimulated in culture as opposed to HSCs in their resting state, both canonical (β-catenin) and non-canonical Wnt genes (Wnt4 and Wnt5a) expressions increased noticeably and ultimately leading to ECM deposition [163].

While these in vitro and cell-based studies provide important mechanistic insights into the interaction between the PAS and HSC activation, their translational relevance remains limited due to the absence of multicellular interactions, immune components, and dynamic microenvironmental cues present in vivo.

In the presence of proteolytically inactive t-PA, there is a decrease in activation markers in HSCs following LRP1 phosphorylation. Compared to control mice, mice lacking either t-PA or LRP1 had larger densities of activated HSCs for longer periods following injury [164]. Although several studies have investigated the PAS in the liver [165, 166], these primarily address regeneration and fibrosis rather than EMT directly, indicating that evidence linking the PAS to EMT in the liver remains indirect.

The level of PAI-1 is noticeably higher in fibrotic livers and is strongly linked to the severity of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) in obese patients [167, 168]. It is reported that PAI-1 deficiency improves cholestatic liver fibrosis induced by bile duct ligation through promoting collagen breakdown [169]. Additionally, it has been shown that siRNA-mediated inhibition of PAI-1 provides protection against liver fibrosis in mice and reduces the fibrotic features of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) [170]. Thus, targeting PAI-1 may be an effective strategy for treating liver fibrosis. However, the majority of evidence supporting PAI-1 as a therapeutic target is derived from preclinical animal models and gene-silencing approaches, with limited clinical validation. Furthermore, the dual role of PAI-1 in regulating both fibrinolysis and cellular signaling introduces context-dependent effects that may complicate its therapeutic targeting in human disease.

HSCs play an important role in the production of PAI-1, and the distribution of PAs and PAI-1 varies at different stages of liver fibrosis progression [166, 171]. The expression of uPA and its receptors increases predominately in the early phases. However, as liver fibrosis progresses to the cirrhotic stage, increased PAI-1 expression obstructs uPA’s performance, which suppresses the production of plasmin and reduces the excessive breakdown of ECM [172, 173]. Heart

The fibroblasts in heart perform a variety of functions. They interact with a variety of cell types in the heart tissue, including lymphocytes, mast cells, macrophages, and cardiomyocytes [174]. Studies using animal models have identified cardiac fibroblasts as essential reparative cells that maintain the structural integrity of the infarcted ventricle and as contributors to heart failure by depositing stiff ECM in the interstitium, thereby reducing myocardial compliance. The analysis of their reparative and maladaptive actions has been complicated by the functional diversity of fibroblast populations, their significant phenotypic plasticity, and the limited understanding of fibroblast characteristics and properties in human myocardial diseases [175]. Their main role is to produce and degrade the ECM, which regulates the extracellular environment. Additionally, they have the capacity to change into myofibroblasts, which are important producers of the ECM. In response to injury, such as myocardial infarction or hypertrophy, fibroblasts develop into myofibroblasts, and the differentiation is primarily driven by cytokines like TGF-β [176]. TGF-β is released by inflammatory cells recruited to the site of the damage. The differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts can also be induced by other chemicals like alarmins and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) generated by injury-associated cells [177]. Furthermore, myofibroblasts contribute to fibrosis by producing collagen and other elements of the ECM like periostin. Additional roles for fibroblasts include the phagocytosis of dead cells and the release of anti-inflammatory cytokines [178].

In the developing myocardium, cardiac fibroblasts are believed to regulate cardiomyocyte proliferation via a fibronectin/β1-integrin–mediated pathway. In adult hearts, normal function may depend on the interactions between cardiomyocytes and the surrounding ECM. Cardiac fibroblasts, integrated into the endomysium and perimysium, likely play a crucial role in regulating the synthesis and turnover of ECM components, thereby maintaining the ventricular structural integrity. Mice with a global germline loss of transcription factor 21, vital for cardiac fibroblast development, showed significantly reduced collagen levels in the cardiac interstitium and had abnormally shaped hearts lacking a distinct apex [174].

Cardiac fibrosis, characterized by the excessive accumulation of ECM proteins, is a hallmark of various cardiac pathologies, including hypertensive heart disease, dilated cardiomyopathy, and ischemic heart disease [179]. Activation of the PAS in cardiac fibroblasts contributes to fibrotic remodeling by promoting ECM synthesis and inhibiting ECM degradation, ultimately leading to increased myocardial stiffness and impaired cardiac function [180].

The PAS promotes EMT in the heart primarily through plasmin-mediated activation of TGF-β3, as demonstrated by ANXA2–dependent plasmin generation regulating EMT in the developing avian heart, where inhibition of plasmin or ANXA2 significantly reduces EMT and is reversible with recombinant TGF-β3 [181]. Earlier research has also associated PAI-1 with diverse stress-related cardiovascular conditions, such as cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis. Loss of PAI-1 has been linked to selective fibrogenesis in the heart of aged mice, with no discernible impact on organs like the lung, kidney, and liver [182]. Additionally, mice lacking PAI-1 globally exhibit heightened susceptibility to severe cardiac fibrosis when subjected to angiotensin II-induced hypertension compared to their wild-type counterparts [183]. These findings underscore the protective role of physiological PAI-1 levels against age-related cardiac pathologies and the prevention of accelerated cardiac fibrogenesis in response to sustained hypertension-induced vascular stress. Thus, PAI-1 exerts protective roles in the heart, in contrast to its profibrotic effects in lung, skin, and liver. These findings highlight the complexity of PAI-1 biology and caution against uniform therapeutic strategies across organs.

Cancer

CAFs play an important role in tumor progression and may be potential targets for tumor therapy [184]. CAFs secrete cytokines, ECM proteins and interact with tumor cells and other cells and contribute to tumor microenvironment [185–187]. The reorganization of tumor microenvironment leads to migration of cancer cells, angiogenesis, and entry into the vascular system.

S100A10 serves as the dominant plasminogen receptor on CAFs and tumor-associated cells, driving pericellular plasmin generation for tumor invasion [25, 188]. Other key Plg-Rs on CAFs include Plg-RKT, ENO1, and CK8, all contributing to ECM degradation and metastasis. In.

CAFs, S100A10 acts as the primary molecular scaffold within the ANXA2/S100A10 heterotetramer to localize and accelerate pericellular plasmin generation, a process responsible for up to 90% of surface proteolytic activity [84]. This concentrated proteolysis enables CAFs to degrade the ECM (ECM) and activate latent TGF-β1, effectively clearing physical pathways for cancer cell invasion and driving the stromal-to-tumor signaling axis [83]. Consequently, high stromal expression of S100A10 serves as a robust biomarker for poor prognosis, and its genetic deletion significantly impairs matrix remodeling and tumor progression in vivo [189]. ENO1 activates latent TGF-β, thereby facilitating stromal-mediated tumor invasion. Additionally, recent studies highlight its metabolic significance, where the SDC1-ENO1 axis drives aerobic glycolysis in CAFs to generate a lactate-rich microenvironment that promotes tumor stemness and radioresistance. Additionally, the non-canonical surface expression of CK8 serves as a major docking site for both plasminogen and uPA, facilitating accelerated pericellular plasmin generation that drives ECM degradation and promotes cellular invasion. While traditionally an epithelial marker, this surface-anchored machinery enables proteolytic activity at the cell-stroma interface, contributing to the invasive niche required for tumor dissemination and path-clearing in the microenvironment [37]. Consequently, the therapeutic targeting of S100A10 and its auxiliary receptors offers a convergent strategy to disrupt the CAF-tumor crosstalk by selectively neutralizing the stromal proteolytic machinery [83].

The anti-thrombolytic property of uPA facilitates in removing the blood clots eventually leading to triggering angiogenesis in tumor cells [190]. uPA also cleaves the arginine–valine bond and thereby catalyzes the activation of plasminogen into plasmin which in turn takes part in the degradation of most ECM proteins by activating the pro-MMPs to active MMPs. These activated forms of MMPs assist in migration and invasion of cancer cells [190–192]. In skin squamous cell carcinoma, uPA is expressed by the cancer cells while in ovarian, breast and colon cancers, it is expressed chiefly by fibroblasts [193]. uPAR expression level is also significantly elevated in cancer during conditions like hypoxia [194].

A defining feature of CAFs compared to normal fibroblasts is their elevated uPA expression. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, uPA enhances proliferation, migration, and invasion through PI3K/AKT and ERK signaling, and high uPA levels inversely correlate with patient survival. These findings position uPA as both a marker of aggressive tumor behavior and a potential therapeutic target, though its broad role in physiological matrix remodeling presents challenges for selective inhibition [195]. The activation levels as well as expression levels of uPA and uPAR in CAFs derived from patients with symptomatic multiple myeloma are high compared to the others stages of the disease. Inhibition of expression of uPAR minimised the proliferative rate and invasiveness of CAFs [196]. uPA expression is higher in myofibroblasts than fibroblasts. uPA plays role in myofibroblast trans-differentiation and proliferation by maintaining uPA receptor on the cell surface. In breast cancer, it is the expression of uPA, uPAR, and PAI-1 in fibroblasts—rather than in tumor cells—that more strongly shapes clinical outcomes. Elevated stromal uPA and uPAR correlate with larger tumor size and shorter relapse intervals, underscoring the dominant role of the microenvironment [197]. This suggests that therapeutic targeting of fibroblast-derived fibrinolytic factors may be more impactful than focusing on tumor-cell expression alone. The expression of uPA in stroma has an association with increased risk of local recurrence in breast cancer. But levels of uPA don’t have correlation with other prognostic factors and prognosis. However, level of fibroblast derived PAI-1 is positively correlated with disease recurrence but not with prognosis [198]. The higher expression levels of PAI-1 are inversely correlated to cancer patient survival [199]. PAI-1 deficient fibrosarcoma fibroblasts are more sensitive to apoptosis than PAI-1 expressing fibroblasts despite the fact that both of theirs proliferation rate is same [200]. PAI-1 released by CAFs promotes esophageal squamous cell carcinoma by cancer cell proliferation and clone formation and by rendering cisplatin resistance [201]. Taken together, these findings highlight that while uPA, uPAR, and PAI-1 derived from CAFs strongly influence tumor proliferation, invasion, and therapy resistance, their roles are complex and sometimes contradictory. uPA promotes matrix degradation, angiogenesis, and fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition, yet its inhibition is complicated by its involvement in normal tissue remodeling. Similarly, stromal PAI-1 supports tumor progression and chemoresistance, but does not consistently align with prognosis across cancers (Fig. 5). Rheumatoid Arthritis

The main type of cells present in the synovial lining of the joint space is fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) [202]. Besides providing structural integrity, the FLS also regulates the content of synovial fluid [203, 204]. FLS releases plenty of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines during RA [205, 206]. These immunomodulatory molecules stimulate this destructive property of FLS leading to their survival and proliferation [207, 208]. PAS is involved in the degradative joint conditions through tissue remodelling. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory disease with hallmark of hypertrophic synovitis and cartilage erosion. Excessive rate of extracellular proteolysis driven by resident synoviocytes and inflammatory cells can pave way for invasion by neoplastic cells and tumor associated angiogenesis [209–212]. This process is followed by erosion of cartilage and bone in the joint. uPA/uPAR signaling in resident fibroblasts and inflammatory cells is a key driver of RA progression [213]. Unlike tPA, uPA is consistently elevated in rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (RA-SFs), which may underlie their tumor-like invasive behavior [211, 214]. Enhanced periarticular fibrinolytic activity through uPA/uPAR interactions promotes cartilage degradation and invasion, highlighting how fibrinolysis contributes not only to matrix turnover but also to the destructive phenotype of synovial fibroblasts. These observations suggest that targeting uPA/uPAR could restrain joint destruction, though preserving physiological fibrinolysis remains a challenge [215].

Survivin, a proto-oncogene and prognostic marker of destructive RA, intersects functionally with the uPA/uPAR pathway. By sustaining uPAR expression and activating PI3K and MAPK signaling, survivin reinforces the aggressive, tumor-like behavior of synovial fibroblasts. Silencing survivin curtails invasive growth, suggesting that dual targeting of survivin and uPA/uPAR may offer a synergistic approach to limit fibroblast-driven joint damage [216, 217]. uPAR is upregulated in RA-SFs and is required for cartilage invasion [218]. By activating the β1 integrin/PI3K/Akt pathway, uPAR drives fibroblast proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, effectively enabling synovial fibroblasts to mimic tumor cells. This highlights uPAR as a central node in RA pathogenesis, though its overlap with normal tissue repair mechanisms complicates its candidacy as a therapeutic target [219]. These studies highlight fibrinolysis as a pivotal pathway in RA, with uPA/uPAR and survivin emerging as promising therapeutic targets to curb synovial fibroblast invasiveness and slow disease progression.

Organ-Specific Paradoxes and Context-Dependent Roles of PAI-1

The roles of PAI-1 in fibrosis, tissue remodelling, and disease progression are highly complex, organ-specific, and their effects in both tissues and diseases are greatly dependent on the context. PAI-1 is mostly profibrotic in pulmonary fibrosis, which promotes fibroblast proliferation and over-accumulation of the ECM (ECM). The experimental inhibition of PAI-1 in lung fibroblasts has been reported to be highly effective in suppression of fibrosis in experimental versions of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) indicating that PAI-1 is centrally implicated in the pathogenesis of fibrosis [220, 221]. Its modulation of apoptosis however makes its role more complicated by the fact that it seems to affect both cell survival and deposition of ECM, and thus plays a dual regulatory role in the pathogenesis of IPF.

The PAI-1 paradox is governed by cell-specific signaling mechanisms that differentially regulate the stability of the master regulator protein p53, thereby producing opposing survival-death outcomes in lung cells [221]. In alveolar type II (ATII) epithelial cells, elevated PAI-1 functions as a pro-apoptotic signal by inhibiting proteasomal activity through direct interaction with its components, preventing the normal degradation of p53. This leads to p53 accumulation and activation of downstream effectors such as caspase-3, ultimately inducing apoptosis or senescence and impairing epithelial repair [222, 223]. In contrast, in lung fibroblasts, PAI-1 promotes cell survival by suppressing p53 expression and phosphorylation. This is mediated through activation of pro-survival PI3K/AKT and ERK signaling pathways, which enhance the activity of MDM2, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that targets p53 for degradation [224, 225]. This coordinated suppression of p53 confers resistance to apoptosis, enabling fibroblast persistence and excessive matrix deposition. Together, these opposing mechanisms create a “death–survival switch,” wherein epithelial cell loss and fibroblast survival drive progressive tissue remodeling and fibrosis.

PAI-1 has a context-dependent dichotomy in liver fibrosis. PAI-1 increases fibrosis in models of chronic bile duct ligation (BDL), which facilitates the deposition of ECM and scarring. On the other hand, PAI-1 appears to be protective in models of acute liver injury (e.g. CCl4-induced hepatotoxicity), promoting hepatocyte proliferation and tissue regeneration [226]. This comparison indicates that the role of PAI-1 in the liver is designed by the temporal relationship of injury and repair of which the overall effect on fibrosis depends on the character of the insult and on the regenerative ability of the tissue.

The role of PAI-1 in cardiac fibrosis is also different. Although its deficiency leads to more fibrosis, the mechanism of action is an augmentation of inflammation and TGF-b signalling, and therefore PAI-1 could in fact be a homeostatic role in the heart, where it limits excessive deposition of the ECM [227]. This protective effect indicates that PAI-1 is able to regulate the inflammatory response and ECM remodeling in a manner which precludes pathological fibrosis, suggesting that there is a balance between the pro-fibrotic and anti-fibrotic activities of PAI-1, which is determined by the context of the disease.

The PAI-1 role in cancer is still more complex. PAI-1 is a product of CAFs in the tumor microenvironment and is involved in tumor progression, as PAI-1 facilitates immunosuppression by promoting anti-tumor immunity inhibition such as PD-L1 upregulation [228]. Also, PAI-1 has been implicated in chemotherapy resistance, which is probably through inhibition of fibrinolysis and stress-induced survival of tumor cells [229, 230]. Although it contributes to ECM turnover, PAI-1 has protumorigenic activity, which highlights the importance of a context-specific perspective in targeting PAI-1 since it can have strikingly different effects on fibrosis relative to its impact on cancer biology.

The organ-selective functions of PAI-1 including fibrotic control in the lung and liver as well as its impact on tumor progression and aging suggest the importance of a more detailed interpretation of its activity. Effective targeting of PAI-1 requires that the expression patterns in tissues and the disease stage are considered, but also responsiveness to dynamic interactions of cellular reactions, inflammatory signaling, and regeneration. Since PAI-1 may serve as either a profibrotic or protective molecule, depending on context, the methods of precision medicine will play a pivotal role in the creation of effective therapies that regulate the activity of PAI-1 without complicating other tissues. Due to its complex and context-dependent activities, therapeutic interventions will need to be carefully targeted to prevent any off-target activity, including the stimulation of fibrosis in a specific organ and inhibition of the same in another.

Targeting the Broader Plasminogen System

A wider range of therapeutic approaches target the PAS at the level of fibrinolysis, proteolytic cascades, or gene regulation, without being restricted to fibroblast-specific contexts.

Antifibrinolytic agents such as tranexamic acid (TXA) inhibit plasminogen activation by blocking plasmin generation rather than directly inhibiting plasmin. Beyond hemostasis, TXA exhibits broader biological effects, including modulation of inflammatory cytokines, reduction in surgical site infections, and inhibition of plasmin-mediated melanogenesis [237]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying these effects remain incompletely defined, including whether they arise solely from reduced plasmin activity or additional mechanisms such as interference with plasminogen receptor interactions.

Plasminogen activators themselves represent another therapeutic axis with distinct functional roles. The tPA-mediated pathway primarily regulates fibrin homeostasis and is clinically established in thrombolytic therapy, including acute ischemic stroke. In contrast, the uPA-mediated pathway is more closely associated with cell migration and tissue remodeling, making it an attractive target in cancer and respiratory diseases. Despite promising experimental results, clinical validation of uPA-targeted therapies remains limited [238, 239]. Additional strategies include modulation of substrate availability, where plasminogen supplementation has been shown to enhance thrombolysis and accelerate wound repair, and has demonstrated potential in severe respiratory conditions such as COVID-19 [237].

Multiple drug classes have been developed to target components of the PAS at different levels. Small-molecule PAI-1 inhibitors show therapeutic potential in respiratory diseases and cancer, although clinical validation remains incomplete [239, 240]. Synthetic uPA inhibitors have evolved from early low-specificity compounds to more potent and selective agents, with some progressing toward early-phase clinical testing [241]. Monoclonal antibodies targeting uPA or uPAR disrupt ligand–receptor interactions, while antisense oligonucleotides reduce expression of uPA, uPAR, or PAI-1 at the gene level. Recombinant approaches, including soluble uPAR and enzymatically inactive uPA, act as competitive or scavenging inhibitors, and naturally occurring uPA inhibitors further contribute to proteolytic regulation [242].

Therapeutic strategies targeting Plg-Rs have evolved substantially, encompassing a diverse spectrum of modalities defined by their mechanism of action, from small molecule inhibitors to antibody-based interventions and advanced gene- and radionuclide-based approaches. Collectively, these approaches aim to disrupt plasminogen activation, receptor engagement, or downstream signaling pathways that drive ECM remodeling, cellular invasion, and pathological tissue remodeling.

Small molecule inhibitors primarily function by attenuating plasminogen activation or preventing its interaction with cell surface receptors, thereby limiting proteolysis-dependent tissue remodeling and tumor progression. Upamostat (WX-671/Mesupron), an orally bioavailable serine protease inhibitor targeting the uPA system, has advanced to Phase II clinical trials in pancreatic and breast cancers, demonstrating utility in combination with chemotherapeutic regimens [243]. Targeting regulatory components of the system, MDI-2517 represents a novel inhibitor of PAI-1, currently under investigation for its potential to mitigate fibrotic remodeling, particularly in pulmonary fibrosis [244].

In parallel, ENOblock, a selective inhibitor of ENO1, disrupts both glycolytic flux and plasminogen receptor activity, thereby reducing cancer cell stemness and enhancing sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin [245]. Additionally, antifibrinolytic agents, including tranexamic acid (TXA) and ε-aminocaproic acid (EACA), have established clinical utility in hemostasis by competitively inhibiting plasminogen–fibrin interactions, illustrating the translational feasibility of targeting plasminogen-binding interfaces [246, 247].

Monoclonal antibody-based strategies offer high specificity by directly targeting plasminogen receptors expressed on the cell surface. Neutralizing antibodies against ANXA2, a key plasminogen-binding protein, have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models by suppressing metastatic dissemination and potentiating immune checkpoint blockade therapies, such as anti-PD-1 [248]. Similarly, the 12C7 monoclonal antibody, directed against ENO1, selectively targets cancer stem cell populations in lung cancer, thereby inhibiting tumor growth and progression [249].

PF-03446962, a human monoclonal antibody evaluated in Phase I and II clinical trials for advanced solid tumors, further exemplifies the clinical translation of receptor-targeted antibody therapeutics, underscoring the feasibility of modulating plasminogen receptor-associated pathways in human disease [250].

Peptide-based inhibitors and gene-silencing approaches represent a mechanistically distinct class of therapeutics aimed at disrupting receptor–ligand interactions or downregulating receptor expression. Synthetic peptides such as M25 and pERERY are designed to interfere with uPAR-mediated signaling by blocking its interaction with integrins and other co-receptors, thereby impairing cancer cell migration and invasive behaviour [243].

TM601, a synthetic, 36-amino acid peptide derived from chlorotoxin, binds to ANXA2 on the surface of tumor and endothelial cells, effectively blocking the co-receptor sites required for tPA-mediated plasminogen activation stimulated by growth factors like VEGF and bFGF. This inhibition disrupts the localized proteolysis and signaling pathways essential for tumor invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis.

Complementing these approaches, RNA interference technologies, including siRNA and shRNA, enable targeted silencing of genes encoding key plasminogen receptors, such as uPAR and ANXA2. Although currently in experimental stages, these strategies offer a high degree of specificity and hold promise for precision modulation of the plasminogen receptor axis at the transcriptional level.

An emerging frontier in Plg-R targeting involves theranostic platforms that integrate diagnostic imaging with targeted therapy. The uTRACE®/uTREAT® system exemplifies this approach by employing uPAR-targeting ligands for both molecular imaging and radionuclide delivery. This platform has demonstrated translational potential across multiple cancer types, enabling real-time visualization of receptor expression alongside targeted radiotherapy, thereby bridging diagnostics and treatment within a unified framework [251].

Translational Perspective and Clinical Outlook

Therapeutic targeting of the plasminogen activator system (PAS) has transitioned from its classical application in acute thrombolysis to a rapidly evolving translational paradigm encompassing cancer, fibrosis, and chronic inflammatory diseases. The current clinical landscape is characterized by the emergence of small-molecule PAI-1 inhibitors, such as TM5614, alongside advanced targeted delivery systems designed to enhance therapeutic precision and overcome the narrow therapeutic window associated with systemic fibrinolytic agents [244].

To address limitations associated with systemic fibrinolysis, innovative delivery strategies are being actively explored. Nanotechnological approaches, including magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) and chitosan-based carriers, enable localized delivery of plasminogen activators to thrombotic sites, thereby enhancing target specificity while minimizing systemic bleeding complications. These platforms are designed to improve target selectivity and enhance local retention of plasminogen activators in occlusive thrombi [252].

In addition, human Glu-plasminogen replacement therapy is currently in Phase II/III clinical trials for congenital plasminogen deficiency, showing potential in improving clinical manifestations [253].

The clinical trajectory of PAS-targeted therapies is increasingly shaped by precision medicine and combination approaches. TM5614 is currently undergoing Phase II/III clinical evaluation in cancer-associated thrombosis, with expanding investigations in immune checkpoint inhibitor-refractory malignancies, including melanoma, where it is expected to mitigate TME-driven immunosuppression [254].

In parallel, advances in thrombolytic therapy are being incorporated into updated clinical frameworks, particularly in acute ischemic stroke, where next-generation nanotherapeutics are designed to enable localized fibrinolysis while resisting PAI-1-mediated inhibition [252]. Additionally, antisense oligonucleotide-based therapies, such as pelacarsen targeting lipoprotein(a)-associated thrombosis, are in Phase III trials and are anticipated to provide new avenues for cardiovascular risk reduction [255].

A key emerging paradigm is biomarker-guided therapy, wherein circulating PAI-1 levels and related molecular signatures are being explored to stratify patients and tailor therapeutic interventions across oncology and cardiovascular disease settings.

Despite significant advances, several challenges continue to limit the clinical translation of PAS-targeted therapeutics. A central issue is the “PAI-1 paradox,” wherein PAI-1 exhibits complex conformational dynamics (active, latent, and vitronectin-bound states) that complicate effective pharmacological targeting. Designing inhibitors capable of overcoming these structural constraints remains a major hurdle [256, 257].

Collectively, future directions in this field will likely converge on the integration of structurally optimized inhibitors, advanced delivery platforms, and biomarker-driven patient selection to fully harness the therapeutic potential of the plasminogen activator system.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the crosstalk between fibroblasts and the PAS is crucial in the pathogenesis of various diseases. Understanding the molecular interactions between PAS and fibroblasts in various conditions could offer insights into novel therapeutic targets and strategies for managing disorders. Future research should focus on developing specific inhibitors and conducting comprehensive clinical trials to better understand their therapeutic potential and optimize treatment strategies.
