The combined effect of statin and vitamin C in patients with pulmonary septic shock: a single-center, double-blind clinical trial
Authors: Behrooz Farzanegan, Navid Shafigh, Morteza Hasheminik, Haleh Alipour, Ali Behzadfar, Nava Safaee, Elnaz Shafigh, Mehdi Kazempour Dizaji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeTreating septic shock requires a multifactorial plan, such as anti-inflammatory drugs and newer drugs to reduce inflammation. This study investigated the effect of a combination (statin and vitamin C) on septic shock mortality and disease symptoms.MethodsIn this double-blind clinical trial study, 60 patients with septic shock hospitalized in the intensive care unit were assigned into two groups: intervention (1 gram of vitamin C was prescribed once a day for 5 days, and atorvastatin 40 mg daily) and control (routine treatment). Inflammatory parameters (C-reactive protein (CRP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and serum ferritin (SF) and lactate), patient conditions, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores were monitored and recorded on the fifth and seventh days of hospitalization, and the day of discharge from the intensive care unit (ICU).ResultsWhile mortality had no difference between the groups (p > 0.05), the duration of receiving inotrope was significantly shorter in the intervention group (p < 0.05). After the intervention, the measures of SOFA score, CRP, LDH, SF, and Lactate in the intervention group were significantly lower than those of the control group (p < 0.05). Besides, the measures of the biomarkers, including CRP, LDH, and SF, were significantly different in the intervention group (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe results of this study showed that the simultaneous use of statin and vitamin C can reduce inflammation indicators in ICU-admitted sepsis patients; however, this short-term intervention had no effect on mortality in the patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11831-4.Keywords: Septic shock, Statin, Vitamin C, Inflammation

Introduction

Sepsis is a complex failure in organ function caused by a disordered systemic response of the host against infection [1]. Manifestations of sepsis depend on various factors, including the complex interaction between the host response and the infected organism causing these conditions [2]. Various studies show that the imbalance between inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses leads to irreversible tissue-damaging disorders and multi-organ failure in sepsis [3]. An adjunctive therapy to limit the inflammatory response, reduce the damage caused by free radicals, and improve metabolic disorders can lead to the fight against sepsis [4].

Administration of vitamin C has demonstrated benefits in severe sepsis and septic shock, including reductions in Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores, shock treatment duration, and inflammatory biomarkers [5, 6]. As an immune-modulating factor and cofactor for iron-containing enzymes, it requires external supplementation in mammals due to the absence of gluconolactone oxidase [7–9].

In various studies, statin, a cholesterol-lowering medicine, has been discussed in terms of their pleiotropic benefits for sepsis [10]. Studies have shown that the use of high-dose statin is associated with lower mortality in sepsis compared to the use of a low-dose statin regime [6]. It is also known that statin has pleiotropic effects independent of cholesterol, such as improving endothelial function, reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, and reducing thrombogenicity, which helps in the treatment of sepsis, which is a systemic inflammatory syndrome caused by infection [10, 11]. Therefore, statin is considered new therapeutic agents that may help to treat and prevent sepsis by targeting the inflammatory and immunomodulatory cascades involved in the development of sepsis [12, 13].

Based on our best knowledge, the effects of the two supportive treatments (statin and vitamin C) have been investigated separately, but so far no research has investigated the combined effect of the treatments in patients with septic shock and severe septic shock. The complementary action mechanisms of vitamin C and statin, particularly relevant in pulmonary septic shock encouraged us to focus on this title. Vitamin C provides antioxidant properties that reduce oxidative stress and inflammation [14, 15], while atorvastatin offers pleiotropic effects including improved endothelial function and reduced thrombogenicity [16, 17]. For this reasons, and considering the extensive complications, the high mortality rate of this disorder, and the need to use cheap and available supportive treatments [18, 19], the present study was conducted to investigate the combined effect of these two factors on sepsis and severe sepsis.

Materials and methods

The study as a single-center and double-blind controlled clinical trial was conducted at Labafi Nejad Hospital, Tehran, Iran, in the Respiratory ICU, a specialized unit for critically ill patients with respiratory issues. After obtaining informed consent, the patients admitted to a referral hospital with sepsis were assigned to control and intervention groups. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, definite diagnosis of pulmonary septic shock according to Sepsis-3 criteria, hospitalization in respiratory ICU, multi-organ involvement (failure of at least two organs or SOFA score ≥ 2 for two or more organ systems), and obtaining informed consent from patient or legal representative. The study was registered on November 29, 2022 (Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials: IRCT20221129066990, https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/66990) and conducted from December 2022 to December 2023. This study was ethically assessed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Ethics approval number: IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1401.410). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

This study was conducted on patients with pulmonary septic shock, defined as septic shock with confirmed pulmonary source of infection. All patients had pneumonia or other pulmonary infections. Sepsis was diagnosed using Sepsis-3 criteria, requiring a SOFA score increase ≥ 2 due to suspected or confirmed pulmonary infection, with clinical assessment, chest imaging, microbiological cultures from respiratory specimens, and review by an infectious disease specialist. Patients with non-pulmonary sources of sepsis (such as catheter-related bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, abdominal infections, or skin/soft tissue infections) were excluded from the study. The intervention was initiated within 6 h of septic shock diagnosis and ICU admission, after confirmation of pulmonary septic shock and completion of baseline laboratory assessments. All the patients were in the inflammatory phase of sepsis (a non-regulated inflammatory response determined by elevated baseline levels of CRP (> 70 mg/L), LDH (> 700 U/L), and serum ferritin (> 500 ng/mL), and a shift in the redox balance to the oxidant state assessed by measuring oxidative stress markers such as malondialdehyde and reduced glutathione levels at baseline), and all of them first received inotrope along with hydrocortisone. All patients received standard septic shock management including fluid resuscitation, vasopressors, antibiotics, and hydrocortisone according to Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines, initiated immediately upon diagnosis.

In the intervention group, one gram of vitamin C was prescribed once a day for 5 days [20–22], and atorvastatin 40 mg was given daily using gavage to the patient [23, 24]. The patients in the control group received routine/standard treatment included fluid resuscitation (30 mL/kg per day crystalloids), vasopressors (noradrenaline titrated to mean arterial pressure ≥ 65 mmHg), hydrocortisone (200 mg/day), and broad-spectrum antibiotics initiated within 1 h of diagnosis, following Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines. Given the pulmonary focus, appropriate respiratory support was provided, including mechanical ventilation when indicated.

Patients were randomized 1:1 to intervention or control groups using a computer-generated random number table. Blinding was maintained by identical packaging of vitamin C and placebo, with atorvastatin/placebo administered via gavage. The patients of intervention group received standard care plus atorvastatin and vitamin C, while control ones received standard care plus matching placeboes for 5 days. All medications were prepared and labeled by a pharmacy department. Both clinicians and outcome assessors remained blinded to group allocation throughout the study period.

The primary endpoint of this study was 28-day all-cause mortality in ICU patients with pulmonary septic shock receiving a combined therapy (vitamin C + atorvastatin) versus routine care. The secondary endpoint included changes in inflammatory biomarkers (lactate dehydrogenase-LDH, C-reactive protein-CRP, serum ferritin-SF, and lactate) from baseline to day 5, disease severity measured using APACHI II and SOFA Scores from baseline to day 5, duration of vasopressor therapy, vasopressor dosage requirements, ICU length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU readmission within 28 days [25].

The sample size of this study was determined by applying this the parameters derived from previous research—such as a significance level of 0.05, 80% power, an estimated standard deviation, and a clinically meaningful difference—we calculated that approximately 30 patients were needed per group. Thus, the total sample size for the two groups (intervention and control) was determined to be 60 patients.

The data were analyzed using a variety of statistical methods to assess different aspects of the results. For comparing continuous variables between groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. Categorical data were examined using Chi-square tests, with Fisher’s exact test applied for small sample sizes where appropriate. To evaluate changes within groups over time, paired t-tests were utilized for normally distributed data. For non-parametric data or when normality assumptions were not met, Wilcoxon’s signed rank test was used as an alternative. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Where applicable, effect sizes and confidence intervals were calculated to provide additional context for the significance of the results.

Results

Of 85 screened patients with pulmonary septic shock, 12 were excluded (5 refused consent, 7 had exclusion criteria including allergies to statins or vitamin C), with enrollment occurring over 12 months (December 2022–December 2023). Thirteen patients did not complete the study, and finally, 60 remained in the study (30 patients for each group), and their data were analyzed (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the combined effect of vitamin C and atorvastatin on clinical outcomes and inflammatory markers in patients with pulmonary septic shock. Our findings demonstrated that while the combination therapy significantly reduced inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, LDH, serum ferritin) and decreased the duration of vasopressor support, it did not significantly impact 28-day mortality rates. The intervention group showed meaningful improvements in SOFA scores and reduced noradrenaline requirements, suggesting potential benefits in organ dysfunction and hemodynamic stability, though these benefits did not translate into survival advantage in this critically ill population with 73.3% mortality in both groups. Our findings showed that the use of vitamin C and statin does not have a significant effect on the death rate of patients. Early research on vitamin C for sepsis treatment began with animal studies. This was followed by human clinical trials examining the potential benefits of high-dose intravenous vitamin C (HDIVC) for sepsis patients. An initial study from 1986 investigated 16 patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). The treatment group received intravenous vitamin C (1 gram every 6 h) along with other antioxidants (including N-acetylcysteine, selenium, and vitamin E). A control group of 16 ARDS patients received only standard care as practiced at that time. This early trial aimed to assess whether vitamin C and antioxidant therapy could improve outcomes in severe respiratory illness associated with sepsis. There was a dramatic reduction in mortality in the vitamin C group—37% versus 71% in the standard care group (p < 0.01) [26]. The largest trial completed on vitamin C to date, the CITRIS-ALI trial, was published in 2019. This multicenter, randomized, double-blinded trial included 167 patients with sepsis and ARDS who were randomized to receive 50 mg/kg every 6 h of HDIVC for 4 days versus placebo and showed a statistically significant difference in 28-day all-cause mortality. The 28-day mortality was 29.8% in the vitamin C group versus 46.3% in the placebo group, although this was a secondary outcome. The statistical effect on mortality remained for up to 60 days following trial completion. The most dramatic reduction in mortality was noted during the period of HDIVC infusion. Furthermore, the HDIVC group had a strong trend towards more ventilator-free days (13.1 in the HDIVC group vs. 10.6 in the placebo group mean difference, 2.47, 95% CI − 0.90–5.85, p = 0.15), ICU-free days to day 28 (10.7 in the HDIVC group vs. 7.7, in the placebo group, p = 0.03), and more hospital-free days (22.6 in the HDIVC group vs. 15.5, respectively, p = 0.04). This trial did not find significant reductions in SOFA scores, C-reactive protein, thrombomodulin, or procalcitonin. Those biomarkers and scores, however, were not measured among the patients who “graduated” early from the ICU (a group that was heavily shifted towards the HDIVC group) or in those patients who died (heavily shifted towards the placebo group), indicating a strong selection bias, which makes these results difficult to interpret [27]. Based on the results of previous experimental models and randomized controlled trials, vitamin D administration is effective for improved injuries and survival [28]; however, several systematic reviews have found no significant effect of vitamin C on the death rate [29–32]. This might be related to the differences between studies in sample size, administration duration, dose of vitamin C, and disease severity of included cases hospitalized in the ICU ward. The cause of sepsis and the patient’s condition should also be considered in vitamin C therapy. For example, based on the findings of a cohort study, high-dose IV vitamin C treatment led to a higher risk of acute kidney injury and mortality in ICU patients [33].

Similar to vitamin D, the findings of earlier studies about the effect of statin on morbidity and mortality in ICU patients presented a heterogeneous feature [34]. Many researchers discussed that more studies are required to shed light on the role of statin therapy in ICU patients’ outcomes [34–37].

The data from our study show that the use of vitamin C and statins reduced the levels of inflammatory indicators in patients with sepsis. At the same time, the investigation of the reduction of the index, such as LDH, as a product of cellular anaerobic respiration emphasizes the antioxidant effects of this drug combination. Vitamin C in the early stages of sepsis could improve the patient’s condition by limiting cytokine and neutrophils that cause pulmonary vascular damage [38]. Concerning statin, a cholesterol-lowering component, they have been frequently advised, because they could remarkably reduce the level of inflammatory indicators [39]. But the main problem is the outcomes of these drugs. The advantages of vitamin C and statin is limited to changes in biomarkers without significant effect on mortality or damages. Besides, it is very important to make a clear drug prescription guideline covering both the patient’s condition and drug use method (dose, duration, and procedure). Finally, in line with the opinion of Mermis et al., we do believe that it is not the time to close the book for the investigation into the role of vitamin C and statin on ICU patients’ health outcomes [40].

Our study employs a rigorous double-blind RCT design with clear randomization and blinding procedures. We demonstrated significant reductions in inotrope duration, vasopressor dosage requirements, and key inflammatory markers. The study assessed meaningful clinical outcomes including 28-day mortality and ICU readmission rates. The homogeneous patient population (all with pulmonary septic shock) reduces variability that might occur with mixed infection sources.

Regarding the apparent discrepancy between mechanical ventilation rates and high mortality, it is important to clarify our respiratory support strategy. In our specialized respiratory ICU, we employed a tiered approach to respiratory support.

Forty eight patients (80%) ultimately required invasive mechanical ventilation12 patients (20%) were initially managed with non-invasive ventilation, though some later progressed to invasive ventilation.

No treatment limitations or “comfort care only” decisions were implemented during the study period. All patients received full intensive care support including appropriate vasopressor therapy, mechanical ventilation when clinically indicated, renal replacement therapy, and other organ support measures as needed. The high mortality rate despite aggressive treatment reflects the inherent severity of pulmonary septic shock with multi-organ failure. Many patients experienced rapid clinical deterioration due to refractory shock and progressive organ dysfunction, which occurred despite maximal medical therapy. This underscores the critical need for early intervention strategies and potentially longer treatment durations to meaningfully impact survival in this high-risk population.

Our study had some limitations that should be acknowledged. The small sample size (n = 60) was designed as a pilot study and may have been underpowered to detect significant differences in mortality outcomes. The exceptionally high mortality rate (73.3%) in our severely ill patient population may limit the generalizability of our findings to less critically ill sepsis patients or those with non-pulmonary sources of infection. The relatively short intervention duration (5 days) may have been insufficient to impact long-term outcomes such as mortality, particularly given that most deaths occurred within this timeframe. Additionally, our single-center design and focus exclusively on pulmonary septic shock may limit external validity to other ICU settings or sepsis populations. Future larger, multi-center randomized controlled trials should consider longer intervention periods, earlier initiation of therapy (potentially in the emergency department), stratification by sepsis severity and infection source, and inclusion of broader sepsis populations to better establish the clinical utility of combination vitamin C and statin therapy.

Conclusion

Based on our results, administration of vitamin C and statin for ICU patients with sepsis reduced the level of LDH, CRP, and SF in the intervention group compared to control ones. However, the short-term intervention was not effective in the case of mortality and disease severity assessed by SOFA and APACH scores. This could be justified that a short-term intervention could not provide disease outcomes, and the intervention has a role in the inflammatory indicators. Further studies are required to assess the effect of dose-dependent and long-term intervention on mortality and morbidity advantages of vitamin C and statin in ICU patients with sepsis.
