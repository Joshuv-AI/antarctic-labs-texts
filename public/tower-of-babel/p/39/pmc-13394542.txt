Dimethyl Fumarate, But Not Rituximab, Reduces Serum GFAP Levels and PIRMA in Relapsing–Remitting MS
Authors: F. Shawket, J. Lycke, J. Salzer, F. Piehl, K. Fink, N. Lange, J. Mellergård, C. Malmeström, P. Sundström, I. Erngren, A. al‐Grety, E. Freyhult, K. Kultima, J. Burman, A. Svenningsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveSerum neurofilament light chain (sNfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (sGFAP) levels are believed to reflect mainly acute and chronic disease processes in multiple sclerosis (MS), respectively. In this study, we investigated whether dimethyl fumarate (DMF) and rituximab (RTX) differentially affect these biomarkers.MethodsRIFUND‐MS was a 2‐year, rater‐blinded, 1:1 randomized controlled multicenter trial comparing DMF and RTX in relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS). Serum samples for analysis of sNFL and sGFAP were collected at baseline and 0, 6, 12 and 24. Log‐transformed biomarker data were analyzed with linear mixed models, based on intention to treat (ITT), per protocol (PP) and accounting for therapy switches. Cox proportional hazards models were performed to evaluate progression outcomes.ResultsOf 200 participants, 197 were analyzed. Based on ITT, sNfL decreased significantly in both arms from baseline to month 24; by 50.7% (CI 43.7%–56.8%; p < 0.001) with RTX, and by 46.4% (CI 38.6%–53.2%; p < 0.001) with DMF, no differences between treatments (global p‐value: ITT = 0.06; PP = 0.08; switch group = 0.15). In contrast, sGFAP remained stable in RTX (3.6% decrease; CI −7.8%–13.8%, p = 0.81) but decreased with DMF (18.4%; CI 8.5%–27.2%; p < 0.001). Global analyses favored DMF (ITT = 0.02; PP = 0.004; switch group = 0.74). The risk of progression independent of relapse and MRI activity (PIRMA) was higher with RTX (HR 3.3, CI 1.1–10, p = 0.04).InterpretationBoth RTX and DMF reduced sNfL levels, consistent with suppression of acute inflammatory disease activity. However, only DMF was associated with a sustained reduction in sGFAP and a lower risk of non‐inflammatory disability progression. These findings suggest that DMF may exert additional effects on astrocyte‐related or compartmentalized CNS pathology beyond peripheral immune modulation.

Introduction

Multiple Sclerosis (MS) is a chronic central nervous system (CNS) disease of autoimmune origin [1]. The disease mechanisms are heterogenous, involving both acute injury as a consequence of focal inflammation in the CNS initiated in the peripheral immune system, as well as chronic degenerative processes largely confined to the CNS, behind the blood–brain‐barrier (BBB) [2]. Acute inflammatory episodes manifest as clinical exacerbations and new focal lesions detected on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). On the other hand, chronic degenerative processes are believed to constitute the primary substrate of the temporal evolution of progression, which is reflected by accumulation of disability and CNS atrophy over several years [1].

The body fluid biomarkers Neurofilament Light (NfL) and Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP), reflecting the above‐described processes, have been extensively explored in MS. NfL is responsible for maintaining cytoskeletal stability in neurons, particularly in myelinated axons [3]. NfL is released during axonal injury and leaks into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), where its concentration mainly reflects the acute focal inflammatory activity in MS [4].

Via still unknown transport mechanisms, NfL is released in 10–100‐fold lower concentrations into the peripheral compartment, allowing NfL concentrations to be determined with accuracy using ultrasensitive assays developed in recent years. The levels in CSF and blood generally correlate well [5], why NfL in serum and plasma has largely substituted CSF levels in MS and other neurodegenerative disorders.

GFAP is expressed in the cytoplasm of astrocytes, maintaining their cytoskeleton and mechanical tension [6], increased levels of which are considered to reflect the morphological process of astrogliosis. Increased levels of GFAP in CSF have been associated with disability and progression in MS [7] and the role of GFAP as a marker of progression has been confirmed in serum in several studies [8, 9]. CSF and serum levels of GFAP probably reflect diverse pathological processes in MS such as injury, metabolic activity, and astrogliosis.

This study aimed to compare the concentrations of serum NfL (sNfL) and serum GFAP (sGFAP) in patients with RRMS and CIS treated with the disease‐modifying drugs (DMDs) rituximab (RTX) and dimethyl fumarate (DMF) as part of the randomized controlled trial RIFUND‐MS (Rituximab versus Fumarate in Newly Diagnosed Multiple Sclerosis; NCT02746744).

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Settings

This study is an analysis of secondary and post hoc exploratory endpoints of CNS injury markers in peripheral blood in patients participating in the RIFUND‐MS trial, which randomized patients with RRMS or CIS to RTX (1000 mg followed by 500 mg every 6 months) or DMF (240 mg twice daily) for a two‐year treatment period. The study was a multicenter phase 3 trial involving 17 Swedish neurology units conducted between July 2016 and April 2021. The main results and details of the trial design have been published previously [10]. Patients were recruited consecutively after signing written informed consent. All patients in the trial agreed to donate blood samples every 6 months.

The study protocol (Supplement S1) was approved by the ethical review board in Stockholm (reference number 2016/473–32) and the Swedish Medical Products Agency (EudraCT 2015–004116‐38). The post hoc control experiment was approved by the Swedish ethical review authority (reference number 2024–07374‐02). The trial was conducted according to good clinical practice (GCP) and the Declaration of Helsinki [11].

Outcome Definition

Treatment effect evaluated via concentrations of sNfL was a predetermined secondary endpoint of the trial. In addition, the same analyses were performed for sGFAP in all patient subgroups as a post hoc analysis. The analyses were performed on both the intention‐to‐treat (ITT) and per‐protocol (PP) subgroups. We also compared the PP RTX arm and the subgroup of the DMF arm that switched therapy to RTX during the trial (“switch” subgroup). The primary outcome was the reduction of sNfL and sGFAP in each subgroup during the trial. The different subgroups are described in Figure 1.

Definition of the different analyzed subgroups. In the intention to treat (ITT) subset, all patient samples were analyzed according to initial randomization, regardless of if this treatment changed during the trial. In the per protocol (PP) subset, only patients remaining on their initial randomization were used in the analysis. In the “switch subset”, patients in the dimethyl fumarate (DMF) arm that during the trial switched therapy to rituximab (RTX) were compared with the PP RTX subset.

Serum NfL and GFAP Measurements

Serum samples were collected at baseline and months 6, 12 and 24 in all patients as part of the RIFUND‐MS study protocol. Samples were centrifuged, and serum was transferred to cryo vials at the participating site. Then, they were mailed at room temperature (RT) to the coordinating site at Danderyd hospital, Stockholm, where it was stored at −80°C until analysis. All samples were thawed only once.

Serum concentrations of NfL and GFAP were determined with the commercial N2PB Kit on the Quanterix SR‐X instrument, which is based on the Simoa technology (Quanterix Corporation, Billerica, MA., USA). According to the manufacturer, the analytical range for serum NfL was 1.60–1800 ng/L and GFAP 4.15–36,000 ng/L. All samples were run together in one batch, in duplicate, by a blinded board‐certified technician with two controls (low/high) on each plate. The intraassay CV was < 10% for both analytes. The sNfL interplate CV was 7.4% for low controls and 7.2% for high controls; and sGFAP interplate CV was 11% for low controls and 10% for high controls.

Estimates of Disability Worsening

Confirmed disability worsening (CDW) was defined according to the study protocol as an increase of at least 1.0 point on the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) from a baseline score of at least 1.0 or an increase of at least 1.5 points from a baseline score of 0. The increase had to be confirmed 24 weeks later.

To further characterize disability progression, we analyzed progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA) and progression independent of relapse and MRI activity (PIRMA). PIRA was defined as confirmed worsening (CDW) occurring in the absence of any documented clinical relapses during the period spanning from 30 days before the initial worsening visit to the 24‐week confirmation visit. PIRMA was defined as CDW fulfilling the PIRA criteria and occurring in the absence of any detectable radiological activity, defined as new or enlarging T2 lesions or new gadolinium‐enhancing lesions. SDMT worsening was defined as a decrease of 8 points in the scale compared to baseline.

In addition, we analyzed if an increase in biomarker concentrations (∆ biomarker) from baseline to month 24 was associated with a corresponding increased risk of any of the measures of progression.

Statistical Analysis

A linear mixed model was fitted with the log2‐transformed biomarker concentration as the dependent variable and month, treatment, time to freeze (days), age and sex as well as the interaction month*treatment as independent variables. A random intercept was included for each patient. The global p‐value for treatment was calculated using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) comparing the full model to the model without treatment and interaction terms. Pairwise comparisons between months within treatment were calculated using estimated marginal means for each subset and biomarker. The pairwise p‐values were adjusted using Tukey's method.

Time‐to‐event analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age and sex. Treatment allocation, RTX vs. DMF, as well as change in biomarker value (∆) over the trial period, was evaluated in relation to time to CDW, PIRA, PIRMA and SDMT worsening. For ∆ biomarkers analyses, the predictor variable represented log2‐transformed change from baseline to month 24. A one‐unit relative increase in delta corresponds to a doubling of the biomarker concentration over time.

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.3. Linear mixed models and pairwise comparisons using estimated marginal means were implemented in the R packages lmerTest and emmeans [12, 13].

Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein in Serum

sGFAP levels were significantly reduced with DMF, but not RTX (Figure 2B). In DMF‐treated patients, there was a significant GFAP reduction in the ITT subset by 18% over the 2‐year trial period (p < 0.001; Table 2), which was more pronounced in the PP subset (23% decrease; p < 0.001; Table 3). However, patients who switched from DMF to RTX during the trial did not display a significant GFAP reduction compared with RTX‐treated patients (Table 3). In the ITT and PP groups, GFAP decreased continuously over time between all time‐points in the DMF arm, while there was only an initial non‐significant decrease from baseline to month 6 in the RTX arm. The mean sGFAP concentration then gradually returned to baseline levels in RTX‐treated patients. For patients who switched from DMF to RTX during the trial, the initial decline in GFAP concentrations was halted by 12 months and remained unchanged thereafter (Figure 2 and Table 2). Concentrations of GFAP over the course of the trial are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

In the RIFUND‐MS trial, which compared RTX with DMF in RRMS and CIS, we demonstrated a substantial reduction in sNfL concentrations in both treatment arms, which was well in line with our reported reduction of relapses and MRI data from the trial [10]. However, contrary to the trial's primary outcomes, we could not show a more pronounced decrease of NfL in the RTX compared with the DMF arm. In addition, we unexpectedly found significantly decreased sGFAP levels during the trial period in the DMF treatment arm, while the same pattern was not observed in patients treated with RTX. Patients that switched from DMF to RTX, mainly because of clinical or radiological inflammatory activity, did not display further decrease of sGFAP. In contrast, patients remaining on DMF (the PP cohort) exhibited a continuous reduction of GFAP throughout the two‐year study period.

Despite the superior efficacy of RTX on inflammatory activity demonstrated in the primary RIFUND‐MS analysis, the hazard of non‐inflammatory progression was higher in the RTX group compared with the DMF group. While the increased risk of CDW and PIRA did not reach statistical significance, a significantly higher hazard was identified for PIRMA in the rituximab arm. However, the wide confidence intervals reflect limited precision due to the relatively low number of progression events (n = 18).

Importantly, PIRMA represents a more stringent definition of disability progression, as it excludes both clinical relapse activity and radiological inflammatory activity. By reducing heterogeneity in progression events and minimizing the influence of overt inflammatory processes, PIRMA may capture a more homogeneous phenotype of progression. This could explain why the associations were strongest and reached statistical significance for PIRMA compared with the broader definitions of CDW and PIRA. The concordance between the borderline associations for CDW and PIRA and the statistically significant association for PIRMA supports the hypothesis that DMF may influence mechanisms related to progression independent of overt inflammatory activity. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature of the analyses.

Taken together, the reduction in sGFAP observed in the DMF arm, along with the association between increases in sGFAP and PIRMA, suggests that DMF may exert specific effects on astrocyte function or integrity that are distinct from its effects on focal inflammatory activity. Such mechanisms could potentially influence processes related to long‐term disability accumulation.

The efficacy of modern DMDs on relapse activity can typically be confirmed in relatively short clinical trials because of the rapid development of acute inflammatory events, both from a clinical and radiological perspective. In addition, the body fluid biomarker of axonal damage NfL has been shown to be a reliable marker of acute inflammatory activity in terms of clinical exacerbation [14] and active MRI lesions displaying contrast enhancement [5, 15, 16]. Treatment effects on both focal MRI lesions and levels of NfL in both CSF and blood correlate well with clinical relapses [5, 17, 18].

In our study, DMF and RTX appeared highly effective in reducing NfL levels immediately after treatment initiation, with no significant difference between the treatment arms. However, without a placebo arm, the fraction of the response representing regression to the mean cannot be assessed. The drop in the RTX arm from baseline to month 24 in our study was 46%, and the corresponding value for ofatumumab in the ASCLEPIOS I trial was 48% [19], indicating a similar effect between these two anti‐CD20 therapies. Furthermore, analyses of sNfL levels in the ozanimod phase 3 trials showed 20%–27% reduction of sNfL over 24 months in the ozanimod arm and a minor corresponding reduction of 13%–16% in the interferon‐beta‐1a arm [20], in both cases a lower degree of reduction than seen with RTX and DMF in this study.

In the case of slowly progressive disease processes, on the other hand, the timeframe to detect definitive changes is much longer, which makes these far more difficult to capture during the limited duration of regular RCTs. The clinical markers for progressive disease have traditionally been disability worsening, evaluated by EDSS, and the development of brain atrophy. However, levels of the astrocyte marker GFAP have recently been shown to be a promising marker for the progressive pathology, since it appears to correlate with future clinical worsening and the development of degenerative pathology on MRI [8, 21, 22]. Support for the notion that astrocytes are an integral part of the progressive MS pathophysiology also comes from the observation that increased levels in CSF of another astrocyte‐related molecule, YKL40, are strongly linked to MS progression [23]. A treatment effect on a biomarker related to this pathology could be an essential tool to evaluate specific effects on the pathophysiological process of progressive MS, even in shorter clinical trials.

The continuous reduction of sGFAP levels in our patients treated with DMF is intriguing and suggests a distinct pharmacodynamic mechanism not shared with RTX. An early effect on sGFAP upon treatment initiation was reported in an observational study of patients starting natalizumab treatment and followed prospectively [24]. However, after 3 months, the values remained stable over the 2‐year follow‐up period, while patients on DMF in our study continued to decrease in GFAP throughout the trial. In another contemporary study from Sweden, no apparent reduction of GFAP over time was seen in a cohort treated with a mixture of DMDs, indicating that GFAP does not decrease as a general consequence of DMD‐induced reduction of inflammatory activity [25]. The same observation was made in a cohort treated with alemtuzumab, leading to a significantly reduced level of sNfL, while sGFAP levels remained unchanged [26]. Finally, recently presented data from the ozanimod phase 3 trial demonstrated that baseline sGFAP concentrations correlated with future disease severity. However, no data were presented regarding sGFAP dynamics during the trial, which would be expected if significant treatment effects had been seen [27].

The pharmacokinetics and the mode of action of DMF make this drug more likely to affect the pathophysiology of innate immune mechanisms within the CNS compared with RTX. Firstly, the primary metabolite of DMF, monomethyl fumarate (MMF), readily passes the BBB, which monoclonal antibodies such as RTX do to a minimal extent [28, 29]. Secondly, DMF and MMF have been shown to inhibit many intrinsic metabolic pathways that enhance innate immune reactions both in glial cultures [30, 31] and in animal models of neuroinflammation [22, 32]. Therefore, experimental data support the relevance of our observations and their possible clinical significance.

Our study has several shortcomings, particularly the limited possibility of concluding the long‐term effects of DMF treatment on progressive disease pathology. Regarding the NfL data, these are highly consistent with previous data [10, 19]. To the best of our knowledge, the continuous decrease of GFAP throughout the trial period, which was only observed in the DMF arm, is a novel finding. Unlike RTX, experimental and pharmacokinetic data support the idea that DMF exerts a biological effect inside the BBB on astrocyte activation. Interestingly, we could see a clinical correlate to the decrease in sGFAP concentration and PIRMA, the most stringent measure of non‐inflammatory progression, but these data should be interpreted with caution because the number of qualifying events was few. Besides that, our findings will be based on comparisons with non‐controlled clinical data indicating GFAP as a marker of progression, not related to relapses and focal MRI activity. However, the primary strength of our data is that it is retrieved in the context of a randomized clinical trial, with all analyses performed blinded on one occasion. Therefore, the pattern of a continuous decrease of sGFAP in DMF‐treated patients indicates an actual biological effect.

In conclusion, the RIFUND‐MS trial demonstrated that RTX and DMF effectively reduce sNfL levels, aligning with previous evidence linking NfL reduction to suppressing acute inflammation. Interestingly, we also identified a selective and sustained decrease in sGFAP levels exclusively in patients treated with DMF, which also was associated with a lesser degree of non‐inflammatory progression. This novel finding suggests that DMF may specifically target astrocyte‐mediated inflammation, reflecting potential effects on the compartmentalized inflammatory processes associated with progressive MS pathology [1, 33]. Future studies should attempt to clarify whether changes in GFAP levels are corroborated by imaging findings and correspond with robust clinical outcomes, such as slowed neurodegeneration and reduced disability progression, thereby informing treatment strategies aimed at halting long‐term MS progression.
