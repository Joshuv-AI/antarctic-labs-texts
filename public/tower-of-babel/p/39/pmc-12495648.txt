Sequential RGB light optimization across developmental stages enhances lettuce growth through carry-over effects
Authors: Eunjeong Lim, Myung-Min Oh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLight is a critical factor regulating plant development and productivity under controlled environment conditions. However, the light conditions are often kept static throughout the cultivation period, potentially overlooking plants’ dynamic responses to changing environmental stimuli over time. This study proposes a stage-specific optimization strategy to maximize lettuce growth, based primarily on shoot fresh weight by adjusting red:green:blue (R:G:B) light ratio at different growth stages. After transplanting 2-week-old seedlings, their growth period was divided into an early stage (ES, the first 2 weeks) and a late stage (LS, after 2 weeks). To account for potential carry-over effects, the ES optimization was designed to evaluate how early-stage light conditions influence final growth performance. Response surface methodology was then employed to identify the optimal spectral combinations for each stage. The optimal R:G:B light ratios were determined to be 44.2:55.8:0 for ES and 25.2:57.8:16.9 for LS. These results suggest that excluding B light during ES promotes morphological traits favorable for light interception, presumably at the expense of immediate photosynthetic efficiency, and ultimately supporting enhanced biomass accumulation during LS. A sequential-optimized lighting strategy combining these two stage-specific light ratios was then evaluated against other lighting strategies, including a static-optimized, a reference, two white LED treatments with different color temperatures of 2700 and 5000 K. While the static-optimized treatment with an R:G:B ratio of 77:23:0 produced the highest shoot fresh weight during ES, the sequential-optimized ultimately delivered the greatest biomass by the end of the growth stage. These findings highlight the importance of stage-specific light requirements and demonstrate that dynamic light management aligned with developmental physiology can significantly enhance crop productivity. Background

With the advancement and widespread adoption of light-emitting diodes (LEDs), artificial lighting in crop cultivation has evolved from a supplementary source to a practical alternative to sunlight, enabling innovative systems such as vertical farming [1, 2]. It is well known that light plays a dual role in plant growth, functioning both as an energy source for photosynthesis and as a signal that regulates developmental processes. Traditionally, red (R, 600–700 nm) and blue (B, 400–500 nm) light have been regarded as more efficient for photosynthesis than green (G, 500–600 nm) light, due to their higher absorption by photosynthetic pigments [3–5]. However, under mixed spectra or higher irradiance conditions, green light has been recognized for its ability to penetrate into deeper leaf tissues and the lower canopy, promoting beneficial effects [6]. Moreover, spectrum-specific signaling by photoreceptors such as phytochromes, cryptochromes, and phototropins induces morphological changes in plants [7]. These photomorphogenic responses influence not only plant morphology but also photosynthetic ability. Thus, plant responses to light spectra are often non-linear due to complex interactive effects among different wavelengths. For example, Spalholz et al. (2020) [8] reported that in green- and red-leaf lettuce, shoot fresh weight decreased as the proportion of B light increased up to 80%, but unexpectedly increased again under 100% B light, even exceeding the biomass under 100% R light, which resemble shade-like morphological responses observed under monochromatic blue light [9]. Similarly, Izzo et al. (2021) [10] observed both linear and quadratic relationships between the percentage of B light and various lettuce growth characteristics. Building upon this understanding, our previous study employed response surface methodology (RSM), a statistical technique for modeling and optimizing complex processes, to optimize the R:G:B light ratio during the seedling stage of lettuce cultivation [11].

Adjustments in light spectral composition can induce short-term differences in photosynthetic rates per unit leaf area [3, 4], while also triggering long-term changes in chlorophyll biosynthesis, canopy architecture, and other morphological traits [5]. These cumulative, time-dependent changes ultimately affect photosynthetic capacity and light interception, influencing growth and developmental outcomes over the cultivation period. Despite this dynamic nature of plant responses, static light conditions are often maintained throughout crop production, potentially overlooking the shifting physiological demands of plants as they progress through different developmental stages. As one such approach, Samuolienė et al. (2021) [12] implemented shifting spectral combinations during seedling, vegetative, and fruiting stages in tomato. Meng and Runkle (2020) [13] evaluated the effects of 36 combinations of seedling- and mature-stage lighting treatments on red-leaf lettuce grown under six different light spectra. Similarly, Son et al. (2017) [14] demonstrated that weekly adjustments in R and B light combinations influenced both growth and nutritional quality of red-leaf lettuce. Considering the dynamic physiological responses of plants, incorporating sequential lighting strategies across different growth stages may expand the scope of light environment optimization in controlled crop production.

This study aimed to develop a stage-specific sequential lighting strategy by optimizing R:G:B light ratios for different growth stages using RSM, and to evaluate its effectiveness on lettuce growth compared with other lighting regimes. The sequential lighting strategy, consisting of the seedling stage [11] and subsequent early and late growth phases, was designed to maximize final biomass by considering carry-over effects from intermediate treatments.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Experiments were conducted in an environment-controlled growth room equipped with ten semi-deep flow technique hydroponic systems (80 × 68 × 10 cm; W × L × H). Seeds of ‘Fairly’ butterhead lettuce (Enza Zaden, Enkhuizen, The Netherlands) were germinated on moistened polyurethane sponges placed in trays covered with transparent lids to maintain relative humidity above 95% for 3 days. The seedlings were grown on the sponges for 2 weeks and then transplanted to the hydroponic systems for 4 weeks of subsequent growth.

Temperature and relative humidity in the growth room were set and maintained at 23 °C and 90% for day before transplanting, and at 20 °C and 70% after transplanting. CO2 concentration was maintained at 500 µmol mol−1 before transplanting and increased to 800 µmol mol−1 thereafter. Temperature and relative humidity in the room were continuously monitored using a humidity and temperature probe (HMP60; Vaisala, Vantaa, Finland), while CO2 concentrations were measured using a CO2 sensor probe (KCP-HP100; Korea Digital, Seoul, Republic of Korea). All environmental data were recorded using a data logger (CR1000, Campbell Scientific, Logan, UT, USA). Air circulation within the growth room was maintained by using three mini fans, with airflow between 0.7 and 1.0 m s−1, measured at each hydroponic module using an anemometer (Testo 405i; Testo, Titisee-Neustadt, Germany). The nutrient solution for lettuce used and detailed composition is provided in Supplemental Table 1 [15]. The pH was adjusted to 5.8, and the solution was replenished every 3 days to maintain the electrical conductivity (EC) at 0.8 dS m⁻¹ before transplanting and 1.2 dS m⁻¹ thereafter. EC values were monitored using a pH/EC meter (WTW Multi 3430 IDS; WTW GmbH, Weilheim, Germany).

Light treatments and mixture design setup

Before transplanting, seedlings were grown at 200 µmol m−2 s−1 photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) with a 16-h photoperiod (16/8 h, day/night) as described in our previous study (R:G:B = 30.6:44.0:25.4) [11]. After transplanting, they were treated with R, G, and B LED lights as mixture components (Fig. 1) to apply RSM using a mixture design.

Overview of stage-specific optimization of red (R), green (G), and blue (B) light ratios for lettuce growth under controlled environment conditions using response surface methodology. (A) RGB color space showing various R:G:B light ratios. (B) Selected experimental treatments plotted on the RGB triangle based on a simplex axial mixture design, representing different R:G:B light ratios. (C) Response surface generated from the growth data collected through exploratory experiments with these treatments. The optimal R:G:B light ratio is identified as the points on the response surface corresponding to the highest response value

All treatments were maintained at a PPFD as 200 µmol m−2 s−1 which was achieved by combining R, G and B light components, but the spectral ratios varied according to the experimental design. A total of ten experimental R:G:B light ratio combinations were selected based on a simplex axial design: R, G, B, R:G = 1:1 (RG1:1), G:B = 1:1 (G:B1:1), R:B = 1:1 (R:B1:1), R:G:B = 4:1:1 (R:G:B4:1:1), R:G:B = 1:4:1 (R:G:B1:4:1), R:G:B = 1:1:4 (R:G:B1:1:4), and R:G:B = 1:1:1 (R:G:B1:1:1). The treatments were provided using custom-made RGB LED fixtures (Bissol LED, Seoul, Republic of Korea). R:G:B light ratios were adjusted at each hydroponic module using dimmable controls based on measurements from a spectroradiometer (JAZ-EL 200; Ocean Optics, Dunedin, FL, USA) and a quantum meter (Li-1400; Li-Cor Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA).

Stage-specific light recipe experiment design

The growth stages were divided into 0–2 weeks after transplanting (WAT) as the early stage (ES) and 2–4 WAT as the late stage (LS), based on our preliminary growth curve of lettuce, in which a distinct change in slope was observed around 2 WAT, indicating accelerated growth. Experiments to optimize R:G:B light ratio for lettuce growth were sequentially conducted in three steps: (1) optimization of R:G:B light ratio for ES using RSM, (2) optimization of R:G:B light ratio for LS using RSM, and (3) validation of the stage-specific sequential lighting strategy against control treatments.

In step (1), seedlings transplanted to ten hydroponic modules were treated with different R:G:B light ratios. After 2 weeks of treatment, a subset of seedlings from each treatment were sampled for growth measurements, while the remaining seedlings were transferred to white LED light to assess the carry-over effects of the initial light ratio treatments on final yield. Shoot fresh weight at 4 WAT was used as the response variable to construct the ES response surface and the R:G:B light ratio producing the highest shoot fresh weight was selected as optimal for ES. In step (2), seedlings grown under the selected ES-optimal light ratio were subsequently treated with the same ten R:G:B light ratio combinations used for ES. Using RSM, the optimal R:G:B light ratio for LS was identified based on its effect on shoot fresh weight at 4 WAT. In step (3), the complete stage-specific sequential lighting strategy (sequential-optimized), including the light condition before transplanting as described by Lim et al. (2023) [11], was validated by comparison with four other lighting regimes: a static-optimized R:G:B light ratio treatment, in which a single RSM-optimized spectral combination was applied consistently throughout cultivation, two white LED treatments at different color temperatures of 2700 and 5000 K, and a previously reported optimal spectral combination for Boston lettuce from Kong et al. (2015) [16]. The static-optimized light ratio was pre-determined through a separate RSM experiment conducted under uniform cultivation conditions. The R:G:B ratios applied to each treatment are summarized in Table 1, while light intensity and photoperiod were consistently maintained at 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and 16 h, respectively.

Measurement of growth characteristics

At harvest, shoot fresh weight was measured using an electronic balance (Si-234; Denver Instr., NY, USA). Leaf area per plant was determined using a leaf area meter (Li-3100; Li-Cor Inc.) and projected leaf area (PLA) was estimated from top-view images using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Chlorophyll contents were assessed as SPAD values in fully expanded leaves using a portable chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502; Minolta Camera Co. Ltd., Osaka, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All treatments for growth measurements were replicated eight times. Eight plants were randomly selected for destructive analysis from 20 plants per treatment. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to test for significant differences among treatments, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Response surface analysis was conducted using Design-Expert software (version 13; Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Results

Optimization of R:G:B light ratio for ES

During ES, distinct differences in growth characteristics were observed among various R:G:B light ratio treatments. Shoot fresh weight was significantly higher with R, G, and R: G1:1 treatments than with B, R:B1:1, R:G:B1:1:4, and R:G:B1:1:1 treatments (Fig. 2A). A similar trend was observed in leaf area measurements (Fig. 2B). PLA was greatest with G treatment, followed by R:G1:1 treatment (Fig. 2C). Although shoot fresh weight and leaf area were not statistically significantly different between R and G treatments, PLA was 55.4% greater with G treatment than with R treatment. SPAD values tended to be higher with the treatments containing B light than with those lacking it (Fig. 2D).

To assess any carry-over effects of the light treatments during ES, shoot fresh weight was measured again at 4 WAT after all plants had been grown under uniform white light conditions between 2 and 4 WAT. Shoot fresh weight at 4 WAT was higher with G and R:G1:1 treatments than with B, R:B1:1, and R:G:B1:1:1 treatments (Fig. 2E). Using shoot fresh weight at 4 WAT as the response variable and R:G:B light ratios during ES as the factor, the response surface was constructed for identifying optimal condition (Fig. 2F). The highest value on the surface was obtained with R:G:B = 44.2:55.8:0 identifying the most optimal condition, labelled as ES_Optimal. With the ES_Optimal treatment, shoot fresh weight at 2 WAT was found to be highest when compared to those with R, G, R:G1:1, or R:G:B1:4:1 treatment, but it was not statistically significantly different from those with R or R:G1:1 treatment (Fig. 2G). At 4 WAT, shoot fresh weight did not differ significantly among treatments, although the highest mean value was observed with the ES_Optimal treatment, followed by R:G1:1, R, R:G:B1:4:1, and G (Fig. 2H).

Optimization of R: G:B light ratio for LS

During LS, lettuce growth was considerably variable with R:G:B light ratio treatments. Shoot fresh weight was significantly higher with R, R:G1:1, R:B1:1, R:G:B1:4:1, and R:G:B1:1:1 treatments than with G, B, G:B1:1, R:G:B4:1:1, and R:G:B1:1:4 treatments (Fig. 3A). The highest and lowest shoot fresh weights were observed with R:G:B1:4:1 and R:G:B1:1:4 treatments, respectively. Among the monochromatic light treatments, shoot fresh weight tended to be higher with R treatment than with G or B treatment. The shoot fresh weight was found to be higher with R:G1:1 and R:B1:1 dichromatic light treatments than with G:B1:1 treatment. However, shoot fresh weight was variable among the trichromatic light treatments. Based on the response surface generated using shoot fresh weight as the response variable, the optimal R:G:B light ratio during LS was determined to be 25.2:57.8:16.9 (Fig. 3B).

With the identified ES_Optimal and LS_Optimal, a complete stage-specific sequential lighting strategy was established, enabling dynamic spectral control throughout the growth period under artificial lighting conditions (Fig. 4). The strategy applied three distinct R:G:B light ratios under a constant light intensity of 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and a 16-h photoperiod, with 44:56:0 and 25:58:17 selected for the early and late stages, respectively (Fig. 4; Table 1).

Verification for stage-specific sequential lighting strategy

With the five light treatments, changes in shoot fresh weight over 6-week growth are shown in Fig. 5. At the end of seedling stage, shoot fresh weight was highest with static-optimized treatment, followed by sequential-optimized and 2700 K white treatments, both of which resulted in higher shoot fresh weight than reference and 5000 K white treatments (Fig. 5). This trend continued through the end of ES, where shoot fresh weight was 69.9% higher with static-optimized treatment than with 5000 K white treatment. Shoot fresh weight was significantly higher with both static-optimized and sequential-optimized treatments than with 5000 K white treatment. At the end of LS, shoot fresh weight was highest with sequential-optimized treatment, which was 21% higher than with 5000 K white treatment. After the 6-week growth, final shoot fresh weight ranked in the following order: sequential-optimized > 2700 K white > static-optimized > reference > 5000 K white treatment. SPAD value was higher with sequential-optimized, 5000 K and reference treatment, following 2700 K and static-optimized (Fig. 5B). Under the same RGB LED conditions, the static-optimized and sequential-optimized treatments showed no significant difference in final shoot fresh weight. However, the sequential-optimized resulted in significantly higher SPAD values, indicating a notable difference in green leaf lettuce quality. The 2700 K white LED treatment, which produced the second highest average biomass after the sequential-optimized treatment, exhibited significantly lower SPAD values.

Discussion

Application of RSM for light optimization

In the present study, the R:G:B light ratio for irradiating lettuce plants was optimized using RSM. Although RSM has been more commonly applied in fields such as architecture, food engineering, and pharmaceutical science [17, 18], it offers significant advantages in agricultural research by accounting for interaction effects among variables and reducing the number of required experimental treatments [19]. A mixture experimental design, specifically a simplex axial design, was adopted to identify optimal component ratios within a fixed total amount and to comprehensively test all mono-, di-, and tri-chromatic light conditions [20]. This approach enabled the generation of continuous response surfaces while efficiently analyzing variable interactions based on a limited number of experimental points.

Shoot fresh weight at harvest was selected as the primary response variable. However, RSM can also incorporate additional growth parameters or weighted combinations of multiple indicators. To fit the experimental data, a special quartic regression model was applied to generate the response surfaces for both ES and LS of lettuce growth, as well as the fixed R:G:B light ratio treatment (Figs. 2F and 3B). Each model yielded an R² value above 0.9, indicating a high accuracy of fit despite some deviation between the predicted and actual values. Overall, RSM proved to be an effective tool for optimizing three or more variables, making it particularly valuable in agricultural research where multiple environmental factors interact to influence plant growth. Furthermore, RSM facilitates the exploration of variable interactions and supports multi-objective decision-making, such as identifying indicators of seedling quality [11].

Effects of red and green light combination during the early stage

In our experiment, the analysis resulted in an optimal spectrum for the early stage of lettuce cultivation as R:G:B = 44.2:55.8:0, a unique combination with no contribution from blue light. At 2 WAT during ES, although shoot fresh weight and leaf area did not differ significantly between the R and R:G1:1 treatments, the significantly higher PLA observed under R:G1:1 suggests a morphological adjustment to enhance light interception under the combined R and G light conditions (Figs. 2A-C). By 4 WAT, shoot fresh weight under R:G1:1 treatment exceeded that under R treatment during LS, further supporting the positive effects of G light addition. This result suggests that, in the absence of blue light, the early improvement in canopy development induced a carry-over effect, contributing to increased biomass accumulation at a later stage. Although photosynthetic pigments absorb G light less efficiently, G light penetrates deeper into leaf tissues and lower canopy layers, enhancing overall light utilization [21]. Furthermore, G light is known to induce shade avoidance responses in plants, promoting leaf expansion, petiole elongation, and changes in leaf angle [21, 22]. These morphological adaptations, which are antagonistic to B light effects [22], can increase light interception area. Consistent with this, during ES, PLA was 72.3% higher under G treatment than under R treatment (Fig. 2C), despite no significant differences in shoot fresh weight or leaf area (Fig. 2A and B). Meanwhile, from an optical power perspective, green light has a lower LED emission efficiency than that of red and blue light, which can be disadvantageous from an economic standpoint (red: 0.022 W nm⁻¹, green: 0.008 W nm⁻¹, and blue: 0.031 W nm⁻¹; [23]). The use of phosphor‑converted LEDs with a specific RGB ratio could be considered, as this may mitigate the low optical efficiency of green‑enriched light. In the long term, identifying the optimal light quality will require further studies that integrate both plant growth performance and light‑use efficiency.

Role of blue light during the late stage and carry-over effects

Unlike the ES_Optimal, the LS_Optimal condition included 16.9% B light (Fig. 3B; Table 1). LS corresponds to a period of rapid biomass accumulation, with approximately 80% of biomass increase occurring in this period. Blue light is primarily absorbed by chlorophyll, making it highly effective for photosynthesis [21]. In the long term, it is also essential for normal plant morphogenesis, including the biosynthesis of chlorophyll [24, 25]. Approximately 15% B light has been reported to be required for normal plant development, aligning with the B light proportion used in LS_Optimal [24]. Although both the sequential-optimized and static-optimized treatments employed a Blue-deprivation strategy during ES, the static-optimized showed higher average shoot fresh weight at 4 WAT (Fig. 5). However, it was ultimately surpassed by the sequential-optimized—which included a portion of blue light during LS—highlighting the critical role of blue light in sustaining growth during later stages. Although monochromatic red light is generally associated with red light syndrome, which is characterized by reduced photosynthetic capacity and diminished leaf pigmentation, the syndrome was not evident in our study [26]. Despite lower SPAD values under red light, shoot fresh weight remained high, indicating that the relatively short exposure period (two weeks per stage) may have prevented the manifestation of severe symptoms. These results suggest the existence of a temporal threshold related to blue light deprivation that could modulate the onset of red light syndrome. Although the static-optimized and 2700 K white treatments accumulated a similar amount of biomass by harvest, both exhibited significantly lower chlorophyll content than the sequential-optimized treatment, likely due to the reduced proportion of blue light during LS. Since chlorophyll content can serve as a quality indicator in green leaf lettuce, it should be considered alongside yield maximization when aiming to improve marketability. As a result, morphological changes induced during ES—particularly leaf expansion associated with shade avoidance—may have enhanced light interception during LS, thereby contributing to increased biomass accumulation. This suggests that early-stage light environments can exert carry-over effects on subsequent developmental stages. Consistent with these observations, our previous study showed that lettuce seedlings grown under mono R light exhibited vigorous early growth but developed abnormal structural traits, which ultimately resulted in lower growth after transplanting to white LED conditions compared with seedlings that had received other light treatments during the period raising seedlings [11]. Similarly, Meng and Runkle (2020) [13] reported that early-stage lighting conditions significantly influenced final growth outcomes, even when not maintained throughout the entire cultivation period.

Application of dynamic lighting in vertical farming

In vertical farming systems that utilize artificial lighting, dynamic light control has been examined in several previous studies as a means of varying the light environment within the crop growth cycle [13, 14, 27, 28]. In the present study, the effects of dynamic control were evaluated during the vegetative growth stage of lettuce; however, such an approach may be more familiar and more commonly applied to fruiting vegetables such as tomato, where developmental stages are more clearly defined [12, 29]. Different light spectral treatments during the reproductive growth stage significantly influence the ripening time, fruit number, and nutrient content [29]. Although the duration of dynamic control in this study was set at two-week intervals, it may range from hours to months depending on the crop and the scope of control. Jishi et al. (2016) [30] demonstrated that, when the same total amount of red and blue light was applied within a day using different methods, lettuce growth was more strongly influenced by the diurnal photoperiod determined by the treatment than by the specific schedule within the day. Four days of sequential daily lighting in kale sprouts produced markedly different appearance, pigment content, antioxidant capacity, and glucosinolate content within the same cultivar and cultivation period [28]. These examples illustrate that optimization targets for dynamic lighting can extend beyond shoot fresh weight to include traits related to quality and secondary metabolites. Furthermore, the scope of dynamic lighting control is not limited to spectral quality; it can also encompass light intensity and photoperiod, enabling broader applications including energy cost optimization [27, 31]. For the same total light integral over the cultivation period, gradually increasing light intensity resulted in significantly higher lettuce dry weight compared with treatments in which light intensity was kept constant or decreased [27]. Another study reported that, with the total light integral held constant, implementing a dynamic lighting system reduced annual electricity costs by an average of 29.8% compared with static treatments [31]. Taken together, these examples indicate that dynamic lighting represents a promising approach in vertical farming, with considerable potential to optimize yield, quality attributes, and energy efficiency in diverse crops and cultivation systems.

Conclusion

In this study, stage-specific sequential R:G:B light ratios delivering a total PPFD of 200 µmol m−2 s−1 were optimized using RSM to enhance lettuce growth. The optimal combination for the ES included only R and G light, promoting morphological traits favorable for light interception, thereby supporting subsequent biomass accumulation during LS. In contrast, the optimal light ratio for the late stage included a modest proportion of B light (16.9%) while maintaining a contribution from G light. These stage-specific light ratio treatments were then combined into sequential-optimized treatment and compared with other lighting regimes, including a static-optimized, a reference, two white LED treatments at 2700 and 5000 K. The sequential-optimized ultimately outperformed all other treatments in terms of shoot fresh weight at harvest and also exhibited higher SPAD values than the static-optimized and 2700 K white LED treatment, indicating not only enhanced biomass accumulation but also improved leaf quality in green leaf lettuce. Overall, this study highlights the importance of aligning light spectra with crop developmental stages. Implementing dynamic, stage-specific light strategies may offer a practical and effective approach for improving yield performance and resource use efficiency in controlled-environment crop production systems. These findings contribute to the growing body of research on dynamic light and environmental control strategies for optimizing crop growth.
