Resistance mechanisms and potential therapeutic strategies in relapsed or refractory natural killer/T cell lymphoma
Authors: Chengji Wang, Liang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural killer/T cell lymphoma (NKTCL) is a malignant tumor originating from NK or T cells, characterized by its highly aggressive and heterogeneous nature. NKTCL is predominantly associated with Epstein–Barr virus infection, disproportionately affecting Asian and Latin American populations. Owing to the application of asparaginase and immunotherapy, clinical outcomes have improved significantly. However, for patients in whom first-line treatment fails, the prognosis is exceedingly poor. Overexpression of multidrug resistance genes, abnormal signaling pathways, epigenetic modifications and active Epstein–Barr virus infection may be responsible for resistance. This review summarized the mechanisms of resistance for NKTCL and proposed potential therapeutic approaches.

Introduction

Natural killer/T cell lymphoma (NKTCL) is a special type of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) that originates from mature NK or T cells, and is characterized by a highly aggressive clinical course. This lymphoma subtype presents an angiocentric and angiodestructive growth pattern accompanied by necrosis and ulceration of the affected tissues.[1] The distribution of NKTCL demonstrates obvious geographical differences and a strong correlation with infection and the genetic background of the Asian population. It predominantly affects Asian and Latin American populations, with a higher predilection for men.[2,3] The pathogenesis of NKTCL involves a multitude of genetic abnormalities and alterations in signal pathways, such as the upregulation of the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT), nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway, and mutations of DEAD-box helicase 3 X-linked gene (DDX3X).[4] The upregulation of programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), an immune escape gene, is also observed. Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) plays a key role in the pathogenesis of lymphoma, particularly in NKTCL.[5] More frequent gene deletions in EBV-infected cells are associated with neoplastic proliferation, but the specific pathogenic mechanism is still under exploration.[6]

Doxorubicin-based chemotherapy treatment regimens are far from satisfactory.[7] The application of asparaginase marked a milestone event, substantially improving the prognosis. The combination of chemotherapy containing asparaginase and radiotherapy resulted in a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of 80%.[8] However, for patients with advanced and refractory/relapse (R/R) NKTCL, poor clinical outcomes remain an inescapable challenge, even after stem cell transplantation.[9,10] The overexpression of the multi-drug resistance (MDR) genes is a crucial factor, and the upregulation of the adenosine 5′-triphosphate-ase (ATPase)-binding cassette (ABC) proteins and permeability-glycoprotein (P-gp) are also correlated with chemotherapy resistance. For relapsed patients who reused to first-line therapy, the median OS was a mere 6.4 months.[11] Epigenetic modifications, such as histone methylation and deacetylation, mediate the silencing of multiple tumor suppressor genes, playing a key role in resistance.[12] Furthermore, resistance involves alterations in various signal transduction pathways, and the upregulation of autophagy signal pathways may contribute to asparaginase resistance.[13,14] EBV-infected tumor cells could increase the expression of programmed death-1 (PD-1) to achieve immune escape, and increased levels of EBV-DNA were detected in plasma to predict a significantly shorter OS and poor response to treatment.[15] These findings suggest that traditional chemotherapy may be insufficient for R/R NKTCL patients.

Immunotherapy and targeted therapy have emerged as promising approaches for the treatment of R/R NKTCL. Application of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies demonstrated significant clinical benefits in advanced and R/R NKTCL.[16] Notably, the combination of epigenetic therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has exhibited encouraging efficacy for R/R NKTCL.[17] Adoptive T-cell therapy targeting EBV is another therapeutic strategy currently under investigation.[18] Nevertheless, a considerable proportion of patients fail to respond to these treatments or experience relapse shortly after an initial response. Therefore, it is imperative to elucidate and analyze the mechanisms underlying NKTCL resistance and to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Mechanisms of Resistance

MDR

The expression of MDR genes is a key factor in the resistance to doxorubicin-based chemotherapy regimens [Figure 1]. P-gp, a downstream product of the MDR1 gene, also known as ATP-binding cassette subfamily B member 1 (ABCB1), mediates the efflux of doxorubicin and vincristine from NKTCL cells and results in resistance.[19] This leads to a lower response rate of the MDR-positive NKTCL subtype to cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (CHOP) or CHOP-like chemotherapy regimens. Wang et al[20] examined P-gp expression in 30 NKTCL patients before treatment and found that 67% displayed positive results. The overall response rate (ORR) and complete remission (CR) rate of the P-gp-positive group was markedly lower than that of negative group after receiving CHOP or CHOP-like chemotherapy. However, contrary to expectations, Kim et al[21] found that 80% of initially treated NKTCL patients expressed P-gp, but it was not shown that survival was associated with expression of P-gp. In general, we hold the view that P-gp expression is one factor in chemotherapy resistance, involved in more complex mechanisms, but it is not correlated with B symptoms or Ann Arbor Stage.

Drug resistance mechanisms mediated by MDR genes. The ABC family, which is the downstream products of MDR genes, participates in chemotherapy efflux in an ATP-dependent manner. Meanwhile, the ABC family is also involved in the apoptotic process by activating the MYC/BCL-2 protein and inhibiting the Bax protein. The ABCC4 and ABCG2 are regulated by IL-10 and the JAK-STAT3 signal pathway. The expression of P-gp, which is also ABCB1, is correlated with the Wnt/β-catenin signal pathway. Besides, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway also increases the expression of MYC, survivin, and CyclinD1 protein, promoting the proliferation of tumor cells. LncRNA is involved in the regulation of P-gp expression through epigenetic modifications. The ABC membrane transporter family is essential for MDR genes to confer resistance by enhancing the efflux of chemotherapeutic drugs through ATP-dependent mechanisms. Wu et al[22] found that the ATP-binding cassette subfamily G member 2 (ABCG2) was highly expressed in side population (SP) cells-SNK-6/ADM-SP (a doxorubicin-resistant NKTCL cell line), which showed increased resistance to doxorubicin, gemcitabine, cisplatin, and cytarabine, while no significant effect on the tumor toxicity of asparaginase was observed. In addition to mediating efflux, the overexpression of ABCG2 decreased the levels of pro-apoptotic proteins (caspase 3 and BCL-2 associated X) and increased the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins (BCL-2 and C-MYC).[23]

In addition to ABCG2, the ABC subfamily member C4 (ABCC4) is also involved in MDR-associated proteins and contributes to doxorubicin resistance. The level of IL-13 was significantly correlated with the expression of ABCC4.[24,25] The upregulation of IL-13 promotes ABCC4 expression and proliferation of tumor cells, and ABCC4 knockdown reverses these effects.[25] Huo et al[26] also found that the upregulation of IL-10 induced ABCC4 expression and was associated with the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, significantly weakening the effects of gemcitabine-mediated tumor killing, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis promotion. The relationship between ABCC4 and chemoresistance has been demonstrated in a variety of tumors.[27–30]

Moreover, epigenetic modifications play a role in P-gp expression and MDR. The long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) small nucleolar RNA host gene 12 (SNHG12) was upregulated in NKTCL samples. Elevated levels of SNHG12 correlate with tumor growth, overexpression of P-gp, and resistance to cisplatin. This process is induced by the upregulated expression of C-MYC, and SNHG12 is a direct transcriptional target of C-MYC.[31]

Compared with its activation in normal or inflammatory NK or T cells, the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signal pathway was detected in NKTCL cells, which upregulated C-MYC and CyclinD1, inducing tumor proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis.[32] The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway correlated with the expression of P-gp, motivating MDR and doxorubicin efflux in cholangiocarcinoma.[33,34] Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a promising drug-resistance target, but research on this pathway in NKTCL remains limited.

Resistance to asparaginase

Compared with normal NK or T cells, it was observed that significant promoter hypermethylation occurred in most NKTCL samples mediating gene silencing, including asparagine synthetase (ASNS). This resulted in asparagine deficiency and dependency of exogenous asparagine.[35] Asparaginase can specifically decompose asparagine in serum. The lack of essential amino acids prevents tumor cells from continuing to synthesize proteins and DNA, ultimately inducing apoptosis.[36,37] After the application of asparaginase in the treatment of NKTCL patients, the efficacy and clinical outcomes have been significantly improved.[38–40] Despite the encouraging efficacy of asparaginase, the 5-year OS rate for patients with advanced NKTCL is only approximately 40%.[9] Primary chemotherapy resistance remains a pressing challenge.

In vitro and in vivo experiments showed that overexpression of ASNS significantly decreased the sensitivity of tumor cells to asparaginase. This might be caused by increased intracellular asparagine synthesis, reducing dependence on exogenous asparagine.[41] Besides, epigenetic regulatory therapy could reactivate ASNS gene expression, leading to asparaginase resistance; however, clinical and experimental evidence is needed to test this hypothesis.

Pegaspargase, a polyethylene glycol-modified asparaginase (PEG-Asp), has been widely used in the treatment of NKTCL. PEG-Asp was more tolerable than asparaginase, with similar efficacy.[42] Serum antibodies against PEG would significantly inhibit asparaginase activity in patients receiving PEG-Asp, causing ineffective PEG-Asp concentrations and poor response.[43] In a clinical study of asparaginase for childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia therapy, specific antibodies for asparaginase were detected in 35.5% of patients. However, due to limited data, no definitive conclusion has been drawn regarding the association between antibodies and resistance.[44]

Wang et al[14] first reported that NKTCL acquires asparaginase resistance by upregulating autophagy-related signaling pathways to inhibit asparaginase-induced apoptosis. The overexpression of brain cytoplasmic RNA 1 (BCYRN1), a type of lncRNA known as BC200, was observed, followed by the increased expression of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 and cell cycle factor CyclinD1. Tumor cells can optimize metabolic processes through autophagy to survive in a nutrition-deprived environment. In addition, the upregulation of the autophagy pathway could also clear the damaged organelles of tumor cells and avoid apoptosis.[45] However, the effect of autophagy on tumors is bidirectional. Autophagy plays a crucial role in blocking the origin and progression of tumors. Beclin 1 is an another essential factor in autophagy, and its expression was detected in 86.2% (56/65) of NKTCL samples. Decreased levels of Beclin 1 correlate with advanced stage and lower response rate to chemotherapy in NKTCL patients. It demonstrates that the absence of autophagy might imply a more aggressive tumor biological behavior and poorer prognosis.[46,47]

Abnormal glutamine metabolism is another metabolic characteristic of NKTCL. The overexpression of solute carrier family 1 member 1 (SLC1A1) increases glutamine uptake and mediates the proliferation and growth of tumor cells. Xiong et al[48] found that the upregulation of SLC1A1 was associated with sensitivity to asparaginase. Reduced SLC1A1 expression indicates poor prognosis and chemotherapy resistance, suggesting that this subgroup might not depend excessively on exogenous nutrients. Immune evasion could be a reason for this, and the upregulation of immune checkpoints was detected.

Resistance to asparaginase has also been linked to epigenetic modifications and the activation of multiple signal pathways, including JAK/STAT and NF-κB signal pathways.[49,50] This part will be discussed in more detail later.

Epigenetic modifications

Many epigenetic modifications have been characterized in NKTCL, including promoter hypermethylation and histone deacetylase recruitment, mediating the silencing of tumor suppressor genes.[12,35] In an analysis of the whole exome sequence of R/R NKTCL patients, the mutation frequency of epigenetic regulatory genes was increased significantly, including lysine methyltransferase 2D (KMT2D), which was correlated with histone methylation.[51] Mutation or the deletion of KMT2D was associated with resistance and poor prognosis in NKTCL.[52]

The constitutive activation of the JAK/STAT pathway is a considerable mechanism in the NKCTL pathogenesis.[53] Receptor-type tyrosine-protein phosphatase κ (PTPRκ), an essential protein regulating the activation of STAT3, was downregulated in NKTCL due to low PTPRκ mRNA expression caused by promoter hypermethylation and deletion. This activates the JAK/STAT and downstream signaling pathways, leading to resistance to SMILE regimens (steroids, methotrexate, ifosphamide, L-asparaginase, and etoposide).[50]

The histone methyltransferase enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) is directly phosphorylated by JAK3 and overexpressed in NKTCL, which is involved in epigenetic regulation and tumor proliferation.[54] The overexpression of EZH2 was detected in 55.2% (21/38) of NKTCL samples, which was involved in trimethylation of lysine 27 on the histone H3 protein subunit, correlated with advanced disease and poor response to therapy.[55]

Promoter methylation analysis revealed the silencing of numerous tumor suppression-related genes, including BCL-2-like 11 (BIM) and DAPK1. BIM is associated with asparaginase sensitivity. In vitro, after restoring BIM expression, an increase in asparaginase-induced apoptosis was observed.[35]DAPK1 is a downstream transcriptional target of P53 and is involved in P53-dependent apoptosis.[56] The downregulation of tumor suppressor gene positive regulatory domain containing I (PRDM1) was discovered in 44% of NKTCL samples, due to promoter hypermethylation and the overexpression of miR-223.[57,58] Absence of PRDM1 predicted poor response to chemotherapy agents and poor OS. After demethylation, PRDM1 expression was upregulated, leading to the inhibition of cell proliferation and increased apoptosis, suggesting that it is involved in apoptosis induced by chemotherapy.[58] Besides, the deletion and downregulation of PRDM1 are associated with increased C-MYC expression, which is a poor prognostic factor in B-cell lymphoma.[59] Another tumor suppressor gene, P73, was also inhibited by promoter hypermethylation in 94% of NKTCL patients, which mediated apoptosis in a manner similar to P53.[60] Epigenetic modifications resulting in the blocking of apoptosis signals might be a crucial factor in chemotherapy resistance.

Resistance induced by EBV and changes of signal pathway

EBV plays a key role in the pathogenesis and development of NKTCL, and was also a predictive factor in resistance and relapse.[61] Before treatment, an increased count of EBV DNA was correlated to poorer response and resistance to asparaginase-based chemotherapy.[62] In patients receiving asparaginase chemotherapy and sequential radiotherapy, EBV DNA positivity after treatment was significantly associated with lymphoma relapse and shorter survival.[63] Through direct stimulation or epigenetic modifications, EBV promotes the activation of a variety of cell proliferation signal pathways and the inhibition of apoptosis, resulting in survival dis advantages and resistance.

Latent membrane protein-1 (LMP-1) is a kind of membrane protein encoded by EBV that is expressed on the surface of the latently infected cells. LMP-1 could significantly activate NF-κB and Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) PI3K/Akt/mTOR signal pathway, mediating tumor growth and apoptotic resistance.[64,65] It was reported that clonal LMP-1 mutations containing 30 bp deletion were correlated with resistance to asparaginase and shorter OS, but the molecular mechanism of this process needs further exploration.[66]

Survivin is an anti-apoptotic protein that inhibits caspase activity, and is overexpressed in NKTCL cells. The upregulation of surviving was driven by the activation of NF-κB signal pathway and LMP-1, which was associated with poor prognosis.[13] Bioinformatics analysis showed that survivin was widely involved in the cell cycle and cell proliferation processes, and was significantly correlated with asparaginase-based chemotherapy resistance.[67]

Wang et al[68] reported that LMP-1 could upregulate IL-2Rα expression through stimulation of Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and NF-κB signal pathways, which was correlated with resistance to gemcitabine, doxorubicin, and asparaginase. This process could be caused by proliferative effect of IL-2Rα, and monoclonal antibodies of IL-2Rα restored sensitivity to chemotherapy. Additionally, DDX3X, a type of RNA helicase gene, was mutated in approximately 20% of NKTCL patients. These mutations were associated with the activation and upregulation of MAPK and NK-κB signal pathway, showing a poorer response to CHOP-based therapy. In addition, patients with mutations in both P53 and DDX3X had significantly shorter OS and progression-free survival (PFS), suggesting a potential mechanism for resistance.[69]P53 inactivation has been associated with poor prognosis in a variety of tumors, and this study also observed that the P53 mutations subgroup showed resistance to chemotherapy. However, Xiong et al[49] reported that the TSIM subgroup, which is characterized by mutations in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and P53, did not present a worse prognosis after treatment with asparaginase-based chemotherapy.

The overexpression of the C-MYC was observed in NKTCL, which was significantly associated with resistance to chemotherapy and poor prognosis.[70,71] Epstein–Barr virus nuclear antigen 2 (EBNA2) and LMP-1 are involved in the increased C-MYC expression.[13] Epigenetic modifications, such as miRNA downregulation, also contributed to this process, and higher expression of downstream targets EZH2 and Runt-domain transcription factor 3 (RUNX3) followed.[72] As mentioned previously, EZH2 overexpression was a factor in resistance, and RUNX3 was subject to C-MYC, involved in cell proliferation, the inhibition of apoptosis, and the activation of the effective gene functions.[73] Moreover, TOX high mobility group box family member 2 (TOX2) was a potential downstream transcription target of RUNX3, whose overexpression was significantly correlated with poor prognosis and resistance. Super-enhancers are one mechanism underlying TOX2 overexpression. TOX2 is necessary for the formation of exhausted T cells in the tumor microenvironment, which might be related to a weakened antitumor immune response.[74,75] Xiong et al[49] reported that the NKTCL subtype, characterized by MGA gene silencing and subsequent C-MYC overexpression, demonstrated a lower response rate to asparaginase-based treatment and significantly poorer prognosis. Enhanced RAS/MAPK signaling and proliferation have been observed during this process.

The activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and the constitutive expression of phospho-STAT3 (p-STAT3) have been observed in NKTCL tumor tissues. The latter correlated with the overexpression of Ki-67 and poorer response to therapy.[71,76] Functional mutations of JAK3 could also lead to JAK-STAT activation, which promotes tumor cell growth.[77] Mutations and the activation of the JAK-STAT pathway were also detected in EBV-infected NKTCL samples, upregulating RAS/MAPK expression and PI3K/Akt signaling.[3] Cells with increased levels of pSTAT3 secrete a variety of anti-inflammatory factors, including IL-10 and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which prevent dendritic cells from maturing and presenting antigens to T cells to suppress antitumor immune effects. It could also motivate cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) to proliferate and remodel the tumor microenvironment, making chemotherapy and targeted drugs less accessible.[78,79] Moreover, the latent membrane protein 2A (LMP2A) of EBV, expressed in EBV-associated malignancies, promotes IL-10 production to inhibit tumor-killing effects. This process is mediated by PI3K signaling and pSTAT.[80,81] The activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway can promote the expression of the downstream transcription target VEGF, as well as the oncogene c-Met and its ligand hepatocyte growth factor (HGF).[53] This process resulted in vascularization of the tumor microenvironment, increasing tumor aggressiveness, and growth advantages. The HGF/c-Met signal pathway was overexpressed in NKTCL samples, increasing the proliferation capacity and secretion of transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) to suppress local antitumor effects.[82]

PDGF and its receptor α (PDGFRα) are founding members of the receptor tyrosine kinase family, interacting with JAK-STAT and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways. PDGF/PDGFRα signaling plays a real role in tumor growth, invasion, and apoptosis resistance, which was associated with poor prognosis in a variety of tumors.[83] Increased PDGFRα levels were observed in NKTCL, compared with the levels in reactive lymphoid hyperplasia of nasopharynx. High levels of PDGFRα predicted a shorter PFS and poorer response.[84] In addition, PDGF signaling pathway is also involved in tumor microenvironment angiogenesis and the inhibition of antitumor effects, and this part will be discussed in detail later.

LMP-1 promoted the expression of immune checkpoints in the tumor microenvironment of various EBV-positive cancers for immune evasion.[85] Before ICIs were introduced into the therapy, a relationship was observed between the positive expression of PD-L1 and poor clinical outcomes in NKTCL patients treated with asparaginase, suggesting that deficiency of the antitumor effect was involved in the mechanisms of asparaginase resistance.[85,86]

Resistance to immunotherapy

As discussed above, NKTCL is closely associated with EBV infection and LMP-1 could drive PD-L1 expression in the tumor microenvironment to promote immune evasion. PD-L1 was proven to be expressed in a significant percentage of NKTCL tumor cells, providing a promising and attractive therapeutic target.[16,86,87] However, some patients either do not respond to ICIs or relapse after remission. Lim et al[87] reported that patients with no response lacked structural rearrangement of the 3′-UTR of PD-L1 (PD-L1MUT), even when PD-L1 expression was positive. The absence of PD-L1 expression in tumor microenvironment was a critical cause of ICIs resistance. Compared with the expression levels in the NK cell-derived NKTCL subtype, PD-L1 expression was lower in the T cell-derived subtype, indicating that this portion of patients might respond poorly to anti-PD-1 mono-antibodies.[49]

In addition to PD-1 or PD-L1 expression, the presence of a sufficient number of infiltrating T cells in the tumor microenvironment is also crucial. The activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signal pathway has been linked to deficiency of chemotactic recruitment factors in melanoma patients, resulting in poor or no response to ICIs.[88] The upregulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling was also observed in NKTCL, suggesting that this could partly explain resistance to PD-1 antibodies.[32]

Tumor cells acquire resistance to the PD-1/PD-L1 blockade by upregulating CD38 expression. This is triggered by the adenosine receptors signaling pathway, suppressing CD8+ T cells.[89] Wang et al[90] first found that 50% of NKTCL patients were detected to express CD38 strongly, which was correlated with poor prognosis after receiving asparaginase chemotherapy. The upregulation of CD38 in tumor cells resulted in the expression of triphosphate/diphosphate nucleotide hydrolase (CD39) and membrane-5′-nucleotide enzyme (CD73), catalyzing the production of adenosine (ADO) from ATP in the tumor microenvironment. This process inhibited the proliferation and function of CD8+ T cells, and promoted the differentiation and recruitment of CD4+ regulatory T cells.[91,92] Moreover, increased expression of CD38 was also detected on CD4+ regulatory T cells, regulatory B cells, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), leading to suppression of CD8+ T cells.[93]

The expression of DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) and EZH2 can suppress C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL) 9 and CXCL10 expression through epigenetic modifications, blocking CD8+ T cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment.[94,95] The overexpression of EZH2 has already been proven in NKTCL, and DNMT1 can be upregulated by LMP-1 and LMP-2A encoded by EBV.[96,97] The deficiency of T-cell recruitment in the tumor microenvironment ultimately leads to a poor response to ICIs. In the tumor microenvironment, high IL-13 concentrations increase recruitment of regulatory T cells and promote polarization of M2-type macrophages, inhibiting the antitumor immune response.[98] Elevated levels of IL-10, another immunonegatively regulated cytokine, were also observed to be associated with poor prognosis after treatment with PEG-asp. This process is mediated by JAK-STAT and PI3K signaling.[99]

PDGF signaling increases vascular density and blood flow in the tumor microenvironment, promoting tumor growth by supplying more nutrients.[100] The activation of the PDGF signal pathway downregulated the expression of VEGFR and reduced the effect of antitumor therapy of VEGF/VEGFR inhibitors.[101] Besides, PDGFR was overexpressed on cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) involved in the recruitment and activation of CAFs.[102] It was reported that CAFs increase glycolysis and pyruvate secretion in the microenvironment, promoting lymphoma cell survival and inhibiting the antitumor immune response.[103] Still, the role of PDGF/PDGFR pathway which plays in the microenvironment of NKTCL requires further trials and studies.

In addition to PD-1 and PD-L1, the positive expression of other immune checkpoint blockades, such as T cell immunoglobulin-3 (TIM-3) and lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3), has been detected in NKTCL tumor tissues.[104] In lung cancer models, TIM-3 was upregulated after blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway.[105,106] Moreover, immune checkpoint receptors on T cells were co-expressed and subjected to co-regulation.[107] This finding suggested that resistance to PD-1 inhibitors could be caused by the expression or upregulation of other immune checkpoints.

Kim et al[108] analyzed gene expression in the tumor microenvironment in NKTCL, and found that the response to ICIs was associated with the expression of PD-L1, CD68, and FoxP3. They identified four subtypes: immune tolerance, immune evasion A, immune evasion B, and immune silencing. The immune-tolerant type was characterized by a high CD4+ regulatory T-cell count and FoxP3 expression, showing the highest response to treatment. Immune silencing significantly decreased the expression of nearly all immune-related genes and resulted in the poorest prognosis. Macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) was upregulated, and MDSCs recruitment in the tumor microenvironment caused CD8+ T cell exhaustion.[109,110] Tissue necrosis was absent in this subtype, which is a common characteristic of NKTCL, suggesting that necrosis might contribute to antitumor effects. The immune evasion subtype showed high PD-L1 expression and intermediate clinical outcomes.

In addition, a tumor microenvironment with hypoxia, consumed glucose, and abundant lactate significantly weakens the antitumor immune response of infiltrating T cells, resulting in immunosuppression and ICIs resistance.[111,112] However, data from the NKTCL tumor microenvironment are still limited, and related clinical studies are needed to test these hypotheses and conjectures. The neovascularization tissue in the tumor microenvironment is incomplete, exacerbating hypoxia and nutrient deficiency. Tumor-associated fibroblasts produce pyruvate, which is secreted into the microenvironment. Together, these factors inhibit the tumor-killing effect of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. Strategies to overcome drug resistance

The analysis of resistance mechanisms allows the identification of potential therapeutic targets. Targeted therapy effectively blocks the expected signaling pathways and the expression of downstream genes, remodeling the tumor microenvironment. Combination targeted inhibitors and chemotherapy or immunotherapy demonstrate synergistic antitumor effects and a promising response rate in R/R NKTCL patients. Chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of lymphoma treatment, and the application of improved chemotherapy, especially mitoxantrone (MTO) hydrochloride liposomes, is encouraging. Epigenetic therapy is a feasible and tolerable treatment option. Adoptive cellular immunotherapy for EBV is currently under development. Improved chemotherapy

Chemotherapy remains the mainstay of treatment for non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, but the effects of multiple chemotherapeutic drugs are far from satisfactory owing to the expression of MDR genes and P-gp. MTO hydrochloride liposomes have shown encouraging results in the treatment of R/R peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL). The ORR was 40.0% and the CR rate was 13.3% in R/R NKTCL patients treated with MTO hydrochloride liposome monotherapy, overcoming the limitation of NKTCL resistance to doxorubicin.[113] The active component of MTO hydrochloride liposomes is MTO, which forms spherically pegylated liposomes with a particle size of 60 nm in the blood to reduce the loss during transportation and accumulate in tumor tissues. The elevated concentration in the tumor microenvironment saturates the ATP-dependent drug efflux of tumor cells, thereby reversing MDR and P-gp-mediated resistance.[114,115] Treatment combining MTO hydrochloride liposomes and PEG-Asp in R/R NKTCL also presented promising clinical outcomes: 61.3% (19/31) patients achieved CR, and ORR was 87.1% (27/31).[116] Wang et al[117] also reported that in nine R/R NKTCL patients treated with MTO hydrochloride liposomes, dexamethasone, and PEG-Asp with or without PD-1 inhibitors, the best response rate was 33.3% CR and 44.4% partial response (PR). One patient who underwent sequential allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) achieved disease-free survival for more than one year. However, there is still room for improvement. Mei et al[118] reported that liposomes with MTO and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO-1) pathway inhibitors triggered an immunogenic cell death and decreased the number of FoxP3+ Treg cells in various cancer models, exceeding the effects of MTO liposome monotherapy. This would also be an attractive target for the treatment of NKTCL.

Bendamustine is a bifunctional alkylating agent widely used in the treatment of non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma.[119,120] Bendamustine strongly inhibits tumor growth and induces apoptosis of NKTCL cells in vitro. It exhibits synergistic toxicity with gemcitabine and etoposide, which significantly inhibits tumor growth. This suggests combined therapy as a potential option.[121] CD30 is a glycoprotein of the tumor necrosis factor receptors superfamily, expressed in nearly half of the tumor microenvironments in NKTCL, and is associated with regulation of the NF-κB and MAPK pathway.[122,123] Brentuximab vedotin (BV), a CD30 antibody-drug conjugate, demonstrated a promising effect in R/R Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) when combined with bendamustine.[124] Jin et al[125] discovered synergistic effects of BV and bendamustine in inhibiting proliferation and promoting apoptosis for NKTCL samples in vitro. Poon et al[126] reported a case of CR in a patient with refractory NKTCL treated with BV and bendamustine. This evidence indicates that bendamustine is a potential chemotherapy agent in R/R NKTCL.

Targeted therapy

As mentioned above, the expression of MDR genes is a critical factor in chemotherapy resistance to doxorubicin and gemcitabine, with the ABC family playing a crucial role in drug efflux induced by ATP.[23,26] Multiple targeted inhibitors were found to suppress the functions of ABCG2 and ABCB1, reverse efflux, and restore sensitivity to chemotherapy. This suggests that doxorubicin chemotherapy may be a viable option for R/R NKTCL patients when combined with ABC transporter inhibitors, although clinical trials on NKTCL are limited.[127–129]

In addition to targeted inhibitors, cytokines are involved in immune regulation during the progression of resistance. Elevated IL-6 levels are associated with tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis by promoting the JAK-STAT3, MAPK, and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways.[130] A relationship between IL-6 and MDR expression has been observed.[131,132] It was discovered that the upregulation of IL-6 was related to poor prognosis in NKTCL.[133] However, conflicting findings exist. Lower IL-6 levels were associated with a higher Ki67 index in NKTCL tumor cells and increased ABCC4 expression. In vitro, IL-6 resensitizes chemo-resistant NKTCL cells to doxorubicin. This progress might be achieved by downregulating the JAK-STAT and NF-κB pathway.[134] This conclusion requires further confirmation in clinical trials.

Persistent activation of the JAK signaling pathway and EZH2 overexpression have been implicated in resistance and poor prognosis. Tofacitinib, a small-molecule inhibitor targeting JAK3, has been shown to significantly suppress tumor growth in vitro. Histone demethylation was observed in this process, suggesting that the inhibition of the signaling pathway also reversed epigenetic modifications.[54,54] The application of tofacitinib is limited due to its pan-JAK pathway-blocking effects. Sim et al reported that PRN371, a novel agent specifically targeting JAK3, demonstrated more durable antitumor effects in a xenograft model of JAK3-mutated NKTCL.[135] Additionally, JAK1/2 blockade showed antitumor effects and reversed the tumor growth advantages. In a clinical study of R/R PTCL, approximately half of the patients with the activation of the JAK signaling pathway and mutations in STAT3 or pSTAT3 expression experienced clinical benefits after therapy with the JAK1/2 inhibitor ruxolitinib.[136,137]

Downregulation or deletion of the tumor suppressor gene phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) was detected in NKTCL, activating the PI3K/Akt signal pathway and resistance.[138] Compared to the expression levels in normal NK or T cells, NKTCL showed increased PI3K expression, which was associated with the shorter OS and insensitivity to therapy.[139] The PI3K/Akt/mTOR signal pathway is a potential and promising therapeutic target, and the application of PI3K mono-antibodies in B-cell lymphoma was encouraging.[140] Yhim et al[141] reported that combination therapy with gemcitabine and copanlisib, a PI3K α/δ inhibitor, demonstrated clinical benefits in R/R PTCL patients: CR rate was 32.0% and ORR was 72.0%. Among three R/R NKTCL patients, one achieved CR and the others achieved PR. In a clinical trial of R/R PTCL patients receiving linperlisib, a novel PI3Kd-selective inhibitor, 15 of 43 patients (34.8%) achieved CR, and the ORR reached 60.5% (26/43).[142] Inhibitors specified at the downstream target mTOR also demonstrated anti-proliferative effects in mouse xenotransplantation models.[143] The mTOR inhibitor temsirolimus exhibited a synergistic effect with the immunomodulatory agent lenalidomide in blocking the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signal pathway. The combination treatment presented evaluable results for R/R classical Hodgkin’s lymphoma: 80% ORR and 35% CR rate.[144] This indicates that the blockade of the PI3K pathway is a direction worth trying in R/R NKTCL.

The application of PDGFRα-specific inhibitors are not prepared in the treatment of lymphoma. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) compete with PDGFRα in binding ATP, inhibiting downstream signaling pathway transduction. In vivo experiments showed that the imatinib, the first generation of TKI, could delay tumor growth in mouse models.[145] Moreover, blocking the PDGF pathway inhibited the formation of vasculature in the tumor microenvironment, suppressing tumor growth and distant metastasis.[146] This clinical evidence suggests that TKI combined with chemotherapy may be a worthwhile direction for R/R NKTCL.

As discussed above, angiogenesis in the tumor microenvironment is essential for invasion and drug resistance, and the VEGF/VEGFR signaling pathway is involved in this process. R/R NKTCL samples showed more vascular centroid and vascular destructive growth patterns than those associated with effectively treated NKTCL.[51] In a study of 16 advanced-stage NKTCL patients, bevacizumab (the VEGF mono-antibody) combined with PEG-Asp, gemcitabine, oxaliplatin, and dexamethasone achieved a CR rate of 87.5% and 100% ORR, superior to traditional asparaginase-based chemotherapy regimens.[147]

Xiong et al[49] identified three molecular subtypes of NKTCL: TSIM (characterized by alterations in tumor suppressors and immune modulators), HEA (characterized by aberrant histone acetylation), and MB (characterized by MGA mutations and loss of heterozygosity in the bromodomain and extra-terminal-containing protein family). Characteristic alterations in the MB subtype result in the increased expression of C-MYC and the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway, which are associated with increased invasiveness, significantly poor prognosis, and reduced response to asparaginase-based chemotherapy. In an MB-like NKTCL model, homoharringtonine (HHT), an C-MYC inhibitor, displayed a prominent antitumor effect. Prolonged OS was observed with HHT in MB-like NKTCL zebrafish xenograft models. In vitro, HHT presented cytotoxic effects on lymphoma, but almost no application in NKTCL was reported.[148,149]

Immune checkpoints inhibitors

Thus, PD-1/PD-L1 monoantibodies have emerged as attractive targets for the treatment of R/R NKTCL. Five of seven R/R NKTCL patients achieved CR when treated with pembrolizumab, a PD-1 inhibitor, and the remaining two patients reached PR.[86] In the ORIENT-4 clinical trial, the CR rate was 75% (21 out of 28 patients) after sintilimab monotherapy in R/R NKTCL patients.[150] Avelumab, a PD-L1 inhibitor, did not yield results as promising as PD-1 monoclonal antibodies in R/R NKTCL patients, with a CR rate of 23.8% (5 out of 21) and an overall response rate (ORR) of 38.1% (8 out of 21).[151] It was encouraging that ICIs were combined with chemotherapy and other targeted therapy. Cai et al[152] reported a 6/9 CR rate in patients with advanced NKTCL treated with PD-1 antibodies with the pegaspargase, gemcitabine, and oxaliplatin (P-GemOx) regimen and adverse events were tolerable. Preclinical data suggested that gemcitabine and oxaliplatin could enhance the antitumor immune response in the microenvironment, synergistically acting with ICIs.[153,154] The addition of PD-1 monoclonal antibodies significantly improved the CR rate and ORR in R/R classic HL patients compared to the same parameters associated with GemOx alone.[155,156] An in vitro NKTCL trial demonstrated a synergistic effect of the histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi) chidamide and ICIs, enhancing T-cell chemokine expression and IFN-γ response.[157] Yan et al[17] presented a case report in which the disease progressed after PEG-Asp-based chemotherapy with PD-1 blockades and the patient achieved durable CR after receiving sintilimab and chidamide. Zhang et al[158] reported that the CPER regimen (Chidamide + Tislelizumab + Lenalidomide + Etoposide) was effective in R/R NKTCL patients, with CR rate of 47.3% (9/19) and ORR of 94.7% (18/19). The SCENT study of 41 R/R NKTCL patients receiving sintilimab and chidamide demonstrated enduring efficacy and manageable toxicity: the CR rate was 44.4% and ORR was 58.3%; the median follow-up time was 38.3 months and only one patient dropped out for treatment-related adverse events.[159] These conclusions were also confirmed in solid tumors.[160,161] This suggests that the combination of ICIs and chemo or targeted therapy could provide the greatest clinical benefits for R/R NKTCL patients. In addition, the application of multiple ICIs is worthy of clinical practice.

Epigenetic targets

Epigenetic modifications, such as histone deacetylation, were notably enriched in R/R NKTCL. Chidamide, a HDACi, suppresses the Akt/mTOR and MAPK signaling pathways and inhibits the proliferation of NKTCL cells in vitro.[162] A 12-year-old female patient with relapsed NKTCL achieved and maintained CR with HDACi.[163] As mentioned earlier, chidamide has primarily been used in combination with ICIs, with or without chemotherapy and other targeted therapies. Song et al[164] found synergistic effects of chidamide and etoposide in vivo and in vitro, inhibiting proliferation and inducing apoptosis. Compared to the effect of chidamide alone, chidamide with chemotherapy showed a better CR rate (10.0% vs. 6.1%) and ORR (50.0% vs. 15.2%) in R/R NKTCL patients.[165] Considering this clinical evidence, chidamide is a promising treatment option for R/R NKTCL.

Histone methylation also occurs along with histone deacetylation, which decreases the expression of tumor suppressor genes. Histone demethylating agents (azacitidine or decitabine) combined with PD-1 blockade led to a 45.5% (10/22) CR rate and a 59.1% (13/22) ORR in R/R NKTCL. In particular, 22 patients (81.5%) had received immune checkpoint blockade and 23 patients (85.2%) had been treated with HDACi. Among the 22 patients treated with ICIs before treatment, half responded to therapy.[166]

CD38 mono-antibodies

Wang et al[90] found that half of NKTCL cases expressed CD38, which correlated with poor clinical outcomes, suggesting that CD38 could be a target for R/R NKTCL. Hari et al[167] reported that a 56-year-old woman with NKTCL failed to respond to asparaginase chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and allogeneic HSCT, but achieved CR with daratumumab (CD38 mono-antibody) monotherapy. In a phase II clinical study involving 32 R/R NKTCL patients treated with daratumumab, the ORR was 25%, and the median OS was 141 days.[168] However, the response to daratumumab was not correlated with CD38 expression. However, this hypothesis remains controversial. The upregulation of CD55 and CD59 was observed in non-responders, whereas CD38 expression did not differ. Furthermore, a high ratio of CD38 to complement inhibitory proteins was found to be related to sensitivity to daratumumab.[169] In another study of cemiplimab (PD1 inhibitor) and isatuximab (monoclonal anti-CD38 antibody) in 37 R/R NKTCL patients, the results were encouraging: 43.2% (16/37) of patients achieved CR, and the ORR was up to 64.8% (24/37). The early stage of the disease and PD-L1 expression were also associated with a higher response rate.[170] This clinical evidence is encouraging, and compared with chemotherapy and other targeted therapies, the CD38 monoclonal antibody is a feasible method that should not be overlooked.

CD30 mono-antibodies

CD30 was expressed in partial NKTCL patients, which was associated with mutations in STAT,[171] but there was no clear conclusion regarding CD30 and prognosis.[123,172,173] Kim et al[174] reported that a 65-year-old woman with refractory NKTCL achieved CR when treated with BV only.[175] As mentioned previously, BV combined with bendamustine also showed efficacy. In a phase II clinical study of seven R/R NKTCL patients receiving BV therapy, one CR and one PR were achieved. Therefore, CD30 monoclonal antibodies are a viable option for CD30-positive R/R NKTCL patients.

EBV targeted therapy

Latency II pattern-associated EBV antigens, including LMP1, LMP2A, LMP2B, and EBNA1, were detected in EBV-infected T or NK cells, which are involved in the activation of a variety of downstream signaling pathways and resistance.[4] However, the expression of EBV latent membrane proteins also provided feasible targets for immunotherapy. LMP2-specific CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes recognized and eliminated EBV-positive T or NK cell malignancy, though the absence of LMP2A proteins.[176] LMP2A was proven to be an ideal target for immunotherapy, and LMP2A-specific T cells presented antitumor effects by lysing LMP2A-bearing cells efficiently.[177] Kalra et al[178] found that BamH1-A right frame 1 (BARF1), an EBV antigen, was detected in 65% of EBV-positive tumor patients, and targeted BARF1-specific T cells demonstrated antitumor effects. It was reported that a patient with primary refractory EBV-associated NKTCL achieved durable CR after receiving autologous LMP-targeting cytotoxic T cells.[179] Among 11 R/R NKTCL patients treated with autologous T cells targeted EBV antigens, three of the six patients with evaluable lesions achieved remission.[180] Kim et al[18] reported 30% CR and 50% ORR in 10 R/R NKTCL patients receiving autologous EBV-specific T-cell transfusion. Additionally, in a clinical trial involving patients with EBV-positive lymphoma after allogeneic HSCT, 2 R/R NKTCL patients achieved and sustained CR after treatment with allogeneic donor-derived LMP1 and LMP2-specific T cells.[181] For R/R NKTCL patients with persistent EBV infection, adoptive immunotherapy is an option to consider.

Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy is recommended as the first-line treatment for the early stages of NKTCL and leads to better clinical outcomes and survival.[182,183] However, most cases of disease progression or relapse are systemic, and limited involvement only accounts for a small proportion. This restricts the application of radiotherapy. However, NKTCL is highly sensitive to radiotherapy. Therefore, radiotherapy is recommended for R/R NKTCL patients who have not previously received radiotherapy and relapse after a long remission period. Even in patients with systemic involvement, radiotherapy is an option when local lesions are poorly controlled.

HSCT

HSCT is applied as consolidation therapy after remission in high-risk or advanced lymphoma patients or as salvage therapy for patients who have failed or relapsed after first-line treatment. In NKTCL patients who do not achieve a CR, autologous HSCT does not significantly prolong survival.[184] In contrast, allogeneic HSCT (allo-HSCT) may be a viable option. Li et al[185] reported that two R/R NKTCL cases achieved durable CR after receiving allo-HSCT. In a retrospective study of 135 NKTCL patients receiving allo-HSCT, the 3-year PFS rate and OS rate were 48.6% and 55.6%, respectively. In the subgroup of patients who did not achieve remission, the probability of 3-year PFS and OS was significantly reduced (37.0% vs. 52.1%, 38.8% vs. 60.6%). But that does not deny it is a proven salvage treatment. It is worth mentioning that the use of ICIs did not increase the probability of graft-vs.-host disease, and no significant association with survival was observed.[186] High EBV DNA levels and tumor residual burden before transplantation were associated with relapse after transplantation.[187] Due to the high risk of treatment-related death and treatment tolerance, allo-HSCT may not be the first therapeutic option for R/R NKTCL patients where other approaches are available. However, this does not negate the fact that allo-HSCT can be an effective treatment for R/R NKTCL patients, even if only PR is achieved before transplantation. However, clinical data on this subject are limited.

Chimeric antigen receptor T cell therapy

Chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy, a novel adoptive cell immunotherapy, is widely applied in the treatment of R/R hematological malignancies and has brought about revolutionary changes. The EBV envelope glycoprotein 350 (gp350) is positively expressed during EBV lytic reactivation. Slabik et al[188] reported that autologous gp350-targeted specific CD8+ T cells demonstrated inhibitory effects on EBV-infected B cell malignant lymphoma in a mouse model. Antitumor effects of gp350-targeted CAR-T cells against gp350-positive lymphoma have also been observed in vitro.[189] In EBV-associated Burkitt’s lymphoma cell lines, gp350 CAR-T cell therapy displayed cytotoxic effects in vitro. However, gp350 CAR-T cells did not suppress tumor cell growth in vivo in mouse xenograft models. EBV DNA levels decreased and the proportion of exhaustion CD8+ gp350 CAR-T increased, suggesting that antigen loss and immune escape might be involved in resistance.[190] Additionally, targeted LMP-1 CAR-T cells presented cytolytic effects on nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells overexpressing LMP-1, and increased release of IFN-γ and IL-2 was observed.[191] LMP-1 CAR-T cells also showed specific cytotoxic effects on LMP-1-positive tumor cell lines infected with EBV in vitro.[192] Apart from EBV-associated antigen CAR-T cells, CD30-specific CAR-T is also under exploration. In a clinical study of nine R/R cHL patients with anaplastic large-cell lymphoma (ALCL) receiving anti-CD30 CAR-T cell monotherapy, seven patients achieved CR, and three of them achieved a durable CR for more than 2 years.[193] Sang et al[194] reported that among 12 patients with R/R CD30-positive lymphoma receiving CD30 CAR-T cells and PD-1 blockade, six patients achieved CR, and the ORR was 91.7% (11/12). Unfortunately, data and studies on CAR-T cell therapy for NKTCL are limited. Nevertheless, this approach is desirable and promising.

Conclusions

Owing to the expression of MDR genes and P-gp, previous CHOP and CHOP-like chemotherapy regimens were far from satisfactory. Application of asparaginase significantly improves prognosis, but for patients who experience first-line therapy failure or relapse with remission for less than one year, long-term survival is dismal. Persistent activation of the Epstein–Barr virus, hypermethylation, and deacetylation, as well as disruption of signaling pathways, are partly responsible for therapy resistance and relapse. The emergence of targeted treatments, especially ICIs and histone deacetylation inhibitors, overcame partial resistance, and remission was achieved. It is essential to perform biopsies to identify therapeutic targets. However, this is still not enough to solve all issues, and more genomic, metabolomic, and proteomic analyses of lymphoma are needed to reveal abnormalities in gene expression and the regulation of signaling pathways during resistance, and to identify potential therapeutic targets. Monoclonal antibodies targeting surface antigens, CAR-T cells, and chimeric antigen receptor NK cells have shown encouraging results in R/R lymphomas. However, maintaining long-term remission is an issue in CAR T-cell therapy. Nevertheless, immunotherapy is still a promising direction for future studies.
