Comorbidity burden outweighs coronary heart disease diagnosis in impairing cardiopulmonary fitness: a cross-sectional study based on cardiopulmonary exercise testing
Authors: Lixian Zheng, Jinhong Xu, Nibing Zheng, Bin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultimorbidity was highly prevalent in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD), yet its independent and cumulative impact on cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) remains incompletely elucidated. This cross-sectional study analyzed the respective effects of comorbidity burden and CHD diagnosis on peak oxygen uptake (VO₂peak) and examined whether obesity modulated the comorbidity–CRF relationship.MethodsA total of 335 adults undergoing symptom-limited cardiopulmonary exercise testing at Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital were enrolled (CHD group: n = 156; non-CHD group: n = 179). Multiple linear regression models assessed the independent and interactive effects of CHD diagnosis, comorbidity count, and body mass index (BMI) on VO₂peak.ResultsDespite older age (63 vs. 53 years, P < 0.001) and a higher comorbidity burden (P < 0.001) in the CHD group, VO₂peak did not differ significantly between the two groups (20.33 vs. 20.88 mL/kg/min, P = 0.234). CHD diagnosis was not a statistically significant predictor of VO₂peak (β=−0.415, P = 0.386), whereas comorbidity count exhibited a dose–response relationship (β=−0.824, P < 0.001), with each additional comorbidity reducing VO₂peak by 0.82 mL/kg/min. As comorbidities increased from 0 to ≥ 3, VO₂peak declined from 21.19 to 19.58 mL/kg/min (P for trend = 0.004), accompanied by parallel deteriorations in oxygen uptake at the anaerobic threshold, oxygen uptake efficiency slope, and ventilatory efficiency. Although the linear interaction term between BMI and comorbidity was non-significant (P = 0.469), stratified analyses confirmed consistent negative trends across all groups, with the overweight group (BMI 24–28 kg/m²) exhibiting a numerically steeper downward trajectory.ConclusionsComorbidity burden outweighed CHD diagnosis per se as the primary driver of CRF impairment, with cumulative effects exhibiting a dose–response relationship. Our findings suggest that the detrimental impact of multimorbidity is robust across different weight statuses. Although a steeper decline was visually observed in the overweight group, further large-scale studies are needed to verify whether specific BMI ranges confer heightened sensitivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05828-3.Keywords: Coronary heart disease, Multimorbidity, Cardiorespiratory fitness, Peak oxygen uptake, Cardiopulmonary exercise testing, Obesity

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remained the leading cause of death globally, with coronary heart disease (CHD) accounting for approximately 9 million deaths annually [1]. Peak oxygen uptake (VO₂peak), the core indicator of cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), objectively measured by cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET), is a powerful independent predictor of both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [2–4]. A recent meta-analysis encompassing 199 cohort studies with more than 20 million observations confirmed that each 1-MET (3.5 mL/kg/min) increase in VO₂peak was associated with a 14% reduction in all-cause mortality risk and a 16% reduction in cardiovascular mortality risk, with this association remaining highly consistent across different age groups, sexes, and ethnic populations [2, 3]. Importantly, the prognostic value of CRF was independent of, and even exceeded, that of traditional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and smoking [4, 5].

However, contemporary patients with CVD presented substantial clinical complexity: the prevalence of multimorbidity in the CHD population reached 65–85% [6, 7], frequently involving multiple organ systems, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and dyslipidemia. Multimorbidity may have synergistically compromised CRF through mechanisms including chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired skeletal muscle mitochondrial function [8, 9].

Despite widespread recognition of the prognostic importance of multimorbidity, its independent and cumulative impact on objectively measured exercise capacity remains unclear. Previous studies predominantly focused on associations between individual diseases and exercise capacity [10] or relied on estimated VO₂peak rather than gold-standard CPET measurements [11, 12]. More critically, the independent effect of CHD diagnosis itself on exercise capacity after systematic adjustment for multimorbidity remains unestablished. Clarifying this issue was crucial for understanding the primary drivers of exercise capacity decline in patients with CVD, specifically whether functional impairment stems primarily from pathophysiological changes inherent to CHD itself or from the cumulative burden of accompanying multimorbidities.

Obesity presents another important challenge in the cardiovascular population, further complicating assessment. Although obesity (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m² by Chinese criteria and ≥ 30 kg/m² by WHO criteria) was an established risk factor for CVD development [13], the “obesity paradox”—whereby overweight or mildly obese patients with CVD may exhibit better survival—suggests a more complex role in populations with established disease [14, 15]. However, whether obesity modulates the impact of multimorbidity on exercise capacity—that is, whether the cumulative effects of comorbidities differed across body weight categories—remains unknown.

While the detrimental impact of individual risk factors on CRF is well-recognized, the relative contribution of the CHD diagnosis itself versus the cumulative burden of multimorbidity remains under-quantified. In clinical practice, functional decline in CHD patients is often attributed primarily to the cardiac condition, potentially overlooking the compounding effect of systemic comorbidities. Therefore, the present study aims to disentangle these factors by statistically isolating the independent effect of the CHD diagnosis from the cumulative comorbidity burden. Furthermore, we uniquely explore the moderating role of obesity, investigating whether excess adiposity amplifies the relationship between multimorbidity and reduced exercise capacity. By providing empirical evidence that multimorbidity burden actually outweighs the CHD diagnosis per se in impairing VO₂peak, this study seeks to support and refine the growing clinical imperative to shift from single-disease labels to a holistic, multimorbidity-centered assessment strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center, cross-sectional study was conducted at Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital from January to December 2024. We consecutively enrolled adult inpatients who underwent symptom-limited CPET for cardiovascular function assessment or cardiac rehabilitation guidance. Participants were categorized into a CHD group and a non-CHD group based on their clinical diagnoses and angiographic evidence (detailed criteria are provided below). The non-CHD group consisted of patients referred for CPET due to suspected cardiovascular symptoms or risk assessment, but who had no evidence of obstructive coronary artery disease. Using this high-risk clinical referral population as a reference group allows us to isolate the specific impact of an obstructive CHD diagnosis from the general burden of background cardiovascular risk factors. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital (Approval No.: K2022-09-091), and all procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Inclusion criteria

(1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) completion of symptom-limited CPET with sufficient effort, defined as a RER ≥ 1.10, or an RER of 1.05–1.09 with ≥ 85% of the age-predicted maximum heart rate, or a Borg rating of perceived exertion (RPE) ≥ 17; and (3) availability of complete clinical and anthropometric data.

Exclusion criteria

(1) acute coronary syndrome or coronary revascularization within the previous four weeks; (2) acute decompensated heart failure; (3) severe valvular disease with significant hemodynamic abnormalities; (4) uncontrolled resting hypertension (≥ 180/110 mmHg); (5) severe musculoskeletal or neurological diseases that adversely affected exercise performance; (6) other absolute contraindications to CPET as defined by the American Heart Association guidelines [16]; and (7) inability to cooperate with CPET or to provide informed consent.

Criteria for data exclusion

To ensure the physiological validity of the analysis, specific CPET sessions were excluded post-hoc if: (1) the test was terminated voluntarily due to non-cardiopulmonary factors (e.g., orthopedic limitation, lack of motivation) without achieving maximal effort criteria (respiratory exchange ratio [RER] < 1.05); or (2) the test was aborted prematurely due to catastrophic events (e.g., sustained ventricular tachycardia, hemodynamic collapse, or syncope) that prevented the collection of stable gas exchange data. Crucially, participants who terminated exercise due to limiting cardiac symptoms (e.g., angina, dyspnea) or significant ischemic ST-segment depression were considered to have reached a valid symptom-limited functional endpoint and were included in the final analysis.

Data collection and variable definition

Data collection included demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, medical history, medication use, laboratory test results, and echocardiographic findings. Laboratory indicators were obtained according to standard procedures in the hospital’s central laboratory. Standard transthoracic echocardiography was performed in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines to assess key parameters, including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), and left atrial diameter (LAD).

CHD was defined as meeting at least one of the following criteria [17]: (1) coronary angiography or coronary computed tomography showing ≥ 50% stenosis in a major coronary artery; (2) a history of myocardial infarction confirmed by electrocardiographic or biomarker evidence; (3) a history of coronary revascularization, including percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG); and (4) use of antianginal medications with objective evidence of myocardial ischemia. Participants meeting none of these criteria were classified into the non-CHD group.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as body mass (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Participants were stratified into normal/underweight ( BMI < 24.0 kg/m²), overweight (BMI 24.0–28.0 kg/m²), and obesity (BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m²) according to specific criteria for Chinese adults [18]. These lower cutoffs were adopted because East Asian populations exhibit higher visceral adiposity and metabolic risk at a given BMI compared to Caucasians.

Comorbidities were ascertained based on International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, physician diagnoses recorded in electronic health records (EHR), and medication usage. Detailed diagnostic criteria and ICD-10 codes for each condition are provided in Additional file 1: Table S1.

The comorbidity burden was calculated as the sum of nine chronic conditions: hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, CKD, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and atrial fibrillation. Notably, heart failure and atrial fibrillation were classified as comorbidities rather than components of the CHD diagnosis to reflect the cumulative functional burden, as these conditions are not exclusive to CHD and can arise from non-ischemic etiologies.

Apart from these two conditions, CHD and obesity were also excluded from this comorbidity count. CHD served as the index condition (exposure variable), while obesity was analyzed as an independent moderator due to its distinct biomechanical and metabolic effects. Consequently, the comorbidity count ranged from 0 to 9. In statistical analyses, the comorbidity count was treated both as a continuous variable (to assess dose–response relationships) and as a categorical variable (0, 1, 2, or ≥ 3) to examine potential nonlinearity.

Cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET)

All participants provided written informed consent. Symptom-limited maximal exercise testing was performed on an electromagnetically braked cycle ergometer (Ergoline 100, Germany). Flow sensors and gas analyzers were calibrated with standard gas mixtures and volume syringes before each test to ensure accuracy.

Protocol

The study employed an individualized Ramp Protocol. The test consisted of four phases: (1) a resting phase (3 min) for baseline data collection; (2) a warm-up phase (3 min) of unloaded cycling (0 W); (3) an incremental exercise phase targeting a total duration of 8–12 min; and (4) a recovery phase. The work rate increment (ramp rate) was individualized (ranging from 10 to 25 W/min) based on the participant’s functional capacity to limit the exercise duration to the ideal physiological window.

Gas analysis and processing

Ventilation (VE), oxygen uptake (VO₂), and carbon dioxide production (VCO₂) were monitored continuously using a breath-by-breath gas analysis system (MasterScreen CPX, Jaeger, Germany). Breath-by-breath data were processed to reduce noise, and VO₂peak was strictly defined as the average VO₂ value during the final 30 s of exercise.

Test Termination Criteria

The exercise test was symptom-limited. The test was terminated when participants achieved volitional exhaustion (Borg rating of perceived exertion ≥ 17) or met specific safety termination criteria in accordance with AHA guidelines. These safety indications included: (1) typical angina; (2) significant electrocardiographic changes (e.g., ST-segment depression > 2 mm or serious arrhythmias); (3) hemodynamic instability (e.g., a drop in systolic blood pressure > 10 mmHg); (4) severe dyspnea; or (5) the participant’s request to stop.

The primary CPET parameters included VO₂peak, anaerobic threshold (AT) determined using the V-slope method combined with ventilatory equivalents, the ventilatory efficiency slope (VE/VCO₂-slope), oxygen pulse, oxygen uptake efficiency slope (OUES), and respiratory exchange ratio (RER = VCO₂/VO₂). All data were independently reviewed by two physicians, and inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which yielded a value > 0.90.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python software (version 3.x) with the statsmodels and scipy libraries. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and the significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Post-hoc power analysis indicated that the sample of 335 participants provides > 0.95 statistical power for multiple linear regression with our main predictors (CHD diagnosis, comorbidity count, BMI, age, sex).

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted for the 335 participants included in the study. Based on the central limit theorem for large samples, continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), regardless of distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences in baseline characteristics and CRF indicators between the CHD group and the non-CHD group were compared using independent-samples t tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Regression analysis and interaction

Multiple linear regression models were constructed to quantify the independent associations between CHD diagnosis, comorbidity count, and VO₂peak. For the main-effect analyses, comorbidity count was entered into the models as either a continuous or an ordinal variable, with adjustment for potential confounders such as age and sex. Unstandardized regression coefficients and their 95% confidence intervals were reported. To examine whether obesity moderated the association between comorbidity burden and CRF, an interaction term between comorbidity count and BMI (comorbidity count × BMI) was included in the regression models.

Group comparisons and trend analysis

To further evaluate the cumulative effect of comorbidity burden, participants were categorized into four groups according to the number of comorbidities (0, 1, 2, or ≥ 3). Differences in CPET parameters (VO₂peak, VE/VCO₂-slope, OUES, and AT) among these four groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant main effects were observed, post-hoc pairwise comparisons (e.g., Tukey’s HSD test) were performed to identify specific between-group differences. Furthermore, linear trend tests were conducted to assess whether these cardiopulmonary fitness indicators demonstrated a significant linear deterioration with increasing comorbidity count.

Results

Baseline characteristics and cardiopulmonary fitness

Among the 385 patients screened, 50 were excluded due to missing clinical data (n = 18), failure to achieve sufficient physiological effort (n = 22), or other exclusion criteria (n = 10), resulting in a final sample of 335 participants for analysis. Based on the grouping criteria described above, 156 (46.6%) participants were classified into the CHD group and 179 (53.4%) into the non-CHD group.

Although free of obstructive coronary disease, the non-CHD group represented a high-risk clinical phenotype with a substantial prevalence of dyslipidemia (72.6%), hypertension (56.4%), and multimorbidity (median count of 2.0), which likely contributes to the relatively low VO₂peak observed in this cohort.

In comparison, participants in the CHD group were significantly older (median 63 vs. 53 years, P < 0.001), had a higher proportion of men, a greater prevalence of smoking history, and a higher comorbidity burden (median 2.0 [IQR 2.0–3.0] vs. 2.0 [IQR 1.0–2.0], P < 0.001). Hypertension, diabetes, and heart failure were more prevalent in the CHD group. Despite these differences in clinical profile, VO₂peak did not differ significantly between the two groups (20.33 ± 4.08 vs. 20.88 ± 4.39 mL/kg/min, P = 0.234), nor were there significant differences in AT or peak oxygen pulse. However, the CHD group exhibited poorer ventilatory efficiency and oxygen uptake efficiency, as indicated by a higher VE/VCO₂-slope (30.41 ± 3.83 vs. 28.70 ± 3.64, P < 0.001) and a lower OUES (1632.84 ± 398.31 vs. 1804.15 ± 618.10, P = 0.004; Table 1).

To further elucidate the impact of varying levels of multimorbidity on CRF, participants were stratified into four groups according to comorbidity count (Fig. 1; detailed statistical comparisons are provided in Additional file 2: Table S2). Significant differences among groups were observed for most parameters. Specifically, as the number of comorbidities increased from 0 to ≥ 3, VO₂peak declined significantly from 21.19 ± 3.40 to 19.58 ± 4.41 mL/kg/min (PANOVA = 0.021, P for trend = 0.004, Fig. 1A), and AT decreased from 12.77 ± 1.75 to 11.72 ± 2.21 mL/kg/min (PANOVA = 0.035, P for trend = 0.004, Fig. 1B). These multidimensional findings collectively demonstrate a significant dose–response relationship between comorbidity burden and CRF.

Discussion

The primary finding of this study is that the cumulative burden of comorbidities outweighs the diagnosis of CHD itself as the fundamental driver of functional impairment in patients referred for cardiovascular assessment. To rigorously test this, we deliberately compared CHD patients with a high-risk non-CHD reference group. Contrary to the traditional diagnosis-centric perspective—which often attributes exercise intolerance primarily to the pathophysiology of CHD—our analysis revealed that VO₂peak did not differ significantly between patients with and without CHD (20.33 vs. 20.88 mL/kg/min, P = 0.234). This lack of difference is particularly notable given the substantial age disparity between groups (mean 63 vs. 53 years). Since functional capacity naturally declines with age, the older CHD group would physiologically be expected to exhibit lower values. The observed similarity in VO₂peak thus suggests that crude comparisons are confounded by this demographic imbalance, potentially masking the extent of functional impairment in the younger non-CHD group. Indeed, after adjusting for age and sex, regression models confirmed that CHD diagnosis held no independent predictive value. Instead, comorbidity count exhibited a robust, inverse dose–response relationship, reinforcing the conclusion that multimorbidity burden outweighs the specific effects of the primary cardiac diagnosis.

This conclusion aligns with and extends recent high-quality evidence. For instance, Martens et al. reported a progressive decline in VO₂peak of 4.5 mL/kg/min as non-cardiac comorbidities accumulated in heart failure patients [19]. Similarly, Zheng et al. demonstrated that in a large cohort of CHD patients, the risk of adverse outcomes was driven sixfold higher by multimorbidity clustering rather than by the coronary disease status alone [20]. By quantifying these relative contributions within a single model, our study confirms that the “penalizing effect” of multimorbidity is a universal phenomenon that transcends individual diagnostic categories, highlighting the need for a more holistic assessment of functional reserve in cardiac patients.

The pathophysiological basis for these findings lies in the distinction between CHD as a single disease entity and the systemic impact of multiple comorbidities. CHD primarily impairs central oxygen transport due to reduced cardiac pump function and insufficient coronary perfusion. In contrast, multiple comorbidities exert systemic effects across the entire oxygen transport chain—from pulmonary oxygen uptake and blood delivery to mitochondrial utilization in skeletal muscles—through multidimensional, multi-target mechanisms [8, 9, 21]. For example, COPD impairs pulmonary gas exchange and ventilatory capacity; diabetes causes mitochondrial dysfunction and reduces peripheral oxygen utilization in skeletal muscle [22]; and heart failure limits cardiac output and central oxygen transport [23]. Furthermore, the cumulative release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), in the context of multimorbidity creates an “inflammatory spillover effect,” inducing a systemic low-grade inflammatory state, promoting cellular senescence, and accelerating muscle wasting [9, 24]. These mechanisms lead to a synchronous decline across all components of the oxygen transport chain, exceeding the additive effects of individual diseases.

Our data confirmed a consistent, inverse dose–response relationship across multiple dimensions of cardiopulmonary function. As comorbidity burden increased, we observed synchronous declines in VO₂peak, AT, and OUES, alongside a progressive worsening of VE/VCO₂-slope (all P for trend < 0.05). Importantly, these associations were consistent regardless of CHD status, reinforcing that multimorbidity exerts a universal penalizing effect across diagnostic categories.

The concurrent deterioration of these multidimensional indicators reflects the multi-system damage to the oxygen transport system caused by comorbidities: the decline in VO₂peak indicates reduced overall aerobic capacity; the decrease in AT reflects an earlier shift from skeletal muscle aerobic metabolism to anaerobic glycolysis; the reduction in OUES demonstrates worsening oxygen utilization efficiency; and the increase in VE/VCO₂ indicates ventilation–perfusion mismatch and increased dead space ventilation. Clinically, each additional comorbidity was associated with a 0.82 mL/kg/min decrease in VO₂peak—a meaningful effect, given that a 1-MET (3.5 mL/kg/min) reduction in VO₂peak corresponds to a 14% increase in all-cause mortality risk and a 16% increase in cardiovascular mortality risk.

Regarding the role of body mass, our study found that BMI exerts a complex influence on CRF. We acknowledge that relative VO₂peak (mL/kg/min) is inherently influenced by body mass; however, it remains the most clinically relevant predictor of prognosis and functional capacity for weight-bearing activities. While BMI showed a significant independent negative association with VO₂peak (β = −0.165, P = 0.007), the formal interaction analysis between BMI and comorbidity count was not statistically significant (P = 0.469). However, stratified analyses based on Chinese BMI categories revealed a noteworthy visual trend: the overweight group (BMI 24.0–28.0 kg/m²) exhibited a numerically steeper decline in CRF compared to the normal-weight and obese groups.

These findings offer a new perspective on the “obesity paradox” which posits that overweight and mildly obese patients with certain cardiovascular conditions may have a survival advantage. This protective effect, however, is highly dependent on preserved CRF and disappears in severe obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m²) [15]. Our results suggest that the overweight phase may mark a critical transition from “paradoxical protection” to “paradoxical failure”: during this period, the accumulation of comorbidities leads to a rapid decline in fitness, surpassing the metabolic reserve benefits of mild obesity, such as greater fat-free mass and neurohormonal buffering capacity. This mechanistic hypothesis is supported by pathophysiological evidence: in the overweight phase, adipose tissue remodeling reaches a critical threshold—adipocyte hypertrophy induces local hypoxia, macrophage infiltration, and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators—which, combined with comorbidity-related metabolic disturbances, may trigger an “accelerated decline” in CRF [25].

This study underscores that multimorbidity burden is a critical determinant of functional capacity, often outweighing the impact of stable coronary disease itself. After adjustment for comorbidity burden, CHD diagnosis had no independent predictive value for VO₂peak (P = 0.386), whereas each additional comorbidity was independently associated with a 0.82 mL/kg/min decrease in VO₂peak (P < 0.001). These findings reinforce that comorbidity profiling should be integral to comprehensive risk assessment and long-term clinical management. Rather than relying solely on the severity of coronary anatomy, clinical decision-making for stable patients implies a need for holistic management that prioritizes the optimization of multiple chronic conditions.

Moreover, the visually steeper decline observed in the overweight group (BMI 24.0–28.0 kg/m²) highlights a potential window of heightened vulnerability. Rather than focusing solely on weight reduction, which might exacerbate muscle wasting in the context of multimorbidity, comprehensive management for these patients should prioritize body composition optimization—specifically the preservation of lean muscle mass—alongside rigorous comorbidity control. These findings advocate for the expanded utility of CPET beyond diagnostic labeling, establishing it as a vital instrument for monitoring systemic physiological reserve and guiding holistic therapeutic strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, regarding generalizability, the single-center nature and the recruitment of symptomatic patients (referral bias)—evidenced by relatively low Peak VO₂ values—may limit the extrapolation of our findings to healthier, asymptomatic populations. In this context, the use of Chinese-specific BMI cutoffs also limits direct comparability with studies using WHO international standards, although the observed physiological trends are likely applicable across populations. Furthermore, the significant age difference between the Non-CHD and CHD groups (53 vs. 63 years) remains a potential confounder influencing crude comparisons of CRF, although we strictly adjusted for age in our multivariable models. Third, limitations exist regarding variable precision: obesity was defined solely by BMI, which does not capture body composition nuances, and comorbidity burden was assessed by a simple count without accounting for disease severity. Relatedly, regarding the non-significant association between CHD diagnosis and VO₂peak (P = 0.386), we cannot strictly rule out the existence of a minor independent effect; the lack of statistical significance may reflect limited statistical power to detect smaller effect sizes compared to the robust impact of multimorbidity. Finally, the absence of biological measurements, such as inflammatory markers, restricts mechanistic insights.

Future research should employ multi-center longitudinal cohort designs integrating weighted comorbidity indices, detailed body composition analyses, more granular characterization of CHD severity (e.g., anatomical complexity or ischemic burden), and biomarker assessments to validate these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms more comprehensively.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that comorbidity burden outweighs the diagnostic label of CHD as the primary determinant of declines in CRF. We observed a significant dose–response relationship between comorbidity count and VO₂peak, with each additional comorbidity resulting in a meaningful reduction in VO₂peak. These findings highlight the profound impact of multimorbidity on cardiorespiratory health and underscore that comorbidity burden is an integral component of cardiorespiratory assessment.

Moreover, our results suggest that the adverse impact of comorbidities on CRF is pervasive across all BMI categories. Although the interaction analysis did not reach statistical significance, visual trends indicate that the overweight phase may represent a potentially sensitive window where this impact is accentuated. Therefore, early intervention and comprehensive management remain essential for patients with multiple chronic conditions, particularly those who are overweight, to mitigate the compounding effects on cardiorespiratory function.
