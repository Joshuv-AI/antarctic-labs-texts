Lifestyle Medicine for Glaucoma
Authors: Tanuj Dada, Shibal Bhartiya
Published: 2026

Glaucoma is a progressive sight-threatening disease with a well-established line of management being control of IOP by medical, surgical and laser modalities.1–3 These modalities have documented side effects and challenges, which opens an avenue of research towards additional therapies.4–6 Lifestyle medicine is an evidence based medical speciality that uses lifestyle interventions for treatment and prevention of chronic diseases like glaucomatous neurodegeneration. This can be used as a primary treatment modality in ocular hypertension and early glaucoma and as adjunctive therapy in moderate to advanced glaucoma where it can reduce the need for medications/surgery and slow disease progression.7 Lifestyle medicine can impact several modifiable risk factors including IOP, optic nerve and occipital blood flow, neuro-inflammation, oxidative stress, allostatic load and age-related neurodegeneration through its action on the psycho-neuro-endocrine-immunology of the patient.8–19

The key lifestyle interventions include nitrate-rich diets, moderate aerobic exercise, stress management through breathing techniques and mindfulness meditation, optimized sleep, cessation of smoking, and limiting caffeine and alcohol use.

Why Neurodegeneration Matters in Glaucoma Now?

Glaucoma has traditionally been conceptualized as a pressure-mediated optic neuropathy, with intraocular pressure (IOP) reduction forming the cornerstone of management. This approach has undeniably reduced the burden of irreversible vision loss worldwide. However, it does not fully account for the heterogeneity observed in clinical practice. Disease progression despite well-controlled IOP, the presence of normal-tension glaucoma, and wide interindividual variability in susceptibility suggest that additional biological mechanisms influence disease behavior.8–23

Over the past two decades, glaucoma has increasingly been recognized as a chronic, age-related neurodegenerative disorder sharing mechanistic features with central nervous system (CNS) neurodegeneration.24–27 These include mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, impaired axonal transport, and trans-synaptic degeneration along the visual pathway.28–30 This evolving paradigm does not displace the central role of IOP lowering, but rather situates pressure within a broader biological context that may explain why similar IOP levels produce divergent clinical outcomes.20–22

Age remains the most significant non-modifiable risk factor for glaucoma. As global life expectancy increases, understanding how aging biology intersects with optic nerve vulnerability becomes increasingly relevant. The optic nerve, composed of retinal ganglion cell (RGC) axons with high metabolic demand and limited regenerative capacity, is particularly susceptible to age-related cellular stress.

Glaucoma, Aging Biology, and Chronic Inflammation

Aging is characterized by a gradual loss of cellular homeostasis, reduced resilience to stress, and accumulation of molecular damage. One of the most consistently described features of aging biology is a state of chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation, commonly referred to as “inflammaging.” This phenomenon has been extensively studied in neurodegenerative diseases, where persistent immune activation contributes to neuronal dysfunction and progressive cell loss.21,22,31–35

In glaucoma, converging evidence supports a role for neuroinflammation at both retinal and central levels. Experimental models and human studies have demonstrated microglial activation, complement pathway dysregulation, and altered cytokine signaling within the retina and optic nerve head. Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), with their long axons and high energy requirements, are particularly vulnerable to age-related mitochondrial decline. Impaired mitochondrial function results in reduced adenosine triphosphate production, increased oxidative stress, and disrupted calcium homeostasis, factors that lower the threshold for neurodegeneration even in the absence of elevated IOP.

These age-related processes are not confined to the eye but reflect systemic biological changes that shape neural vulnerability more broadly. From this perspective, glaucoma represents not only localized ocular pathology but also a manifestation of broader aging-associated neurodegenerative susceptibility.19–22,31,32,36

Alzheimer's Disease, Aging, and Shared Neurodegenerative Pathways with Glaucoma

Alzheimer's disease (AD) and glaucoma share several converging biological mechanisms that extend beyond superficial epidemiologic associations. Both are age-related neurodegenerative conditions characterized by progressive neuronal loss, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, impaired axonal transport, and chronic neuroinflammation.

In AD, accumulation of amyloid-beta and tau pathology is accompanied by synaptic dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, and microglial activation, leading to progressive neuronal vulnerability. While glaucoma does not share identical proteinopathies, RGC degeneration occurs within a similarly hostile biological environment marked by metabolic stress, impaired energy homeostasis, and inflammatory signaling. Vascular dysregulation and reduced perfusion reserve, recognized contributors in both AD and certain glaucoma phenotypes, further reinforce this overlap.

Importantly, aging research in AD has highlighted the long preclinical phase during which neurodegenerative processes evolve silently before clinical manifestations become evident. A similar paradigm is increasingly recognized in glaucoma, where structural vulnerability and neural stress may precede detectable functional loss by years. This parallel strengthens the rationale for viewing glaucoma through a neurodegenerative and aging-biology lens, emphasizing early detection and long-term risk modulation rather than reactive intervention alone.31,32,37–39

Lifestyle-associated Biological Stressors: Modifiers Rather than Causes

Lifestyle is often discussed in oversimplified or moralized terms within medical discourse. In the context of glaucoma and neurodegeneration, it is more appropriate to view lifestyle-associated factors as biological modifiers rather than direct etiological agents. Systemic metabolic health, vascular regulation, sleep patterns, physical activity, and chronic psychological stress influence inflammation, mitochondrial efficiency, oxidative stress, and autonomic balance, pathways central to neuronal survival. Glaucoma patients are in a state of allostatic overload (physiological dysfunction), which may be a causal factor for glaucoma.14,15,31,37–41

Metabolic dysfunction, including insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, has been associated with increased risk of neurodegenerative disease and may contribute to impaired neuronal energy metabolism.42–44 Vascular dysregulation, a recognized feature in certain glaucoma phenotypes, can be exacerbated by systemic endothelial dysfunction and impaired autoregulation.45 Sleep disturbance and circadian rhythm disruption have also been linked to altered neuroinflammatory signaling and impaired lymphatic clearance in the CNS.46

These associations do not imply that lifestyle modification alone can prevent or treat glaucoma. Rather, they suggest that systemic biological stressors may influence the resilience of the aging optic nerve, contributing to interindividual variability in disease onset and progression.

Physical Activity, Autonomic Balance, and Neurovascular Health

Regular physical activity has well-established benefits for cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurological health. Exercise improves endothelial function, enhances cerebral and ocular perfusion, modulates autonomic tone, and increases neurotrophic factors that support neuronal survival.9–15,17–19

Observational studies have reported associations between higher levels of physical activity and reduced risk of incident primary open-angle glaucoma, as well as slower rates of visual field progression, effects that appear largely independent of sustained IOP reduction.17–19

Yoga-based interventions have received particular attention in glaucoma. Certain postures, especially those involving prolonged head-down positions, can cause transient elevations in IOP, underscoring the need for posture-specific counseling. Conversely, modified practices and aerobic activity may confer systemic benefits without adversely affecting IOP in most patients.14,15,47

Nutrition, Oxidative Stress, and Mitochondrial Vulnerability

Retinal ganglion cells are highly metabolically active and particularly susceptible to oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Diets rich in antioxidants, polyphenols, omega-3 fatty acids, and nitrates may theoretically mitigate these processes by reducing oxidative injury, improving vascular function, and supporting mitochondrial efficiency.

Mediterranean-type dietary patterns are associated with reduced systemic inflammation and improved vascular health. Epidemiologic data suggest that higher intake of leafy green vegetables, a major source of dietary nitrates, is associated with a lower risk of primary open-angle glaucoma, potentially via nitric oxide–mediated improvements in ocular perfusion.9,15,17,19,48,50

Evidence supporting specific micronutrient supplementation in glaucoma remains inconsistent. At present, dietary counseling should emphasize overall dietary quality rather than isolated supplements.

Sleep, Circadian Rhythms, and Optic Nerve Perfusion

Sleep disturbances and circadian rhythm abnormalities are increasingly recognized in patients with glaucoma, particularly those with normal-tension disease. Nocturnal hypotension, impaired autoregulation, and altered cerebrospinal fluid dynamics may contribute to optic nerve vulnerability during sleep.

Associations have been reported between obstructive sleep apnea, poor sleep quality, and glaucoma progression. While direct evidence that improving sleep alters glaucoma outcomes is limited, screening for sleep disorders may be reasonable in selected patients with progressive disease despite controlled IOP.42,45,46,51

Even though the association between sleep position and glaucoma progression is not definitive, glaucoma patients may be advised to avoid sleeping laterally with an arm pressing on the globe.46,51

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

Recognizing glaucoma as an age-related neurodegenerative condition influenced by lifestyle-associated biological pathways reinforces the need for early detection, sustained disease stability, and refined risk stratification. Importantly, lifestyle considerations should be framed strictly as adjunctive risk modifiers. IOP-lowering therapies remain the only proven disease-modifying interventions in glaucoma.

Incorporating Lifestyle Medicine in Current Practice

While enthusiasm for lifestyle-based interventions must be tempered by the limitations of existing evidence, several principles can be stated with confidence:

Meditation: There is evidence that mindfulness-based stress reduction may result in a significant decrease in IOP and serum cortisol, as well as improvement in optic nerve head perfusion and QOL in ocular hypertensives.7,47,52,53 This includes breathing practices such as the 365-breathing technique, which may also decrease IOP and serum cortisol, and improve autonomic dysfunction in glaucoma patients.12

Meditation, aligning with psychoneuroendocrine immunology interests, may complement stress reduction, though direct glaucoma trials are limited.

Diet: Diets rich in nitrates from leafy greens (e.g., kale, beets) reduce glaucoma risk potentially via nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation and improved aqueous outflow. Antioxidant-packed foods such as fruits, vegetables high in vitamins C/A, carotenoids (lutein/zeaxanthin), and flavonoids (berries, dark chocolate) mitigate oxidative stress on RGCs, with higher intake linked to lower glaucoma odds in cohorts. Mediterranean or MIND diets show promise, though evidence on supplements such as omega-3s or B3 (nicotinamide) remains mixed and warrants caution without further RCTs.9–15,17–19,48–50

Exercise: Regular physical activity has well-established benefits in limiting inflammaging and fostering neuroprotection. Moderate aerobic exercise may lower IOP transiently and potentially slow visual field loss by enhancing ocular perfusion and neuroprotection via BDNF upregulation. Avoid high-risk activities such as inverted yoga poses, heavy weightlifting, or tight swimming goggles, which spike IOP.9,10,18,19

Sleep: Sleep disturbances and circadian rhythm abnormalities are increasingly associated with glaucoma, especially normal-tension disease. Elevating the head of the bed and avoiding face-down sleeping may reduce nocturnal IOP elevation. Obstructive sleep apnea increases the risk of glaucoma progression via hypoxia and oxidative stress, while CPAP therapy stabilizes progression. Patients must aim for 6–7 hours of quality sleep.17,18,41,46

Smoking, caffeine, alcohol: Smoking may result in glaucoma progression, and long-term smoking cessation may lower the risk of glaucoma progression. However, there is no definitive evidence to this effect yet.54,55

Glaucoma patients may be advised to limit caffeine intake due to the possibility of a clinically significant increase in IOP associated with caffeine consumption.

Similarly, even though some meta-analysis suggests an association between alcohol use and glaucoma progression, patients may be advised moderation in alcohol consumption.56,57

Conclusion

Glaucoma occupies a critical intersection between ophthalmology and neurodegeneration, shaped by aging biology and modulated by lifestyle-associated biological stressors. Framing the disease in this way does not replace established management principles but enriches them. As populations age globally, integrating insights from neurodegenerative and aging biology into glaucoma thinking may advance both clinical care and research, fostering a more comprehensive understanding of this complex disease.
