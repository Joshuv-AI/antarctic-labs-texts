Neuroprotective response against the onset of ischemic stroke by upregulation of histone H3Y99 sulfation
Authors: Li Jiang, Junchang Xie, Jianfeng Wang, Yili Yi, Runxin Zhou, Dingyuan Guo, Yu Wang, Xiao Zeng, Mingxuan Shi, Jianing Ding, Jiadi Wu, Jun Zhao, Siyu Feng, Nan Wang, Qian Shen, Yuping Yin, Mingchang Li, Yugang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryProtective cerebral responses against stresses are fundamental quests of medical science. Here, we report that upregulation of histone sulfation is a protective cerebral response against ischemic injury. Ischemia upregulates the SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1 axis, which mediates the transportation of sulfate into cells, conversion of sulfate into PAPS, and catalysis of histone sulfation (H3Y99sulf) using PAPS, respectively. Upregulated H3Y99sulf promotes metabolic genes transcription and glycolysis, sustaining cell survival in ischemic stress. In the mouse model of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion, both PAPSS1 overexpression and sulfate supplementation can boost the neuroprotective H3Y99sulf mechanism, reduce brain injury, and improve neurological functions; disruption of H3Y99sulf exacerbates ischemia-induced brain injury and counteracts the neuroprotective effect of sulfate. Ischemia patients with higher serum sulfate levels are prone to have smaller infarcts, alleviated severity assessments, and better clinical outcomes. This study unearths an undocumented protective cerebral response against ischemia that might be targeted for ischemic stroke treatment.Keywords: histone sulfation, ischemic stroke, neuroprotective response

Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke is the second leading cause of both disability and death globally.1 Reperfusion therapy is the primary strategy in clinical practices.1,2,3,4 However, it has restricted time-window and treatment-associated complications, and a substantial number of patients experience limited recovery postreperfusion.5 Neuroprotective therapy is another promising strategy. While increasing number of neuroprotective agents have displayed promising efficacy in animal experiments, few studies have been successfully translated into clinical practices,6 suggesting that deep insights in the biology of ischemic stroke are desired to illuminate alternative targets and interventions.7,8 Typically, neuroprotective agents interrupt the biological responses that mediate cerebral tissue injury during or after ischemia.9 As the other side of a coin, cerebral tissue also has protective responses to the onset of ischemic stroke. Awakening these protective brain responses against stroke might illustrate alternative treatments for ischemic stroke patients.10

Ischemic stroke can induce rapid changes in the activities and expression of enzymes regulating histone modifications that translate the fatal stress into the adjustments of gene expression and govern cell fate.11,12 For instance, early studies revealed that ischemia-reperfusion caused a 40% decrease in histone H3 and H4 acetylation that might lead to chromatin condensation, decreases of anti-inflammatory gene transcriptions, and activation of apoptosis. Aiming to counteract these deleterious mechanisms and validate neuroprotective agents, histone deacetylase inhibitors were developed and studied.13,14 However, this epigenetic strategy is controversial and poorly validated in clinical practices. Indeed, changes in histone modifications do not necessarily cause deleterious effects in the progression of diseases. Especially at the onset of stress-induced diseases, reprogramming of the histone modification profile is used to mediate protective responses that maintains physiological homeostasis and sustain cell survival.15,16,17 As such, provoking these onset histone modification changes might enhance the endogenous protective responses of cells and tissues against stress. Recently, we reported that tyrosine sulfation is an undocumented histone modification that occurs at the tyrosine-99 residue of histone H3 (H3Y99sulf).18 It can be upregulated by hypoxia, regulating gene transcription, and sustaining cell survival under the hypoxic stress.19 Hypoxia also occurs in ischemia and severely damages cerebral tissues.20 Whether H3Y99sulf could mediate protective brain response to the onset of ischemic stroke remains elusive.

Results

H3Y99sulf is dynamically regulated during the process of ischemia-reperfusion

To understand the regulation of H3Y99sulf during cerebral ischemia-reperfusion, we established the mouse model of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO). The H3Y99sulf level in the ischemic hemisphere was upregulated at the ischemic stage and downregulated at the reperfusion stage following the tMCAO surgery (Figures 1A and 1B). By performing H&E (hematoxylin and eosin) staining analysis, we identified the infarct cores, peri-infarct areas, and non-infarct areas in the collected mouse brains (Figure 1C). We also performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis that illustrates the heterogeneous and time course distribution of H3Y99sulf in the collected brains (Figure 1D). The tMCAO surgery significantly upregulated the H3Y99sulf level in the peri-infarct areas of the ischemic cerebral hemisphere (Figure 1D). On the third day of reperfusion following the tMCAO surgery (tMCAO-3d), we observed gradient descents of H3Y99sulf levels, from the non-infarct area (contralateral area) to peri-infarct area and finally to the infarct core (Figure 1D). On the seventh day of reperfusion after the tMCAO surgery (tMCAO-7d), the H3Y99sulf levels in the peri-infarct areas were equivalent to non-infarct areas (Figure 1D). A dynamic regulation of H3Y99sulf during the pathophysiological process of cerebral ischemia-reperfusion occurred in the tMCAO mice (Figures S1A and S1B): in the peri-infarct areas, the H3Y99sulf level is upregulated by ischemia, downregulated in the following reperfusion process, and gradually return to the normal level at the later stage of reperfusion.

To understand whether the regulation of H3Y99sulf occurs in certain types of brain cells, we analyzed the colocalization of H3Y99sulf and molecular markers of brain cell types. Immunofluorescence analyses revealed that H3Y99sulf colocalizes with NeuN, Iba1, GFAP, Olig2, and CD31, which are the hallmarks of neuron, microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and vascular endothelial cells, respectively (Figure S1C). Fluorescence intensity analysis revealed that the H3Y99sulf-positive levels of these cells were significantly upregulated by the treatment of tMCAO (Figure S1C). We next obtained primary neuron, microglia, astrocytes, and endothelial cells and established the model of oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) (Figure S1D and S1E). The H3Y99sulf levels in these cells were significantly upregulated by the OGD treatment (Figure 1E). We also established the model of oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) by using a human neuroblastoma cell line (SY5Y) and a human microglial cell line (HMC3). Immunoblotting assays revealed that the H3Y99sulf is upregulated by OGD treatment and reduced in the following reperfusion process (Figure 1F). The results are in line with the dynamics of H3Y99sulf in the cerebral tissues of tMCAO mice (Figures 1B, 1D, S1A, and S1B).

These findings illustrate that H3Y99sulf is dynamically regulated in the pathophysiological process of ischemia/reperfusion, which is a universal mechanism in multiple types of brain cells.

Ischemia onset upregulates H3Y99sulf via the SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1 axis

We next investigated the mechanism regulating H3Y99sulf responding to the onset of ischemic stroke. Sulfate is a metabolite that can be obtained from diet and the catabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids, such as methionine and cysteine. PAPS synthase (PAPSS) can utilize the intracellular sulfate to catalyze the generation of PAPS,21 which is the universal donor of the sulfate group for the sulfation reactions in cells. Therefore, addition of sodium sulfate and methionine dose dependently and time dependently upregulated H3Y99sulf in cultured cells (Figures 2A andS2A). By performing real-time PCR analyses, we found that OGD treatment significantly suppressed the transcription of genes regulating methionine and cysteine catabolism (Figures S2B and S2C), suggesting that the catabolism of sulfur-containing amino acids might not fuel PAPS for the OGD-induced upregulation of H3Y99sulf. By contrast, the mRNA and protein levels of SLC26A1, which is the major sulfate transporter in human cells,22,23 were upregulated by the OGD treatment (Figures 2B and S2D). PAPSS1 is the major PAPS synthase that utilizes sulfate to generate PAPS in cerebral tissue and cells.21,24 Its mRNA and protein levels were also upregulated by the OGD treatment (Figures 2B and S2D). SULT1B1 is the enzyme that utilizes PAPS to catalyze H3Y99sulf.18 Its expression was also stimulated by the OGD treatment (Figures 2B andS2D). Consistently, OGD treatment also increased the protein levels of SLC26A1, PAPSS1, and SULT1B1 in primary neuron and microglia (Figure 2C). Therefore, we speculated that upregulation of the SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1 axis might be the causal mechanism mediating the OGD-upregulated H3Y99sulf.

To validate this assumption, we tested the effects of OGD-upregulated SLC26A1 on the influx of extracellular sulfate and H3Y99sulf level. The intracellular sulfate concentration was sensitive to the addition of sulfate (Figure S2E), ectopic overexpression of SLC26A1 (Figure S2F), and depletion of SLC26A1 (Figure 2D). OGD treatment upregulated the intracellular sulfate concentration (Figures 2D, S2E, and S2F), which was further increased by the addition of sulfate and antagonized by the depletion of SLC26A1 (Figure 2D). SLC26A1 depletion did not affect the protein levels of PAPSS1 and SULT1B1 but effectively reduced H3Y99sulf and antagonized sulfate-induced H3Y99sulf upregulation (Figures 2E andS2G). SLC26A1 overexpression significantly upregulated H3Y99sulf (Figure 2F). These results demonstrate that SLC26A1-mediated sulfate influx from the extracellular pool plays substantial role in regulating H3Y99sulf under the OGD condition. We next tested the effects of OGD-upregulated PAPSS1 on the H3Y99sulf level in cells. OGD treatment significantly upregulated the protein level of PAPSS1 but not PAPSS2 (Figure S2H). PAPSS1 overexpression and depletion effectively increased and reduced the H3Y99sulf level in the cultured cells, respectively (Figures 2G and 2H). Depletion of PAPSS1 counteracted the effect of sulfate addition on H3Y99sulf (Figure 2I). These findings demonstrate that PAPSS1 is necessary for the regulatory effect of sulfate-SLC26A1 on the H3Y99sulf level in cells. SULT1B1 is the enzyme that utilizes PAPS to catalyze H3Y99sulf.18 SULT1B1 depletion substantially reduced H3Y99sulf and antagonized sulfate-induced upregulation of H3Y99sulf in cells (Figure 2J). These results support the conclusion that OGD treatment upregulates H3Y99sulf via promoting the SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1 axis in cells, which increases the influx of extracellular sulfate, PAPS synthesis, and histone sulfation installation reaction (Figure 2K). Depletion of any component of the SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1 axis significantly diminished the effect of OGD treatment on H3Y99sulf level in cells (Figures 2L–2N). In the tMCAO-0h mice, the protein levels of SLC26A1, PAPSS1, and SULT1B1 in the ischemic cerebral areas were significantly higher than that in the contralateral areas (Figure 2O). Consistently, the H3Y99sulf level in the ischemic cerebral areas were upregulated (Figures 2O, 1B, and 1D).

These findings demonstrate that ischemia onset upregulates H3Y99sulf via boosting the SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1 axis (Figure 2K). Extracellular pool is the major source of sulfate fueling this mechanism axis to support the upregulation of H3Y99sulf by ischemic stroke.

Upregulation of H3Y99sulf is a neuroprotective response against ischemic stroke

To understand whether the ischemia-induced upregulation of H3Y99sulf is a deleterious or protective response, we disrupted the H3Y99sulf installation mechanism by depleting SLC26A1, SULT1B1, and PAPSS1 in cells, respectively. Disruption of the H3Y99sulf installation mechanism significantly reduced cell survival after OGD/R treatment (Figures S3A and S3B). Boosting the H3Y99sulf installation mechanism by overexpression of these proteins significantly improved cell survival under OGD/R stress (Figures S3C and S3D). Sulfate supplementation upregulated H3Y99sulf and cell survival under OGD/R stress (Figures 2A,S3E, and S3F). Disruption of the SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1 axis diminished the beneficial effect of sulfate supplementation on cell survival (Figures S3G and S3H). In primary neuron and microglia cells, PAPSS1 depletion significantly exacerbated the LDH release of these primary cells treated by OGD/R stress (Figures S3I and S3J). Both PAPSS1 overexpression and sulfate supplementation significantly reduced the LDH release of these primary cells in the model of OGD/R (Figures S3K–S3M). Depletion of PAPSS1 antagonized the inhibitory effects of sulfate on the LDH release from primary cells in the model of OGD/R (Figure S3N). These results collectively suggest that upregulation of the SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1-H3Y99sulf axis is a protective mechanism of cells responding to oxygen-glucose deprivation to sustain cell survival under the OGD stress.

To validate the neuroprotective effects of the H3Y99sulf mechanism axis in tMCAO mice, we utilized adeno-associated virus (AAV) to manipulate the protein level of PAPSS1 in mouse cerebral tissue and assessed brain injuries in the tMCAO-3d mice. Overexpression of PAPSS1 upregulated H3Y99sulf (Figures S4A and S4B), reduced the infarct volume of ischemic brains, from 26.29% ± 3.01% to 13.76% ± 3.38% (Figure 3A), and improved neurological functions of the tMCAO-3d mice (Figure 3B). It also significantly reduced the number of TUNEL-positive neurons and antagonized the loss of Nissl-stained survival neurons in the ischemia-affected cerebral regions (Figures 3C and 3D). Compared to the cerebral tissue infected with AAV-vector, PAPSS1 overexpression reduced the pro-inflammatory CD86-positive microglia and increased the anti-inflammatory Arg1-positive microglia (Figure 3E). Consistently, PAPSS1 overexpression reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-1β and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) (Figure 3F), and increased anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10 (Figure 3F), in the cerebral tissues of the tMCAO-3d mice. AAV-mediated PAPSS1 knockdown significantly reduced H3Y99sulf level in the mouse cerebral tissue (Figures S4C and S4D). It did not directly induce brain injury but significantly increased the infarct volume from 24.58% ± 2.77% to 31.40% ± 2.41% (Figure S4E), worsened neurological functions (Figure S4F), accelerated neuronal apoptosis (Figure S4G), and exacerbated the loss of Nissl-stained neurons (Figure S4H). The ischemia-reperfusion-induced changes in the CD86-positive microglia, inflammatory cytokine expression, and Arg1-positive microglia were also exacerbated by the AAV-mediated depletion of PAPSS1 (Figures S4I and S4J). These findings demonstrate that upregulation of the H3Y99sulf mechanism can protect cerebral tissue against ischemia injury.

Sulfate can fuel the H3Y99sulf mechanism axis (Figure 2K). Intravenous injection of sulfate increased H3Y99sulf level in mouse cerebral tissues (Figure S4K). It phenocopied the neuroprotective effects of PAPSS1 overexpression, such as reduced cerebral infarct volume, from 26.25% ± 2.98% to 14.50% ± 4.15% (Figure S4L), and improved neurological functions in the tMCAO-3d mice (Figure S4M). AAV-mediated PAPSS1 knockdown significantly diminished the neuroprotective effects of sulfate, including the sulfate-reduced infarct volume (Figure 4A), improved neurological functions in tMCAO-3d mice (Figure 4B), alleviated neuron apoptosis and injury (Figures 4C and 4D), reduced the pro-inflammatory CD86-positive microglia, and increased anti-inflammatory Arg1-positive microglia (Figure 4E). Sulfate addition suppressed the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β and TNF-α (Figure 4F), and increased the anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10 (Figure 4F), in the cerebral tissues of tMCAO-3d mice. PAPSS1 depletion significantly counteracted the effects of sulfate addition on the expression of these inflammatory cytokines (Figure 4F). These findings demonstrate that sulfate protects cerebral tissues against the ischemia injury via fueling the H3Y99sulf mechanism.

H3Y99sulf promotes glycolysis and sustains cell survival in ischemic stroke

H3Y99sulf can regulate gene transcription.18,19 To understand the mechanism underlying H3Y99sulf-mediated neuroprotection against ischemic injury, we assessed the chromatin occupancy of H3Y99sulf and its distribution dynamics by performing chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis. The assays identified 13,247 and 16,675 H3Y99sulf-enriched chromatin regions (peaks, fold enrichment [FE] ≥5; p < 0.01) in SY5Y cells cultured in normal condition and OGD condition, respectively (Figure S5A). It is consistent with the finding that OGD upregulates H3Y99sulf in cells (Figures 1E and 1F). These OGD-induced H3Y99sulf peaks were localized in the promoter regions of 766 genes (Table S1). Integrated ChIP-seq and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data analyses revealed that these OGD-regulated and H3Y99sulf-enriched genes, such as HK2, PDK1, PFKP, and PKM (Figure 5A), regulate glycolysis pathway (Figures S5B–S5D). Real-time PCR assays following ChIP experiments validated that enrichment of H3Y99sulf in the promoter regions of these genes is significantly upregulated by OGD stress (Figure 5B). The protein and mRNA levels of these genes were also upregulated by OGD treatment (Figures 5C and S5E). Disruption of H3Y99sulf-installation mechanism diminished the OGD-induced H3Y99sulf enrichment in gene promoter regions (Figure S5F), including the genes of HK2, PDK1, PFKP, and PKM (Figure S5G). The transcription of 87.7% (293 out of 334) of OGD-induced and H3Y99sulf-regulated genes was significantly antagonized in cells with deficiency in H3Y99sulf-installation mechanism (Figure S5H), including these studied metabolic genes (Figures 5D and S5I). Because these metabolic genes are central players in glycolysis, disruption of the H3Y99sulf mechanism significantly antagonized OGD-promoted glycolysis (Figures 5E and 5F), exacerbating the loss of ATP production (Figure 5G). Sulfate fuels the H3Y99sulf mechanism (Figure 2K). Therefore, sulfate addition promoted OGD-upregulated H3Y99sulf (Figure S5J), increased the OGD-stimulated expression of PDK1, PFKP, PKM2, and HK2 (Figures 5H and S5K), boosted glycolysis (Figures 5I and 5J), and enhanced ATP generation (Figure 5K). Disruption of the mechanism axis significantly diminished the effects of sulfate supplementation on the glycolysis and ATP production in the OGD-treated cells (Figures 5L–5N). Interrupting or provoking the H3Y99sulf mechanism axis did not affect the glycolysis of cells cultured in normal condition (Figures S5L and S5M). Manipulation of the H3Y99sulf mechanism axis did not affect the oxidative phosphorylation of cells cultured under both normal and OGD conditions (Figures S5N–S5Q). Sulfate addition can specifically promote the glycolysis of cells cultured in OGD condition (Figures 5I and 5J). The increased lactate production might lead to the upregulated maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity of the OGD-treated cells (Figure S5Q), which was not observed in cells cultured in normal condition (Figure S5P). These findings illustrate that the H3Y99sulf mechanism can specifically promote ATP generation via increasing glycolysis in cells under OGD condition.

Glycolysis is pivotal to maintain the bioenergetic homeostasis of cerebral tissue affected by ischemia.25,26,27,28 These findings demonstrate that, in response to the onset of ischemic stroke, the upregulated H3Y99sulf mechanism stimulates the expression of glycolytic genes to maintain the bioenergetic homeostasis, enhancing the resistance of brain cells against ischemia and protecting cerebral tissues against ischemic injury.

Serum sulfate concentration associates with the severity of ischemic stroke

Sulfate is a metabolite that plays roles in human growth, development, and maintenance of physiological homeostasis.29,30 It can fuel the H3Y99sulf mechanism and protect cerebral tissues against ischemia injury. It is worth asking whether the serum sulfate, at least, correlates with the prognosis of ischemic stroke patients.

To validate this hypothesis, we retrospectively analyzed 145 acute ischemic stroke patients (symptom onset ≤24 h) from a consecutive cohort (Table S2; Table S3). By measuring the sulfate concentration in the peripheral blood of ischemic stroke patients and healthy volunteers (Figure S6A; Tables S3–S5), we found no difference in the serum sulfate concentration between ischemic stroke patients and healthy volunteers (Figure 6A). However, in the group of ischemic stroke patients, the serum sulfate concentration was negatively correlated with the severity assessment (measured by the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale Score [NIHSSS]),31 cerebral infarct volumes, and clinical outcomes (measured by the modified Rankin Scale [mRS]) (Figures 6B and 6C).32 The severity of ischemic stroke is largely affected by the sites of occlusion and infarction.33,34 We found that the negative correlation between the serum sulfate and the severity of ischemic stroke occurs in all the analyzed subgroups (Figures 6D–6F), regardless the sites of occlusion and infarction.

We next performed multivariable linear regression analyses by setting the onset serum sulfate concentration as an independent predictor variable (Figure 6G; Tables S6 and S7). The NIHSS score and infarct volumes were set as the outcome variable, and other parameters in the clinical records, such as age, systolic BP, gender, diastolic BP, total cholesterol, smoking, atrial fibrillation, infarct location, acute treatment, and occluded vessel, were set as the confounding variables. After initial variable screening using univariate linear regression analyses, multiple linear regression analysis was performed to study the effects of the included variables. The results illustrate that the onset serum sulfate concentration, the occluded vessel, the infarct location, and the systolic BP significantly correlates with the clinical severity of ischemic stroke (Figure 6G; Tables S6 and S7), which are in line with previous publications.33,34,35 Remarkably, the analysis also revealed that the onset serum sulfate concentration is a valuable prognostic marker of the ischemic stroke patients (Figure 6G). Moreover, the analysis revealed that each 0.1 mmol/L elevation in the onset serum sulfate concentration is associated with significant reductions of 2.18 points in the NIHSS score and 2.52 cm3 in the infarct volume (Table S7). These findings illustrate that patients with higher onset serum sulfate concentration are prone to have smaller ischemic infarct volumes and better clinical outcomes.

Blood coagulation and fibrinolysis play central roles in the pathophysiology of ischemic stroke.36 Addition of sulfate did not change the coagulation and fibrinolysis of the peripheral blood obtained from healthy volunteers (Figures S6B–S6F). Recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) is the first-line therapeutics to treat ischemic stroke. Addition of sulfate did not affect the fibrinolysis induced by rt-PA (Figures S6B–S6F). These findings suggest that sulfate does not affect the blood coagulation and fibrinolysis of ischemic stroke patients.

These findings provide additional supports to the conclusion that the H3Y99sulf mechanism is an inherent and protective brain response to the onset of ischemic stroke.

Methylsulfonylmethane phenocopies the neuroprotective effect of sulfate

Methylsulfonylmethane (MSM) is an organosulfur compound commonly consumed by humans.37 It contributes sulfate to mammals and humans.38 MSM treatment increased H3Y99sulf levels in cells (Figures S7A and S7B). It also improved the survival of cells suffering OGD/R treatment (Figure 7A). Disruption of the H3Y99sulf mechanism axis antagonized the protective effects of MSM on the cells treated by OGD (Figure S7C), suggesting that MSM can improve the survival of cells following OGD/R treatment via the H3Y99sulf mechanism.

MSM injection also upregulated H3Y99sulf in the cerebral tissues of mice (Figure S7D). To test the effects of MSM on the mouse model of tMCAO, we injected the compound (500 mg/kg) to the mouse at different stages of the tMCAO modeling (Figure S7E). Regardless of the time of MSM administration, it significantly reduced the cerebral infarct volume and improved the neurological function of tMCAO mice (Figures S7F–S7I). Low dosage of MSM (50 mg/kg), injected at the beginning of the tMCAO surgery prior to the reperfusion stage, significantly decreased the cerebral infarct volume (Figure 7B), from 25.72% ± 1.76% to 13.16% ± 2.42% (Figure 7B), improved the neurological functions (Figure 7C), reduced the number of TUNEL-positive neurons (Figure 7D), and antagonized the loss of Nissl-stained survival neurons of the tMCAO mouse (Figure 7E), protecting the brain tissue of tMCAO-3d mice from ischemic injury. MSM treatment reduced the pro-inflammatory CD86-positive microglia and increased the anti-inflammatory Arg1-positive microglia (Figure 7F), reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β and TNF-α (Figure 7G), and increased the anti-inflammatory cytokine, such as IL-10 (Figure 7G), in the cerebral tissues of tMCAO-3d mice, illustrating that MSM, an alternative sulfate supplement, is a neuroprotective agent against ischemic stroke injury. PAPSS1 depletion significantly diminished these neuroprotective effects of MSM (Figures 7B–7G).

These findings illustrate that MSM, an alternative sulfate supplement, can also fuel the H3Y99sulf mechanism and phenocopy the neuroprotective effect of sulfate against ischemic stroke. It consolidates the conclusion that H3Y99sulf mechanism protects cerebral tissue against the onset of ischemic stroke.

Discussion

Provoking neuroprotective responses is an important strategy to protect cerebral tissues against ischemia injury. To awaken endogenous protective mechanism and enhance the tolerance of brain cells against ischemia/reperfusion stress, transient exposure to brief ischemia was attempted to activate the multitude cascade of biomolecules that evolutionarily cooperate to enable cells to take advantage of adapting the pathophysiological stresses.39,40 This strategy has consistently shown favorable effects in animal and humans.41,42 However, there are several inevitable problems that damper the translation of preconditioning stimuli into clinical practices. First, the preconditioning stimuli might have the potential to cause fatal damage.43 Second, the effective preconditioning methods remain demanding. Third, determining the proper time window and dosage of preconditioning treatments is challenging. Deeper insights in the mechanisms of brain responding to ischemic stroke remain demanding.44 This study demonstrates that upregulation of the SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1-H3Y99sulf axis is an unrecorded protective mechanism of brain responding to the onset of ischemic stroke. In response to ischemia, the H3Y99sulf levels in multiple types of brain cells can be upregulated by the axis of SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1, enabling the brain cells to take advantage of adapting ischemia stress. It provides us an unprecedent insight in the mechanisms of brain responding to ischemic stroke. Sulfate is an endogenous metabolite in human but its physiological function is poorly understood. This study reveals that sulfate can flow through the SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1 axis and contribute a sulfate moiety to the histone sulfation (H3Y99sulf) in brain cells, provoking the endogenous neuroprotective H3Y99sulf mechanism of brain responding to the onset of ischemic stroke. It illustrates that the protective brain responses against stroke can be provoked by alternatives, such as addition of sulfate, other than preconditioning strategies, shedding light to the findings of neuroprotective agents. Furthermore, this study provides evidences that patients with high serum sulfate levels are prone to have less onset severity and better clinical outcomes. It is in line with the conclusion that sulfate can provoke the protective H3Y99sulf mechanism of brain responding to the onset of ischemic stroke. It also suggests that the serum sulfate concentration might serve as a prognostic marker for ischemic stroke patients. Given that sulfate is inexpensive, widely available, simple to administer, and well tolerated,37 it might be a safe alternative to treat ischemic stroke patients, despite that this prospective needs to be validated by more studies.

Magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) is an intensively studied neuroprotective agent that shows convincing neuroprotective profile in preclinical studies but failed in translating clinical benefits in a large-scale human clinical trial.45,46 It is reported that the magnesium ion (Mg2+) can protect brain from the ischemia injury by antagonizing the ischemia-induced deleterious mechanisms, such as release of glutamate,47 intracellular calcium overloading and glutamatergic excitatory signaling,48 and calcium-stimulated mitochondrial dysfunctions.49 Whether these ischemia-induced mechanisms constantly lead to deleterious effects remains elusive. Magnesium ion might unbiasedly block the protective mechanism of brain responding to ischemia. Separate validations of the effects of sulfate and magnesium would be the ultimate way to address the arguments.

Cerebral ischemic stroke has dynamic and complicated pathophysiological mechanisms, so that a single neuroprotective agent might not interdict enough pathways in the molecular elaboration of ischemic injury.50 Comparatively, a single pathway might not sufficiently explain the mechanism of a neuroprotective agent neither. Sulfation is an important chemical reaction that occurs in a wide range of bioactive molecules, such as steroids and proteins.51 PAPS is the universal sulfate moiety donor for sulfation reactions other than histone sulfation. Sulfated molecules have significant biological and pharmacological roles in diverse processes, such as cell signaling, immune response, and metabolism.52,53,54 Whether other sulfation pathways are involved in the biology of brain responding to ischemia remains elusive.

In summary, upregulation of histone sulfation via the axis of SLC26A1-PAPSS1-SULT1B1 is an endogenous mechanism of brain responding to the onset of ischemic stroke. It promotes the transportation of extracellular sulfate into cells, PAPS generation, and H3Y99sulf deposition. H3Y99sulf regulates gene transcription and reprograms cell metabolism, maintaining bioenergy homeostasis and sustaining cerebral tissue survival in ischemic stroke. Addition of sulfate can rapidly provoke this neuroprotective mechanism and effectively protect the brain against ischemic stroke. This study provides an undocumented insight into the biology of cerebral ischemic stroke, illustrates an unreported neuroprotective response against ischemic stroke, and expands the functional scenario of histone sulfation in human diseases.

Limitations of this study

This study has several limitations. First, activations of microglia and macrophages in response to ischemic stroke are significant. The potential roles and mechanisms of sulfate regulating microglial activation, as well as the associated neuroinflammation, remain elusive. Second, the mouse model of tMCAO involves the insertion of a surgical filament into the internal carotid artery (ICA) to occlude the origin of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), resulting in a cessation of blood flow and subsequent brain infarction in the MCA territory.55,56 It cannot mimic the process of blood coagulation and fibrinolysis. While the results of this study revealed that sulfate does not affect blood coagulation and fibrinolysis, whether intravenous injection of sulfate influences rt-PA-induced thrombolysis in animals and humans remains elusive. Third, whether sulfate could perform neuroprotective effects via other mechanisms, such as antioxidation and regulation of neuroinflammatory responses, remains elusive. Fourth, the long-term benefits of boosting H3Y99sulf mechanism to the ischemic stroke patients remain elusive. Lastly, the brain tissue of mice and humans is different. Clinical trials validating the neuroprotective effects of sulfate are expected.

Experimental models and study participant details

Human sample collection and processing

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee Affiliated Nanhua Hospital, University of South China (Approval No. 2025-KY-017) in compliance with Article 39 of China’s Ethical Review Measures for Life Science and Medical Research. Written informed consent was waived for the retrospective patient cohort, which was specifically approved by the Ethics Committee. All patient data were anonymized prior to analysis. For the healthy volunteer cohort, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. A total of 198 consecutive acute ischemic stroke patients (symptom onset ≤24 h; January 2022- December 2024) were screened, with 145 meeting inclusion criteria: age 18–80 years, absence of systemic comorbidities (chronic kidney disease, active malignancies), and no sulfate medication use within 48 h. Diagnostic DWI verification was completed in 113 patients, while DWI data were not available for 32 patients due to MRI contraindications (claustrophobia, metallic implants) or technical limitations. All enrolled patients were included in subsequent analyses (Table S2; Table S3). Univariate analysis assessed the association between serum sulfate and NIHSS, infarct volume, and 90-day mRS. To control for confounding, we stratified by occluded vessel and infarct location and repeated the analysis. Finally, we built a multivariable linear regression model to evaluate the predictive value of sulfate for stroke severity. Detailed cohort selection is illustrated in the Figure S8. Besides, 41 healthy volunteers (Table S4) were recruited as controls.

Peripheral blood from patients was collected pre-therapeutically using serum separator tubes. Serum samples were isolated by centrifugation (2,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C) was aliquoted into cryovials and stored at −80°C (±2°C) for sulfate ion analysis via ion chromatography (ECO IC, Metrohm, B&W Tek Optoelectronic Equipment (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.). All samples were processed within 2 h to ensure analyte integrity.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. [2024–4623]). Male C57BL/6J mice (7–8 weeks old) were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Production License No. SCXK [2021-0002]). The decision to use male mice was made to minimize confounding effects of hormonal fluctuations in females on stroke outcomes, as commonly practiced in preliminary mechanistic studies. The animals were housed in rooms with controlled humidity (60%) and temperature (24°C) and maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle. They were provided with free access to complete food and water. Before the experiment, the animals were acclimatized for at least 1 week. The timeline of the animal experimental design and group classification is presented in Figure S9 and Table S8.

Primary cell cultures

Four types of primary brain cells were utilized in this study: cortical neurons, microglia, brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs), and astrocytes. All cells were commercially available from the Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Specifically, mouse cortical neurons (Catalog #: CP-M143), microglia (Catalog #: CP-M110), brain microvascular endothelial cells (Catalog #: CP-M108), and astrocytes (Catalog #: CP-M157) were purchased. Each cell type was cultured in its corresponding specialized medium provided by the manufacturer (CM-M195, CM-M188, CM-M130, and CM-M187, respectively). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2, and the culture medium was refreshed every two to three days. All primary cells were used within passages 3–5 and were confirmed for purity (>95%) by immunofluorescence staining for cell-type-specific markers prior to experiments.

Cell lines and cell culture conditions

The human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y and the human microglia cell line HMC3 were purchased from the Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The human embryonic kidney HEK293T cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cells were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. All cell lines were regularly tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. The HEK293T cells were specifically used for lentivirus packaging.

Method details

Mouse model of middle cerebral artery occlusion

The mouse model of middle cerebral artery occlusion was established as previously described.57 Briefly, the blood was blocked by a silicon-coated monofilament that was introduced into the internal carotid artery via a cut in the external carotid artery. Blood flow was restored 1.5 h later by removal of the monofilament to mimic the restoration of blood flow after lysis of a thromboembolic clot in humans. The animal study was approved by Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

Laser speckle contrast imaging for cerebral blood flow monitoring

Cerebral blood flow (CBF) was monitored using a laser speckle contrast imaging system. After exposing the skull, baseline images were acquired. Immediately upon filament insertion, a sharp drop in CBF (>70%) was confirmed, indicating successful occlusion. After 1.5 h of occlusion, reperfusion was initiated by filament withdrawal, and the subsequent restoration of blood flow was recorded. Relative CBF was quantified as a percentage of the baseline value within the ischemic core region.

Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene knockdown and overexpression

Adeno-associated virus was packaged using the plasmids of pAAV[shRNA]-CAG>EGFP: T2A: Puro-U6>Papss1, pAAV[shRNA]-EGFP-U6>Scramble_shRNA, pAAV[Exp]-CAG>Papss1[NM_011863.3](ns):P2A:EGFP:WPRE, and pAAV[Exp]-CAG>EGFP: WPRE). The virus injection was carried out using a stereotaxic instrument. Each mouse was subjected to four sites in the cortex and striatum (0.5μL per site): point 1, 0.4 mm anterior to the bregma, 2.3 mm lateral, 1.8 mm deep; point 2, 0.4 mm anterior to the bregma, 2.3 mm lateral, 3.5 mm deep; point 3, 2.2 mm anterior to the bregma, 1.8 mm lateral, 1.5 mm deep; point 4, 2.2 mm anterior to the bregma, 3.0 mm lateral, 1.5 mm deep; All the target points were in the hemisphere ipsilateral to the subsequent MCAO surgery. The needle was withdrawn for 10 min 21 days after injection of the virus, mice were subjected to animal studies.

Assessment of the modified neurological severity score

The neurological functions were evaluated by the modified neurological severity score (mNSS) as previously described.58 The mNSS tests were performed by investigators blinded to the experimental groups on days 1, 2, and 3 after MCAO. The sensory, motor, reflex, and balance were evaluated in the mNSS test. Scoring for this test ranges from 0 to 12, where higher scores indicate worse neurological impairments.

Rotarod test

On the day prior to testing, each mouse received one training session consisting of 2 min on the stationary rod followed by 2 min at a constant low speed (4 rpm). For the formal test, the rotarod was set to accelerate from 4 to 40 rpm over a 4-min period. Each mouse was placed on the rod facing away from the experimenter, and the latency to fall was recorded with a maximum cut-off time of 240 s. Each animal underwent three trials with a 30-min rest interval between trials, and the average latency from the three trials was used for statistical analysis. All testing was performed by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups.

Grip strength test

The mouse was gently held by the base of the tail and allowed to grasp the triangular pull bar with its forepaws only. The mouse was then pulled horizontally backward at a consistent, slow speed until it released its grip. The peak force (in grams-force) was recorded automatically by the instrument. This procedure was repeated five times for each mouse with a 1-min rest interval between consecutive measurements. The highest and lowest values were excluded, and the average of the remaining three measurements was calculated as the representative grip strength for that animal.

2,3,5-Triphenyl tetrazolium chloride staining

The 2,3,5-Triphenyl tetrazolium chloride staining analysis was performed as previously described.59 Briefly, the cerebral infarct volume was determined using 2,3,5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining. Coronal brain sections were immersed in a 2% TTC solution for 20 min at 37°C. Non-infarct areas were shown in red color, while the infarct areas were shown in white color. The infarcted and non-infarcted hemispheres were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis

Brain tissues were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning into 5 μm coronal sections. General histopathological alterations in the mouse cortex were examined through hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, following a standard protocol.60 Neuronal damage was further assessed using Nissl staining.

For immunohistochemical analysis, the expression and distribution of H3Y99sulf were detected using a specific primary antibody, with nonspecific IgG serving as a negative control. Staining was performed using a DAB Horseradish Peroxidase Color Development Kit (Fuzhou Maixin Biotech. Co., Ltd., DAB4033) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cellular alterations were further characterized by immunofluorescence. Apoptotic neurons were identified by co-staining for the neuronal marker NeuN and TUNEL. The phenotypic polarization of microglia was evaluated by double immunofluorescence for Iba1 co-labeled with either CD86 (M1 phenotype) or Arg1 (M2 phenotype). To determine the cell-type-specific localization of H3Y99sulf, multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed using the custom H3Y99sulf antibody alongside markers for neurons (NeuN), microglia (Iba1), endothelial cells (CD31), oligodendrocytes (Olig2), and astrocytes (GFAP).

Image acquisition and quantification

For histopathological and IHC analysis, whole-slide images were digitally captured using a MECA slide scanner. For all immunofluorescence experiments, high-resolution images were acquired using the same MECA scanner equipped with fluorescence modules. All subsequent quantitative analyses (e.g., positive cell counting, fluorescence intensity) were performed in a blinded manner on these digitized images using ImageJ software.

Real-time metabolic flux analysis

Cellular metabolic function was assessed using an Agilent Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer. The Seahorse XF Glycolysis Rate Assay Kit (Cat#103344-100) and the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Cat#103015-100) were employed to evaluate glycolytic flux and mitochondrial respiration, respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, after OGD or normoxic treatment, SY5Y cells were equilibrated in XF assay medium. The glycolytic rate was measured by sequential injections of rotenone/antimycin A (Rot/AA) and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) to derive parameters including basal glycolysis, compensatory glycolysis, glycolytic reserve, and the percentage of proton efflux rate (PER) derived from glycolysis. Mitochondrial function was assessed via the mitochondrial stress test using sequential injections of oligomycin, FCCP, and Rot/AA, yielding parameters of basal respiration, ATP production, maximal respiration, and spare respiratory capacity. Data were normalized to protein content per well. Results are presented as the mean ± SD for bar graphs and mean ± SEM for kinetic curves from at least three biologically independent experiments.

Biochemical metabolite assays

Metabolic endpoints were quantified using specific biochemical kits. Intracellular ATP levels and lactate production in the culture supernatant were measured using their respective commercial assay kits (Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat# S0026 and Cat# S0208S), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Measurement of brain cytokine levels

The levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, IL-1β, and TNF-α) in mouse brain tissue homogenates were measured using specific ELISA kits (Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Cat# ml985632, Cat# ml665241, and Cat# ml551236) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunoblotting analysis

Immunoblotting assays were performed as described previously.18 Proteins were extracted from cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS) with protease inhibitor cocktail. The insoluble fraction in the lysate was spun down at 14000 r.p.m for 15 min at 4°C to discard. Samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (catalog no. 66485, PALL Bio Trace NC membrane). The membranes were blocked in 5% Bovine Albumin (BSA, catalog no.4240, Biofroxx) for 1h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with corresponding primary antibodies. Membranes were then washed three times with TBST (0.05% Tween 20, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl), incubated with horseradish peroxidase-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG or anti-mouse IgG for 1h at room temperature, washed three times with TBST, exposed with Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL), and then visualized and recorded using a Tanon chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5200).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using an RNA Simple Total RNA kit (catalog no. DP419, TIANGEN). Complementary DNA was prepared using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit purchased from Vazyme Biotech (catalog no R333-01). Quantitative real-time PCR analysis was performed with 2 × Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (catalog no. RK21203, Abclonal Technology) in the following conditions: 3 min at 95°C, and then 40 cycles at 95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 25 s, using a Real-Time System (Q225, Kubo Technology). Data were normalized according to the expression of a control gene (ACTB) in each experiment. The primers used for quantitative real-time PCR are listed in Table S9.

Oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) treatment

The in vitro ischemia-reperfusion injury model with modifications was established using an OGD/R procedure as previously described.61 Briefly, cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated in glucose-free DMEM (Gibco, USA) without FBS. For the OGD phase, the culture plates were placed in a dedicated hypoxic incubator pre-set to 1% O2, 94% N2, and 5% CO2 at 37°C for the indicated durations. Following OGD, cells were either immediately harvested for analysis (representing pure OGD injury) or subjected to reperfusion. Reperfusion was initiated by replacing the glucose-free medium with normal complete growth medium, after which cells were returned to a standard normoxic incubator (95% air, 5% CO2) for the designated reperfusion periods before subsequent analysis.

DNA constructs

The coding sequences for human SLC26A1, PAPSS1, and SULT1B1 were PCR-amplified and cloned into a pHAGE/NEO(+)-Flag vector. The coding sequence of mouse Papss1 was cloned into the Lenti-CAG-HA vector to generate an N-terminal HA-tagged protein. The oligonucleotides, which were inserted into the pLKO.1/Puro (+) vector to generate plasmids to knock down the protein level of corresponding genes, are listed in the key resources table.

Lentivirus packaging and infection

HEK293T cells (2×106) were seeded in a 100-mm cell culture dish and transfected with PEI. The transfection experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For expressing target proteins, we co-transfected the cells with the plasmids of psPAX2, pMD2.G, and core plasmid (pHAGE-flag-PAPSS1, SULT1B1, SLC26A1 and Lenti-CAG-HA-Papss1) to generate lentivirus; for knock-down target proteins, we co-transfected the cells with the plasmids of pMDL, pVSVG, pREV, and pLKO.1 (sh-Control, sh-PAPSS1, sh-SULT1B1, sh-SLC26A1, sh-Papss1) to generate lentivirus. For lentivirus infection, 500 μL of virus-containing medium was added to the 5×104 cells with 1% polybrene. Primary neurons were infected at DIV 5–7, while primary microglia were infected after cell attachment. The efficiency of gene overexpression or knockdown was verified by immunoblotting.

Cell viability assays

Cell viability was assessed using a cell counter (Tuger Automatic Fluorescence Cell Analyzer, Newton Optics, China) and LDH release measurement. Following the respective treatments, cell viability was first quantified by trypan blue exclusion using an automated cell counter, with the results presented as cell density of the analyzed samples. In parallel, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release into the culture supernatant was measured as an indicator of cytotoxicity using a commercial kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat# C0017) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

ChIP and ChIP-seq assay

ChIP was performed using a SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (catalog #9003, Cell Signaling Technology). Chromatin prepared from cells in a 15-cm dish was used to determine total DNA input and was incubated overnight with specific antibodies. ChIP sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform with PE150 strategy in Novogene Bioinformatics Technology, according to effective library concentration and data amount required. The primers used for ChIP-qPCR Primer are listed in the key resources table.

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNA Simple Total RNA kit (#DP419, TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Illumina-compatible libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. In brief, using fragmented mRNA as a template and random oligonucleotides as primers, the first strand of cDNA was synthesized in the M-MuLV reverse transcriptase system. In the DNA polymerase I system, dNTPs were used as the primer. The raw material is used to synthesize the second strand of cDNA. The purified double-stranded cDNA undergoes end-repair, A-tailing, and ligation of sequencing adapters. The 250- 300bp cDNA is screened with AMPure XP beads, PCR amplification is performed, and the PCR products are purified again with AMPure XP beads to obtain the library. After the library is constructed, use a Qubit2.0 Fluorometer for preliminary quantification, dilute the library to 1.5ng/ul, and then use an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer to detect the insert size of the library. After the insert size meets the expectation, real-time RT-PCR measures the effective concentration of the library.

Bioinformatic analysis

The sequencing reads were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie 2 2.2.5.62 Samtools 1.6.0 was used to sort and index the alignment files.63 Picard 2.26.6 was utilized to remove PCR-derived duplicates and minimize amplification bias. MACS264 was used to call ChIP-seq peaks (p value <0.01). The peak coordinates were annotated for genomic features using Bedtools 2.30.0.65 STAR66 and RSEM67 were used to quantify gene expression based on RNA-seq data, and DESeq268 was used to identify differentially expressed genes (Wald test, p < 0.05). IGV69 was used to visualize ChIP-seq data. The other analyses were conducted on R 4.3.1. We applied GSEA analysis70 on the differentially expressed genes in SY5Y cells derived from RNA-Seq analysis. The hallmark database used in the GSEA analysis was downloaded from MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/) (Fisher’s Exact Test, p < 0.05). GSEA results were visualized using GSEA 4.3.2.

Human blood sample analysis

The sulfate concentration in each blood sample was analyzed by ion chromatography as described previously.71,72 Briefly, 100 μL of serum from human blood samples was treated with 100 μL of 0.5 M trichloroacetic acid buffer to precipitate proteins. The supernatant was filtered and injected into ion chromatography (ECO IC, Metrohm, B&W Tek Optoelectronic Equipment (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.) for analysis. The National Institute of Health Stroke Scale Score (NIHSSS) of each patient was assessed as previously described.31

For coagulation analyses, peripheral blood from a subset of healthy volunteers (n = 10).

was collected separately and immediately processed for testing, including prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), thrombin time (TT), fibrinogen (FIB) concentration, and D-dimer levels, were analyzed using the STA-R Evolution analyzer (Stago Diagnostica, France). PT was measured with the STA-Neoplastin CI Plus reagent, APTT with STA-Cephascreen reagent, TT with STA-Thrombin reagent, and FIB levels were determined via the Clauss method using STA-Fibrinogen reagent. D-dimer quantification was performed using an immunoturbidimetric assay (STA-Liatest D-Di reagent). All procedures were strictly followed by the manufacturer’s protocols.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification and statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism (v.10.3.1). Data are presented as mean ± SD, except for Seahorse assay kinetics which are shown as mean ± SEM. Normality and variance homogeneity were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. For two-group comparisons, two-tailed Student’s t-tests (parametric) or Mann-Whitney/Wilcoxon tests (non-parametric) were used. Multi-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test, or their non-parametric equivalents (Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s correction). Longitudinal data were assessed by repeated-measures ANOVA. In clinical analyses, variable selection was performed via univariate linear regression, followed by multivariable linear regression to identify independent predictors. Correlations were evaluated using Spearman’s rank test. A p-value <0.05 was considered significant. Statistically significant differences are denoted as ∗p < 0.05 and ∗∗p < 0.01.
