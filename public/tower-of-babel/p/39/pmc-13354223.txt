The future of long-term care financing in Ghana: preferences among dual-role unorganised caregivers of older adults
Authors: Daniel Offei, Ulrika Enemark, Robert Darko Osei, Charles Godfred Ackah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-term care (LTC) demand in Ghana is increasing due to rapid population ageing, raising concerns about how care can be sustainably financed. In the absence of a formal LTC system, care is largely provided and financed by unorganized family caregivers who often assume both caregiving and financial responsibilities. The study examined preferences for LTC financing models among such caregivers and assessed how these preferences are shaped by caregiving beliefs, caregiver-recipient relationships, and specific caregiving tasks. A cross-sectional survey of 1116 dual-role caregivers across three regions of Ghana was conducted. Multinomial logistic regression models were estimated, and results were presented as average marginal effects comparing preferences for insurance-based and tax-financed models relative to family or out-of-pocket financing. The results show that greater intensity of personal care provision was associated with a higher probability of preferring insurance-based financing, increasing the probability by about 3.6 percentage points (dy/dx = 0.036, p = 0.002). Similarly, greater provision of emotional support increased the probability of preferring tax-financed models by approximately 7.8 percentage points (dy/dx = 0.078, p < 0.001). These associations varied by caregiver-recipient relationship and caregiving beliefs. Sustainable LTC financing in Ghana should therefore reflect caregiving intensity, beliefs, and income differences among caregivers.Subject terms: Health care, Health humanities, Medical humanities, Social policy

Introduction

The demand for long-term care (LTC) is growing, raising urgent questions about how such care should be financed and delivered as Ghana’s population continues to age1. In Ghana, rapid population ageing is occurring in the absence of a formal LTC system. As a result, care for older adults is predominantly provided by unorganized caregivers, often family members who not only deliver care but also finance it out of pocket2. These caregivers operate with limited state support, as formal LTC services and financing structures are currently absent, and Ghana lacks a dedicated national LTC policy for older adults. As a result, caregivers bear both the emotional and financial burdens of care provision. Understanding their preferences for future LTC financing mechanisms is essential for designing equitable and context-sensitive policies, particularly in low- and middle-income settings where state capacity is constrained.

Globally, a variety of LTC financing models exist, including social insurance systems, tax-funded schemes, and means-tested public subsidies3,4. The adoption of these models often reflects institutional history, public preferences, and cultural values. In Ghana, however, formal LTC financing mechanisms are largely absent, leaving caregiving highly privatized and largely dependent on family support. This arrangement creates unequal caregiving experiences, particularly across income groups. Caregivers with lower incomes are often more vulnerable, as they typically lack financial reserves or adequate social protection to support their caregiving roles5.

Caregiving intensity, defined by the type and frequency of caregiving tasks performed, is an important determinant of caregiver burden and may also shape financing preferences6. Caregiving tasks vary substantially in their time, physical effort, and financial implications. For instance, personal care activities such as bathing and feeding often require significant time and physical effort, while tasks such as household support, mobility or errand support, and emotional support may involve different levels of commitment and resource demands7. Higher-intensity caregiving can increase financial and opportunity costs, which may encourage caregivers, particularly those with limited resources, to favor greater public support for long-term care.

In addition to caregiving intensity, beliefs about caregiving responsibility play a critical role in shaping attitudes toward LTC financing. In Ghana, caregiving has traditionally been viewed as a family responsibility, rooted in norms of filial obligation and intergenerational reciprocity8,9. However, these traditional expectations are increasingly being challenged by urbanization, labor migration, and changing economic conditions. While some caregivers continue to believe that families or care receivers should primarily bear the responsibility for care, others, particularly those experiencing financial strain, may be more supportive of government involvement in financing and providing long-term care services10.

The caregiver-care receiver relationship may also influence caregiving experiences and financing preferences. Caring for a spouse or parent often involves stronger emotional ties and greater care intensity compared to providing care for extended relatives or non-family members11. These relational dynamics may affect caregivers’ expectations regarding public support for long-term care. For example, caregivers providing intensive care to close family members may be more likely to support publicly financed LTC systems than those providing less intensive care to more distant relatives.

Despite growing evidence on long-term care needs and informal caregiving in Ghana, limited empirical research has examined how caregiving tasks, caregivers’ beliefs about responsibility, and socio-economic characteristics jointly shape preferences for LTC financing models. Understanding these factors is particularly important for caregivers who both provide and finance care, as their experiences may strongly influence their views on the need for formal financing mechanisms.

This study therefore, focuses on unorganized caregivers in Ghana who not only provide care but also directly finance the long-term care of older adults. Their out-of-pocket expenditures place them in a uniquely vulnerable position, making them a critical group for understanding the urgency and potential direction of LTC financing reforms.

Specifically, the study addresses the following research questions: How do the intensity and type of caregiving tasks influence caregivers’ preferences for family-based, insurance-based, and tax-financed LTC models? How do caregivers’ beliefs about caregiving responsibility shape these preferences? Do these associations vary by caregivers’ socioeconomic characteristics, particularly income levels and the caregiver-care receiver relationship? To answer these questions, the study employs multinomial regression models and interaction analyses to examine how caregiving experiences and socio-economic factors jointly shape LTC financing preferences within the Ghanaian context.

This study contributes to literature in several ways. First, it examines LTC financing preferences among dual-role caregivers who both provide and finance care, a group that remains understudied in Ghana. Second, it captures the multidimensional nature of caregiving by distinguishing between different caregiving tasks, including personal care, household support, mobility or errand support, and emotional support. Third, the study incorporates caregivers’ beliefs about responsibility for long-term care. By integrating these dimensions, the study provides new empirical evidence to inform policy discussions on sustainable LTC financing in Ghana and contributes to the growing international literature on caregiving and long-term care systems in low- and middle-income countries.

This study adopts a multidimensional and hypothesis-driven conceptual framework to explain LTC financing preferences among unorganized caregivers in Ghana. The framework is informed by welfare pluralism theory, which recognizes that care provision and financing may involve varying combinations of family, market, and state actors12. In contexts such as Ghana, where caregiving is predominantly family-based and culturally embedded, preferences for LTC financing are expected to reflect the interaction between caregiving burden, socio-cultural norms, relational dynamics, and economic capacity.

Figure 1 presents the conceptual framework guiding the analysis. The framework posits that caregivers’ preferences for long-term care financing models are influenced by several interconnected factors. These include the type of caregiving tasks performed, caregivers’ demographic and socio-economic characteristics, caregiving context (such as the caregiver-care receiver relationship and beliefs about responsibility for care) and self-reported caregiving outcomes such as negative health effects. Together, these factors shape caregivers’ preferences for different LTC financing arrangements, including family or recipient financing, insurance-based models, and tax-financed systems.

Conceptual framework for long-term care financing preferences among caregivers.

The framework illustrates how caregiving tasks (personal care, household support, mobility and errand support, and emotional support), demographic and socio-economic characteristics (age, gender, education, marital status, income, and employment), caregiving context (caregiver–care recipient relationship and beliefs about care responsibility), and caregiving outcomes (negative health effects) are associated with preferences for long-term care financing models, including family/out-of-pocket financing, insurance-based financing, and tax-financed systems.

At the core of the framework is the caregiver’s preference for one of three LTC financing models: family or out-of-pocket financing by the recipient, insurance-based financing and tax-financing. These options represent a continuum from informal to formalized systems of care financing.

Caregiving intensity is conceptualized through four domains: personal care, household support, mobility and errand support, and emotional support. These tasks differ in physical, emotional, and time demands, and therefore in the level of burden they impose. Recent literature suggests that more emotionally and physically demanding caregiving tasks may increase caregivers’ preference for formal financing mechanisms that provide financial relief, compensation, or access to professional care services13. Overall, the framework expects that higher caregiving intensity across domains reduces preference for purely family-based financing and increases support for formal LTC systems, though the direction and magnitude of effects may differ by task type.

In terms of demographic and socio-economic characteristics, age, gender, education, marital status, income, employment status, and locality are expected to influence preferences through access to resources, financial capacity, and exposure to formal systems. Prior research has shown that older adults may be less inclined toward insurance-based models due to trust issues or perceived lack of benefit, while women, who dominate informal caregiving roles, may prefer public or family-based financing due to economic vulnerability14,15. Education and income often positively influence support for formal financing through increased understanding and ability to pay15. Hence, higher income and education are expected to increase support for insurance-based financing while lower income caregivers may show greater preference for tax-financed systems due to affordability considerations.

The type of relationship between caregiver and care receiver shapes both emotional attachment and perceived obligation. It is hypothesized that caregivers supporting close relatives (e.g., parents or partners) will be more inclined toward family-based financing due to stronger relational and cultural expectations. Caregivers assisting more distant relatives may show greater openness to formal financing arrangements.

Beliefs regarding who should bear responsibility for care are central to financing preferences. In Ghana, strong filial expectations often position caregiving as a moral obligation of the family. The framework therefore, posits that stronger belief in family responsibility will decrease preference for tax-based financing. Caregivers who believe care should be shared between family and government will be more supportive of formal insurance or tax-funded systems.

Caregiving-related consequences, particularly health strain, may reinforce preferences for formal financing mechanisms. Caregivers experiencing negative health are expected to favor institutionalized or publicly supported LTC systems. Caregiving can have significant effects on caregivers’ own lives, particularly in terms of work productivity and health status16,17. These consequences may act as mediating factors, reinforcing the desire for formal support systems. For instance, caregivers who experience health deterioration or reduced work hours may be more likely to advocate for public financing or insurance-based LTC systems18.

Drawing on the welfare pluralism perspective, which emphasizes the shared roles of families, markets, and the state in welfare provision, this study expects that caregivers’ preferences for LTC financing models will vary according to their caregiving experiences and socio-economic positions. Caregivers providing high-intensity personal care are expected to show stronger support for insurance-based financing, as insurance mechanisms can help distribute the financial risks associated with intensive care needs. In contrast, caregivers who strongly believe that caregiving should remain a family responsibility, or those engaged primarily in routine household support tasks, are expected to show greater preference for family or out-of-pocket financing. Socioeconomic factors such as income and caregiver-care receiver relationship are also expected to shape these preferences by influencing both the capacity and willingness of caregivers to rely on market- or state-based financing arrangements. Overall, the framework proposes that LTC financing preferences are shaped by the interaction of socio-economic factors, relational proximity, beliefs, caregiving burden and health impact.

Caregivers who supported the insurance model provided the highest number of personal care hours (mean = 14.6 h), compared to those who preferred self/family (mean = 11.4 h) or tax-based models (mean = 11.4 h), with the differences reaching statistical significance. Emotional support hours were also significantly different across groups, with caregivers favoring tax-based financing reporting the highest levels.

In terms of age, caregivers who supported the insurance model were slightly younger on average (mean = 43.9 years) than those in the other groups (p = 0.021). Education level also varied significantly, with family/recipient financers more likely to have only basic education, while those supporting insurance or tax-based models had higher proportions of secondary or tertiary education. Furthermore, caregivers in the insurance group reported the lowest average personal income compared to the other groups.

Beliefs about caregiving responsibility were markedly different. An overwhelming majority (91.3%) of those preferring self/family financing viewed caregiving as a family obligation, while only 64.0% and 61.9% of insurance and tax-based supporters, respectively, shared this view. Correspondingly, the belief that caregiving is a responsibility of the government or others was more prevalent among those favoring the insurance and tax-based models.

Self-reported health impacts of caregiving also differed across financing preferences. Caregivers who supported the insurance model reported the most health-related challenges (31.6%) compared to those preferring self/family (20.3%) or tax-financing (22.3%). No significant differences were observed across the groups in terms of gender, marital status, employment status, locality (urban vs. rural), or relationship to the care receiver and those providing household support and mobility or errand support. The description of the variables used in the model is found in Table S3 in the supplementary information.

Preferences for Tax-Financing Model

Married/partnered caregivers were substantially more likely to prefer LTC insurance (dy/dx = 0.094, p = 0.001; RRR = 2.569, p = 0.001), as were divorced/separated/widowed caregivers (dy/dx = 0.050, p = 0.022; RRR = 2.213, p = 0.022), compared to never-married caregivers. Higher personal monthly income (log) reduced the likelihood of preferring LTC insurance (AME = –0.015, p = 0.010; RRR = 0.881, p = 0.010). Paid employment significantly increased preference for insurance (dy/dx = 0.112, p = 0.026; RRR = 2.359, p = 0.026). For care context and beliefs, those who believed that care is primarily the responsibility of families were significantly less likely to prefer LTC insurance (dy/dx = –0.087, p < 0.001; RRR = 0.187, p < 0.001).

Additionally, caregivers reporting negative health effects from caregiving were more likely to prefer insurance (dy/dx = 0.072, p = 0.020; RRR = 1.624, p = 0.020). The multinomial probit estimates were consistent in direction, magnitude, and statistical significance with the multinomial logit results, confirming the robustness of the findings.

Table 3 reports the multinomial logistic regression results for preference for tax-financing, using family/recipient financing as the reference category. Providing more household support reduced the probability of preferring tax-funded financing by about 7.0 percentage points (dy/dx = −0.070, p = 0.005; RRR = 0.664, p = 0.005). In contrast, providing more emotional support significantly increased the probability of preferring tax-based financing by approximately 7.8 percentage points (dy/dx = 0.078, p < 0.001; RRR = 1.501, p < 0.001).

Personal care and LTC financing preferences by income, beliefs, and relationship

Figure S1 presents the marginal effects of personal care on preferences for LTC insurance and tax-based financing models, moderated by income level, belief in care responsibility, and caregiver-care receiver relationships. As shown in Fig. S1a, as the logged personal care hours increased, higher-income caregivers were more likely to prefer LTC insurance.

Figure S1b showed both low- and high-income caregivers exhibiting a lower likelihood of preferring tax-based financing as personal care intensity increases. Among caregivers who believed that caregiving is primarily the responsibility of the government or external actors or family members, higher logged personal care hours were associated with a higher probability of preferring LTC insurance, particularly among those in higher income brackets as shown in Fig. S1c. Significant results also indicate that caregivers providing personal care to parents were more likely to prefer the LTC insurance model as income increases as shown in Fig. S1e.

Household support and LTC financing preferences by income, beliefs, and relationship

Figure S2 presents the marginal effects of household intensity on preferences for LTC financing models, highlighting notable differences across income levels, beliefs in care responsibility, and the caregiver-care receiver relationship.

For low- and high-income caregivers, greater household support was associated with a lower likelihood of preferring tax-based financing as shown in Fig. S2b. However, this negative effect was stronger and statistically significant among low-income earners. Figure S2c demonstrated that caregivers’ beliefs about caregiving responsibility significantly moderate the relationship between household support and preferences for tax-based financing.

Caregivers who believe caregiving is primarily a family responsibility, increased household support intensity was associated with a negative marginal effect on preference for tax financing as shown in Fig. S2d. Among caregivers providing support to parents, an increase in household support intensity was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of preferring the tax-based financing model as shown in Fig. S2f.

Mobility support and LTC financing preferences by income, beliefs, and relationship

From Fig. S3a, as the intensity of mobility and errand support increases, caregivers in the very low-income group showed a negative marginal effect on preference for the LTC insurance model.

Figure S3c and S3d, based on estimates from Model 3 in Tables S4 and S6 respectively, further indicated that among caregivers who believe caregiving is primarily the responsibility of the government or other external actors, increasing mobility and errand support were associated with a lower probability of preferring LTC insurance, particularly at very low-income levels.

Figure S3e and S3f (based on estimates from Model 3 in Tables S6 and S7) showed that increased mobility and errand support were negatively associated with preference for the LTC insurance model among lower-income caregivers. In contrast, a positive marginal effect of low income on preference for tax-based financing was observed. However, these effects were not statistically significant across the models.

Emotional support and LTC financing preferences by income, beliefs, and relationship

Figure S4 illustrated the marginal effects of emotional support intensity on LTC financing preferences, moderated by income level, beliefs, and caregiver-care receiver relationship. Figure S4a (based on estimates from Model 4 in Table S4) and Fig. S4b (based on Model 3 in Table S5) showed that the marginal effects of emotional support on preferences for LTC insurance and tax-based financing models varied across income levels. Notably, the results indicate that an increase in emotional support was significantly associated with a greater likelihood of preferring tax-based financing across all income levels.

Similarly, Fig. S4c (based on estimates from Model 4 in Table S6) and Fig. S4d (from Model 4 in Table S7) demonstrated that caregivers’ cultural beliefs about emotional caregiving responsibilities significantly moderate the relationship between emotional support intensity and LTC financing preferences. Among caregivers who viewed caregiving as primarily the responsibility of the government or other external actors, increased emotional support intensity was positively associated with preference for tax-based financing, with the effect becoming stronger as income increased. Conversely, among those who believed caregiving is a family responsibility, the marginal effect of emotional support on tax-financing preferences was also positive, but the effect diminished with rising income.

From Fig. S4e and S4f, based on estimates from Model 4 in Tables S8 and S9 respectively, the effect of emotional support intensity also varied by the caregiver’s relationship to the care receiver. Significant results indicate that caregivers providing emotional support to parents consistently preferred tax-financing across all income levels.

Discussion

This study examined preferences for LTC financing models among unorganized caregivers in Ghana who simultaneously provide and finance care for older adults. It explored how these preferences vary with the intensity of caregiving tasks, personal care, household support, mobility or errand support, and emotional support, and how caregiver beliefs, relationships with care recipients, and income levels shape these preferences. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships, as reverse causality and unobserved factors cannot be ruled out.

A key finding of this study is that caregivers who provided more intensive personal care were more likely to prefer insurance-based LTC financing. Personal care tasks, such as bathing, feeding, and assisting with daily living, are typically the most physically and emotionally demanding aspects of caregiving. In the Ghanaian context, where formal long-term care systems remain limited and families bear most caregiving responsibilities, caregivers providing intensive personal care may view insurance-based mechanisms as a potential strategy to mitigate the financial risks associated with prolonged care needs.

This finding aligns with evidence from recent Ghanaian studies showing that intensive caregiving, particularly in cases of dementia, can lead to significant financial strain and emotional burnout19. Insurance-based models may therefore be perceived as a way to spread the financial burden of care across a broader risk pool. Interaction results further suggest that this association was stronger among higher-income caregivers. This may reflect the higher opportunity costs of caregiving among wealthier individuals as well as their greater financial capacity to contribute to insurance premiums. Prior studies have similarly shown that private insurance uptake in Ghana tends to be concentrated among wealthier populations who are better positioned to navigate formal financial systems1.

Interestingly, caregivers who reported health deterioration due to caregiving were also more likely to prefer LTC insurance. This supports previous findings that caregivers experiencing physical or mental health decline may be more inclined to seek financial protection mechanisms, such as insurance, to mitigate the long-term implications of caregiving burdens5.

In contrast, caregivers who spent more time providing household support were less likely to prefer tax-financed LTC models. Household support tasks, including cooking, cleaning, and other domestic chores, are often culturally normalized in Ghana and may not be perceived as burdensome enough to justify formal public intervention.

This finding is consistent with earlier research suggesting that domestic caregiving tasks are strongly embedded within traditional gender roles, particularly among women (Mba, 2010)20. As a result, these activities may be viewed as routine household obligations rather than forms of care requiring institutional support. Moreover, a study using WHO SAGE data shows that poor living conditions, including unimproved cooking and sanitation facilities, are associated with increased LTC needs in Ghana21. Despite these structural challenges, caregivers may still perceive household tasks as part of ordinary family life rather than as care responsibilities warranting public financing.

The interaction analysis further showed that both low- and high-income caregivers were less likely to support tax-based LTC financing as their personal and household caregiving intensity increased, with the effect being stronger among low-income caregivers. This may reflect broader concerns about the effectiveness and reliability of public welfare programs in Ghana, where low tax compliance and perceptions of inefficiency in government service delivery often shape attitudes toward publicly funded programs22.

Another important finding is that caregivers who provided more emotional support were more likely to prefer tax-financed LTC models. Emotional caregiving, such as companionship, reassurance, and psychological support, is often less visible than physical caregiving but plays a crucial role in maintaining the well-being of older adults.

In Ghana’s extended family system, emotional caregiving is culturally expected but frequently underrecognized in formal policy discussions. As caregivers face increasing social and economic pressures, publicly funded support mechanisms may be perceived as necessary to sustain long-term caregiving roles. This is particularly relevant given the limited availability of mental health and psychosocial support services in Ghana23.

Interaction results provide further insight into this pattern. Caregivers engaged in high levels of emotional support expressed stronger support for tax-financed LTC regardless of income level, suggesting that emotional caregiving may be widely perceived as a collective societal concern. Among caregivers who believed that caregiving should be the government’s responsibility, support for tax-financed models increased markedly with emotional caregiving burden, particularly among higher-income caregivers. This finding echoes international evidence that emotional caregiving demands are associated with increased use of publicly funded services24.

Notably, caregivers providing emotional support to parents consistently favored tax-financed LTC regardless of income. Filial caregiving relationships are often characterized by strong emotional bonds and heightened psychological strain25. In Ghana, where life expectancy is increasing and formal psychosocial support services remain limited, adult children caring for ageing parents may be particularly supportive of state involvement when emotional caregiving becomes prolonged or demanding26,27.

Caregivers’ beliefs about caregiving responsibility emerged as a key factor shaping financing preferences. Caregivers who believed that caregiving should remain primarily a family responsibility were consistently less supportive of both insurance- and tax-financed LTC models. This finding reflects the strong cultural norm of familialism in Ghana, where caring for older relatives is often considered a moral and kinship obligation28.

However, these beliefs interacted with socioeconomic circumstances in important ways. Among caregivers who believed in family responsibility but had higher incomes, increasing personal care intensity was associated with greater support for insurance-based financing. This suggests that some caregivers may seek ways to reconcile cultural expectations with the financial realities of intensive caregiving by supporting financing mechanisms that complement rather than replace family care. Policy efforts aimed at developing long-term care financing mechanisms in Ghana should be sensitive to prevailing beliefs surrounding family responsibility. Communication strategies and co-designed interventions with communities could frame LTC insurance not as a replacement for family care but as a complementary mechanism that enables families to sustain caregiving responsibilities and honor older relatives by ensuring reliable and dignified care.

The caregiver-recipient relationship also played an important role in shaping financing preferences. Caregivers supporting parents or spouses were more likely to prefer insurance-based LTC financing, possibly reflecting both the intensity of these relationships and the long-term financial implications of caring for close family members. While caring for elderly parents remains a deeply embedded cultural expectation in Ghana, growing financial and emotional pressures on middle-aged caregivers may be increasing openness to formal support mechanisms29.

Finally, several demographic factors also influenced financing preferences. Age was negatively associated with preference for insurance-based LTC models, possibly reflecting reduced perceived benefits from long-term financial instruments among older caregivers and stronger adherence to traditional family-based care arrangements. Marital status was positively associated with preference for LTC insurance, perhaps reflecting greater long-term financial planning among married individuals and the desire to protect spouses from caregiving burdens in later life30.

Contrary to expectations, higher income was associated with a lower overall preference for LTC insurance. One possible explanation is that wealthier households may rely more on privately arranged care, such as paid domestic workers or other informal arrangements, thereby reducing perceived reliance on pooled-risk financing systems. However, the present dataset does not include variables capturing the use of paid care services or broader measures of household wealth, limiting the ability to empirically test these mechanisms. Future research should therefore examine how private care arrangements, wealth, and insurance literacy shape LTC financing preferences in Ghana.

Employment status, on the other hand, was positively associated with preference for insurance-based LTC financing. This may reflect higher opportunity costs among working caregivers who must balance employment with caregiving responsibilities. Familiarity with insurance mechanisms through formal employment benefits or pension systems may also make insurance-based LTC financing more attractive to employed caregivers31.

The findings also contribute to emerging discussions on the care economy in Ghana. Unorganized caregivers provide substantial unpaid labor that supports the functioning of health and social systems, yet this contribution remains largely unrecognized in policy and financing frameworks. The observed associations between caregiving intensity and financing preferences highlight the economic pressures experienced by caregivers who simultaneously provide and finance care. As Ghana’s population ages, strengthening LTC financing mechanisms will be critical not only for improving the well-being of older adults but also for sustaining informal caregiving within the broader care economy.

The predominance of female caregivers in the sample reflects the gendered nature of caregiving in Ghana and many other low- and middle-income countries. Women continue to shoulder a disproportionate share of unpaid caregiving responsibilities, often balancing these roles with income-generating activities and other household duties. This imbalance has important implications for labor market participation, financial security, and well-being. Policies aimed at strengthening long-term care systems in Ghana should therefore adopt gender-sensitive approaches that recognize and support the contributions of women caregivers while addressing the unequal distribution of unpaid care work.

Although this study focuses on Ghana, the findings also contribute to broader global discussions on long-term care financing in low- and middle-income countries, where rapid population ageing is occurring in contexts characterized by limited formal care systems and strong reliance on family caregiving.

The findings carry several implications for the development of long-term care systems in Ghana. First, the association between intensive personal caregiving and support for insurance-based financing suggests that caregivers facing higher care demands may be more receptive to contributory LTC insurance schemes. Second, the role of cultural beliefs about family responsibility highlights the need for policy approaches that complement rather than replace existing familial caregiving traditions. Finally, the observed income differences in financing preferences suggest that equitable LTC systems may require mixed financing models that combine contributory insurance mechanisms with targeted public support for lower-income households.

These findings should also be interpreted within Ghana’s broader social protection landscape. Existing schemes such as the National Health Insurance Scheme and pension programmes administered by the Social Security and National Insurance Trust provide important foundations for financial protection in health and old age but do not currently cover long-term care needs. As Ghana’s population ages, integrating LTC financing mechanisms within these existing structures could provide a feasible policy pathway. For instance, contributory LTC insurance schemes could potentially be linked to existing social insurance platforms, while tax-funded community care services could complement current social protection programmes targeting vulnerable older adults.

This study employed a quantitative design to assess LTC financing preferences among caregivers in Ghana, which represents one of its key strengths. The approach enhanced generalizability across a broad sample, enabled subgroup comparisons, and generated policy-relevant quantitative evidence. However, one limitation is that the dependent variable measuring LTC financing preference was based on a single survey item with three response options (family/self, LTC insurance, and tax financing), which may limit measurement precision. In particular, the category “LTC insurance (private and social)” combines conceptually distinct financing arrangements that respondents may have interpreted differently, potentially introducing heterogeneity in responses. Although brief explanations of financing options were provided, they may not have fully standardized respondents’ understanding of key features such as premiums, eligibility, or coverage scope. Consequently, variation in interpretation could affect response validity and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Another limitation is the absence of a qualitative component, which restricts the ability to explore the deeper motivations, beliefs, and meanings underlying these preferences. As a result, the study provides limited insight into the contextual and experiential perspectives of caregivers and care recipients. Future research should adopt mixed-method approaches that integrate both quantitative and qualitative techniques to provide a more comprehensive understanding of LTC financing preferences.

Additionally, the study did not include variables capturing the use of paid domestic help, household wealth, or respondents’ understanding of long-term care insurance. This limits the ability to examine potential mechanisms underlying the observed income-related differences in financing preferences. Furthermore, the study focused exclusively on caregivers who both provided hands-on care and contributed financially to care expenses. By excluding caregivers who provide only time-based support, the study narrows its generalizability. Including this group might yield different insights; for example, time-only caregivers may prioritize respite services or care provision rather than financing mechanisms. As such, the findings primarily reflect the perspectives of dual-role caregivers, and future research should incorporate diverse caregiver types to provide a more comprehensive picture of LTC financing preferences.

While the findings provide important insights into caregivers’ preferences for long-term care financing in Ghana, they should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships, given the cross-sectional nature of the data. The policy implications should therefore be viewed as indicative rather than definitive and considered alongside the study’s limitations. Future research should build on these findings through mixed-methods studies that explore the motivations and contextual factors shaping caregivers’ financing preferences. Also, key variables such as caregiving hours and health impacts were self-reported and may therefore be subject to recall or reporting bias. In addition, discrete choice experiments could help assess caregivers’ willingness to pay for different long-term care financing options, while pilot testing alternative LTC financing models would provide valuable evidence on the feasibility and acceptability of such schemes in Ghana.

Overall, the findings point to the potential value of a mixed LTC financing model that combines contributory insurance mechanisms with targeted public support while also strengthening caregiver support programmes and community-based services to ensure sustainable care provision in the context of population ageing.

In considering the development of formalized LTC financing in the future, these findings suggest several practical policy directions for strengthening long-term care systems in Ghana. First, policymakers could consider piloting contributory LTC insurance schemes targeting caregivers who simultaneously provide and finance care for older adults. Second, caregiver education on available LTC financing options could be embedded within community health programmes to improve awareness and understanding of financing mechanisms. Finally, including caregiver representatives in the design and development of LTC policies could help ensure that emerging financing models reflect the lived experiences and needs of those most directly involved in caregiving.

Methods

Study design and selection of participants

The study design and participant selection followed those described in Offei et al.32 The data for this study were collected from Greater Accra, Ashanti, and Northern regions of Ghana from July to August 2023. These regions were purposively selected to reflect Ghana’s geographic and socioeconomic diversity. Greater Accra represents a highly urbanized setting with better access to formal services and infrastructure. Ashanti Region, a mix of urban and peri-urban areas, captures middle-income characteristics and dense population dynamics. The Northern Region reflects a predominantly rural context, marked by lower income levels and limited access to services.

The sampling frame was drawn from the 2021 Population and Housing Census conducted by the Ghana Statistical Service and consisted of households with at least one adult aged 60 years or older who reported functional difficulties in areas such as vision, hearing, mobility, cognition, self-care, or speech. An Enumeration Area (EA) is defined as a geographic unit averaging about 750 households, with urban EAs generally larger than rural ones33. A stratified two-stage cluster sampling design was employed. Each region was first divided into urban and rural strata, yielding six strata in total. Within these strata, 200 EAs (143 urban and 57 rural) were selected proportionally as primary sampling units. Household listings were then conducted in each selected EA, from which 10 households were randomly chosen as secondary sampling units, producing an initial sample of 2000 households. This allocation balanced statistical representativeness with fieldwork feasibility.

The study achieved a 99.5% response rate, resulting in 1990 participating households. Non-response was minimal and mainly due to temporary absence or refusal; no replacements were made, and analyses were based on completed interviews only. The responding households included 1990 older adults and their corresponding caregivers aged 18 years or older. The analysis focused on dual-role caregivers, those who both provided care and contributed financially. Caregivers were classified as eligible if they provided regular assistance with daily living activities and directly supported health or long-term care expenses. These contributions were identified through caregiver self-reports during the survey. Applying these criteria yielded a final analytic sample of 1116 caregivers.

Sampling weights were constructed to account for differential selection probabilities across strata and to ensure representativeness. All analyses used weighted data. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethical and Protocol Review Committee of the College of Health Sciences, University of Ghana (Approval No. CHS-Et/M.11-P4.10/2022-2023). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Dependent variable

LTC financing preference was a categorical variable derived from the question, “Care for older people with physical or mental disabilities or problems related to ageing comes at a cost. In your opinion, how should the cost of caregiving ideally be covered financially?” The responses were “solely by the care receivers and/or their families(coded as 1),” “LTC insurance model(coded as 2),” and “a government package of LTC funded through tax-financing(coded as 3). Family/care receiver financing was used as the reference category in the estimation.

Independent variables

Age was treated as a continuous variable. Gender was categorized and coded as “1” for females and “0” for males. Educational attainment was coded as “0” for no formal education, “1” for primary or Junior High School (JHS), and “2” for Senior High School, vocational training, or tertiary education. Marital status was grouped into three categories: never married (coded as 0), married or partnered (coded as 1), and divorced, separated, or widowed (coded as 2). The personal monthly income variable was derived from the income of caregivers for a month and treated as a continuous variable. Personal monthly income was included in the regression models in log-transformed form to address right-skewness and reduce the influence of outliers. In addition, as part of robustness analyses, income was classified into sample-based quintiles: Very Low (1st quintile), Low (2nd), Moderate (3rd), High (4th), and Very High (5th). Employment was categorical, with those unemployed coded as 0, self-employed (coded as 1) and salaried workers (coded as 2). The locality was coded as 0 for living in a rural community and 1 for urban dwellers.

The relationship variable was categorized as non-family (coded as 0), partner (coded as 1), parent (coded as 2) and extended family (coded as 3). The question, “In your opinion, who should be mainly responsible for ensuring that needed care is available?” was used to assess respondents’ beliefs about who holds primary responsibility for care for older adults. Response options included government and others, such as NGOs (coded as 0) and family members, both immediate and extended (coded as 1).

The caregiving tasks variables were grouped into four and measured as a continuous variable. The personal care variable captured the total weekly hours devoted to helping with personal care activities, including dressing, bathing, grooming, toileting, eating, drinking, and administering medication. Household support was the number of hours per week spent providing practical household assistance, such as cooking, cleaning, ironing, and managing financial responsibilities like paying bills or overseeing money-related tasks with their resources.

Mobility and errand support measured the time spent each week supporting the care receiver with mobility-related needs, such as walking, using mobility aids, moving around the home or outdoors, attending medical appointments, visiting relatives, shopping, and completing other errands. Emotional support measured the weekly hours dedicated to offering emotional care, including providing companionship, checking on the well-being of the care receiver, supporting them in managing pain, disability, or emotional distress, and spending meaningful time together or with family members. Caregiving task variables were measured in hours per week and log-transformed due to their skewed distribution, with a constant added before transformation to accommodate zero values.

The subjective self-reported outcome was health implications because of caregiving. Health implication was coded as “0” for respondents who reported no impact on their health and “1” if otherwise.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 16. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sample characteristics, with means and standard deviations reported for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. No missing data were observed for any variables included in the analysis, therefore, all models were estimated using the full analytic sample without imputation or casewise deletion. Chi-square tests were conducted to examine the association between categorical variables and LTC financing model preferences. Differences in continuous variables across LTC financing preference groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for variables that approximately satisfied normality assumptions. For variables with skewed distributions, specifically mobility/errand support hours, emotional support hours, and personal monthly income, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed as a non-parametric alternative.

To examine factors associated with preferences for LTC financing models, a multinomial logistic regression model with robust standard errors was employed to account for potential heteroskedasticity, and average marginal effects were reported. The three options (family/self-financing, insurance-based financing, and tax financing) do not represent a strict or universally agreed ranking in terms of preference or progression, and respondents were not asked to evaluate them along an ordered scale. Because no natural or empirically defensible ordering could be assumed, a multinomial logistic regression model was considered more appropriate. This approach also enabled the simultaneous comparison of multiple financing options and the assessment of how caregiving task intensity, caregiving responsibility beliefs, caregiver-recipient relationship, and income jointly influenced preferences. As a robustness check, a multinomial probit model was also estimated. Statistical significance was evaluated at the 5% and 1% levels.

The Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) assumption underlying the multinomial logit model was tested using Hausman, SUEST-based Hausman, and Small-Hsiao procedures. The conventional Hausman test yielded negative chi-square values, indicating violation of asymptotic assumptions and thus unreliable estimates; interpretation therefore relied on the more robust SUEST-based and Small-Hsiao tests. Results from both tests as shown in Table S1 and S2 showed no statistically significant evidence of IIA violations across outcome categories (all p > 0.05), supporting the suitability of the multinomial logit specification for modeling caregivers’ financing preferences.
