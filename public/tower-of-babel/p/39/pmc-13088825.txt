A latent class analysis of public opinions about gambling policy goals: a population-based survey in Finland
Authors: Kalle Lind, Susanna Raisamo, Jani Selin, Jukka Kontto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsThe significance of a reciprocal link between public opinion and policy making is widely acknowledged in literature. The aim of this study is to explore public opinion on gambling policy by identifying distinct latent classes of attitudes towards policy goals. The findings can provide insights into future gambling regulation and policy making.MethodsData were derived from a 2023 population-based Finnish Gambling Study (n = 5977) representing the population aged 15-74-year-olds. A set of questions regarding gambling policy attitudes were measured. By using a latent class analysis (LCA) we identified classes of individuals with regard to their respond patterns towards gambling policy questions, considering age, gender, gambling frequency and problem gambling status. Logistic regression was then used to predict class membership by demographic background, gambling behavior, and attitude towards gambling.ResultsHarm prevention, crime reduction, and control of unauthorized operators were the most endorsed gambling policy goals. Women, non-gamblers, and those affected by others’ gambling more often supported harm reduction and marketing restrictions, while men and recent gamblers prioritized consumer protection, player choice, and competition. Latent class analysis identified three groups: Traditionalists (54.8%), Harm-reducers (32.5%), and Libertarians (12.7%). Compared to Traditionalists, Harm-reducers were more likely to be younger and non-gamblers, while Libertarians were typically male, offshore, with at-risk/problem gambling, and held more pro-gambling attitudes.ConclusionsMost respondents supported harm-reduction or even stricter gambling policies, while only a minority – mainly younger males, offshore gamblers, and those with at-risk or problem gambling behavior – favored liberalization and increased consumer choice. This reveals a clear mismatch between public preferences and current policy trends in Finland, which are moving toward more liberal gambling policy. Attitudes were closely tied to personal gambling behavior, with younger non-gamblers supporting gambling reduction and older gamblers without harms backing stronger regulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26755-0.Keywords: public opinion, gambling, gambling policy, latent class analysis (LCA)

Introduction

The significance of a reciprocal link between public opinion and policy making is well known in the literature (1, 2); public opinions set a boundary within which policy is formulated, and in turn, a change in policy may also bring public opinion along with it (3–5).

Within a public health frame, research has mainly addressed on public opinions towards regulations and policies that aim to promote people’s health and well-being, such as tobacco, alcohol and drugs control policies (6–9), nutrition and preventive obesity regulations (6, 10, 11). Studies have typically found stronger support for least effective policy measures such as education campaigns and treating interventions, and modest support for the most effective regulation that control, for example, the physical availability and accessibility of the products (6). In general, opinions and attitudes are shown to differ across population segments, depending upon, for example, people’s socio-demographic features and individual behaviors (12–14). Regarding behavioral patterns, it has been established that people tend to support interventions that are unlikely to affect their own behavior. Individuals with lower levels of participation or exposure typically show stronger support for restrictive measures, whereas those with higher involvement or experiencing greater harm are more likely to oppose them (6, 14).

When it comes to gambling research field, earlier studies have mostly focused on prevalence, gambling behavior patterns and other correlates of public attitudes towards gambling in general (e.g. (15, 16), . Although attitudes towards specific gambling policy measures such as monetary deposit limit setting have been explored (17, 18), there is paucity of detailed quantitative research on attitudes and views toward the broader existing gambling policy and regulatory measures at the whole population level (4, 14, 16). In qualitative studies, Finnish gamblers have been generally content with their system (19), but also see contradictions in its goals, questioning whether it prevents harm or promotes gambling (20).

Public attitudes toward gambling policies, such as restrictions or increased regulation, are strongly influenced by perceptions of gambling-related harm. However, findings across studies are somewhat contradictory, possibly reflecting the cognitive dissonance arising from the conflicting goals of gambling policy: harm prevention and revenue generation (21). In an Australian study in which people experiencing gambling harm (PGSI 1+) were overrepresented in the sample, it was found that more restrictive advertisement and electronic gambling machine (EGM) regulations were supported by the respondents (22). In a Finnish study it was found that among people with problematic gambling behavior more restrictive policies had less support when compared to other gamblers and non-gamblers (14). Yet, the researchers also found that EGM gamblers were more likely to oppose the monopoly model in use in Finland, advertisement restrictions, and the reduction of availability and accessibility of EGMs.

In a Canadian study, citizens generally accepted gambling as a legitimate recreational activity yet expressed concern about its social consequences and the government’s conflicted role as both regulator and beneficiary of gambling revenues (23). Similarly, Australian research found that individuals more exposed to gambling-related harms tended to favor stronger preventive and regulatory measures (24). In contrast, a UK study reported that respondents with higher PGSI scores and a greater number of active online gambling accounts were less supportive of proposed financial risk assessments and vulnerability checks (25). While individuals with problem gambling may recognize the risks associated with gambling, their lower support for restrictive measures may stem from a perceived threat to their autonomy.

Overall, the literature indicates that population groups differ in their opinions toward gambling policy, ranging from support to opposition, with these differences often associated with gambling participation, problem gambling status, and socio-demographic characteristics. This pattern is consistent with the broader public health literature on attitudes toward regulatory interventions. Unlike in alcohol and tobacco research, however, there is one attempt to use a scale for measuring general attitudes toward gambling policy: general attitude measures can account for a substantial share of variance in policy-related outcomes (26). Moreover, to our knowledge, the public health literature does not offer established conceptual typologies for grouping populations based on policy opinions or attitudes. Instead, it is common to operationalize policy attitudes along a presupposed continuum from opposition to support, with population segments, such as supporters and opponents, typically identified inductively. The present study follows this prevailing approach, while placing particular emphasis on opinions on specific gambling policy measures and aims.

Our study strengthens the current literature of public opinions towards gambling by examining more thoroughly the public opinions of regulation framework (3, 4, 14, 16, 27). Specifically, this study explores public opinion on gambling policy by identifying distinct latent classes of attitudes towards policy goals. It also examines how demographic factors (age, gender, employment status), gambling behaviors, and attitudes towards gambling predict class membership using logistic regression models. This approach provides more nuanced insights into the diversity of public opinion on gambling regulation and its determinants than previous studies.

Data and methods

Finnish gambling study

The Finnish Gambling Study 2023 examines gambling behaviors, gambling-related problems, and attitudes towards gambling among Finns aged 15–74 living in mainland Finland. Conducted by the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (THL) and commissioned by the Ministry of Social Affairs and Health under the Lotteries Act (§ 52), the study highlights key trends and factors influencing gambling in Finland. The questionnaire covered themes including attitudes and opinions about gambling, gambling behaviors, personal relationship with gambling, gambling by close family and friends, digital gaming, items on health and well-being as well as background demographics.

The target population represents 64.4% of the total population (3,610,339 individuals out of 5,608,881, according to Statistics Finland on November 22, 2023). Individuals living in institutions, those experiencing homelessness, expatriates (including residents of Åland), and individuals whose primary language was not Finnish, Swedish, or Sámi, were excluded from the study.

The data collection incorporated a mixed-method approach, combining online and postal surveys. A random sample of 16,250 individuals was drawn, yielding 5,977 responses, corresponding to a response rate of 36.9%. The overall response rate reflects a combination of web and postal surveys (response rate 35.7%) and a supplementary telephone survey (response rate 50.7% after weighting adjustments). Both the web and postal questionnaire was pretested at Statistics Finland and THL, with target response rates set at > 35% for web/post surveys and > 50% for telephone interviews. Historically, response rates in the Finnish Gambling Study have ranged from 25.0% to 61.9% across survey years.

The data were weighted to allow generalization to the Finnish adult population. Weighting accounted for age, sex, region, and urban–rural classification. Each respondent received a sampling weight and an analysis weight to ensure estimates reflect the target population (N = 3,610,339; Statistics Finland, November 2023). The weights were derived using the CALMAR2 calibration method, aligning the distributions of selected variables in the sample with those in the target population, thereby reducing bias from nonresponse and improving estimation efficiency. All percentages and analyses reported in the manuscript are based on these analysis weights.

In the 2023 survey, non-response occurred in both web/post and telephone components. In the web/post survey, 9,628 individuals did not respond, with reasons known for only a small proportion; 222 surveys were incomplete, 64 individuals explicitly refused, and 23 were duplicates. In the telephone survey, 610 individuals did not respond, including 238 refusals and 217 unreachable cases. Some sampled individuals no longer belonged to the target population. While non-response may affect prevalence estimates, the use of administrative register data and weighting for age, sex, region, and urban–rural classification reduces potential bias, as previously demonstrated in Finnish Gambling Surveys (e.g., 2016, [28]). Non-response continues to be monitored and analyzed to ensure reliable population-level estimates.

Missing data were not replaced with multiple imputation. For indicator creation and to retain the maximum number of respondents in the models, missing values were defaulted to zero, which may lead to slight underestimation of results. For categorical variables (except past-year gambling participation), missing values were handled using most frequent category imputation, where missing entries were replaced with the most common value (“no” in our study). We acknowledge that this single imputation method may slightly distort distributions and relationships, but it allowed inclusion of the full sample in analyses.

The study received ethical approval from THL’s Research Ethics Committee (THL/1700/6.02.01/2023). Statistical analyses were performed primarily using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27), with some graphics and verification runs conducted using R (version 4.3.3). The dataset (FSD3917) used in this study is publicly accessible through the Finnish Social Science Data Archive (FSD; www.fsd.tuni.fi/en/).

Gambling severity

Since 2023, gambling severity in Finland has been assessed exclusively using the Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI) (29). PGSI is a nine-item scale designed for measuring various levels of gambling behaviors and associated harms during the previous 12 months. It provides a more precise classification than earlier tools, such as the South Oaks Gambling Screen (SOGS), particularly when using the threshold of eight points to identify severe gambling problems (13). This standardized approach ensures consistency in evaluating gambling-related harm across studies.

The following cut-offs were used: non-gambling, non-problem gambling (score = 0), low-risk (1–2), moderate-risk (3–7), and problem gambling (≥ 8). The non-gambling category included individuals who reported no gambling activity in response to the question, “Think about the past 12 months, how often did you gamble?”. Due to the small size of the subgroups, PGSI scores were grouped into three categories for the logistic regression models: 0, 1–2, and 3 or more.

Attitudes Toward Gambling Scale (ATGS)

Attitudes Toward Gambling Scale (ATGS (30, 31), was used to measure gambling attitudes, with higher scores reflecting more favorable views toward gambling.

Respondents were asked to indicate how much they agreed or disagreed with eight statements related to gambling. The response scale ranged from 1 to 5 (1 = completely agree, 5 = completely disagree). A total score was calculated based on the responses, with a possible range from 8 to 40 points. Scores were only computed for respondents who answered all eight questions. The cutoff for negative and positive attitudes is set at 24 points: a score below 24 indicates a negative attitude, 24 represents a neutral attitude, and a score above 24 indicates a positive attitude.

Data analysis

First, descriptive information of the study variables and their bivariate relationships was assessed by cross-tabulations. Statistical significance was tested using Pearson’s chi-squared tests (p-value < 0.001). Latent class analysis (LCA; (32, 33) with three-step maximum likelihood approach (34) was used to identify groups with distinct response patterns regarding the main purposes of Finland’s gambling policy. LCA detects latent groups based on observed categorical variables (“manifest variables”) and assumes independence of these variables within each group (35).

The optimal number of latent classes was determined by the researchers using goodness-of-fit statistics (AIC and BIC values) and previous research. Researchers interpret the meaning of each class and the distribution of manifest variables. The poLCA R package (35) was used in SPSS and R to fit LCA models with 3 to 6 classes. Models were built using dichotomous indicators for different gambling policy purposes.

Logistic regression assessed the relationship between each predictor and class membership probability. For categorical predictors, odds ratios (ORs and 95% confidence intervals) indicate the likelihood of belonging to a specific class compared to others. For example, in a three-class model, the OR for “Class 1” represents the odds of females being in Class 1 versus Classes 2 or 3. For ATGS, the OR reflects the change in odds per standard deviation increase in the predictor.

Those who had gambled during the previous 12 months (see Supplementary Table S5) were more likely to highlight consumer protection, preventing gambling on unauthorized operators, channeling gambling to authorized markets, generating state revenue and increasing freedom of choice and competition. Non-gamblers tended to favor restricting gambling marketing and reducing gambling in general.

Respondents with a PGSI score of three or more shared similar opinions with non-gamblers about reducing gambling and restricting gambling marketing. Offshore gamblers (see Appendix 1) were more likely than non-offshore gamblers to favor increased freedom of choice and competition between operators. Those affected by another person’s gambling leaned toward restricting marketing and reducing gambling more than those not affected.

The survey also included an open-ended question to elaborate about “Something else”. These answers consisted of opinions about how gambling-profits should be used and to which societal efforts and initiatives they should be targeted to, as well as monopoly and gambling-research related criticism.

Latent class analysis

A three-class solution was selected based on model diagnostics (AIC = 59,813.66; BIC = 60,027.77; entropy = 0.69), indicating acceptable model fit and classification certainty. The latent classes were labelled Traditionalists (54.8%), Harm-reducers (32.5%), and Libertarians (12.7%) based on response profiles across regulatory attitudes (Fig. 1). These classes differed in their emphasis on public health, consumer protection, and individual freedom in relation to gambling regulation. The largest class, traditionalists, comprising 54.8% of respondents, prioritized channeling gambling to domestic markets, preventing unauthorized operations, reducing crime, generating state revenue, and mitigating gambling-related harm. The second class, harm-reducers, representing 32.5% of respondents, placed the greatest emphasis on reducing overall gambling participation and preventing associated harm, while also supporting restrictions on unauthorized operators and gambling-related marketing. The smallest class, libertarians, accounting for 12.7% of respondents, focused on increasing players’ freedom of choice and fostering competition among gambling operators, alongside other regulatory objectives.

Latent class analysis

Classification quality was acceptable, with strong alignment between latent and assigned classes (Appendix Table S9). Most individuals were correctly assigned to their latent class, particularly Class 1 (Traditionalists, 97.3%) and Class 2 (Harm-reducers, 84.5%). Class 3 (Libertarians) showed slightly lower classification accuracy (72.8%). Compared to the reference group (Traditionalists), Harm-reducers were more likely to be assigned to their latent class, whereas Libertarians showed the highest separation from both other classes, indicating distinct attitudinal patterns after adjusting for misclassification.

Bias-adjusted multinomial logistic regression was applied using transformed probabilities to account for classification uncertainty. Multinomial logistic regression examined predictors of latent class membership, using Traditionalists (Class 1) as the reference group. Compared to Traditionalists, individuals in the Harm-reducers class (Class 2) were less likely to be aged 30–74 years (e.g., 60–74: OR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.19–0.37, p < 0.001), to have gambled in the past year (OR = 0.70, 95% CI 0.57–0.85, p < 0.001), and to report higher ATGS scores (OR = 0.76 per unit, 95% CI 0.74–0.78, p < 0.001). They were more likely than Traditionalists to report using both PAF and offshore operators (OR = 1.73, 95% CI 1.07–2.81, p = 0.026).

Membership in the Libertarian class (Class 3) was strongly associated with male gender (OR = 0.25 for women, 95% CI 0.18–0.33, p < 0.001), at-risk or problem gambling (OR = 2.14, 95% CI 1.50–3.06, p < 0.001), and offshore gambling (OR = 3.27, 95% CI 2.04–5.24, p < 0.001). More positive attitudes toward gambling (ATGS) also predicted libertarian membership (OR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.11–1.18, p < 0.001), while older age groups continued to show significantly lower odds of belonging to this class (Table 2).

Discussion

Based on population data, three latent classes were identified on the basis of their policy attitudes and opinions: Traditionalists, Harm-reducers, and Libertarians. Traditionalists supported policy aims associated with the existing monopoly framework: reducing crime, generating state revenue, and mitigating gambling-related harm, as well as restrictions on unauthorized gambling. The Harm-reducers supported reducing gambling participation and harm prevention with restrictions on unauthorized gambling also considered important. The Libertarians considered players’ freedom of choice and competition among gambling operators important. Overall, majority of respondents support either traditional harm-reduction approaches or stricter policies aimed at actively reducing gambling. Liberal positions were endorsed by only a minority, primarily individuals who engage in at-risk or problem gambling, hold favorable attitudes toward gambling, and participate in offshore online gambling. Despite limited public support for liberalization, gambling policy in Finland is increasingly shifting towards liberal direction, with the government planning to dismantle the current state monopoly and replace it with a license-based competitive system, covering online casino gambling and sports betting.

A further finding from this study was that individual gambling behavior and preferences were associated with preferences toward the goals of gambling policy. The profiles of the latent classes identified in this study are largely consistent with previous public opinion segmentation research on alcohol, tobacco, and gambling control policies (6). Across these literatures, individuals with lower levels of participation or exposure tend to express stronger support for restrictive policies, whereas those experiencing higher levels of involvement or harm are more likely to oppose such restrictions. Yet, it is also noteworthy that a prior Finnish study found that both non gamblers and higher risk EGM gamblers tended to support more restrictive gambling policies (14). In contrast, the present study indicates that higher risk gamblers were opposing more restrictive policies. Future research should more systematically examine how attitudes toward gambling policy vary across different gambling products. Moreover, older respondents who gamble but report no associated harms were more likely to support stricter regulatory measures. Conversely, younger non-gamblers more often endorsed policies aimed at reducing gambling participation overall. Notably, younger respondents were more likely than older ones to view gambling reduction as a primary policy goal. This is line with previous Australian study (McAllister et al. 2014) and may reflect generational differences in the perceived role of gambling in society; older adults may have stronger cultural attachments to the monopoly model and its use in funding civil society. Yet, this finding contrasts with a previous Finnish study in which older respondents were more supportive of restrictive measures on advertising and EGM availability (14), warranting further research into age-related differences in gambling policy opinions. A distinct subgroup of younger males, however, supported liberalization and increased consumer choice, possibly reflecting dissatisfaction with the monopoly system and broader market restrictions. Those who preferred more liberal goals were also more likely to gamble at-risk or problem level.

An interesting finding was that gambling on unauthorized online sites was quite common among the Harm-reducers. Such link between supporting harm reduction while engaging in unauthorized gambling has not been reported in previous studies, it is likely attributable to the younger age profile of this group and their correspondingly higher exposure to online gambling environments. Future studies should elaborate this relationship.

The results are particularly interesting in light of the major policy reform underway in Finland. Currently, Finland is a country where gambling regulation is based on a monopoly regime, with only one state-owned company having the legal right to operate gambling. In 2022, discussion on abandoning the monopoly regime commenced, and at the time of writing a bill on introducing a multi-licensing regime for online gambling is discussed in the Parliament. The new regulatory framework is intended to mitigate gambling-related harm by reducing participation in offshore online gambling. This objective is pursued by encouraging unauthorized operators to enter the licensed market and provide attractive, regulated gambling services to Finnish consumers. In this framing, unregulated gambling constituted the core policy problem, and its reduction was portrayed as delivering dual benefits: reducing gambling harm and boosting state revenue.

Both harm reducers and libertarians viewed increasing state revenue as a goal of gambling policy. While this may seem contradictory and even suggests cognitive dissonance, it likely reflects a moral balancing between economic and social objectives, shaped by the tradition of funding civil society through gambling profits. Prior research has brought up this tension between revenue generation and harm minimization (36, 37). For many, gambling revenues are morally acceptable when they serve the public good or gambling-harm treatment, reflecting an effort to reconcile welfare- and revenue-oriented policy goals.

Overall, the bill under discussion is quite liberal: it increases the availability and accessibility on online casino games and sports betting, relaxes advertisement, removes mandatory monetary limits for gamblers, and discontinues existing payment blocks targeting offshore gambling companies.

Such regime is likely to appeal primarily to libertarians, while traditionalists, and potentially harm-reducers, may regard it as less credible or effective. Consequently, the legislative framework may face legitimacy challenges arising from its misalignment with the broader priorities of the public. Moreover, the government’s liberal approach may also be an indication of a closer alignment with the interests of the gambling and media industries, both of which have substantial financial stakes in a more competitive gambling market. The ongoing discussion on the future of the monopoly regime may have influenced the views of the respondents as it commenced in 2022.

Study limitations

First, a cross-sectional study design does not allow establishing causality between individual gambling behavior and policy attitudes. Second, all data were self-reported, which may introduce recall bias and social desirability effects, regarding gambling behaviors and personal harms. Third, the study did not separately assess different types of gambling activities, which may carry distinct social perceptions and levels of acceptability; respondents may have different attitudes depending on the game type.

In addition, Finland is a relatively socioeconomically and ethnically homogeneous country with a distinctive history of gambling regulation and a welfare state model. Public perceptions of gambling policy goals are thus shaped by specific institutional, cultural, and regulatory contexts, which may limit the direct generalizability of the findings to more heterogeneous societies or to countries with different welfare regimes or gambling systems.

The phrasing and fixed order of policy goal options in the survey may have introduced response bias, potentially encouraging endorsement of the first-listed goals. Another plausible limitation to acknowledge is that while the sample was population-based, certain subgroups, such as individuals experiencing severe gambling harm or those not engaged with digital platforms, may have been underrepresented, limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

Our results indicate a clear mismatch between current liberalizing changes in gambling policy and public preferences in a Finnish context, which lean toward protective measures. Most respondents supported either harm reduction or stricter gambling-reduction policies. Support for liberalization was primarily observed among individuals with at-risk or problem gambling behaviors. In contrast, younger respondents with negative attitudes toward gambling, often non-gamblers or offshore gamblers without problems, favored gambling reduction, while older respondents who gambled within the monopoly regime and held positive views on gambling preferred harm-reduction approaches. Considering that public opinion is not in favor of current liberalizing trends occurring in gambling policy field, policymakers should carefully consider public opinion when shaping further decisions of policy. The more liberal policies may encounter legitimacy challenges due to their misalignment with citizens’ priorities.
