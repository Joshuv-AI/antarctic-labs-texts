Synthesis, Structure, and Thin Film Optical Properties of a Chiral Benzothiazole‐Derived Squaraine
Authors: Marvin F. Schumacher, Gregor Schnakenburg, N. Serdar Sariciftci, Arne Lützen, Manuela Schiek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Through functionalization with enantiomerically pure substituents, semiconducting dye molecules such as squaraines can be directed into homochiral arrangements, in which molecular chirality is transferred to a supramolecular level resulting in excitonic circular dichroism. Replacing the sterically demanding CMe groups in indolenine‐derived squaraines with sulfur atoms promotes aggregation and structural order in otherwise homogeneous thin films of benzothiazole‐derived squaraines. Here, we investigate solvent‐processed and thermally annealed thin films of a benzothiazole‐derived squaraine functionalized with homochiral citronellyl groups. The crystalline arrangement of the thin films does not correspond to the single crystal structure found, which consists of a solvent‐containing pseudo‐polymorph. The spectral shape of the excitonic circular dichroism in spin‐cast thin films is sensitive to the processing solvent and annealing temperature. Uniaxial anisotropy, evidenced by X‐ray diffraction and spectroscopic ellipsometry, shows excitonic transitions that are exclusively polarized either in the plane or perpendicular to the plane of the thin film. We interpret this in terms of a Davydov splitting into spatially separated upper (in‐plane) and lower (out‐of‐plane) Davydov components. This rarely documented arrangement changes our reading of optical spectra, including circular dichroism, recorded under standard normal incidence conditions, which probe only transitions with an in‐plane dipole‐moment component.

Introduction

Squaraine dyes are a highly potent class of small semiconducting organic molecules, exhibiting exceptional optical properties and, thanks to their straightforward synthesis and structural versatility, have driven decades of research [1, 2, 3, 4]. Typically described as resonance‐stabilized, charge‐neutral zwitterions with a donor–acceptor–donor structure, they show remarkable environmental stability and intense visible to near‐infrared spectral signatures. Seen as a feature, the self‐assembly beneficially influences the optoelectronic characteristics. It is one of the main reasons for the successful application in various fields of optoelectronics such as photovoltaics [5], biomedical applications [6, 7], nonlinear optics [8, 9, 10], and sensor applications [11, 12, 13] featuring chiral sensing [14].

Among the diverse classes of squaraines, Scheme 1, anilino squaraines are the best studied due to their prototypical aggregation patterns [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Anilino SQs are primarily modified by a terminal substitution at the nitrogen atom, which controls packing in the aggregated state. For bulky alkyl moieties, Coulomb interactions are prevalent, and the optical spectra are characterized by H‐ and J‐type Frenkel excitons, which are often shaped by a Davydov splitting [20, 21, 22]. Linear alkyl chains enable close slip‐stack packing and intermolecular charge transfer (ICT) interactions, which result in characteristic spectral double‐hump signatures that are interpreted as hybrid Frenkel–ICT excitons [18, 23, 24]. Chiral functionalization is a powerful molecular design parameter for implementing circular dichroism (CD), which is the differential absorption of circularly polarized light of opposite handedness [25, 26]. Here, the chiroptical response is mediated by the chiral supramolecular arrangement of the molecular backbones that carry the optical transition dipole moment, while the chiral groups are not part of the chromophoric unit [16, 27]. ICT interactions appear to play a central role in the giant excitonic CD response in thin films of chiral proline‐derived anilino SQs [28, 29].

Structural motifs of squaraines (SQs): anilino squaraine, indolenine squaraine, benzothiazole squaraine, and core‐substituted indolenine / benzothiazole squaraines. The terminal N‐substitutions R are typically linear, branched, or cyclic alkyl moieties, which may also be chiral. R may also be different within a molecule.

Indolenine‐derived SQs have also been extensively discussed in the literature. These can be synthesized on the basis of 2,3,3‐trimethylindolenine furnished with various alkyl substituents at the nitrogen [30, 31]. Among them are (3S)−3,7‐dimethyloctyl (citronellyl) chains, which can be enantiomerically pure [17], but typically are racemic and serve solely as solubilizing chains [32, 33, 34]. Chiral functionalization can alternatively target the 3‐position of the indolenine subunit by substituting the two methyl groups—either with chiral side chains or by creating a chiral center using two different substituents, which is considerably more common [35, 36, 37]. Not being part of the aromatic system, the sp‐hybridized quaternary carbon atom is angled toward the central plane, preventing efficient π–π stacking, which limits research mainly to solution‐based experiments. Substitution with even bulkier groups in an attempt to induce chirality amplifies this problem. A strategy to enhance aggregation behavior involves the substitution of the CMe group. Derivatives based on 2‐methyl benzoxazole and 2‐methyl benzothiazole, incorporating oxygen or sulfur as a secondary heteroatom at the 3‐position within the five‐membered ring, have previously been reported with various substituents, tremendously improving aggregation and crystalinity [38, 39, 40, 41]. Furthermore, an acceptor core substitution is possible, which alters the molecular symmetry and leads to a permanent dipole moment of otherwise quadrupolar SQs [42, 43]. This core substitution has a beneficial effect on photovoltaic performance as it promotes packing, which leads to ICT interactions in addition to the otherwise prevailing Frenkel excitons [12, 40, 44].

Here, we present the chiral benzothiazole‐derived squaraine (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ and discuss its synthesis, structure, and optical properties in thin films processed from various solvents. Compared to previously studied indolenine SQs, first, the quaternary carbon atom in position 3 of the five‐membered ring (usually a CMe group) is replaced by a sulfur atom as second heteroatom [17], and second, chirality is introduced into the system by N‐alkylation with enantiopure citronellyl substituents [32, 33]. A chiral single crystal structure demonstrates the ability to form enantiomerically pure aggregates in the condensed state, even though it differs from the thin film structure. Thin films processed from various solvents and subsequently annealed at different temperatures essentially exhibit uniaxial orientation of molecular aggregates and an excitonic CD. A solvent and annealing dependent spectral shape of the bisignate excitonic CD signature is observed. In addition to the in‐plane component visible in normal incidence transmission spectra, the anisotropic complex refractive index reveals another spectral signature: an out‐of‐plane component (perpendicular to the plane of the thin film) that is redshifted relative to the in‐plane component. We interpret these two spectral features as Davydov bands. Such a splitting of Davydov bands into a purely in‐plane and a purely out‐of‐plane component is a rarely documented phenomenon [45]. This is in contrast to the more common case in which two (effective) Davydov components are both visible in‐plane along certain azimuthal polarization directions [17, 22, 46]. This changes the possible interpretation of the optical spectra, absorbance and CD, measured under standard normal incidence conditions.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis

The complete synthetic pathway of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ, a benzothiazole SQ with homochiral citronellyl groups attached to the N‐atoms, is depicted in Scheme 2. The citronellyl group was chosen because it is easily accessible and improves the solubility of the resulting squaraines due to its branched structure, which is advantageous for solution‐based processing methods. The synthesis began with the heterogeneous hydrogenation of (3S)−3,7‐dimethyloct‐6‐en‐1‐ol ((S)‐citronellol, purity > 98% and ee > 96%) 1 using 10% palladium on carbon, yielding 2 in 95%. To activate alcohol 2 for the following substitution reactions, 4‐toluenesulfonyl chloride (TsCl) was added at room temperature under inert gas conditions, yielding the alkyl tosylate 3 in 56% [47]. Next, an S2 reaction was performed by refluxing the alkyl tosylate 3 in the presence of 2‐methylbenzothiazole 4 in dry nitromethane. The resulting N‐alkylated benzothiazolium tosylate species 5 was precipitated from the solution by adding diethyl ether. This alkylation step was reported for similar 2,3,3‐trimethylindolenine compounds (CMe group instead of sulfur) with reaction times of approximately 24 h and yields around 60% [48]. In contrast, the sulfur‐containing compound 4 required a reaction time of more than 6 days and produced only 35% of the desired benzothiazolium salt. This quite different reaction behavior and the difference in the final yield can be explained by the decreased nucleophilicity of the nitrogen atom as a consequence of changing the system to an aromatic benzothiazole unit. Since enough benzothiazolium tosylate 5 was obtained for the next reaction step, no further reaction optimization or investigation of this phenomenon was performed.

Synthesis of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ, starting from S‐citronellol (1).

Upon azeotropic removal of water by a 1:1 mixture of toluene and 1‐butanol, the benzothiazolium tosylate 5 was converted to the corresponding squaraine (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ upon treatment with 0.5 equivalents of squaric acid 6 in the presence of pyridine. Once the condensation reaction was completed, the reaction flask was slowly cooled and stored in a freezer overnight. The solvent was subsequently filtered off and the crude product purified by column chromatography on silica gel eluting with a solvent gradient. Initially, pure dichloromethane was used to remove the majority of side products and unreacted starting materials. After 3.5 vol.‐% methanol was added to the eluent, the desired benzothiazole‐derived squaraine began to elute and could be isolated. Further purification was performed by recrystallization from chloroform/cyclohexane 1:4 (v/v) and yielded the crystalline product in—accounting for the incorporated solvent—51%. It is interesting to note that although (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ is almost insoluble in cyclohexane, it proved to be crucial that it is present during crystallization. The attempt to recrystallize it from chloroform/n‐hexane 1:4 (v/v) did not result in any crystal formation. However, when cyclohexane was added dropwise to the cooled chloroform/n‐hexane solution, crystal formation was observed at the drop‐in location. This has led to the inclusion of cyclohexane in the crystal structure, which can be seen in the single crystal structure analysis.

Single Crystal X‐Ray Structure

The single crystal structure found for (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ is a pseudo‐polymorph as it contains cyclohexane solvent molecules. It is depicted in Figure 1 from different perspectives, excluding the solvent molecules. Figure S1 in the Supporting Information shows the crystal structure with solvent molecules. A monoclinic unit cell with a chiral C2 space group was determined, containing four (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ molecules per unit cell and four cyclohexane molecules. The unit cell parameters are listed in Table 1 together with those of the previously published monoclinic I2 pseudo‐polymorph of the indolenine SQ (S,S)‐bCi‐SQ [17]. The space group C2 is not primitive but base‐centered, so that two of the molecules contained are translationally equivalent, which is analogous to the body‐centered space group I2. In both cases, two translationally nonequivalent molecules remain whose electrostatic interactions enable a Davydov splitting in optical spectra [20]. See also Figure S2 regarding the possible polarization directions of the Davydov components for the benzothiazole SQ pseudo‐polymorph.

A view along the crystallographic b‐axis is given in Figure 1a, which is the direction of molecular stacking. The stacking distance of the benzothiazole SQ is slightly increased to about 10.1 Å compared to the indolenine SQ, with about 9.4 Å. The stacking of anilino SQs can be substantially tighter, about 6.2 Å in case of a monoclinic polymorph of a compound with iso‐butyl chains exhibiting Frenkel excitonic spectral signatures [21], and about 5.2 Å for n‐alkyl anilino SQs adopting triclinic structures with hybrid Frenkel–ICT excitonic spectral signatures [15]. Anilino SQs maintain complete coplanarity through the presence of intramolecular hydrogen bonds between the hydroxy‐functionalized aniline moiety and the central squaric acid core. In contrast, the central squaric acid core of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ is modestly tilted relative to the two coplanar benzothiazole units, exhibiting a torsion angle of about 11. The absence of stabilizing hydrogen bonds in the benzothiazole analog introduces steric repulsion, leading to the observed deviation from coplanarity and likely also to an extended molecular stacking distance. Substituting the CMe on the five‐membered ring of the indolenine SQ with a sulfur atom to obtain the benzothiazole SQ reduces steric hindrance but only slightly alters the molecular symmetry and packing geometry.

The direction of molecular side‐by‐side packing without any staircase offset runs along the c‐axis, as depicted in Figure 1b. The chiral citronellyl chains at the nitrogen atoms adopt a trans configuration and occupy the interplanar space defined by the (n00) series (n=1,2,3,…) of planes. Figure S1 shows how the cyclohexane solvent molecules fit into the cavities between the citronellyl groups. The translationally equivalent molecules organize in interpenetrating columnar stacks tilted relative to each other. Columnar stacking directions run along [110] and [11¯0] within the unit cell. Views along these directions with a slight displacement to better illustrate the stacking are shown in Figure 1c,d, respectively. The packing motifs of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ and the previously published indolenine SQ are very similar overall, including the solvent inclusions, although in the case of the indolenine SQ, these are n‐hexane molecules. The unit cells differ in the choice of lattice centering, which is why the axes of the side‐by‐side packing are switched. In case of the benzothiazole SQ, the lateral distances are smaller, 17.6 Å compared to 19.1 Å for the indolenine SQ, which is why the unit cell volume of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ is also smaller, even with a slightly extended molecular stacking distance.

Aggregation in Thin Films

A series of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin films were spin‐cast onto glass substrates from a chloroform solution and subsequently thermally annealed with a stepwise temperature increase per sample, starting at 60C and ending at 190C and including a nonannealed sample. Each sample was placed individually on a preheated hot plate under ambient conditions, but covered with a glass container for this purpose. Unpolarized transmission intensity spectra were recorded at ambient temperature and are shown as absorbance (−log(transmission)) in Figure 2a. Photos of samples at selected annealing temperatures visualize the homogeneous appearance of the thin films and their intense azure blue color changing to turquoise upon annealing, see Figure 2b. This color change already hints at a significant transformation in the optical properties, which could be due to a structural phase transition.

(a) Absorbance spectra of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin films, spin‐coated from a chloroform solution under the same conditions and thermally annealed for 30 min at the specified temperatures (r.t. = room temperature, i.e. no annealing). (b) Photos of selected thin film samples. (c) Optical microscopy images between crossed polarizers of thin film samples annealed at the indicated temperatures.

The spectrum of a nonannealed (r.t.) layer showed broad absorbance with a maximum at around 710 nm and a shoulder at around 640 nm. This is a typical spectrum for condensed but not specifically aggregated SQ molecules, such as anilino SQs and indolenine SQs [17, 21]. As a rapidly evaporating solvent, chloroform allows the molecules little time to arrange themselves into a state of equilibrium, which is why this nonannealed layer may be described as an amorphous glassy phase [49]. The amorphous character was further supported by the absence of an excitonic CD signal, which is discussed in a later section. Even relatively low annealing temperatures (60C, 75C, and 90C) induced molecular aggregation, with the shoulder at 640 nm disappearing in favor of an emerging redshifted shoulder at around 760 nm. Such a change in spectral signature is significant and indicates a structural phase change from amorphous to aggregated or crystallized. Fortunately, this behavior was different from that of the previously discussed indolenine SQ, which did not show molecular aggregation in thin films upon annealing but desorbed from the substrate already at temperatures above 90C [17]. As the annealing temperature was increased (105C to 175C range) for (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin films the absorbance sharpened and the peak maximum blueshifted to around 700 nm, accompanied by the formation of shoulders at shorter and longer wavelengths. Increasing the hot plate temperature further (190C) altered the shape of the spectrum, shifting it to the red and blurring the signatures. This was associated with the formation of discrete crystallites, which can be seen in microscopy images between crossed polarizer, Figure 2c. None of the microscopy images displayed signs of a liquid crystalline phase [50]. The spectral change did not indicate a structural phase change, but rather a disruption of the homogeneous layer structure accompanied by a sudden formation of high roughness. Ultimately, the benzothiazole SQ melted, evaporated and/or decomposed at temperatures above 200C, leaving small, colorless droplets on the glass surface that appeared completely dark under crossed polarizers.

To detect the formation of a crystalline phase and its possible out‐of‐plane orientation, X‐ray diffraction in Bragg–Brentano geometry was performed on a (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin film sample annealed at 135C, see Figure S1. A single diffraction peak is visible, indicating a crystalline phase with a preferred orientation of one crystallographic plane parallel to the substrate (out‐of‐plane orientation). Since no in‐plane crystalline or liquid crystalline texture could be recognized in the polarized microscopy image, Figure 2c, the thin film can thus be classified as uniaxially anisotropic. A comparison with the calculated powder pattern, Figure S1, of the single crystal structure of the monoclinic C2 pseudo‐polymorph clearly shows that a thin film spin‐cast from chloroform adopted a different crystalline phase structure. Intermolecular interactions are expected to dominate substrate‐molecule interactions, as the glass substrate used interacts little with SQ molecules, similar to the case of an anilino SQ [21]. The crystalline thin film structure is likely another bulk polymorph of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ and not a substrate‐induced phase [51, 52]. To further support this, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) experiments were conducted with a microcrystalline powder sample consisting of the monoclinic pseudo‐polymorph, see Figure S3. The DSC behavior is best explained by solvent loss and irreversible crystalline phase transformation. This means that (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ can adopt another stable crystalline bulk phase induced by thermal annealing, but unfortunately, its structure is not yet known.

At this point, the absorbance spectra of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin films cannot be interpreted conclusively. The spectral profile is reminiscent of an H‐aggregate containing vibronic signatures, with the presumed (0–1) transition at around 700 nm having the greatest oscillator strength [20]. The shoulder at 760 nm could be interpreted equally as a (0–0) transition or as a lower Davydov band without information about the polarization characteristics of the signatures. A thin film structure with multiple translationally nonequivalent molecules within an aggregate unit can be assumed, but the domain size of the obtained crystallites, Figure 2c, was too small to be analyzed by polarized spectro‐microscopy [17, 21]. However, such homogeneous thin films are well suited for determining effective dielectric functions using spectroscopic ellipsometry.

Uniaxial Anisotropic Complex Refractive Indices: Solvent and Annealing Dependence

To better understand the aggregation behavior of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ in thin films, series of samples were produced from different solvents: chloroform, dichloromethane, and cyclohexane:dichloromethane 1:1 which are abbreviated in the following as CHCl, DCM, and CyHex, respectively. Pure cyclohexane does not offer sufficient solubility to dissolve the required 5 mg/mL for good film formation in the thickness range of about 15–90 nm. In addition, the optical properties of diluted solutions in the selected solvents were characterized. The (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ exhibits a negative solvatochromic effect, whereby the peak maximum shifts to shorter wavelengths as the polarity of the solvent increases, see Figure S4. The spectral signatures remain unchanged, consisting of a pronounced maximum with a weak vibronic shoulder. The extinction coefficient in dichloromethane was determined to be ε = 267 000 Mcm at the peak maximum of 672.5 nm, see Figure S5. While rapid light‐induced decomposition occurred in solution, see Figure S6, all spin‐cast (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ samples remained stable under ambient conditions for several months without any signs of degradation.

For a reliable determination of the uniaxial dielectric function of thin film samples using variable angle spectroscopic ellipsometry (VASE), samples with different layer thicknesses must be evaluated together with transmission spectra in a multisample analysis (MSA) [15, 53]. Additionally, three annealing temperatures were considered: no annealing (as‐cast), 90C and 135C, for a more systematic understanding of temperature‐induced aggregation. The importance of considering multiple samples becomes immediately evident when comparing as‐cast samples with different layer thicknesses produced by different spin‐coating parameters, see Figure S7. The absorbance spectra depended on the layer thickness (or spin‐coating parameters), which indicated varying extents of molecular interaction. In case of chloroform, differences were manifested in the position of the peak maximum and its ratio to the shoulder. They were so pronounced that it was not possible to reliably determine a single representative dielectric function, even if uniaxial anisotropy was taken into account. The matrix in Figure 3 shows the complex refractive indices for all other combinations of the three solvents and annealing temperatures, which are all uniaxially anisotropic except for as‐cast DCM conditions. Uniaxial anisotropy refers to a set of an ordinary refractive index and extinction coefficient within the plane of the thin film, n and k , as well as a set of extraordinary components, n and k , perpendicular to it (out‐of‐plane). Note that the spectral signature of the extraordinary extinction coefficient is not visible in transmission spectra measured under normal incidence, since the transition dipole moment is perpendicular to the plane of the layer. Also note that transmission spectra of thin films consist of absorption within the layer (Lambert–Beer) as well as reflection at the interfaces and their interference (plus scattering, if applicable) [54]. This means that both the real refractive index and the extinction coefficient contribute to the shape of a transmission spectrum, and the exact peak position and peak ratios may differ from those of the extinction coefficient. The representation of a transmission spectrum as absorbance, −log(transmission), does not change this [55].

The isotropic layer for the as‐cast DCM case showed the typical spectral pattern of a complex refractive index for condensed but not specifically aggregated squaraines (amorphous glassy phase), such as an anilino SQ with isobutyl chains (SQIB) [21] or the analogous indolenine SQ [17]. The extinction coefficient slightly exceeds 1 at its maximum and lies within the typical “uniformity range” of extinction coefficients found for conjugated polymers [56]. The same applies to the as‐cast CyHex layers, but here the layer exhibited already uniaxial anisotropy, with the extraordinary component showing a noticeably redshifted signature. Annealing at 90C induced a noticeable structural phase transformation from amorphous glassy to crystalline textured in the thin films of all three processing solvents and, as a related effect, uniaxial anisotropy. At the same time, the ordinary extinction coefficients increased to values greater than 2 at maximum. The aggregation caused the emergence of an extraordinary spectral signature that is redshifted compared to the ordinary component. In the case of CyHex‐processing, the extraordinary component was even dominant and further shifted to the red compared to samples processed from CHCl and DCM. An increase in annealing temperature above the crystallization threshold to 135C did not alter the spectral signatures but led to a further amplification of the extinction coefficients in samples processed from CHCl and DCM with the ordinary extinction coefficient now significantly exceeding 3. This is consistent with an increase in coherence length by densification, improved texture, and reduced structural disorder within the layers, which amplifies the effective oscillator strength of optical transitions. No noticeable structural phase change occurred at this point. The extraordinary extinction coefficient exhibited a further redshift, as already observed for CyHex processing at 90C annealing. 135C annealing caused a relative weakening of the extraordinary extinction coefficient in CyHex processed samples, but no further enhancement of the ordinary extinction coefficient, compared to the medium annealing temperature. CyHex processing mixture thus appeared to be a better crystallization promoter, as the same degree of order was already achieved at a lower annealing temperature compared to DCM and CHCl processing.

The enhancing effect of aggregation and crystallization on excitonic spectral signatures has already been observed in the uniaxial dielectric function and the giant CD of prolinol‐derived anilino SQ layers [29] and in the dielectric tensor of orthorhombic microcrystallites of another anilino SQ [57]. In case of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ all layers retained their homogeneity for all processing solvents, even at annealing temperatures of 135C. Microscopy images between crossed polarizers displayed sub‐microtexturing and, correspondingly, rather uniform birefringence, see Figure 4a. The microscopy images provided no indicators of the formation of a liquid crystalline phase [50]. X‐ray diffraction showed, in agreement with ellipsometry, a preferred out‐of‐plane orientation for all processing solvents with the same interplanar spacing (2θ = 8.65) for 135C annealing, see Figure S1. From this, it can be inferred that all three sample types adopt the same crystalline thin film structure polymorph, which, however, does not correspond to the resolved single crystal pseudo‐polymorph. The use of CyHex processing solvent did not appear to cause the intended inclusion of cyclohexane molecules into the thin film as in the bulk single crystal structure. But this resulted in an increased tendency toward aggregation, as already the as‐cast CyHex layers manifested uniaxial anisotropy. Furthermore, the MSE values of the ellipsometric fits were about 20, and were systematically larger compared to values of about 10 and smaller for the samples processed from the neat chlorinated solvents, see Caption Figure 3 for detailed values. The rise in MSE for CyHex processing could be due to an increased roughness of the layers, which in turn could be caused by larger crystallite sizes.

For all three processing solvents, the ordinary extinction coefficient of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin films shows a distinct shoulder at shorter wavelengths, which is most pronounced at 135C annealing. The energetic difference between the peak maximum and the shoulder is approximately 0.17 meV, which is within the range of a vibronic signature [20]. A shoulder at longer wavelengths could not be resolved here, but can be assumed based on the spectral signatures in the absorbance spectra for CHCl‐processing and annealing temperatures above 135C, see Figure 2a. Nevertheless, we hypothesize that the peak maximum of the ordinary spectral signature is a (0–1) and the shoulder at shorter wavelengths is a (0–2) vibronic progression. The ordinary spectral signature is thus reminiscent of an H‐aggregate or the upper Davydov component (UDC) of a Davydov splitting [20]. This determines the extraordinary spectral signature at longer wavelengths for the lower Davydov component (LDC), and the energetic splitting between the two peak maxima for the apparent Davydov splitting. As a reminder: the extraordinary spectral signature is not visible in transmission spectra under normal incidence, and therefore neither is the Davydov splitting. The energetic splitting between the maximum values of ordinary and extraordinary extinction coefficients (apparent Davydov splitting) is approximately 0.1 eV for CHCl and DCM processing solvents at 90C annealing and increases to approximately 0.2 eV at 135C annealing. For CyHex processing, the apparent Davydov splitting is somewhat larger, amounting to approximately 0.3 eV at both 90C and 135C annealing temperatures. As a reminder: A transmission spectrum (also in the representation as absorbance) consists of contributions from the real refractive index and extinction coefficient, which means that peak position and energetic separation in an absorbance spectrum and the extinction coefficient may differ from each other. In addition, the extraordinary components can be determined with less accuracy than the ordinary ones [53]. Nevertheless, the magnitude of the apparent Davydov splitting in annealed (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin films can be classified as typical for pronouncedly interacting small molecules, such as perylene diimide crystallites [58] or pentacene polymorphs [59]. Remarkable is the division of the Davydov components into a purely ordinary (in‐plane) and a purely extraordinary (out‐of‐plane) component, which is a rarely documented case, such as with a merocyanine dye [45]. In most other examples, both Davydov components show at least a partial projection into the plane of a thin film, making both Davydov bands accessible by polarization resolved spectro‐microscopy in normal incidence transmission [17, 22, 46].

Circular Dichroism: Solvent and Annealing Dependence

Due to the chirality of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ, excitonic CD can be used as an additional metric to investigate molecular aggregation. The enantiomerically pure terminal citronellyl group directs a homochiral aggregation of the chromophoric SQ backbones, which then exhibit an excitonic CD signal in tandem with the excitonic absorbance. For this purpose, the thin film samples using three different processing solvents (chloroform, dichloromethane, cyclohexane:dichloromethane 1:1) and annealing temperatures (as‐cast, 90C, 135C) were also investigated by Mueller matrix polarimetry in normal incidence transmission. Microscopy images between crossed polarizers reveal a sub‐microtexture and correspondingly a relatively uniform birefringence in all layers annealed at 135C, see Figure 4a. Mueller matrix polarimetry is particularly advantageous for such thin films, as the obtained CD is free of contributions from linear dichroism [60]. Rotation and flipping of the sample do not influence the measurement result, as is the case with a conventional CD spectrometer [61, 62].

Typical thickness normalized CD together with the corresponding normalized absorbance spectra are shown in Figure 4b. As discussed in the previous section, the absorbance spectra of the as‐cast (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ layers exhibit spectral signatures of condensed but not specifically aggregated molecules [17, 21]. For the processing solvents CHCl and DCM the spectra looked very similar with a main peak at around 713 nm and a vibronic shoulder at around 638 nm. The CyHex solvent mixture resulted in a slightly broadened absorbance with the main peak at around 695 nm and a shoulder at around 610 nm with an increased aspect ratio due to emerging aggregation [63]. A similar tendency toward onset aggregation even without annealing has also been observed in CHCl‐processed layers depending on the spin‐coating parameters, see Figure S7. As expected with no significant aggregation, none of the as‐cast layers showed a detectable CD signal, see left panel in Figure 4b. To avoid confusion: A single (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ molecule can indeed exhibit a CD signal originating from the enantiomerically pure citronellyl functionalization. This terminal group is not conjugated to the chromophoric SQ backbone and would show a CD signal in the deep UV spectral range regardless of molecular aggregation.

Thermal annealing for 30 min at 90C induced significant changes in the absorbance spectra suggesting aggregation, which is supported by the appearance of CD signals, see middle panel in Figure 4b. The layers processed from the neat chlorinated solvents showed very similar absorbance spectra with a maximum at around 706 nm and a shoulder at around 745 nm. CyHex‐processing resulted in a slightly broader absorbance due to the formation of a second shoulder at a shorter wavelength (644 nm) and a slight blueshift of the maximum to about 695 nm. Overall, the absorbance spectra of all layers appeared sharper after annealing than without. Based on the results of the uniaxial anisotropic refractive index in the previous section, we interpret this absorbance pattern obtained by aggregation as the UDC of a Davydov splitting [20]. The peak maximum is then the vibronic (0–1) transition of the UDC, and the LDC remains invisible in transmission spectroscopy under normal incidence because it is oriented exclusively perpendicular to the layer. Compared to monomer absorbance, which varies between 650 and 680 nm depending on the solvent, see Figure S4, the UDC thin film absorbance was, in any case, shifted to the red spectral range. This does not match the simple definition of H‐like (UDC) and J‐like (LDC) transitions based on their spectral blue‐ and redshifts, respectively, relative to the monomer transition (Kasha definition). The assignment via vibronic signatures is more robust, as interactions in the solid state can lead to a redshift of both transitions, particularly in quadrupolar molecules such as SQs [64]. However, since Mueller matrix polarimetry was performed in transmission under normal incidence only, all CD signals can be attributed solely to the UDC.

The shape of the CD signals was similar for the layers of all three processing solvents for 90C annealing, see middle panel in Figure 4b. The CD spectra started with a small negative lobe at around 695 nm, transition to a more pronounced positive lobe at around 720 nm, followed by the most intense negative lobe at around 745 nm, and ended with a small positive lobe at around 780 nm. In the case of CyHex‐processing, the first positive and second negative peaks were slightly redshifted, so that in this case the zero crossing was at 745 nm, which corresponds to the presumed (0–0) shoulder in the absorbance spectrum. The magnitude of the first negative signature (695 nm) increased in the following order of processing solvents: DCM, CHCl, and CyHex, while the magnitude of the last positive signature (780 nm) in this sequence decreased. Overall, the CD signals were small: The thickness‐normalized CD values range from +6.1 to –7.2 mdeg nm, suggesting weak Coulombic intermolecular interactions. Thus, CD spectra took the complex form of a series of vibronic bisignate couplets, as is the case in the weak coupling regime of an H‐aggregate or a UDC with pronounced vibronic signatures [65]. Only in the case of strong coupling does a CD spectrum have the simple form of a first derivative of the absorbance spectrum (bisignate Cotton effect) with a zero crossing at the absorbance maximum [66, 67, 68].

Increasing the annealing temperature further to 135C resulted in minor changes in the absorbance and pronounced changes in the CD spectra as shown in Figure 4b, right panel. Again, the absorbance spectra of the layer processed with neat chlorinated solvents appeared the same, whereas CyHex processing resulted in a broadened absorbance. All three absorbance spectra have sharpened compared to 90C annealing and exhibited a common main peak at around 701 nm plus two shoulders around 644 and 747 nm. The CD spectra still resulted as a series of vibronic bisignate couplets with small CD values, but looked considerably different compared to 90C annealing and were clearly different from each other. The initial deceptive impression was that the increased annealing temperature for the CyHex‐processed layer had led to an inversion of the CD signal handedness, and thus possibly to a change in the aggregate structure.

In fact, the exciton chirality method is a common experimental approach to determine the absolute configuration of supramolecular aggregates based on the sign of a bisignate CD couplet [62, 69]. However, the method was developed for colloidal aggregates in solution, where anisotropy does not play a role, and only applies to a full extent in the regime of strong coupling, that is, when there is no vibronic coupling involved. In solution, it is comparatively easy to control the handedness of molecular aggregates by using different solvents and additives, which can cause a sign inversion of the CD signal [70, 71, 72, 73]. Kinetic control by flows in solution and even the direction of rotation during spin‐coating can also impact the handedness of aggregation [74, 75]. Therefore, it is noteworthy that the direction of rotation in this study was always clockwise. Also, from a theoretical perspective, recent considerations indicate that the influence of handedness and coupling energy on the sign of a CD couplet remains enigmatic [76].

In case of the (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin film samples, neither the X‐ray diffraction analysis nor all other spectroscopic investigations pointed to any significant structural reorganization comparing the processing solvents. The broadening of the absorbance spectra in the case of CyHex‐processing is rather attributed to an increased tendency toward crystallization with the formation of larger crystallites and the associated increased layer roughness. Therefore, we do not consider the variable form of the CD spectra as a feature and refrain from drawing further conclusions from it. The peculiar behavior of the CD spectra can be crudely postulated as a sum of multiple bisignate signatures, one for each vibronic progression, all associated with a single electronic transition, which are subject to a change in rotational strength through thermal annealing [65, 66, 67, 68, 76, 77]. The fact that the CD values were small reinforces the assumption of a weak coupling regime based on electrostatic interactions (Frenkel excitons) only. Contributions from charge transfer interactions have shown a clear and even giant amplification of the CD response in thin films of a homochiral anilino SQ [17] and prolinol‐derived SQs [27, 28], respectively. With the prolinol‐derived SQs thin films, the dominant in‐plane component has a redshifted J‐like character and, compared to the weaker blueshifted H‐like mainly out‐of‐plane component, carries the majority of both oscillator and rotational strength [29]. In (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin films, the redshifted out‐of‐plane component (LDC) appeared to have an oscillator strength similar to the in‐plane component (UDC) based on the uniaxial complex refractive indices, as discussed in the previous section. Unfortunately, we cannot comment on the associated rotational strength of a possible CD signal of the LDC. The LDC of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ is oriented exclusively perpendicular (out‐of‐plane) to the thin films and is thereby inaccessible with our experimental methods. Therefore, it remains unresolved whether there is a similar redistribution of rotational strength to the LDC also in the case of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin films.

Summary and Conclusion

We successfully synthesized the chiral benzothiazole‐derived squaraine (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ, functionalized with enantiomerically pure citronellyl groups, and evaluated its aggregation in solution‐processed thin films by means of their uniaxial complex refractive indices and excitonic CD supported by structural analysis. The resolved single crystal structure is a chiral pseudo‐polymorph adopting a monoclinic C2 space group, similar to a previously published chiral pseudo‐polymorph of a citronellyl‐functionalized indolenine SQ, both suffering from the inclusion of solvent molecules. The introduction of the second heteroatom sulfur at the 3‐position of the five‐membered ring to substitute the dialkylated sp‐hybridized carbon atom in the respective indolenine derivatives not only cause an extension of the conjungated π‐system but also reduces the steric hindrance between neighboring molecules. This enables the formation of homogeneous layers with distinct molecular aggregation, which was not the case for the indolenine SQ analog.

All thermally annealed (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ layers, regardless of the three processing solvents of choice: chloroform, dichloromethane, and dichloromethane:cyclohexane mixture, exhibit the same uniaxial anisotropic thin film structure, which is different from the single crystal structure. From the uniaxial anisotropic complex refractive index, we learned that, in addition to the in‐plane component, there is an out‐of‐plane component that is invisible in normal incidence transmission spectroscopy. The out‐of‐plane component is redshifted relative to the in‐plane component, which also has distinct vibronic signatures. Even though we do not know the structure of the crystalline thin films, we assume two or more translationally invariant molecules in the aggregate. Therefore, we interpret the spectral features as Davydov splitting: the exclusively in‐plane polarized signature corresponds to the UDC, while the exclusively out‐of‐plane polarized signature (perpendicular to the plane of the thin film) corresponds to the LDC. This is a rarely documented case, to the best of our knowledge, to date, only for thin films of a merocyanine.

Because the molecular aggregation is chiral, the layers exhibit an excitonic CD, which unfortunately turns out to be weak. Mueller matrix polarimetry allows us to determine the true CD signal without contributions from linear dichroism and influence from sample orientation. However, since it is a normal incidence transmission measurement, only the CD signals of the in‐plane component, that is, UDC, can be captured. We observe multifaceted bisignate CD spectra whose shapes depend on the processing solvent and annealing temperature, whereby a sign inversion may mislead to the interpretation of a structural rearrangement. However, the peculiar shape of the CD spectra can rather be interpreted as the sum of several bisignate features, one for each vibronic progression, all associated with a single electronic transition, which are subject to relocation of rotational strength because of processing conditions.

This leaves the splitting of the Davydov bands into a strictly in‐plane (UDC) and a strictly out‐of‐plane component (LDC) as the most striking feature of the (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin films. This is in contrast to the usual situation in thin films, where both Davydov components are at least partially projected into the plane of the film. Only transitions with a dipole moment component in the plane can be seen in conventional normal incidence spectroscopy, which means that the LDC of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ is invisible in conventional normal incidence spectroscopy. It therefore remains unresolved whether the LDC is paired with a CD signal with significant rotational strength.

The structure contains a pseudo crystallographic inversion center in the midpoint of the squaric acid core that renders almost all atoms in both half of the molecule identical to their respective counterparts. This inversion is only broken by the chiral centers at C15 and C33. The structure can be solved and refined in the corresponding centrosymmetric space group C2/c where the broken chirality is expressed by the generation of split positions at the former chiral centers having site occupancies of approximately 30%. The refined wR2 value of this centrosymmetric model is 1.5 times higher than the chiral refinement (wR2 = 36.8%). This structural feature hampers the determination of the correct twin law, as inclusion of a twin law for a rotational twinning retaining the chiral information gives the same twin batch scale factors and R‐values as the use of a racemic twin law. This is most likely the reason why the Flack parameter is rather high and identical to the batch scale factor of the rotational twining model, as well as the site occupancies if refined in the space group C2/c. Because we used enantiopure starting materials, we believe that the crystal was rotationally twined.

Thin Film Preparation

Solutions of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ were prepared in chloroform (HPLC grade, amylene stabilized, VWR), dichloromethane (HPLC grade, Sigma Aldrich) and cyclohexane/dichloromethane mixtures (1:1, v:v, HPLC grade, Sigma Aldrich) at a concentration of 5 mg/mL and stirred under light protection for 24 h before use. Glass substrates were cut from 1 mm thick float glass objective slides (VWR) in 15×15 cm or 25×25 cm. Thin films were prepared by spin‐coating 65 or 120 μL of the solution for 60 s at different rotation speeds and acceleration 2000, 400 rpm/s (thick); 3000, 1000 rpm/s (medium); and 4000, 4000 rpm/s (thin) onto the glass substrates. The direction of rotation during spin‐coating was always clockwise as preset by the device (LabSpin 6 SÜSS Microtec). The “air” side of the float glass was preselected for coating [53]. Samples were thermally annealed on a preheated hotplate for 30 min at various temperatures (exact temperatures are specified in the main text). Individual samples were fabricated for each annealing temperature. All preparation steps were carried out under ambient conditions.

Optical Microscopy

A Nikon Eclipse LV100ND equipped with a halogen lamp, variable objectives and a digital camera (Nikon Digital Sight DS‐Fi2) was used to visualize the samples, which were imaged in brightfield reflected light (epi‐illumination) between crossed polarizers. Images were captured using the provided NIS‐Elements software.

Optical Characterization

A J.A. Woollam M2000‐DI single rotating compensator ellipsometer (PCSA) with a horizontal sample stage and CompleteEASE version 5 software was used for optical data acquisition. The spectral range from 193 to 1690 nm is recorded using two CCD detector arrays, which simultaneously offer 705 wavelengths with a bandwidth of 5 nm in the UV–vis range and 10 nm in the NIR spectral range.

Intensity spectra were recorded in normal incidence transmission mode and are shown as absorbance or optical density, i.e. −log(transmission). The diameter of the light beam is approximately 2 mm, so that the possible microtexture of the thin films is averaged. Transmission in thin films consists of absorption, reflection, and, in the case of textured/rough films, scattering, and includes contributions from absorption and reflection of the glass substrate. Note that absorbance and absorption refer to different quantities.

VASE in reflection in combination with transmission intensity spectra was used to determine the uniaxial complex refractive indices, while partial Mueller matrix polarimetry in transmission enabled to determine the CD.

Uniaxial Complex Refractive Index by Variable Angle Spectroscopic Ellipsometry

Using conventional VASE in reflection, Ψ and Δ thin film spectra were measured at different angles of incidence (AOIs) from 45 to 70 in steps of 5. A MSA plus transmission intensity strategy, as described in detail previously [53], was employed to obtain the uniaxial complex refractive indices of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ samples spin‐cast from various solvents and subjected to different annealing temperatures. This MSA combines Ψ‐Δ‐scans of multiple samples prepared in the same way (same solvent and annealing conditions) but with different spin‐coating conditions, i.e. different layer thicknesses. Adding the transmission intensity to the fitting procedure allows for improved determination of the out‐of‐plane component of the uniaxial refractive index. Such an anisotropic complex refractive index consists of an ordinary (in‐plane) and an extraordinary (out‐of‐plane) part, within the plane of the thin film, and perpendicular to it, respectively. A B‐spline regression, as provided by CompleteEASE version 6, was implemented to accurately model the ellipsometric data without enforcing a specific physical interpretation through an oscillator model. The optimization criteria of the fitting routine were a “visual best match” of the measured and modeled data, especially the transmission intensity data, and the minimization of the mean square error (MSE). The complex refractive index of the glass substrate was determined beforehand, and care was taken to deposit the (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ layers on the ”air” side of the float glass.

We refer interested readers to a publication that presents aspects of determining a uniaxial complex refractive index on organic thin films in a more fundamental but application‐oriented manner [53].

Circular Dichroism by Mueller Matrix Polarimetry

The normalized partial Mueller matrix (m11=1, last row missing) was recorded in normal incidence transmission with a custom‐made sample holder. The complete Mueller matrix M can be reconstructed for non‐depolarizing samples using an algebraic method [60]. The physical interpretation of the differential Mueller matrix L can be seen in the following equation:

(1) L=ln (M)=(0−LD−LD′CD−LD0CBLB′−LD′−CB0−LBCD−LB′LB0)

with CD = circular dichroism, CB = circular birefringence, LD = horizontal linear dichroism, LD’ = 45° linear dichroism, LB = horizontal linear birefringence, and LB’ = 45° linear birefringence.

Selected differential Mueller matrices of (S,S)‐bCi‐BtSQ thin films for different solvents and annealing temperatures are shown in the SI in Figures S8, S9, S10, S11, S12, S13, S14, S15, and S16.

To obtain the CD values, the matrix elements l14 and l41 are averaged and converted from radians to millidegrees (mdeg). For better comparability, all CD spectra shown are normalized to the thin film thicknesses obtained from the ellipsometric analyses.

Note that CD is not identical to the ellipticity θ as the typical output of a conventional CD spectrometer:

(2) θ=arctan(eCD−1eCD+1)

Only for small CD values the following approximation applies: θ=CD/2. An obvious advantage of the Mueller matrix polarimetry approach for CD studies is that the CD value provided is free of linear dichroism contributions. This makes this approach particularly suitable for the analysis of thin film samples. No workarounds such as sample rotation or flipping are necessary, as is the case with a conventional CD spectrometer [61, 62]. Nevertheless, it is reasonable to average the CD value across measurements at different sample spots to compensate for possible layer thickness inhomogeneities.
