Endoscopic Debridement and Repair of Proximal Hamstring Calcific Tendinopathy
Authors: Jacob Maier, Jordan Read, Marc Philippon, Michael Banffy, Jovan Laskovski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcific tendinopathy of the proximal hamstring has few descriptions in the literature. Although very similar to the same pathology in other anatomic locations, diagnosis can be difficult, because many physicians aren't familiar with its occurrence in this location. This pathology can cause significant pain and debility for affected patients; therefore, accurate diagnosis and treatment is essential. This Technical Note discusses the diagnosis and management, specifically endoscopic debridement and repair, of proximal hamstring tendinopathy.

Calcific tendinopathy is a pathologic condition characterized by the deposition of calcium crystals within tendons, often causing substantial pain.1 The condition is most commonly seen in the rotator cuff but has also been described in the elbow, hip, and knee.2, 3, 4, 5 In the shoulder, treatment options range from conservative management to ultrasound-guided lavage and arthroscopic debridement with tendon repair.6,7 Similar approaches have been used in other hip tendons such as the gluteus medius and rectus femoris.8,9 Of the locations of calcific tendinopathy, the proximal hamstring is one of the least common. Our practice has recently seen several cases of proximal hamstring calcific tendinopathy, and this Technical Note aims to discuss patient presentation, diagnostic workup, and nonoperative management as well as provide a detailed description of our operative technique.

Surgical Technique

Our operative treatment for calcific tendinopathy that is unresponsive to conservative management is endoscopic debridement and repair, which is demonstrated in Video 1. Patient setup is the same as for the endoscopic hamstring repair technique described by Laskovski et al.,10 where the patient is placed prone on a standard operating table with 2 arm boards angled 45° away from the bed for the operative leg.

A midcentral viewing portal and a medial working portal are created in the fashion described by Laskovski et al. (Fig 1). Care should be taken to clear the subgluteal bursa with the cannula and blunt trocar before placing the scope to create a working space and facilitate a good view of the ischium.

Portal placement for left hamstring repair, as shown on the left leg of a patient in the supine position. 1: midcentral portal; 2: medial portal; 3: lateral portal; 4: suture management portal. The patient is positioned prone with the operative leg abducted to 45°. These are the basic portals used for endoscopic management of proximal hamstring pathology by the authors.

Calcific tendinopathy may be identified at this time, with the tendon often appearing similarly to a partial-thickness tear. Calcification can typically be expressed from the tendon insertion by trephination with an 18-gauge spinal needle (Fig 2). Using an ablator through the medial portal, a vertical opening is made through the tendon insertion and is taken down in the sight of calcification or partial-thickness tear down to bone. Calcification is then identified and debrided with a reciprocating shaver. It is important to debride all affected tissue and establish an adequate bone bed for tendon repair (Figs 3 and 4). Debridement is typically complete when no more calcification is visible and this can be confirmed with fluoroscopy. At this point, the surgeon should assess the tissue quality to determine whether a biologic scaffold should be used to augment the repair and promote healing.

Viewing from the midcentral portal, calcification can be seen being expressed from the proximal hamstring tendon by trephination with a spinal needle. In addition to localization of the calcific deposition, trephination can be used to perform the bulk of the tendon debridement. The spinal needle can be inserted through either the lateral or medial portal depending on which provides the best trajectory.

Viewing from the midcentral portal, a shaver is used to further debride calcific deposits from the proximal hamstring tendon. To ensure completion of the debridement, a shaver is used after trephination to further remove calcifications and any pathologic tissue. Fluoroscopy can be used if the surgeon wishes to further confirm complete resection of the calcific tendinopathy.

On endoscopic examination through the midcentral portal, the high-grade partial tear of the proximal hamstring with calcific tendinosis is again visualized. The tendon is split longitudinally and a clean bone bed is prepared at the footprint with a coblation wand to facilitate anchor placement and created a clean tendon-bone interface.

With good visualization of the bare ischial tuberosity and an open subgluteal space, the lateral portal is established using direct endoscopic visualization. The final position should optimize access to the entire ischium and subgluteal bursa to facilitate placement of anchors into the ischium. The authors prefer an AlphaVent 4.75-mm triple-loaded PEEK suture anchor with 1.8-mm suture tape (Stryker Corporation, Greenwood Village, CO) or 4.5-mm Mitek Healix Advance PK triple-loaded DYNACORD suture anchor (Depuy Mitek, Inc., Raynham, MA). A suture-management portal may then be established at the superior most aspect of the subgluteal space to allow for passage of sutures superiorly out of the operative field. Through the lateral portal, 2 or 3 triple-loaded vented suture anchors are placed into the ischium, depending on the size of the created tear. If indicated, an interpositional biologic scaffold can be shuttled down the sutures to the ischial footprint at this point. The authors' interpositional scaffold of choice is a Rotium Bioresorbable Wick (Atreon Orthopedics, Dublin, OH). Sutures are then passed through the medial and lateral tendon leaves from the medial portal using a retrograde suture passer and antegrade suture passer. The sutures are then passed superiorly to improve visualization and improve suture management (Fig 5).

In this image from the midcentral viewing portal, sutures from the triple-loaded anchors have been passed through the medial and lateral leaflets of the hamstring tendon insertion. These will then be tied to complete the repair. The origin of the suture anchors in the bony footprint can be seen between the leaves of the tendon.

Once all sutures have been passed, the knee will be flexed to 90° and sutures are tied through the medial portal with a standard arthroscopic sliding knot, we prefer the SMC knot with 2 alternating half-hitches. Once all knots are tied, the knee is relaxed and the repair site can be evaluated.

At this point, further biologic augmentation can be added, typically a structural acellular dermal allograft (BioBrace; ConMed Linvatech, Largo, FL) that is fixed directly on tendon at the repair site using knotless suture anchors. Once the repair and any other debridement is complete, instrumentation is removed and the portals are closed and dressed in the typical fashion.

After surgery, immediate weight-bearing status can vary depending on the patient. Patients have done well with flat-foot weight-bearing with crutches immediately after surgery, but many surgeons prefer non weight-bearing on the operative extremity. At the 2-week mark, they begin physical therapy, range of motion, and advance weight-bearing. Typically, by week 6, patients are full weight-bearing without restrictions.

Discussion

Although the shoulder is the most commonly affected and studied site of calcific tendinopathy, other large joints, such as the elbow and hip, can also be involved, though these occurrences are relatively rare. Among the tendons associated with the hip, cases have been reported in the biceps femoris, rectus femoris, and the gluteus medius and minimus. Despite being studied extensively, the etiology of calcific tendinopathy remains largely unknown. Although a history of trauma or overuse could be possibilities, the literature indicates that they are uncommon. Notable associations have been observed with metabolic disorders such as diabetes mellitus, thyroid dysfunction, and nephrolithiasis, however, the pathology is relatively rare as part of systemic disease,.11,12

Proximal hamstring calcific tendinopathy typically presents with chronic, atraumatic posterior hip or buttock pain, often worsened by sitting, rising from a seated position, or climbing stairs. This is in contrast to calcific tendinopathy of the shoulder and many other joints, which frequently presents with acute, severe pain and disability. Clinical examination findings are typically indistinguishable from other proximal hamstring pathology. A physical examination may reveal tenderness at the ischial tuberosity and pain with resisted hip extension or knee flexion, though findings are often nonspecific.

Imaging is crucial to diagnosis. Radiographs are routinely obtained in an initial and comprehensive work up for posterior hip pain. In addition to anteroposterior and lateral views, a false profile view and Dunn lateral may help locate and define calcific changes. Calcific changes can be visualized near the ischial tuberosity or in adjacent soft tissue and hamstring tendon. Similar to rotator cuff calcific tendinopathy, calcifications may appear sharply defined, heterogenic, or barely visible. In most cases of proximal hamstring calcific tendinopathy, calcifications may not be apparent on plain films.

Patients will often need a musculoskeletal ultrasound for diagnosis. On ultrasound, calcium deposits often appear as hyperechoic foci with posterior acoustic shadowing (Figs 6A and B); however, this can be variable. Posterior thigh anatomy can be deep and complex which may increase the difficulty of defining calcification of the proximal hamstring. However, in the hands of an experienced technician, ultrasound is the best method for finding and defining proximal hamstring calcific lesions.

Magnetic resonance imaging has been used to evaluate proximal hamstring injuries in detail, describing signal changes, tendon retraction, and bone edema, but not calcific changes.13 Calcific deposits can be difficult to visualize with magnetic resonance imaging and often present with edema and signal changes mimicking tendon tear, as evidenced by Mohan et al.14 Conservative treatment should be exhausted before progressing to more invasive measures. Anti-inflammatory medications and physical therapy are the mainstay of non-operative management for calcific tendinosis.15 In most cases, patients need to be unresponsive to at least 6 months of conservative management before more invasive measures are considered, specifically the procedure described in this Technical Note.

Although ischial tuberosity corticosteroid injections have been described in the literature as both therapeutic and diagnostic for proximal hamstring pathology, the authors prefer to avoid corticosteroid injections to this site due to the increased risk of tendon rupture. Platelet-rich plasma injections have had variable outcomes in the literature with regards to rotator cuff calcific tendinopathy.16 We have tried platelet-rich plasma injections as another form of treatment before considering operative management, but have not seen any notable improvement in symptom resolution.

The described endoscopic technique enables direct visualization, precise debridement of calcific deposits, and anatomic tendon repair at the ischial footprint. Its advantages include minimal invasiveness, preservation of surrounding soft tissue, and the ability to address both degenerative and calcific pathology simultaneously (Table 1). When indicated, biologic augmentation with interpositional scaffolds may support tendon healing and reinforce the repair.

Advantages and Disadvantages of the Technique

Technical pearls include thorough subgluteal bursectomy to optimize visualization, careful palpation of the tendon footprint to detect residual calcium, and strategic portal placement for anchor placement and suture management (Table 2). However, the deep location and limited working space pose challenges, and the procedure has a steep learning curve. Incomplete debridement or poor visualization may lead to persistent symptoms, though fluoroscopy can aid in confirming complete removal. This approach offers a reproducible and effective surgical solution for patients with recalcitrant proximal hamstring calcific tendinopathy.

Pearls and Pitfalls of the Technique

With regards to biologic augmentation, biologic scaffolds may be used to supplement tendon repair and promote healing. In rotator cuff repairs, collagen-based biologic scaffolds and interpositional matrices have been associated with improved structural integrity and reduced retear rates.17,18 Despite variability in preparation methods and study design, clinical studies consistently report either neutral or favorable results without evidence of harm. Although data specific to the proximal hamstring are lacking, we have seen good results in our patient population with use of biologics for this repair when indicated. The authors' preference is for an interpositional matrix at the bone-tendon interface with or without a collagen scaffold fixed over the repaired tendon.

In summary, we describe a relatively uncommon diagnosis of calcific tendinopathy of the proximal hamstring. Presentation is similar to other degenerative hamstring pathology and imaging often helps direct the diagnosis. We recommend ultrasonography as a diagnostic tool when suspicion is high and recommend endoscopic management for those patients who are nonresponsive to nonoperative management.
