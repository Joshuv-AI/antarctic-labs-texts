Materialogenetics: an emerging and promising framework for cell-specific genetic manipulation
Authors: Fengtao Luo, Tao Song, Hangang Chen, Min Jin, Hua Chen, Song Li, Yangli Xie, Lin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genetic manipulation technologies (GMTs) serve as potent instruments for elucidating the molecular and cellular mechanisms that underly both physiological and pathological processes. However, the increasing recognition of the cellular heterogeneity in terms of their spatial distribution, cellular subtypes, and functional states in complex tissues, has posed new challenges to the applicability and precision of these technologies. While current GMTs facilitate precise genetic interventions at the molecular level, their capacity for achieving cell-specific genetic regulation remains constrained. In this context, we utilize research on fibroblast growth factors (FGFs)/FGF receptors (FGFRs) signaling in osteoarthritis (OA) as a case to demonstrate the technical limitations faced by existing GMTs. To address these constraints, we propose a conceptual framework termed Materialogenetics, which integrates advances in biomaterials science with genetic manipulation approaches. Advanced biomaterials with active-targeting and stimuli-responsive properties have been widely explored in disease diagnosis and therapy. When combined with GMTs, these materials can theoretically greatly improve cell-specific and spatiotemporally controlled genetic manipulation at both molecular and cellular levels. Moreover, biomaterial-assisted genetic manipulation offers several practical advantages, including cost-effectiveness, operational simplicity, and a high degree of tunability, rendering the platform highly adaptable to a wide range of research contexts. Collectively, Materialogenetics establishes a conceptual framework that bridges the fields of materials science and genetics, representing a versatile and promising platform for achieving precise genetic manipulation in complex biological systems.

Introduction

Since the mid-20th century, following the establishment of the gene concept and the central dogma of genetic information transfer, researchers have consistently endeavored to elucidate the biological functions of genes that govern organ development, disease progression, and tissue regeneration, etc. To address these research needs, a diverse array of genetic manipulation technologies (GMTs) has been developed, gradually forming an integrated technical framework that encompasses gene cloning, transfection, and gene expression regulation. In general, the applications of GMTs in basic biomedical research include at least these key areas: (1) elucidation of molecular functions and examination of intracellular or intercellular signaling mechanisms [1], (2) labeling of specific cell populations, tracing of cell lineages, and exploration of cell–cell communication [2], (3) investigation of the cellular microenvironment, including the detection and monitoring of chemical molecules, physical factors, and biomolecules within and/or surrounding cells [3], and (4) execution of preclinical studies for molecular diagnosis, disease monitoring, and therapeutic intervention [4] (Fig. 1). With the deepening of research into the physiological and pathological mechanisms of complex organs and tissues, the demands on genetic manipulation technologies (GMTs) have steadily increased. Current GMTs face limitations in cell targeting precision and cellular state responsiveness, which hinder their ability to meet current research needs. To address these issues, we herein propose a conceptual framework termed “Materialogenetics”. It is an interdisciplinary approach that resides at the confluence of biomaterials and genetic manipulation. These technologies are broadly applied in biomedical research.

Unlike nanomaterial-assisted gene delivery systems that are primarily developed to improve the in vivo delivery efficiency and bioavailability of nucleic-acid therapeutics, Materialogenetics is motivated by the limitations of current genetic manipulation technologies in biomedical research, particularly in terms of cell-specific targeting, responsiveness to stimuli, and regulatory precision. We highlight that the central goal of Materialogenetics is to provide a conceptual framework for spatiotemporally specific, subpopulation-specific, and state-specific genetic manipulation, thereby enabling more precise interrogation of the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying a variety of physiological and pathological processes at the genetic level.

The development and application of GMTs

GMTs have emerged as indispensable tools in contemporary biomedical research, facilitating the regulation of gene expression and function at the DNA, RNA, and protein levels in both physiological and pathological contexts. Traditional methodologies, such as transgenic and gene-targeting approaches, permit the direct manipulation of genes of interest to elucidate their biological roles. For instance, targeted mutations of the fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) gene have been employed to develop murine models mimicking human achondroplasia, thereby confirming the pivotal role of FGFR3 in skeletal development and disease pathogenesis [5,6]. Conditional genetic systems, such as the Cre-loxP platform, can perform high precision gene manipulation in specific tissues. In this system, Cre recombinase identifies loxP sites flanking specific exons, allowing for tissue-specific or temporally controlled gene deletion (insertion or point mutation) when used in conjunction with Cre-expressing transgenic lines (Fig. 1). Utilizing this approach, conditional FGFR3 knockout mice have been generated to explore the role of FGFR3 in cartilage development and homeostasis.

Recent advances in programmable genome-editing technologies, including ZFNs [7], TALENs [8], and particularly CRISPR/Cas systems [4,9], have revolutionized genetic research. The CRISPR/Cas9 system employs guide RNA to direct Cas9 nuclease to specific genomic loci, generating double-strand breaks that are repaired through non-homologous end joining or homology-directed repair, thereby enabling targeted gene knockout, insertion, or correction. In addition to DNA editing, gene regulation can also occur at the RNA level through RNA interference (RNAi) [10] or CRISPR/Cas13-mediated RNA cleavage [11].

GMTs can additionally regulate cellular functions at the protein level [12] and confer environmental responsiveness. For example, hypoxia-responsive CreERT2-ODD systems enable selective gene activation under low-oxygen conditions [13]. Stimulus-activated technologies such as optogenetics [14], sonogenetics [[15], [16], [17]] and magnetogenetics [18] provide spatiotemporal control of gene expression and cellular activity in vivo [19,20]. Furthermore, genetically encoded biosensors, such as dopamine sensors based on circularly permuted GFP, enable real-time monitoring of signaling molecules [21]. Collectively, these rapidly evolving GMTs provide increasingly powerful tools for genetic manipulation and functional investigation in biomedical research (Fig. 1).

Precise genetic manipulation at cellular level is needed in fundamental biomedical research

As investigations into the mechanisms underlying physiological and pathological processes become more profound, it is imperative to elucidate the specific contributions of distinct tissue or cell types to phenotypic outcomes, especially within complex biological systems/tissues composed of multiple interacting components. For example, our research group has developed a mouse model carrying the P253R point mutation in the fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2), which faithfully replicates the phenotypic characteristics of human Apert syndrome [22]. This model demonstrates craniofacial malformations affecting the brain, calvarial bones (sutures), and cranial base synchondroses. The intricate developmental interactions among these tissues have complicated the identification of the specific roles of distinct tissues—namely the brain, calvarial bone, and cranial base synchondroses—in contributing to craniofacial malformations, since mutant FGFR2-P253R protein is globally expressed in multiple tissues including them. This complexity has impeded the determination of the primarily affected tissue mainly responsible for craniofacial deformation, thereby affecting the development of targeted surgical therapies for Apert syndrome. To address this issue, we employed Cre-driver mice to induce tissue-specific expression of FGFR2-P253R in the brain (using Nestin-Cre), calvarial bone (using OC-Cre), and cartilage (using Col2a1-Cre), respectively. Our results demonstrated that activation of mutant FGFR2 in any of these tissues led to varying degrees of skull phenotypes [23]. These findings suggest that the craniofacial deformities observed in Apert syndrome arise from the collective developmental abnormalities of the brain, calvarial bones, and cranial base cartilage. Consequently, current surgical therapies that focus solely on the calvarial bone (suture) may be insufficient. This study underscores the critical importance of tissue-specific genetic interventions in elucidating disease etiology.

However, tissues are composed of heterogeneous cell populations, and targeting an entire tissue does not resolve the functional contributions of individual cell lineages or subtypes within that tissue. Therefore, the ability to achieve cellular subtype-specific genetic manipulation has become increasingly essential. While current GMTs exhibit high precision in molecular targeting, their precision at the cellular level remains inadequate. Moreover, these technologies generally lack the capability to respond to and manipulate cellular functional states, which is a significant limitation given the increasing demand for precision in biomedical research. Present cell-targeting strategies primarily rely on tissue-specific promoters (TSPs), spatially controlled physical activation, or the tissue tropism of viral vectors. However, viral tropism can result in construct expression across a broad range of tissue types, complicating the precise targeting of specific cell populations within those tissues [24]. Physical stimuli such as light and heat is difficult to penetrate into deep tissues or cells [25]. Although ultrasound and magnetic fields can penetrate more deeply, they provide poor cellular resolution [25].

GMTs utilizing TSPs, such as the widely used Cre-loxP system, remain a common approach for cell-specific gene targeting; however, their specificity is often insufficient. For example, although the promoter of the chondrocyte marker gene Col2a1 predominantly facilitates the expression of target genes (such as CreER) in chondrocytes, our research group, along with others, has observed its activity in additional cell lineages, including mesenchymal precursor cells [26]. This observation suggests a compromise in the targeting specificity of CreER when regulated by this promoter. Furthermore, many cell types lack thoroughly characterized TSPs, which complicates precise genetic manipulation at cellular level. Importantly, advancements in single-cell omics have revealed increasingly detailed cell populations and significant functional heterogeneity among cell subtypes [27]. Consequently, traditional genetic targeting approaches that rely on a single promoter are inadequate to address such complexity. To enhance cellular targeting precision, researchers have developed advanced strategies such as molecular complementation (e.g., Split-Cre [28,29], Split-Cas9 [30]) and dual-recombinase systems [31]. For instance, in dual-recombinase technology, Cre and Dre recombinases are governed by distinct TSPs, resulting in the creation of TSP1-Cre and TSP2-Dre constructs. When combined with their specific recognition sites, loxP and rox, this system facilitates precise targeting of cells in regions where TSP1 and TSP2 expression overlap or are mutually exclusive, thereby aligning with specific research objectives. In comparison to single-recombinase systems, dual-recombinase systems provide a more restricted targeting range, thereby enhancing the precision of cell-specific targeting. For example, to trace the fate of senescent (p16+) macrophages (F4/80+) or senescent endothelial cells (Cdh5+) during liver injury and repair, Zhou's research group crossed F4/80-Dre or Cdh5-DreER mice with p16-CreER mice and R26-RL-tdT reporter mice (Rosa26-rox-STOP-rox-loxP-STOP-loxP-tdTomato). In this experimental configuration, only cells co-expressing both Dre and Cre, such as senescent macrophages or senescent endothelial cells, underwent excision of both stop cassettes, leading to tdTomato expression. This strategy enabled more precise cell-specific lineage labeling and functional analysis [32].

Nevertheless, as the classification of cell subpopulations involved in physiological and pathological processes becomes increasingly refined, dual-recombinase technology often falls short of achieving the precision necessary for targeting specific cell subpopulations. Theoretically, enhancing precision could be achieved by increasing the number of TSPs and recombinases in multi-recombinase systems. However, identifying multiple TSPs with perfect overlapping expression patterns presents a significant challenge. Furthermore, limitations such as the labor-intensive and time-consuming process of generating transgenic animals with multiple genetically modified alleles, coupled with low combinatorial efficiency, restrict the feasibility of these approaches.

Challenges in targeted genetic manipulation of cells defined by spatial localization, cellular subtype, and functional state

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent degenerative joint disease and a leading cause of disability worldwide. Clinically, OA is characterized by joint swelling, impaired mobility, inflammation, and chronic pain. OA impacts multiple joints, and is characterized by the involvement of various joint tissues, each consisting of multiple cells with different subtypes and functional states [33]. Consequently, it is essential to elucidate the functions and mechanisms of specific cell populations, defined by joint locations, tissue origins, subtype identities, and functional states, to enhance our understanding of OA pathogenesis.

In prior investigations, using classic GMTs, our group has thoroughly examined the roles and associated molecular mechanisms of fibroblast growth factors (FGFs)/FGF receptors (FGFRs) signaling in the onset and progression of OA [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. Nonetheless, throughout these studies, we have increasingly recognized the limitations of current GMTs in achieving precise genetic manipulation at the cellular level. This underscores the urgent necessity for the development of advanced, high-resolution, cell-specific research methodologies. By utilizing research of the roles of FGFs/FGFRs signaling in OA as a case study, we underscore the pressing need for advanced research methodologies that enable more accurate, cell-specific genetic manipulation to more thoroughly dissect the OA mechanisms.

Targeted genetic manipulation of cells within specific joints and distinct articular tissues is required. As depicted in Fig. 2a and b, OA is a systemic condition that impacts multiple joints such as the hip, knee, and temporomandibular joints, and involves a variety of articular tissues including the synovium, meniscus, articular cartilage, and subchondral bone. Early studies have shown that global deletion of Fgfr3 gene (Fgfr3−/−) in mice results in the development of OA in the knee joint [38]. However, such systemic knockout models are limited in their ability to elucidate site-specific or cell-type-specific functions of Fgfr3 across various joints, and among distinct articular cell populations such as chondrocytes, synovial macrophages, and synovial fibroblasts. To address this limitation, our group developed a chondrocyte-specific conditional knockout mouse model by crossing Col2a1-CreERT2 mice with Fgfr3fl/fl mice. Utilizing this model, we demonstrated that conditional deletion of Fgfr3 in Col2a1+ chondrocytes exacerbates osteoarthritis pathology in both the temporomandibular and knee joints [36,37]. However, because Col2a1+ chondrocytes are ubiquitous across joints, this strategy inevitably yields a multi-joint phenotype, which represents a key limitation for dissecting joint-specific roles of Fgfr3 in maintaining articular homeostasis. Furthermore, although the Col2a1 promoter is predominantly active in chondrocytes, it has also been reported to drive gene expression in certain non-chondrocytic cell populations, including macrophages and skeletal stem cells. Consequently, research utilizing Col2a1-CreERT2; Fgfr3fl/fl mice also lacks sufficient specificity to unambiguously delineate the joint site-specific and cell-type (chondrocyte)-specific functions of FGFR3 signaling within articular tissues. Precise genetic manipulation of chondrocytes within specific spatial domains of articular cartilage is required. Articular cartilage (AC) is a highly specialized connective tissue that covers and protects the ends of bones within synovial joints. Chondrocytes constitute the sole resident cell population in AC, and play a pivotal role in maintaining cartilage homeostasis and driving disease progression [39]. The degeneration of AC is recognized as the primary pathological feature of OA. At tissue level, AC can be divided into distinct spatial domains, including the superficial zone (SZ), middle zone (MZ), deep zone (DZ), and calcified cartilage zone (Fig. 2c). Emerging evidence suggests that FGFR3 is broadly expressed in chondrocytes across the SZ, MZ, and DZ. Likewise, the Col2a1 promoter is transcriptionally active in chondrocytes throughout these cartilage zones. As a result, the commonly employed Col2a1-CreERT2 genetic model, which facilitates the conditional knockout of FGFR3 in all Col2a1+ cells, does not allow for precise targeting of chondrocytes within specific zones of AC. This lack of spatial precision hampers rigorous dissection of the zone-dependent functions of FGFR3 signaling during OA initiation and progression. Notably, OA progression is characterized by pronounced spatial heterogeneity within articular cartilage (Fig. 2c), with degenerative and normal chondrocytes unevenly distributed across different cartilage regions. Distinguishing chondrocytes residing in degenerative versus non-degenerative regions, followed by the targeted genetic manipulation of these distinct chondrocyte subpopulations, remains technically challenging with current GMTs.

Consequently, development of conceptual framework that facilitate the more precise genetic manipulation of cells within defined spatial domains is critically important for advancing mechanistic studies.

Targeted genetic manipulation of specific chondrocyte subtypes is required. The advancement of single-cell omics technologies has enabled a more refined characterization of cell heterogeneity. Single-cell transcriptomic studies have demonstrated that chondrocytes, once considered a relatively homogeneous population, comprise multiple distinct subtypes. These subtypes may be defined by characteristic gene expression signatures involving more than three genes, and are associated with different differentiation trajectories and biological functions. In a previous study, chondrocytes associated with OA were categorized into various subpopulations, including homeostatic chondrocytes (HomCs), proliferative chondrocytes (ProCs), hypertrophic chondrocytes (HTCs), fibrocartilage chondrocytes (FCs), and inflammatory chondrocytes (InfCs) (Fig. 2d). Notably, each subpopulation is defined not by a single marker gene but by the coordinated expression of multiple markers. For instance, the HomCs subpopulation is distinguished by the concurrent expression of HSPA1B, HSPA1A, HSPA6, DDIT3, and JUN [40].

Despite these advances in cellular classification, dissecting their distinct function and underlying mechanisms is important but challenging, since genetically manipulating these transcriptionally defined subpopulations selectively is needed but remains largely impractical with current GMTs. Theoretically, multi-recombinase strategies enabled by expanding the repertoire of site-specific recombinases and their corresponding target recognition sequences (TRSs) based on the strategy similar to that of dual-recombinase systems, could facilitate the selective genetic manipulation of specific cell populations. In practice, however, their application is constrained by major barriers, including complex experimental design, long timelines for generating and breeding genetically modified animal models with more exogenous alleles, and inherently low efficiency when multiple recombinases and TRSs are combined.

Targeted genetic manipulation of cells in specific functional states is required. During disease progression, cells of the same lineage, and even the same cell at different time points, may adopt distinct functional states. These cellular states include, but are not limited to, variations in pH, reactive oxygen species (ROS), metabolic activity, and senescent signatures. Beyond the lack of strategies to target specific subpopulations, current GMTs also encounter considerable challenges in recognizing and selectively manipulating cells based on these functional states. To date, reliable technologies capable of state-responsive genetic manipulation are largely unavailable (Fig. 2e).

Here, we use the following mechanistic research in OA as an illustrative example. During the early stages of disease progression, a subset of chondrocytes experiences oxidative stress and shifts into an aberrant high-ROS state, while other chondrocytes maintain normal ROS levels. The proportion of high-ROS chondrocytes changes dynamically as OA advances. Therefore, the ability to accurately identify high-ROS chondrocytes, monitor their state in real time, and selectively manipulate target genes of them at early stages of OA would be highly valuable for dissecting initiating pathogenic events of OA. Such insights could, in turn, inform strategies to delay or potentially prevent articular cartilage degeneration. Nonetheless, achieving precise identification and targeted genetic manipulation of chondrocytes in different functional states remains a significant challenge that is difficult to address by current GMTs alone.

Consequently, there is a pressing demand for innovative technologies that enable precise and targeted genetic manipulation of specific cells in terms of their spatial localization, phenotypic subpopulations, and functional states.

Convergence of engineered nanomaterials and genetic manipulation technologies

In recent years, significant advancements have been made in the development and application of biomaterials in biomedical fields, particularly in the contexts of disease diagnosis and therapy. Among these, biomedical nanomaterials have garnered considerable attention and have been extensively utilized as delivery platforms for therapeutic agents, including small-molecular drugs, growth factors, and nucleic acids, etc. [41,42]. By rationally designing their chemical composition, microstructure, and surface properties, nanomaterials can be endowed with active-targeting and stimuli-responsive capabilities, significantly enhancing biosafety, targeting specificity, and therapeutic efficacy [43]. In our previous paper, we conducted a systematic review of engineered nanomaterials for the treatment of intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD), emphasizing how active-targeting and stimuli-responsive designs facilitate more precise therapeutic interventions [44]. Building on these insights, we propose that these features are well aligned with the core requirements of cell-specific genetic manipulation. Consequently, the utilization of biomedical nanomaterials constitutes an innovative and promising approach for the development of cell-specific GMTs.

Cell-specific genetic manipulation using active-targeting nanomaterials. Active targeting nanomaterials refer to nanomaterials-based delivery systems functionalized with specific targeting ligands that enable selective binding to target cells or extracellular matrix components through ligand–receptor or ligand–matrix interactions [45,46]. Common targeting moieties include small molecules, antibodies, peptides, aptamers, and biomimetic cell-membrane coatings. By exploiting these recognition mechanisms, ligand-modified nanocarriers allow for the preferential delivery of therapeutic agents (such as small-molecule drugs, growth factors, and nucleic acids) to specific tissues or cell populations, thereby enhancing delivery efficiency while minimizing systemic exposure and off-target effects. For instance, Chen et al. demonstrated that the expression of protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2) is significantly upregulated on the plasma membrane of osteogenically differentiated valvular interstitial cells (OD-VICs). Accordingly, they developed a nanocarrier functionalized with PAR2-targeting hexapeptide for active targeting drug delivery to OD-VICs, aiming to alleviate aortic valve calcification [47].

Building on these insights, we propose that genetic manipulation molecules (GMMs) can also be encapsulated within nanomaterials, and combined with surface-presented targeting moieties to enable cell-specific genetic manipulation (Fig. 3a). Here, GMMs refer to molecules that regulate gene expression/function, and primarily include direct executors (e.g., DNA, RNA and proteins) and inducers (e.g., tamoxifen and doxycycline). Upon administration, these engineered nanomaterials will preferentially accumulate at target sites, facilitating the precise delivery of GMMs to specific tissues or cell populations (Fig. 3b). As a result, targeted genetic manipulation can be achieved in cells defined by particular spatial localization or cell subtypes. Using research in OA as an example, the differences in the extracellular matrix composition between cartilage and other joint tissues (such as the meniscus and synovium) can be exploited by designing nanomaterials that target cartilage-specific matrix components, including type II collagen (Col2a1) and aggrecan, thus enabling chondrocyte-specific genetic intervention [48]. Furthermore, by optimizing the size and surface charge of the nanomaterials, GMMs could theoretically be delivered to distinct layers of articular cartilage, allowing for the manipulation of chondrocytes within specific layers. Moreover, the compositional variations between degenerative and healthy cartilage regions present additional opportunities for region-specific targeting. In a recent study, Dewani et al. developed anionic nanoparticles to specifically target OA lesions by leveraging the progressive loss of negatively charged glycosaminoglycan (GAG) in the extracellular matrix. This strategy facilitated lesion-specific, severity-responsive delivery of ghrelin mRNA, which demonstrated substantial therapeutic effectiveness in OA, as evidenced by reduced cartilage degeneration, decreased subchondral bone thickening, and alleviated nociceptive pain [49]. Referring to this strategy, we propose that lesion-targeted genetic manipulation can be achieved through the combination of such anionic nanoparticles with GMMs. Additionally, calcified cartilage regions exhibit an enrichment of hydroxyapatite compared to non-calcified cartilage [50]. By functionalizing nanomaterial surfaces with hydroxyapatite-binding molecules, such as bisphosphonates or tetracycline, we expect that targeted delivery of GMMs to cells within calcified cartilage regions can be achieved.

In parallel, advancements in single-cell technologies have facilitated the classification of articular chondrocytes into multiple subpopulations based on distinct gene expression profiles. By identifying surface markers unique to individual chondrocyte subtypes, it is possible to conjugate corresponding antibodies to nanomaterial surfaces. This approach may enable selective accumulation of nanocarriers to target chondrocyte subpopulations through specific ligand–receptor interactions, facilitating precise delivery of GMMs and efficient execution of genetic regulation.

Cell-specific genetic manipulation using stimuli-responsive nanomaterials. Stimuli-responsive nanomaterials represent a class of materials that are sensitive to external stimuli (such as temperature, ultrasound, light, and magnetic fields) or endogenous signals (such as pH, enzymes, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and glucose) [51]. In response to respective stimuli, these nanomaterials undergo dynamic changes in their physical structures or chemical properties, which subsequently trigger the release of the loaded cargos, thereby allowing for on-demand delivery [52]. These nanomaterials have been extensively investigated for their potential in controlled therapeutic release at lesion sites associated with diseases such as cancer, atherosclerosis, and OA, resulting in enhanced therapeutic safety and efficacy. Building on these advancements, we propose their novel application in the realm of genetic manipulation. By encapsulating GMMs within stimuli-responsive nanomaterials, the release of GMMs can be precisely controlled under specific stimulatory conditions (Fig. 3a and b). This approach facilitates selective genetic intervention in cells exhibiting distinct functional states, thereby addressing the persistent challenge of distinguishing and targeting cells with varying physiological or pathological conditions.

In OA, for instance, a subset of chondrocytes experiences oxidative stress during OA progression, displaying significantly elevated intracellular ROS levels compared to normal chondrocytes [53]. Conventional GMTs are limited in their capacity to selectively target genes within chondrocytes with high-ROS, thus constraining related mechanistic studies. By encapsulating GMMs within ROS-responsive nanomaterials, such as poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid)–thioketal–polyethylene glycol (PLGA–thioketal–PEG) nano micelles [54], the release of GMMs can be precisely activated by elevated ROS levels. This approach facilitates targeted gene editing in degenerative chondrocytes, offering a robust tool for elucidating the molecular mechanisms. Additionally, this method allows for the labeling of cells in other functional states, enabling the longitudinal tracking of their developmental trajectories. Beyond single-cue responsiveness, the pathological microenvironment is characterized by a complex array of regulatory factors that exhibit dynamic and stage-dependent variations throughout disease progression. Through rational material design, it is therefore possible to engineer multi-stimuli-responsive nanomaterials that can simultaneously respond to multiple cues. In the future, with continued advances in in vivo probe detection and functional materials science, it may become possible to precisely distinguish relevant molecular/signal levels in physiological and pathological settings. Further refinement of material design may then enable higher-precision genetic regulatory systems.

Tunability and convenience of nanomaterial-assisted genetic manipulation systems. In addition to their advanced active-targeting and stimuli-responsive capabilities, nanomaterial systems demonstrate remarkable structural diversity and design flexibility, with their physicochemical properties (e.g., composition, structure, size, and charge) being adaptable to meet specific research requirements. This versatility enables the efficient delivery of a wide range of functional cargos, including small molecules, nucleic acids, and proteins, thereby accommodating the diverse demands of genetic manipulation. Furthermore, biomedical nanomaterials offer advantages in terms of ease of fabrication and operational simplicity. Their modular design allows for rapid customization through the assembly of material frameworks and GMMs, facilitating flexible adaptation to various experimental contexts.

Collectively, the integration of nanomaterials with conventional GMTs is anticipated to enhance more precise and efficient genetic regulation at both molecular and cellular scales. This combination provides a robust platform for investigating the cellular and molecular mechanisms of organ development, disease pathogenesis, and tissue regeneration.

Conceptual framework of Materialogenetics. Based on the aforementioned considerations, as illustrated in Fig. 4, we propose a genetic manipulation strategy that leverages the flexibility, designability and multifunctionality of biomaterials. This strategy integrates functional modules, such as targeting moieties and stimuli-responsive units, into a single biomaterial platform. Furthermore, these biomaterial platforms are combined with well-established genetic technologies, including the Cre-loxP system and CRISPR-mediated gene editing, to achieve more precise genetic manipulation of target cells. This approach is anticipated to facilitate comprehensive investigations of molecular and cellular mechanisms with enhanced spatial and cellular resolution. Such biomaterial platforms are then combined with well-established genetic technologies, including the Cre-loxP system and CRISPR-mediated gene editing tools, to achieve more precise genetic manipulation of target cells. This conceptual strategy holds broad potential for applications in fundamental biomedical research.

As a promising platform for genetic manipulation, Materialogenetics achieves goals that are challenging to accomplish with conventional GMTs alone. These include but are not limited to the ability to target cells within specific spatial regions through extracellular matrix-mediated recognition, and to selectively regulate cells in distinct functional states via stimuli-responsive mechanisms. Furthermore, this approach can facilitate the synergistic effects of multiple regulatory modules of biomaterials, thereby enhancing the precision and flexibility of genetic manipulation. For instance, the integration of active-targeting and stimuli-responsive modules allows for highly precise spatiotemporal genetic manipulation of cells. Additionally, by integrating biomaterial systems with classical genetic technologies, such as dual recombinase strategies and fluorescent reporter-based labeling, Materialogenetics enables precise cell labeling, lineage tracing, and functional regulation of target cells defined by specific anatomical tissues/regions, cellular subtypes, and functional states.

Collectively, Materialogenetics holds immense potential to improve our ability to more precisely control and manipulate genetic processes, paving the way for advanced studies in mechanism elucidation and therapeutic development with enhanced spatial and cellular specificity.

The application of “Materialogenetics” concept in disease diagnosis and therapy

Currently, there is a limited number of studies exploring the application of Materialogenetics in elucidating cellular and molecular mechanisms. Nonetheless, an increasing body of research in the fields of disease diagnosis and therapy has successfully integrated strategies similar to Materialogenetics. In a previous study, Du et al. developed gadolinium-doped carbon dots (Gd-CDs) as imaging probes and modified their surface with folic acid (FA) to actively target liver cancer cells. These nanoprobes not only enable effective and dynamic monitoring of tumors but also minimize toxic and side effects on surrounding normal tissues [55]. In a separate investigation, Wang et al. incorporated small interfering RNA (siRNA) against ADAM19 (ADAM19 siRNA) into mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) that were modified with a polygalactose coating, thus creating an intelligent delivery system aimed at senescent chondrocytes. Upon uptaken by healthy chondrocytes, the polygalactose coating of the nanocarrier maintains its structural integrity, thereby preventing the premature release of ADAM19 siRNA [56]. In contrast, when internalized by senescent chondrocytes, the polygalactose coating undergoes specific degradation by β-galactosidase, an enzyme that is highly expressed in senescent cells. This degradation facilitates the targeted release of ADAM19 siRNA, which effectively suppresses the catabolic activity of these chondrocytes. Although these targeting nano-systems are primarily designed for disease diagnosis and therapy, they can act as proof-of-concept cases to demonstrate the feasibility of the key elements in Materialogenetics, namely active targeting and stimuli-responsive, suggesting that similar strategies can be adapted for fundamental biomedical research such as cell labeling/lineage tracing, gene function and disease mechanism studying.

Outlook

The integration of biomaterials with genetic technologies (e.g., CRISPR and RNAi) has demonstrated considerable potential in genetic manipulation for disease diagnosis and therapy [57]. These advancements have further spurred the expansion of biomaterial-enabled genetic strategies into fundamental biomedical research. In this context, we introduce Materialogenetics, a conceptual framework that provides facile, versatile, and promising toolkits for investigating the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying a variety of physiological and pathological processes including organ development, disease pathogenesis, and injury repair. Given the pronounced heterogeneity of cells in terms of their spatial distribution, cellular subtypes, and functional states, traditional genetic manipulation technologies face inherent challenges in accurately identifying and modulating specific cell populations within complex biological systems. We propose that Materialogenetics can partially overcome these challenges by incorporating active-targeting and/or stimuli-responsive nanomaterials into genetic manipulation, thereby facilitating cell-type-selective delivery and spatiotemporal activation of genetic payloads. Besides enhancing cell targeting, Materialogenetics has the potential to greatly transform the experimental framework of genetic manipulation by enhancing cell and tissue specificity, and reducing the duration of experiments and improving their efficiency. Firstly, the delivery method can be customized to be either systemic or localized, thereby accommodating a wide range of experimental needs. Secondly, it allows for the delivery of various genetic manipulation related agents, compatible with different genetic modulation systems. For example, in animal models expressing CreER and carrying a floxed allele, biomaterial systems can be engineered to deliver tamoxifen (TM) to induce recombination at specific target cells. Alternatively, biomaterials can be designed to deliver CreER-encoding vectors to specific tissues or cell populations, with subsequent local or systemic TM administration to initiate genetic modification at specific cell. Thirdly, Materialogenetics offers significant potential both as a framework for mechanistic studies and as a platform for therapeutic delivery. In this evolving technological context, a research paradigm focused on “identifying pathogenic mechanisms, enabling precise diagnosis, and implementing targeted intervention” is expected to become increasingly feasible. Such advancements have the potential to establish a cohesive technological foundation that bridges mechanistic research with personalized therapy. Collectively, these characteristics position Materialogenetics as a promising field for both fundamental research and clinical translation.

However, despite these advantages, Materialogenetics remains in its nascent stages and encounters significant technical challenges. Multifunctional nanoplatforms must balance particle size, surface charge, targeting ligands, responsive groups, and cargo loading efficiency. Integrating these functions often creates design trade-offs, making it difficult to simultaneously achieve high targeting specificity, strong responsiveness, good biocompatibility, and efficient gene delivery. In addition, nanomaterials may show cytotoxicity, immunogenicity, off-target accumulation, and unpredictable metabolic behavior in vivo. During circulation, they are also affected by protein corona formation, biological barriers, and host immune responses, all of which may reduce targeting efficiency and complicate controlled delivery.

Within the framework of Materialogenetics, the physicochemical properties of nanomaterials profoundly affect their cellular uptake, intracellular trafficking, and endosomal escape efficiency, thereby influencing the delivery efficiency and precision of GMMs. In addition, stimulus signals in the cellular microenvironment may induce physicochemical changes in nanomaterials, which may affect cell phenotypes, biological functions, and the cellular microenvironment.

Although biomaterials exhibit considerable diversity, existing nanomaterial platforms are inadequate to fulfill the heterogeneous delivery requirements across various tissues and cell types. Consequently, context-adapted design strategies are necessary to facilitate tissue- and cell-specific control. Furthermore, ongoing enhancements in targeting efficacy and response sensitivity are crucial to augment the efficiency and precision of cell-type-specific genetic manipulation.

Current genetic manipulation technologies (GMTs) inherently face challenges in achieving complete gene modulation efficiency. While biomaterials have the potential to enhance cellular targeting and stimulus responsiveness, the incorporation of multiple regulatory components may further diminish overall efficiency. Consequently, Materialogenetics is currently more appropriate for applications with relatively low efficiency demands, such as cell lineage tracing (including sparse labeling), where only a subset of cells requires labeling [58,59]. Future research should prioritize improving the efficiency of biomaterial delivery and increasing payload capacity. Although Materialogenetics can enhance cellular targeting, the imperfect specificity (“leakiness”) of individual targeting elements poses challenges in completely eliminating off-target effects. As a result, unintended modulation of non-target cells may still occur. Employing multiplexing targeting strategies, such as co-displaying two or more distinct targeting ligands, could theoretically enhance specificity and mitigate the risk of off-target effects.

Beyond issues described above, the in vivo application of Materialogenetics still faces several key bottlenecks, and several potential solutions may help address these challenges. First, after entering the body, nanomaterials may form protein corona that prevents surface targeting ligands, thereby reducing targeting efficiency and affecting cell uptake specificity. In the future, surface engineering strategies such as zwitterionic polymers and polyphenol-based modification, may help reduce nonspecific protein adsorption and improve targeting efficiency. Second, ligands such as antibodies and peptides are prone to enzymatic degradation or conformational inactivation in the bloodstream or joint cavity, which can reduce targeting performance. The use of highly stable small-molecule ligands may improve stability. Third, off-target uptake remains a concern, even with active targeting capability, nanomaterials may still be taken up by non-target cells in vivo. Further optimization of material size, surface charge, and shape, combined with a “dual-lock” strategy based on two cell-surface markers, may improve cell selectivity. Fourth, regarding long-term toxicity, researchers should prioritize widely studied materials with favorable biodegradability and well-defined clearance pathways, such as LNPs and PLGA. Moreover, future studies should also establish long-term in vivo evaluation systems to ensure safety. By rationally integrating material composition, targeting ligands, and responsive units, conflicts among multiple functions can be effectively balanced. Combined with synergistic optimization at the physical, chemical, and biological levels, Materialogenetics is expected to enable more efficient, precise, and translatable in vivo applications.

The development of emerging cutting-edge technologies is expected to provide further impetus for the iterative optimization of Materialogenetics. High-resolution omics technologies, such as single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, can provide powerful tool for precise evaluation of cell-type-specific transcriptional responses, dynamic phenotypic transitions, and spatial alterations following material-mediated genetic manipulation. Emerging computational technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI), virtual cells, and digital twins may further promote the development of Materialogenetics. AI-based approaches have already demonstrated strong capabilities in high-throughput drug screening, multidimensional biological data analysis and biomaterials design for oral oncology [60], the similar strategies could be adapted to accelerate the screening and design of nanomaterial within the framework of Materialogenetics. In addition, virtual cell and digital twin technologies may provide predictive platforms for simulating interactions among materials, cellular microenvironments, signaling pathways, and gene regulatory networks prior to in vivo validation.

With ongoing advances in biotechnology, particularly in the discovery of cell-specific marker molecules and optimization of gene editing systems, as well as progress in materials science, including AI-assisted material screening and optimization, and the design of novel active-targeting/stimuli-responsive materials, Materialogenetics is anticipated to become a pivotal enabling technology for next-generation precision life-science research and personalized medicine.
