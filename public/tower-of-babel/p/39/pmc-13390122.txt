mRNA delivery to the retina restores REP1 function in choroideremia
Authors: Luisa de Lemos, Pedro Antas, Cláudia Carvalho, Mariana Castro, Diogo Bruno, Ana F. Fonseca, Shuvajit Rakshit, Miguel C. Seabra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inherited retinal diseases (IRDs) represent a major cause of blindness, yet current viral vector-based gene therapies are limited by high cost, restricted cargo capacity, and safety concerns related to immunogenicity. We explore here retinal delivery of non-viral, in-vitro-transcribed mRNA using lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) as a safe, cost-effective gene augmentation strategy for choroideremia (CHM). CHM is an X-linked IRD caused by loss-of-function mutations in CHM that encodes Rab escort protein-1 (REP1), which is essential for Rab GTPases prenylation and intracellular trafficking. We demonstrate that subretinal delivery of human CHM-mRNA (hCHM-mRNA) efficiently targets the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and choroid, achieving expression that is detectable up to 15 days post-injection, with lower and transient inflammatory response compared to AAV2 vectors. Furthermore, hCHM-mRNA delivery in vitro to human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived RPE cells from a CHM patient and in vivo via subretinal injection in the Chm± mouse model successfully restored REP1 levels and reversed Rab prenylation defect. Concordantly, Chm± treated with hCHM-mRNA showed functional rescue of retinal activity measured by electroretinography 24 h post-injection. Overall, these results provide a strong proof-of-concept for LNP-mediated mRNA technology in the retina, supporting the development of scalable, non-viral gene approaches for IRDs, including CHM.

Introduction

Inherited retinal diseases (IRDs) are a diverse group of genetic disorders that cause progressive retinal degeneration, leading to severe visual impairment or even blindness. It is predicted that IRDs affect approximately 1 in 2,000 people worldwide.1 Due to their overall impact, significant progress has been made in decoding the genetic origins of IRDs, with around 300 causative genes identified to date.1 These blinding conditions result from monogenic mutations that affect photoreceptors and/or the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), both of which are crucial to support retinal function and vision. The most prevalent IRDs include Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA), retinitis pigmentosa (RP), and choroideremia (CHM).2

Gene-based therapeutics hold significant promise as potential treatment strategies for patients with IRDs. The eye is a prime target for novel therapies due to its accessibility, capacity for non-invasive monitoring, compartmentalization, immune-privileged status, and optical transparency. Moreover, in translational studies and controlled trials, the contralateral eye can serve as an internal control, providing more robust outcome analyses.3,4,5 Among gene delivery methods, adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors have emerged as the most effective vector for targeting the retina, and, to date, are the only vectors with proven benefits in treating IRDs.6,7,8 The approval of voretigene neparvovec-rzyl (Luxturna) by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for LCA has significantly advanced retinal gene therapy, establishing subretinal delivery of AAV as the leading approach for treating various monogenic IRDs.9 Gene replacement therapy currently forms the basis of most active clinical trials for IRDs, delivering a functional copy of the pathogenic gene into target retinal cells via viral vectors.10,11,12,13 Despite advances in viral-based therapies, safety concerns associated with mutagenic integration, immune activation, limited cargo capacity, and repeated administration highlight the need to explore new alternatives of gene therapy for IRDs.14,15,16,17,18,19,20 Moreover, a recent report has identified the presence of contaminating plasmid sequences and disrupted vector genomes during the manufacturing of AAV-based gene therapies. These production artifacts, together with the risk of cellular immune responses to the vector or transgene product, further emphasize the urgent need to develop safer non-viral delivery systems.21

Given the limitations of current viral approaches for eye diseases, non-viral nanoparticles (NPs) offer a unique set of advantages such as being easily synthesized and manipulated. Furthermore, they have a low production cost, a large vector capacity, and low immunogenicity.22,23 Over the past decade, a large number of non-viral vectors have been developed for delivering DNA as the therapeutic choice. However, DNA delivery using NPs is inherently inefficient due to the nuclear entry requirement in post-mitotic retinal cells.22,24 Recently, in-vitro-transcribed mRNA has emerged as an efficient tool to achieve high gene expression. mRNA-based gene delivery avoids genome integration and associated safety concerns, as it enables rapid protein synthesis directly in the cytoplasm without requiring nuclear entry.24 Its relatively short half-life makes mRNA particularly suitable for applications requiring transient protein expression.25 These features have positioned mRNA at the forefront of both preclinical and clinical research across several applications, including protein replacement, cancer therapy, infectious disease vaccines, gene editing, and tissue engineering.26,27,28,29

Recent studies focusing on the delivery of mRNA to the retina using non-viral particles have shown high expression of mRNA using lipid-based carriers in human retinal cells and mouse retinas.30,31,32 These findings highlight the translational potential of mRNA-based therapies for retinal diseases, while emphasizing the critical need to develop affordable and scalable therapies for widespread access.

In this study, we explore an mRNA-based system as a proof-of-concept gene augmentation strategy for CHM. CHM is a rare, monogenic X-linked IRD caused by loss-of-function mutations in CHM, which encodes Rab escort protein-1 (REP1).33,34 This retinal dystrophy is characterized by progressive vision loss during childhood and eventual blindness by mid-adulthood.35 REP1 plays a critical role in the prenylation of Rab GTPases, a post-translational modification essential for the proper functioning of intracellular vesicle trafficking. Dysfunctional REP1 impairs vesicular transport, resulting in the degeneration of RPE and photoreceptors.36 Currently, treatment options for CHM remain very limited. The recent multicenter clinical trial, REGENERATE, which used an AAV vector encoding REP1, has not met primary endpoints at 24 months post-treatment, highlighting the need for continued long-term monitoring of patients.37

Here, we investigated whether delivering human CHM-mRNA (hCHM-mRNA) using a lipid carrier can promote REP1 expression and restore its function in CHM retinal cells. Our study indicates that the delivery of hCHM-mRNA to human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived RPE from a CHM patient and via subretinal injection in a CHM mouse model successfully restores REP1 levels and enhances prenylation of Rab proteins, revealing functional recovery of intracellular trafficking pathways.

Results

GFP-mRNA delivery in hiPSC-RPE cells

To test the transfection capacity and efficiency of the mRNA-based strategy in non-dividing RPE cells, hiPSC-RPE cells were treated with mRNA coding for the green fluorescent protein (GFP-mRNA) in comparison to plasmid DNA (pDNA) or AAV2 vectors also containing GFP. For mRNA delivery, the cells were transfected with JetMESSENGER, a lipid carrier designed specifically for mRNA delivery, achieving a higher efficiency of 78.90% ± 5.29% GFP-positive cells. In CHM, the absence of functional REP1 leads to impaired Rab prenylation and consequent accumulation of unprenylated Rabs (uRabs), particularly Rab27A, in the cytosol.40 To evaluate the prenylation rescue potential of this therapy, CHM− hiPSC-RPE cells were analyzed 3 days post-treatment with hCHM-mRNA and compared with untreated CHM− and healthy control (CHM+) hiPSC-RPE cells. Western blot analysis revealed the presence of uRabs in untreated CHM− cells and a significant decrease in uRab levels in the treated CHM− hiPSC-RPE cells (Figures 2B and 2C). This decline is comparable to that observed in CHM+ hiPSC-RPE cells (Figure 2C), indicating that the restoration of REP1 in treated CHM− hiPSC-RPE cells is sufficient to rescue cytosolic Rab prenylation.

Comparison of ocular delivery routes using Cre-mRNA in Ai9 mice

Numerous clinical trials for IRDs have employed subretinal delivery as the primary and well-established retinal route for administering gene therapy to the eye.41,42,43 The in vivo mRNA delivery study started by examining the ability of mRNA complexed with the lipid reagent in vivo-jetRNA+ to target the RPE layer of the mouse eye. We compared three different ocular delivery routes (subretinal, intravitreal, and subconjunctival), using Ai9 mice carrying a Cre/lox system, which enables direct and robust fluorescence visualization of Cre protein activity in the tissue (Figure 3).44 The results demonstrated that a single subretinal injection of Cre-mRNA in Ai9 mice efficiently activated Cre recombination and induced tdTomato expression in RPE cells within 48 h (Figure 3B). This analysis confirmed the subretinal route’s superior efficiency in overcoming ocular barriers to specifically reach RPE cells, and, thus, this route was adopted for the present study. Subretinal delivery of GFP-mRNA in the mouse retina

Subsequently, we analyzed the subretinal delivery of GFP-mRNA by using the lipid-based reagent in vivo-jetRNA+ in wild-type (WT) mice and assessed the temporal expression of GFP in the mouse eye. Guided by optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans, we assessed the precise site for subretinal injection and the consequent transient retinal detachment (Figures 4A and 4B), which is commonly observed in both mice and human eyes after administering subretinal therapy.45,46 Next, by immunostaining, we confirmed the specific location of GFP within the RPE layer, contacting directly with the cone photoreceptors labeled by Peanut agglutinin (PNA) (Figures 4C and 4D), which demonstrated that the subretinal injection delivers the GFP-mRNA exclusively to the target cells, the RPE layer. The durability of GFP in the RPE flatmount was measured over 15 days post-injection, highlighted in green (Figures 4E–4H) and quantitatively represented by the mean fluorescence intensity (Figure 4I). The analysis of GFP in the RPE flatmount showed a stronger GFP signal at 2 days post-injection, followed by a significant decrease up to 15 days (Figure 4I). Subretinal delivery of hCHM-mRNA and inflammatory response induced by hCHM-mRNA or AAV2-hCHM in the mouse retina

To study an mRNA-based therapeutic approach for IRDs, specifically CHM, we produced hCHM-mRNA to deliver subretinally in the mouse eye. Using RNAscope in situ hybridization, we assessed the spatial and temporal distribution of the exogenous hCHM-mRNA across the retina. Our analysis demonstrated that the hCHM-mRNA was predominantly dispersed throughout the RPE and choroid and detected up to 15 days post-injection (Figure 5A). The mRNA molecule is considered quite unstable and can be rapidly degraded by nucleases in biological tissues.47,48 However, in the mouse retina, hCHM-mRNA remained detectable for 15 days post-injection, demonstrating that the in-vitro-transcribed mRNA, which includes a Cap-1 structure at the 5′ end and a poly(A) tail at the 3′ end, can effectively mimic endogenous mRNA and be protected against degradation by exonucleases.39 In addition, degradation may be slower in non-dividing cells, where mRNA turnover is reduced, allowing transcripts to be retained for longer periods. Furthermore, we assessed the inflammatory response following subretinal injection of hCHM-mRNA and AAV2-hCHM by analyzing immunoreactivity of GFAP and Iba1, markers of astrocytes and microglia/macrophages used as surrogate indicators of neuroinflammation (Figure 5B). Both markers showed an increase in fluorescence intensity after mRNA delivery, indicative of transient glial activation and microglial recruitment. However, this response was effectively controlled by administration of the most commonly used corticosteroids, prednisolone and dexamethasone,50 at 3 days post-injection (Figures 5C and 5D). In contrast, subretinal injection of AAV2-hCHM elicited a more sustained inflammatory response of GFAP and Iba1, even in the presence of corticosteroid treatment (Figures 5C and 5D), which is consistent with previous findings of prolonged innate immune activation following AAV administration.51,52

Additionally, we evaluated the expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines (Il-1α, Tnf-α, and Ifn-γ) at 1 and 3 days following subretinal injection. Both AAV2-hCHM and hCHM-mRNA delivery induced a mild expression profile for Il-1α and Tnf-α. In contrast, Ifn-γ expression levels were mitigated by the presence of corticosteroids following hCHM-mRNA injection when compared with the viral vector AAV2-hCHM (Figures S1A and S1B), consistent with the histochemical analysis of Iba1 and GFAP. Despite these localized immune responses, the overall structural integrity was maintained. Evaluation of total retinal thickness by in vivo OCT demonstrated a preserved retinal morphology on central and peripheral B scans at 1 and 3 days after subretinal injection of hCHM-mRNA or AAV2-hCHM, compared with uninjected controls (Figures S1C and S1D).

Furthermore, to evaluate the efficiency of hCHM-mRNA delivery in restoring retinal function, electroretinography (ERG) was conducted 24 h post-injection. The maximum a-wave amplitude, reflecting photoreceptor activity, was significantly altered in Chm± compared to WT but improved following hCHM-mRNA treatment (Figure 6C). Discussion

Recent advances in mRNA engineering have opened new avenues for the development of non-viral mRNA therapeutic approaches, offering a promising alternative to current gene therapies.39 IRDs, resulting in progressive vision loss and blindness, are prime candidates for such innovative therapies. Although viral vector-based gene augmentation therapy has been the gold standard for treating IRDs, exemplified by the FDA-approved AAV2 vector for treating type 2 LCA, these approaches face limitations related to cost, restricted vector capacity, and potential host immunogenicity.9,55,56

To address these limitations, our study demonstrates the robust potential of a non-viral, lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-based mRNA delivery system for gene augmentation therapy in the eye. In this study, we focused on CHM, a disease we have been studying for years.57,58 We initially established the superior transfection efficiency of our mRNA strategy in vitro compared with both pDNA and AAV2 vectors in hiPSC-RPE cells. Enhanced efficacy was achieved, while maintaining high cell viability compared with the AAV2-GFP strategy. Following subretinal delivery of GFP-mRNA, protein expression localized exclusively to the RPE and choroid layers and was detectable for up to 15 days post-injection. This persistence is notable given the rapid turnover of non-engineered mRNA, although GFP is known to have slow turnover. Furthermore, a comparative analysis of the inflammatory response (microglial activation via Iba1 and glial activation via GFAP) revealed that while the hCHM-mRNA NPs caused a transient, acute response, this effect was effectively mitigated by standard corticosteroid co-administration. Importantly, the AAV2-hCHM injection elicited a sustained inflammatory signature, even in the presence of corticosteroids, further supporting the lower innate immunogenicity profile of the non-viral mRNA system.55

While our study evaluated the administration of corticosteroids after injection, steroid pretreatment is widely established in clinical human gene therapies and represents a promising strategy for future research to further control the initial immune response.59,60

Beyond the co-administration of corticosteroids, the inherent immunogenicity of mRNA-LNPs can be further minimized by incorporating mRNA modifications using chemically modified nucleotides and optimizing the NP surface properties.61,62,63

For IRDs, subretinal delivery remains the most effective method to target RPE and photoreceptors in clinical applications. Despite being an invasive technique, it offers superior access to the posterior pole of the eye compared with intravitreal injection, making it the preferred route for diseases affecting the RPE and photoreceptor layers. While retinal inflammation is a known concern following subretinal injection, it is generally less severe than the intraocular inflammation commonly associated with intravitreal injections.64,65 In our comparison of delivery routes using Cre-mRNA in Ai9 reporter mice, we confirmed that the subretinal space is the optimal route to bypass ocular barriers and specifically target the RPE layer, consistent with clinical experience for posterior pole diseases. However, a primary concern regarding its clinical feasibility for repeated dosing is that local disruption can prime the immune system, potentially inducing a stronger immune response upon re-administration in the same eye.66

Advancements in NP functionalization are essential to materialize the full potential of non-viral gene therapy, transforming the field toward safer, more re-dosable, and scalable treatments for a broader range of retinal diseases.67,68 Analyzing NPs in the context of retinal diseases associated with a compromised inner limiting membrane (ILM) could reveal whether LNPs can cross this barrier and reach the RPE or inner retinal cells following less invasive delivery. Moreover, in vitro and ex vivo studies have demonstrated that applying the clinically approved dye indocyanine green to the ILM, followed by pulsed laser irradiation, generates vapor nanobubbles that transiently disrupt the ILM and significantly enhance retinal delivery after intravitreal injection.69,70,71

The core therapeutic value of our system was confirmed in both patient-derived cells and the mouse model, providing a robust proof-of-concept for its therapeutic efficacy. Treatment of CHM patient’s hiPSC-RPE cells with hCHM-mRNA successfully restored REP1 levels and, more critically, rescued Rab prenylation function. This molecular rescue was faithfully replicated in vivo in the Chm± mouse model, showing a significant decrease in uRab proteins in RPE extracts 24 h post-injection. This functional complementation is critical, as the loss of REP1 function and subsequent accumulation of uRabs are the direct molecular cause of CHM pathogenesis.40 As shown in this study, the molecular rescue translated into tangible functional improvements at the tissue level. ERG analysis in Chm± mice showed a clear positive shift in the overall waveform and improvement in the maximum amplitudes of the a-wave (photoreceptor activity) and b-wave (bipolar and Müller cell activity). While the extent of functional recovery at this early 24-h time point is encouraging, it likely reflects the rapid onset of REP1 expression and subsequent restoration of essential vesicular trafficking.72

Despite the high transfection efficiency and functional rescue achieved with this non-viral mRNA approach, a main limitation of the transient nature of the expression remains. For chronic, progressive diseases like CHM, which require long-term gene replacement, sustained expression is generally necessary. We envision that mRNA-based therapeutics of IRDs will be highly advantageous for applications requiring temporary therapeutic activity, such as gene editing. Transient mRNA delivery enables temporary expression of genome-editing tools, like CRISPR-Cas nucleases, base editors, and prime editors, thereby significantly reducing the potential for off-target effects and eliminating the risks associated with the prolonged presence or genomic integration of viral vectors.73,74 Moreover, the limitation of transient expression for chronic conditions may soon be overcame by next-generation mRNA technologies designed for enhanced durability. Self-amplifying RNA (saRNA), derived from alphaviruses, incorporates its own replication machinery, allowing the transcript to multiply within the cell, thus dramatically increasing and prolonging protein expression from a small initial dose.75,76 Even more recently, circular RNA (circRNA) has emerged as an exceptionally stable platform. Unlike linear mRNAs, circRNAs is resistant to exonuclease degradation, promoting substantially longer protein production in vivo.77,78

In summary, we provide a robust proof-of-concept that non-viral mRNA therapy for CHM, delivered via LNPs, can rapidly and efficiently restore REP1 function in CHM patient-derived hiPSC-RPE cells and achieve functional rescue in the Chm± mouse model. The favorable safety profile, high efficiency, and transient expression kinetics position this platform as a powerful, cost-effective, and scalable alternative for the development of novel gene augmentation and gene editing therapies for a wide range of IRDs. Furthermore, the application of this strategy to deliver gene-editing tools would enable a permanent correction with a single treatment, eliminating the need for repeated dosing and the potential risks of cumulative toxicity and immune priming.

Future work should focus on optimizing LNP formulations and mRNA structure to allow for easier delivery to the retina and potentially extend the expression half-life for long-term gene replacement applications.

Material and methods

Animals

In our studies, all mice used were on B6 genetic background. Male and female C57BL/6 J were used WT mice for mRNA delivery experiments. Chmflox and PGK-Cre mouse colonies have been previously described54,79 and were kindly provided by Dr. Mariya Moosajee (Crick Institute, London, UK). Chmflox were bred to PGK-Cre to generate Chm± heterozygous female mice. RCL-tdTomato (Ai9; Jackson Laboratories; Strain #007909) mouse line was bred and maintained at Champalimaud Research rodent facility. All mice were generally used for experiments at 8–10 weeks of age. Mouse colonies were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in 12/12-h light/dark cycles at 21°C and 50% humidity, at the Champalimaud Research rodent facility. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) guidelines and following the approved national and local institutional committees, Direcção Geral de Veterinária (DGAV) and Champalimaud Animal Care Committee (ORBEA), respectively.

Molecular cloning and plasmids

The expression cassettes of eGFP, Cre, hCHM, and hCHM-V5, were cloned into the pT7 UTR-containing backbone with the inactivated T7 promotor (#178113, Addgene), as described previously,80 using a two-step approach with a Gibson Assembly Cloning kit (New England Biolabs). All standard molecular cloning procedures were carried out by the Molecular and Transgenic Tools Platform (MTTP) at the Champalimaud Research (Lisbon, Portugal).

mRNA in vitro transcription

To generate in-vitro-transcribed Cre-mRNA, GFP-mRNA, hCHM-mRNA, and hCHM-V5 mRNA, the previously described pT7 plasmids were amplified by PCR, using either Phusion U Green Multiplex PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific) or Platinum SuperFi II PCR Master Mix (Invitrogen) and the in vitro transcription (IVT) primer sets, previously reported by David Liu et al.81

PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen) and served as template for subsequent IVT. IVT reactions were performed with the HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA synthesis kit (New England Biologicals), in the presence of CleanCap AG (TriLink BioTechnologies) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, reactions were incubated for 2 h at 37°C, followed by DNAse I treatment (New England Biolabs) for 15 min at 37°C to remove residual DNA templates. The transcribed mRNAs were then purified using the MEGAclear Transcription Clean-Up kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The mRNA concentration of purified mRNAs was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific), and RNA size integrity and degradation were assessed by electrophoresis on E-gel 1% agarose gels (Invitrogen).

hiPSC culture

hiPSC lines, Rbi001-A (#66540025, ECACC) and the CHM male patient-derived hiPSC line CRFi001-A, which was kindly provided by David Gamm Lab (Wisconsin University), were cultured and maintained, as previously described.82

hiPSC-derived RPE differentiation

hiPSCs were differentiated into RPE following a previously described protocol,83 which involves sequential addition of growth factors for 14 days in a retinal differentiation medium (RDM). Briefly, on day 14, immature RPE was collected and plated at 1 × 105 cells.cm−2 onto Matrigel basement membrane matrix-coated 6-well plates (Corning) and allowed to mature in X-VIVO medium (LONZA Bioscience) supplemented with 1% Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Gibco, Invitrogen) for 30 days, with medium changes twice per week. By day 30, when cell pigmentation became evident, the cells were passaged and further matured for an additional 30 days. Mature hiPSC-RPE cells were then used for experiments approximately 30 days after this passage.

hiPSC-RPE treatments

For hiPSC-RPE treatments, transfections with the pDNA or mRNA carriers (JetOPTIMUS (Polyplus, Sartorius) or JetMESSENGER (Polyplus, Sartorius), respectively) were performed to obtain a final concentration of 250 ng of mRNA per 100,000 cells. Lipoplex mixtures were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions to obtain different μg:μL ratios. A 1:1 ratio was used for GFP-mRNA, while a 1:2 ratio was used for hCHM-mRNA. Further details about the lipoplex characterization are provided in Figure S3. The cells were incubated with the resulting complexes, and the culture medium was replaced 24 h after transfection. For comparison with non-viral carriers in hiPSC-RPE, an AAV2-CAG-GFP vector (5.0 × 1010 vg/mL) was used. The optimal mRNA concentration and AAV2-CAG-GFP dose for hiPSC-RPE were determined previously through dose-response studies (data not shown).

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was conducted on hiPSC-RPE cells treated as described above with GFP-mRNA/JetMESSENGER, GFP-pDNA/JetOPTIMUS, or AAV2-CAG-GFP (5 × 1010 vg/mL). Initial treatments were analyzed 48 h post-treatment, and the cells treated with GFP-mRNA/JetMESSENGER were additionally analyzed at 3-, 5-, 10-, 15-, and 30-days post-treatment. Following transfection or transduction, the cells were detached from the plate surface with TrypLE Express Enzyme (Gibco, Invitrogen) for 10 min at 37°C. The enzyme was neutralized with X-VIVO medium (LONZA Bioscience), and the cells were centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min. After washing with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), the cells were incubated with 1.43 μM of 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min at room temperature to assess cell viability. The cells were then washed twice in DPBS, resuspended in DPBS after centrifugation, passed through a 40 μm cell strainer (FALCON, Corning), and then processed for analysis. Data acquisition was performed on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), using excitation wavelengths of 488 nm for GFP and 359 nm for DAPI signals. A total of 10.000 events were acquired per condition using BD FACSDiva software (v.6.1.3.). Data were subsequently analyzed with FlowJo 10 (v.10.10.0).

Immunofluorescence of hiPSC-RPE

Differentiated hiPSC-RPE cells grown on Matrigel-coated coverslips were fixed for 30 min with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Thermo Scientific), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS-T) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min, followed by 1 h of blocking with 10% donkey serum in PBS at room temperature. hiPSC-RPE cells were then incubated with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4°C in blocking solution: goat anti-GFP (SICGEN, #AB0020; 1:250) and Alexa Fluor 568 Phalloidin probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A12380, 1:250). After washing with PBS, hiPSC-RPE cells were incubated for 1 h with the secondary antibody donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, #A11055, 1:1000), followed by 5-min incubation with 1.43 μM DAPI solution in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich). Coverslips were mounted in Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories) and acquired on a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope. Images were processed using ImageJ2 (v.2.16.0, NIH).

Intraocular mouse injections

Adult mice (8- to 10-weeks old) were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine-xylazine mix (100 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine), and pupils were dilated with 1% of tropicamide ophthalmic solution (TropicilTop, 10 mg/mL). Ocular injections were administered under the stereoscope, using an RPE intraocular injection kit connected to an automatic UltraMicroPump3 (UMP3T-2, WPI), and mRNA complexed with in vivo-jetRNA+ (Polyplus, Sartorius) was delivered at 100 nL/s. For both intravitreal and subretinal injections, a small incision was made with a 30-gauge needle to puncture a hole slightly below the limbus. Subretinal injections were administered through this incision by using a 35-gauge blunt needle coupled to the Nanofil RPE intraocular injection kit (WPI) to deliver 1.5 μL of mRNA complexed with in vivo-jetRNA+ (Polyplus, Sartorius) into the subretinal space between the retina and RPE. Intravitreal injections were administered using a 36-gauge blunt needle connected to the same system, delivering 1.5 μL of the complex at 100 nL/s into the vitreous through the limbal incision. Subconjunctival injections were administered using a 35-gauge beveled needle connected to the automated syringe pump for delivering 3 μL of mRNA complexed with in vivo-jetRNA+ (Polyplus, Sartorius) into the temporal bulbar conjunctiva at 100 nL/s. After injections, the eyes were covered with 1% carmellose sodium (10 mg/mL, Celluvisc, Allergan) to prevent cornea dehydration during recovery. OCT using Spectralis HRA + OCT (Heidelberg engineering, Heidelberg) was performed 24 h after the procedure to confirm successful subretinal injections. In vivo mRNA complex injections contained 200 ng of mRNA, corresponding to 1.34 × 109 to 2.25 × 109 particles per injection, with an average particle size of approximately 210–250 nm, based on dynamic light scattering analysis (Figure S3C).

In vivo anti-inflammatory treatment

After subretinal injection of 1.5 μL hCHM-mRNA/in vivo-jetRNA+ (1.52 × 109 particles per injection) or 1.5 μL AAV2-CAG-CHM (1.10 × 109 vg per injection), the animals were treated with anti-inflammatory corticosteroids once daily for 3 days post-injection. Topical prednisolone acetate (5 mg/g corticil, VAPP company) was administered to the injected eyes in combination with the subcutaneous dexamethasone (0.5 mg/mL). Control eyes received 1.5 μL of 0.9% saline or 1.5 μL of mRNA buffer (Polyplus, Sartorius). Following 3 days of anti-inflammatory treatment (Ai), the animals were euthanized, and eyes were collected and processed for immunostaining.

Eye collection and processing

The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the eyes were enucleated. For RNAscope and immunofluorescence assays, the eyes were immediately fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin solution (VWR Chemicals) for 24 h at room temperature, followed by incubation in 70% ethanol overnight at room temperature. The samples were then processed and embedded in paraffin blocks. Serial formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections of 4 μm were mounted onto Epredia SuperFrost Plus slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For RPE flatmount imaging, the eyes were fixed in 4% PFA (Thermo Scientific) for 30 min at room temperature, washed twice with PBS with 0.1% sodium azide, and processed for eye dissection in PBS under the stereoscope. After removing the connective tissue around the eyeball, the anterior cornea was carefully excised by making an incision around the limbal circumference. After removing the cornea, lens, and vitreous, the neural retina was gently peeled off. The resulting RPE eyecup was then flattened by making several long radial cuts and transferred to a 48-well plate to proceed with the immunostaining assay.

For western blot, freshly enucleated eyes were dissected as described above in ice-cold PBS under the stereoscope. The resulting flattened RPE eyecup was incubated with 50 μL of protein sample buffer (Cell Signaling Technology) with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min on ice. Following incubation, a pellet pestle was used to mechanically release RPE cells into the lysis buffer. The lysates were then stored at −80°C. After freezing, the samples were centrifuged at 8,000 g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was collected for protein quantification and western blot analysis.

Immunofluorescence on mouse eyes

FFPE eye sections were previously baked at 60°C for 30 min and dewaxed by xylene followed by sequential ethanol hydration. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer (10 mM sodium citrate, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6) for 20 min in a steamer. The sections were then rinsed with distilled water and washed with PBS. For immunostaining, both FFPE sections and RPE flatmounts were permeabilized using 0.25% Triton X-100 PBS (0.25% PBS-T) for 15 min. The samples were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a blocking solution (1% BSA, 5% donkey serum in 0.25% PBS-T), followed by incubation with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4°C in blocking solution: rabbit anti-Iba1 (Wako, 019–19741, 1:250), mouse anti-GFAP (Santa Cruz, #sc-33673, 1:200), and goat anti-GFP (SICGEN, #AB0020; 1:250). The samples were then washed twice with 0.25% PBS-T and incubated with the following secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature: donkey anti-mouse 568 (Invitrogen, #A10037, 1:1000), donkey anti-rabbit 488 (Invitrogen, #A21206, 1:1000), and donkey anti-goat 488 (Invitrogen, #A11055, 1:1000). This was followed by incubation for 10 min with 1.43 μM DAPI in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich). After PBS washing, the samples were mounted with Mowiol mounting medium, and confocal images were acquired on a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope. Images were processed using Zen blue 3.0 (Zeiss) and ImageJ2 (v.2.16.0, NIH).

RNAscope in situ hybridization

FFPE mouse eye tissues were used to perform the detection of hCHM-mRNA using RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent kit V2 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Bio-Techne) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 4 μm FFPE eye sections were baked, dewaxed, and subjected to hydrogen peroxide treatment, target retrieval incubation, and protease digestion before hybridization with the target probe. Thereafter, Hs-CHM-C1 probe (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Bio-Techne, #1257651) was hybridized to the sections for 2 h at 40°C. Signal amplification and development were performed using HRP-C1 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Bio-Techne) and TSA Vivid Fluorophore 570 (Tocris, Bio-Techne). Slides were washed, counterstained with DAPI, and mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mounting Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Confocal images were acquired on a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope and processed using Zen blue 3.0 (Zeiss) and ImageJ2 (v.2.16.0, NIH).

Confocal microscopy and fluorescence image analysis

Confocal z stacks of the immunostained sections were acquired using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope, using Pl-Apochromat 20×/0.80 dry objective. A minimum of 10 stacks were used for each maximum-intensity Z projection. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ2 (v.2.16.0, NIH) by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity of five different retinal sections per condition. RPE flatmounts and whole-eye sections were imaged using a PI-Apochromat 10×/0.45 dry objective. Orthogonal projections and image processing were performed using Zen blue 3.0 (Zeiss) and ImageJ2 (v.2.16.0, NIH).

In vitro prenylation assay

Prenylation assays were performed as described previously,84 with minor modifications.85 Briefly, the treated hiPSC-RPE cells or mouse RPE extracts were dissociated in 100 μL prenylation/lysis buffer (25 mM HEPES, pH 7.2; 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTE, 20 μM GDP, 1× protease, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail). Cell suspensions were snap frozen at −80°C overnight and then mechanically disrupted by passing them through a 23-G syringe needle 20 times. Cytosolic protein fractions were isolated by ultracentrifugation at 100.000 × g for 30 min at 4°C in an Optima XL-100 K (Beckman Coulter), and proteins were quantified using the Pierce BCA protein Assay kit (Thermo Scientific). After quantification, 50 μg of cytosolic protein extracts were subjected to an in vitro prenylation assay using 20 μM GDP (Sigma-Aldrich; #G7127), 0.5 μM recombinant Rab geranylgeranyl transferase (Jena Bioscience; #PR-103), 0.5 μM recombinant His-tagged REP-1 (Jena Bioscience; #PR-105), and 5 μM biotin-labeled geranyl pyrophosphate (Jena Bioscience; #LI-015), followed by incubation at 37°C. Biotin incorporation was then evaluated by western blot using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin (Jackson Immunoresearch; #016-030-084; 1:2.500) and β-actin-HRP (Sigma-Aldrich; #A3854; 1:25.000) or GAPDH (Sicgen; #AB0049; 1:1000) as a loading control.

Western blot analysis

Mouse RPE samples and hiPSC-RPE cell lysates were quantified using Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. A total protein of 30 μg was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane by using a TransBlot system (Biorad). The membranes were blocked for 1 h with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich) (TBS-T) at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with the following primary antibodies at the indicated dilutions: goat anti-REP1 (SICGEN; #AB0123; 1:500) and rabbit anti-V5-Tag (Cell Signaling; #13202; 1:1000). After 3 washes with TBS-T for 5 min each, the blots were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with an anti-rabbit HRP-linked antibody (Amersham, Cytiva, #NA934) or anti-goat HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A16005) diluted 1:5.000 in 5% non-fat dry milk in TBS-T. Subsequently, the membranes were re-incubated for 1 h at room temperature, using β-actin-HRP (Sigma-Aldrich; #A3854; 1:20.000), for loading control detection. Chemiluminescence signals were developed using the enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (ECL, Amersham, Cytiva) and imaged using the ChemiDoc Imaging system (Bio-Rad).

ERG analysis

Before the ERG procedure, the mice were dark adapted for 12 h, and the experimental preparation was performed under dim red light. The mice were anesthetized with a single intraperitoneal injection of ketamine-xylazine mix (100 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine), and pupils were dilated with 1% of tropicamide ophthalmic solution (TropicilTop, 10 mg/mL), followed by 0.3% hypromellose gel (Systane, Alcon). The mice were placed on a heated Diagnosys Celeris Rodent ERG device (Diagnosys LCC), and the ocular electrodes were placed on the corneas. To measure a- and b-wave functions, stimulations of an intensity series of single white flashes at 0.01, 0.1, and 1.0 cd.s.m−2 were performed using dark-adapted full-field ERG protocol. The responses were analyzed from the averaged ERG waveform by using Espion V6 software (Diagnosys LLC).

Statistical analysis

Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test or Brown-Forsythe and Welch’s tests, as appropriate, using GraphPad Prism (v.10.1.1). Statistical significances were defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. The number of biological replicates (n) of each experiment is provided in the corresponding figure legend.
