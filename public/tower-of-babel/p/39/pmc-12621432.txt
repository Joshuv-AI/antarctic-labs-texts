Evaluation of infection prevention and antibiotic stewardship programs at a tertiary-care hospital in Southwest Ethiopia: An opportunity to leverage the synergy
Authors: Mulualem Tadesse, Selam Tesfaye, Abebe Dukessa, Belay Zawdie, Birhanu Yenealem, Kisi Chemeda, Addisalem Gebre, Daniel Dana, Henok Gulilat, Tadele Akeba Diriba, Amare Assefa, Diriba Fufa, Gemeda Abebe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundEffective infection prevention and control (IPC) and antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) are essential components of hospitals. This study aims to evaluate the status of IPC and ASPs at Jimma University Medical Center (JUMC), a tertiary care hospital, in Southwest Oromia, Ethiopia.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted in two parts. In part one, data on the core components of IPC were collected using the WHO Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF) tool. The hospital's IPC level was determined based on the total scores for the core components. In part two, ASP assessment tool was used to gather data on the core elements of ASPs, and the hospital's ASP status was determined based on the total scores.ResultsThe overall IPC scores of JUMC was 455, corresponding to an intermediate level. The highest scores were observed for the IPC program (70), monitoring and feedback of IPC practices (67.5), and multimodal strategies (65). The lowest scores were for hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) surveillance (25) and IPC education and training (50). Regarding ASPs, the overall score was 51.2%, indicating a moderate level of ASP. Significant gaps were found in ASP core elements; education (0%), tracking antibiotic use (27.3%) and hospital leadership commitment (42.9%).ConclusionsThe IPC and ASP programs at JUMC are performing at intermediate and moderate levels, respectively. Attention is required to strengthen HAIs surveillance, tracking of antibiotic uses, and education and training. The overlap between IPC and ASPs presents an opportunity for future collaborative efforts to enhance both programs.

Introduction

Hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) are major global health challenges particularly affecting low-and –middle income countries (LMICs) [1,2]. HAIs and AMR adversely impact patient outcomes, leading to considerable morbidity and mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs [3,4]. Patients receiving health care in the hospital are at risk of developing infection due to their compromised health, underlying medical conditions, or invasive medical procedures [5,6]. The spread of HAIs particularly antimicrobial resistant pathogens are reaching an alarming levels in Ethiopia [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. If the current trend of HAIs and AMR in the country remain unchecked, curable infections could become major health problems particularly among vulnerable populations. IPC and ASP are separate but complementary programs in healthcare system aimed at reducing HAIs and AMR, and ultimately improving patient outcomes [11,12].

IPC is a cross-cutting issue in hospitals [11]. All IPC programs are key in preventing HAIs including AMR pathogens [[13], [14], [15]]. Adequate implementation of IPC programs and strictly following IPC principles and practice can significantly reduce HAIs [16]. The WHO recommends that all hospitals should implement IPC programs and all healthcare providers (HCPs) should adhere to IPC practices consistently while caring for patients [15,17]. However, many hospitals in LMICs lack effective IPC programs. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, the status of IPC program implementation has not been systematically evaluated in many hospitals in Ethiopia. The WHO has recommended the IPCAF tool [17] to assess IPC program implementation in healthcare facilities. IPCAF is a standardized tool designed to assess institutional capacity and compliance with IPC guidelines. However, data on the current state of IPC implementation is scarce in most Ethiopian hospitals.

On the other hand, the increasing incidence of AMR may be attributed to poor implementation of antimicrobial stewardship program (ASP) in hospitals [18,19]. ASP is a coordinated program that promotes the appropriate use of antibiotics and decrease the spread of AMR infections [19,20]. Effective implementation of ASPs, alongside IPC, in hospital settings can reduce AMR and HAIs and improve patient outcomes. In 2018, a practical guide to ASP in Ethiopian hospitals was developed and endorsed for use [21]. Since then, some hospitals have successfully integrated ASPs into their healthcare systems. ASP has seven core elements including hospital leadership commitment, accountability, pharmacy expertise, action implement interventions to improve antibiotic use, tracking antibiotic use and outcomes, reporting antibiotic use and outcomes, and education [20]. These core elements are designed to guide healthcare facility management to address barriers toward the successful implementation of ASPs in LMICs. The concept of hospital-based ASP is relatively new in Ethiopia and there is paucity of data on the extent to which the core elements of ASPs have been implemented in Ethiopian hospitals.

IPC and ASP programs are closely interlinked and addressing both simultaneously can lead to significant improvements in efficiency [22]. Given the substantial overlap between IPC and ASP metrics, strengthening both IPC and ASP activities in parallel is a cost-effective approach that can significantly reduce HAIs and AMR. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have concurrently assessed the level of implementation of IPC and ASP in Ethiopian hospitals. Therefore, this study was aimed to evaluate the status IPC and ASP implementation at Jimma University Medical Center, a tertiary care hospital, in Southwest Oromia, Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study setting and period

This study was conducted at Jimma University Medical Center, a tertiary care hospital located in the Southwest Oromia region of Ethiopia, from July to October 2023. JUMC is situated in Jimma town, approximately 352 km southwest of Addis Ababa. As one of the oldest teaching hospitals in the southwestern part of Ethiopia, JUMC offers specialized and referral services to a wide catchment population of about 20 million people. The hospital handles around 18,000 inpatient admissions, 200,000 outpatient visits, 15,000 emergency cases, and 4,500 deliveries annually. JUMC is composed of 51 clinical service units, ten wards, approximately 800 beds, and over 1,500 professional staff.

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional, institution-based study was conducted in two parts at JUMC. In the first part, the IPC focal person and committee members were asked to complete the IPCAF tool to assess the current IPC programs and practices at the hospital. In the second part, the principal pharmacist, who served as the hospital's ASP chairperson, along with committee members, were invited to complete the CDC Hospital ASP assessment tool to evaluate the implementation of the core elements of ASPs at JUMC. Participants were purposively selected based on their key roles at JUMC, ensuring they had the relevant first-hand knowledge to accurately respond to the questionnaires.

Data collection procedures

Part one: assessment of core components of IPC

A WHO Infection Prevention and Control Assessment Framework (IPCAF) tool was used to assess the level of implementation of IPC core elements of a hospital. IPCAF is a structured, closed-ended questionnaire with an associated scoring system, widely used to assess IPC activities and identify strengths and weaknesses at healthcare facilities [17]. It comprises eight sections highlighting the eight IPC core components (CCs). The eight parts of the IPCAF questionnaire are IPC program, IPC guidelines, IPC education and training, health care-associated infection (HAI) surveillance, multimodal strategies for implementation of IPC interventions, monitoring/audit of IPC practices and feedback, workload, staffing and bed occupancy, and built environment, materials and equipment for IPC. The data collector, who was the IPC expert trainer, conducted the facility IPC assessment. The hospital IPC focal person and committee members were self-administered IPCAF tool and were asked to complete the IPCAF tool. The hospital IPC focal person and committee members completed the IPCAF tool independently, under the guidance of the IPC expert trainer.

The IPCAF tool is structured according to the recommendations in the WHO guidelines on CCs of IPC programs. The IPCAF tool has eight core components of IPC. Each CC in the tool has equal score, i.e. 100. The maximum cumulative score of all eight components is thus 800. Data were analyzed by using Microsoft Excel and descriptive statistics. The overall IPCAF score was obtained by summing the scores from all eight core components. Part two: assessment of core elements of hospital ASPs

An updated CDC-hospital ASP assessment tool was used to evaluate the implementation of core elements of ASPs [20]. The tool comprises of seven core elements of hospital ASPs: hospital leadership commitment, accountability, pharmacy expertise, action (implement interventions to improve antibiotic use), tracking antibiotic use and outcomes, reporting antibiotic use and outcomes, and education. Each core element has specific questions with a response of “YES” or “NO”. The tool consists of a total of 43 questions covering these seven core elements.

Data about the implementation of core elements of ASPs was collected by in person meeting with the hospital ASP chairperson and ASP committee members. These people were purposively targeted due to their in-depth knowledge of ASP establishment and implementation at the hospital. The data collector was present by the time the ASP chairperson and committee members were answering to each question of the ASP core elements. Although the tool was self-administered, the in person meeting with the ASP chairperson and committee members could potentially enhance and ensure quicker and error-free responses.

For each question, the response was recorded as either “YES” or “NO”. A score of 2 was given to “YES” response and score of 0 to “NO” response. Since the seven core elements comprises of different number of questions, we summed up the scores of each core element and converted to 100 (%) scale in order to compare the individual core elements. The higher the percentage, the better adherence to the specific core element of ASPs. To assess the ASP level of the hospital, all points obtained from the 43 questions of the seven core elements were summed up and converted to 100% scale. Based on the total percentage score, the hospital ASP level was classified as “Poor” (0–33%), “Moderate” (34%–64%), “Good” (65%–85%), or “Advanced” (86%–100%).

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted as part of a larger research project titled “Combating Infectious Diseases, Antimicrobial Resistance, and Malnutrition among Cancer Patients at JUMC: A Prospective Follow-up Study” (CIDARM Project). Ethical approval for this study, as part of the CIDARM project, was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Institute of Health at Jimma University (Ref No: JUIH/IRB/542/23). Ahead of data collection, detailed information about the study was provided to the study participants and informed consent was granted from them.

Results

Characteristics of IPC at JUMC

JUMC has established IPC program with clearly defined objectives and annual activity plan. A senior physician was appointed as the IPC chairperson and more than three full-time professionals serve as IPC team members. However, there was limited commitment and support from top leadership and managements for the IPC program. Almost no budget was allocated for IPC activities and no executive level meetings were held with the IPC team. While the hospital has guidelines for most IPC activities and multimodal strategies to implement IPC interventions, no formal training on IPC was provided to healthcare providers (HCPs) or cleaners. Only an orientation about IPC was usually given for new employees. Regrettably, much attention was not given to HAIs surveillance, and no designated individual was responsible for surveillance activities at JUMC. Surveillance was not conducted for most HAIs except surgical site infections and no antimicrobial drug resistance data was analyzed on a regular basis. Hand hygiene and sanitation facilities were available but supplies including personal protective equipment were not consistently accessible. Full details of the IPCAF tool, along with the total and subtotal scores for JUMC regarding the eight core components of the IPC program, are presented in supplementary file 1 (Supplementary File 1).

Characteristics of ASPs at JUMC

Using a tool adapted from CDC, the core elements of ASPs were assessed for JUMC. A formal ASP has been implemented at the hospital, with a senior pharmacist appointed to lead the stewardship program. The hospital provided office and computers to ASP chairperson and committee members to manage the stewardship programs. However, the ASPs received very limited financial support from the hospital and from key departments. The allocated budget for antimicrobial stewardship activities, such as training and IT support, was minimal. With regards to monitoring and feedback, there was no prospective audit and feedback conducted for specific antibiotic agents at JUMC. Additionally, the hospital's ASP did not routinely perform medication use evaluations to assess the appropriateness of therapy for selected antibiotics. Full details of the ASP assessment tool, along with the individual and total scores for JUMC regarding the seven core ASP elements, are presented in Supplementary File 2 (Supplementary file 2).

Discussion

Preventing HAIs and AMR in healthcare settings is fundamental for achieving quality care and improving patient outcomes [14,15]. Effective implementation of all components of IPC and ASP are required to build functioning programs leading a significant reduction of HAIs and AMR in hospitals. Healthcare facilities including hospitals that provide clinical services to patients should be assessed to determine the status of their IPC and ASP implementation. In this study, we utilized standardized tools to evaluate the extent of implementation of IPC and ASPs at a tertiary care hospital in Southwest Oromia, Ethiopia.

In the present study, it was shown that JUMC has an established IPC program with an overall intermediate level of IPC score. This finding is similar to a study done in Ghana, which also reported an intermediate level of IPC programs in two of the four hospitals assessed [23]. A WHO global study that analyzed data from 81 countries reported that advanced levels of IPC (IPCAF median score of 605 points) were predominantly found in high-income countries [24]. Similarly, high IPC levels were noted in Austria [25], Turkey [26] and China [27]. In contrast, lower levels of IPC scores were reported for low-income countries [24]. In Islamabad, the capital of Pakistan, it was reported that all the five hospitals that were evaluated had IPC level of inadequate (scored less than 200) [28]. High IPC levels are generally expected in high income countries, where infrastructures and more resources support the implementation of IPC programs.

A detailed analysis of the different core components of IPC revealed diverse results. Our findings showed that most aspects of the IPC core components (CC) were appropriately implemented at JUMC. The highest scoring IPC core components were IPC program (CC-1), monitoring/audit of IPC practices and feedback (CC-6), and multimodal strategy (CC-5). On the other hand, some core components of IPC were poorly implemented and practiced at JUMC. HAI surveillance (CC-4) and IPC education and training (CC-3) were reported as the lowest-scoring at JUMC. These core components (HAIs surveillance and IPC education and training) were also lowest scoring for low income countries as shown by in an international study conducted in 81 countries [24]. Strong and effective implementation of all core components of IPC have the ability to reduce HAIs and AMR, while improving the quality of care and patient outcomes. As a tertiary care and teaching hospital, JUMC should continue to enhance the scope and quality of its IPC program implementation across all core components.

We found some interesting results regarding the use of multimodal strategies for implementing IPC interventions. JUMC, one of the biggest teaching hospitals in Ethiopia, has successfully integrated multimodal strategies into its hospital system, which have increasingly been utilized in both clinical care and IPC practices. As a teaching hospital, JUMC's integration of academic and clinical services likely contributed to the higher score achieved in multimodal strategy. In contrast to the present study, several studies have reported lower scores for multimodal strategies [23,25,26,29]. Even hospitals in high income countries such as Austria and Germany have reported low scores for implementation of multimodal strategies in IPC [25,29]. This suggest that multimodal strategy is relatively a new concept in IPC program and it appears that awareness for implementation of multimodal strategies are not yet fully addressed in some countries.

In the present study, the lowest scoring IPC core component was HAIs surveillance (CC-4). This result was not expected for JUMC given that it is a tertiary care hospital with adequate microbiology and laboratory capacity to support such surveillance. The hospital is equipped with an accredited microbiology lab capable of reliably identifying pathogens and antimicrobial resistance patterns in a timely manner. HAIs surveillance is one of the key core component of hospital IPC program that supports the estimation and response strategies regarding the HAI and AMR incidents in the hospital settings. A well-functional surveillance system in the healthcare settings can quickly detect outbreaks. The second lowest score was obtained for CC-3 (IPC education and training). Despite being a teaching hospital, no regular training was offered to healthcare workers, cleaners and other personnel directly involved in patient care. Infection prevention and control is a dynamic and evolving discipline. Periodic IPC education and training for healthcare workers as well as orientation for new employees are vital in hospitals. JUMC should strengthen its capacity in these key areas (CC-3 and CC-4) for better implementation of IPC programs and reduce the burden of HAIs and AMR.

Our study also showed that JUMC has a formally established antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) with dedicated time for ASP leader to manage the program. Based on the overall ASP score, JUMC was categorized at a moderate level of ASP. The concept of ASP is new to most hospitals in Ethiopia and the overall score achieved by JUMC was encouraging. Two notable strengths were observed in the core elements of ASP: accountability (core element 2) and pharmacy experts (core element 3), both of which received the highest percentage scores. However, this findings also indicate a poor to moderate adoption and implementation of other core elements of ASPs at JUMC. As a tertiary care hospital representing an advanced settings, it is unfortunate that the hospital scored less than half in the three core elements of ASPs. The most significant deficiencies were observed in the core elements of education (core element 7), tracking antibiotic use and outcomes (core element 5), and hospital leadership commitment (core element 1). This indicates significant gaps in the practice of ASPs and if left unsolved, they could negatively impact the appropriate use of antibiotics in the hospital, potentially leading to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance Education is a key component of ASPs and should be provided to prescribers and other healthcare professionals to optimize hospital antibiotic use. Monitoring and tracking of antimicrobial resistance is another important core element of ASPs that helps to identify opportunities for quality improvement in the utilization of antibiotics in hospitals. The ASP teams should provide regular updates on key audit findings on appropriate use of antibiotics and antibiogram to clinicians, nurses, pharmacists and other prescribers as well as hospital leadership teams.

In the current study, core element 1 (hospital leadership commitment) was also found to have a low score highlighting another gap in antimicrobial stewardship practices. The top leadership teams showed low management priority and limited financial support for ASPs. The stewardship committee members had very few meetings with leadership teams and other healthcare providers. Likewise, this poor leadership support for ASPs has been also reported in studies in other African countries and LMICs [[30], [31], [32], [33]], which could pose a serious threat to the effective implementation of ASPs in hospitals. To ensure the sustainability of ASPs, hospital leadership should prioritize antimicrobial stewardship activities, provide financial support, and emphasize the importance of optimizing antibiotic use.

IPC and ASPs programs have many similarities in terms of personnel, infrastructure, strategies and metrics [22]. In addition, there are numerous opportunities for IPC and ASPs to synergistically collaborate on activities including HAIs and AMR surveillance, data review, audit and feedback, as well as interventions such as healthcare worker education and training. In our study, deficiency in both programs were observed in similar components: lack of commitment from hospital leadership, limited budget and resources, insufficient education and training for healthcare providers, poor HAIs and AMR surveillance and tracking. The IPC and ASP teams can work jointly to address weaknesses identified in this study. The interaction between IPC and ASP seems to be bidirectional. Effective IPC programs minimize HAIs, decrease the use of additional antibiotics, and help to reduce the emergence of AMR. On the other hand, utilizing ASPs as one of the IPC care strategies to prevent the development of AMR, helps hospitals effectively use limited resources for other IPC activities and improve patient outcomes. Previous studies have also demonstrated the potential to joint implementation of IPC and ASP in hospital settings [22,34,35]. Integrating IPC and ASP activities can help streamline workflows, minimize duplication of efforts, and enhance operational efficiency. This coordinated approach can lead to a reduction in healthcare-associated infections and strengthen the overall response to antimicrobial resistance.

To improve the IPC and ASP performance at Jimma University Medical Center, targeted actions are recommended based on identified gaps. For IPC, strengthening hospital-acquired infection (HAI) surveillance through standardized data collection and improved laboratory support is essential, along with enhancing IPC education through structured, regular training for all healthcare workers. Existing strengths such as the IPC program and monitoring mechanisms should be sustained through continuous quality improvement. For ASP, introducing formal education programs for clinical staff, improving tracking of antibiotic use via data systems, and securing stronger leadership commitment through institutional support and resource allocation are critical. Cross-cutting strategies include integrating IPC and ASP efforts, enhancing microbiology capacity, and establishing a joint steering committee to coordinate and sustain improvements.

The present study has some limitations. This is a single-center study conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Ethiopia. This could limit the generalizability of our findings and conclusions within and beyond Ethiopia for primary and secondary hospitals. Data for this study were captured from IPC and ASP chairpersons and committee members, whom we considered were experts in the area and able to provide reliable data. These individuals may have provided limited perspectives and an increased potential for biases. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive assessment of the IPC and ASP programs at a tertiary care hospital in Ethiopia. This findings are equally useful for other tertiary care hospitals in LMICs and highlights the importance of regular evaluation of IPC and ASP programs at tertiary care hospitals.

Conclusions

Overall, Jimma University Medical Center demonstrates an intermediate level of IPC program, with notable variations in the scores across the individual IPC core components. The highest scores were recorded in IPC programs (CC-1), monitoring of IPC practices and feedback (CC-6), and multimodal strategies (CC-5), while the lowest scores were seen in HAI surveillance (CC-4) and IPC education and training (CC-3). This study also revealed that the hospital has a moderate level of antimicrobial stewardship program, though there are significant gaps in the implementation of certain core elements of ASPs. The results of this study warrant developing targeted interventions using the existing resources to strengthen both IPC and ASP components at the facility. Given the shared goals, strategies, and metrics between IPC and ASPs, there is considerable potential for synergy, which should be further explored to integrate both programs effectively in tertiary care hospitals.
