Differences in pelvic tilt and spinal alignment according to age in patients with hip dysplasia
Authors: Hiroto Funahashi, Yusuke Osawa, Yasuhiko Takegami, Hiroaki Ido, Takamune Asamoto, Shiro Imagama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe aimed to establish age-related characteristics of posture-dependent pelvic tilt and standing spinopelvic alignment in patients with symptomatic hip dysplasia (HD) from adolescence to middle age.Materials and methodsA total of 101 patients with HD indicated for eccentric rotational acetabular osteotomy (ERAO) between 2016 and 2024 were included. Pelvic tilt in supine and standing positions was quantified using the anterior pelvic plane (APP) angle estimated from computed tomography-based 3D pelvic models matched to supine and standing anteroposterior hip radiographs. The change from supine to standing was calculated as standing minus supine; negative values indicated a posterior change. Three-dimensional acetabular coverage during postural transition was compared across age groups. Standing radiographic hip and spinopelvic parameters were also examined using multivariable regression analysis to identify factors associated with age.ResultsAge was correlated with the supine-to-standing change in the APP angle (r = 0.47, p < 0.001). The change shifted closer to zero with increasing age, with younger patients showing a larger posterior change compared with older patients. Patients in their teens showed a more pronounced decrease in anterosuperior acetabular coverage at the 2 o’clock position compared with those in their 40 s (p = 0.017) and 50 s (p = 0.040). Furthermore, anterior wall index in standing (β = 0.26, p = 0.007), sagittal vertical axis (β = 0.24, p = 0.015), and sacral slope (β = 0.24, p = 0.014) were identified as independent factors associated with age.ConclusionsYounger patients with HD showed a larger posterior change in pelvic tilt during postural transition compared with middle-aged patients. These findings support preoperative assessment of functional acetabular coverage in the standing position, particularly in the anterosuperior region in younger patients, when planning ERAO. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether maintaining anterior pelvic tilt during standing contributes to preservation of joint integrity into middle adulthood.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00402-026-06281-9.Keywords: Hip dysplasia, Age, Acetabular coverage, Spinal alignment

Introduction

Hip dysplasia (HD) is the most common morphological abnormality of the hip joint [1]. Insufficient acetabular coverage associated with HD leads to labral and cartilage damage, contributing to osteoarthritis (OA) progression [2–4]. Periacetabular osteotomy (PAO), including eccentric rotational acetabular osteotomy (ERAO), is a hip-preservation procedure that requires osteotomy around the acetabulum and appropriate realignment for symptomatic acetabular dysplasia [5]. These approaches can normalize hip joint contact pressure by reorienting the acetabulum [6, 7], alleviating pain and preventing OA progression. However, acetabular coverage deficiency in HD varies not only by individual differences but also by sex [8] and posture [9], necessitating patient-specific modifications in acetabular fragment repositioning [9–11].

Pelvic tilt changes during postural transitions affect acetabular coverage [9, 11], underscoring the need to characterize pelvic tilt dynamics in symptomatic HD. Furthermore, pelvic tilt is closely linked to spinal flexibility and alignment; therefore, comprehensive evaluation of spinopelvic alignment is essential. Consistently, in patients with HD, spinal alignment is associated with pelvic tilt, and age is among factors that may influence both parameters. In older patients with HD and advanced OA, pelvic posterior tilt was shown to increase, and lumbar lordosis to decrease with aging [12]. However, age-related changes in pelvic tilt and spinopelvic alignment during postural transitions in symptomatic HD, particularly in younger to middle-aged patients, remain unclear. Additionally, due to anterior acetabular deficiency, HD patients are thought to have a tendency toward greater anterior pelvic tilt in the standing position compared to healthy individuals [13]. However, it remains unclear which age groups among HD patients are more likely to exhibit this characteristic. Understanding age- and posture-related characteristics of pelvic tilt and spinopelvic alignment in young to middle-aged patients with HD may help optimize preoperative planning and tailor hip preservation surgery to each patient. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between age and posture-related pelvic tilt variation in symptomatic HD. Specifically, we addressed the following questions: (1) Do changes in pelvic tilt and acetabular coverage during postural transitions differ by age? (2) Are acetabular coverage and spinopelvic alignment associated with age?

Methods

Patients

This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of our institution (2020−2027). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. We reviewed 172 patients who were indicated to our institution between January 2016 and December 2024 for evaluation of hip pain due to HD, and who were recommended for ERAO by orthopedic surgeons specializing in hip preservation. At our institution, ERAO is indicated for patients with hip pain due to HD for whom long-term joint preservation is anticipated. Patients were excluded if they lacked surgical indication, declined surgery, had a center–edge angle ≥ 25°, had a history of hip surgery on either side, had Tönnis grade ≥ 2, had marked pelvic or femoral deformities, or had incomplete data (Fig. 1). Finally, 101 hips in 101 patients who underwent ERAO were included in the analysis. No patients had a history of spinal or lower extremity surgery, nor were any receiving treatment for spinal disorders such as spondylolisthesis or spondylolysis. In all patients, standing and supine anteroposterior (AP) hip radiographs, sagittal spine radiographs, and computed tomography (CT) scans from the pelvis to the knee with a slice thickness of 1 mm were obtained. Patient demographics were further examined by age group (10–19, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, and 50–59 years) (Table 1). There were no significant between-group differences in sex distribution, body mass index, laterality, or Tönnis classification.

Evaluations

The CT data were transferred to ZedHip version 17.0.0 (Lexi Co. Ltd.) for analysis. This software allows for three-dimensional coordinate adjustments of CT data and enables the reconstruction of digitally simulated radiographs. The pelvic coordinate system was aligned to the anterior pelvic plane (APP), and digital AP hip radiographs were generated. Subsequently, actual supine and standing AP hip radiographs were matched to the digital AP radiographs using the pelvic ring ratio (Fig. 2). This technique has been validated for reconstructing pelvic tilt [14] and predicting spinal alignment [15]. The pelvic tilt in the supine and standing positions was quantified as the APP angle, defined as the deviation from the APP coordinate system (Fig. 2). Negative values indicated posterior pelvic tilt. The change in APP angle from supine to standing was defined as standing minus supine; positive values indicated an anterior change, whereas values below zero indicated posterior change. Changes in pelvic tilt from the supine to standing positions were categorized in 5° increments, and their distribution was analyzed across different age groups. Acetabular coverage was measured using a clock-face coordinate system with the cranial direction in the APP coordinate system set to 12 o’clock. The coverage was assessed radially in the anterior pelvic direction. Acetabular coverage was quantified as the angle between the line extending from the acetabular edge to the center of the femoral head and the x-axis passing through the center of the femoral head. The measurement orientation remained unchanged in the supine and standing positions (Fig. 2). The center edge angle (CEA), anterior wall index (AWI), and posterior wall index (PWI) were measured using supine and standing AP hip radiographs (Fig. 3a). On sagittal spinal radiographs, the following spinopelvic parameters were measured: sagittal vertical axis (SVA), thoracic kyphosis (TK), lumbar lordosis (LL), sacral slope (SS), pelvic tilt, pelvic incidence (Fig. 3b).

Statistical analysis

Authors HF and HI independently conducted all assessments on two separate occasions, with an interval of over one month between sessions. CT-based measurements demonstrated high reliability both within and between observers. Intraobserver reliability coefficients ranged from 0.84 to 0.92, while interobserver reliability values ranged from 0.80 to 0.91. For subsequent analyses, we used the mean of the two measurements obtained by HF for each parameter. Measurements by HI were used to assess interobserver reliability. For group comparisons, the normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons across the five age groups, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired comparisons (supine vs standing). Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test with Holm’s correction. Associations between continuous variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Factors associated with age were evaluated using multiple linear regression analysis incorporating radiographic parameters and patient background characteristics. To avoid excluding potentially relevant predictors, variables showing an association with age (p < 0.20) in univariate analyses were included in the regression model. Statistical analyses were performed using EZR version 1.41 (64-bit) (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University), a graphical user interface for R [16] with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

In the correlation analysis, the associations between age and pelvic tilt differed by position. In the supine position, age was negatively correlated with the APP angle (r = − 0.23 [95% CI: − 0.41 to − 0.04], p = 0.020), indicating smaller anterior pelvic tilt with increasing age. In contrast, in the standing position, age was positively correlated with the APP angle (r = 0.23 [95% CI: 0.03 to 0.41], p = 0.022), indicating greater anterior pelvic tilt with increasing age. Consistently, age was positively correlated with the supine-to-standing change (standing minus supine) (r = 0.47 [95% CI: 0.31 to 0.61], p < 0.001), indicating a smaller posterior change during the postural transition (Table 2). Thus, younger patients showed a greater posterior change, whereas older patients showed a smaller posterior change (values closer to zero).

Analysis of the distribution of supine-to-standing changes in the APP angle indicated that the 50–59-year group had fewer patients with a greater posterior change than the 10–19-year group (p = 0.004) (Fig. 4). Three-dimensional acetabular coverage did not significantly differ among age groups in the supine or standing positions. With respect to acetabular coverage changes, a greater decrease at the 2 o’clock position was observed in the 10–19-year group (15 ± 9º) than in 40–49-year group (5 ± 10º, p = 0.017) and 50–59-year group (5 ± 11º, p = 0.040) (Fig. 5).

In the radiographic data, the CEA and AWI in the supine position (CEA: median = 13, [interquartile range (IQR): 8 to 19], AWI: median = 0.31, [interquartile range (IQR): 0.25–0.37]) were higher than those in the standing position (CEA: median = 11, [interquartile range (IQR): 6–19], AWI: median = 0.23, [IQR: 0.18 to 0.30]) (p < 0.001) (Table 3). On standing AP hip radiographs, age was positively correlated with AWI (r = 0.24 [95% CI: 0.04–0.41], p = 0.019), indicating greater anterior acetabular wall coverage in older patients. Regarding sagittal spinal alignment, age was positively correlated with both SVA (r = 0.32 [95% CI: 0.13–0.49], p = 0.001) and SS (r = 0.27 [95% CI: 0.08–0.44], p = 0.007), indicating greater forward trunk translation and a larger sacral slope with increasing age (Table 4). Multiple regression analysis evaluating factors associated with age demonstrated that AWI (β = 0.26, B = 29.9[95% CI: 8.44–51.40], p = 0.007), SVA (β = 0.24, B = 0.16 [95% CI: 0.03–0.30], p = 0.015), and SS (β = 0.24, B = 0.36 [95% CI: 0.07–0.66], p = 0.014) were independently associated with age (Table 5). The multiple regression model explained 20.5% of the variance (R2 = 0.205).

Discussion

In patients with HD, posture-related changes in pelvic tilt cause functional alterations in acetabular coverage, which influence joint contact pressure [17]. However, age-related functional changes in pelvic tilt from adolescence to middle age have not been clarified. Understanding these changes is useful for performing appropriate reorientation of the acetabular fragment for each patient undergoing ERAO. In this study, younger patients showed greater posterior change during the transition from the supine to standing position, accompanied by a marked decrease in anterosuperior acetabular coverage. In contrast, middle-aged patients tended to show a smaller posterior change. Factors associated with age included the AWI, SS, and SVA in the standing position.

In patients with HD, the pelvis often tilts posteriorly during the transition from a supine to a standing position [18, 19], but age-related differences in this posture-dependent change have not been clarified. In this study of adolescent to middle-aged patients eligible for osteotomy, older age was associated with greater anterior pelvic tilt in the standing position and greater posterior pelvic tilt in the supine position. Moreover, the change in pelvic tilt from supine to standing shifted toward a less posterior direction with increasing age, indicating reduced posterior pelvic tilt during the transition. In healthy individuals, hip and spinal range of motion decrease with age after the age of 20 years [20, 21], and pelvic mobility is also likely to decline, leading to smaller posture-dependent variations in pelvic tilt. Patients with HD generally present with greater anterior pelvic tilt than healthy individuals as a compensatory mechanism to enhance femoral head coverage [17–19]. Our results indicate that this compensatory anterior tilt in the supine position is more pronounced in younger patients with HD. Increased joint hypermobility, which has been reported in these patients [22], may contribute to greater posture-dependent pelvic tilt variations compared to healthy individuals. In older patients with hip OA, decreased trunk muscle strength and progressive lumbar kyphosis have been reported to increase posterior pelvic tilt [12]. However, because the patients with HD in this study were relatively younger than those in whom lumbar kyphosis and muscle weakness become prominent, their posture-dependent pelvic tilt changes may follow a different pattern.

Regarding acetabular coverage changes associated with pelvic tilt variation, anterior acetabular coverage in patients with HD is known to decrease with posterior pelvic tilt during the transition from supine to standing [9, 11]. In the present study, no significant differences in acetabular coverage among age groups were observed in either the supine or standing positions. However, the 10–19-year group showed a significantly greater decrease in coverage at the 2 o’clock position compared with the 40–49- and 50–59-year groups. This result is attributed to the greater posterior pelvic tilt during postural transition in younger patients, leading to a larger reduction in anterior acetabular coverage. The 2 o’clock position corresponds to the anterior inferior iliac spine, beyond which bony coverage decreases sharply in the anterior direction [23]. This region is particularly susceptible to coverage loss with posterior pelvic tilt and is also a common site of labral and cartilage damage in patients with HD [24]. Therefore, reduction in coverage at this site in younger patients with HD may be one of the factors contributing to earlier progression of hip OA.

In older healthy individuals with OA, age-related spinal alignment changes such as decreased LL and increased SVA have been reported [25]. Furthermore, in severe developmental dysplasia of the hip classified as Crowe type IV, the pelvis tends to tilt anteriorly in the standing position [26]. However, the relationship between age and sagittal spinal alignment in relatively younger patients with HD who are candidates for pelvic osteotomy remains poorly understood. In HD, the pelvis tends to tilt anteriorly to enhance acetabular coverage [27], suggesting that age-related changes in pelvic and spinal alignment in this population may differ from those in healthy individuals. In the present study, both the AWI on standing AP radiographs and the sagittal spinopelvic parameters SVA and SS increased with age (Supplemental Fig. 1). Okuda et al. compared spinopelvic alignment between healthy individuals and patients with HD, reporting that in those in their 50 s, SS was higher in HD patients than in healthy controls, whereas no distinct difference was observed between the two groups among those in their 30 s [28]. This suggests that compensatory anterior pelvic tilt in HD becomes more prominent in middle-aged patients, such as those in their 50 s, than in younger individuals.

In the present study, standing AWI was identified as one of the factors associated with age. Previous studies have reported that sufficient AWI is important in the natural course of HD [29] and serves as a prognostic factor for postoperative outcomes in hip dysplasia [30]. In our cohort, patients who could not maintain anterior pelvic tilt in the standing position tended to develop hip pain at a younger age, whereas those who maintained anterior tilt appeared able to preserve their native hips into middle age without undergoing pelvic osteotomy. To validate this consideration, longitudinal evaluation of individual pelvic and spinal alignment is required.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, the assessment of pelvic tilt variation in the sagittal plane was derived from AP hip radiographs rather than from direct measurements. This may have caused measurement errors; however, the accuracy of the method used in this study has been validated, and estimated values are considered highly consistent with the actual measurements [14]. Second, this study only included Japanese patients, which may have limited the generalizability of the findings to non-Asian populations. However, other studies have reported no substantial differences in spinal parameters between Asian and Western populations [16], suggesting that our findings may also apply to other ethnic groups. Third, although we assessed acetabular coverage on radiographs, we did not collect comprehensive clinical data that may have been related to pelvic tilt and spinopelvic parameters, including hip range of motion, hip and low back pain, functional outcomes, and impingement-related findings. Accordingly, we could not determine whether age-related differences in posture-dependent pelvic tilt and spinopelvic alignment are associated with differences in clinical presentation, and causal inferences cannot be made. Future studies integrating radiographic evaluation with standardized clinical assessments are warranted. Fourth, spinopelvic parameters were assessed only preoperatively, and postoperative or follow-up measurements after osteotomy were not available. Therefore, we could not evaluate longitudinal changes in posture-dependent pelvic tilt and sagittal alignment or the effects of osteotomy over time. Finally, this was a cross-sectional study. While we demonstrated pelvic tilt characteristics across different age groups spanning 10–59 years in patients with HD, these findings only described preoperative characteristics in patients scheduled for PAO and did not reflect longitudinal changes. Future longitudinal studies are needed to investigate age-related changes in posture-dependent pelvic tilt and spinopelvic alignment over time.

Conclusions

In symptomatic patients with HD, younger patients exhibited greater posterior shift during the transition, resulting in reduced anterosuperior acetabular coverage. In contrast, middle-aged patients maintained anterior pelvic tilt in standing. These findings highlight age-related differences in posture-dependent pelvic tilt and support the use of standing AP hip radiographs to assess functional acetabular coverage. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to determine whether maintaining anterior pelvic tilt in the standing position contributes to native hip preservation.
