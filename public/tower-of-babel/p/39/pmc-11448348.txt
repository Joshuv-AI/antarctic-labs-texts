Promoting mental health in higher education: towards a model of well-being factors in emerging adulthood
Authors: Ariadna Martínez-García, Susana Valverde-Montesino, Mercedes García-García
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThis study aims to contribute to the development of a theoretical model that is useful for enhancing well-being/positive mental health with pedagogical resources that enable the acquisition of skills and knowledge, particularly during Emerging Adulthood.MethodsThis paper enquires into the role of well-being promotion in higher education following the dual-factor model of mental health difficulties and wellbeing. The study narratively reviews the main well-being models and presents a compared theoretical synthesis examining the dimensions that promote or facilitate the presence of well-being using a promotion approach.ResultsThe study identifies 14 dimensions involved in wellbeing—emotional, psychological and social factors—that can be potentially improved through learning processes. Drawing on empirical and theoretical studies on emerging adult population, a factorial model is proposed. Preliminary factor interactions are examined paying close attention to the cognitive processes that explain or affect their relationship with well-being, the possible correlations between them and particularities of emerging adults.ConclusionWellbeing factors can be particularly targeted by educational promotion, as they can be developed through learning processes. An integrative model that provides a broad perspective can assist in pedagogical design and defining educational goals for these learning processes involved in wellbeing promotion.KEYWORDS: Well-being, mental health, mental health promotion, emerging adulthood, student wellbeing, higher education, university

Introduction

Over the past decade, the mental health of new generations has shown signs of deterioration that have alarmed health and education professionals. Compared with the mid-2000s, more adolescents and early adults in the late 2010s experienced major depression, suicidal ideation and more attempted and committed suicide. This situation implies a generational change in mood disorders incidence and suicide-related concerns rather than an overall increment across all age groups, considering this trend is weak or absent among people aged 26 and over (Twenge et al., 2019). Poor mental wellbeing was found to be of particular concern among higher education students: in 2018, one in three first-year college students tested positive for at least one common DSM—IV disorder (Auerbach et al., 2018).

Majority of students (98,97%) enrolled at bachelor or equivalent degree are 18–29 years of age (OECD Statistics, 2023). To a considerable extent, most of the students are part of the population group now suffering from the greater increase in mental health difficulties. Furthermore, this life period also entails significant consequences on posterior adult years mental health as approximately 75% of adulthood mental disorders have their first onset before age 25 (Kessler et al., 2007) and subthreshold depression in the beginning of this period elevates risks of later depression and suicidal behaviours (Fergusson et al., 2005). While mental health conditions start developing during adolescence, they predominantly manifest in early adulthood (Kessler et al., 2005), comprising the higher education study period. This manifestation of mental health difficulties is related to the fact that university students face the life stage in which the greatest number of transitions, acquisition of responsibilities, critical decision-making, emotional maturation and deepening of identity exploration occur (Caspi, 2002; Iarovici, 2014). These developmental transitions require major life changes within individuals and in social roles and contexts and can contribute to mental health alterations. The transition from adolescence to adulthood is a critical life transition as it generally entails contextual and social role changes that frequently occur simultaneously (Schulenberg et al., 2004).

The acute risk increment of mental health difficulties in emerging adults implies consequences for the future social and emotional functioning of this generation. In order to act on the mental health issues that affect the rising generations and design educational interventions at higher education institutions, it is necessary to examine the specific characteristics of this developmental period related with their well-being.

Specially after the publication of the Okanagan Charter (2015)—an international charter on health promoting institutions of higher education–, universities and education professionals are progressively more interested in providing learning experiences that enhance and support student well-being (Stanton et al., 2016). Higher education constitutes an important agent in emerging adults’ wellbeing promotion, as it offers a unique opportunity to access Emerging Adulthood population, and its settings provide a prime developmental context for addressing mental health promotion broadly (Conley et al., 2013). Additionally, the transitional nature of higher education, particularly university, is a chance to acquire strengths and skills that can assist students in dealing with new developmental challenges, role transitions and stressors (Schulenberg et al., 2004).

The present study explores the dimensions that promote or facilitate the presence of well-being/positive mental health. This study aims to contribute to the development of a theoretical model that is useful for enhancing these strengths with pedagogical resources that enable the acquisition of skills and knowledge during Emerging Adulthood. Specifically, the study addresses the research questions:

“Which are the factors influencing mental well-being that can be developed or learned in higher education?”

“What specific characteristics of the developmental stage of Emerging Adulthood might influence these factors of mental well-being?”

As mental health education and resilience education are receiving particular attention from researchers and educators (Ye, Chen, et al., 2024), this study aims to contribute to filling the gap in the literature for specific wellbeing promotion models that are suitable for the design of educational interventions and pedagogical practices. Mental health and well-being models have been mainly applied for diagnostic purposes and preventive efforts, but the need of mental health promotion initiatives entails exploring well-being through an educational lens. Contributions towards the configuration of theoretical models must take into consideration both the factors necessary for the presence of well-being and the possibility of their development through learning processes. For this purpose, this study answers the research questions by providing a narrative review of the main well-being models and by presenting a comparative theoretical synthesis that examines the dimensions of well-being using a well-being promotion approach. After identifying the dimensions that can potentially be improved through learning processes, a preliminary factorial model is proposed by drawing on empirical and theoretical studies on emerging adult population.

Conceptualising mental health and well-being

The terms “mental health” and “well-being” are often used together in the literature due to the connections that exist between the meaning of both terms, however their relationship is not frequently explored (Norwich et al., 2022). Nowadays, two main conceptions explain the relationship between mental health and well-being, a one-dimensional and a dual-factor model. Depending on how education professionals understand mental health and well-being, the design of promotion initiatives could have different educational aims.

Initially, conceptualizations of health emerged as an opposition to illness implying a pathogenic model that focused on disorders, where illness and health were mutually exclusive. This meaning has been transformed with the influence of later perspectives and connected with wellbeing-related concepts. In 1979, the paradigm was firstly challenged by Antonovsky, who proposed a salutogenic model that focused on health-promoting factors (Antonovsky, 1979). The model discussed health as a continuum where different states other than complete health or disease are possible and claimed salutogenic theory as a guide for health promotion (Antonovsky, 1996). Years later, since the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion in 1986 (World Health Organization, 1986), World Health Organization (WHO) stablished a connection between health and wellbeing. Health concept was described as a state of physical, mental and social wellbeing, rather than the absence of illness (World Health Organization, 1986). In 2013, mental health was conceptualized aligned with this health as wellbeing meaning acquiring a positive meaning in terms of wellbeing (Norwich et al., 2022). Thereby, WHO defined mental health as a state of wellbeing that allows individuals to realize their own abilities, cope with life normal stress, work productively and contribute to their community (World Health Organization, 2013). As a result, this conception would constitute a one-dimensional model that considers positive mental health or wellbeing in one end of the spectrum and negative mental health or mental illness on the other end (Norwich et al., 2022).

A different conception of mental illness and mental health addresses their relationship as separate factors. Keyes (Keyes, 2005) presents a dual-factor, or two continua, model that finds mental illness and mental health (or wellbeing) as two different but related dimensions. Both dimensions can be represented in a continuum, one that indicates the presence or absence of mental health/wellbeing, and the other the presence or absence of mental illness (Westerhof & Keyes, 2010). First continuum displays no mental health difficulties in one end of the continuum opposed to mental health disorders on the other end. Second continuum is defined by flourishing (high mental health) and languishing (low mental health) each on every end of the continuum (Keyes, 2002, 2014b; Keyes, 2010; Westerhof & Keyes, 2010).

Norwich et al. (Norwich et al., 2022) critically considerate school aims in terms of wellbeing using this model, arguing that the two-continua model is useful for underpinning mental health and well-being promotion and it allows further discussions on the role of higher education institutions. The two-continua model implies framing mental health and mental difficulties differently, each one with their own intervening factors, despite the relation between both continua. Mental difficulties continuum—mental disorder—absence of difficulties addresses illness—is based on psychopathology concerns, therefore the efforts for public health improvement refer preventive, supportive, coping and therapeutic interventions (Keyes, 2013a; Norwich et al., 2022). Designing interventions and practices to address this dimension include the multiple possibilities all over the continuum. For this purpose, the strategies on mental difficulties can be developed based on conceptions about risk factors (preventative efforts when the absence of difficulties) and symptomatology (remedial efforts when suffering mental difficulties).

The mental health continuum—languishing-flourishing—is grounded in a broader framework that includes not only psychological but also personal and social aspects of wellbeing. The term flourishing is related with equity, social justice and personal dignity (Norwich et al., 2022). Flourishing or high mental health individuals both function well and feel good about their lives (Keyes, 2010). Consequently, Norwich et al. (Norwich et al., 2022) claim that flourishing promotion is an essential aspect of education, and from an educational policy perspective, it is aligned with historic ideas about schooling purposes regarding society and individual’s development.

In the same way, higher education involves flourishing promotion as it supports new generations learning of future life skills and knowledge that will enable them to participate and contribute to their society. Moreover, higher education is a major institution in modern society that takes part in the educational process for emancipation, critical thinking and intellectual and moral development (Barnett, 1988).

Supporting emerging adults in this learning process could encompass flourishing promotion by potentiating the factors that help people with positive mental health. In the view of the fact that the absence of mental difficulties does not result in the presence of mental health, protective conditions against mental illness do not necessarily promote positive mental health (Keyes, 2013a). Keyes (2014b) highlights the need for researchers to explore how to achieve more individuals to stay or become flourishing. Author suggests a shift in policy and practice by disclosing that mental health promotion cannot seek health by focusing on activities directed towards illness. Efforts contributing to mental health must focus on positive mental health by promoting flourishing and protecting against its loss (Keyes, 2014b).

The evolution of well-being approaches

Nowadays, there are two major approaches to conceptualizing well-being: hedonic well-being and eudaimonic well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2001). These approaches originated from two philosophical traditions and became fundamental for the development of modern well-being conceptions in psychological science (Heintzelman, 2018).

First approach features individuals’ evaluation of their own life, emotionally and cognitively (Tov, 2018). This notion evolved from the philosophical tradition of hedonic wellbeing (HWB), presented by philosophers such as Aristippus, Bentham and Mill (Tatarkiewicz, 1976). Hedonic conception raised from the idea that pleasure or hedonic enjoyment is the highest good (Heintzelman, 2018). HWB is often referred to as subjective wellbeing (SWB) because of its relation with individuals’ assessment of how well their life is going (Tov, 2018).

Following this approach, Diener’s stablished the Tripartite Model of subjective wellbeing (E. Diener, 1984), in which SWB is determined by (i) frequent pleasant feelings, (ii) infrequent unpleasant feelings and (iii) cognitive appraisal that life is satisfying. The components of the Tripartite Model of SWB distinguish between cognitive wellbeing (life satisfaction) and emotional wellbeing (positive emotions and negative emotions) (Tov & Diener, 2013). The three components are usually related as individuals who are satisfied with life frequently experience positive emotions and infrequently experience negative emotions. This model relation with educational promotion relies on the fact that SWB components may be enhanced as they are responsive to cognitive, social, affective and behavioural skills and practices (Kushlev et al., 2017). Diener and colleagues (Heintzelman et al., 2020; Kushlev et al., 2017) research on SWB promotion demonstrate that the three SWB components can be enhanced through the development knowledge and skills related to happiness and well-being.

The second approach originated from philosophical perspectives on eudaimonia. This approach of eudaimonic well-being (EWB) has been fundamental for the development of modern models of psychological well-being (Heintzelman, 2018). The concept of eudaimonia was first presented by Aristotle (Aristotle & Crisp, 2014) as “activity expressing virtue” which describes well-being and happiness as living in accord with one’s true nature or “daimon”. Therefore, eudaimonia is related with the development of one’s virtue and excellence but also refers to that which is worth pursuing in life (Huta & Waterman, 2014). In line with Aristotle’s view of eudaimonia, EWB approaches emphasize living up to one’s personal potential (Tov, 2018). EWB approaches stablish certain needs or qualities that are essential for a person’s psychological growth and development and the fulfilment of these needs enables the individual to reach their full potential (Ryan & Deci, 2001). Eudaimonia has been adopted into well-being psychological science, but it is now treated as a subjective state, and this subjectivity is a central feature of the construct (Heintzelman, 2018). From an eudaimonic approach, subjective well-being is also related with a broader range of experiences, as it comprises values, goals and need fulfilment (Ryan & Deci, 2001). As a consequence, eudaimonic well-being involves pursuing activities and goals that are aligned with one’s identity and values (McGregor & Little, 1998; Waterman, 1993). Key theoretical research that follow from this eudaimonic perspective are Riff’s Six-Factor Model of psychological well-being (Ryff, 1989) and Deci’s Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000). These models have different conceptions of eudaimonia, and their related research provides different involved factors according to them.

Ryff suggests a broader, trait-like manner definition, in which eudaimonia means individuals fully psychologically function and success despite the existential challenges of life (Heintzelman, 2018). Ryff claims that there are more aspects involved in SWB than the three pillars of the Tripartite Model (Ryff, 1989; Ryff & Singer, 1996). Author reflected on well-being research mainly focusing on happiness, feelings and life satisfaction, and suggested a more inclusive conceptualization of well-being that captured an extended range of what being well means. As a result of reviewing the characteristics of well-being described in the theoretical literature, the author concluded that many theorists were describing similar features of positive psychological functioning (Ryff & Singer, 1996). These found points of convergence became the basis for the resultant components of the Six-Factor Model of psychological well-being (Ryff, 2014). These factors are (i) purpose in life, (ii) autonomy, (iii) personal growth, (iv) environmental mastery, (v) relatedness and (vi) self-acceptance. An important notion in Riff’s model is that these components can be enriched through positive experiences (Ryff et al., 2014). Thereby, psychological well-being can be promoted with activities that involve experiencing positive self-regard, having good relationships with others, feeling a sense of mastery and purpose in everyday life, and noticing growth and development in one’s competences.

Ryan and Deci’s eudaimonic approach of wellbeing (Ryan & Deci, 2000) conceptualize eudaimonia as living a life in full accord with one’s potential. The authors establish a set of basic psychological needs, which are essential and necessary for psychological growth, integrity and well-being (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Ryan et al., 2008). Their Self-Determination Theory (SDT) is a set of smaller theories that explore the interplay between extrinsic forces that act on individuals, intrinsic motives and human inherent needs. The theory concludes that people have a natural tendency to seek personal growth, mastery of further challenges and integration of new experiences to build a sense of self coherently. These developmental predispositions need social and psychological supports and “nutriments”. Included in SDT is the Basic Psychological Needs Theory (BPNT) which elaborates the concepts of psychological needs and well-being. In this theory, those nutriments are frequent experiences of (i) relatedness, (ii) competence and (iii) autonomy. These types of experiences enable the person to develop and function optimally and enjoy wellness (Ryan & Deci, 2002). In consequence, SDT conceptualization entails the role of education as a facilitator of experiences for well-being. Learning experiences and higher education environments are main contributors to the fulfilment of these well-being needs that support relatedness, autonomy and competence.

Nevertheless, HWB and EWB approaches are not mutually exclusive in overall mental well-being, and researchers have also theorized conceptualizations that combine hedonic and eudaimonic components. Moreover, measures of hedonic and eudaimonic well-being highly correlate, which suggest that positive feelings and positive functioning are often present simultaneously (Waterman, 1993). Two main models combining HWB and EWB are Keyes’ Mental Health Continuum and Seligman’s PERMA Model of flourishing.

One of these models, is suggested by Keyes (2002) who also offered the previously mentioned conception of flourishing to describe a condition characterized by high levels of both hedonic and eudaimonic well-being. Keyes research focused on a social well-being dimension following an eudaimonic approach and developed operational dimensions that illustrate the challenges individuals face being part of society, groups, institutions and communities (Keyes, 2013a). In his work, he identifies five components of social well-being: (i) social integration, (ii) social contribution, (iii) social coherence, (iv) social actualization and (v) social acceptance (Keyes, 1998). The author collaborated with Ryff, integrating social well-being into the conceptualization of eudaimonia. Results of confirmatory factor analysis models showed strong support for the proposed the five-factor theory of social well-being and the Six-Factor model of psychological well-being as the best-fitting model in a representative sample of adult population in the US (Ryff & Keyes, 1995). Keyes research on well-being measurement reflected the three distinct but correlated latent factors: emotional well-being, psychological well-being and social well-being (Keyes, 2006). The resulting model includes psychological and social well-being factors (eudaimonic) as well emotional well-being aspects (three hedonic dimensions of the Tripartite model). These components are not static but change over the life span just as levels of positive mental health (Keyes, 2010). According to the theory, mental well-being promotion is a process of optimization of the different eudaimonic and hedonic components that allow individuals to function well and feel good about life (Provencher & Keyes, 2013).

Furthermore, Seligman (Seligman, 2011) also suggests a conceptualization that involves HWB and EWB. The author presented the PERMA model of flourishing, a conception of flourishing that combines psychological strengths and positive emotions. The model is characterized by each dimension contributing to well-being, but not defining individuals’ well-being state. Seligman identifies that people who enjoy high levels of wellbeing show: (i) Positive emotions, (ii) Engagement, (iii) Relatedness, (iv) Meaning in life, (v) Accomplishments (PERMA). Individuals have greater or lesser innate capacities to experience the PERMA elements, however, the model affirms that people are able to strengthen their capacities. Author claims that the techniques of thriving can be taught and learnt (2011). Therefore, PERMA model implies that well-being promotion includes education as a main agent in the development of these “teachable” and “learnable” factors.

Well-being models consider their components may be enhanced and in consequence, well-being can be improved. The conceptions of these factors as acquirable skills and traits have great implications for educational institutions since they are the main agents in youth development. With the purpose of designing learning and training opportunities for their development, specifically in emerging adults, a factor comparison and synthesis is needed. This synthesis provides specific target areas that allow pedagogical design minding educational aims.

Synthesis of factors and dimensions for well-being promotion

The breakdown on the development of well-being concept, main approaches and different theoretical structures throws light on the diverse factors that should be involved in well-being promotion initiatives. The design of these well-being promotion activities needs a previous determination of intervention areas, so that actions are stablished with specific educational targets. With this aim, a comparison and synthesis of components supports the selection of components involved in mental wellbeing—emotional, psychological and social dimensions—that can be potentially improved through learning processes.

Emotional dimensions

Emotional well-being components are included in Tripartite, Mental Health Continuum and PERMA models. This wellbeing area is related with experiencing positive and enjoyable emotions and feeling satisfied with own’s life. However, the Tripartite model and the Mental Health Continuum Model—which bases its emotional dimension on the Tripartite model—also include negative emotions as a dimension negatively related with well-being. In the Tripartite model, both positive and negative emotions interplay in well-being, and moreover the balance of people’s positive and negative emotions contributes to judgements of life satisfaction (E. Diener & Larsen, 1993). Negative emotions are a risk factor of well-being decrease, so intervention perspectives directly working on them need to use a preventive perspective. Taking into account the well-being promotion approach, negative emotions are not a factor to potentiate but positive emotions. Nevertheless, positive emotion potentiation shares common nodes with negative emotion diminution as positive emotion promotion strategies are related with emotion regulation, which helps managing emotions arising from environmental stimuli. Thus, following the promotion approach, emotional factors comprise positive emotions and life satisfaction.

Positive emotions. Positive emotion dimension is included in the Tripartite, Mental Health Continuum and PERMA models. In Diener’s conceptualization, positive emotions are those emotions that have a pleasant valence (Larsen & Diener, 1992); Keyes explains that positive emotions are characterized by positive affect and vitality (Keyes, 2002); and in Seligman’s model, positive emotions are those which entail pleasure (Seligman et al., 2004).

Life Satisfaction. Life satisfaction component is included in Tripartite and Mental Health Continuum models, but, moreover, life satisfaction is a key well-being construct in positive psychology. Life satisfaction is an important factor in psychological development: it is not only a consequence of favourable life experiences, but also an antecedent of positive life outcomes (Huebner et al., 2013). Following Diener’s model, life satisfaction refers to a cognitive, judgemental process. Judgements of satisfaction are dependent upon a comparison of one’s circumstances with what is thought to be an appropriate standard of “good life” (E. D. Diener et al., 1985). In the same way, Keyes conception of life satisfaction is also an assessment of the different life domains satisfaction of the person (Keyes, 2014a).

Psychological dimensions

Psychological wellbeing refers to how people psychologically function. This well-being area is focused on the challenges encountered by individuals in their personal lives (Keyes, 2013a). Psychological components of well-being are found in Six Factor, SDT, Mental Health Continuum and PERMA models. Particularly, Six-Factor model and Health Continuum share the six components of psychological well-being and their conceptualization.

In addition, despite the different concept development and arguments used to stablish their components, some theories share very similar conceptualizations under different factor terminologies. Psychological well-being components included in the theories are: purpose or meaning in life; relatedness or positive relations; autonomy; competence, environmental mastery or achievements; personal growth; and self-acceptance.

Purpose and meaning in life. Purpose and meaning in life are components in Seligman, Ryff and Keyes models. For Seligman, meaning in life plays a role in the fulfilment of the need of feelings of purpose and contribution to higher goods (Seligman, 2002). In the view of Ryff and Keyes, this factor consists in the belief that one’s life is purposeful and meaningful (Ryff & Keyes, 1995). Purpose in life includes a person’s aims and objectives for living, such as the presence of life goals and a sense of directedness. People with high purpose consider their present and past life as meaningful and find life has a direction and meaning in everyday life (Keyes, 2013a).

Relatedness or positive relations. This factor is essential for psychological well-being in every of the models that use an eudaimonic approach. In PERMA model, positive relation factors refers to feeling supported, valued and loved by others (Seligman, 2011). In SDT, relatedness is a basic psychological need that mobilizes people to pursue relationships (Deci & Ryan, 2014). SDT includes Relationships Motivation Theory (RMT) “mini-theory”, which explains why not all relationships are of high quality and satisfy this relatedness need. RMT finds that people not only need to form warm relationships, they need to experience autonomy and also provide autonomy support to others. For Ryff and Keyes, positive relation factor is the possession or ability to form trusting personal relationships with others (Ryff & Keyes, 1995). This factor comprises concern for other people welfare and the ability to empathize, ability to cooperate and ability to compromise (Keyes, 2013a).

Autonomy. Autonomy component is SDT, Six Factor and Mental Health Continuum models. In Ryan and Deci’s model, individuals are autonomous when their behaviour is experienced as willingly performed and when they fully support the actions in which they engage, or the values that the actions express. Thus a person is most autonomous when acting according with their own interests or desires and values (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Furthermore, in Six Factor and Mental Health Continuum models, autonomy is the seeking of self-determination and is related with being guided by own internal standards and values and the abilities to resist social pressure to behave or think in a certain way (Keyes, 2013b).

Competence, environmental mastery and achievements. The factor competence, environmental mastery and achievements come from different conceptual roots, but they all ultimately entail feelings of capacity. These factors share some aspects related with competence, however with different nuances.

The competence factor belongs to the SDT model. In this theory, competence is based on the notion that individuals enjoy the experience of challenging themselves and learning new capacities. In the view of Ryan and Deci, competence is a basic need evident in people seeking out and mastering challenges, and finding experiences of effectiveness and mastery to be intrinsically rewarding (Deci & Ryan, 1985).

PERMA model understands the achievement components as making progress towards goals, feeling capable to effectively manage everyday activities and having a sense of accomplishment (Seligman, 2011).

Six Factor and Mental Health Continuum include the environmental mastery factor. These models understand the component as a capacity to effectively manage one’s life and surrounding world. This includes other abilities as managing daily activities, making effective use of environmental opportunities and choosing or creating environments suitable to personal needs. When individuals recognize personal needs, feel capable to take an active role in obtaining what they need from the environment, a sense of mastery results (Keyes, 2013a).

Personal growth. Personal growth factor is included in the Six-Factor model of psychological well-being and thereby is also part of the Mental Health Continuum model. In these models, personal growth is defined as a sense of continued development as a person (Ryff & Keyes, 1995). Personal growth includes the ability to remain open to new experiences and ability to recognize challenges in a variety of circumstances. This factor also implies searching for skills and opportunities for personal development with the purpose of realizing one’s potential (Keyes, 2013a).

Self-acceptance. Self-acceptance constitutes a factor in Ryff and Keyes’ models. This component is defined as holding positive attitudes towards oneself, evaluating oneself and one’s past life positively (Ryff & Keyes, 1995). Moreover, self-acceptance implies acknowledging the multiple aspects of the self and being acceptant with them, including unpleasant aspects. Individuals must strive the criterion of self-acceptance in order to feel good about themselves (Keyes, 2013a).

Engagement. Of the studied models, PERMA model of flourishing is the only model in which the engagement factor for well-being is found. Engagement is a psychological state in which individuals are absorbed by and focused on the activity or task they are doing (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). Seligman (Seligman, 2011) explains engagement is about flow, a state of being one with an activity in which individuals experience a sense of time stopping, and the loss of self-consciousness. Reaching experiences of flow is relevant for psychological well-being because it invokes a sense of virtuosity resulting from high-level performance (Kubovy, 1999).

Social dimensions

Social wellbeing (positive social functioning (Keyes, 1998)) refers to individuals’ relations with the social world (Tov, 2018). It represents a public experience that is centred on the social challenge individuals find in their communities and social structures (Keyes, 2013a). Social well-being factors are related with community dynamics and social net cohesion building, so despite these factors are only found in the Mental Health Continuum model, social well-being is an essential area in emerging adults positive mental health. As previously explained, social development and learning is especially important during EA as the social brain is still developing and young adults continue acquiring crucial social skills.

Social well-being consists of five dimensions—integration, contribution, coherence, actualization and acceptance—that indicate whether and to what degree individuals are functioning well in their social world (Keyes, 1998).

Social integration. Social integration is the assessment of the quality of one’s relationship with society and community. This factor corresponds with the degree in which individuals have a sense of belonging to, and comfort and support from, a community.

Social contribution. Social contribution refers to individuals’ assessment of their value to society. This is the belief that one is an essential member of society and provides valuable things to offer the world.

Social coherence. This is the perception of the operation, organization and quality of the social world. Social coherence also involves concern for world knowledge, and it is analogous to meaningfulness (includes judgement that society is discernable, sensible and predictable).

Social actualization. Social actualization is the evaluation of the evolution or positive growth of society. This is related with the belief that society potential is realized through citizens and institutions.

Social acceptance. This is the interpretation of society through generalizing qualities of other people. Social acceptance is related with favourable views of human nature: feeling comfortable with other people, trusting them, believing that people can be industrious and thinking others are capable of kindness (Keyes, 2013a).

Emerging adulthood

Young people aged between 18 and the mid-twenties are usually referred in the literature as late adolescents, early adults or young adults. However, this period of transition from adolescence to adulthood has been conceptualized as a different life stage characterized by dynamism and multiple transitional life events that are simultaneously experienced and imply an unstable stage sensitive to specific stressors, i.e.,: attending college (Asberg et al., 2008; Conley et al., 2013; Hicks & Heastie, 2008; Peer et al., 2015; Ramya & Parthasarathy, 2009). First model of Emerging Adulthood (EA) was established by Arnett (Arnett, 1998), describing a profoundly bounded to Westernized cultural phase experienced from the late teen years until the mid-twenties. Nevertheless, the model was later updated by the author, finding this stage to continue until approximately 29 years of age (Arnett, 2004). EA theory claims that this stage is heavily impacted by social and cultural pressures in industrialized and postindustrialized societies such as longer life periods dedicated to education and training, delay in adulthood milestones such as marriage, increase in gender equality and increased social freedom (Arnett, 1998). The model originated from the fundamentals of this environment influencing the transition to adulthood to be more gradual, delaying the sense of becoming fully adult until the late twenties (Arnett, 2006). EA model examines shared experiences about social, relational and identity formation aspects that cannot be found in other life stages (Arnett, 2000).

This theory conceptualized EA as a developmental period with specific characteristics that differ from both adolescence and adulthood. A fundamental feature of this stage is the social and culturally related role transitions. Because emerging adults’ development is defined by changing demands, roles and experienced life events (Salmela-Aro et al., 2016) and individuals are faced with more transitions and life decisions than at any other stage (Caspi, 2002), this period is characterized by its instability. During this time young people face multiple stressors as they experience significant life changes, important decision-making and outstanding relationship dynamics transformation (Iarovici, 2014). Family relation dynamics change with the gain of autonomy, diminution of dependence from caretakers gaining autonomy and relationship becoming more egalitarian. Specially related with attending university, or other higher education institutions, is the change of environment and/or address, distancing from friends and relatives, and the establishment of new close social relations. Due to this, the first challenges are related to creating a new social support network, a task that sometimes results in feelings of loneliness (Roberts, 2018). Simultaneously, identity exploration is a major developmental activity during this stage (D. Wood et al., 2018), and this period offers the most opportunities for identity exploration in the areas of relationships, work and ideology (Arnett, 2000). Furthermore, EA implies a self-focused period as individuals focus on themselves and grow personal areas of their life. They learn knowledge, skills and self-understanding required for later adult life. Additionally, Arnett’s theory describes two feelings frequently experienced by emerging adults: “feeling in between” meaning individuals not identifying either as adolescents nor as adults, and “multiple futures remain possible” describing emerging adults feelings of different life possibilities and opportunities in their life path are yet to experience (Arnett, 2004).

Despite not all developmental or life course scientists agree that Emerging Adulthood is a developmental stage (D. Wood et al., 2018), and its original underpinning is less likely applicable to non-Western cultures (Schwartz, 2016), there is consensus on economic and social circumstances have broaden the transition into adulthood introducing significant role and developmental demands beyond the traditional adolescence period (Côté, 2014). At the beginning of EA, individuals aged 17–18 typically attend Secondary Education, start engaging in romantic relationships, and live with their caretakers, whom they depend on. By the end of the stage, mid to late twenties, most emerging adults have clear career paths ahead of them, are in long-term romantic relationships and live independently. In the course of this time, people start to learn and develop the necessary skills and traits for becoming self-sufficient, engage in committed mature relationships, assume adult roles and responsibilities and achieve the educational level and training for their future work years (D. Wood et al., 2018). Conceptualizing EA as a life course stage has been found useful to explain the social, cognitive and psychological development that occurs during this time. Further justification for considering EA as a stage is provided by research demonstrating extended brain development into the late twenties (Spear, 2000).

Neuropsychological aspects of emerging adulthood

Emerging adults’ brain continues developing and keeps learning essential social and emotional skills. EA is a crucial developmental stage for individuals’ mental health with implications for later adult life wellbeing. Due to this, education has an important role as a contributor to new generations mentally healthy development by offering suitable learning opportunities that act on EA mental health educational needs.

Brain structure and function throughout EA influence the ongoing developmental trajectories that support well-being in adulthood (Taber- Thomas et al., 2014). The brain continues to develop structurally, differing from its development during adolescence and later adulthood years. Cortical grey matter volume peaks in childhood, declines during adolescence and stabilizes around age 25 (Aubert-Broche et al., 2013; Mills et al., 2016; Tamnes et al., 2017; Wierenga et al., 2014). Although adolescent brain experiences grey matter reduction and white matter increasement, and both processes continue during EA, they do not evolve simultaneously in all brain areas. As Emerging Adulthood approximates, brain regions going through these processes change to higher-order association (frontal executive cortex and frontolimbic cortex) (Taber- Thomas et al., 2014). This neurodevelopment supports emerging adults’ roles promoting adaptive future-oriented behaviour through the strengthening of prefrontal regulation of limbic function and an emerging balance between prefrontal subregions. Therefore, functional development is influenced by increased top-down prefrontal function, and the arising balance between ventromedial and ventrolateral prefrontal regulatory function (Taber- Thomas et al., 2014). As during EA exists an overlap between two neurodevelopmental activities, individual differences in neurodevelopment might explain both the differences in functioning, and the risk of suffering mental disorders (Taber- Thomas et al., 2014).

Moreover, EA is a period of heightened risk for psychopathology associated with emotional dysregulation. Developing adaptive implicit and explicit emotion regulation skills is essential for mental wellbeing and during EA there is a shift in the regions that are more likely recruited for emotion regulation. For the implicit regulation process, adolescents usually recruit regions implicated in the early stages of emotion processing, whereas emerging adults exhibit activation of higher-order regions involved in the extraction of semantic meaning (Pozzi et al., 2021). Adolescents more consistently recruit the amygdala, fusiform gyrus and thalamus than emerging adults, who usually recruit the posterior superior temporal sulcus (Taber- Thomas et al., 2014).

Particularly related with social aspects, structural changes in the “social brain” occur during adolescence and Emerging Adulthood. Social brain is the network of brain regions associated with “mentalizing”, an aspect of social cognition that refers to the ability to infer other people's hidden mental states (Kliemann & Adolphs, 2018). These regions consist of medial prefrontal cortex, temporoparietal junction, posterior superior temporal sulcus and anterior temporal cortex. Social brain regions structural development stabilizes around the early twenties except for the anterior temporal cortex, which shows a different trajectory, levelling off around the mid-twenties (Mills et al., 2014). As this area is linked to limbic structures and medial prefrontal cortex through one of the last white matter tracks to reach maturity—the uncinate fasciculus—(Lebel et al., 2008), findings suggest that late myelination of the anterior temporal cortex allow a long window for learning social scripts (Mills et al., 2014). If the social brain continues forming until the mid-twenties, its developing process can be enhanced through educational practices. This would have educational implications that concern higher education institutions, their curriculum design and the need of social skills teaching and learning. In addition, mentalizing is related to student wellbeing as it influences social interactions in the peer environment (Andrews et al., 2021) and research suggest that mentalizing may be involved in the successful adaptation to stressful social interactions (Schmälzle et al., 2017). Furthermore, imbalances in mentalizing are associated with suffering from mental disorders and entail vulnerability to psychopathology (Luyten et al., 2020).

Towards a model for well-being promotion in emerging adults

The resulting integrative model of approaches and factors includes dimensions by employing a wellbeing promotion approach, focused on dimensions that can be enhanced through learning processes. As discussed, the resulting theoretical corpus consists of 14 dimensions that are subject for later specific empirical testing in an emerging adult population. The resulting dimensions are almost entirely consistent with the Mental Health Continuum model, which previous confirmatory factor analysis studies have found to have two main suitable factorial structures. Studies have found that best fitting a correlated three-factor model (De Carvalho et al., 2016; Guo et al., 2015; Peña Contreras et al., 2017; Petrillo et al., 2015) and a bifactorial model (Echeverría et al., 2017; Piqueras et al., 2022; Yeo et al., 2022). Latest contributions to the field have introduced exploratory structural equation modelling in the analyses (ESEM), finding a bifactor ESEM as the best fit (De Bruin & Du Plessis, 2015; Hides et al., 2016; Joshanloo & Lamers, 2016; Jovanović, 2015; Lamborn et al., 2018; Longo et al., 2020; Schutte & Wissing, 2017). Moreover, Joshanloo and Lamers (Joshanloo & Lamers, 2016) encourage the study of the three factors in the context of ESEM rather than CFA or SEM due to its better capacity to comprehend cultural differences in well-being.

The resulting 14 dimensions need to be discussed considering the cognitive processes that explain or affect its relationship with well-being, the possible correlations between them and particularities of emerging adults.

First, the two dimensions resulting from the emotional area have not only been found in the well-being theories featured in this analysis but have also been studied by other authors with regard to their relationship with well-being. Specifically, there is an independent, more extensive volume of research on positive emotions and well-being developed by Fredrickson. Theorist work on positive emotions has resulted in the formulation of the broaden-and-build theory (Fredrickson, 1998; Fredrickson et al., 2004) which is useful to explain the relation between positive emotions and well-being promotion. The model explains that, unlike negative emotions (which narrow people’s repertoires of thought-action), positive emotions expand individuals’ thought-action repertoires. By broadening people though-action repertoires, they are encouraged to discover new ideas and actions, and so they build their physical, intellectual, social and psychological resources. Positive emotions broaden the scope of cognition by (a) potentiating novel cognitive associations, (b) extending people’s cognitive categories and (c) supporting creative thinking (Isen, 2005). As socioemotional and intellectual skills are built, brain development is enhanced (Fredrickson et al., 2000). In addition, positive emotions predict future increases in positive emotions through these effects on broadened thinking (Fredrickson & Joiner, 2002). Moreover, experiencing positive emotions seems to have a buffering effect on the later impact of dealing with negative emotions (Kok et al., 2008). Evidence suggests that cultivating experiences of positive emotions at opportune moments allow individual to cope with negative emotions and improve well-being (Fredrickson, 2000).

Furthermore, the second emotional dimension, life satisfaction, entails limitations that are of great relevance for culturally sensitive well-being research. Life satisfaction is related with the evaluation of the balance between positive and negative affect, and for this reason it constitutes a highly culturally influenced factor. The explanation of the cultural differences in life satisfaction conception, is that different cultures have very diverse perspectives on what a “good life” is and value life aspects differently (Tov & Diener, 2009). Example of these findings are about contrasts between collectivist cultures (which have a tendency to be more concerned with the group’s needs, goals and interests than with individual concerns) and individualist cultures (those with a tendency to be more concerned about individual needs, goals and interests than with group-oriented concerns (Trubisky et al., 1991). In collectivist cultures, life satisfaction is predicted both from one’s emotions and the perceived norm for life satisfaction (Kim & Tov, 2010). In contrast, life satisfaction in individualist cultures is more strongly predicted from emotions than from perceived social norms (Suh et al., 1998).

Despite these differences and the difficulty of culturally appropriate promotion, an approach that helps conceptualizing life satisfaction promotion is its relationship with feelings of gratitude. Gratitude is an essential concept to explain life satisfaction in PERMA model and overall positive psychology. Wood, Joseph and Linley (A. M. Wood et al., 2007) findings in college students’ population show a significant positive correlation between gratitude and both satisfaction with life and happiness. More grateful individuals were likely to focus on positive features in their lives and view the world as an enjoyable place. Moreover, grateful students were found to reach more for social support and use positive coping strategies.

When discussing the promotion of psychological factors, despite the analysis of these theoretical models, there are relationships between factors and other cognitive processes specifically relevant for higher education. These will be important for the establishment of educational objectives and actions that promote healthy development and positive psychological functioning.

To begin with, there is extensive research on well-being and meaning in life that is independent to these models’ frameworks. A growing body of research identifies meaning in life as a fundamental human need that strongly influences psychological well-being (Routledge & FioRito, 2021). Meaning and purpose are mostly presented together in the literature. Heintzelman and King (Heintzelman & King, 2015) studied diverse suggested definitions of meaning in life and concluded that the idea of a meaningful life is commonly characterized by a sense of significance, purpose and coherence. Martela and Steger (Martela & Steger, 2016) work on these three features and explain significance—a sense of life’s inherent value and having a life worth living, purpose—sense of core goals, aims and direction in life and coherence—comprehensibility and one’s life making sense.

An ability that is not only related with purpose in life, but also the factors autonomy, achievements/competence/environmental mastery, engagement and personal growth is the skill for stablishing and adjusting goals. Goal setting and goals content has been particularly studied in emerging adults, specifically college students, due to its great relevance for predicting student well-being (Salmela-Aro, 2009, 2010; Salmela-Aro & Nurmi, 1997; Salmela-Aro et al., 2012; Shulman & Nurmi, 2010). Healthy goal stablishing and goal adjustment have consequences for wellbeing as personal goals that match developmental tasks of a particular life stage are adaptive in directing one’s life and one’s sense of wellbeing (Salmela-Aro, 2010). The life-span model of motivation (Salmela-Aro, 2009) proposes different the mechanisms for goal setting and disengagement, from which one that acquires relevance in emerging adulthood is the compensation mechanism (Salmela-Aro, 2009, 2010). In the process of dealing with transitions, young people receive feedback on their successes and failures. This feedback requires them to compensate in adulthood by adjusting goals, plans and mindsets or ways of thinking in order to successfully meet future challenges presented by their developmental trajectories. This mechanism is not as relevant in adolescence, but as individuals mature, they are required to deal with failure more frequently than in adolescence (Salmela-Aro, 2010). As an example, goal disengagement is a protective strategy for well-being as letting go of a “dream” is an adaptive strategy for well-being when one’s goals do not match one’s current life situation.

Specifically related with the types of goals that higher education students set for themselves, low wellbeing has been found to be related with setting self-focused goals. Self and identity issues are normalized as part of adolescence, but if they are not correctly adjusted, they become negative self-goals, i.e.,: “Not to be a failure”. On the contrary, emerging adults who set family and interpersonal related goals show a higher level of wellbeing (Salmela-Aro, 2010). Moreover, Salmela-Aro and colleagues research (Salmela-Aro et al., 2012) suggests that goals related to one’s family, life philosophy and education have the potential to reduce the association between self-focused goals and depressive symptoms.

Motivation and goal setting are also present in the Australian higher education promotion project Enhancing Student Well-Being: Resources for University Educators (Baik et al., 2016b). The project fundaments the educational initiatives (curriculum design, teaching strategies, institutional policy design and learning environments) for emerging adults around the SDT concept of autonomous motivation, which influences the well-being essentials: experiences of autonomy, feelings of competence, positive relationships and sense of belonging (this last one is not included in BNPT). These five essentials are resources for psychological well-being that sustain and enhance autonomous motivation and the engagement (which flows from it). For this purpose, one of the keys to promoting well-being is to redesign practices and environments that demotivate or control students, orient learning towards extrinsic rather than intrinsic goals, undermine a sense of belonging, or frustrate experiences of close relationships, autonomy and competence (Baik et al., 2016a). In addition, Seligman notes a relation between engagement and feelings of competence, as he finds experiences of success or achievement are reinforcing (Seligman, 2011). As a result, experiences of competence are important for maintaining, engagement, persistence and positive task motivation.

Furthermore, the analysis of the PERMA model conducted with university students Based on emotion-action-experience-outcome found interactions between its components (Ye, Wu, et al., 2024). In this model, affect is positive emotion, the action involves both engagement and achievement, and the experience involves interpersonal relationships and meaning in life, and the outcome is sustainable wellbeing. The authors reported that positive emotions have a positive effect on engagement and a positive effect on achievement; engagement was found to have a positive effect on interpersonal relationships and meaning in life; and achievement had a positive effect on interpersonal relationships and positive effect on meaning. Theoretical models that aim to explain the interactions between well-being components are useful for understanding other relevant mechanisms underlying emerging adults’ well-being. Although further research is needed to explain the underlying mechanisms, these findings are relevant to the configuration of well-being models because of their potential to inform well-being pathways, particularly for the EA population.

Moreover, to develop a model that explains well-being during EA, it is important to note the findings reported by Ryff and Keyes (Ryff & Keyes, 1995) about two of the psychological well-being factors, which might have implications for research in this developmental stage. In their work, factorial analyses indicated that the two theoretical constructs self-acceptance and environmental mastery were highly correlated. The data suggested a possible five-factor model that would combine indicators of self-acceptance and environmental mastery. However, examination of the structure through other analyses, specifically life course profiles, indicated that self-acceptance and environmental mastery had different age profiles. The factor self-acceptance showed little variation with age while environmental mastery showed incremental age differences. The younger age group in Ryff’s initial model validation sample (Ryff, 1989) (aged 18–29) and the younger group in Ryff and Keyes study (Ryff & Keyes, 1995) (aged 25–29) scored in both studies significantly lower than the middle and older age groups in environmental mastery. These results, which indicate that the two theoretical constructs are indeed two different factors when considering life course profiles, might not replicate when performing factor analysis in EA population. Further studies to confirm a well-being model useful for EA population must consider the possible outcomes regarding these factors correlation when conducting factorial analyses.

Furthermore, factors that explain psychological well-being in EA may be subject of gender differences. Research on psychological well-being consistently finds that women score higher than men in the dimension positive relationships (Ahrens & Ryff, 2006; Lindfors et al., 2006; Matud et al., 2019; Ryff, 1991; Ryff & Keyes, 1995) and men score higher than women in autonomy (Ahrens & Ryff, 2006; Lindfors et al., 2006; Matud et al., 2019). However, these studies were conducted with adults, so these differences may not hold true for emerging adulthood, as there is evidence that factors of psychological well-being differs with age (Lindfors et al., 2006; Ryff, 1989, 1991; Ryff & Keyes, 1995). Indeed, Matud et al. (Matud et al., 2022) found no significant differences in the emerging adult population in scores of autonomy nor positive relations related to gender.

Other gender differences are found in the factors of personal growth (Ahrens & Ryff, 2006; Baik et al., 2016a; Matud et al., 2022; Ryff, 1989) and purpose in life (Lindfors et al., 2006), where women score higher. These differences by gender have also been found in emerging adults, with emerging adult women scoring higher in both personal growth and purpose in life than emerging adult men (Matud et al., 2022).

With regard to the social determinants of well-being, these appear to be of particular importance during EA due to the role transitions that people experience as new adults, their still-developing views of the world, society and personal identity. Special attention needs to be drawled to their new social role acquisition as adults belonging to a society. Social well-being factors proposed by Keyes (Keyes, 1998) are a reflection of the social challenges that adults face, these are comprised in classic sociological theory and are reiterated in social psychological conceptions of self in society. As these imply new challenges for emerging adults, learning processes and social experiences underly the development of these factors. The results of Keyes validation study (Keyes, 1998) only found small correlations between the social well-being dimensions. The dimensions, just as the psychological dimensions, also revealed to be related with age, as some decrease linearly, others increase linearly, and others increase but at a decelerating rate. These results suggest that the resources, skills and experience acquired through education, as well as through ageing processes, are crucial for meeting the challenges of social life.

Promoting the development of social well-being dimensions could also entail emotional well-being enhancement, as social well-being dimensions have been found to influence emotional well-being. Particularly, social inclusion has also been found to have a strong predictive effect on youth life satisfaction and positive emotions (Arslan et al., 2020, 2023). Young people with a high sense of social inclusion are more likely to experience positive emotions as happiness and so, higher life satisfaction (Arslan et al., 2023). Moreover, perceived social inclusion might have an effect on the sense of social contribution, as socially included individuals perceive themselves as a valuable part of a social group or environment (Arslan, 2021).

The need-to-belong theory (Baumeister et al., 2012) describes the relationship between social inclusion and mental health and well-being outcomes. This theory highlights that individuals have a fundamental need to belong to a group or community, which entails forming and maintaining positive and meaningful social relationships. Consequently, social inclusion and positive relations not only share their positive effect on well-being but also appear to be dimensions that interact between them.

In the research of a model intended for its application in educational well-being promotion in such a significantly different population as emerging adults, both the previously described underlying learning mechanisms and component interactions need to be considered. Conclusions

The comprehensive review of the well-being theories provides an opportunity for the development of a model that is useful for the promotion of wellbeing, more specifically in emerging adult population. As the promotion of wellbeing entails potentiating the factors that help people positive mental health, the use of the dual-model perspective has enabled the identification of such positive aspects that can be enhanced to achieve emerging adults flourishing. These resulting aspects compel emotional factors (positive emotions and life satisfaction), psychological factors (purpose or meaning in life; relatedness or positive relations; autonomy; competence, environmental mastery or achievements; engagement; personal growth; and self-acceptance) and social factors (social integration, social contribution, social coherence, social actualization and social acceptance).

Furthermore, this review provides guidance on the design of a theoretical model suitable for the EA population that can be empirically tested. This study presents five hypothesized models useful for further studying well-being promotion. In order to disclose the best fitting configuration for the model, future research is highly encouraged to investigate the model running both confirmatory factorial analysis and exploratory structural equation modelling. Using ESEM is expected to be particularly beneficial for researching such a culturally bound construct as well-being. For this purpose, follow-up studies with emerging adults will collect quantitative data of the 14 included dimensions, as well as the three factors (emotional, psychological and social well-being) and a general well-being/mental health factor. Moreover, this review proposes a series of interactions between dimensions that are also open for future investigation through the use of predictive and correlational analysis.

Further study must consider providing meaning to the model through emerging adults population particular characteristics and experiences. For this purpose, another direction for the future research is conducting qualitative research on constructs such as life satisfaction, personal experiences and mediating cognitive processes as goals establishing. Acquiring better knowledge of emerging adults’ life interpretations and challenges would provide meaning to the theory. The elaboration of a well-being model in EA, both through confirmatory factor analysis and qualitative data, could entail the better understanding of such a complex and multifaceted issue.

Research for well-being educational promotion would greatly benefit from having a model that is useful for this particular life stage as it will allow the design of more accurate and effective pedagogical initiatives for higher education. The pedagogical implications for higher education institutions essentially entail the value of the theoretical model for educational interventions. Rather than focusing on mental illness (i.e.,: preventative interventions designed around risk factors), mental health promotion initiatives are strength-based learning processes and may be included in the overall conception of higher education. As the model is expected to be practical for mental health enhancement, longitudinal studies that observe the impact of educational interventions for well-being promotion could benefit from using the model to guide its design and explore the later potential improvements in the well-being of students. The possibilities to promote well-being factors through pedagogical actions in higher education include curricula design, didactic strategies, academic experiences and academic environment. Higher education, education professionals and educational environments can purposefully promote well-being by considering aspects such as facilitating opportunities to experience positive emotions, exercise gratitude, develop psychological functioning factors and ability to establish and adjust goals, build positive relations, feel a sense of social coherence, actualization, acceptance, inclusion and participation.

Providing a broader perspective through an integrative model helps stablish educational goals for wellbeing promotion in EA population. Wellbeing factors can be particularly targeted by educational promotion, as they change throughout life, can be developed and depend on the learning of skills. This conclusion is significant for promoting mental health, as higher education can play a crucial role in fostering the acquisition of knowledge and skills, as well as providing opportunities to experience and develop all the components of well-being. Therefore, higher education institutions must design impactful learning and training opportunities for emerging adults’ wellbeing, as they are the primary agents in youth development.
