Mycobacterium smegmatis MfpC is a GEF that regulates mfpA translationally to alter the fluoroquinolone efficacy
Authors: Yu Huang, Qinglei Shen, Hongxiang Xu, Lingxi Huang, Shasha Xiang, Peibo Li, Lin Fan, Jianping Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, remains a serious threat to global public health. Fluoroquinolones (FQs) are effective against M. tuberculosis; however, resistant strains have limited their efficacy. Mycobacterium fluoroquinolone resistance protein A (MfpA) confers intrinsic resistance to FQs; however, its regulatory mechanisms remain largely unknown. Using M. smegmatis as a model, we investigated whether MfpC is necessary for FQ susceptibility. MfpC mutants were sensitive to moxifloxacin, indicating that MfpC is involved in FQ susceptibility. By testing the mfpC inactivation phenotype in different mutants and using mycobacterial protein fragment complementation, we demonstrated that the function of MfpC depends on its interactions with MfpB. Guanine nucleotide exchange assays and site-directed mutagenesis confirmed that MfpC acts as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor to regulate MfpB. We propose that MfpB influences MfpA at the translational level. In summary, we reveal the role of MfpC in regulating the function of MfpA in FQ resistance.Subject terms: Bacterial genetics, Bacterial transcription, Gene regulation, Antibiotics

Introduction

Tuberculosis, caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, remains a leading cause of death1. According to the World Health Organization, in 2022, approximately 1.3 million people died of tuberculosis2. Novel compounds against M. tuberculosis have been intensively explored3. Fluoroquinolones (FQs) targeting DNA gyrase are key to the treatment of multidrug-resistant M. tuberculosis infections4, however, strains resistant to FQs are problematic5. Most intrinsic FQ resistance in Mycobacteria can be attributed to mutations in the quinolone resistance determining regions of DNA gyrase, which decrease its affinity for FQs5, the remainder is caused by intracellular antibiotic accumulation via reduced permeability and increased antibiotic efflux6,7, and a specific mechanism mediated by Mycobacterium fluoroquinolone resistance protein A (MfpA)8.

MfpA is a pentapeptide repeat protein (PRP) with an unclear origin, evolution, and mechanism of action. Other bacterial PRPs, including Qnr from Klebsiella pneumoniae9, AlbG from Xanthomonas albilineans10, and McbG from E.coli have been documented to protect DNA gyrase from antibiotics11. PRPs consist of a repeat motif [S,T,A,V][D,N][L,F][S,T,R][G] that folds to mimic double-stranded DNA11. MfpA was proposed as a DNA mimic that binds to the DNA gate of gyrase, protects gyrase from FQs, and inhibits its supercoiling activity12,13. However, the mechanism underlying MfpA regulation remains largely unknown.

MfpA is preceded by a highly conserved gene locus (conservon)14 in the genome, suggesting that it likely plays a role in the regulation of MfpA. This conservon is also observed in the genome of Streptomyces, indicating a conserved role in Actinobacteria15. There are four conservon proteins: a Ras-like GTPase protein (MfpB), a protein with an unknown DUF742 domain (MfpC), a roadblock/LC7 family protein (MfpD), and a membrane protein with a predicted histidine kinase domain (MfpE)14. This conservon has been proposed to encode a novel signal transduction system, similar to the eukaryotic small G protein-coupled regulatory system14,15.

MfpB interacts directly with MfpA and influences its interaction with DNA gyrase16. MfpB is a small GTPase, and only the GTP form of MfpB affects MfpA, consistent with the hypothesis. CvnD9, a homolog of MfpB, exhibits GTPase activity and may function as a small GTPase15. MfpD is involved in the regulation of MfpA by acting as a GTPase-activating protein (GAP) of MfpB17, consistent with the hypothesis that the mode of mfp conservon regulation is similar to the regulation of small G proteins in eukaryotes. Additionally, transcriptomic analysis of the Streptomyces coelicolor conservon demonstrated that the conservon encodes signaling systems similar to those of eukaryotic G protein-coupled receptors18.

We characterized MfpC and established it as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) of MfpB. Using M. smegmatis as a surrogate model, we found that MfpC is required for FQ susceptibility. Epistasis and mycobacterial protein fragment complementation (M-PFC) assays demonstrated that the interaction between MfpB and MfpC plays a vital role in MfpA-mediated FQ resistance. Further biochemical and genetic experiments indicated that MfpC acts as a novel GEF for MfpB. Finally, we confirmed the effect of MfpB on MfpA expression at the translational level. Taken together, these results indicate that MfpC is a GEF of MfpB, that translationally controls mfpA expression.

Results

MfpC is located in an operon that is highly conserved among Mycobacterium

To determine whether the mfp operon is clinically relevant, we collected the genomic sequences of clinical FQ resistance-related M. tuberculosis isolates from the National Center for Biotechnology Information. After excluding mutations in DNA gyrase, we identified 1731 strains harboring nonsynonymous single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the mfp operon, including seven types of SNP in mfpC (Table S1). MfpC is an uncharacterized protein with an unknown function. Domain analysis showed that MfpC is a member of the DUF742 protein family, with a typical DNA-binding helix-turn-helix (8–121 residues). Multiple sequence alignment of MfpC proteins from the M. tuberculosis complex, as well as from other mycobacteria, showed that MfpC is conserved among mycobacteria (Fig. S1a). M. smegmatis MSMEG_1639 is a homolog of M. tuberculosis MfpC, encoded by Rv3363c, with 66% amino acid sequence similarity. All orthologs of mycobacterial MfpC are located within the mfp operon (Fig. S1b). M. smegmatis, a fast-growing Mycobacterium, was used as a surrogate to characterize mycobacterial MfpC to satisfy the biosafety requirements in subsequent experiments.

MfpC is involved in mycobacterial FQ susceptibility

To test the function of MfpC in vitro, we constructed ΔmfpC by deleting the region containing residues 10–111 in MfpC (MfpC has 121 residues; Fig. S2a). ΔmfpC showed no growth defects (Fig. 1a). To study the role of MfpC in FQ susceptibility, we compared the growth of these strains in the presence of moxifloxacin. As shown in Fig. 1b and c, ΔmfpC was sensitive to moxifloxacin in both liquid and solid media. To confirm that MfpC was responsible for the observed difference in FQ susceptibility, we complemented the ΔmfpC mutant with another copy of mfpC under its native promoter. However, the susceptibility to FQ was not restored to wild-type levels upon complementation with mfpC (Fig. 1d). Interestingly, even with higher expression levels (due to the higher copy number and strong promoter of pMV-261), complemented ΔmfpC strains were unable to rescue the phenotype. As shown in Fig. 1e, the same phenotype was observed in the complemented ΔmfpC2 (deletion of amino acids 10–81; Fig. S2a).

Therefore, we speculated that susceptibility to FQs might be caused by the polar effects of mfpC deletion. Hence, a 300 bp sequence upstream of mfpB, which includes the coding region of mfpC, was cloned into the promoterless lacZ reporter plasmid peJ41419, giving rise to peJ414PrmfpB. The β-galactosidase assay was performed during the log phase of M. smegmatis. The peJ414 vector was used as a negative control. The weak constitutive rel promoter20, was used as a positive control. The Miller units of peJ414PrmfpB were nearly two-fold higher than those of the rel promoter (Fig. 1f), suggesting a strong promoter in the coding sequence of mfpC. In addition, deletion of mfpC resulted in a considerable reduction in mfpB and mfpA transcripts (Fig. 1g). This further supported the hypothesis that polar effects play a major role in the ΔmfpC phenotype.

Next, to exclude the polar effect due to the deletion of mfpC, we constructed ΔmfpCD2- by replacing D2 with a stop codon in the native chromosomal site. The growth of ΔmfpCD2- was similar to that of M. smegmatis in drug-free media (Fig. S3a). As shown in Fig. 1h, ΔmfpCD2- was also FQ-sensitive, and ΔmfpCD2-::mfpC reversed sensitivity to wild-type levels, confirming that the function of MfpC can be studied using ΔmfpCD2-. Moreover, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of FQs in ΔmfpCD2- decreased, whereas the MIC of other antibiotic types were similar, indicating that the phenotype of FQs is specific (Fig. S4). Taken together, these results confirmed that MfpC plays a critical role in mycobacterial FQ susceptibility.

MfpC acts via the same pathways as MfpB by interacting directly with MfpB

Because the mfp operon genes are cotranscribed and ΔmfpCD2- is sensitive to FQs, we sought to study the effect of MfpC on MfpA-mediated FQ resistance in Mycobacterium. Previous studies have shown that MfpD, a GAP of MfpB, is involved in MfpA-mediated FQ resistance17. Based on these data, we attempted to identify the MfpC targets. We combined ΔmfpCD2- with other mfp mutants and quantified colony-forming units in a medium supplemented with moxifloxacin. Firstly, ΔmfpD was less susceptible to FQs, which is consistent with the results of previous studies. The ΔmfpDΔmfpCD2- mutant was more sensitive than the single mutant ΔmfpD, indicating that mfpD and mfpC comprise independent and additive regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 2a). Additionally, ΔmfpA was hypersensitive to FQs, and the growth of ΔmfpAΔmfpCD2- in media containing moxifloxacin was indistinguishable from that of ΔmfpA (Fig. 2b), implying that MfpC acts upstream of MfpA and affects the susceptibility to FQs. Importantly, ΔmfpB was more sensitive than ΔmfpCD2-, and ΔmfpBΔmfpCD2- had the same phenotype as ΔmfpB (Fig. 2c), indicating that the effect of mfpC was only significant in the presence of mfpB and that MfpC acts via the same pathway as MfpB.

Because epistasis exists between mfpC, mfpB, and mfpA, we explored the possibility of MfpC-MfpA and MfpC-MfpB protein-protein interactions using the mycobacterial protein fragment complementation (M-PFC) assay21. The mfpC-encoding open reading frame was cloned into integrative plasmids pUAB400, MfpB, and MfpA, which were expressed in the episomal plasmid pUAB300. M. smegmatis harboring the plasmid pairs was cultured on 7H10/kan/hyg medium in the absence or presence of trimethoprim (TRIM). Cells expressing MfpC and MfpA displayed growth defects in the presence of TRIM (Fig. 2d), whereas cells expressing both MfpC and MfpB showed clear growth under identical conditions (Fig. 2e), suggesting an MfpC-MfpB interaction. Importantly, cells containing empty vectors also showed growth defects on 7H10/TRIM plates, whereas all strains grew well on 7H10 plates lacking TRIM. Collectively, the interaction between MfpC and MfpA did not underlie the phenotype of ΔmfpCD2-. Furthermore, these observations the hypothesis that MfpC acts via the same pathway as MfpB by interacting directly with MfpB.

MfpC has MfpB GEF activity

In eukaryotes, the GDP-GTP cycle of Ras-like GTPases depends on two types of regulators: GEFs, which facilitate the exchange of GDP for GTP and GAPs, which stimulate low intrinsic GTPase activity22. MfpD is a GAP of MfpB; however, the GEF of MfpB remains unknown. Moreover, phylogenomic analysis revealed that mfpC had nearly the same genomic distribution as mfpB and mfpD (Fig. 3a). The co-occurrence of mfpC with mfpB and mfpD suggests a functional association between these proteins, and we speculated that MfpC might act as a GEF for MfpB.

To confirm this hypothesis, we monitored the GDP to GTP exchange by observing the fluorescence of tyrosine when the nucleotide-bound state of G proteins changes from GDP to GTP. There was no tryptophan in Ras, but MfpB contained two conserved tryptophan residues located in the switch II domain (Fig. 3b). Preliminary fluorescence measurements of MfpB demonstrated that the fluorescence decreased by 6% upon GDP/GTP exchange (Fig. 3c). To amplify the alteration in fluorescence values, we designed the MfpB (F84W) mutation (equivalent to L56W in Ras) and the MfpB (F92W) mutation (equivalent to Y64W in Ras) according to a previous study23. The fluorescence decreased by approximately 18% for MfpB (F92W) and 8% for MfpB (F84W) (Fig. 3c). Therefore, MfpB (F92W) is capable of monitoring GDP/GTP exchange. In this experiment, MfpB (F92W) was preloaded with GDP before MfpC or negative control (bovine serum albumin; BSA) was added and the exchange reaction was initiated by adding a 10-fold excess of GTP. Remarkably, MfpC stimulated nucleotide exchange via MfpB (F92W). In addition, BSA did not affect the nucleotide exchange by MfpB (F92W), and the intrinsic fluorescence of MfpC was extremely stable (Fig. 3d). Thus, the GEF assays supported the hypothesis that MfpC possesses MfpB GEF activity.

To further demonstrate that MfpC has MfpB GEF activity, we monitored GDP to GTP exchange by MfpB using N-methylanthraniloyl (MANT)-labeled GDP. As shown in Fig. 3e, the negative control (BSA) did not affect nucleotide exchange by MfpB. In contrast, MfpC stimulates nucleotide exchange in a concentration-dependent manner. In summary, both GEF assays indicated that MfpC possesses MfpB GEF activity.

MfpC function as a GEF of MfpB in vivo

Analysis of mutants locked in the GTP-bound state has provided insights into the in vivo roles of G proteins in eukaryotic cells. To verify these biochemical findings, we used a similar strategy to confirm the role of GEF in MfpC, and constructed MfpB mutants with likely similar effects on GEF binding and hydrolysis. The MfpB (G23V) mutant, corresponding to RasG12V (Fig. 3b), has been demonstrated to be permanently in the GTP-bound form because of a considerable reduction in GTPase activity24. The MfpB (T27/28N) mutant was designed based on the assumption that its properties are similar to those of RasS17N (Fig. 3b), which binds tightly to GEF25. G21V has similar locations to RasG12V, the effects of which are discussed in the following experiments. First, we attempted to quantify the strength of the MfpC-MfpB mutant interactions using M-PFC assays. Cells containing MfpC/MfpB (T27/28N) displayed better growth than those containing MfpC/MfpB on TRIM plates, demonstrating that MfpC has a stronger interaction with MfpB (T27/28N), which is a characteristic of GEF. Surprisingly, clones harboring MfpC/MfpB (G21V) or the empty vector showed similar growth on TRIM plates, indicating a loss of interaction between MfpB (G21V) and MfpC (Fig. 4a). To verify these observations, we measured the binding affinities of the MfpB and MfpB mutations to MfpC using surface plasmon resonance. As shown in Fig. 4b, MfpB (T27/28N) bound to MfpC with a higher affinity (~10-fold) than to MfpB, whereas MfpB showed a higher affinity (~40-fold) than MfpB (G21V).

Next, we combined ΔmfpCD2- with mfpBG21V or mfpBG23V and measured their growth in the presence of moxifloxacin. MfpBG21V and mfpBG23V did not affect the growth of the strains in an antibiotic-free medium (Figure S3b and S3c). The growth of ΔmfpCD2-mfpBG21V in a medium containing moxifloxacin was indistinguishable from that of mfpBG21V (Fig. 4c), confirming that the function of MfpC depends on its interaction with MfpB in vitro. In contrast, ΔmfpCD2-mfpBG23V was more sensitive than mfpBG23V (Fig. 4e), indicating that the GTPase activity of MfpB is dispensable for the function of MfpC. Together with the biochemical data, we confirmed that MfpC is the GEF for MfpB in vivo.

The effect of MfpB on MfpA occurs at translation level

The initial assumption was that GTP-MfpB interacts with MfpA and may help MfpA protect DNA gyrase from FQs in vivo16, but biochemical results suggest that MfpB has a negligible effect on MfpA in vitro12. To reconcile this paradox, we propose two working hypotheses. One hypothesis is that the effect of MfpB on MfpA is post-translational, such as an allosteric effect or post-translational modifications, and the contradictory results are caused by different conditions in vivo and in vitro. As MfpB does not influence the transcription level of mfpA16, an alternative hypothesis is that MfpB influences the translation level of mfpA.

To verify the first hypothesis, we first generated ΔmfpA::hsp60mfpA by deleting the coding region of mfpA and integrating another copy of mfpA under the hsp60 promoter, and constructed mutants in ΔmfpA::hsp60mfpA to test their growth in the presence of moxifloxacin. As shown in Fig. 5a, ΔmfpA::hsp60mfpA was more resistant than M. smegmatis. The reason for this phenomenon may be that the hsp60 promoter is stronger than the native mfpA promoter (Fig. S5). If MfpB regulates MfpA at the post-translational level, the same mutant of the wild-type in ΔmfpA::hsp60mfpA will yield similar results. However, regardless of the mutant constructed (Fig. 5a), it did not change the sensitivity of ΔmfpA::hsp60mfpA to FQs. Furthermore, no significant differences were observed in the growth of these strains (Fig. S3d). These results rule out the possibility of the first hypothesis.

To confirm the second hypothesis, mfpA was replaced with lacZ to construct ΔmfpA::lacZ (Fig. S2b), which was used to characterize the MfpA translation level. The lacZ RNA levels were not affected by the various mutants (Fig. 5b), ruling out the possibility that the effects of MfpB were due to transcriptional regulation. As shown in Fig. 5c, compared with ΔmfpA::lacZ, ΔmfpCD2-ΔmfpA::lacZ and ΔmfpBΔmfpA::lacZ exhibited lower β-galactosidase activity, whereas ΔmfpDΔmfpA::lacZ and mfpBG23VΔmfpA::lacZ showed higher activity. As the results of the β-galactosidase experiment were similar to those of the above-mentioned antibiotic experiments, we concluded that MfpB regulates mfpA expression at the translational level.

The function of MfpC is conserved in Mycobacterium

Given that the mfp operon is widely conserved in Mycobacterium, we investigated whether the function of MfpC is conserved in M. tuberculosis. We generated a ΔmfpE-mfpA strain (ΔEA; Fig. S2c) by deleting the MsmfpE-mfpA coding region from its chromosomal location to eliminate the possible effect of MsMfp protein regulation. Integrative plasmids containing MtbMfpE-MfpA and MtbMfp mutants, based on residue conservation (Fig. 3b), were transformed into ΔEA to confirm the effects of MtbMfp mutations. Using the same assay, we observed that MtbMfp mutants behaved similarly to the MsMfp mutants (Fig. 6a). In addition, a guanine nucleotide exchange assay showed that MtbMfpC possessed MtbMfpB GEF activity (Fig. 6b). Integrative plasmids with MtbMfpE-MfpB-LacZ (MtbMfpA was replaced with LacZ) were used to characterize MtbMfpA translation levels. As shown in Fig. 6c, mutations in MtbMfp did not result in differential lacZ transcription. Moreover, the β-galactosidase results indicated that MtbMfpB also regulates MtbmfpA expression at the translational level (Fig. 6d). These data demonstrate that the function of MfpC is conserved in Mycobacterium.

Discussion

FQs play a pivotal role in controlling bacterial infections, including those caused by M. tuberculosis. Protection of DNA gyrase by MfpA contributes to the high intrinsic FQ resistance in mycobacteria. Although the mechanism of action of MfpA has become clear in recent years, its regulation by the mfp operon is poorly understood. Moreover, we identified approximately 1700 FQ resistance-related clinical strains containing SNPs in the mfp operon. Therefore, it is important to understand the mechanisms underlying Mfp-mediated FQ resistance by Mfp proteins. Here, we present the molecular details of MfpC and its role in FQ susceptibility.

When MfpC was inactivated by introducing a stop codon at D2, drug susceptibility increased, and complementation with the integrated vector restored the phenotype of ΔmfpCD2- (Fig. 1). Thus, we hypothesized that MfpC is involved in FQ susceptibility. Previous studies have established that MfpB and MfpD can affect FQ susceptibility16,17, therefore, we explored their relationship using genetic analyses and M-PFC assays. As shown in Fig. 2, MfpC interacts with MfpB and is involved in the MfpA-dependent pathway. Regulation of MfpA is controlled by the MfpB GTPase cycle16, Although it has been established that MfpD functions as a GAP for MfpB17, proteins that function as GEFs for MfpB have not been identified. Given that MfpC participates in MfpB-mediated signaling and that MfpC co-occurs with MfpB and MfpD, we hypothesized that MfpC is a GEF of MfpB. One piece of evidence supporting this hypothesis is that the addition of MfpC stimulates nucleotide exchange with MfpB (Fig. 3). Second, the effects of MfpB mutants and MfpC are similar to those of Ras proteins and GEFs in eukaryotes (Fig. 4). Moreover, previous studies have reported conflicting evidence concerning the effects of MfpB on MfpA12,16. After ruling out the possibility that the effect of MfpB on MfpA occurs after protein translation, to reconcile this paradox we propose that MfpB influences MfpA at the translational level (Fig. 5). Finally, we confirmed that the function of MfpC is conserved in Mycobacterium (Fig. 6). Together with previous observations, our findings provide a working model for explaining the regulation of MfpA in mycobacteria (Fig. 7). In this model, MfpC, which functions as a GEF, promotes MfpB-GTP accumulation, resulting in increased MfpA translation, which causes FQ resistance in Mycobacterium. MfpD, which functions as a GAP, showed the opposite effect.

Proposed model for MfpC in the regulation of MfpA-mediated FQ resistance.

MfpC is a GEF of MfpB, which accelerates the exchange reaction of MfpB, leading to GTP-MfpB accumulation due to the higher cellular concentration of GTP compared to GDP. GTP-MfpB activates unknown effector(s) to increase the translation level of MfpA, resulting in FQ dissociation from the DNA-DNA gyrase-fluoroquinolone ternary complex and FQ resistance in Mycobacterium.

GTP-binding proteins are widely distributed molecular switches in prokaryotes that are involved in the regulation of cellular processes26. In prokaryotes, small GTPases belonging to the TRAFAC class of GTP-binding proteins play vital roles in regulating important functions, including cell polarity, development, and antibiotic resistance16,27,28. In general, small GTPases are regulated by GEFs and GAPs, which affect nucleotide exchange and GTPase activity, respectively22,29. Regulation is common in eukaryotic signaling pathways but is unusual in prokaryotes. The most well-studied bacterial reports indicate that the Ras-like GTPase MglA in Myxococcus xanthus is regulated by the GAP protein MglB and the GEF protein complex RomR-RomX30,31. To the best of our knowledge, MfpC is the first GEF identified in Mycobacterium that regulates its cognate GTPase, MfpB, and participates in FQ susceptibility.

We complemented the G protein-mediated signaling model used to control mfpA expression; however, two questions remain unanswered. For example, which protein is the effector of MfpB? If MfpA is an effector protein, when we replace MfpA with LacZ, a similar effect will not occur; however, we obtained the opposite results. Therefore, we assume that either MfpC may be its effector protein because it contains a predicted DNA-binding domain, or that some proteins are yet unidentified. Transcriptome, proteome, and ChIP-seq analyses of each mfp mutant will help answer this question. Next, we investigated the GEF function of MfpC. Although we demonstrated that MfpC may use a pattern similar to that of GEFs in eukaryotes, the crystal structure of the MfpC/MfpB complex is required to explore its mechanism of action.

More importantly, mutations in MfpA have not been clinically reported in FQ-resistant strains because overexpression of mfpA confers high-level FQ resistance in Mycobacterium. However, when mutations in DNA gyrase were removed, there were approximately 1700 FQ resistance-related strains harboring SNPs in the mfp operon (Table S1), indicating that Mfp-dependent FQ-resistant strains may be present in clinical settings. Overexpression of mfpA in strains with the GyrA D94G mutation also increases ciprofloxacin resistance32, therefore, it is worthwhile exploring the phenotype of mfp mutations in DNA gyrase mutants. Thus, Mfp proteins may be potential targets for novel compounds against FQ-resistant M. tuberculosis.

In conclusion, our data define the DUF742 protein MfpC as a GEF involved in FQ susceptibility by increasing MfpA at the translational level. These results provide new insights into MfpA regulation and can inform the search for new compounds against Mfp proteins to control the clinical FQ resistance caused by mfp mutations.

Methods

Bacterial strains, plasmid, primer and growth conditions

E. coli DH5α was used for cloning, and BL21 for protein purification. M. smegmatis and mutants were grown in 7H9 medium containing 0.2% glycerol and 0.05% Tween 80 or 7H10 supplemented with 0.5%. Whenever required, kanamycin (50 μg/mL for E. coli, 25 μg/mL for M. smegmatis) and hygromycin (75 μg/mL for M. smegmatis, 150 μg/mL for E. coli) were added in the medium. Plasmids, strains, and primers are described in Table S2.

Construction of strains and plasmids

Gene deletion and point mutants were constructed using CRISPR-Cas12a (Cpf1)-assisted recombination33. Briefly, two plasmids were used in this system. pJV53-Cpf1 was transformed into M. smegmatis to expression recombinase and Cpf1 protein. Then, two complementary oligonucleotides containing the target adjacent to 5’-YTN-3’ (PAM motif) were synthesized, annealed to produce a protospacer cassette with Hind III and Bpm I overhangs at the 3’ and 5’ ends, and then ligated into pCR-Hyg plasmid. For deletion mutants, homologous fragments including 500 bp upstream and downstream of the target gene were amplified by using overlap PCR. For point mutans, for example, mfpBG21V, in addition to the target mutated sequence, the homologous fragments also contain synonymous mutations which usually mutate the T in the PAM motif, allowing point mutants could escape screen by CRISPR-Cas12a. For ΔmfpA::lacZ, crRNA expression plasmid was designed to target mfpA, the lacZ gene was amplified from peJ414 using primers with homology region of mfpA to generate homologous fragments. Homologous fragments and crRNA expression plasmid were transformed into M. smegmatis harboring pJV53-Cpf1. After the recovery, cells were plated on 7H10 agar supplemented with hygromycin, kanamycin, and anhydrotetracycline (induces Cpf1) to select transformants. For ΔmfpCD2- related double mutants (for example, ΔmfpCD2-mfpBG21V), ΔmfpCD2- was used as background strain and the same method was used to construct the second mutants. MfpC was amplified from genomic DNA with primers bearing the restriction site, and cloned in pMV-361 which integrates at the phage L5 attB site of M. smegmatis for complementing, or pMV-261 to overexpression. DNA sequence containing 500 bp upstream of MtbMfpE (retain its native promoter) to MfpA was amplified from M. tuberculosis H37Rv and cloned into pMV-361 to yield pMV-361-MtbMfpE-MfpA via Gibson assembly. Then pMV-361-MtbMfpE-MfpA was used as a template to construct point mutation. Plasmids were transformed into ΔmfpE-mfpA to construct strains in Fig. 6a. 500 bp upstream of MtbMfpE to MfpB was amplified from pMV-361-MtbMfpE-MfpA and ligated with lacZ from peJ414 by overlap PCR. Overlapping fragments were cloned into pMV-361 to yield pMV-361-MtbMfpE-MfpB-LacZ via Gibson assembly. Then pMV-361-MtbMfpE-MfpB-LacZ was used to construct point mutation. Plasmids were transformed into ΔmfpE-mfpA to construct strains in Fig. 6c and d.

Antibiotic sensitivity assays

Wild type and mutant strains were grown to an OD600 of 0.8–1.0. Then, strains were tested for their susceptibility to moxifloxacin by spotting a 10-fold serial dilution on 7H10 plates supplemented with the indicated concentration of moxifloxacin. For antibiotic susceptibility in liquid media, inoculating 10-fold dilution desired strain in liquid media containing the indicated concentration of moxifloxacin, the cultures were incubated at 37 °C and OD600 was measured every 4 hours.

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was defined as the lowest concentration of antibiotic that inhibited the visible bacterial growth of strains after 48 hours incubation (OD600 <0.05).

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Strains were grown to log phase and collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm. RNA extraction was done according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Tiangen, China). cDNA was generated using a reverse transcript kit (Takara). Three replicates were set for each sample and cDNA were amplified using the Bio-Rad PTC-200 fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument. Gene expression was normalized to sigA (MSMEG_2758) and quantified using ΔΔCt method.

β-galactosidase assays

M. smegmatis containing lacZ on plasmids (pEJ414 vector, peJ414PrmfpB, and peJ414Prel) or on the chromosome was cultured in 7H9 to log phase. Then 100 μL suspension was mixed with 900 μL of Z-buffer (60 mM Na2HPO4, 40 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM MgSO4), 20 μL of 0.1%SDS and 40 μL of chloroform, and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min to lyse cells. 200 μL of 4 mg/mL ONPG was added as substrate at 37 °C. When all tubes become yellow, stop the reaction by adding 200 μL of 2.5 M Na2CO3. After centrifuging at 12000 rpm for 5 min, 200 μL of supernatant was collected, OD420 and OD550 values were measured. Then activity in Miller units was calculated by the following formula:

Miller Units:1000∗(OD420-1.75∗OD550)/(T∗V∗OD600)

OD600 represents the cell density in the cell suspension, T is reaction time (min). V is the volume of bacterial supernatant used in the assay (mL).

Mycobacterial protein fragment complement (M-PFC) assay

The interaction between proteins in vivo was confirmed using M-PFC21. MfpC was cloned in pUAB400 and transformed in M. smegmatis. Likewise, MfpA and MfpB were amplified from genomic DNA using primer pairs 300-AF/300-AR, and 300-BF/300-BR, respectively (Table S2), and cloned in pUAB300. Next, plasmids were transformed in M. smegmatis containing pUAB400-MfpC and selected on 7H10/kan/hyg plates. Positive clones were selected in the presence of TRIM to investigate protein-protein interactions. Plasmid pairs containing empty vector were used as negative control.

The purification of MfpB and MfpC

The purification of MfpB as described previously17. MfpC was expressed with N-terminal 6×His-SUMO tag on pET28a for purification in E.coli BL21 DE3. Cells were grown at 37 °C to OD600 = 0.6, then IPTG was added to a final concentration of 0.2 mM to induce protein expression at 18 °C. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 4000 g for 30 min at 4°C and resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH = 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Triton X-100). Resuspended cells were sonicated 200 times, each with a 3 s pulse and 5 s rest. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 6000 g for 30 min at 4 °C and loaded onto a nickel affinity column. After washing with 10 column volumes of washing buffer (25 mM HEPES, pH = 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 20 mM imidazole), proteins were eluted in elution buffer (washing buffer with 250 mM imidazole) and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Next, SUMO protease was added in proteins mixture to remove SUMO tag and dialyzed versus two changes of dialysis buffer (25 mM HEPES, pH=7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2,). The cleaved protein was passed over a new nickel affinity column and concentrated using Amicon Ultracel centrifugal filters (Millipore). Other mutation proteins and proteins of M. tuberculosis were also purified using the above methods.

Guanine nucleotide exchange assay

Exchange of GDP for GTP from MfpB was measured according to previous study23. Briefly, MfpB and mutations were preloaded with GDP for 600 s at room temperature in 25 mM HEPES, pH = 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2,1 mM DTT 2 mM EDTA, the reaction was stopped by addition of MgCl2 to a final concentration of 2 mM. Then, 5 μM GDP-MfpB and mutations were mixed with different concentration of MfpC or BSA according to experimental requirements. The exchange reaction was initiated by addition of GTP to a final concentration of 50 μM. The decrease in fluorescence intensity was measured by monitoring fluorescence of tyrosine of proteins with excitation at 280 nm and emission at 345 nm every 2 second for 1200 s.

Also, exchange of MANT-GDP (Sigma) for GTP from MfpB was measured as described with modifications34. Put simply, MfpB was preloaded with MANT-GDP for 90 min at 20 °C in MANT-GDP loading buffer (20 mM HEPES pH = 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT). After changing the buffer to exchange buffer (40 mM HEPES pH = 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT) by NAP-5, the loading MANT-GDP on MfpB was completed. MANT-GDP MfpB (final concentration, 10 μM) was preincubated with proteins according to experiments designed for 10 min at room temperature in exchange buffer. The reaction was initiated by addition of GTP to final concentration of 10 mM and the decrease in fluorescence intensity associated with the exchange MANT-GDP to GTP by MfpB was measured every 20 s for 1500 s. Excitation at 360 nm and emission at 460 nm were used to monitor the fluorescence of MANT-GDP.

SPR measurements

The binding kinetics of MfpC and MfpB proteins were determined by using surface plasmon resonance binding (SPR) assays (Nicoya Life-science, Waterloo, Canada). The running buffer for these experiments consisted of PBS, 0.05% Tween 20. Purified MfpC was coated directly on the COOH sensor chip by using a standard amine procedure. The analyte (MfpB proteins or BSA) was injected at a flow rate of 20 μL/min, and dissociation was monitored for 9 min. Dissociation constants (KD) were calculated with Tracedrawer software by fitting the data to a kinetic model.

In silico studies

The protein sequences were retrieved from Mycobrowser (https://mycobrowser.epfl.ch)35. The blastp of MfpC was searched with non-redundant protein sequences. For multiple sequence alignment, Espript web server was used to analyze motif and active site residue36.

To identify similar sequences with D (represents DUF742 domain of MfpC), R (represents Roadblock/LC7 domain of MfpD), and G (represents GTP-binding domain of MfpB), we retrieved all genes containing D, R or G domain and their bacterial classifications from Uniprot and removed redundant genomes at the species level37. Utilizing locus_tags are arranged in order on the genome, we identify and count the number of bacteria containing continuous DRG domains.

Statistics and reproducibility

All the data are representative of at least three independent experiments. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. The data were analyzed using Student’s T test or one-way ANOVA. Significance was depended on P values (***, P < 0.001; **, P < 0.01; *, P < 0.05; ns, not significant).
