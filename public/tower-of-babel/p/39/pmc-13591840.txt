One case of acute encephalopathy associated with 16p11.2 deletion and PRRT2 gene mutation
Authors: SiYu Shang, QiuHong Wang, JiaNing Wang, HuiFang Song, Jiao Zhang, XiaoYang Liu, Xin Gao, ChangHong Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground PRRT2 is associated with autosomal dominant paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia (PKD), benign familial infantile epilepsy (BFIE) and other diseases.ObjectiveTo explore the phenotypic spectrum associated with PRRT2 mutations, we analyzed a patient carrying a 16p11.2 deletion including PRRT2.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical and genetic characteristics of a patient with a 16p11.2 deletion containing PRRT2. A literature search was performed in CNKI, Wanfang, and PubMed from the establishment of the databases to December 2025, using the keywords "acute encephalopathy", "PRRT2", "paroxysmal non-kinesigenic dyskinesia", "ataxia", "copy number variation of chromosome 16", and "16p11.2 deletion", to identify case reports similar to the present case.ResultThis patient presented with infection-induced acute encephalopathy, acute-onset non-motor-induced movement disorders and ataxia phenotype. Genetic testing indicated a 16p11.2 deletion involving PRRT2, de novo. After immunotherapy and rehabilitation treatment, the patient achieved a favorable prognosis. Literature review identified only two complete international case reports similar to this case (specifically regarding ataxia), and genetic analysis showed that they were respectively a 16p11.2 deletion containing PRRT2 and a PRRT2 gene variation.ConclusionThe 16p11.2 deletion containing PRRT2 has been reported for the first time to present as an acute encephalopathy phenotype. Rare cases of paroxysmal non-motor-induced movement disorder (PNKD) and ataxia have also been reported. These findings underscore the importance of timely genetic testing and appropriate genetic counseling for patients presenting with unexplained acute encephalopathy and/or acute episodic non-motor-induced movement disorders, as well as those with ataxia symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05124-9.Keywords: PRRT2 gene, 16th chromosome copy number variation, Acute encephalopathy, Acute episodic movement disorder, Acute episodic ataxia

Proline-rich transmembrane protein 2 (PRRT2) is a synaptic protein highly expressed in the cerebellum, cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, and hippocampus, where it participates in the release of neurotransmitter vesicles. The encoding gene, PRRT2, is located on chromosome 16 (16p11.2) and consists of 4 exons [1].

The PRRT2 gene mutation exhibits phenotypic heterogeneity, which can manifest as PKD, BFIE, PKD with infantile convulsions (PKD/IC) syndrome, and hemiplegic migraine (HM) [2, 3]. Rarely, it has also been reported to present as PNKD and episodic ataxia (EA) [4, 5]. To date, no cases of acute encephalopathy associated with PRRT2 mutations have been reported. Here, we describe a pediatric patient with a 16p11.2 deletion involving PRRT2, confirmed by chromosomal microarray analysis of PRRT2, who presented with acute encephalopathy, PNKD, and ataxia. For patientwith acute encephalopathy, PNKD, and ataxia, the possibility of 16p11.2 deletion should be considered.

Clinical data

Patient, male, 3 years and 9 months old. Admitted to the hospital due to “fever for 5 days and episodic movement disorder for 1 day”. Five days before admission, the patient developed fever with the highest temperature reaching 40.2℃, accompanied by cough. Azithromycin was administered for 3 days, and the body temperature dropped to a low fever level. One day ago, the patient experienced 5 paroxysmal events during sleep. The manifestations were as follows: both eyes were fixed, both upper limbs were stiff, both lower limbs were weak, with clear consciousness, no cyanosis of the face or lips, and each episode lasted from several seconds to several tens of seconds (video 1). Presentation form 2: Intermittent high muscle tone in the limbs, each episode lasting from several seconds to several tens of seconds; during intervals, the patient has poor mental state. This is considered as paroxysmal non-motor-induced movement disorder. After admission, the patient still has paroxysmal movement disorders, manifested as being conscious, staring with both eyes, or deviating to the right with both eyes, head tilting backward, opisthotonus, lasting for about 5–10 s and can be relieved spontaneously. The episodes occurred while the patient was awake, with a frequency of approximately 10 times per day, and resolved after sleep. Two days later, the patient exhibited neuropsychiatric symptoms, including intermittent and paroxysmal crying, screaming, irritability, alternating with drowsiness. During the intervals, the patient showed indifferent responses, refused to eat, spoke less, occasionally made sounds but with unclear articulation, and had decreased coordination of the limbs and unstable head lifting. The patient had normal perinatal and growth development milestones. At the age of 2, he had a history of febrile seizure. The grandfather had episodes of unexplained loss of consciousness, but the details are unknown.

Physical examination: Weight 12 kg, head circumference 52 cm. The patient appeared obvious restlessness, emaciation, indifferent response, sparse spontaneous speech, poor visual pursuit, excessive sweating, head tilting and unstable sitting and standing. Horizontal nystagmus was observed. Pupils were bilaterally equal and round, with a brisk light reflex. The breathing sounds in both lungs are coarse, and rales can be heard; the muscle tension in the limbs was decreased, and the muscle strength test is uncooperative, though the limbs were able to resist some resistance. Bilateral knee and Achilles tendon reflexes were symmetrically elicited. The pathological signs were negative, and the meningeal irritation sign was also negative. The finger-to-nose test was unstable, and intention tremor was observed.

Auxiliary examination: Blood biochemistry, including liver and kidney function, myocardial enzymes, lactic acid, blood ammonia, etc. were generally within normal ranges. Blood cytokine IL-6 was normal. In terms of hematological findings: Nucleic acid assays yielded positive results for both Mycoplasma pneumoniae DNA and RNA, alongside a positive nasal viral nucleic acid test result. The number of white blood cells in the comprehensive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was 9.00 × 106/L. with normal biochemical parameters. The levels of CSF cytokines IL-6: 1575.00 pg/ml, IL-8: 524.39 pg/ml. The autoantibody profiles for blood and CSF autoimmune encephalitis, the central nervous system demyelinating antibody profile, and the MOG antibody were all negative, and the pathogen was negative. The electroencephalogram (EEG) showed a background rhythm that was mildly slow for the patient’s age. The parents reported that the episodes did not involve abnormal discharges (Fig. 1). Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated diffuse swelling of both cerebellar hemispheres, with shallow and narrow cerebral sulci and cisterns. There were heterogeneous T2WI and FLAIR signals in the brain parenchyma, patchy slightly hyperintense signals on DWI in the left cerebellar peduncle and cerebellar white matter, with corresponding hypointense signals on the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map. Slightly low ADC signals were also noted at the brainstem margin and right parietal cortex. The fourth ventricle was narrowed, while the supratentorial ventricles appeared mildly dilated. The cerebral sulci in both hemispheres were slightly deepened (Fig. 2).

Treatment and prognosis: After admission, the patient received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a dose of 2 g/kg as emergency treatment. Methylprednisolone sodium succinate was administered intravenously at a dose of 20 mg/kg every day for 4 days, 10 mg/kg every day for 3 days, and 5 mg/kg every day for 3 days. Acyclovir was administered intravenously at a dose of 10 mg/kg per day for antiviral treatment. Azithromycin was administered intravenously for anti-infection treatment. Intravenous infusion of mannitol was used to reduce intracranial pressure and sub-hypothermic brain protection was provided. Oral clonazepam and other symptomatic treatments were also given. One week after admission, the child’s irritability significantly improved. The patient was able to track visual stimuli with both eyes, speak in simple sentences, and articulate compound words such as “mommy” and “grandma”, though speech remained mildly dysarthric. After 12 days of hospitalization, the patient was able to express simple requests on his own. After 21 days of hospitalization, he was discharged. At discharge, he could laugh, recognize colors, express simple words on his own, and could execute simple instructions. When discharged from the hospital, the patient still had unstable head position and decreased muscle tone in all limbs. The muscle strength of the left upper limb was grade III+, the right upper limb was grade IV, the left lower limb was grade IV+, and the right lower limb was grade III+. There were no involuntary movements. Continuous rehabilitation training was provided outside the hospital. Regular follow-up examinations were conducted. After six months, the patient was completely cured with no motor disorders. He could run, jump, respond, and was the same as normal children of the same age. The muscle strength and tension of all four limbs had returned to normal (video 2, video 3). No obvious abnormalities were found in the brain MRI.

Genetic characteristics

Given the patient’s previous history of febrile seizures, family history, and unexplained encephalopathy, a genetic etiology was suspected. After obtaining the written informed consent from the patient’s guardian, whole exome sequencing (WES) and whole-genome copy number variation (CNV) detection of the family were performed. 3 ml of peripheral blood was collected from the patient and the parents using EDTA anticoagulation tubes, and DNA was extracted. The IDT The xGen Exome Research Panel v2.0 whole-exome capture chip was used to capture and sequence the DNA, with the target sequence sequencing coverage approximately reaching 99%. Finally, Sanger sequencing was used for verification. The whole genome CNV sequencing revealed a pathogenic heterozygous deletion of approximately 539.28 Kb at the position of chromosome 16 (chr16:29675049–30214327) [seq[GRCh37]del(16)(p11.2p11.2)|NC_000016.9:g.29675049_30214327del]. According to the 2020 ACMG grading guidelines, the patient was rated as pathogenic (1 A + 2 A+2G + 3B+5 A, a total score of 1.6), 1 A: containing protein-coding genes or known important functional components (0). 2 A: complete overlap with a known HI gene or HI genome region (1 point); 2G: the CNV overlapped with the benign CNV and contained other genomic content (0 score); 3B: the number of protein-coding genes contained or partially contained in this region was between 25 and 34 (0.45 points); 5 A: the CNV was Denovo mutation and the parents did not carry the mutation (0.15 points). In conclusion, this CNV is pathogenic. (chromosome copy number distribution map is shown in Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study presents the first reported case of a patient with a de novo 16p11.2 microdeletion involving the PRRT2 gene presenting with acute encephalopathy as the initial symptom. The patient presented with three distinct clinical phenotypes, including acute encephalopathy, acute episodic non-motor-induced movement disorders, and ataxia. The cranial MRI showed significant features, suggesting diffuse swelling of the bilateral cerebellar hemispheres, with patchy slightly high signals in the left cerebellar peduncle and white matter. After immunotherapy and rehabilitation treatment, the patient achieved a favorable prognosis. A 16p11.2 microdeletion is one of the most common causes of neurodevelopmental disorders, including motor/language delays, autism spectrum disorders, intellectual disabilities, congenital malformations and/or obesity [6]. These phenotypic features are likely attributable to haploinsufficiency of the 16p11.2 gene [7]. The 16p11.2 microdeletion encompasses PRRT2 and other protein-coding genes. Among them, the PRRT2 mutation can lead to PKD, PKD/IC, BFIE, HM and rare PNKD, EA, etc [2, 3]. The clinical phenotype observed in this patient is consistent with that associated with PRRT2 mutations. Therefore, within the context of the 16p11.2 microdeletion, the condition considered is one where there is a PRRT2 mutation genetic background, with acute encephalopathy as the initial symptom, along with clinical manifestations such as PNKD and ataxia. The occurrence of symptoms related to PRRT2 mutations is age-dependent, possibly due to the different expression of PRRT2 in the cortical and subcortical regions during brain development [1]. Epilepsy typically presents in infancy, whereas episodic ataxia, movement disorders and hemiplegic migraine are more commonly observed in adolescence or adulthood [2]. PRRT2-related disorders result from various mutation types, including heterozygous mutations, homozygous mutations, compound heterozygous mutations, as well as 16p11.2 deletion. Among them, heterozygous mutations are the common type of mutations, and c.649dupC; p.Arg217Profs*8 is a mutation hotspot [8]. A few patients with pure and compound heterozygous mutations exhibit severe phenotypes, such as developmental delay and intellectual disability [9].

Regarding the acute neurological symptoms in the context of PRRT2 gene mutations in this study have not been previously reported in the international literature. In identifying acute encephalopathy, it is essential to perform CSF analysis, infection-related pathogen examinations, and immunological encephalitis-related antibodies to differentiate from encephalitis [10, 11]. The CSF routine and biochemical tests of this patient were all normal, and the antibody profiles related to infection and immune encephalitis were all negative. Most importantly, MRI of the head showed that the lesion was located in the cerebellum, while the lesion was located in the basal ganglia or corpus callosum as reflected by non-mycoplasma-related encephalitis. Therefore, the diagnosis of encephalitis was basically not supported. For the PRRT2-related PNKD phenotype, there have been relatively few reports in the past [12, 13]. During the diagnosis and treatment, PNKD can sometimes be difficult to distinguish from epilepsy [14], and thus it may be misdiagnosed as epilepsy. EEG is the key to differentiating the two. For the patient in this study, no abnormal discharges were present during the seizure events, so the diagnosis of epilepsy was not supported.

This patient presented with three concurrent clinical manifestations: acute encephalopathy, PNKD, and ataxia. Based on this, we conducted a literature search using the keywords “acute encephalopathy”, “PRRT2”, “episodic non-motor-induced movement disorder”, “ataxia”, “16th chromosome copy number variation”, and “16p11.2 deletion” to conduct a search for the indexed literature in China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform, and PubMed since their databases were established until December 2025. No cases of PRRT2 mutations exhibiting all three phenotypes were identified. However, two cases presenting with symptoms partially similar to those of our patient (specifically ataxia) were found. A relevant case report from the international literature describes clinical and imaging findings partially similar to those observed in our patient [15].This case represents the first instance of acute cerebellar ataxia caused by PRRT2 gene mutation, characterized by hyperintense signals on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and corresponding hypointense signals on apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) mapping in the cerebellum. The patient is a 21-year-old adult female who developed ataxic gait following swimming, with clinical manifestations including dysarthria and ataxia. Brain MRI showed a DWI hyperintensity in the right cerebellar hemisphere with markedly reduced ADC values. The patient’s brother, who began experiencing episodic movement-induced movement disorders after adulthood, responded well to carbamazepine. Both the patient and the brother were found to have truncating mutations in the PRRT2 gene.

Another case report described a patient with recurrent ataxia (Episodic Ataxia, EA) caused by a deletion of 16p11.2 (including the PRRT2 gene) [5]. This patient, a 2-year-old girl, experienced three episodes of obvious ataxia and gait imbalance, each triggered by infection-related factors. During the seizure, this child exhibited decreased muscle tone. Comprehensive laboratory evaluations, including blood, biochemical, and metabolic tests, revealed no abnormalities, and both electroencephalogram and cranial magnetic resonance imaging were also normal. The genetic test report indicated a 16-chromosome heterozygous deletion including PRRT2 (chr16:29603655–30188484 (GRCh38)). At the age of one, this child had one episode of epilepsy and mild language delay. After taking carbamazepine, the ataxia improved compared to before. During the 3-month follow-up, there were no more seizures.

This study presents the first report of 16p11.2 deletion involving PRRT2 gene mutation presenting with acute encephalopathy, acute episodic non-motor-induced movement disorders, and acute ataxia in the context of infection. These findings expanded the clinical phenotype spectrum associated with 16p11.2 deletion, demonstrating that acute encephalopathy can also be linked to 16p11.2 deletion containing PRRT2 gene mutation. Notably, this phenotype demonstrated a favorable prognosis following immunotherapy and rehabilitation treatment.
