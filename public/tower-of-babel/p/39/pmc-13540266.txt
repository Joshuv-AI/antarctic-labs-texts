Observed Weight Gain in Patients With Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension Treated With Sotatercept
Authors: Rebecca A Greene, Darlene Tat, Allyson Sherman‐Roe, Tiffaney Cayton, Jacqueline Lopes, Alexander Jorrin, James Klinger, Christopher J Mullin, Kathryn Needle, William M Oldham, Crossandra Osgood, Navneet Singh, Corey E Ventetuolo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSotatercept, an activin signaling inhibitor for pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), has demonstrated significant improvements in clinical outcomes though its off‐target effects are still an area for discovery. Anecdotal increases in total body weight not attributable to fluid retention have been observed. Understanding the potential for weight gain and body composition changes is important as excessive weight gain in the PAH population is deleterious whereas increases in lean body mass may favorably affect exercise capacity. Therefore, we conducted a single‐center retrospective observational cohort study of adult Group 1 PAH patients seen at a Pulmonary Hypertension Association‐accredited Comprehensive Care Center treated with sotatercept for a minimum of 12 weeks. The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients who experienced clinically significant weight gain of ≥ 5% with sotatercept treatment. Secondary endpoints were the frequency of sotatercept dose adjustments required due to changes in body weight. Exploratory analyses were conducted to identify potential relationships between weight change and covariates of interest. Of 23 patients included, clinically significant weight gain was observed in 43.5% (n = 10). The median absolute change from baseline weight was 3.18 kg (Q1–Q3, −0.80–7.43 kg), and the median percent change was 4.43 (Q1–Q3, −0.78–9.01). Dosage adjustments based on dosing weight changes (increase or decrease) occurred at an incidence of 1.56 per patient‐year. These results suggest the importance of ongoing monitoring of body weight throughout sotatercept treatment and that weight gain is commonly associated with treatment, although not previously observed or evaluated in clinical trials to date.Keywords: activin signaling, body composition, pulmonary arterial hypertension, pulmonary vascular disease, sotatercept

Introduction

Sotatercept, an activin signaling inhibitor for pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), has demonstrated significant improvements in exercise capacity, hemodynamics, and other clinical outcomes in PAH patients [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Weight gain has not been reported as an adverse event in clinical trials to date, which may be a consequence of short trial durations. With the use of sotatercept in clinical practice, anecdotal increases in total body weight not attributable to fluid retention have been observed. Determining whether weight gain occurs with sotatercept use, to what magnitude, and in which patients would provide emerging real‐world evidence and valuable insights into a key component of the care of PAH patients.

Excess adiposity is associated with higher biventricular filling pressures, cardiac output demand, and worse functional status in Group 1 PAH patients [6]. Increased body mass index (BMI) is also associated with higher pulmonary arterial pressures and markers of left heart remodeling [7]. A recent meta‐analysis of clinical trials for approved PAH treatments found that higher BMI attenuated treatment response [8]. Conversely, it is plausible that sotatercept may cause weight gain through an increase in lean body mass as opposed to fat mass as the activin signaling pathway is a therapeutic target for both sarcopenia and obesity. Bimagrumab, a monoclonal antibody against activin type II receptors with mechanistic overlap with sotatercept increased lean mass by 3.6%–7% and decreased fat mass by 10%–21% in clinical trials [9, 10]. Understanding the potential for weight gain and body composition changes is important as excessive weight gain in the PAH population is deleterious whereas increases in lean body mass may favorably effect exercise capacity [8, 11].

As sotatercept uses weight‐based dosing, changes in weight may also play a role in adequate drug exposure. Sotatercept clinical trial protocols required measurement of body weight and recalibration of dose immediately prior to each injection [12]. While this is impractical in a postmarketing setting, dose changes may still be required throughout treatment necessitating ongoing monitoring of body weight.

The purpose of this study was to determine whether sotatercept is associated with clinically significant changes in body weight and to identify associated risk factors for weight change in patients with Group 1 PAH. We hypothesized that sotatercept patients experienced clinically significant weight gain during treatment.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a single‐center retrospective observational cohort study of patients seen at a Pulmonary Hypertension Association‐accredited Comprehensive Care Center (Brown University Health Center for Advanced Lung Care) between March 26, 2024 and April 2, 2026. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB 2383139); patient consent was not required.

Selection of Participants

Subjects were identified through a query of the electronic medical record (EMR) for sotatercept orders and were included if they received at least 12 weeks of sotatercept for Group 1 PAH during the study period, had a pre‐treatment (index) body weight available and at least 1 in‐person weight recorded after at least 12 weeks of therapy, and were at least 18 years of age. Patients receiving a glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonist were only included if they were on a stable dose throughout the study period. Patients were excluded if they were pregnant, incarcerated, if they initiated a GLP‐1 receptor agonist or sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) during the study period, discontinued a GLP‐1 agonist within 6 months before sotatercept initiation due to potential rebound weight gain, had advanced malignancy, uncontrolled thyroid disease, bulimia, or anorexia nervosa.

Exposure

At our center, sotatercept prescribing followed approved dosing if laboratory parameters (hemoglobin and platelets) were within acceptable ranges. Patients were initiated at 0.3 mg/kg given subcutaneously, followed by 0.7 mg/kg given subcutaneously every 21 days. Doses may have been reduced to 0.3 mg/kg or held for bleeding, erythrocytosis, or thrombocytopenia per standard practice and according to prescribing information. Dispensing specialty pharmacies were to notify our practice site if a patient reported a weight change of ±4.5 kg (10 pounds) to update the dosing weight used for sotatercept.

Outcome Measures

The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients who experienced clinically significant weight gain with sotatercept treatment. Weight gain was evaluated by comparing baseline (pre‐sotatercept) total body weight obtained immediately prior to the first dose to the second (final) weight assessment at the end of sotatercept therapy or the end of the study period. Follow‐up duration varied among subjects. A weight change of 5% or more from baseline was considered clinically significant, consistent with criteria used in studies evaluating medication‐induced weight gain in other therapeutic areas [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

Secondary endpoints were to describe the frequency of sotatercept dose adjustments required due to changes in body weight. Exploratory analyses using ridge regression were conducted to identify potential relationships between weight change and covariates of interest.

Covariates

Covariates necessary to describe the study cohort and those that may impact weight gain or loss were recorded, including menopausal status and type 2 diabetes. Common concomitant medications known to cause weight loss, including GLP‐1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors were reviewed for any recent dose changes and start/stop dates relative to sotatercept and either included or excluded per the above criteria. Information on concomitant use of prostacyclin therapy was collected, including baseline use, dose changes, and discontinuations throughout the study period. Information pertinent to volume status was also collected, including baseline diuretic use, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP). Any brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) values were converted to NT‐proBNP [19]. Risk scores (REVEAL Lite 2) were calculated at baseline and at the end of sotatercept treatment [20]. For laboratory data, values were included if obtained within 1 month before sotatercept initiation for baseline assessment and within 1 month prior to the end of the study period for follow‐up assessment.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous data were presented as medians with an interquartile range (Q1–Q3), while categorical data were presented as a simple count and percentage for the entire cohort. The outcome of the proportion of patients experiencing clinically significant weight gain was reported as a simple count and percentage of the overall cohort. Percent change from baseline and absolute change from baseline weight were reported using both mean and median values. The number of sotatercept dose adjustments made for changes in body weight were reported as incidence per patient‐year on sotatercept.

Two ridge regression models were used to examine various predictors on the primary outcome. Ridge regression was chosen as our primary analysis to handle collinearities and to reduce overfitting given the small sample size and correlated predictors. Our ridge regression models aimed to indicate the direction and approximate magnitude of association of covariates with the percent change in weight and the odds of weight gain over 5%, after shrinkage. For both models, the penalty parameter (lambda) corresponding to the minimum cross‐validated mean‐squared error was selected to optimize predictive performance. Covariates selected for inclusion in the model included age, female menopausal status, baseline BMI, medications associated with weight loss (GLP‐1 and SGLT2i), sotatercept duration of treatment, prostacyclin exposure during the study period, prostacyclin deescalations (step‐downs from parenteral to oral/inhaled), number of prostacyclin dose increases and decreases, any diuretic dose increases and decreases/interruptions. For categorical variables with more than two categories, we used the following as reference groups: male for menopausal status and no prostacyclin exposure to escalation for prostacyclin escalation groups. For binary categorical variables, such as prostacyclin and weight loss medication use, exposure was compared to nonexposure.

Results

Study Cohort

A total of 36 unique sotatercept patients were identified between March 26, 2024 and April 2, 2026. Of these, 13 patients were excluded (n = 5 were not followed at our center, n = 4 did not complete 12 weeks of treatment, n = 2 started a GLP‐1 or SGLT2i during the study period, n = 1 had advanced malignancy, n = 1 had uncontrolled thyroid disease despite treatment). The remaining 23 patients comprised the study cohort and were predominantly female (91.3%) with a median age of 58 years and a median baseline BMI of 28.6 kg/m2 (overweight) (Table 1). All patients had Group 1 PAH with the majority being functional class III (52.2%). There was high utilization of PAH therapies with 74% of the cohort on a prostacyclin, 91.3% on an endothelin receptor antagonist, and 96% on a phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitor or soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator. The median duration of sotatercept treatment was 35.9 weeks (Q1–Q3, 20.5–63.1).

There was high baseline utilization of diuretics (91.3%) with 56.5% of the cohort on a loop diuretic, 26.1% on both a loop diuretic and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA), and 8.7% on a loop diuretic, MRA, and thiazide‐like diuretic. During sotatercept treatment, 39% of patients required increased doses of diuretic, and 43% required decreased diuretic doses or treatment interruption. Median (Q1, Q3) baseline NT‐proBNP was 274 (70, 468) pg/mL, which decreased to 105 (56, 138) pg/mL after sotatercept. Estimated GFR slightly decreased to a median of 80 (58, 97) mL/min/1.73 m2 from a baseline of 92 (70, 102) mL/min/1.72 m2. Reveal Lite 2 risk scores were similar before [median 5 (5, 8)] and after sotatercept treatment 5 (4, 6). Of the patients on a prostacyclin at baseline, 58.8% down‐titrated their dose at least once since initiating sotatercept, while 17% increased their dose at least once. Of the patients on prostacyclin at baseline, 17% were able to deescalate from parenteral to oral or inhaled therapy, and no patient required escalation in prostacyclin modality.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome, defined as weight gain of ≥ 5% from baseline, was observed in 43.5% (n = 10) of sotatercept patients (Table 2). Some patients experienced excessive weight gain with 34.8% (n = 8) and 26.1% (n = 6) gaining ≥ 7.5% and ≥ 10%, respectively. Of the patients who had ≥ 5% weight gain, the mean absolute change from baseline weight was 9.96 kg [SD (standard deviation) 7.33 kg] and median was 8.44 kg (Q1–Q3, 5.68–10.91 kg). Of the overall cohort, the mean absolute change from baseline weight was 3.90 kg (SD 7.58 kg) and median was 3.18 kg (Q1–Q3, −0.80–7.43 kg). The mean percent change from baseline weight was 5.20 (SD 9.19) and median was 4.43 (Q1–Q3, −0.78–9.01). Three patients (13%) lost ≥ 5% of their baseline weight.

Secondary Outcomes

Dosage adjustments based on dosing weight changes (increase or decrease) occurred at an incidence of 1.56 per patient‐year. Dosage increases due to weight gain had an incidence of 1.11 per patient‐year of sotatercept. Dosage decreases due to weight loss occurred at a rate of 0.45 per patient‐year of sotatercept.

Exploratory Analyses

The first ridge regression model examined percent change in weight as a continuous outcome where coefficients were interpreted as shrunken estimates of association and were used primarily to assess the direction and relative magnitude of predictor contributions rather than statistical significance (Table 3). Diuretic dose decrease or interruption was associated with an increased percent weight change (1.35%) along with duration of sotatercept exposure (1.28% per year). Both peri‐menopausal females and females with an unknown menopausal status were associated with a minimally increased percent weight change compared to males (1.06% and 1.22%, respectively). Both prostacyclin dose decreases and prostacyclin de‐escalation were associated with a minimally increased percent weight change (0.57% per dose decrease), whereas prostacyclin dose increases were associated with lower predicted percent weight change (−0.50% per dose increase) along with diuretic dose increases (−0.83%), GLP‐1/SGLT2i exposure (−0.85%), and pre‐menopausal status (−1.01%). Age and BMI contributed minimally to the fitted model.

The second ridge regression model evaluated the odds of ≥ 5% weight gain as a binary outcome. All exponentiated coefficients were close to 1.0, suggesting only weak associations between individual predictors and the outcome after shrinkage largely due to sample size.

Discussion

Clinically significant weight gain was observed in 43.5% of patients treated with sotatercept for Group 1 PAH for at least 12 weeks. The median weight change in the overall cohort was +3.18 kg representing a 4.4% median increase from baseline. Dosage adjustments based on dosing weight changes (increase or decrease) occurred at an incidence of 1.56 per patient‐year. These results suggest the importance of ongoing monitoring of body weight throughout sotatercept treatment and that while weight gain was not observed or evaluated in clinical trials to date, a large percentage of our cohort demonstrated weight gain reaching the 5% threshold for clinical significance.

Our exploratory analyses attempted to identify associations with changes in body weight on sotatercept treatment as numerous potential contributors are plausible. Our first ridge regression model showed that age and baseline BMI were not associated with weight gain. Diuretic adjustments influenced weight gain while accounting for patient and treatment characteristics though it is unknown if this is a sotatercept‐dependent effect. At baseline the overall cohort had a low median NT‐proBNP, though between group differences show these values were slightly higher in the subset of patients who experienced ≥ 5% weight gain, suggesting a possible component of fluid retention, however, median NT‐proBNP decreased after sotatercept treatment, consistent with its known clinical effects [1, 2, 21, 22]. Weight appeared to increase with longer duration of sotatercept exposure, which may explain why this effect was not captured in shorter‐duration clinical trials. A minimal, but present, impact of prostacyclin exposure also was observed. Prostacyclin therapy is known to cause gastrointestinal adverse effects, including nausea, anorexia, diarrhea, and resultant weight loss in a dose‐dependent manner [23]. In the current cohort, 58.8% of patients treated with sotatercept and a prostacyclin reduced their prostacyclin dose at least once and 17% were able to deescalate from parenteral to oral or inhaled. We speculate that with sotatercept treatment, weaning prostacyclins may alleviate gastrointestinal side effects causing return of appetite, improved absorption, and subsequent weight gain [5, 24].

Sotatercept inhibits activin signaling, acting as a ligand trap for members of the transforming growth factor beta (TGF‐β) superfamily, with a high binding affinity for activin A, activin B, growth differentiation factor (GDF)‐11, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)‐10, and GDF‐8 [11]. Activin signaling is implicated in innumerable biological processes, including sex hormone regulation and modulation of bone and skeletal muscle homeostasis via GDF‐8 (myostatin) inhibition [11, 25]. Animal models have also shown that activin A reduces adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)‐stimulated dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)‐stimulated DHEA, decreases pregnenolone and DHEA formation, and enhances conversion of exogenous pregnenolone and DHEA into testosterone [26, 27]. Activin A also plays an important role in the regulation of glucocorticoid and androgen production [28]. Given the known abnormal sex hormone profiles in PAH marked by high estradiol‐2 and low DHEA‐sulfate, sotatercept may alter this hormone signaling and in turn, levels of hormones known to play a significant role in body composition [29, 30, 31]. Limited sample size across menopausal groups and biological sex and lack of measured hormone levels precluded analysis of this in our study.

Based on pharmacologic rationale, prior literature showing an increase in bone formation markers in patients with osteolytic lesions of multiple myeloma, and the clinical trial profile of bimagrumab, it is possible sotatercept's inhibition of myostatin is causing anthropometric changes not fully elucidated by measurement of total body weight alone [9, 10, 25, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. A meta‐analysis of bimagrumab treatment for age‐ and medical condition‐associated sarcopenia showed significant increases in thigh muscle volume and lean body mass, while decreasing body fat mass compared to placebo [35]. Despite the anthropometric changes observed, no significant improvements were seen in hand‐grip strength, gait speed, or 6‐min walk distance in the majority of included studies [35]. Changes in body composition may not always result in increased total body weight. In fact, one randomized, controlled trial of bimagrumab versus placebo in obese patients with type 2 diabetes found a total body weight decrease of 6.5% (vs. −0.8% with placebo), attenuated by a 3.6% increase in lean body mass (vs. −0.8% with placebo) [36]. Further evaluation of potential anthropometric changes with sotatercept treatment is therefore warranted as lifestyle interventions and anti‐obesity pharmacotherapies target both adipose and lean body mass indiscriminately.

This study should be interpreted in light of the following limitations. Our small sample size limited causal associations and was predominantly composed of White females at a single center. The median duration of sotatercept treatment was 35.9 weeks, but individual follow‐up periods varied. Some diuretic and prostacyclin dose changes may not have been documented in the EMR as patients frequently titrate independently. Changes in body composition may have caused the observed weight gain, including an increase in lean body mass and/or fluid retention, however most of the cohort was on diuretics at baseline, NT‐proBNP decreased with sotatercept treatment, and the reported incidence of fluid retention in clinical trials did not differ from placebo [1]. Future investigations should include a larger, diverse cohort, and evaluate anthropometric changes over total body weight.

Sotatercept was associated with clinically significant weight gain in our study, which represents the first published report to date on this novel adverse event. Whether weight gain is due to an increase in lean body mass as opposed to fat mass is yet to be determined. Sotatercept likely has additional off‐target and pleotropic effects due to the complexity of the TGF‐β signaling pathway, and improved understanding of these will inform risks, benefits, appropriate monitoring, and potentially future drug development.
