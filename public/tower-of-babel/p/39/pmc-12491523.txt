Protocol for chromatin immunoprecipitation and hematovascular function assays with differentiated mouse embryonic stem cells
Authors: Raham Lee, Minseong Kim, Changwon Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) as a valuable model for studying developmental processes. This protocol details step-by-step differentiation of mESCs into embryoid bodies (EBs), followed by single-cell dissociation and chromatin preparation for chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), including crosslinking and sonication. We also describe procedures to assess hematovascular differentiation including EB vascular sprouting assay, hematopoietic colony formation assay, and flow cytometry. This protocol enables the investigation of the dynamic interplay between epigenetic modifications and hematovascular lineage commitment during mESC differentiation.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Kim et al.1 and Park et al.2Subject areas: Cell Biology, Cell culture, Cell isolation, Cell-based Assays, Flow Cytometry, Microscopy, Molecular Biology, Gene Expression, Chromatin immunoprecipitation, ChIP, Stem Cells, Cell Differentiation

Before you begin

This protocol describes the procedures used to differentiate mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) into embryoid bodies (EBs, three-dimensional aggregates of differentiated mESCs) and to perform downstream biochemical experiments such as ChIP and functional assays. The methods outlined here are used to investigate molecular mechanisms and hematovascular lineage commitment during early differentiation, as described in our associated studies.1,3,4,5,6 Although this protocol is optimized for cells differentiated from mESCs, it can be adapted for other pluripotent stem cell lines with appropriate modifications. To study gene regulation and cell fate decisions, we detail how to (1) culture and differentiate mESCs into EBs, (2) dissociate EBs and crosslink single cells, and (3) perform ChIP-qPCR to assess protein-DNA interactions. In addition, functional assays including EB sprouting, hematopoietic colony formation, and flow cytometry are described to evaluate hematovascular differentiation.

Innovation

This protocol advances conventional mouse embryonic stem cell (mESC) differentiation methods by systematically optimizing critical steps and integrating functional and molecular analyses into a unified workflow. In standard protocols, EB dissociation and chromatin crosslinking often result in variable cell yield and chromatin quality, especially at early differentiation stages, compromising downstream applications such as ChIP-qPCR. By refining these procedures, we improve the recovery of viable single cells and preserve chromatin integrity, enabling reliable parallel analysis of gene regulation and functional differentiation outcomes within the same batch. The protocol incorporates EB vascular sprouting, hematopoietic colony formation, and flow cytometry assays, allowing researchers to directly link epigenetic changes with hematovascular lineage commitment. Our protocols provide detailed step-by-step instructions, troubleshooting notes, and highlighted critical steps drawn from extensive laboratory experience for those who have limited knowledge on pluripotent stem cell culture and its subsequent analysis. Practical quality control strategies such as storage conditions of various reagents and cell handling are emphasized to minimize potential variations of expected results.

Preparation of poly(HEMA)-coated dishes

Timing: 1 day

Prior to differentiating mESCs, the poly(HEMA)-coated dishes must be prepared to minimize cell adhesion to the bottom of the bacterial culture dishes.

4.Dissolve poly(HEMA) in 95% EtOH.a.Make 400 mL of 95% EtOH in a 1 L bottle.b.Add 8 g of poly(HEMA) into the 1 L bottle containing 400 mL 95% EtOH and dissolve at 65°C for 4–5 h using a magnetic stirrer with approximately 100 rpm. The final concentration of the poly(HEMA) solution is 20 mg/mL.c.Store the solution at 22°C–25°C for up to 3 months.

Bead preparation for ChIP

Timing: 1 day

Note: Use 50 μL of 50% bead slurry per IP for the pre-clearing step and an additional 50 μL for antibody binding during ChIP (i.e., 100 μL of 50% bead slurry per IP is required).

6.Prepare protein A/G PLUS agarose bead slurry.a.Transfer 100 μL of the protein A/G PLUS agarose bead solution into a 1.7 mL microcentrifuge tube.b.Wash the beads by adding 1 mL of cold ChIP dilution buffer and incubating on a rotator (70 rpm) at 4°C for 5 min.c.Centrifuge the tube at 2,500 × g for 30 s at 4°C, and carefully discard the supernatant.d.Repeat steps 6b and 6c twice.Note: To account for bead loss due to adhesion to tube wall or pipette tips, prepare at least an extra 20 μL of 50% bead slurry per IP.Note: To minimize bead loss during the washing steps, carefully aspirate the supernatant using a 3 mL syringe fitted with a 26G needle or a similarly fine-gauge needle.e.After the final wash, resuspend the beads in 1 mL of cold ChIP dilution buffer supplemented with BSA (100 μg/mL) and salmon sperm DNA (500 μg/mL).f.Incubate the tube for 14–18 h at 4°C on a rotator (70 rpm).g.Centrifuge the tube at 2,500 × g for 30 s at 4°C, discard the supernatant, and resuspend the beads in ChIP dilution buffer to make a 50% slurry.h.Store the bead slurry at 4°C until use (steps 13b, 13f).Note: The beads can be stored at 4°C for up to 1 week.Note: This step should be performed one day before the EB dissociation.

Preparation of mESC culture dish containing mitomycin C-treated MEFs

Timing: 1 day

This section describes how to prepare a cell culture dish coated with mitomycin C-treated mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), which serve as feeder cells to support the growth and maintenance of mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs). The process includes gelatin-coating of the dish and plating of mitomycin C-treated MEFs to create a supportive environment for subsequent mESC culture.

1.Prior to thawing mitomycin C-treated MEFs, gelatinize the cell culture dish for 20 min by adding 0.1% gelatin solution.a.Add 2 mL of 0.1% gelatin solution to a 60 mm cell culture dish.b.Swirl the dish gently to ensure that the entire bottom surface of the dish is covered evenly.c.Incubate the dish at 22°C–25°C for 20 min.d.Aspirate the excess gelatin solution and keep the dish until dry completely.

Part 1: ChIP with homemade recipes

Timing: 2 days

This section provides a detailed protocol for performing chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) with differentiated embryoid bodies (EBs) using homemade recipes. The steps guide users through cell harvesting, crosslinking, chromatin fragmentation, immunoprecipitation, DNA purification, and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis. This workflow enables researchers to investigate protein–DNA interactions and epigenetic modifications in EBs (Figure 2).

10.Fragment genomic DNA with a sonicator.a.Resuspend the pellet in 200 μL of ice-cold pre-lysis buffer (CP3A, Epigentek) supplemented with 1× PIC. Incubate the tube on ice for 10 min.b.Centrifuge the tube at 2,500 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Discard the supernatant and resuspend the pellet in 200 μL of ice-cold lysis buffer (CP3B, Epigentek) with 1× PIC.c.On ice, vortex the tube for 5 s every 3 min, for a total of three rounds.d.Centrifuge the tube at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C.e.Transfer the supernatant to a 1.5 mL Bioruptor microtube and close the cap.f.Place the tubes into the DNA sonicator and sonicate chromatin for 10 cycles, each consisting of 20 s 'on' followed by 30 s 'off'.

CRITICAL: DNA shearing efficiency is highly influenced by cell type, cell density, crosslinking duration, and sonication parameters. Optimize sonication settings empirically for your specific system. Record input cell number and DNA yield per ChIP to ensure reproducibility.

Alternatives: For chromatin shearing, a probe (bar) sonicator can be used as an alternative to a water bath sonicator. When using a probe sonicator, be sure to optimize the settings (power, duration, and pulse interval) to achieve fragment sizes similar to those obtained with a water bath sonicator.

Expected outcomes

To ensure a sufficient number of mitomycin treated MEFs for mESC culture, we typically begin with one vial of primary MEFs (approximately 5.0 × 106 cells at passage 3) seeded in a 150 mm dish. After 1–2 days of culture, once the cells reach >80% confluence, they are split into three 150 mm dishes. When these cultures reach ∼80% confluence, the cells are further expanded into six 150 mm dishes. Upon reaching passage 5 and >80% confluence, the MEFs are treated with mitomycin C (10 μg/mL for 3 h) to inactivate proliferation. The mitomycin C treated MEFs are resuspended at 0.8–1.0 × 106 cells/mL in freezing medium and aliquoted into cryovials (1 mL per vial). This process typically yields 30–40 cryovials of mitomycin C-treated MEFs from one vial of primary MEFs at passage 3, providing sufficient stock for downstream applications. When mESCs (8.0 × 105 cells) are seeded on a 60 mm culture dish containing mitomycin C-treated MEFs, daily medium replacement supports healthy growth and pluripotency maintenance. After 2 days of culture, approximately 3.0–4.0 × 106 mESCs can be obtained per 60 mm dish, depending on the initial quality of the mESCs and the MEF confluence. When 4.0 × 105 mESCs are seeded on a gelatin-treated 60 mm dish and maintained with daily medium change, the cells typically reach ∼80% confluence after 2 days. At this point, 1.5–2.0 × 106 cells can be obtained per 60 mm dish.

For EB formation, mESCs cultured on gelatin-coated dishes in ES-IMDM medium are added at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/mL into a poly(HEMA)-coated 100 mm non-adherent dish. Differentiation is carried out for 4–6 days using either serum-containing or serum-free medium. From the serum-differentiation condition, approximately 4.0 × 106 cells can be recovered from one 100 mm dish. To obtain 1.0 × 107 cells per IP, at least 3 of 100 mm dish should be prepared. In the serum-free condition, approximately 2.0 × 106 cells can be recovered from one dish. Therefore, at least 5 dishes are required to obtain sufficient cells for an IP under this condition. These estimates allow for accurate planning of differentiation scale and ensure reproducibility of downstream chromatin immunoprecipitation assays. If mESCs are differentiated under a serum-free condition, mESCs differentiate up to the stage of Brachyury expressing mesoderm. To further differentiate into downstream lineages like hematovascular cells, some factors such as BMP-4 and VEGFA should be added or expression of lineage specific transcription factors such as ETV2 should be induced.1,3,4

Flow cytometric analysis shows that approximately 40% of gated EB cells express FLK1 (VEGFR2) on day 3.5–4.0 of differentiation. By day 6, around 20% of the cells express human CD4 (hCD4), a surrogate marker of hematopoietic progenitors, and approximately 30% express VE-cadherin (CDH5), an endothelial marker.1,3,4,5,6,7 These proportions may vary depending on experimental conditions such as batch differences, timing of harvest, and culture parameters.

Following chromatin shearing by sonication, input samples should be evaluated to confirm that DNA fragments fall within the optimal size range of 200–800 bp. Fragmentation quality should be verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. This quality control step is essential to assess whether the sample is suitable for ChIP. Only samples that demonstrate both appropriate shearing and sufficient DNA quantity should proceed to downstream ChIP analysis. This input DNA quality check provides a critical checkpoint that ensures consistency across experiments and supports reliable interpretation of ChIP results.

Limitations

Embryoid bodies (EBs) derived from mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) serve as a robust in vitro model for studying the early stages of embryonic development but are less suited for investigating later developmental processes. While EBs can partially recapitulate three-dimensional spatial cues, their capacity to receive and transmit signals likely differs from that of embryos developing in vivo. Therefore, it is essential that findings derived from EBs be validated in vivo to confirm their physiological relevance. Despite these limitations, the mouse embryonic stem cell-derived EB (mESC-EB) system has proven to be a powerful tool, providing a wealth of novel and detailed molecular and cellular insights into early development that would be otherwise difficult or impossible to obtain using mouse embryos alone.

One potential limitation of the EB-based protocol is the sensitivity of downstream applications - such as chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) - to the method of EB dissociation. As detailed in the protocol, EBs should be dissociated using pipette tips with two different orifice sizes, and the resulting single-cell suspension should be maintained at a density of approximately 1.0 × 107 cells/mL to ensure optimal performance.

Given the cellular heterogeneity of EBs, researchers should consider employing cell sorting techniques if the goal is to elucidate molecular events within specific cell populations.

Additionally, when adapting this protocol for large-scale experiments, there are specific considerations for each step. Early steps such as mESC expansion and EB formation are straightforward to scale up by increasing the number of culture dishes or using larger flasks. For example, to generate a large number of EBs, one can prepare multiple plates or use larger culture dishes under identical conditions, ensuring that the amount of each reagent is scaled appropriately. In contrast, the EB dissociation and single-cell suspension preparation steps require extra care when scaling up. Processing too many EBs in a single tube can result in incomplete or uneven dissociation, increased cell clumping, and higher risk of cell damage. Therefore, if dissociating more than 1.0 × 107 cells, it is recommended to divide samples into several tubes and process them in parallel (e.g., Two 15 mL tubes for 2.0 × 107). Gentle and consistent pipetting is essential to avoid damaging the cells, and the ratio of EBs to buffer and the number of pipetting strokes should be kept consistent across all tubes. For downstream applications such as ChIP, it is important to scale all reagents (e.g., antibodies, beads, buffers) proportionally to cell number and to ensure that washing and mixing steps are thoroughly performed. With large sample volumes, avoid exceeding the capacity limits of equipment such as centrifuges and cell strainers by splitting samples into multiple batches and processing them separately. Finally, considering the inherent cellular heterogeneity of EBs, if the analysis of specific cell populations is required, cell sorting techniques such as FACS or MACS should be used in parallel with this protocol. By following these recommendations, this protocol can be successfully applied not only to small-scale experiments but also to large-scale studies.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

mESC colonies on mitomycin C treated MEFs display flattened and fibroblast-like morphology (related to step 6 in the step-by-step method details).

Potential solution

mESC colonies displaying flattened and fibroblast-like morphology may experience loss of pluripotency. One potential solution is to carefully select compact, dome-shaped colonies using a micropipette under a phase contrast microscope. Avoid colonies with flattened or irregular morphology, as these suggest spontaneous differentiation. Alternatively, cells expressing SSEA-1, CD324 and/or other cell surface markers8 can be sorted with flow cytometry. To prevent unnecessary differentiation during the culture, prepare the ES culture medium freshly, store LIF properly and change the medium daily.

Problem 2

Low efficiency of EB formation from mESCs (related to step 7 in the step-by-step method details).

Potential solution

EB formation efficiency is significantly influenced by mESC passage number and MEF quality. For best results, use mESCs within passages 2 to 5 post-thawing, as later passages may exhibit reduced pluripotency. Ensure that MEFs used for mESC maintenance are thoroughly inactivated with mitomycin C; residual proliferative MEFs can interfere with EB formation. In addition to mitomycin C treatment, γ-irradiation (5000 rads) is also effective in preventing MEF proliferation and may serve as an alternative method for MEF inactivation.

Problem 3

Poor genomic DNA quality after EB dissociation (related to steps 10–12 in the step-by-step method details).

Potential solution

To ensure high-quality chromatin for ChIP, it is important to minimize dead or damaged cells before proceeding. Dead cells from EBs can potentially affect the quality of genomic DNA and subsequent sonication process. First, ensure complete dissociation into single cells for EB formation. Second, dissociate EBs into single cells with the procedures (step 8). Alternatively, depending upon the purpose of your experiment, proceed only when the proportion of propidium iodide (PI)+ (dead or damaged) cells is below 5%. Take 5.0 × 105 cells from total EB cells and stain with PI before proceeding. Alternatively, sort PI- cells with cell sorter.

Problem 4

Low yield of immunoprecipitated DNA in ChIP (related to step 14 in the step-by-step method details).

Potential solution

Low DNA recovery during ChIP may be caused by inefficient chromatin shearing dynamics, low-abundance target proteins, or weak antibody binding affinity. To improve yield, increase the number of input cells to enhance signal detection. Alternatively, use antibodies that target more accessible or different epitopes, or optimize antibody concentrations to improve binding efficiency. Cross-linking efficiency can also influence the recovery yield; consider using chemical crosslinkers (e.g., EGS, DSG)9,10 that are compatible with both the target protein and downstream applications.

Problem 5

Poor differentiation of mESCs (related to steps 24–27 in the step-by-step method details).

Potential solution

It may be due to the reduced pluripotency of mESCs (see problem 1). Pre-screen FBS for optimal differentiation of mESCs before mESC differentiation experiments.

Problem 6

Cell loss during flow cytometry analysis (related to steps 24–27 in the step-by-step method details).

Potential solution

To minimize cell loss and ensure accurate flow cytometry results, resuspend cells in cold staining buffer containing 4% FBS to maintain viability and prevent clumping. Prior to flow cytometric analysis, filter single-cell suspensions through a 50 μm CellTrics filter to eliminate aggregates and reduce the risk of nozzle clogging. This step is particularly important when analyzing EB-derived samples, which are prone to clumping. Avoid excessive centrifugation or prolonged staining, as these may lead to the loss of fragile cell populations. Minimize handling between staining and flow cytometry whenever possible to further improve cell recovery.
