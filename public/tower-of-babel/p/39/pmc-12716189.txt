Nanosecond Laser‐Assisted Direct Integration of Whitlockite onto Bone Implant Surfaces for Enhanced Bone Regeneration
Authors: Daehyeok Kwon, Hee Jae Hwang, Sun Hee Lee, Soon Jin Choi, Sangmin Song, Ji‐Young Lee, Hyeok Kim, Seung‐Hoon Um, Soyeon Park, Hyewon Kim, Yeon Jae Chang, Yu‐Chan Kim, Hojeong Jeon, Jimin Park, Hyung‐Seop Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhitlockite (WH), a magnesium‐enriched bone mineral, offers significant potential for bone regeneration due to its high bioresorbability and osteogenic properties. Despite these advantages, its application has been hindered by challenges in synthesizing WH directly on implant surfaces. To address this, a laser‐assisted strategy is developed for the in situ formation of WH‐containing coatings on bone implants. To overcome the thermodynamic and kinetic barriers of WH synthesis, a magnesium calcium phosphate (MCP) intermediate is employed, a kinetically accessible and magnesium‐rich phase, as a precursor to WH. The MCP intermediate is readily produced via laser‐induced hydrothermal processing from a magnesium‐enriched solution. Subsequent localized laser irradiation enabled partial transformation of the MCP into WH. Multiscale analyses confirmed successful WH formation, showing rhombohedral morphology and distinct chemical features. In vivo testing in a rat femoral defect model showed that WH‐integrated implants significantly enhanced bone–implant integration and early‐stage vascularization, attributed to the sustained release of osteogenic calcium and magnesium ions. This laser‐assisted method can offer a scalable and effective strategy for implant surface modification in regenerative medicine.

Introduction

Whitlockite (WH), a magnesium‐enriched calcium phosphate (CP) and the second most abundant bone mineral phase, has emerged as a promising candidate for bone regeneration due to its excellent biocompatibility, high bioresorability, and strong osteogenic potential.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] Its natural occurrence in human bone indicates its safety as a material in contact with bone, highlighting the clinical potential of WH‐based coatings and composites. As compared to hydroxyapatite (HAp), the most widely used CP phase for bone regeneration, WH degrades more rapidly under physiological conditions, facilitating the release of calcium (Ca2+) and magnesium (Mg2+) ions within the bone microenvironment.[ 4 , 5 ] These released ions can stimulate the proliferation and differentiation of bone‐forming cells (osteoblasts) while inhibiting the activity of bone‐resorbing cells (osteoclasts). Furthermore, these ions, particularly Mg2+ ions, can further enhance the bone remodeling process by stimulating angiogenesis, which involves the formation of new blood vessels near the bone.[ 6 , 7 , 8 ] These characteristics position WH as a compelling alternative to HAp, offering enhanced capabilities for both angiogenesis and osteogenesis.

Despite the growing interest in WH, its application as a coating layer on the bone‐implant remains limited. Conventional strategies often rely on pre‐synthesizing WH particles via hydrothermal synthesis or precipitation methods, followed by post‐deposition of synthesized particles onto the implant surface through plasma spraying.[ 9 ] This multi‐step approach, comprised of separate synthesis and coating processes, poses several challenges for practical applications, including limited interfacial stability, potential phase change during the post‐deposition process, and increased processing time and complexity.[ 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 ] Direct synthesis of WH onto bone implants using biomimetic coating or laser‐induced hydrothermal synthesis has also been challenging, given that WH formation is both thermodynamically and kinetically less favorable compared to HAp, which tends to dominate under physiological conditions.[ 14 , 15 , 16 ] Put together, challenges associated with WH synthesis and coating have hindered the development of reliable WH‐containing coatings on bone implants, limiting the application of WH at the bone‐implant interface.

To address these challenges, we developed a laser‐assisted process that enables the in situ formation of WH‐containing coating layers at the bone‐implant surface without requiring separate synthesis and coating procedures (Figure 1a). To overcome the thermodynamic and kinetic barriers associated with WH formation, we rationally employed magnesium calcium phosphate (MCP), a kinetically accessible and Mg2+‐rich intermediate phase,[ 2 , 17 , 18 ] as a precursor to WH. The MCP coating layer can be readily synthesized via laser‐induced hydrothermal synthesis in the presence of excess Mg2+ ions, and the subsequent laser treatment partially converted the MCP into the WH. This sequential laser‐based approach bypassed the need for pre‐synthesized WH particles, fabricating WH‐containing coatings over a 5×5 mm2 field within ≈20 min total processing time. The resulting WH‐containing coating layer onto the titanium (Ti) implant exhibited faster ion release kinetics as compared to the HAp or MCP coating layer, which in turn significantly enhanced both angiogenic and osteogenic responses in rat femurs. Notably, the WH‐containing coating layers promoted early‐stage angiogenesis and seamless bone integration, underscoring their therapeutic potential in accelerating bone remodeling at the implant surface (Figure 1b).

Results and Discussion

Formation of MCP Intermediates on Ti Implant via Laser Irradiation

To generate MCP intermediates on Ti surfaces, we adapted a laser‐induced hydrothermal synthesis method previously developed for CP coatings.[ 19 ] In this method, nanosecond laser irradiation was applied to Ti substrates immersed in a modified cell culture medium containing 100‐fold increased concentrations of Ca2⁺ and PO4 3− ions compared to human body fluid (HBF). To favor MCP formation and suppress CP crystallization, we further modified the medium by increasing Mg2⁺ ion concentration by 100‐fold compared to HBF (Table S1, Supporting Information). Laser parameters previously optimized for CP formation (power density: 3539 W/mm2, loop number: 100, repetition rate: 500 kHz, marking area: 2.5 × 2.5 mm2) were applied to these modified conditions, and the resulting coatings were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope equipped with energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectrometry (SEM‐EDS) (Figure 2a).

Laser‐induced hydrothermal synthesis of MCP intermediate layers on Ti surfaces. a) Schematic illustration of the laser‐induced hydrothermal synthesis process for CP and MCP coatings. CP coatings were synthesized in the presence of excessive Ca2+ and PO4 3− ions, while MCP coatings were formed using a medium containing excessive amounts of Ca2+, Mg2+, and PO4 3 ions. b–d) EDS elemental mapping of the Ti surface after laser irradiation at various pH levels b), Ca2+ concentration c), and Mg2+ concentration d) in the modified medium. e) Top and cross‐sectional SEM images of the bare Ti, CP‐coated Ti, and MCP‐coated Ti substrates after laser‐induced hydrothermal synthesis. Coating layers with characteristic particle‐like morphology can be clearly visualized within the yellow boundary. f) XRD patterns of bare Ti, CP‐coated Ti, and MCP‐coated Ti substrates. A shift in the main HAp peak can be shown in the MCP‐coated group, indicating the substitution of Mg2+ ions in the HAp lattice. g) SEM images and EDS elemental mapping of Mg2+ for CP‐coated Ti and MCP‐coated Ti substrates.

Under excess Mg2+ conditions, MCP formation was found to be highly sensitive to the reaction pH. At acidic pH (≤5), EDS revealed strong Ti signals, indicating poor coating formation due to the thermodynamic instability of MCP at low pH.[ 20 , 21 ] At pH 6, a uniform MCP layer formed with minimal Ti exposure, suggesting efficient nucleation and growth of MCP on the substrate. Interestingly, despite the high stability of MCP at neutral conditions, MCP formation on the substrate was negligible at pH 7.[ 22 ] This was attributed to homogeneous nucleation and precipitation of MCP in the bulk solution.[ 23 ] The resulting particulates scattered and refracted the laser beam, reducing energy delivery to the substrate and preventing effective coating (Figure 2b; Figure S1, Supporting Information). These results underscore a trade‐off between coating stability and laser accessibility, with pH 6 identified as optimal.

We next investigated the combined effect of Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ concentrations on MCP deposition. At lower Ca2⁺ (50×), insufficient supersaturation led to poor coating formation, while excessive Ca2⁺ (200×, 400×) triggered bulk precipitation, again interfering with laser interaction and coating efficiency. A 100× increase in Ca2⁺ produced the most uniform and effective coating, with minimal Ti signal (Figure 2c; Figure S2, Supporting Information). Similarly, Mg2⁺ levels above 100× (e.g., 200×, 400×) disrupted coating, whereas both 50× and 100× Mg2⁺ supported uniform deposition. Among these, 100× Mg2⁺ was chosen to ensure sufficient Mg incorporation into the MCP phase (Figure 2d; Figure S3, Supporting Information). Together, these findings highlight the critical role of pH and ion concentrations in the laser‐induced hydrothermal synthesis of MCP intermediate layers.

Ti surfaces treated under the optimal conditions (100× Ca2⁺, 100× Mg2⁺, and pH 6) exhibited uniform and stable MCP coatings characterized by distinctive powder‐like surface structures. Cross‐sectional SEM images further confirmed the presence of a continuous MCP layer, with surface roughening attributed to localized melting and resolidification of the Ti substrate during laser irradiation (Figure 2e).[ 24 , 25 ] Furthermore, X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed a noticeable shift in the main HAp diffraction peak (θ = 32.2°) in the MCP‐coated samples compared to the CP samples. This peak shift verified the successful substitution of Mg2⁺ ions into the HAp lattice, akin to the previous studies (Figure 2f).[ 26 , 27 ] The presence of Mg2+ ions in the MCP‐coated samples was further confirmed by EDS element mapping analyses (Figure 2g).

Partial Phase Transformation of MCP to WH on Ti Implant via Laser Treatment

After the laser‐induced hydrothermal synthesis of the MCP intermediate layer, subsequent laser treatment was applied to induce the partial phase transformation of MCP into the WH phase (Figure 3a). Prior studies showed that MCP can be partially converted into WH phase in the presence of excessive amounts of Mg2+ at high temperatures (> 350°C).[ 17 ] Considering that the nanosecond laser treatment can increase the temperature of the coating surface above 350°C,[ 28 ] we hypothesized that the laser irradiation could be potentially used to induce the phase transformation of MCP to WH.

Effects of subsequent laser irradiation on MCP intermediate layers. a) Schematic illustration of the strategy to partially convert MCP intermediates into WH through laser irradiation. b) EDS‐EDS mapping images of MCP‐coated Ti substrates after subsequent laser irradiation at various laser intensities, showing surface changes with increasing laser power. c) SEM and d) XRD analyses of MCP‐coated Ti substrates subjected to laser irradiation at 1 W, as a function of the number of irradiation cycles. Partial conversion of MCP to WH was observed after 1 cycle of laser exposure, while the additional irradiation cycles induced the formation of the Ca2P2O7 byproduct. e) XPS spectra of the P2p region of MCP‐ and MCP/WH‐coated Ti samples. The black line and blue line represent P2p peaks associated with PO4 3− and HPO4 2− groups, respectively.

To test this hypothesis, we first investigated the effect of laser power intensity on the morphology of the MCP intermediate layer. SEM analysis revealed that increasing the laser power led to the disappearance of coating layer and increased exposure of the underlying Ti substrate. The detachment of the intermediate layer at higher laser powers (≥2W) is presumably attributed to the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between the pre‐deposited MCP coating layer and the metallic substrate.[ 29 , 30 , 31 ] Among the tested parameters, a laser power of 1 W effectively preserved the coating integrity, maintaining a stable surface morphology (Figure 3b). Under these conditions, the coating layer remained intact after multiple laser exposure cycles. There was only an increase in particle and agglomerate size with each cycle. No significant detachment or surface degradation was observed. This indicates that 1 W laser power was sufficient to preserve the structural stability of the MCP coating, regardless of the number of irradiation cycles (Figure 3c).

We then examined how the laser irradiation at the power intensity of 1W affected the phase composition of the MCP intermediate layers with XRD analysis. After 1 cycle of laser exposure, diffraction peaks corresponding to WH (JCPDS 04‐009‐2106) appeared, indicating the partial transformation of MCP intermediate layer into WH (Figure 3d). X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses further confirmed the presence of WH after 1 cycle of laser exposure. While MCP coatings displayed P–O bonding characteristic of Mg‐substituted HAp, a noticeable shift in the P2p binding energy was observed in the coating containing a mixture of MCP and WH phases (MCP/WH). This shift indicated the presence of H–P–O bonding, a signature feature of the WH phase (Ca18Mg2(HPO4)2(PO4)12 (Figure 3e).[ 4 , 32 ]

The characteristic WH (1 0 10) diffraction peak (2θ ≈ 26.0°) became prominent with successive irradiation cycles, demonstrating the further formation and crystallization of the WH phase. However, following the 2 cycles of laser exposure, a diffraction peak corresponding to calcium pyrophosphate (Ca2P2O7, JCPDS 00‐003‐0604) byproduct emerged near 2θ = 29.4°. This phase likely originates from the thermal decomposition of HPO4 2− groups within the WH structure under excessive heat, which leads to the generation of P2O7 4− and the subsequent formation of Ca2P2O7.[ 33 , 34 , 35 ] These findings indicate that the WH phase formed during 1 cycle of laser exposure begins to degrade when subjected to additional thermal exposure. To prevent the emergence of undesirable phases and to maintain the integrity of the MCP/WH coating layer, the laser treatment was therefore optimized to be applied 1 cycle at a power of 1 W. Under these conditions, no statistically significant difference in thickness was observed compared to the MCP group (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Accordingly, all biological evaluations in this study were performed on specimens that were irradiated once at 1 W, and had an intact MCP/WH coating. No biological assays were conducted during the laser screening series.

To further validate the phase transformation induced by laser treatment and gain deeper insight into the microstructural evolution of the MCP intermediate layers, cross‐sectional analyses of CP‐, MCP‐, and MCP/WH‐coated Ti samples were conducted using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The samples were prepared under the optimized laser conditions described previously, and cross‐sections were obtained via focused ion beam (FIB) milling.

TEM observations revealed that CP‐coated samples exhibited the needle‐like morphology, which is a characteristic feature of HAp, whereas MCP‐coated samples were poorly crystalline (nanocrystalline) Mg‐HAp, as indicated by spotty ring SAED and short lattice fringes in HRTEM (Figure S5, Supporting Information). This poor crystallinity is likely related to Mg substitution in the apatite lattice. In contrast, the MCP/WH‐coated samples displayed well‐defined rhombohedral particles, a structural hallmark of WH,[ 14 , 36 , 37 ] in agreement with the XRD findings (Figure 4a). Elemental analysis through TEM/EDS line profiling of these rhombohedral particles showed a (Ca+Mg)/P ratio of approximately 1.43, which closely matches the theoretical stoichiometry of WH. In comparison, the MCP‐coated samples exhibited a significantly higher (Ca+Mg)/P ratio, around 1.67, consistent with Mg‐substituted HAp (Figure 4b). These compositional differences further supported the partial phase transformation from Mg‐HAp to WH following the subsequent laser irradiation.

Microstructural evolution of MCP intermediate layers after the subsequent laser treatment. a) Cross‐sectional TEM images of CP‐, MCP‐, and MCP/WH‐coated Ti samples and stepwise magnification of the TEM images. The regions highlighted by yellow boxes in the upper images correspond to the areas analyzed in the images below. b) TEM/EDS line scanning analysis to determine (Mg+Ca):P ratio. The yellow arrow indicated the location for the line scanning analysis. The red and green dot lines represent the theoretical (Mg+Ca):P ratio of HAp (≈1.67) and WH (≈1.43), respectively.

Taken together, these multi‐scale analyses confirmed that the additional laser treatment effectively induced the partial transformation of MCP into WH. The first laser treatment resulted in the formation of a poorly crystalline MCP (Figure S5, Supporting Information). The heat generated during the additional laser treatment likely provided a local annealing effect under Mg‐rich conditions, including the formation of WH. The lower power and short pulse width limited the total heat delivered and treatment time. This inhibited the preservation of the coating layer and the decomposition of P2O7 4−. Concurrently, Mg2+ likely inhibited the pathway to the apatite phase, stabilizing the HPO4 2− and WH frameworks and leading to the transformation into WH.[ 14 , 38 ] Through the synthesis of a kinetically accessible phase and its phase transformation solely using laser irradiation, we have, for the first use of a two‐step laser protocol, generated a WH‐containing phase on the bone implant surface without pre‐synthesized particles.

In Vivo Applications of MCP/WH‐Coated Ti Implant for Effective Bone Regeneration

To evaluate the biological relevance of MCP/WH‐coated Ti implants in promoting bone remodeling, in vivo studies were performed using a rat femoral defect model. Artificial defects were created in the femoral heads of rats, followed by the implantation of MCP/WH‐coated Ti implants into the defect sites. Bare Ti, CP‐coated, and MCP‐coated implants served as control groups (Figure 5a). Each rod‐shaped Ti implant was coated with CP, MCP, or MCP/WH using the previously optimized laser‐assisted process specific to each coating type. SEM analysis confirmed that the coatings were uniformly distributed and strongly adhered to the implant surfaces (Figure 5b). This robust adhesion is attributed to the laser‐induced formation of a mixed interfacial layer between the Ti substrate and the coating,[ 19 , 39 ] as observed in FIB/TEM cross‐sectional analyses (Figure S6, Supporting Information). This laser‐induced interfacial mixing is expected to improve mechanical interlocking and enhance bonding strength,[ 19 , 40 ] contributing to the long‐term stability and integration of coating layers.

For statistical analyses, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by an ordinary ANOVA test was used. A p‐value < 0.05 *, p‐value < 0.01 **, p‐value < 0.001 ***, respectively was considered statistically significant.

Histological evaluation using Goldner's Trichrome staining revealed distinct differences in bone regeneration and implant integration across the groups over time. At both 2 and 8 weeks post‐implantation, the MCP/WH‐coated implants significantly enhanced new bone generation and exhibited closer bone‐to‐implant contact compared to the CP‐ and MCP‐coated groups. Quantitative measurements of the bone‐implant gap at 2 weeks post‐implantation showed that the Ti group had the widest gap (18.917 ± 10.35 µm), followed by CP (7.652 ± 7.154 µm), MCP (6.574 ± 9.551 µm), and MCP/WH (6.056 ± 1.802 µm) (Figure 5c, d). At 8 weeks post‐implantation, this trend became more pronounced. The MCP/WH group exhibited a markedly reduced gap of 1.481 ± 2.789 µm, representing a nearly 80% reduction from the gap value measured at 2 weeks post‐implantation. In contrast, the Ti, CP, and MCP groups showed minimal improvement, with gaps of 19.825 ± 6.697 µm, 7.599 ± 5.038 µm, and 6.117 ± 3.976 µm, respectively (Figure 5e,f). At 8 weeks post‐implantation, this trend became more pronounced. The MCP/WH group exhibited a markedly reduced gap of 1.481 ± 2.789 µm, representing a nearly 80% reduction from the gap value measured at 2 weeks post‐implantation. In contrast, the Ti, CP, and MCP groups showed minimal improvement, with gaps of 19.825 ± 6.697 µm, 7.599 ± 5.038 µm, and 6.117 ± 3.976 µm, respectively (Figure 5e,f). Furthermore, new bone formation was analyzed based on GT staining. At week 2, the new bone volume was 6.761 ± 10.440% for Ti, 11.369 ± 15.848% for CP, 20.280 ± 16.074% for MCP, and 27.601 ± 23.681% for MCP/WH. The MCP/WH group showed the highest bone volume/total volume (BV/TV) value (Figure S7, Supporting Information). At week 8, the new bone volume further increased in all groups: 42.992 ± 25.289% for Ti, 52.510 ± 29.120% for CP, 54.197 ± 26.213% for MCP, and 60.773 ± 35.038% for MCP/WH. These results highlight the MCP/WH coating's accelerated bone healing and excellent implant integration. These results highlight the accelerated bone healing and superior implant integration of the MCP/WH coating.

To support the observed improvement in bone‐implant interface in the MCP/WH group, a quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed at the cellular level on an MCP/WH specimen (Figure S8, Supporting Information). In a differentiation experiment using the MCP/WH extract, ALP, RUNX2, and OCN increased significantly at both 7 and 14 days compared to the control group. Notably, ALP increased 9‐fold at seven days and 14‐fold at 14 days compared to the control group. These results suggest that osteoblast differentiation was actively induced early on, and the Mg2+, Ca2+ release pattern observed in MCP/WH can be interpreted as a biological basis for promoting an early bone regeneration microenvironment. In addition, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on the 2‐week specimens for the osteogenic differentiation markers collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1) and osteoprotegerin (OPG). The COL1A1 signal was relatively high at the MCP/WH implant interface, suggesting active bone matrix synthesis around the interface (Figure S9, Supporting Information). OPG was strongly expressed around the bone in all groups (Figure S10, Supporting Information), suggesting that a microenvironment unfavorable for osteoclast activity was created. Taken together, these results suggest that, in addition to the high expression of cellular osteogenic markers and the increased COL1A1 signal around the MCP/WH interface, the high OPG expression was observed near the bone suggests a favorable microenvironment for osteogenesis. Accordingly, the MCP/WH group exhibited the highest new bone formation near the interface, which led to a reduced bone‐implant gap and increased bone mass.

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the enhanced bone–implant integration observed in the MCP/WH group, we investigated its ion release profile and compared it with those of the control groups. Ion release from bioactive coatings plays a pivotal role in modulating the local biological microenvironment, particularly through the sustained delivery of osteogenic and angiogenic ions such as Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺.[ 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 ] These ions are known to act synergistically to promote both bone regeneration and neovascularization, processes essential for successful osseointegration.[ 45 , 46 , 47 ] The cumulative release of Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ ions from MCP/WH‐coated specimens was monitored over 14 days, with bare Ti, CP‐, and MCP‐coated specimens used as controls. As anticipated, the MCP/WH coating exhibited the highest levels of Mg2+ and Ca2+ release (Figure 5g,h). Notably, the measured peak Mg2+ concentration in DPBS (approximately 1.1 mM) falls within the beneficial low‐to‐mid millimolar window that supports MSC/osteoblast proliferation and early osteogenic signaling (≈1–5 mM).[ 48 , 49 ] This concentration also overlaps the early inhibitory window for osteoclastogenesis (≈1–3 mM).[ 4 , 50 , 51 ] This concentration remains far below the supraphysiologic range (>5–10 mM), which is linked to mineralization inhibition[ 52 , 53 ] or osteoclast activation under strong RANKL/VD3 stimulation.[ 54 ] Thus, the MCP/WH coating delivers biologically meaningful yet non‐excessive Mg2+, positioning the release profile away from concentrations known to impede regeneration. Considering that there was no significant difference in coating thickness between the MCP and MCP/WH groups (Figure S4, Supporting Information), this behavior can be attributed to the low thermodynamic stability and high solubility of the WH phase under physiological conditions.[ 4 , 14 , 15 ] After 14 days of immersion, the laser‐modified layer remained interact and adhered to the Ti substrate in all specimens. No interfacial delamination or edge lifting observed in the cross‐sections (Figure S11, Supporting Information). These results suggest that the laser‐modified layer retains its integrity under physiological condition and with appropriate mechanical agitation. Furthermore, the coating structure and interface remained intact despite ion release over time. These results support controlled ion release without structural damage.

This elevated ion release correlated with histological observations showing a denser network of blood vessel‐like structures surrounding the MCP/WH implants (CD31+ blood vessel counts per 40x field: 4.133 ± 2.261 for Ti, 4.800 ± 2.856 for CP, 5.533 ± 2.418 for MCP, and 6.666 ± 2.454 for MCP/WH), particularly 2 weeks post‐implantation (Figure 5i; Figure S12, Supporting Information). In contrast, the Ti, CP, and MCP groups displayed more limited vascularization, likely due to their lower or negligible release of Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ ions. Given the essential role of vascularization in supplying oxygen and nutrients to regenerating bone tissue, the enhanced early‐stage angiogenesis observed in the MCP/WH group may contribute to accelerated bone healing.[ 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 ] In vitro biocompatibility assays in which MC3T3‐E1 preosteoblasts were cultured on CP‐, MCP‐, and MCP/WH‐coated samples revealed no cytotoxic effects in any group (Figure S13, Supporting Information). In the 2‐week specimens, matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP‐9) expression was generally weak and comparable to that observed in established biocompatible specimens such as Ti and CP, which are known to exhibit excellent biocompatibility and only minimal immune responses (Figure S14, Supporting Information). GT staining also showed no signs of excessive inflammation, such as necrosis or abscesses. Furthermore, the progressive bone formation between weeks 2 and 8 further supports that there was no significant inflammatory response that could have limited the bone generation.

These results collectively demonstrate the enhanced biological functionality of MCP/WH‐coated Ti implants fabricated under optimized laser conditions. The presence of the WH phase not only improved osteogenic activity but also promoted early‐stage angiogenesis, both of which are critical for successful bone regeneration. The synergistic release of Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ ions from the coating played a pivotal role in modulating the local microenvironment, resulting in accelerated new bone formation and bone‐implant integration (Table S2, Supporting Information). The superior performance of the MCP/WH‐coated implants highlights their strong potential for applications in orthopedic implantology, where rapid and stable osseointegration is essential.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a novel laser‐assisted method for the in situ integration of WH onto bone implant surfaces. Central to this approach is the laser‐induced hydrothermal synthesis of the MCP intermediate layer, a kinetically favorable precursor for WH, under Mg2+‐rich conditions. Optimization of process parameters, including pH and ion concentrations, was critical to achieving uniform MCP intermediate layers on Ti implant surfaces. Following this, localized laser irradiation induced a partial phase transformation of MCP into WH, as confirmed by multiscale analyses. The phase transformation was found to be strongly dependent on laser power intensity and treatment cycles. The resulting MCP/WH coatings exhibited characteristic rhombohedral morphology, theoretical stoichiometry (Mg+Ca:P ratio), and HPO4 2− groups. In vivo evaluation using a rat femoral defect model demonstrated that WH‐integrated implants significantly enhanced bone‐implant integration and promoted early‐stage vascularization. These regenerative effects were attributed to the sustained release of angio‐osteogenic Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ ions from the WH phase. Overall, this two‐step laser‐assisted method provides a scalable, efficient strategy for modifying bone implant surfaces, with promising applications in orthopedic, dental, and bioresorbable devices.

Experimental Section

Preparation of Ti6Al4V Substrate

Ti6Al4V (titanium grade 5) substrates with dimensions of 5 mm × 5 mm × 1mm were used. The substrates were polished sequentially using sandpapers with grit sizes of #300, #600, #1000, and # 2000 to achieve a smooth surface. After polishing, the substrates were washed thoroughly with 99.9% ethanol and distilled water to remove any debris and contaminants.

Preparation of Coating Solutions

Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM, LM001‐09, WELGENE) was used as the base solution for preparing the coating precursors. The CP coating solution was prepared by increasing the concentration of Ca2+ and PO4 3− ions in DMEM by 100‐fold compared to the HBF. To achieve this, 2 M CaCl2 solution was prepared by dissolving CaCl2 (C5670, Sigma‐Aldrich) in DI water, and 1 M H3PO4 solution was prepared using 85 wt% H3PO4 (3453245, Sigma‐Aldrich). These solutions were added to the DMEM base solution immediately before laser processing. MCP and MCP/WH coating solutions were prepared by increasing the concentration of Ca2+, PO4 3−, and Mg2+ ions in DMEM by 100‐fold compared to HBF. To achieve the required magnesium concentration, MgCl2·6H2O (M0250, Sigma‐Aldrich) was dissolved directly in DMEM. Similar to the CP solution case, 2 M CaCl2 and 1 M H3PO4 solutions were prepared and added to the DMEM base solution immediately before laser processing.

Laser Treatment

A nanosecond ytterbium fiber laser was used in this study. The laser featured a 50 µm spot size and could deliver a peak power of 20 W, a repetition rate ranging from 1.6 to 1000 kHz, and a pulse duration between 4 and 200 ns. A low‐power thermal sensor connected to a power meter was employed to measure the laser output. For CP and MCP coatings, the laser parameters were set as follows: power of 10 W, repetition rate of 500 kHz, pulse duration of 200 ns, and a scan speed of 0.5 m s−1. The treated area for each condition was 2.5 × 2.5 mm2, with a line spacing of 10 µm. The scanning was repeated for 100 loops (one loop = one complete line‐by‐line raster of the stated area at 10 µm spacing). For the MCP/WH coating, an additional laser treatment was applied to the MCP‐coated samples. The laser treatment was conducted at a power of 1 W, a repetition rate of 500 kHz, a pulse duration of 200 ns, and a scan speed of 0.5 m s−1. This was done over an area of 5 × 5 mm2 with a 10 µm line spacing. Scanning was repeated for 100 loops (defined as above) to perform 1 irradiation cycle. After laser processing, all treated samples were washed three times with DI water, dried, and stored in a desiccator until further analysis.

Analysis of Surface Morphology and Elemental Composition

The surface structure changes after laser treatment were analyzed using a field‐emission scanning electron microscope (FE‐SEM, Inspect F50). Before SEM analysis, all samples were coated with a platinum layer using a sputter coater at 15 mA for 60 seconds. Elemental composition of the surface was evaluated using EDS at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV. TEM cross‐sectional samples of the laser‐treated coatings were prepared using a FIB (Hitachi‐NX5000). The structure and elemental mapping of the coating layers were analyzed using a TEM (Tecnai F20) equipped with EDS. Cross‐sectional line‐scan measurements were acquired in scanning TEM (STEM, Talos F200X) using high‐angle annular dark‐field imaging (HAADF) to determine (Ca+Mg):P atomic ratios. The TEM‐EDS line scan data were quantified using the instrument's standardless Cliff–Lorimer routine with automatic background subtraction and peak fitting. No external standards or user‐defined k‐factors were applied. Due to the thin‐film geometry, the values were reported as semi‐quantitative and were to be used for relative comparisons only.

Coating Thickness Measurement

MCP and MCP/WH specimens were embedded in epoxy resin, polished with sandpaper (#100, #300, #600, #1000, #2000) followed by 3 µm/1 µm diamond suspensions. After rinsing and drying, cross sections were imaged by SEM. The elemental compositions of the coating layers were evaluated using EDS maps. Thickness was quantified at randomly selected locations based on EDS maps using ImageJ.

Surface Phase Analysis

Phase analysis of the surface coating layers was conducted using a thin‐film XRD (TF‐XRD, D/MAX‐2500) with CuKα radiation. The incident X‐ray angle was fixed at 2°, and the diffraction patterns were collected over a 2θ range of 20‐50° with a step size of 0.02° and a scan rate of 4° per minute. Phase identification was carried out using the standard JCPDS files for hydroxyapatite (00‐009‐0432), whitlockite (04‐009‐2106), and calcium pyrophosphate (00‐003‐0604).

Surface Chemical Analysis

The bonding states of PO4 3− and HPO4 2− groups in the coating layers were analyzed using XPS (PHI 5000) with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). The C 1s peak at 28404 eV was used as the reference for calibration.

Ion Release Analysis

To evaluate the bioactivity of the coating, ion release was measured for all samples over 14 days. Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ ion release was analyzed using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP‐OES). The samples were immersed in Dulbecco's phosphate buffered saline (DPBS) solution and incubated in a heated oven at 37 °C for 14 days under standard conditions. At each time point, the solution was collected and analyzed to quantify the concentration of released ions.

Immersion Stability Test

Specimens (Ti, CP, MCP, and MCP/WH) were immersed in DPBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 14 days. After immersion, the samples were rinsed three times in DI water and dried. The samples immersed for 14 days were then sonicated in a laboratory ultrasonic bath (40 kHz, ≈100 W) for 10 min, 3 times, in fresh DI water at 23–25 °C (solution replaced each cycle). The specimens were embedded in epoxy resin, polished with sandpaper (#100, #300, #600, #1000, #2000), and then polished again with 3 µm and 1 µm diamond pastes. The specimens were examined by cross‐sectional SEM/EDS for spallation or interfacial opening. Note that this sonication step was an indirect robustness probe and was not a standardized adhesion test.

Cell Maintenance

The mouse calvaria‐derived pre‐osteoblastic cell line MC3T3‐E1 (subclone 4; ATCC, CRL‐2593) was cultured in α‐Minimum Essential Medium (α‐MEM; Welgene), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Evaluation of Cell Proliferation

To monitor the proliferative activity of MC3T3‐E1 pre‐osteoblasts over time, a metabolic activity‐based assay utilizing Alamar Blue was performed on day 1 and day 4 following cell seeding. Cells were seeded into 96‐well culture plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well using α‐MEM (Welgene), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin. Cultures were maintained under standard incubation conditions (37°C, 5% CO2, humidified atmosphere). At each evaluation point, the culture medium was gently aspirated and replaced with fresh medium containing 10% (v/v) Alamar Blue reagent (Invitrogen). Following a 2‐hour incubation at 37°C, protected from light, fluorescence signals were recorded using a microplate reader set to 560 nm excitation and 590 nm emission.

Cell Culture and Osteogenic Induction

MC3T3‐E1 pre‐osteoblasts (ATCC CRL‐2593, Manassas, VA, USA) were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells per well and maintained in α‐MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U mL−1 penicillin, and 100 µg mL−1 streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. After 48 h, growth medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium consisting of the same basal formulation further supplemented with 10 mM β‐glycerophosphate (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 50 µg mL−1 ascorbic acid (Sigma‐Aldrich). Differentiation medium was refreshed every two days.

RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. One microgram of RNA was reverse‐transcribed using Maxime RT premix (iNtRON) to generate cDNA. Quantitative PCR was performed with Power SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a real‐time PCR system following the manufacturer's cycling recommendations (initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s). GAPDH served as the internal reference gene. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in Table 1 .

Animal Surgery

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of KIST (KIST‐IACUC‐2025‐001‐1). Eight‐week‐old male Sprague‐Dawley rats (250‐270 g; Central 32 Lab. Animal Inc.) were acclimated under standard housing conditions for at least one week prior to surgery. All surgical procedures were performed under inhalation anesthesia. A minimally invasive incision was made on the femoral head, and a cylindrical bone defect (1 mm in diameter, 2.5 mm in depth) was created using an electric drill. The site was rinsed with sterile 0.9% saline, and cylindrical implant specimens (Ø 1 × 2.5 mm) of Ti, CP, MCP, and MCP/WH groups were inserted into the defect (n = 4 per group per time point). To prevent infection and manage postoperative pain, gentamicin (5 mg kg−1) and ketoprofen (5 mg kg−1) were administered intramuscularly once daily for 3 consecutive days. At 2‐ and 8‐weeks post‐implantation, animals were anesthetized using inhalation anesthesia, and transcardiac perfusion was performed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) followed by 2.5% glutaraldehyde for tissue fixation. The femur containing the implants was carefully harvested and processed for subsequent histological evaluation.

Histological Assessment

After leaving the extracted specimens for one week in formalin, they were rinsed with flowing water for 9 h and then cut for processing. The specimens were dehydrated with increasing concentrations of EtOH in the order of 70%, 95%, and 100% EtOH. Then, the tissue sample was infiltrated with ethyl alcohol and Technovit 7200 Resin (Heraeus KULZER, Hanau). Finally, penetration was performed by vacuuming the stock solution of Technovit 7200 resin for 2 d. The resin was then cured using a UV embedding system (KULZER EXAKT 520®, Heraeus KULZER, Hanau). Finally, the resin block was cut in half using an EXAKT Diamond cutter (KULZER EXAKT 300®, Heraeus KULZER, Hanau) to produce a cross‐section around the implant and bone tissue. The thickness of the initial section was 300 ± 50 µm, which was polished to a thickness of 60 ± 5 µm using an EXAKT grinding machine (KULZER EXAKT 400CS®, Heraeus KULZER, Hanau). Prepared tissue sections were stained using Goldner's Trichrome (GT) staining protocol for evaluation of mineralized tissue formation and implant integration.[ 59 ]

Immunohistochemical Analysis

All tissues were decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 21 days. Subsequently, the implant was carefully removed from the femur. The removed bone tissue was progressively dehydrated with different concentrations of ethanol and embedded in paraffin to produce 5 µm thick longitudinal sections. The paraffin sections were preheated at 60 °C for 30 min, deparaffinized with xylene (3 × 10 min), rehydrated in 100–70% ethanol series (5 min each), and transferred to distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed in retrieval solution (low pH) at 115 °C for 15 min, then cooled to room temperature for 15–20 min, followed by washing with PBS. To remove autofluorescence, sections were incubated with freshly prepared NaBH4 solution (0.1% w/v, PBS) for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by three washes (5 min each) with PBS. To reduce nonspecific binding, after 30 minutes of blocking with 5% normal serum (TBST) at room temperature, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against COL1A1, OPG, CD31, and MMP‐9 (each 1:100, diluted in blocking buffer) in a humidified chamber at 4 °C overnight. The sections were washed three times with TBST the next day, and then incubated with species‐specific fluorescent secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor, 1:200) for 2 hours at room temperature under light protection, followed by three washes with TBST. Nuclear staining was performed with DAPI (1 µg mL−1, 5 minutes) and then mounted with anti‐fade media. Fluorescence images were acquired under the same laser power, gain, and offset conditions with a confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000), and the original files were saved as 16‐bit TIFF files.

Analysis of the Gap Between New Bone and Implant

The gap between new bone and implant was analyzed by histological imaging of tissues implanted with Ti, CP, MCP, and MCP/WH groups. The observations were focused on an area of approximately 100 × 100 µm2 around the implanted material in the histological images. The gap between the implant and new bone was quantified using Slide Viewer software. Measurements were made at least 10 different locations for each tissue image. Results were presented by pooling data obtained from 50 to 100 locations in all samples.

BV/TV Quantification

Histological images of tissues with Ti, CP, MCP, and MCP/WH implants at 2 and 8 weeks were utilized. The area of newly formed bone, located 100 µm away from the edge of the implanted material where it interfaces with bone, was evaluated through the analysis of histological images at 40× magnification. The percentage of newly formed bone area within a unit area of 100 µm × 100 µm at the edge region of the implanted specimen was measured using ImageJ.

Quantification of Collagen Expression

Collagen expression was quantified from 20× immunohistochemical images. For each specimen, a fixed region of interest (ROI) measuring 100 µm × 100 µm was positioned 100 µm away from the implant–bone interface along the implant edge. The images were analyzed using ImageJ. The collagen‐positive signal was thresholded identically across groups, and the collagen‐positive area fraction (percent) within the ROI was measured. The ROI placement and threshold parameters were kept constant for all groups.

Microvessel Counting

Microvessels were assessed using 40× immunohistochemical images (CD31). In each image, the number of CD31⁺ microvessels per field was counted using the following criterion: The counts were recorded as CD31⁺ microvessels per 40× field and averaged per specimen. The field size, magnification, and counting rules were identical across groups.

Statistical Analysis

One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by ordinary ANOVA test was used. A p‐value < 0.05 *, p‐value < 0.01 **, p‐value < 0.001 ***, respectively was considered statistically significant.
