Long-term outcomes after cervical cancer screening in El Salvador: primary human papillomavirus screen-and-treat compared with cytology
Authors: Karla Alfaro, Leticia Lopez, Montserrat Soler, Gabriel Conzuelo Rodriguez, Mario Meléndez, Marina A Chacon, Jose M Velis Artero, Elizabeth Torres, Miriam Cremer, Julia C Gage, Juan Felix, Kathleen M Schmeler, Preetha Ramalingam, Melissa Lopez Varon, Rachel Masch, Mauricio Maza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFrom 2012 to 2017, the Cervical Cancer Prevention in El Salvador (CAPE) piloted and scaled up a human papillomavirus (HPV) screen-and-treat intervention. Findings resulted in El Salvador’s adoption of the strategy as part of the national programme, but long-term clinical outcomes are unknown. Here, we compare the detection of high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or higher (CIN2+) and HPV infection after recommended screening intervals between two groups: women who participated in CAPE and a comparable group screened via cytology.MethodsCAPE participants who had undergone screening at least 5 years previously (screen-and-treat group) and women in the same age range with conventional cytology screening 2 to 3 years previously (cytology group) were recruited for repeat screening with primary HPV testing. Women with positive HPV results were referred for colposcopy and cervical biopsy to determine further management. Women with negative HPV results received recommendations for routine future screening according to national guidelines.ResultsA total of 6631 women were enrolled (screen-and-treat = 4087; cytology=2544). Significantly less CIN2+ was detected in the screen-and-treat group at 0.7% (29/4087) than in the cytology group at 2.1% (54/2544) (p<0.001) with a risk ratio of 0.41 (95% CI 0.26 to 0.61). HPV positivity was also lower in the screen-and-treat group at 9.5% (388/4077) compared with the cytology group at 11.5% (293/2445) (p=0.008).ConclusionAt the first round of repeat screening after the implementation of CAPE, women who underwent HPV testing in a screen-and-treat strategy had significantly less CIN2+ and HPV positivity compared with those who underwent cytology. These outcomes occurred despite a longer screening interval for HPV testing than cytology. Findings provide reassurance for women and health systems that primary HPV screen-and-treat programmes with extended screening intervals, like the one in El Salvador, are achievable and effective in low- and middle-income settings.Keywords: Epidemiology; Health systems evaluation; Public Health; Screening; Other infection, disease, disorder, or injury

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

In low-resource settings, screen-and-treat strategies for cervical cancer prevention require simpler infrastructure and have higher adherence than multi-step cytology programmes. El Salvador was among the first countries to implement a primary HPV screen-and-treat initiative, but there is a lack of data on the long-term effectiveness of this approach.

Introduction

Cervical cancer mortality has significantly decreased in high-income countries over the last 50 years, but remains a leading cause of cancer-related death for women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 In El Salvador, cervical cancer is the second leading cause of cancer death for women aged 15 to 44, with an estimated age-adjusted mortality rate of 7.4 per 1 00 000 women in 2023.2 Cytology has been available for decades, but historically, the quality has been poor and coverage has been low.3 To increase access to cervical cancer prevention in resource-limited settings, the WHO has endorsed ‘screen-and-treat’ approaches where treatment is offered after a positive human papillomavirus (HPV) test or visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA), with or without triage.4 El Salvador was one of the first countries to critically evaluate the programme advantages of a primary HPV screen-and-treat strategy and to adopt it as a national programme. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the long-term performance of the screen-and-treat strategy compared with cytology.

To assess the feasibility of the screen-and-treat approach, in 2010 the El Salvador Ministry of Health (MoH) partnered with Basic Health International (BHI), a non-profit organisation, to develop the Cervical Cancer Prevention in El Salvador (CAPE) demonstration project.5 CAPE compared a screen-and-treat strategy using HPV testing followed by cryotherapy for those with positive results. Prior to cryotherapy, women underwent visual assessment for treatment to ensure that they were eligible for ablative therapy. Those who did not meet the criteria were referred for colposcopy. This group was compared with women screened with cytology followed by colposcopy and cervical biopsies for those with abnormal results. Those diagnosed with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or higher (CIN2+) were referred to treatment. The first two phases of CAPE were carried out from 2012 to 2014. A higher proportion of screen-positive women in the screen-and-treat group completed recommended treatment compared with those undergoing cytology followed by colposcopy (90.4% vs 48.1%).6 7 In the third and final phase of CAPE, 17 965 women participated in only HPV screen-and-treat with a 75% completion rate.8

The success of CAPE led to El Salvador’s adoption of the strategy in 2020 as part of its cervical cancer control programme.9 As women screened with HPV during CAPE became due for their 5-year routine screening, a unique opportunity arose to evaluate long-term outcomes of the screen-and-treat strategy in the context of the public health system. Here, we present results of a follow-up of women who participated in the early phases of CAPE compared with a comparison group due for routine cytology.

Materials and methods

A mixed retrospective and prospective cohort study was designed to compare the proportion of biopsy-confirmed CIN2+in two groups of women due for routine screening who had undergone different screening strategies. At the time, HPV testing was only available at selected locations but was gradually expanding to other areas. The first group included women who had undergone HPV testing in the first two phases of CAPE at least 5 years previously (the HPV screen-and-treat group); and the second group included women who had undergone conventional cytology screening 2 to 3 years prior (the cytology group). Secondary outcomes were the rate of HPV positivity, result of visual assessment with acetic acid (VIA) and colposcopic findings. The selected screening intervals for both the HPV and cytology groups reflected national guidelines.

The study targeted a sample of 4071 women in the HPV screen-and-treat group and a sample of 2539 in the cytology group based on an assumption of approximately 10% loss to follow-up (ie, women not attending a follow-up colposcopy and biopsy visit) and anticipated CIN2+ incidence (0.8% among women in the HPV screen-and-treat group and 1.6% in the cytology group).10 11 These estimates would yield at least 80% power to detect a minimal difference in proportions of 0.8% (0.8% vs 1.6%) between the two groups while maintaining an alpha of 0.05.

Enrolment took place over 46 months from June 2018 to April 2022. Originally, the project was scheduled to be completed in 36 months, but enrolment was suspended temporarily due to COVID-related restrictions. For the screen-and-treat group, research team members used previous study logs to identify women aged 35–55 who had participated in CAPE between 2012 and 2014. These women were invited to the study via calls and home visits and scheduled for a screening appointment at their local clinic. For the cytology group, MoH collaborators obtained records from municipalities with similar sociodemographic characteristics based on census data. Women aged 35–55 screened with cytology 2–3 years earlier were eligible to participate. Community health promoters (CHPs) invited women to be screened at participating clinics as part of their regular duties.

Exclusion criteria for both groups were history of hysterectomy or cervical cancer diagnosis, current pregnancy or inability or unwillingness to provide informed consent. Women in both groups remained eligible for study enrolment regardless of previous screening result (positive or negative) or previous referral to colposcopy and biopsy. However, criteria for referral differed for each screening strategy: women in screen-and-treat were referred only if they were HPV-positive and had contraindications to immediate ablation treatment, while referral was indicated for all women with abnormal results in the cytology group.

On arrival at screening clinics, informed consent procedures were carried out by trained research assistants, followed by a brief background questionnaire. Women then underwent sample collection for careHPV (Qiagen, Gaithersburg, MD) testing performed by the clinic’s physician. Clinic staff scheduled a second visit for all participants to receive their results after approximately 30 days, as is routine in the current programme. Women with a negative HPV test were asked to return to the same clinic and were counselled to undergo repeat screening after 5 years. Those with a positive result were scheduled to attend a regional clinic where a study gynaecologist (ET) with extensive experience in high-volume studies performed VIA, colposcopy and biopsy. Women who agreed to participate in the study and scheduled a screening visit but failed to attend received another home visit from the local CHP to reschedule the appointment. If this was missed a second time, a third home visit was carried out to either schedule a new appointment or offer the woman a self-collected careHPV test preceded by informed consent procedures. Women who declined both options were excluded.

All HPV samples were sent for analysis to the regional MoH laboratory where careHPV tests from the national programme are routinely processed. Histopathology analyses were carried out at a local private pathology laboratory and confirmed by an expert gynaecologic pathologist (JF). Discordant results were resolved via a third adjudicating pathologist (PR), and this third diagnosis was considered final in the analysis. Referral to further care at MoH facilities was based on local histopathology results.

Central tendency measures and percentages were used to describe demographic and clinical characteristics. Differences between the screen-and-treat and the cytology groups were calculated using one-way analysis of variance or Wilcoxon Rank Sum test for continuous variables and χ2 test for discrete variables. The final proportions of CIN2+and specific histopathological outcomes were estimated by group. Additionally, we estimated risk ratios (RR) for HPV test results, as well as histological outcomes (CIN2+) between the two groups using a generalised linear model (binomial family, log link). All statistical analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

The study recruited slightly more women than anticipated, and a total of 6631 women were enrolled. Patient flow and clinical outcomes of the current screening, including follow-up rates, are shown in figure 1. Of the 5087 women who had previously undergone HPV testing in the first two phases of CAPE, 4087 participated. In addition, 2544 women from the cytology programme attended their scheduled screening visit and were then recruited to the study. HPV samples were collected in clinics by providers for all but 11 participants who did not attend the screening visit and were offered self-sampling at home. There were no meaningful differences in sociodemographic and health history characteristics between the screen-and-treat and cytology groups (table 1). Previous HPV positivity for the HPV screen-and-treat group was 11.5%. In contrast, all but four women in the cytology group had normal results at their original screening (of those, one had a low-grade diagnosis, two had insufficient samples and one had missing data). The mean time between previous screening and the study screening visit was 67±4.6 months in the screen-and-treat group and 28.5±4.6 months in the cytology group (p=0.01).

Discussion

Our findings show that women in El Salvador who participated in HPV screen-and-treat during the early phases of CAPE had improved outcomes compared with those in the cytology screening comparison group at the first re-screening visit (5 years for HPV testing and 2–3 years for cytology). Those in the HPV screen-and-treat group had a lower proportion of CIN2+. HPV positivity was also lower among women in the HPV screen-and-treat compared with the cytology group, even though twice as much time had passed since their last screening. These results suggest that primary HPV-based screening is effective and support the use of primary HPV screening over cytology to detect high-grade cervical precancer.

The study was not powered to detect disparities in CIN3, a better predictor of invasive cancer than CIN2+12,14 or in cancer itself, but there were differences between groups and subgroups. Overall, the proportion of CIN3 was lower in the HPV screen-and-treat than in the cytology group, and women with previous HPV negative results had the lowest proportions of both CIN2+and CIN3 of all subgroups (ie, previous HPV positive and previous cytology). Additionally, four cases of cervical cancer (including one AIS) were found in the HPV screen-and-treat group and one AIS in the cytology group. After review of medical records of the screen-and-treat group, it was determined that one of these women had originally tested HPV negative and another had a previous HPV positive result but did not attend treatment. The other two had received cryotherapy, but no records were found to indicate that they had returned for the recommended follow-up visit after 1 year. This highlights the importance of closely monitoring treated women. However, a larger sample would be needed to determine if observed differences in CIN3 or cervical cancer are meaningful in these screening populations. Another secondary finding was the high rate of unsatisfactory VIA evaluations in both groups. VIA continues to be widely used as a screening method in LMICs due to its ease and low cost, but results from this study reinforce the need to rely on more accurate screening technologies.

The study has several limitations. First, women were not randomly assigned to either screening modality. Although women in the cytology comparison group were selected to closely resemble the CAPE population that had undergone HPV screen-and-treat, it is possible that unknown characteristics impacted the detection of CIN2+ or HPV positivity. However, there were no significant differences in cervical cancer risk factors between groups. Second, all but four women in the cytology group had normal cytology results. This was due to two reasons: one, historically poor cytology in El Salvador, as in other low-resource settings, tends to overwhelmingly underdiagnose cervical disease.15 Thus, there were likely very few women with recorded abnormal results. Two women with abnormal cytology are referred to a multistep process (ie, colposcopy, biopsy and potential treatment) with long wait times that can take several months.16 Women in this subgroup who recently completed or were in the process of completing referral procedures were less likely to be invited to re-screening by the CHPs. Nevertheless, this imbalance would have biased results in the opposite direction, ie, with the cytology group presenting lower CIN2+ and HPV rates than the HPV screen-and-treat group. A third limitation is that women in the cytology group were invited to attend screening as part of routine practice and were not recruited to the study until arrival at the clinics; thus, reasons for non-attendance were not available for this group. Finally, the proportion of women with previous referral to colposcopy and biopsy and any resulting treatment is unknown. Based on previous CAPE data, it is estimated that approximately 10% of HPV-positive women in the screen-and-treat group were referred to colposcopy and biopsy. However, we were unable to ascertain how many attended the procedures (historically, only about half of referred women attend). None of the participating women in the cytology group were referred to colposcopy and biopsy since they all had normal results.

The present study demonstrates the long-term success of a primary HPV screen-and-treat approach over cytology. Cytology remains a widely used screening method despite its poor performance compared with HPV testing.17 18 In El Salvador, primary HPV screen-and-treat has been shown to be a more cost-effective approach to cervical cancer screening,19 20 and this finding has been replicated in other LMICs.21,24 Results from this study provide reassurance of the extended clinical outcomes of such a strategy.

Conclusion

In El Salvador, a significant amount of high-grade precancer was prevented in the HPV screen-and-treat group after one round of screening, and it is likely that reductions in cervical cancer will be observed in future evaluations. A sustained HPV screening strategy may also translate to more efficient use of resources as screening intervals are lengthened. Although the high cost and processing requirements of commercially available HPV tests limit the applicability of this strategy,25 there are low-cost assays in development that are likely to make this approach more widely available in the near future.26 Results from El Salvador provide a model for other LMICs that are considering adoption of a primary HPV screen-and-treat strategy.
