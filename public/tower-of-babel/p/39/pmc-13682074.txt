The efficacy and safety of adalimumab in treating axial spondyloarthritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bo Dong, Ani Ma, Weidong Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAxial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic inflammatory rheumatic disorder. Multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have verified the efficacy of adalimumab for axSpA. Nevertheless, few integrated analyses explore its time-varying efficacy across different disease subtypes and age groups, and the optimal treatment time point and full respiratory disorders remain unclear.MethodsWe searched four electronic databases for eligible RCTs up to September 1, 2022. Two researchers independently screened literature and extracted data. Methodological bias was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool, and Stata 15.0 was adopted for quantitative meta-analysis.ResultsNine studies involving 1113 patients were included. At week 12, adalimumab significantly increased ASAS20 [RR = 2.36, 95% CI (1.94, 2.87)], ASAS40 [RR = 3.16, 95% CI (2.46, 4.06)], ASAS5/6 [RR = 4.07, 95% CI (3.09, 5.37)] and ASAS partial remission [RR = 5.29, 95% CI (3.02, 9.26)] response rates. Disease activity indicators, including ASDAS [SMD = -1.31, 95% CI (-1.52, -1.09)] and BASDAI [SMD = -0.81, 95% CI (-0.99, -0.63)], were significantly lower, while CRP [SMD = -0.52, 95% CI (-0.70, -0.35)], total back pain [SMD = -0.68, 95% CI (-0.86, -0.49)], nighttime back pain [SMD = -0.69, 95% CI (-0.88, -0.50)], BASMI [SMD = -0.39, 95% CI (-0.58, -0.20)] and BASFI [SMD = -0.60, 95% CI (-0.77, -0.42)] were also markedly reduced. No significant between-group difference was detected in the incidence of adverse events.DiscussionAdalimumab achieves peak efficacy at week 12, with comparable efficacy in radiographic and non-radiographic axSpA, and works effectively in adolescents aged ≥ 12 years. Despite possible infections, respiratory disorders and liver dysfunction, adalimumab possesses a favorable safety profile. These results underpin its standardized clinical use for axSpA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10251-y.

Introduction

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease, which mainly involves the axial joints (i.e. sacroiliac joints) and the spine [1]. Commonly seen presentations include SI joint inflammation, spine, entheses, and other associated extra-articular features, such as anterior uveitis, IBD, and psoriasis [2]. AxSpA is associated with new bone formation in the mesoaxial skeleton, which leads to ankylosis and dysfunction of the intervertebral and small joints, as well as proliferative arthritis of the peripheral joints [3]. Its major clinical manifestations are chronic pain and stiffness in the pelvis and the lower back. AxSpA may also lead to complications such as cardiovascular disease (CVD), depression, and teoporosis [4]. Newer statistics show that the prevalence of axSpA ranges between 0.1% and 1.4% globally [5]. While 44.6% of male patients with axSpA switch to less physically demanding jobs, with an average retirement age of 36, which undoubtedly adds to the burden on society [6]. Currently, it is classified into non-radiographic axSpA (nr-axSpA) and radiographic axSpA (r-axSpA) (also known as ankylosing spondylitis), depending on whether there are obvious structural changes in the sacroiliac joint under imaging [7]. Studies have found that the progression rate of nr-axSpA to r-axSpA is 10%-20% in the first year, and 20.3% in the second year [8]. Juvenile-onset ankylosing spondylitis (JoAS), a subtype of axSpA occurring at 12–18 years old, accounts for 10–15% of total axSpA cases. JoAS shares highly similar disease progression, clinical phenotype and therapeutic response with adult axSpA.

Adalimumab is a fully human, recombinant, monoclonal IgG1 antibody specific for the cytokine tumour necrosis factor-α [9]. It eliminates TNF’s biological effects by specifically binding to TNF and blocking TNF’s interaction with TNF receptors. The antibody can also lyse TNF-expressing cells in the presence of complement. The mechanism of anti-TNF-α therapy includes downregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and IL-8, inhibiting cartilage and bone damage, and even achieving repair effects. Adalimumab has been approved for use in a number of inflammatory diseases including axSpA. The Assessments of SpondyloArthritis International Society and the European League Against Rheumatism (ASAS/EULAR) recommend that patients with high disease activity despite conventional treatment with NSAIDs and other drugs should be treated with biologic inhibitors, including adalimumab [10].

Previous reviews suggested that adalimumab may have certain efficacy in treating axSpA [11–15]. However, this treatment approach may induce adverse reactions such as liver function abnormalities, infections, and tuberculosis [16, 17]. Large-sample quantitative analysis on the efficacy and safety of adalimumab for axSpA is lacking. There is no clear consensus on its optimal therapeutic time point, and there are differences in its efficacy in different disease subtypes and age groups, and specific incidence of adverse reactions. This meta-analysis included 9 high-quality RCTs (n = 1113), representing one of the largest meta-analyses on adalimumab for axSpA to date. It is the first to conduct subgroup analyses by time point, disease subtype, and age, clarifying the temporal trends of efficacy and population heterogeneity. It systematically analyzed the types and incidence rates of adverse events (AEs) associated with adalimumab, providing evidence for clinical safety. It verified that the efficacy of adalimumab was consistent in nr-axSpA and r-axSpA, offering evidence-based support for biologic therapy in nr-axSpA.

Data and methods

This meta-analysis was conducted according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (for details, see http://training.cochrane.org/handbook) and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [18]. The study protocol was approved in PROSPERO (ID: CRD42023403651).

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they met the following conditions: (1) Interventions: non-adalimumab treatment was received in the experimental group, or non-placebo was received in the control group; (2) No available data on therapeutic response and safety outcomes of adalimumab; (3) Patients with previous effective response to DMARDs (excluding patients with initial response but subsequent treatment failure).

Search strategy

Computer searches were conducted in The Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases for studies on adalimumab in treating axSpA. The search period was from database inception to September 1, 2022. The search strategy employed both MeSH terms and free keywords, including “Adalimumab” and “Axial spondyloarthritis”. No restrictions were imposed on language. The specific search strategy is shown in Table S1.

Literature screening and data extraction

We imported the retrieved literature into Endnote X9 and automatically and manually removed duplicates. Then, we removed literature that was not consistent with the purpose of this study (e.g. reviews, guidelines, expert opinions, replies, letters, animal experiments, etc.) after reading the titles and abstracts. The full texts of the remaining studies were downloaded and reviewed to determine eligible studies. Data extraction was performed on the original studies that met the study criteria. A standardised digital extraction form was developed before carrying out the data extraction. Extracted information included author, publication year, publication type, study region, number of included participants, participant age, intervention method, intervention duration, and outcome measures. The above literature screening and data extraction were carried out independently by two researchers and cross-checked upon completion. In case of disagreement, a consensus was reached through discussion with a third researcher.

Risk of bias assessment for included studies

Two researchers independently assessed the risk of bias in the included studies and cross-checked the results. The Cochrane Handbook’s recommended tool for bias assessment was used to evaluate the risk of bias in the included studies, and the assessment involved random sequence generation (selection bias), allocation concealment (selection bias), blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias), blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias), incomplete outcome data (attrition bias), selective reporting (reporting bias), and other biases.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata 15.0, which involved various analyses such as tests for heterogeneity, publication bias analysis, and sensitivity analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as standard mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Dichotomous variables were expressed as relative risk (RR) with 95% CI. Q statistics and I² test were used to quantify heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was selected if P > 0.1 and I² ≤ 50% (acceptable heterogeneity). A random-effects model was selected if P ≤ 0.1 or I² > 50% (significant heterogeneity). Publication bias was visually assessed using funnel plots and quantified using Egger’s test. P < 0.05 indicated a significant publication bias. Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially removing individual included studies to re-calculate the pooled effect size, so as to evaluate the stability of the meta-analysis results. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to follow-up time points (week 2/8/12/24) and disease subtypes (nr-axSpA/r-axSpA) to explore the time-dependent therapeutic trend and efficacy differences in different subtypes. To ensure data independence and avoid unit bias, we conducted independent analyses at each follow-up time point and did not treat data from multiple time points in the same study population as independent observations to merge them. The “metabias” command was used to detect publication bias in the included studies, and differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.

Results

Literature search results

A total of 5,324 records were identified from four databases, including the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase. The retrieved records were imported into EndNote X9, where 983 duplicates were removed. After screening based on titles and abstracts, 4,069 irrelevant studies were excluded. Then 261 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, 9 eligible articles were included. The selection process and results are presented in Fig. 1.

Meta-analysis results

ASAS response rate

The results of the meta-analysis indicated that, compared to placebo, adalimumab treatment significantly increased the ASAS20 response rate [(RR = 2.36, 95% CI (1.94, 2.87)], ASAS40 response rate [RR = 3.16, 95% CI (2.46, 4.06)], ASAS5/6 [RR = 4.07, 95% CI (3.09, 5.37)], and ASAS partial remission [RR = 5.29, 95% CI (3.02, 9.26)] in patients with axSpA. Subgroup analysis based on different follow-up time points showed that adalimumab significantly outperforms the placebo group in ASAS20 at week 2, week 8, week 12, and week 24. The response rates for ASAS40 and ASAS5/6 at week 12 and week 24 were superior to placebo, and the response rate for ASAS partial remission at week 12 was better than placebo (as shown in Fig. 3 and Table S3-4).

CRP

Five articles reported improvements in CRP levels, including a total of 550 patients, with 332 in the experimental group and 218 in the control group. The results showed that adalimumab was superior to placebo in reducing CRP levels [SMD = -0.52, 95% CI (-0.70, -0.35)] (as shown in Fig. 4-C).

Total back pain and nighttime back pain

The experimental group showed greater relief in patient pain assessment indices for total back pain [SMD = -0.68, 95% CI (-0.86, -0.49)] and nighttime back pain [SMD = -0.69, 95% CI (-0.88, -0.50)] at week 12 compared to the placebo group (as shown in Fig. 5-A and 5-B).

BASMI

Three articles reported improvements in BASMI, including a total of 472 patients, with 289 in the experimental group and 183 in the control group. The results showed that adalimumab was superior to placebo in improving BASMI within 12 weeks [SMD = -0.39, 95% CI (-0.58, -0.20)] (as shown in Fig. 5-C).

Five articles reported improvements in BASFI, including a total of 550 patients, with 332 in the experimental group and 218 in the control group. Safety analysis

Adverse events

Five articles [19, 23–26] reported AEs and included 929 patients, with 571 in the experimental group and 358 in the control group. A random-effects model was used to analyze the data (I2 = 53.0%, P = 0.074). The results showed that there was no statistical difference in the incidence of AE between the adalimumab treatment group and the placebo group [RR = 1.12, 95% CI (0.94, 1.34)]. Subgroup analysis based on different follow-up time points showed that there was still no statistical difference in the incidence of AEs between the adalimumab treatment group and the placebo group at week 12 [RR = 1.11, 95% CI (0.84, 1.47)] and week 24 [RR = 1.16, 95% CI (0.87, 1.55)] (as shown in Figure S1).

Severe adverse events

Four articles [19, 23–25] reported severe adverse events (SAEs) and included 883 patients, with 549 in the experimental group and 334 in the control group. A fixed-effect model was used to analyze the data (I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.754). The results showed that there was no statistical difference in the incidence of SAEs between the adalimumab treatment group and the placebo group [RR = 1.31, 95% CI (0.51, 3.36)]. Subgroup analysis based on different follow-up time points showed that there was still no statistical difference in the incidence of SAEs between the adalimumab group and the placebo group at week 12 [RR = 1.59, 95% CI (0.32, 7.88)] and week 24 [RR = 1.18, 95% CI (0.37, 3.80)] (as shown in Figure S2).

Discussion

This meta-analysis systematically evaluated the efficacy and safety of adalimumab for axSpA by pooling data from 9 high-quality RCTs involving 1113 patients, and further elucidated the therapeutic effect of adalimumab at different time points and in different axSpA subtypes. This study provided more precise medical evidence for the clinical application of adalimumab in the treatment of axSpA.

Therapeutic efficacy and time-dependent characteristics

Disease activity, serving as a valuable reflection of the disease process in patients with axSpA, aids physicians in assessing treatment outcomes and prognosis [27]. Research has shown that higher disease activity correlates with reduced mobility in patients [28]. ASDAS [29] and BASDAI [30] are the main indicators for evaluating the disease activity of axSpAs. Meta-analysis results showed that adalimumab treatment effectively reduced disease activity and relieved symptoms in patients with axSpA.

CRP is an acute reactant marker in patients with axSpA [31], and although there is still debate about its specificity for diagnosing axSpA, its concentration level is considered to be positively correlated with the degree of spinal structural damage [32]. Berg et al. [33] found that high levels of CRP in patients with axSpA may be associated with atherosclerosis, thereby elevating the risk of cardiovascular disorders. Meta-analysis results showed that adalimumab treatment effectively reduced CRP levels, thereby reducing the degree of spinal structural damage and possibly lowering the incidence of cardiovascular diseases to a certain extent. The study by Poddubnyy et al. [34] suggested that there was only a weak difference between high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and conventional CRP. The results of a study [23] comparing hs-CRP with other conventional CRP included in this study also confirmed this view.

The ASAS response criteria were used to comprehensively evaluate the therapeutic effect of adalimumab, including overall patient condition, pain, function and inflammation. The results showed that adalimumab significantly increased ASAS20, ASAS40, ASAS5/6 and ASAS partial remission rates at 12 weeks compared with placebo. Furthermore, the therapeutic effect was time-dependent. Adalimumab had an insignificant effect for ASAS20 with high heterogeneity in the early stage (2–4 weeks), while the effect became stable and superior to placebo after 4 weeks. All ASAS response rates reached the optimal level at 12 weeks. This is consistent with the conclusion of Pedersen et al. [35], that is, the most significant symptom improvement in axSpA patients treated with anti-TNF-α drugs is observed within the first 12 weeks. This study further quantified the effect size of the therapeutic response of adalimumab, providing a clear quantitative reference for the evaluation of clinical short-term efficacy. Due to the a limited number of research on ASAS70, it was not included as an outcome measure in this study.

Back pain is the main symptom of axSpA. The prevalence of axSpA in patients with chronic back pain is approximately 5%. However, among patients with inflammatory back pain of varying diagnostic criteria, this value can be as high as 30% [36, 37]. As the disease progresses, patients often experience worsening nocturnal back pain, especially during the early morning hours [38]. The analysis results showed that adalimumab exhibited a relieving effect on both patient back pain and nighttime back pain. Additionally, it was found to ameliorate sleep problems induced by nighttime back pain [39].

The spinal deformity and poor posture caused by axSpA can severely affect the body shape and are associated with increased rates of anxiety and depression [40]. BASMI is the most commonly used objective assessment tool for the clinical evaluation of spinal mobility. The results of this study showed that adalimumab significantly improved spinal mobility in patients with axSpA.

Safety profile of adalimumab

Safety analysis showed no statistically significant difference in the overall incidence of AEs and SAEs between the adalimumab group and the placebo group (RR = 1.12 for AEs, RR = 1.31 for SAEs), which confirmed the basic clinical safety of adalimumab in the treatment of axSpA. However, the adalimumab group was more likely to have specific adverse reactions. Infections (34.1%), respiratory system diseases (13.1%) and liver function abnormalities (11.3%) were the most common, and their incidence rates were significantly higher in the adalimumab group than those in the placebo group (21.8%, 7%, and 3.2%, respectively).

Common respiratory adverse reactions are nasosinusitis and respiratory tract infection. Liver function abnormalities are mainly manifested as elevated alanine transaminase, aspartate transaminase or alkaline phosphatase, whose mechanism may be related to immune regulation and immune-mediated liver eosinophilic infiltration [41]. It is uncertain whether such abnormalities resolve after drug discontinuation. As adalimumab is administered subcutaneously, skin reactions are also a common adverse effect. The incidence of skin reactions in the experimental group (13.46%) is significantly higher than that in the placebo group (4.69%), consistent with the findings of Noah Scheinfeld [42]. These results remind clinicians to implement targeted safety monitoring during the use of adalimumab, such as regular detection of liver function indexes and timely intervention for infectious and respiratory symptoms. These findings make up for the lack of quantitative analysis of specific adverse reactions in previous studies [42].

Efficacy in different disease subtypes and age groups

JoAS usually occurs between 12 and 18 years old, accounting for 10–15% of all axSpA cases. Its disease progression and clinical phenotype are highly similar to adult axSpA. Furthermore, the ASAS/EULAR Guidelines for the Management of Juvenile Spondyloarthritis (2022) recommend 12 years old as the diagnostic starting age for juvenile axSpA. Therefore, this study defined the age threshold at ≥ 12 years, including adult juvenile patients to expand the sample size and provide evidence for medication use across all age groups in clinical practice. One study [24] included in this study focuses on adolescent AS patients (the age (+/-standard deviation(SD)) of the experimental/intervention group: 15.1 ± 1.5/15.5 ± 1.7) and does not show significant heterogeneity compared with other study results. This result indicates that adalimumab has a good therapeutic effect on JoAS. which is also supported by the study of R.A. Russo [43]. However, the small sample size of adolescent axSpA studies leads to insufficient evidence, which is a key direction for future research to expand sample size and focus on special populations.

The results of previous studies have shown [44, 45] that there is no difference between nr-axSpA and r-axSpA in terms of patients’ disease burden, therapeutic effects on biologics, NSAIDs, DMARDs, and glucocorticoids, except for a certain degree of difference in the imaging presentation. We further performed subgroup analysis by nr-axSpA and r-axSpA to investigate the heterogeneity of the efficacy and safety of adalimumab across different axSpA populations. With the 12-week ASAS20 and ASAS40 response rates as the core efficacy endpoints, the results confirmed the consistent therapeutic effect of adalimumab in both subtypes. The ASAS40 response rate in the nr-axSpA subgroup was 54.87%, significantly higher than 29.66% in the placebo group (RR = 1.85, 95% CI[1.30,2.63]). The treatment response rate reached 62.17% in the r-axSpA subgroup, also significantly higher than 25.62% in the placebo group (RR = 2.43, 95% CI[1.88,3.14]). The heterogeneity test showed no significant interaction between the two subtypes (I²=9.1%, P = 0.29), indicating that the improvement effect of adalimumab on ASAS40 response was not affected by radiographic changes of the sacroiliac joint.

Similarly, adalimumab had a significant therapeutic effect in improving the ASAS20 response rates in both subtypes. Among individuals with nr-axSpA, with a relatively small sample size, the ASAS20 response rate in the adalimumab group was 54.55%, much higher than the 12.50% in the placebo group (RR = 4.36, 95% CI[1.40,13.58,]). Among individuals with r-axSpA, the ASAS20 response rate in the adalimumab group was 42.61%, significantly higher than 12.40% in the placebo group (RR = 3.44, 95% CI[2.35,5.03, ]). The heterogeneity for ASAS20 response rate between the two subtypes was not statistically significant (I²=0%, P = 0.70), further confirming that disease subtype was not a modifying factor for the therapeutic effect of adalimumab in axSpA.

For the ASAS20 response rate, the RR value of adalimumab in the nr-axSpA subgroup was slightly higher than that in the r-axSpA subgroup, which may be related to the relatively small sample size of the nr-axSpA subgroup, leading to a wider 95% confidence interval. Therefore, it cannot be concluded that there is an essential difference in efficacy between the two subtypes. Additionally, the ASAS40 response rate of adalimumab in the r-axSpA subgroup was slightly higher than that in the nr-axSpA subgroup. This may be associated with the longer disease course and more pronounced clinical symptoms in r-axSpA patients, where clinical improvement after anti-inflammatory treatment is more significant in objective evaluations. However, these slight differences in effect sizes did not lead to statistical heterogeneity between subgroups, providing clinical evidence-based support for the use of adalimumab in both subtypes.

Impact of study quality on results

This meta-analysis included 9 RCTs. The overall quality of the included studies was moderate to high, and no studies of very low quality were identified. Studies that did not report random sequence generation might have selection bias. Nonetheless, due to the small sample size of these studies, their impact on the overall results was limited. Detection bias, due to a lack of blinding of outcome assessors, could lead to an overestimation of efficacy outcomes. However, the core efficacy endpoints of this study (e.g., ASAS20/BASDAI) are composite indicators combining both objective and subjective assessments, thus minimizing the impact of such bias. Studies that did not report data on loss to follow-up might have attrition bias. Nevertheless, the loss to follow-up rates in the included studies were all < 10%, which met the standard for RCTs. Overall, the risk of bias in the included studies was low to moderate, with no high risk of bias. This suggests that the results of this analysis are minimally affected by study quality and are therefore reliable.

The funnel plots for publication bias for all outcomes reported in ≥ 3 studies were generally symmetrical, and Egger’s test results were not statistically significant. These results suggested that the studies included in this meta-analysis were not significantly affected by publication bias. This further enhances the reliability of the conclusions of this meta-analysis. Sensitivity analysis revealed that after sequentially excluding each study, the pooled effect sizes for the primary outcomes did not change materially, suggesting that the conclusions of this study were robust. This indicates a high degree of homogeneity among the included studies, and the efficacy of adalimumab is not affected by the potential bias introduced by any single study. In addition, the sensitivity analysis, publication bias assessment, subgroup analysis of axSpA (nr-axSpA/r-axSpA), and detailed results of specific types and incidences of adverse events have been described in our study. Due to space limitations, the above-mentioned relevant data are not shown in the figures and tables.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. As there is a lack of unified diagnostic criteria for axSpA, both the revised New York criteria and the classification criteria of ASAS may have possibilities of misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis [32]. Therefore, this study did not make clear restrictions when formulating inclusion criteria, only requiring the fulfillment of any axSpA diagnostic criteria. Moreover, due to the limited number of RCTs on adalimumab in treating adolescent axSpA patients, this study combined them with adult axSpA patients for analysis, which may mask the specific characteristics of adalimumab in adolescent populations. In addition, in terms of safety analysis, the included studies did not clearly classify infectious AEs, which may affect the accuracy of the findings to some extent.

Strengths

This study is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to quantitatively evaluate the efficacy and safety of adalimumab in the treatment of axSpA with a large sample size. The study incorporated multiple high-quality, multicenter RCTs to systematically evaluate the treatment effect of adalimumab and potential adverse reactions in axSpA. The participants in these clinical trials were recruited from diverse countries and regions, enhancing the study’s population representativeness and the relevance of its results. Given that the duration of medication may influence treatment effects, several outcome measures were evaluated at different follow-up time points. Furthermore, the time-dependent therapeutic effect of adalimumab and the optimal therapeutic time window (12 weeks) were clarified, providing a clear time reference for clinical treatment of axSpA.

Conclusion

The meta-analysis results demonstrated that adalimumab was effective in improving disease activity, reducing inflammatory markers, alleviating back pain, and increasing spinal mobility. Moreover, it tends to achieve the optimal treatment effect at week 12. Despite the occurrence of adverse reactions, such as infections, respiratory diseases, and liver abnormalities, adalimumab remains an acceptable treatment. This study also confirmed that there was no significant difference in the therapeutic effect of adalimumab in nr-axSpA and r-axSpA, and that the therapeutic effect in juvenile onset ankylosing spondylitis (JoAS) was similar to that in adult axSpA.
