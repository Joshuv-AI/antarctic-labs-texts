Anti-inflammatory treatment confirms rsfMRI and TSPO PET as biomarkers of functional connectivity and neuroinflammation in rat contusion spinal cord injuries
Authors: Chaoqi Mu, Jamie L Reed, Feng Wang, M Noor Tantawy, Xinqiang Yan, Ming Lu, John C Gore, Li Min Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA cascade of biological responses to spinal cord injury (SCI), including neuroinflammation, plays a pivotal role in determining long-term outcomes and has become a primary therapeutic target. Riluzole, a neuroprotective agent, has demonstrated efficacy in preserving tissue integrity and improving motor function following SCI. The study aims to use this established treatment to verify that resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) functional connectivity (rsFC) and TSPO PET metrics are reliable biomarkers of SCI severity, progression, and treatment response. 16 male rats with a moderate lumbar contusion injury were administered Riluzole or HBC vehicle. rsfMRI and TSPO PET scans were collected post-SCI alongside motor-sensory behavioral tests. After SCI, significantly stronger rsFC between dorsal-to-dorsal gray matter horns rostral to the SCI was observed in the riluzole group, compared to the vehicle group. A majority of horn pairs rostral and caudal to injury exhibited significant decrease in rsFC over time for both groups and correlated with post-injury behavioral deficits and recovery. TSPO-PET detected increased SCI neuroinflammatory activity. Our results demonstrate reductions in rsFC disruption, validating the role of rsFC as biomarkers of SCI severity and progression. The imaging biomarkers can be used to evaluate the responsiveness to treatment and efficacy of novel therapies in preclinical studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42844-x.Keywords: Functional MRI (fMRI), TSPO PET, Spinal cord, Motor and somatosensory behaviorSubject terms: Biomarkers, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Traumatic spinal cord injury (SCI) causes immediate structural damage, followed by a cascade of secondary biological responses that further compromise the spinal cord tissue. These secondary reactions—commonly referred to as the secondary injury1—are characterized by inflammation resulting from the release of cytokines and free radicals2 by activated immune cells3, excitotoxicity due to excessive glutamate release by overactivated neurons4, and ischemia and hypoxia caused by reduced blood flow5. Neuroinflammatory-induced molecular activity is a key component of the acute injury phase and contributes significantly to functional recovery6. The combined effects of primary trauma and secondary pathology lead to long-term neurological deficits. In some cases, functional reorganization driven by neural plasticity7 has been linked to partial recovery of neurological function8–10. Given the pivotal role of neuroinflammation in determining long-term outcomes, it has become a primary therapeutic target aimed at reducing further damage and preserving viable tissues. Riluzole, an anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective agent, has demonstrated efficacy in both pre-clinical and clinical studies in preserving spinal cord tissue integrity and improving motor function recovery following SCI11,12. Here we used this established treatment to verify that specific imaging metrics change is response to injury and treatment, thereby verifying them as imaging biomarkers for assessing SCI progression and treatment response.

Noninvasive quantitative imaging is well-suited to serve as a biomarker of injury severity, for monitoring and quantifying the multiplicity of pathological changes that occur in the injured spinal cord, and response to treatments. For example, Positron Emission Tomography (PET) imaging enables high-resolution mapping of molecular distribution and activity through the injection of radiotracers that specifically bind to biomolecule targets13. TSPO is widely used as an imaging biomarker for neuroinflammation14, as its expression is upregulated in activated astrocytes and microglia during neuroinflammatory responses. TSPO PET has been used in the diagnosis of neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease15, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)16, and Parkinson’s disease17. More recently, our group has implemented TSPO-PET to longitudinally assess neuroinflammation in the injured rat lumbar spinal cord18,19.

Resting-state functional MRI (rsfMRI) detects correlations between the blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) signals from different regions of interest to indirectly measure neural intrinsic functional connectivity in the brain and spinal cord20,21. While rsfMRI has been extensively used to probe functional connections in the brain21, its application in the spinal cord remains limited due to several recognized technical challenges22. These challenges stem from the spinal cord’s smaller and more uniform anatomical structure compared to the brain, and its location, surrounded by different tissues including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), muscle and adipose tissue, and vertebral bones23. CSF, in particular, pulsates and flows in synchrony with the cardiac cycle24, contributing to motion artifacts, while magnetic field inhomogeneities are induced by changes in susceptibility at interfaces. These factors pose significant challenges to spinal cord MRI25.

Despite these factors, we and others have successfully conducted spinal cord rsfMRI studies and pioneered both animal and human investigations by acquiring rsfMRI images with customized coils and applying optimized imaging acquisition and preprocessing techniques26. We have shown that resting state functional connectivity (rsFC) is a sensitive biomarker of the functional integrity of the spinal cord gray matter circuits27. Spinal injury or pathology primarily disrupts intraspinal circuits, as shown in human MS patients with spinal cord disorders, as well as in monkeys and rats with SCI28–30. Human spinal cord rsfMRI studies have consistently identified detectable and reproducible rsFC networks within the spinal cord of healthy subjects31,32. Similarly, rat spinal cord rsfMRI has also reliably detected functional changes in rsFC networks in both cervical and lumbar regions27,30, particularly within the gray matter horns. Spinal cord rsfMRI can capture functional network changes at the injury region, providing longitudinal insight into functional network disruptions, potential recovery, or reorganization after injury. This capability is especially valuable for correlating imaging findings with behavioral deficits and recovery trajectories over time following traumatic SCI.

In the current study, we aim to test the hypothesis that a combination of TSPO PET imaging and rsMRI, which provide molecular and functional information from the injured and surrounding regions of interest (ROI), can serve as imaging biomarkers for assessing the severity, progression, and recovery dynamics of SCI. By evaluating the effects of a treatment for neuroinflammation using the neuroprotective drug riluzole, we aimed to assess the sensitivity of these two imaging modalities and confirm their utility as biomarkers of response. By acquiring multimodal MR and PET imaging data from riluzole treatment rats after SCI, and measuring motor and somatosensory function using established behavioral tests, we quantified injury severity and recovery differences between treatment animals and vehicle controls. The results confirm the diagnostic potential and sensitivity of rsfMRI/rsFC and TSPO-PET imaging biomarkers in evaluating SCI and response to treatment. The validation of non-invasive, diagnostic, and predictive biomarkers for quantifying multi-faceted pathologies provides essential tools to support the development and assessment of potential pharmacological therapies for SCI.

Comparison of resting state functional connectivity (rsFC) between gray matter horns within the spinal segments rostral and caudal to the spinal cord injury in riluzole vs vehicle treatment groups

rsFC values were calculated across all 4 gray matter horns within Slice 2 and Slice 4—representing spinal segments rostral and caudal to the injury site at L1 (Fig. 3A) and caudal to the injury site at L2 (Fig. 3B). We intentionally exclude quantification of the axial slice at the epicenter of the injury region, as the structural degradation and degeneration of gray matter-white matter contrast after injury made it impossible to accurately delineate gray matter horns from the Slice 3 L1-L2 structural images, as shown in Fig. 1. In general, connectivity values were highly variable in rostral and caudal spinal segments in both treatment and vehicle groups. At the Week 1 post-injury time point, the average dorsal-dorsal gray matter horn correlation strength at Slice 4 L2 was significantly stronger in the treatment group than the vehicle group (r = 0.49 ± 0.12, mean ± sd), compared to (r = 0.72 ± 0.09, p = 0.001). Comparison with previously acquired normal control and untreated SCI fMRI data showed that the dorsal-dorsal horn rsFC in normal control rats were found to be r = 0.54 and decreased 24-h after injury to r = 0.43, dorsal to the injury region30. Statistical comparison between the normal controls, untreated SCI, treatment and vehicle groups indicated that the DD rsFC connectivity is significantly higher in the treatment group compared to vehicle and untreated SCI rats (Fig. 3C). No significant differences in connectivity strength were observed in other gray-matter horn pairs between the riluzole treatment and vehicle groups. During the four-week post-injury recovery period, both groups show a similar trend of decreasing connectivity strength as the injury progresses for the majority of the gray matter horn connections, regardless of rostral or caudal to the injury region, suggesting a continuous degradation of intraspinal cord rsFC. A 2-way ANOVA test was used for each gray matter horn connection to determine if injury progression time or administration of riluzole treatment influenced functional rsFC values. Most of the gray matter horn connections showed significant change in connectivity strength with time, including the rostral DD (p = 0.0446), VV (p < 0.0001) and cDV (p = 0.0006) connections, and the caudal VV (p = 0.0222) and iDV (p = 0.0083) connections.

TSPO PET imaging detected reduced neuroinflammation after administration of riluzole treatment

To increase sample sizes, we included TSPO PET imaging data from untreated SCI rats that did not receive any injections and recovered spontaneously from injury, and sham injury control rats19 that did not receive an injury and only a surgical incision (Fig. 5A–D). The sham injury validated that the neuroinflammatory activity in the spinal cord is due to SCI and not the surgical procedure. First, only comparing the treatment and vehicle groups, we observed lower 18F-8302 uptake during Week 1 post-injury in the treatment group (1.177%ID/g ± 0.445) than that in the vehicle group (1.751%ID/g ± 0.676), indicating higher TSPO concentrations associated with increased neuroinflammatory activity (Fig. 5E). No statistical difference was found in 18F-8302 uptake between Week 1 to Week 3, so further analysis combining data from both time points for comparison of all 4 groups of interest (Fig. 5F–G) was conducted. Statistical comparison showed significantly increased 18F-8302 uptake in the untreated SCI injury group compared to the sham injury group (p = 0.0076, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Fig. 5G). No statistical difference was observed between the SCI and vehicle injury groups, so we merged the subjects from both groups and considered a combined injury group as untreated recovery from SCI. The average radiotracer uptake increased significantly at the injury epicenter for the combined untreated SCI injury group (1.890%ID/g ± 0.710, mean ± SD, Fig. 5H) compared to the sham group (1.019%ID/g ± 0.425, p = 0.004) but was not significantly different from the treatment group.

Riluzole treatment improved motor and somatosensory functional recovery

The behavior tests identified recovery differences between the Riluzole treatment and vehicle groups, in both motor and somatosensory function (Fig. 6). The treatment group showed improved motor function recovery during the later weeks post-injury, compared to the vehicle group, particularly in terms of BBB score measurement, which measures hindlimb functionality post-injury. During the later weeks post-injury at Week 4, the BBB score for the treatment group was significantly higher compared to the vehicle (Week 4 treatment = 13 ± 4.99, vehicle = 8.75 ± 4.37, p = 0.0370, non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test) The treatment group also showed trending longer response times on the hot plate test compared to the vehicle group from Weeks 2 to 8 (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

RsFC as an imaging biomarker of riluzole treatment efficacy.

Gray matter horn rsFC differences between the riluzole treatment and HBC vehicle groups were identified. In particular the dorsal-to-dorsal horns connection caudal to the injury site within the first week after injury was significantly higher for the treatment group compared to the vehicle group, indicating neuroprotective effects of riluzole and reduced rsFC disruption following SCI. A similar decrease in the DD horn connection was found in our previous rat spinal cord injury rsfMRI study immediately after the initial injury, and the vehicle group rsFC values are consistent with the untreated SCI group, and both groups show decreased DD rsFC connectivity compared to treatment. However, temporal analysis indicated that, despite the significant difference in DD gray matter horn connection at Week 1 post-injury, the rsFC changes over time did not identify significant recovery differences over time between the treatment and vehicle. This could suggest that the neuroprotective effects of riluzole treatment are effective only during the early stages of recovery, or that continuous administration of the treatment is necessary for prolonged improvements in functional recovery after injury. Another consideration is that the moderately severe injury leads to significant deficits that eventually lead to partial functional recovery in the later weeks post-injury, but these rats typically do not regain normal pre-injury baseline levels of motor or somatosensory function33,34. Even after the administration of treatment, none of the rats exhibited motor function recovery beyond a BBB score of 13–15, corresponding to a lack of higher-level motor function such as coordinated gait. This is verified by our behavior results, where we see trending improvements in behavioral functional recovery in the treatment group, but few significant differences between treatment and vehicle. As a result, the temporal recovery differences between treatment and vehicle were likely not significant enough to be detectable using rsfMRI. The initial difference in rsFC measures after injury between the treatment and vehicle groups could indicate that rsFC is a more sensitive biomarker of rsFC integrity that can be used to indicate degree of network disruption and injury severity.

Similar fALFF values between treatment and vehicle reinforce rsFC differences between groups

No significant differences in fALFF values at each gray matter horn were found between the treatment and vehicle groups, across all time points and gray matter horn regions, both rostral and caudal to the spinal cord injury site. While these results suggest that there are not significant changes in spinal cord spontaneous neuronal activity in SCI rats that are administered riluzole compared to the vehicle group, this also appears to reinforce the rsFC results, as the functional connectivity differences observed between treatment and vehicle groups are not due to global power amplitude fluctuations in the BOLD signal.

Relationship between rsFC functional connectivity metrics and behavior

RsFC metrics showed strong temporal correlations with corresponding behavioral metric changes over time, indicating that the intra-horn correlations of BOLD signals could have behavioral relevance. Both treatment and vehicle groups showed strong and significant correlations with all corresponding motor and somatosensory behavioral metric changes over time, especially the VV, iDV, and cDV connections. This is especially crucial, as our previous studies have shown that structural and molecular biomarkers of SCI may correlate with certain motor or somatosensory behavioral metrics19,35. rsFC metrics are more sensitive to behavioral function temporal trend changes after injury, and correlated to different measured aspects of motor and somatosensory functional deficits and recovery, which is potentially useful for validating spinal cord injury treatment efficacy. However, while the treatment group demonstrated improved trends in behavioral recovery compared to the vehicle group, the differences were not significant. This is likely due to underpowered sample size for a neurobehavioral study. Despite these limitations, the results are still promising, as the rsFC metrics did correlated over time with behavioral changes, even if the treatment group did not show significant improvement in motor and somatosensory behavioral recovery.

TSPO PET captures differences in neuroinflammatory activity between spinal cord injury animals and sham animals without injuries, but did not identify differences between treatment and vehicle animals

TSPO PET data compared between the four rat groups demonstrated that TSPO PET using 18F-8302 detected increased neuroinflammatory activity after SCI but did not identify neuroinflammation reductions with the administration of riluzole. Neuroinflammation activity in the treatment group was shown to be not significantly different from both the sham injury group that received no injury and the vehicle and spontaneous recovery SCI groups. The combined vehicle and spontaneous recovery SCI groups (untreated SCI group) indicated elevated 18F-8302 radiotracer uptake at the spinal cord injury region compared to sham injury, showing that the vehicle group experienced increased neuroinflammatory activity relative to animals without injury. The riluzole treatment group results suggest that while there is a potential decreasing trend in neuroinflammatory activity after injury, PET imaging did not identify significant differences in neuroinflammatory activity for the riluzole treatment group compared to untreated or sham injury animals. Post-mortem histological analysis did identify significantly reduced activated microglia in treatment animals, indicating that riluzole does have anti-neuroinflammatory properties.

Clinical relevance

We have validated the utility of rsfMRI imaging biomarkers for non-invasively monitoring SCI severity, recovery, and responses to anti-inflammatory treatment. In this preclinical pharmacological study, rsFC measures showed improvements in riluzole treatment rats compared to vehicle controls, along with corresponding sensorimotor behavioral improvements after injury. Both rsfMRI36 and TSPO PET37 imaging are translatable imaging modalities for assessing human spinal cord injuries. In addition to clinical translation, these validated biomarkers can also be applied in future preclinical pharmacological studies to assess the efficacy of novel SCI treatments.

Limitations and future considerations

The current study used riluzole as the pharmacological treatment of interest to use for the multi-modal imaging study. The efficacy of riluzole in treating spinal cord injuries has been previously verified in pre-clinical and clinical studies38. Future studies should be conducted with a novel treatment that has not undergone clinical trial experiments. Due to scheduling and scan time constraints, pre-injury baseline rsfMRI data were not acquired from the treatment and vehicle groups. The main statistical comparisons were performed between the treatment and vehicle groups, but pre-injury data would provide corresponding baseline comparisons as relations for both groups to verify connectivity changes before and after injury. Finally, spatial comparisons between MRI and PET were difficult due to the large difference in spatial FOV between the two modalities. A combined MR-PET acquisition would be useful for the spatial co-registration of the MR and PET imaging data. Finally, as mentioned previously, for a neurobehavioral study, 8 subjects per subject group is underpowered statistically. Increasing the sample size with more subjects would improve the measurement of recovery differences between treatment and vehicle groups.

Conclusion

Quantitative MRI and PET imaging biomarkers of functional connectivity and neuroinflammation are able to identify and differentiate spinal cord injury severity and recovery changes in SCI rats that were administered riluzole as a neuroprotective treatment. Both imaging modalities can serve as imaging biomarkers of SCI and rsFC metrics can be used to evaluate the responsiveness to treatment and efficacy of novel therapies for treating spinal cord injuries longitudinally over time.

Methods

Animal preparation

16 male Sprague–Dawley rats (Envigo [Inotiv], Lafayette, Indiana, United States) weighing 340 ± 24 g (mean ± std, approximately 8–10 weeks in age) were studied, and all underwent contusion injury at the lumbar L1-L2 level. 8 rats received Riluzole treatment while the other 8 received vehicle treatment. Serving as control subjects for the fMRI experiment, we have also included rsfMRI subject data from a previous study30, including normal control rats (N = 4), and untreated spinal cord injured rats (N = 5) that recovered from injury spontaneously. Controls for the TSPO-PET imaging experiments19 included previous acquired untreated spinal cord injured rats (N = 10) that recovered from injury spontaneously and sham injury controls (N = 8) that received an incision and no SCI. All untreated SCI subjects had the same demographics and underwent the same contusion injury type as the treatment and vehicle groups.

Each animal was initially sedated with 2–3% isoflurane and then kept free breathing while under anesthesia using isoflurane (0.5–1.5%) delivered by O2 during surgery and PET scans, and 70/30 N2O/O2 mixture for MRI scans. Respiration rates were maintained between 30–60 breaths/min by adjusting isoflurane concentrations. Vital signs, including respiration pattern and rectal body temperature, were monitored throughout each imaging session. Subjects were euthanized at the end of the experiment via isoflurane inhalation overdose at 5% and decapitation using a rodent guillotine. All the procedures were performed according to NIH animal research guidelines and were approved by our local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Vanderbilt University, and are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Creation of spinal cord injury

A 5–6 cm incision was made to expose the L1-L2 spinal cord segments, followed by a laminectomy on the dorsal side of the spine. An Infinite Horizon impactor (IH-0400, Precision Systems and Instrumentation, Kentucky, United States) was used to create a midline moderate contusion injury at the lumbar L1 segment. The injury was created using a 2 mm diameter probe, applying a constant force of 160 kDynes with a 1-s dwell time. Standard post-operative care included administration of analgesics (5–15 mg/kg carprofen), antibiotics (15–20 mg/kg Baytril), and manual expression of the bladder when necessary.

Riluzole treatment and 2-hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HBC) vehicle preparation and administration plan

We developed our Riluzole preparation procedures based on the methods provided in Wu et. al.11, and further described in our previous publication35. Riluzole was dissolved in 30% w/v of HBC to create a final stock of 8 mg/mL concentration. The final product was filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter. The Riluzole treatment group received 4 mg/kg of Riluzole every 12 h while the vehicle control group received HBC through intraperitoneal (IP) injections immediately post-injury for two weeks. The injection schedule, MRI and PET data acquistion, and behavioral testing timelines, are outlined in Fig. 9.

Experimental timeline of spinal cord injury procedure. Riluzole treatment or HBC vehicle was administered twice a day vis IP Injection after contusion injury for 2 weeks. MRI images were acquired at Weeks 1, 2, 4 and 8 post-injury. PET images were acquired at Weeks 1 and 3 post-injury. In vivo MRI data acquisition

All MRI scans acquired in the study were performed on a Bruker 9.4 T MRI pre-clinical system (Bruker Corporation, Massachusetts, United States). A customized 2 × 2 cm transmit-receive quadrature radiofrequency (RF) surface coil specifically designed for rat spinal cord imaging was used for MRI data acquisitions39. High-spatial resolution MTC-weighted structural images were acquired in all three imaging planes using an offset Gaussian RF preparation pulse (flip angle of 820° at 5000 Hz; TR/TE = 200/3 ms; FOV = 32 × 32 mm2; Matrix size = 256 × 256 [axial] or 128 × 128 [sagittal and coronal], Slice thickness = 3 mm (axial), 0.50 mm (coronal) and 0.75 mm (sagittal), flip angle = 35°). The same axial orientation structural image FOV was used for fMRI acquisitions. Resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) images were acquired using the same multi-shot gradient-echo EPI sequence used in our previous rat spinal cord studies30 (6 shots, TR/TE = 500/6.70 ms; FOV = 32 × 32 mm2; Matrix size = 128 × 128, Slice thickness = 5 slices × 3 mm, 180 volumes).

fMRI data analysis pipeline

The fMRI data analysis followed a processing pipeline developed previously for rat spinal cord resting-state fMRI data27,30, using a combination of MATLAB (Mathworks, Massachusetts, United States), FSL and SPM fMRI processing toolboxes. First, the fMRI images were pre-processed using rigid-body 3D motion correction in all 3 directional planes. Alignment and registration were performed using the high-resolution MTC contrast axial orientation anatomical images acquired in the same FOV. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to identify and regress out “nuisance” noise signals based on ROIs defined in the muscle, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), white matter, and all other non-spinal cord external signals. The first three to five signal principal components from the PCA output that accounted for at least 70% of the cumulative signal variance, and the previously calculated motion correction parameters, were used as signal regressors in a general linear model for further pre-processing of the fMRI BOLD signal data. A Chebyshev Type II bandpass filter was applied to the pre-processed fMRI data with a band-pass frequency range between 0.01 Hz to 0.13 Hz.

ROI-based analysis using anatomical structure informed ROI selection of gray matter horns was performed by correlating the averaged gray matter horn region of interest time-series vs. the averaged time-series in a different horn using Pearson’s correlation coefficient.

For statistical comparison, a 2-way ANOVA was first calculated to determine if injury progression time or administration of riluzole treatment are factors that significantly influenced the rsFC values. Next, the average correlation coefficients between the 4 gray matter horn regions from all fMRI acquisitions collected from both treatment and vehicle groups were further compared using non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, corrected for false-discovery rates. The injury epicenter slice 3 between L1-L2 was excluded from fMRI analysis, due to anatomical deformation and lack of gray matter-white matter structural contrast due to the injury. Slices 1 and 5 (approximately T13 and L3) were also excluded from the analysis, due to the decrease in SNR in these MRI slices acquired distant from the isocenter of the rat spinal cord surface coil.

Power spectral analysis was performed by calculating the fractional amplitude of low frequency fluctuations (fALFF), in order to identify potential intrinsic spinal cord neuronal activity and spontaneous activations40. This was executed in AFNI using the preprocessed rsfMRI data by defining the BOLD signal low frequency spectra range of 0.01 Hz to 0.08 Hz, and calculating the ratio of the low-frequency range BOLD signal power versus the BOLD signal power across the full frequency spectrum. The fALFF voxel maps were segmented using the same gray matter horn ROIs defined for functional connectivity analysis, to determine the average fALFF values for each gray matter horn. Similar to the functional connectivity analysis, a 2-way ANOVA was first calculated to determine if injury progression time or treatment administration are significant factors that influence rsFC values. Next, the gray matter horn fALFF values were compared between treatment and vehicle groups using non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, corrected for false-discovery rates.

In vivo PET data acquisition and analysis

Riluzole and HBC vehicle groups underwent PET scans at Week 1 and Week 3 post-injury. Rats were administered 18 MBqw of TSPO-ligand binding radiotracer, 7-chloro-N,N,5-trimethyl-4-oxo-3(6-[18F]fluoropyridin-2-yl)-3,5-dihydro-4H-pyridazino[4,5-b]indole-1-acetamide (18F-8302); compound 18F-8302 as referenced in Cheung et. al.41, via tail vein injection while under 2% isoflurane anesthesia. An hour post-injection, the rats were re-anesthetized and imaged for 30 min using an Inveon microPET (Siemens Preclinical Solutions, Pennsylvania, United States). Immediately after the PET scans, both the rat and the in-house designed rat bed were transferred to a NanoSPECT/CT (Bioscan, Washington DC, United States). A CT scan was acquired with an x-ray voltage of 45 kVp and a beam intensity of 90 mAs. Images were reconstructed into 170 × 170 × 414 slices with a voxel size of 0.04 × 0.04 × 0.04 cm3. The PET and CT images were rigidly co-registered using AsiPro (Siemens Preclinical Solutions). From the CT images, attenuation maps were generated. The PET data sets underwent correction for attenuation and scatter, and were then reconstructed using the OSEM3D method with 2 iterations, followed by MAP algorithm with 18 iterations at a beta value of 0.01. This yielded a resolution of 1.5 mm, producing 128 × 128 × 159 slices with a voxel size of 0.078 × 0.078 × 0.08 cm3. The PET/CT images were analyzed using Amide software (www.souceforge.net). The PET images were normalized based on the injected dose. Three 3-dimensional box ROIs were delineated around the apparent injury site on the spine, as well as above and below the injury site. Line profiles of radiotracer uptake along the spinal cord axis were generated to quantify the 18F-8302 uptake spatial distribution. Statistical comparisons between riluzole and HBC vehicle groups were performed using a non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Behavioral data acquisition and analyses

Hindlimb locomotor and somatosensory deficits after SCI were quantified using a battery of behavioral tests. Baseline measurements were collected pre-SCI, followed by data collection on Day 1 post-injury until 8 weeks post-injury. Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan (BBB) Locomotor Rating scores42and Open Field43 were used to measure aspects of locomotor function. Somatosensory functions, including tactile, thermal, and nociceptive responses, mechanical allodynia, and thermal hyperalgesia, were evaluated using Von Frey filament tests44 and hot plate latency44 measurements. Open field measured locomotor activity in an empty plexiglass open field chamber using a system of infrared beams. Movement in the chamber resulted in beam breaks, which were recorded as a measured movement activity value. Each rat was habituated in the open field chamber for at least one 30-min session. Baseline BBB scores were also collected for each hindlimb during the open field sessions. The BBB scoring system is the canonical method developed for quantifying locomotor function and coordination deficits post-SCI. Manual Von Frey filament tests were used to evaluate tactile response and mechanical allodynia in rats. The animals were placed in a small plexiglass chamber with a mesh grid floor underneath. Monofilaments of specific force values were applied perpendicularly to the hindpaw, and each filament was applied 10 times consecutively for 1 s durations (2 g, 4 g, 6 g, 8 g, 10 g, 15 g, 26 g, and 60 g). A positive response was recorded if the rat demonstrated paw withdrawal or any other physical stimulus response, such as licking or shaking their paw after the filament was applied. The hot plate test was used to measure thermal and nociceptive responses in the rats. The test was performed by placing the rats on a hot plate set at 55 °C, and the time to elicit a withdrawal or pain response was measured, with a 15 s time limit. The hot plate test was not performed during the initial 2 days post-injury, due to the administration of post-operative analgesics. Statistical comparison between treatment and vehicle groups at different time points was performed using a non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Post-mortem histological analysis

Animals (n = 2 treatment rats × 2 sections per stain, N = 2 vehicle rats × 2 sections per stain) were euthanized 8 weeks post-injury. Spinal cord tissues were perfused and fixed with 10% formalin, paraffin-embedded, transversally sectioned, and processed in our Translational Pathology Shared Resource Core. Histological staining included blue Hematoxylin for nuclei, brown DAB (3,3′-diaminobenzidine) for selected neuroinflammatory cell biomarkers of interest, and H&E (hematoxylin and eosin) for tissue microstructure. Immunostaining included iba-1 (ionized calcium-binding adaptor protein-1) for activated microglia45 and GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein) for astrocytes46.

Histological section images of the stained spinal cord tissues were captured at the Digital Histology Shared Resource Core at VUMC, using a high throughput Leica SCN400 Slide Scanner automated digital image system from Leica Microsystems. Whole slides were imaged at 20X magnification to a resolution of 0.5 µm/pixel. The open source analysis software QuPath47 was utilized for quantifying iba-1 positive activated microglia cells and GFAP positive astrocytes, by identifying the brown DAB “positive cells” from the blue hematoxylin “DAB negative cells”. The percentage of positive cells was identified for each section and were averaged across all cohort datasets, and compared between riluzole treatment and HBC vehicle sections using an unpaired-t test (2-way, alpha = 0.05).
