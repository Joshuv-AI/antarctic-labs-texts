Alcohol exposure induces ferroptosis-dominated programmed cell death in esophageal epithelial cells
Authors: Dongxue Wang, Zhenyu Jiang, Zhiyi Meng, Wei Hou, Tong Dang, Xianmei Meng, Jianyuan Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlcohol consumption has been a risk factor for nearly 200 diseases. Although swallowing an alcoholic beverage takes only a few seconds, the substance can stick to the esophageal lining longer after drinking. What alcohol does to the esophageal cells is not fully understood. This study explored the issue through in vitro experimentation.ResultsIt was found that < 0.5% alcohol or a < 30-minute exposure benefited cell growth primarily through autophagy, while > 0.5% alcohol or a > 30-minute exposure caused cell death, including apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis, but not pyroptosis. Autophagy took place within the first 30 min of alcohol exposure, followed by apoptosis, which peaked at 2 h, and then was gradually replaced by necroptosis as the alcohol exposure continued. Background

Alcohol consumption is a leading risk factor for global diseases and death, connected to ~ 200 diseases, including cancer [1–2]. According to the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study [3], there were 100.4 million new cases of alcohol-related diseases and 3 million alcohol-related deaths documented in 2016. The situation has not gotten any better in the last decade, especially in Eastern Asia, where the alcohol market has expanded by 104% from 1990 to 2017 [4–6].

Alcohol is primarily metabolized in the liver, where ethanol is converted into more harmless acetate. For this reason, the majority of published alcohol-related studies are about alcohol liver disease [7]. This process of alcohol detoxification causes an electron overflow in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, leading to a surge of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Excessive ROS production directly damages mitochondria, triggering autophagic activity to remove the damaged organelles [8]. As part of mitochondrial damage, mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) occurs to release mitochondrial contents, thereby inducing programmed cell death (PCD) [9–10]. The balance between cell survival and cell death determines whether alcohol consumption leads to a pathological condition.

PCD is a risk management system that helps maintain our internal health. When we face a challenge, regardless of whether it is environmental (e.g., radiation, pollution) or biological (e.g., infection, toxification), we can endure it by regulating cell survival and death, retaining the valuable cells while eliminating those that are unwanted. If the PCD system is paralyzed somehow, those unwanted cells accumulate, forming tumors. This is how a cancer develops. PCD can occur in various forms, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and others, which may act simultaneously or individually, depending on the underlying cause. Based on the literature, alcohol exposure can induce all of these PCDs in hepatic cells [8–9].

Alcohol consumption is harmful not only to the liver but also to other organs. The esophagus is the first organ that alcohol passes through when we drink. Although swallowing an alcoholic beverage takes only a few seconds, the substance can stick to the esophageal lining for several minutes or even longer after drinking. An average drinking event usually lasts several hours, during which the esophageal epithelium is constantly exposed to alcohol. So, what happens to the esophageal epithelial cells after a drinking event, besides the potential hazardous effects (if any) of the alcohol metabolites coming from the liver through blood circulation?

This study investigates the question by exposing esophageal epithelial cells to various concentrations of alcohol for different times.

Methods

Cell culture and reagents

Human esophageal epithelial cell lines Het1A (American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, USA) and HEEC (BeNa Culture Collection, Beijing, China) were maintained in KBM-2 medium supplemented with Lonza (Walkersville, USA). An equivalent number of cells was plated in 6-well plates (for protein isolation) or on coverslips (for cell staining) and cultured to 80% confluency for subsequent experiments. The cell cycle was then synchronized in basal medium for 6 h before ethanol treatment. For dose-dependency experiments, cells were cultured in the complete medium containing 0.5%, 5%, 13%, or 40% alcohol for 2 h. For time-dependent experiments, cells were incubated in 5% alcohol for 5–30 min, or 2, 6, or 12 h. Cells were harvested for assays after resting in the growth medium for an hour. Chloroquine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA, #C6628) was used at 10µM to block autophagy, Necrosulfonamide (C18H15N5O6S2) (Sigma-Aldrich, #480073) was used at 1 µM to block necroptosis, Ferrostatin-1 (TargetMol Chemicals, #T6500) was used at 1 µM to block ferroptosis, and Z-YVAD-FMK and Z-LEHD-FMK (Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, USA, #ALX-850-237) were used at 2µM to block apoptosis and pyroptosis, respectively.

Cell viability assay

Cell Counting Kit 8, or CCK-8 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), was used to assess cell viability after the indicated treatments according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, an equivalent number of cells was distributed per well in a 96-well plate at 100 µl/well. The plate was incubated at 37˚C and 5% CO2 for 24 h and then switched to the serum-free medium for 6 h. Five duplicated wells per condition. After the indicated treatments, 10 µl CCK-8 reagent was added to each well of the cell culture. The plate was then put back in the incubator for an additional 4 h. Cell viability was measured using a plate reader at 450 nm.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were cultured till the desired confluence on coverslips pre-coated with type I collagen. Two duplicated coverslips per condition. After serum starvation and indicated treatment, cells were fixed for 10 min in 4% paraformaldehyde. After incubation with a primary antibody for 2 h, cells were washed in PBS and incubated with a FITC- or Texas Red-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, USA) for an hour. Nuclei were counter-stained with DAPI (Invitrogen). Images were taken under a Nikon Eclipse Ni-E or Olympus BX41 microscope. Three views per coverslip were quantified. The following antibodies were used in cell staining: PCNA (OriGene Technologies, Inc. #TA800894), LC3B (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-271625), endoG (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-365359), GSDMD (Cell Signaling Technology, #69469), BAX (Invitrogen, #5K8 × 4), and MLKL (OriGene Technologies, Inc. #TA332779).

Iron cell staining

Cells were cultured till the desired confluence on coverslips pre-coated with type I collagen. Two duplicated coverslips per condition. Brussian Blue Iron Stain kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, #60533ES60) was used to detect intracellular iron. Two duplicated coverslips per condition. After indicated treatment, cells were fixed in the Fixation Buffer provided by the manufacturer for 15 min. After washing in tap water and then distilled water twice each, cells were incubated with Potassium Ferrocyanide Solution for 30 min and then in Nuclear Fast Red solution for 5 min. Cellular iron is stained in blue, while the rest of the cell is stained in red. Images were taken under a Nikon Eclipse Ni-E or Olympus BX41 microscope. Three views per coverslip were quantified.

BODIPY 581/591 cell staining

Cells were cultured till the desired confluence on coverslips pre-coated with type I collagen. Two duplicated coverslips per condition. A C11-BOGIPY 581/591 kit (Amgicam, Wuhan, China, #ajci64572) was used to detect lipid peroxidation following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, after indicated treatment, cells were incubated with the BODIPY reagent at 5µM for 30 min at 37℃ in the dark and washed with PBS 3 times. Images were taken under a Nikon Eclipse Ni-E microscope. Three views per coverslip were quantified.

Glutathione Peroxidase (GPX) assay

Cellular GPX activity was measured using a kit (Abcam, ab102530) following the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 2 × 10^6 cells per well. After the indicated treatment, cells were harvested and resuspended in 200 µl of Assay Buffer (provided by the manufacturer) and homogenized by pipetting. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 10,000 g for 15 min at 4℃. Fifty microliters of the collection from each well was combined with 40 µl of Reaction Mix (provided by the manufacturer) and incubated at room temperature for 15 min to deplete the oxidized glutathione disulfide. Ten microliters of cumene hydroperoxide (provided by the manufacturer) was added to each sample to measure GPX activity at 340 nm. GPX activity was measured again after incubation for another 5 min at 25℃. The difference between the two readings was applied to the NADPH standard curve to get the quantity of NADPH in each sample. GPX activity was calculated by dividing the amount of NADPH of each sample by 250.

ROS detection assay

ROS Detection Cell-Based Assay Kit (TCI, Shanghai, China, #I1265) was used to detect intracellular ROS. An equal number of cells was seeded in 96-well plates and cultured to 70% confluence. Five duplicated wells per condition. After indicated treatment, cells were washed in the Reaction Buffer provided by the manufacturer and stained with the Reaction Dye for 30 min in the dark. Cells were washed again, and the plates were read at 480/520 nm excitation/emission.

Protein isolation, immunoprecipitation (IP), and western blotting (WB)

Cells were cultured in petri-dishes till the desired confluence. Following synchronization and indicated treatment, total protein was extracted from cells using a modified RIPA lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 1% NP-40. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 4℃, 10,000 x g for 10 min. For immunoprecipitation of cellular protein extracts, 100 µg of each protein sample was adjusted to 1 µg/ml concentration and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with protein A-agarose beads (Invitrogen) coated with 1 µg of a specific antibody against MLKL. The beads were washed twice in the lysis buffer and boiled in the sample buffer for 5 min to release the protein attached. For conventional Western blotting, equal amounts of protein samples (30–50 µg each) were resolved in SDS-PAGE at 100 V and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane at 390 mA for 90 min. Subsequently, the membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with a primary antibody for 2 h at room temperature on a shaker. After three 10-minute washes with TBST buffer, the membrane was incubated with an appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, USA) on a shaker for 1 h. The signal was developed in ECL solution (Amersham Pharmacia, Netherlands), and images were captured and quantified using Gel Doc XR (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA). GAPDH or β-actin was used as the loading control. The data were collected from at least three independent experiments and analyzed for statistical significance. The following primary antibodies were used: Beclin-1 (Origene, #TA502643), PCNA, CASP1 (Abcam, #ab179515), CASP3 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-271759), CASP8 (Origene, #TA374288), CASP9 (Origene, #TA4227045), endoG, GPX4 (Origene, #TA423164M), GSDMD, IL-1β (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-12742), MLKL, phosphor-MLKL (Abcam, #ab196436), SLC7A11 (Origene, #TA423232), LC3B, BAX, GAPDH (OriGene, #TA800894), and β-actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. #sc-69879).

Statistical analysis

All numerical data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using single classification one-way ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Low concentrations of alcohol promote cell growth, but high concentrations promote cell death

Commercial alcoholic beverages are commonly classified into four categories based on their ethanol concentration: ≤ 3% (e.g., rio), ~ 5% (e.g., beer), ~ 13% (e.g., wine), and ≥ 40% (e.g., liquor). To determine how alcohol concentration affects esophageal cell growth, we cultured an equal number of cells for 2 h in either the complete medium (control) or the medium containing 0.5%, 5%, 13%, or 40% ethanol. Cell population was assessed using CCK-8 assays. As shown in Fig. 1A, cells grew better in the presence of 0.5% ethanol compared to the control; however, their viability fell at any other concentrations of alcohol tested, in a dose-dependent manner. After 2-hour exposure to 40% ethanol, ~ 75–80% cells went dead (P < 0.01, n = 5).

A short-term exposure to alcohol promotes cell proliferation, but a long-term exposure promotes cell death

Human esophageal epithelium is normally covered with a layer of mucus secreted by the underlying glands to protect the epithelial cells from any potentially harmful substance passing through the esophagus. For this reason, the ethanol in direct contact with the epithelial cells in vivo is much less concentrated than the ethanol in the actual drinks that are consumed. Nevertheless, the real physiological condition in vivo is impossible to reproduce precisely by in vitro experimentation. Based on our numerous tests, 5% alcohol seemed to be a more manageable concentration for our experiments. It should be remembered, though, that this does not imply our results are equivalent to the effects of drinking a beer. Bearing this in mind, we next measured cell viability after exposure to 5% alcohol for different times, namely, 5–30 min, or 2, 6, and 12 h. As shown in Fig. 1B, cell growth was actually accelerated during the first 30 min of alcohol exposure, with about 25 ± 3% more viable cells in the treatment group than in the control (P < 0.05, n = 5). However, the cell population sharply declined afterward. At 2 h of alcohol engagement, only 45% of the planted cells were alive. The surviving cells, however, seemed to regain their proliferative capability after that. After an overnight exposure to alcohol, the cell population nearly reached a comparable level to the control. This result was verified under the microscope (Fig. 1C), which showed that in the normal growth medium (control), cells formed a cobblestone monolayer; during the first 30 min of contact with alcohol, more and more cells dissociated from each other and rounded up while the cell population was obviously expanding; after 2 h alcohol exposure, many cells were lost, forming wider and wider intercellular gaps. After 6 h in contact with alcohol, though, the surviving cells started to proliferate, forming colonies and gradually filling the intercellular gaps with new cells.

Autophagy supports cell growth in the initial contact with alcohol

First, we wanted to know how alcohol exposure promoted cell growth within the first 30 min. Among all forms of PCD, autophagy is the only one that does not always result in cell death [11]. Instead, it mostly saves the life of the cell by consuming some non-essential or damaged cellular components of its own to get by certain cellular crises, such as starvation. Autophagy is a complex operation involving dozens of molecules, among which Beclin-1 initiates the formation of autophagosomes and microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 β (LC3B) brings autophagosomes to the lysosomes for degradation and recycling of the unwanted cell components [12–14]. Thus, we chose LC3B and Beclin-1 as markers to monitor autophagic activity.

We repeated the time-course experiment above on the coverslips and stained the cells for LC3B. As shown in Fig. 2A, more and more cells developed LC3B puncta in the initial 30 min of contact with alcohol, indicating a rise in autophagy. LC3B became barely detectable afterwards. When we added chloroquine (CQ) to block the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes (Fig. 2B and C), the cell viability at 30 min of alcohol exposure was dropped by 36 ± 5% compared to the alcohol treatment alone (P < 0.05, n = 6), suggesting that the alcohol-induced early cell growth depends on autophagy. To confirm this result, we stained cells for proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), an indicator of cell proliferation. As shown in Fig. 2D, after 30 min of alcohol exposure, nearly 95 ± 8% of the cells (both HEEC and Het1A) stained positive for nuclear PCNA, while inhibition of autophagy with CQ reduced proliferating cells to 36 ± 4% in HEEC and 14 ± 2% in Het1A (P < 0.01, n = 6). In addition, Western blot analyses displayed that 30 min of alcohol exposure increased LC3B levels by 3.0-fold in HEEC cells and 2.4-fold in Het1A cells, and increased PCNA by 3-fold in both HEEC and Het1A (Fig. 2E and G, P < 0.05, n = 3). In the presence of CQ, however, PCNA expression dropped by 80 ± 7% in HEEC and 50 ± 5% in Het1A (Fig. 2E and G, P < 0.05, n = 3), confirming that autophagy is essential for alcohol-stimulated cell proliferation. This conclusion is also supported by Beclin-1 accumulation in the presence of CQ, as it is normally degraded after the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes.

Apoptosis contributes to the cell death caused by long-term alcohol exposure, but it loses its role gradually

Next, we wanted to know which form(s) of PCD are involved in alcohol-induced cell death. Apoptosis can take place through either the intrinsic or extrinsic pathway. While the intrinsic apoptosis is initiated by mitochondrial permeabilization and CASP9 activation, the extrinsic apoptosis is triggered by CASP8 activation following a specific interaction between a death ligand and its cognate death receptor. Both pathways eventually activate CASP3, which can disable > 1300 essential cellular proteins, resulting in cell death [11, 15]. To determine whether alcohol exposure induces apoptosis in esophageal epithelial cells, we first examined whether alcohol exposure activates CASP8, CASP9, or CASP3 by Western blotting. After 2-hour treatment with 5% alcohol, CASP8 remained in its procaspase form (55 kDa), but CASP9 and CASP3 were cleaved to generate their active fragments, CASP9/p35 and CASP3/p17, respectively, suggesting that alcohol exposure may induce an intrinsic apoptosis (Fig. 3A and B). In the presence of Z-LEHD-FMK (LEHD), a CASP9 inhibitor, alcohol treatment failed to induce a cleavage in either CASP9 or CASP3, suggesting that CASP3 cleavage was mediated by CASP9 activation. To further validate this conclusion, we examined the subcellular localization of endoG, which is normally confined to the mitochondria but translocates to the nuclei to mediate DNA fragmentation in case of intrinsic apoptosis. Based on the immunofluorescence cell staining, 16 ± 3% of the cells showed nuclear localization of endoG after 2 h of alcohol exposure (P < 0.05, n = 6). However, this cell population shrank gradually as alcohol treatment continued (Fig. 3C and D), suggesting that the cells may have found a way to repair mitochondrial leakage. Furthermore, CCK-8 assays showed that blocking apoptosis with LEHD significantly improved cell viability by 36 ± 4% and 24 ± 3% in HEEC and Het1A, respectively, at 2 h of alcohol exposure (Fig. 3E and F, P < 0.05, n = 5), indicating that apoptosis contributed to the alcohol-induced cell death, but it weakened as the alcohol exposure extended.

Necroptosis contributes to the cell death caused by long-term alcohol exposure in a time-dependent manner

Necroptosis is mediated by the phosphorylation of mixed lineage kinase domain-like pseudokinase (MLKL), which oligomerizes on the cell membrane, causing cell leakage and breakdown, triggering inflammation [16]. To determine whether alcohol exposure induces necroptosis in esophageal epithelial cells, we repeated the time-course experiment on the coverslips and stained cells for MLKL. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, MLKL started to appear on the membrane of some cells as early as 30 min in contact with alcohol. Then more and more cells developed membrane localization of MLKL as the alcohol treatment continued, reaching 15 ± 3% of the cells after 12 h (P < 0.01, n = 6). Immunoprecipitation of MLKL demonstrated that, compared to control, in which MLKL presented as a monomer only (54 kDa), it showed not only the monomers but also the oligomers (> 100 kDa, Fig. 4C lower panel) in response to alcohol treatment, and also, only the oligomers were phosphorylated (Fig. 4C upper panel), suggesting that MLKL forms oligomers once phosphorylated. Western blot analyses of the input cell lysates confirmed the result (Fig. 4D). In the presence of Necrosulfonamide (NECS), a specific inhibitor of necroptosis, however, MLKL phosphorylation and oligomerization both became undetectable (Fig. 4C and D). In addition, CCK-8 assays demonstrated that blocking necroptosis with NECS improved cell viability by 18 ± 3% under alcohol exposure for 12 h (Fig. 4E and F, P < 0.05, n = 5), suggesting that necroptosis contributes to the cell death induced by long-term alcohol exposure. Unlike apoptosis, which grew weaker as the alcohol treatment continued, necroptosis became stronger.

Alcohol exposure does not trigger pyroptosis

Pyroptosis, like necroptosis, also induces cell lysis via cell membrane permeabilization. Unlike necroptosis, pyroptosis is mediated by the cleavages of CASP1, gasdermin D (GSDMD), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), rather than MLKL phosphorylation. Upon CASP1 activation, GSDMD is cleaved to generate an N-terminal fragment that oligomerizes on the cell membrane, resulting in the release of cytokines (e.g., IL-1β), triggering inflammation [17]. To determine whether alcohol exposure triggers pyroptosis in esophageal epithelial cells, we repeated the time-course experiment on the coverslips and stained the cells for GSDMD. As shown in Fig. 5A, GSDMD was never accumulated on the cell membrane compared to the positive control (Fig. 5A, lower panel), in which cells were exposed to lipopolysaccharides (LPS), a well-known pyroptosis inducer, suggesting that alcohol exposure may not induce pyroptosis. Furthermore, Western blot analyses did not show any cleavage in either GSDMD or CASP1 or IL-1β (Fig. 5B and E), supporting our conclusion that alcohol exposure did not cause pyroptosis. Also, adding Z-YVAD-FMK (YVAD), a pyroptotic inhibitor, to the cell culture did not significantly change the growth curve of the cells (Fig. 5F and G), compared to the alcohol treatment group without YVAD, confirming that pyroptosis was not a part of the alcohol-induced cell death.

Ferroptosis plays a major role in alcohol-induced cell death

Next, we examined whether alcohol exposure triggers ferroptosis. Ferroptosis occurs when excessive iron (Fe+++) accumulates in a cell, inducing ROS generation by the Fenton reaction. The excessive ROS reacts with the polyunsaturated fatty acids in the cell membrane, increasing lipid peroxidation and cell lysis [18]. Normally, solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) maintains a healthy level of intracellular glutathione, and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) converts intracellular lipid peroxidates into non-toxic fatty alcohol and H2O. Therefore, downregulation of these two molecules is a hallmark of ferroptosis. A deficiency in SLC7A11 leads to the downregulation and inactivation of GPX4. Thus, we first examined how alcohol exposure affects SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression in HEEC and Het1A cells. As illustrated in Fig. 6A and B, GPX4 levels in both cell lines started declining as soon as they were exposed to alcohol and kept falling rapidly as the alcohol exposure continued. After 2 h of alcohol exposure, GPX4 levels dropped by 70 ± 6% and 80 ± 8% in HEEC and Het1A cells, respectively (P < 0.01, n = 3). SLC7A11 was downregulated as well in response to alcohol (supplementary data Fig. S1A/B). Meanwhile, GPX activity was dropped correspondingly (supplementary data Fig. S1C). We also detected iron accumulation in cells exposed to alcohol by Prussian Blue staining. Unlike the cells in normal growth medium (Fig. 6C1), in which iron was nearly undetectable, the cells in contact with alcohol displayed iron staining immediately. It first appeared as blue dots near the nuclei while cells were still attached in a monolayer (Fig. 6C2). As the alcohol treatment continued, the cells dissociated from each other with iron increasingly accumulated around the nuclei (Fig. 6C3). When the cells were completely detached from each other and had become rounded up, their cytoplasm was filled with iron, surrounding their condensed nuclei (Fig. 6C4-5). Then the cells collapsed totally and the iron particles scattered out everywhere (Fig. 6C6-8). The cell population containing detectable iron, regardless of its density, increased by 5-fold and 10-fold in HEEC and Het1A, respectively, after 5 min of alcohol contact, and then came down by 19 ± 3% and 55 ± 9%, respectively, after 30 min of alcohol exposure, possibly due to autophagic activity. After 2 h of the alcohol treatment, however, the iron-stained cell population went up again, reaching a 7.88-fold increase in HEEC compared to the control, and getting to a similar level as it was at 5 min of the treatment in Het1A, reflecting a failure of self-rescue effort. Afterward, the number of cells with detectable iron declined, returning to the normal levels after 12 h of alcohol exposure (Fig. 6D and E), suggesting that the cells found a way to deal with the alcohol shock.

Next, we examined how the cellular iron responded to different concentrations of alcohol. We cultured cells in the complete medium containing 0.5%, 5.0%, 13%, or 40% ethanol for 2 h. As shown in Fig. 7A—7B, alcohol treatment upregulated cellular iron levels at any concentration tested, particularly at 5%, which increased the cell population containing detectable iron by 6-fold in HEEC cells and 8-fold in Het1A cells (P < 0.01, n = 6). On the other hand, the cellular ROS was upregulated by alcohol dose-dependently (Fig. 7C), suggesting that iron accumulation may not be the only source of ROS. When we added ferrostatin-1 (an inhibitor of ferroptosis) to the cell culture, levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 were restored (Supplementary data Fig. S1A/B), GPX activity was regained (Supplementary data Fig. S1C), lipid peroxidation returned to normal, and more importantly, cell viability was significantly improved (Fig. 7D and E, P < 0.05, n = 3), especially at 2 h of alcohol exposure.

Long-term alcohol exposure triggers anastasis

From the beginning of this study, we had noticed that the esophageal cells started to recover from the alcohol-induced cell death after 6 h of alcohol exposure and reconnected with each other gradually, forming regional monolayers after 12 h of alcohol exposure (Fig. 1). During this recovery process, fewer and fewer cells were engaged in apoptosis (Fig. 3). We wondered if anastasis was developed. Intrinsic apoptosis is usually triggered by BAX-mediated MOMPs. When MOMP is incomplete, cells may survive via anastasis, in which BAX can be found in the mitochondria along with the mitochondrial proteins such as endoG [19]. So, we examined the colocalization of BAX with endoG. As shown in Fig. 8A, BAX was elevated after 2 h of alcohol exposure compared to the control, but became almost undetectable after 12 h of alcohol exposure. BAX and endoG colocalization was started to appear in some cells from the 30-minute exposure and reached a peak at 2 h but came down at 12 h when BAX expression went down (Fig. 8B), indicating that the mitochondrial integrity was improving as the alcohol treatment extended, which is consistent with the results of apoptosis analyses (Fig. 3). This was also reflected by the change in the ratio of endoG to BAX in each cell as the alcohol exposure continued (Fig. 8C). In addition, Western blot analyses showed that BAX expression was upregulated by 4.3-fold and 6.8-fold in HEEC and Het1A cells, respectively, after in contact with alcohol for 2 h (Fig. 8D and E), but came down by 30 ± 5% and 70 ± 11% in HEEC and Het1A cells, respectively, after 12 h (P < 0.05, n = 3), indicating that cells recovered from the alcohol-induced apoptosis gradually as the alcohol exposure prolonged.

Discussion

The term “programmed cell death” (PCD) first appeared in the literature in 1965 when Lockshin & Williams described the cell replacement during metamorphosis of a silkworm [20], while we already knew as early as the 19th century that some cells die in a “programmed” manner to allow the whole organism to grow more healthily. Here, we should give some credit to a pioneer of PCD - cornification, a type of programmed cell death that occurs in keratinocytes exclusively. Cornification was studied years before the terminology of PCD was invented [21]. Since the term PCD was defined, this subject has rapidly attracted more and more researchers worldwide. We can get a general idea just by looking at the number of publications collected in PubMed. There were only 24 papers published on PCD in the 1st decade (1965–1975) after the term was coined but it hit 158 in the 2nd decade (1975–1985), 4,107 in the 3rd decade (1985–1995), and 93,999 in the 4th decade (1995–2005), increasing by 558%, 2,499%, and 2,189% respectively, each compared to the previous 10 years. In the recent two decades (up to October 31, 2025), the number of publications on the various forms of PCD has continued growing, reaching 203,050 (2005–2015) and 336,589 (2015–2025), respectively (Fig. 9), telling us that the topic of PCD still fascinates an increasing number of researchers worldwide after 60 years of extensive study. Of course, our knowledge on this subject has improved tremendously in both physiology and pathology. For instance, we now know that PCD can take place in many forms (apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, etc.), and each has its unique characteristics, making exceptional contributions to our health and sickness.

The number of publications on programmed cell death since 1965

PCD is a risk management system that we are born with. An average adult human body consists of ∼3.72 × 1013 cells, and every day nearly 1011 of them undergo PCD without our awareness [22]. As Gerry Melino (head of the Apoptosis and Cancer Laboratory, University of Leicester, UK) once said, without PCD, an 80-year-old person would have a 16 km long intestine and two tons of bone marrow and lymph nodes [23].

Cell survival and death are a constant battle taking place in our bodies. This is especially true under stress (e.g., alcohol, smoking). As mentioned earlier, the alcohol detoxification process in the liver can directly cause mitochondrial damage, leading to autophagy and apoptosis in hepatic cells [7–10]. Autophagy aims to save the cells, while apoptosis intends to kill them. Mitochondria originated from an ancient endosymbiotic bacterium billions of years ago; however, their contents are still foreign substances to our cells even today [24–25]. They can trigger an immune response once leaked. Over the long history of evolution, we have carefully retained these symbionts within our cells only as an energy provider. Any damage to this organelle can cause severe consequences. To name a few, cytochrome c, once in the cytosol, can activate a chain reaction, from CASP9 to CASP3 to apoptosis; endoG, once in the nucleus, can break down DNA; AIF (apoptosis-inducing factor), once in the nucleus, can induce parthanatos, another form of PCD independent from caspase involvement.

Based on our study of esophageal epithelial cells, autophagy was active only within the first 30 min of alcohol exposure (Fig. 2), followed by a peak of apoptosis (Fig. 3), as the essential components of autophagy (e.g., Beclin-1) are substrates of CASP3 [26]. Then apoptosis was gradually replaced by necroptosis, which also suppresses autophagy [27]. That is why autophagy disappeared after 2 h of alcohol exposure. Apoptosis depends on caspase activation, while necroptosis is inhibited by caspase activation. This explains why necroptosis grew stronger while apoptosis faded away when alcohol exposure continued (Figs. 3 and 4). Theoretically, alcohol ingestion increases intestinal permeability and provides the opportunity for microorganism infection, releasing LPS into the portal circulation to activate pyroptosis [28–29]. However, in our study, we did not detect pyroptosis in esophageal cells after alcohol exposure (Fig. 5). This might be due to the in vitro study method. Even if there is a microorganism invasion in the gut, it would not get to the esophageal cells in cell culture.

Based on our data, ferroptosis seemed to be the main cause of esophageal cell death in response to alcohol. Ferroptosis is triggered by intracellular accumulation of iron, which is common in patients with chronic alcohol consumption [30]. Precisely, our study found that iron started to appear inside esophageal cells almost immediately when they came into contact with alcohol, and it accumulated as alcohol exposure continued until the cells were broken down (Figs. 6 and 7). On the other hand, we also found that after autophagy failed to save the stressed cells, the cells continued to struggle to revive from the edge of apoptosis via anastasis (Fig. 8). After 12 h of alcohol exposure, the surviving cells behaved like normal epithelial cells, establishing monolayer patches in the culture vessels (Fig. 1).

Between autophagy and ferroptosis is another story. Normally, iron is taken up by cells in the ferrous form and stored within the spherical cavities formed by the ferritin complexes. In the presence of alcohol, autophagy can sensitize cells to ferroptosis by degrading ferritin, but it can also undermine ferroptosis by removing lipid peroxides [31]. In our study, these two events took place almost simultaneously at the beginning of alcohol exposure. At 2 h of alcohol exposure, however, autophagy turned out to be barely detectable while ferroptosis became increasingly active. This is likely due to multiple mechanisms, as long-term exposure of hepatocytes to alcohol reduced mTOR activation [32], elevated lysosome pH [33], impaired lysosomal enzyme trafficking [34], and inhibited the transcription of the essential molecules for lysosome biogenesis and autophagy [35]. Nevertheless, iron progressively accumulated as alcohol exposure continued, resulting in more ferroptotic cell death and making ferroptosis the dominant form for eliminating the irreparable cells. Yet, life is tenacious [36]. Under constant alcohol exposure, some esophageal cells developed the ability to escape death. Anastasis was one of the living strategies for these cells, if not the only one. These cells found a way to downregulate BAX (Fig. 8), removing the threat to mitochondrial integrity. However, we have to be cautious about these cells as they have experienced mitochondrial stress when they were exposed to alcohol. Even if it was just a minority of MOMP, these cells are no longer the same as before. They might carry genomic mutations because CASP3 activation, endoG/AIF nuclear translocation, and others can alter the cell genotype to some extent. Therefore, these surviving cells can be the potential seeds for esophageal malignancy. This can be an interesting topic for future studies.

Finally, we have to remind our readers of the limitations of this study, as it is based on in vitro experimentation almost exclusively. The results may not reflect the actual physiology of the people after drinking alcohol. Nevertheless, it at least gives us a perspective on how esophageal cells respond to alcohol exposure.

Drinking alcohol has a long history in human culture. Despite the awareness of its harmful effects on our health, the advocate of teetotalism has never gotten too far. For a lot of people, drinking alcohol is still a big part of their daily activity today. We hope this study can raise more awareness of alcoholism and improve our life expectancy more healthily and objectively.

Conclusions

Low concentrations of alcohol or a short exposure promote esophageal cell growth owing to autophagy, while higher concentrations or a longer exposure cause multiple forms of cell death, including apoptosis, necroptosis, and particularly, ferroptosis, which is the dominant force to limit cell growth under alcohol influence. Yet, cells can recover from alcohol insult through anastasis as the exposure extends, which could be a contributor to esophageal malignancy.
