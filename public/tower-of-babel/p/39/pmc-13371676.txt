Organizational silence and work alienation: a study on public hospital health employees
Authors: Damla Envergil, Macide Artac Ozdal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to examine the relationship between organizational silence and work alienation among healthcare employees and to explore whether these variables differ according to sociodemographic characteristics. This cross-sectional study was conducted among health employees working in public hospitals in Northern Cyprus. The study population consisted of 1,755 employees, and 446 participants were included in the sample. Data were collected using the Organizational Silence Scale, the Work Alienation Scale, and a sociodemographic information form. Statistical analyses included correlation analysis to assess relationships between subdimensions. The findings indicated significant relationships between several subdimensions of organizational silence and work alienation. A weak negative correlation was found between defensive silence and prosocial silence, as well as between prosocial silence and acquiescent silence. A moderate positive relationship was observed between defensive and acquiescent silence. Among the work alienation subdimensions, powerlessness was positively associated with meaninglessness, and meaninglessness was positively related to self-estrangement. Additionally, prosocial silence showed a low-level positive correlation with powerlessness. The present results indicate that organizational silence is associated with dimensions of work alienation among health employees. Healthcare managers should address factors contributing to silence and alienation and implement strategies to enhance employees’ sense of meaning and engagement at work. Future research should consider cultural and contextual differences when assessing work alienation in healthcare settings.

Introduction

Today, the incessant evolution of technology, relentless competition, and detrimental managerial attitudes towards employees have resulted in the neglect of the reality that employees are social beings and are crucial to organizations [1].

Organizational silence is the failure of employees to openly express their views and ideas on organizational issues or problems and to keep them to themselves [2]. When employees feel hopeless about organizational goals or processes and believe that they cannot change anything, they become dissatisfied with their jobs and become silent [3].

According to Van Dyne et al. (2003) [4], individuals’ behaviour of remaining silent is often perceived by other individuals as not actively communicating. Pinder and Harlos (2001) [5], who consider silence as a reaction to injustice, define employee silence as withholding one’s sincere thoughts about behavioural, cognitive and/or emotional evaluations of issues related to organizational situations from people who are perceived to have the ability to affect/correct change.

In the literature on organizational silence, Morrison and Milliken (2000) [2] and Pinder and Harlos (2001) [5] made a binary classification of silence types, whereas Van Dyne et al. (2023) [4] made a triple distinction. Van Dyne et al. used the work of Pinder and Harlos in the design of their classification and added a third type, which they determined as prosocial silence, to the two types of silence they presented. In the present study, organizational silence is conceptualized within the three-dimensional framework of acquiescent silence, defensive silence, and pro-social silence. Acquiescent silence represents a passive and resignation-based form of withholding behavior, in which employees refrain from expressing their ideas, knowledge, or concerns due to the belief that voicing them will not lead to meaningful change in the existing conditions [6]. In contrast, defensive silence reflects a more intentional and self-protective orientation; individuals deliberately choose to remain silent out of fear of potential negative repercussions, even when they are aware of viable alternatives that could improve the situation [5]. Pro-social silence, however, is grounded in relational and collective considerations. It involves the conscious withholding of work-related ideas, information, or opinions in order to protect organizational interests or maintain interpersonal harmony, reflecting an other-oriented and cooperative motive [4]. This tripartite conceptualization underscores that employee silence is not a unitary construct but rather a multidimensional phenomenon shaped by distinct motivational bases. Accordingly, acquiescent silence is associated with submission and disengagement, defensive silence is driven by fear and self-preservation, and pro-social silence—also referred to as silence for the benefit of the organization—is characterized by the intentional withholding of work-related input out of concern for organizational or others’ interests [4].

Work alienation is the result of the behaviours and distress experienced by employees with regard to other individuals within the business and the loss of a sense of belonging [7]. According to Clark, alienation is explained as “the psychological state of an individual who is alienated from social interactions physically, mentally, or both” [8]; Seeman defined alienation as a situational phenomenon rather than a behaviour or personality trait [9].

Alienated employees break cognitive and emotional ties to their work [10]. If employees in organizations do not find what they expect in their workplaces, if they may believe that they are seen as only robots or machines engaged in production, or if they are faced with managers’ strict or harsh behaviours, they may react by becoming alienated or exhibiting aggressive behaviours (cynicism). Dissatisfaction experienced in the workplace can also be characterized as failure to resolve employee complaints; arbitrary, private and unfair behaviours of managers; and the negative feelings that develop because of the above, lack of workplace discipline, work unrest and other dissatisfaction [11].

Building upon the multidimensional understanding of organizational silence, it is essential to examine its potential implications for employees’ psychological relationship with their work, particularly in relation to work alienation. Although the concept of work alienation has been extensively examined in the organizational behavior literature [10, 12, 13], its antecedents—especially communication-related factors such as organizational silence—have received comparatively limited empirical attention [2, 5].

In the present research, work alienation is conceptualized as a multidimensional construct reflecting employees’ psychological disconnection from their work, operationalized through the three subdimensions proposed by Mottaz (1981): powerlessness, meaninglessness, and self-alienation [13]. The powerlessness dimension refers to employees’ perceived lack of control over work-related decisions; meaninglessness reflects difficulties in understanding the purpose or significance of one’s role; and self-alienation denotes a sense of detachment from work and an inability to fully engage oneself in job activities [12]. Although widely examined in organizational research, work alienation is not culturally neutral and may vary across social and institutional contexts [10, 13]. Empirical studies conducted in non-Western and public-sector settings have highlighted variability in its manifestation and measurement, underscoring the importance of context-sensitive investigations [10, 14]. Organizational silence may be associated with employees perceiving themselves as powerless and insignificant within the organization. Such perceptions undermine employees’ sense of control over their work environment and diminish the perceived significance of their roles, fostering feelings of powerlessness and meaninglessness [12]. Prolonged exposure to organizational silence can consequently prompt individuals to psychologically withdraw from their work roles, thereby exacerbating work alienation. Empirical studies support this relationship, demonstrating that organizational silence is positively correlated with work alienation and its core dimensions, especially powerlessness and meaninglessness [14, 15]. Thus, organizational silence can be regarded as a significant antecedent of work alienation within organizational contexts [2].

The healthcare sector entails substantial emotional labor and continuous interpersonal demands. Within this context, organizational silence may serve as a coping mechanism, inhibiting employees from expressing concerns and reporting issues. However, prolonged silence can result in psychological withdrawal and diminished job commitment [2, 16]. These observations conceptually correspond with the experience of job alienation among health employees.

The main traits observed in alienated individuals include a lack of innovation, dissatisfaction, fatalism, questioning the meaning of life, a deficiency in moral values, and extreme egoism. Employees who feel alienated view their jobs merely as obligations. This alienation creates an unhappy and uncomfortable environment for them and for those around them, and they often do not make efforts to improve this situation [13]. Alienated employees, including middle managers, tend to be uninterested in the quality of the products or services their business offers. Instead, they focus on the negative aspects of the work processes they experience. This mindset can lead to conflict within the organization. To address these issues and help businesses achieve their goals within the framework of their organizational culture, it is essential to improve the quality of employees’ work life and reduce their alienation from their work [14].

Existing research has largely treated organizational silence and work alienation as distinct constructs; however, investigations into their interrelationship, particularly within healthcare organizations, remain relatively limited. Cross-cultural research indicates that both organizational silence and work alienation are shaped by cultural and institutional contexts. Studies conducted in high power-distance and collectivist cultures indicate that employees are more likely to withhold opinions and refrain from voice behaviors due to hierarchical norms and fear of negative consequences [2, 5]. Similarly, research in non-Western and public-sector settings has demonstrated that experiences of work alienation may manifest differently, as employees’ perceptions of powerlessness and meaning are embedded in culturally specific norms regarding authority, job security, and organizational loyalty [7, 10]. These observations suggest that the relationship between organizational silence and work alienation cannot be assumed to be universal and may vary across cultural and organizational contexts.

Considering the communication-intensive and emotionally demanding nature of healthcare work, this paucity of empirical evidence represents a significant research gap, especially in under-explored regions such as Northern Cyprus. Therefore, this research aims to examine the relationship between organizational silence and work alienation among healthcare employees working in public hospitals in Northern Cyprus, and to test the proposed hypotheses derived from the relevant literature.

Beyond this primary objective, the present study makes several contributions to the existing literature. First, the study adopts a multidimensional perspective by examining organizational silence and work alienation through their respective subdimensions, enabling a more nuanced understanding of their interrelationships [4, 12]. Second, it provides a detailed analysis of how different forms of organizational silence (acquiescent, defensive and prosocial) and associated with distinct dimensions of work alienation (powerlessness, meaninglessness, and self-estrangement), thereby responding to calls for more fine-grained analyses in the literature [17]. Third, it extends existing research by focusing on the healthcare sector, where communication demands, hierarchical structures, and high emotional labor may intensify both silence and alienation [18]. By including a wide range of occupational groups within hospitals, the study offers a more comprehensive perspective compared to prior research that has typically focused on specific professional categories.

Finally, by examining these constructs within an under-researched context, the study provides context-specific insights and contributes to a better understanding of how organizational silence and work alienation are manifested in practice. In addition, the study offers insight into the potential mechanisms linking silence behaviors to alienation outcomes, consistent with theoretical perspectives emphasizing motivation and psychological processes underlying employee behavior [17, 18].

Theoretical framework

Social Exchange Theory provides an important perspective for understanding relationships between employees and organizations. According to this theory, workplace relationships are based on reciprocal exchanges of resources such as support, recognition, and opportunities for participation [19, 20]. Within this framework, employees expect that their ideas, opinions, and contributions will be valued by the organization. Organizational silence refers to employees’ intentional withholding of ideas, opinions, or concerns related to organizational issues [4]. When employees perceive that expressing their views may lead to negative consequences or that their voice will not influence organizational outcomes, they may choose to remain silent. Such conditions may indicate an imbalance in the exchange relationship between employees and the organization and may reduce employees’ psychological investment in their work. Over time, this imbalance may contribute to work alienation. Work alienation represents a psychological state in which employees experience feelings of powerlessness, meaninglessness, and self-estrangement in relation to their work roles [12, 13]. When employees are unable to express their ideas or participate in organizational decision-making processes, they may perceive limited control over their work environment, which may foster feelings of powerlessness. Similarly, the inability to contribute to organizational outcomes may diminish the perceived meaning of their work and encourage psychological withdrawal from work roles [2]. Acquiescent silence, which reflects passive acceptance of existing organizational conditions, may reinforce feelings of powerlessness because employees believe that speaking up will not lead to meaningful change [4, 5]. Defensive silence, which is motivated by fear of negative consequences, may lead employees to psychologically distance themselves from their work roles, contributing to self-estrangement [2, 4]. In contrast, prosocial silence is typically motivated by the intention to protect colleagues or the organization and may therefore have a weaker association with work alienation [4]. Based on the theoretical framework and the existing literature on organizational silence and work alienation, the following hypotheses were developed:

H4g: There are weaker relationships between prosocial silence and alienation dimensions.

Materials and methods

Study design

This research is an analytical cross-sectional study conducted in public hospitals in Northern Cyprus from January 26–May 4, 2022. Participants were selected using a convenience sampling method due to practical constraints such as accessibility and organizational conditions within the hospitals. The sample size was determined using a 95% confidence interval and a 5% margin of error, resulting in 446 participants from a total target population of 1,755 health employees. Analytical cross-sectional studies aim to gather data on exposures and outcomes at a certain point in time and enable precise comparisons of disparities in results between exposed and unexposed individuals [15].

Data collection and participants

The population of the study consisted of physicians, nurses, midwives, other health employees and administrative staff working in public hospitals. The population comprised healthcare professionals working in five public hospitals in Northern Cyprus: Dr. Burhan Nalbantoğlu State Hospital, Gazimağusa State Hospital, Dr. Akçiçek Hospital, Cengiz Topel Hospital, and Barış Mental and Neurological Diseases Hospital. These hospitals are located in the districts of Lefkoşa, Girne, Gazimağusa, and Güzelyurt.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study included all healthcare employees actively working in the selected public hospitals during the data collection period, regardless of occupational role. This inclusive approach was adopted to capture a comprehensive representation of the hospital workforce. Participation was voluntary, and individuals who were not actively working during the data collection period (e.g., on leave) or who declined to participate were excluded.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected using a single structured questionnaire administered in both online and paper-based formats to maximize accessibility and participation. The online version of the questionnaire was distributed via institutional communication channels, while printed versions were delivered directly to hospital units. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study, and confidentiality and anonymity were assured. No identifying information was collected, and participation was entirely voluntary. The use of mixed-mode data collection (online and paper-based) is recommended to improve response rates and reduce coverage bias [21].

Organizational Silence Scale: The organizational silence scale used in this study was adapted from the Organizational Silence Scale developed by Van Dyne, Ang, and Botero (2003), which consists of three types of silence: acquiescent silence, defensive silence, and prosocial silence. The scale includes a total of 15 items, with five items for each subdimension (acquiescent silence: items 1–5; defensive silence: items 6–10; prosocial silence: items 11–15). Responses were measured using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Scale scores were calculated by averaging the item responses, with possible scores ranging from 1 to 5. Higher scores indicate stronger levels of organizational silence, while lower scores indicate weaker levels of silence. Previous studies have reported acceptable reliability for the scale, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients generally above 0.70. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the organizational silence scale was 0.776.

Work Alienation Scale: Work alienation was measured using the Work Alienation Scale developed by Mottaz (1981). The scale consists of 21 items and includes three subdimensions: powerlessness (items 1–7), meaninglessness (items 8–14), and self-estrangement (items 15–21). Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The scale scores were calculated by averaging the item responses, with possible scores ranging from 1 to 5. Higher scores reflect greater levels of work alienation, whereas lower scores reflect lower levels of alienation. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the work alienation scale was 0.519.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). A statistical significance level of p < 0.05 was accepted for all analyses. General information about the participants is presented in frequency tables, with the number of cases.

(S) and percentages (%). Information about the scales was analysed according to the categories of all independent variables, and the scores are reported as mean (M), standard deviation (SD), and median values. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. The Shapiro–Wilk test was preferred for small to moderate sample sizes due to its higher power in detecting deviations from normality, whereas the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to larger samples [22, 23]. Levene’s test was used to assess homogeneity of variances. Based on these results, appropriate parametric or nonparametric statistical methods were selected. Since the data did not meet parametric distribution assumptions, nonparametric tests were used. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied for two-category independent variables, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for variables with more than two categories. In cases where significant differences were found, pairwise comparisons were conducted. Relationships between scales were examined using the Spearman correlation test. Reliability analyses were performed to assess internal consistency. The organizational silence scale demonstrated acceptable reliability (α = 0.776). In contrast, the work alienation scale showed lower reliability (α = 0.519). The Cronbach’s alpha values for the work alienation subdimensions were low, with the highest reaching 0.31, indicating limited internal consistency at the subdimension level. Corrected item–total correlation analyses were also conducted, but all correlations were below the commonly recommended threshold of 0.30, suggesting weak associations between individual items and the overall construct. Given these psychometric limitations, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was considered methodologically inappropriate and therefore was not performed. Instead, the results were interpreted with caution, and the limitations related to the measurement properties of the work alienation scale were explicitly acknowledged in the discussion section.

Sampling considerations

A convenience sampling approach was employed to facilitate access to a diverse group of healthcare employees within organizational settings where controlled sampling procedures are often difficult to implement. This approach allowed the inclusion of participants from various professional roles, thereby providing a broad perspective on the phenomena under investigation.While convenience sampling may impose certain limitations regarding representativeness, it is widely used in organizational research under similar practical constraints [24]. Therefore, the outcomes should be interpreted within this context.

Results

The sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. The sample was predominantly female (70.4%) and married (63.0%), with most participants aged 40 years or younger (61.4%). More than half of the participants (62.1%) reported having children. Regarding educational background, a considerable proportion held postgraduate degrees (37.9%), while 15.7% had completed medical school and/or obtained a medical specialty qualification. In addition, 30.3% had a four-year university degree and 13.5% held an associate degree. Approximately one-fifth of the participants (21.7%) reported working in hospital management positions. In terms of geographical distribution, 55.2% of the participants were employed in Nicosia, followed by Famagusta (19.7%), Kyrenia (13.0%), and Güzelyurt (12.1%). The sample included both physicians (19.3%) and nurses (19.3%), alongside other healthcare and administrative personnel. With regard to professional characteristics, the majority of respondents (37.0%) had 6–19 years of work experience. A total of 22.2% reported working in both day and night shifts, while 17.3% worked exclusively in shifts. Nearly one-third of the participants (29.4%) reported caring for 56 or more patients per day. Finally, 51.3% of participants were permanent staff, and 46.4% reported a low monthly income, whereas 21.3% reported a high income level.

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

The results presented in Table 2 indicate that there were no statistically significant differences in organizational silence dimensions according to age, gender, nationality, marital status, or parental status (all p > 0.05), suggesting that these sociodemographic variables were not associated with variations in silence behaviors. However, statistically significant differences were observed in relation to educational status. Specifically, defensive silence (p = 0.003) and acquiescent silence (p < 0.001) varied significantly across education levels. Participants with higher educational attainment generally reported lower acquiescent silence scores, indicating a weak-to-small effect of education on silence behaviors. Similarly, defensive silence showed minor variation across educational groups, suggesting limited practical magnitude despite statistical significance. A significant difference was also found according to place of work for both defensive silence (p = 0.011) and acquiescent silence (p < 0.001). Employees working in Güzelyurt reported higher levels of both dimensions compared to other regions, although the differences indicate small effect sizes, suggesting limited but notable regional variation. Regarding occupational groups, prosocial silence differed significantly across professions (p = 0.030). Nurses reported lower prosocial silence compared to administrative staff, indicating a small effect size and suggesting that occupational role may have a limited but meaningful influence on prosocial silence behaviors. Total work experience was significantly associated with acquiescent silence (p = 0.032), with a slight decrease in acquiescent silence observed among more experienced employees. However, the effect size was small, indicating that the practical impact of experience is limited. Working style was significantly associated with both prosocial silence (p = 0.045) and acquiescent silence (p = 0.013). Employees working day shifts reported slightly higher prosocial silence, whereas shift workers demonstrated higher acquiescent silence. Nevertheless, these differences were small in magnitude. Finally, monthly income level was significantly associated with acquiescent silence (p = 0.014). Employees with lower income levels exhibited slightly higher acquiescent silence compared to higher-income employees, although the effect size suggests a weak association.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between organizational silence and work alienation in healthcare staff employed in public hospitals in Northern Cyprus. The variability observed in organizational silence and work alienation across sociodemographic and professional characteristics suggests that healthcare employees’ perceptions and behavioral responses may be associated with individual-level resources and organizational-structural conditions. Differences associated with education level, occupational role, work experience, employment pattern, and income level may be indicative of disparities in access to decision-making processes, differentiated expectations regarding professional autonomy, and varying degrees of exposure to organizational demands and pressures. Importantly, the findings of this study support the view that organizational silence should not be treated as a uniform construct. Instead, different forms of silence appear to be associated with different dimensions of work alienation, highlighting the importance of a multidimensional approach. This finding is consistent with prior research suggesting that distinct motivational bases drive silence behaviours and may lead to different psychological outcomes [4, 17]. Overall, the results suggest that the consequences associated with silence may vary depending on underlying motivational factors.

The strong positive correlation between defensive and acquiescent silence (r = 0.560, p < 0.001) suggests that fear-based and resignation-based silence may co-occur in healthcare settings where hierarchical structures and perceived lack of voice opportunities are prominent. This pattern suggests that employees who refrain from speaking due to fear of negative consequences may also show higher levels of passive acceptance of organizational conditions. Furthermore, the interrelationships among the subdimensions of organizational silence highlight the non-uniform and multifaceted nature of silence behaviors within healthcare organizations. In contrast, prosocial silence exhibits a distinct relational pattern, suggesting that silence enacted for altruistic or organizationally oriented reasons may not necessarily be associated with psychological withdrawal or disengagement. Consistent with this pattern, a small but statistically significant positive correlation was found between prosocial silence and powerlessness (r = 0.124, p < 0.05), indicating that even silence motivated by concern for others may be related to perceptions of reduced influence in organizational processes. Additionally, the associations among powerlessness, meaninglessness, and self-estrangement provide empirical support for conceptualizing work alienation as an interconnected and cumulative psychological experience rather than a set of isolated dimensions. The observed relationships suggest that these dimensions of alienation are interrelated within the studied context.

An additional finding that deserves further interpretation concerns the relationship between prosocial silence and the powerlessness dimension of work alienation. While prosocial silence is generally conceptualized as withholding information in order to protect colleagues or the organization [4], the positive association observed in this study suggests that silence motivated by prosocial concerns may also coexist with feelings of reduced influence over organizational decisions. In highly structured healthcare environments, employees may choose to remain silent to maintain professional harmony or avoid potential conflict with superiors, even when they perceive problems in organizational processes. Such dynamics may be associated with perceptions of limited control, which in turn are reflected in feelings of powerlessness. These outcomes can be interpreted through underlying psychological mechanisms linking organizational silence to work alienation. When employees refrain from expressing their views, they may experience reduced control over organizational processes, which may be associated with feelings of powerlessness [25]. At the same time, silence driven by fear or resignation may be associated with psychological withdrawal, thereby contributing to self-estrangement [12, 18]. In contrast, prosocial silence, which is motivated by concern for others, may not involve the same disengagement processes [17].

Building on these mechanisms, organizational silence can be further interpreted as a multidimensional construct with distinct motivational bases. Some relationships between silence and alienation subdimensions were non-significant or weak, further reinforcing the differentiated nature of these constructs. Acquiescent silence, characterized by passive acceptance, appears more closely associated with powerlessness and meaninglessness, whereas defensive silence, driven by fear and self-protection, is more strongly linked to self-estrangement. Prosocial silence, however, reflects an other-oriented motive and therefore shows a differentiated pattern of association with alienation dimensions [4]. Overall, these results suggest that the psychological consequences of silence may vary depending on its underlying motivational structure. Although several statistically significant relationships were identified, most correlation coefficients indicated small to moderate effect sizes, suggesting that the practical strength of these relationships is limited. The generally small effect sizes observed may reflect the complex and multifactorial nature of both organizational silence and work alienation, which are influenced by broader organizational culture, leadership style, and psychological safety conditions.

In the study, a statistically significant difference was observed between the education status of health employees and powerlessness scores, and higher education levels were associated with higher powerlessness scores. A literature review revealed that health employees who graduated from high school (x̄=3.006) experienced higher levels of powerlessness and determined whether there was a significant difference between education and this dimension (p < 0.05) [16]. Another study revealed that health employees’ total work alienation score differed significantly depending on their education level [26]. Söyük and Şengün (2016) also studied workplace alienation among health employees and observed a relationship with employees’ education level [27].

Another finding of the study is that the place of work had a statistically significant effect on meaninglessness (p < 0.05). No study has considered the possibility that the alienation behaviours of health employees in Northern Cyprus may vary according to the city where the individuals work. The behaviours of individuals who feel alienated from their work may vary depending on their thoughts about the work they are doing, which are shaped by factors such as the facilities available in the city hospital, the density of the hospital, and the management style.

When the health employees’ scores were compared by occupation, the powerlessness and meaninglessness scores differed significantly. The powerlessness scores of physicians were higher than those of individuals in other occupations. The meaninglessness scores of the nurses were high, while the meaninglessness scores of the administrative staff were low. Similarly, previous studies have shown that health employees’ total work alienation scores differ significantly depending on their position [26, 28, 29].

A statistically significant difference between health employees’ total work experience and powerlessness scores were observed. A positive association was observed between total work experience and powerlessness scores. Comparatively, a literature review revealed that nurses who had worked in the profession for fewer years had a higher level of alienation from their work (p < 0.05) [30].

It was shown that income had a statistically significant association with powerlessness and self-estrangement. Higher income levels were associated with higher powerlessness scores. This finding is consistent with a previous study that reported significant differences in work alienation levels based on income status [31].

Analysis of the research outcomes suggests that participants tended to exhibit acquiescent silence less frequently as their level of education rose. Similarly, one study reported that levels of organizational silence differed significantly according to education level; it was found that higher education levels were associated with higher levels of organizational silence among employees [29]. A statistically significant difference was identified between the levels of defensive silence and acquiescent silence among healthcare workers based on the workplace (the city) in which they worked. For instance, healthcare workers in the Güzelyurt region reported higher scores of acquiescent silence. Additionally, a statistically significant difference was detected when prosocial silence levels were examined on the basis of occupation type, where nurses reported lower prosocial silence scores. The results of one study revealed that compared with technicians, health employees who were doctors, nurses, and secretaries exhibited higher levels of organizational silence [29]. Another study conducted in public hospitals revealed that nurses, midwives, and health officers tend to present silence behaviours more than other healthcare workers do. This phenomenon may be attributed to several factors: these professionals are in direct and intense contact with patients and their relatives, they work in high-stress and high-pressure environments, and they are involved in nearly every aspect of activities aimed at improving patient health. Additionally, their frequent communication with other healthcare professionals may contribute to this quieter demeanour [32].

The study also revealed a statistically significant correlation between employees’ total work experience and their tendency to exhibit acquiscent silence; specifically, as years of experience increased, their acquiscent silence scores decreased. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that employee silence behaviours vary according to individual and job-related characteristics such as professional experience and seniority. Furthermore, the present study identified significant differences in the working styles of healthcare workers in terms of their tendencies to display prosocial and acquiescent silence. Participants who worked continuously throughout the day reported higher prosocial silence scores, whereas those who worked shifts reported greater levels of acquiescent silence. Although research directly examining the effects of work schedules on silence behaviours is limited, studies in healthcare contexts have emphasized that work environment characteristics, such as job demands, shift work, and psychological safety, are related to employees’ willingness to express concerns versus remaining silent [33, 34]. These findings are consistent with the notion that employees in more stable or continuous work settings may engage more in prosocial silence due to stronger interpersonal bonds and perceptions of support, while those in shift-based or more fragmented work contexts may exhibit higher acquiescent silence due to stress and lower psychological safety.

Finally, an examination of income levels revealed that healthcare workers with low income had higher scores of acquiescent silence. In contrast, as income levels increased, the scores of acquiescent silence among participants tended to decrease. A previous study revealed a significant relationship between income status and silence scales and concluded that there were significant differences in terms of the individual silence subdimension [35].

A positive association was observed between organizational silence and work alienation in the present study, which is consistent with previous research [36]. However, findings in the literature are not fully consistent, suggesting that these relationships may vary across organizational and cultural contexts. Therefore, further research is needed to better understand these associations across different settings [26].

Strengths and limitations of the study

A major strength of this study lies in its focus on an under-researched cultural and institutional context. By examining organizational silence and work alienation among healthcare employees working in public hospitals in Northern Cyprus, the study offers empirical insights from a setting that is rarely represented in the organizational behavior and healthcare management literature. This context-specific focus supports the ecological relevance of the results and contributes to understanding how silence behaviors and alienation experiences may manifest in public healthcare systems characterized by hierarchical structures, high job security, and intensive emotional labor demands.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of the study does not allow causal interpretations of the relationships between organizational silence and work alienation. Second, the use of self-reported data may introduce common method bias; however, Harman’s single-factor test indicated that this bias is unlikely to be a major concern, although it cannot be fully excluded. Third, the Work Alienation Scale demonstrated relatively low internal consistency in the present sample (α = 0.519), particularly at the subdimension level. This limitation suggests that results related to work alienation should be interpreted with caution, as measurement reliability may have influenced the observed associations. Accordingly, the results should be considered indicative rather than conclusive.

Finally, the study is based on a single-country context and a specific healthcare setting, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other cultural and organizational environments. Future research is encouraged to use longitudinal designs, larger and more diverse samples, and more robust psychometric validations to strengthen the evidence base in this field.

Recommendations and future studies

From a practical perspective, the results of this study suggest the relevance of strengthening open communication and employee voice mechanisms within healthcare organizations [16, 37]. Hospital administrators may benefit from fostering psychologically safe work environments in which healthcare professionals feel comfortable expressing concerns, suggestions, and potential errors without fear of negative consequences [16, 33]. Leadership development programs that emphasize participative and supportive management styles may be associated with lower levels of defensive and acquiescent silence behaviors [38]. In addition, establishing formal reporting and feedback channels, such as anonymous communication systems and structured staff consultation mechanisms, may contribute to increased transparency and may be associated with lower levels of perceived alienation among healthcare workers [37].

This study was conducted exclusively in public hospitals in Northern Cyprus, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other healthcare settings. Future research is therefore encouraged to examine organizational silence and work alienation among healthcare employees working in private hospitals and to conduct comparative studies across public and private healthcare institutions. Such comparisons may help to better understand the role of organizational structure and employment conditions in shaping silence behaviors and alienation experiences.

In addition, future studies should employ larger and more diverse samples to enable more robust statistical analyses and to further explore subgroup differences. Given the cultural and contextual specificity of work alienation, future research may also benefit from adapting and validating measurement instruments across different cultural and institutional contexts, particularly within healthcare systems.

As the present study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period characterized by heightened workload, uncertainty, and emotional strain among healthcare workers, future studies could replicate the research under more stable conditions to examine whether and how organizational silence and work alienation may vary over time. Finally, longitudinal, mixed-method, or qualitative research designs may provide deeper insights into the dynamic and context-dependent nature of these phenomena.

Conclusions

This study identified significant associations between organizational silence and work alienation among healthcare employees, both of which were related to various sociodemographic and professional characteristics. The results indicate that silence behaviors and experiences of alienation may represent complex and multidimensional phenomena that can coexist within healthcare settings. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, causal interpretations cannot be made. However, the findings highlight patterns that may be relevant for understanding employee experiences in communication-intensive and high-pressure healthcare environments.

From a practical perspective, healthcare managers and policymakers may consider organizational practices that encourage employee voice and support employees’ sense of meaningful work. Approaches such as promoting open communication, involving employees in decision-making processes, and fostering supportive work environments may be associated with lower levels of silence and alienation among healthcare professionals.
