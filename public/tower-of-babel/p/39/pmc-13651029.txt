Developing a contextually relevant framework for assessing older adults’ access to primary healthcare in Iran: an expert-based qualitative study
Authors: Zahra Solbi, Mohsen Shati, Parastoo Amiri, Mohammadreza Sohrabi, Vahidreza Borhaninejad, Maryam Mohseny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & aimGiven the increasing elderly population in Iran, addressing older adults’ access to healthcare services is essential. This study aimed to develop a contextually relevant framework for assessing older adults’ access to primary healthcare in Iran. MethodsThis study utilized a conventional qualitative content analysis approach through focus group discussions with experts in elderly care. Participants were selected using purposive sampling with maximum diversity. Data were collected through a checklist developed based on the Levesque framework, and the collected and transcribed data were analyzed using content analysis.FindingsThe main dimensions of older adults’ access to health center services were categorized into two core domains aligned with the Levesque framework: health system characteristics (supply-side) and population capabilities (demand-side)>>. The health system dimension was further subcategorized into < < availability, acceptability, accommodation, affordability, and appropriateness>>. The demographic context of accessibility was categorized into subcategories of < < the ability to perceive, seek, reach, pay for, and engage with healthcare services>>. Based on feedback from subject-matter experts, appropriate components were proposed for a detailed examination of each subcategory, and a final consensus was reached.ConclusionThe contextually relevant framework derived from this study can help identify reasons for older adults’ reluctance or inability to access elderly care services at health centers. Future studies can apply the proposed framework to thoroughly examine the challenges of implementing the national aging care program across various universities of medical sciences in Iran.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07972-z.Keywords: Aging, Primary Health Care, Integrated Care, Evaluation, Iran

Introduction

The world is undergoing unprecedented demographic changes. With increased life expectancy and declining birth rates, many countries are experiencing population aging [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), developing countries are witnessing the fastest growth in the elderly population compared to all other demographic groups, with a projected growth rate four times higher than the overall population growth by 2030, making it a major demographic issue [3]. Projections indicate that the proportion of the elderly population in Iran will rise from 9.6% of the total population in 2016 to approximately 30% by 2050, signifying a faster rate of demographic change compared to other countries [4]. The WHO has established “Active Aging” as a global policy response to population aging. Active aging refers to maximizing health, participation, and security for older adults to enhance their quality of life [5]. The population of Iran has experienced a major socio-demographic transition in a relatively short amount of time. The global proportion of people aged 60 years increased from 7.2% in 2006 to 12% in 2025 and is expected to reach more than 25% by 2040 in Iran [6]. Aging presents a significant, additional risk of becoming or remaining poor and older persons face a higher risk of income loss and increased healthcare expenses [7]. In Iran about 25% of the older adults are below the poverty line [8].

Given the importance of this issue, the Older Adults Health Department was established in 2003 under the Ministry of Health and implemented two comprehensive programs: 1-Integrated older adult care: The program focuses on providing comprehensive and coordinated healthcare services for the elderly, ensuring they receive continuous, multidisciplinary support. 2- healthy lifestyle promotion during aging: The program aims to encourage healthy behaviors and prevent age-related diseases through lifestyle education and interventions [9]. As the elderly population grows, their healthcare needs and reliance on health services are expected to increase significantly [10]. Although Iran’s health network system was originally designed to achieve universal coverage for both urban and rural populations, current service coverage, particularly in urban areas, remains incomplete and below established standards. Consequently, the healthcare system is not fully equipped to address the growing complexities of care for an expanding elderly population. This is particularly concerning given that primary healthcare systems are fundamentally expected to ensure continuity of care across the aging process and timely access to essential health services [11]. Access to healthcare is a multidimensional concept intertwined with the performance of health systems [12] and various frameworks exist to evaluate dimensions of access to healthcare [13]. Among them, the conceptual framework by Levesque et al. provides a more comprehensive analysis [14] by considering both health system performance and patients’ perspectives on access [15]. This framework can offer a thorough and precise understanding of the dimensions of older adults’ access to healthcare services. Given the suboptimal coverage of elderly care services and the critical need to understand the multidimensional deficiencies in service accessibility for older adults, this study was designed to explore structural and operational deficiencies of access to primary health care services for older adults.

Methodology

Study design and approval

This qualitative study was conducted after obtaining ethical approval from Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences to explore dimensions of older adults’ access to primary healthcare services. The study employed a conventional qualitative content analysis approach, selected due to limited prior research on the topic in the Iranian context. Data collection and analysis followed a seven-stage process (detailed below), with verification using Lincoln and Guba’s trustworthiness criteria.: dependability, confirmability, credibility, and transferability.

The conceptual framework

After reviewing the extensive literature, the project’s scientific committee selected the Levesque [5] framework (one of the widely used tools for assessing access to healthcare services across all age groups). The Levesque framework comprehensively evaluates the complex and dynamic process of access in health systems and demographic contexts, viewing access as a process extending from pre-entry to healthcare facilities to the end of treatment. This framework defines access as an opportunity to identify, seek, reach, obtain, and use healthcare services. This model conceptualizes access as a dynamic, dual-level process spanning from service seeking to treatment completion:

The framework was operationalized through a checklist developed with input from geriatric experts and Ministry of Health specialists.

After compiling the Levesque framework with feedback from experts and specialists in geriatric programs, a checklist was developed to assess the status of healthcare access. This checklist included dimensions of acceptability, availability, accommodation, affordability, and appropriateness of health systems, along with five corresponding capabilities of the population: understanding, searching, reaching, paying for, and participating in healthcare.

In the next stage, two focus group discussion (FGD) sessions were held to prioritize elderly services with the participation of policymakers, decision-makers, and experts in the field of aging. In the first session, a report on the service coverage status, the categorized results, as well as the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) program recommended by the World Health Organization, were provided to the focus group participants. At this stage, the results of integrating opinions and the output of this alignment produced a draft of priorities for integration into elderly services. After summarizing the results, the six capacity areas and five levels of service delivery in ICOPE capacities were finalized in detail and with the final summary of experts and policymakers, the initial draft program was finalized with a specific score and prioritization.

Ethical detail

Before starting the focus group discussions, the participants were fully informed about the research objectives, and their questions were answered with care. Before the interviews, permission for recording the interviews was obtained from the participants. The participants were assured that all the information obtained would be kept confidential, and their names would not be mentioned in the study.

Results

The total coverage of elderly care (four services: falls, cardiovascular prevention, depression, and nutrition) in 2023–2024 is equivalent to 32% (nationwide), around 8% in Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (based on statistics extracted from the Ministry of Health’s Sib system) and 17.9% (based on statistics extracted from Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences’ Sib system), and in the five selected centers of SPakdasht 46.6%, Damavand 46.9%, East 13.7%, North 12%, and Shemiranat 10.7%. The most provided service is in falls and depression services, and the least provided is in cardiovascular disease prevention service. Also, the two services of colorectal cancer prevention, which is performed only in elderly people aged 60 to 69 years, and breast cancer, which is performed only in elderly women aged 60 to 69 years, are not included in the coverage of elderly care and are examined separately.

Service providers

In total, 5 focus group sessions were held, with 6 to 8 experts attending each session. A total of 18 experts participated, including 10 from the Ministry of Health, 3 from university and health network management, and 5 from the university faculty. The Ministry of Health experts were gerontologists, general practitioners, MPH professionals specializing in geriatrics, family health experts, and older adult health specialists. The university managers included epidemiologists and geriatric experts, while the university faculty members consisted of geriatrics specialists and epidemiologists. Among 18 providers, 44.44% (8 people) assessed the public sector as more suitable than the private sector for providing services to the elderly due to the existence of appropriate and integrated guidelines and staff commitment. In contrast, 55.55% (10 people) believed that the existing workforce in the public sector was not sufficient to provide services to the elderly, and considering the more specialized and time-consuming nature of these services, they considered increasing the workforce a necessity for the health system. However, 38.8% (7 people) considered proper management in service delivery to be important and believed that the workforce in the system was sufficient, but the time and management of service delivery were of great importance. All providers (100%) considered the six existing services to be appropriate and did not want to remove any of them. At the same time, 27.7% (5 people) agreed with adding vision services, 22.2% (4 people) with adding hearing services, 11.1% (2 people) with adding home care, 5.5% (1 person) with adding education on optimal communication with the elderly as a service, 5.5% (1 person) with adding dementia service, 5.5% (1 person) with empowering the service providing workforce, and 11.1% (2 people) with providing specialized services and referrals in centers (Figs. 1 and 2).

Discussion

The present study aimed to evaluate and improve the system of providing primary health services to the elderly using ICOPE in the population covered by Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences at the experts of services. In reviewing the current status of elderly care coverage based on statistical information from the SIB system, there is a significant difference in the care coverage of selected centers, Shahid Beheshti University, and the country, which is a noteworthy point. The results showed that a combination of personal, cultural, and environmental factors are effective in receiving services, which are not distinguishable from each other. It is important to mention that Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences has a diverse population. The highest coverage rate is in the Damavand network due to the implementation of the Irapen codified program and the average population structure of the covered area, followed by the Pakdasht network due to the population structure of the area and the low-income status of the elderly and their (reception/acceptance) of services from the centers, and the lowest rate is in the Shemiranat health network. Another point is that participants consider all existing elderly services necessary. After reviewing the results of the study and matching them with ICOPE, the priority is to integrate and improve elderly services with dementia, followed by social, visual, and hearing services. Older adults in Iran, like the global trend, are on the rise [16], and in light of this increase, the Ministry of Health has implemented a comprehensive program for integrated elderly care and promoting a healthy lifestyle during aging [17]. However, access to the implemented program is of paramount importance for the success of the program. Access is defined as the degree of alignment between the user and the service. The better the alignment, the better the access. Access to healthcare services has been continuously increasing worldwide over the past decades. A global study on the burden of disease (GBD) has shown a continuous increase in global healthcare access scores, from 37.6 in 1900 to 42.4 in 2000 and 54.4 per hundred people in 2016 [18]. Despite this increase, many populations in countries, especially low-income countries, still lack sufficient access [19], and low universal access to health services remains a significant health challenge [20]. The healthcare access index is a complex and multidimensional indicator that may be affected by various individual and environmental factors [21]. The access index includes geographic access dimensions, such as distance to healthcare centers and access to public transportation systems, affordability of treatment costs at the center and health insurance coverage, informational access with effective communication about centers and services, and access to health information resources. It also includes cultural access, which refers to the alignment of services with individual cultures and beliefs. These dimensions are essential for accurately measuring access to healthcare services and evaluating the performance of health systems. A comprehensive assessment of these dimensions can contribute to identifying the best strategies for improving and speeding up access to healthcare services.

Various frameworks have been proposed over the past decades to determine healthcare accessibility dimensions [22]. The initial frameworks were developed based on the healthcare behavior model [23] and taking individual needs into account [24]. However, newer frameworks consider various dimensions as important. One of the most comprehensive frameworks for access is Levesque’s conceptual framework for access to healthcare. This framework provides a multidimensional view of access to healthcare in the context of health systems with a systemic perspective and dimensions of access, including acceptability, availability/compatibility, affordability, and appropriateness. It also incorporates a demographic perspective with dimensions such as understanding, searching, reaching, paying, and participating in healthcare. Thus, this framework can provide a more precise interpretation of healthcare access.

This study extracted the relevant questions for each dimension, which can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the reasons behind the low participation of older adults in the elderly care program, despite its comprehensiveness. The findings indicated that understanding the location of the center, the quantity, quality, and adequacy of services, and their acceptability, as well as information dissemination, access to the center, main and ancillary costs, and the quality of interaction with clients/visitors were key aspects of examining the dimensions of access to health systems. The key questions regarding older adults’ capabilities for access to the elderly care program included trust, information sources, barriers to financial access, frequency of visits, required services, and the level of participation in services. Older adults face multiple challenges in accessing healthcare services [25]. On one hand, cognitive decline or social communication limitations make it difficult for older adults to identify services [26]. Physical limitations or disabilities in older adults can restrict physical access to health centers and services [27]. Additionally, high treatment costs may cause this population to forgo healthcare services [28]. Even when entering a center, due to communication issues or lack of ability to interact, older adults may not fully benefit from healthcare services [29]. Often, services are rarely fully aligned with the specific needs of older adults [30]. Identifying the dimensions of these complex challenges requires a comprehensive and holistic approach.

In general, various studies have shown that the Levesque framework is effective, particularly for older adults, in identifying barriers to healthcare access comprehensively [31]. The World Health Organization has declared its global policy for population aging as “Active Aging”. Active aging refers to the process of maximizing health, participation, and security for older adults to enhance their quality of life [32]. Active participation of older adults in aging programs requires considering all dimensions of access in older adults. The only way to increase participation is to understand the hidden aspects of access to programs, which the Levesque framework can illuminate. This framework enables researchers to offer more effective solutions to the problems of access to healthcare for older adults and helps health decision-makers and planners design policies and services tailored to this age group.

Limitations

The approach adopted in this study focused on the opinions of experts at the national level in the capital; however, there may be other significant issues in rural and remote areas, which are often far from urban healthcare centres. A similar study is needed at the national level to investigate these aspects. “This article focused on service providers; the complementary perspectives of service recipients are presented in a separate publication.”

Conclusion

Examining access to elderly care programs using the Levesque framework requires focusing on both systemic and demographic dimensions. Addressing all these issues can reveal the hidden dimensions of the low participation of older adults in the national aging program. Thus, future studies can address these issues nationwide and on a broader scale.
