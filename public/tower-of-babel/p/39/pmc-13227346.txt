Incidence and Risk Factors for Post-Thyroidectomy Hypocalcemia: A National Study from Saudi Arabia
Authors: Saad M. Alqahtani, Bader E. Alsharari, Abdulaziz H. Al-shahrani, Shehata F. Shehata, Moneer E. Almadani, Yousef S. Alalawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:Post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia is the leading cause of post-thyroidectomy hospitalization, adversely affecting patient quality of life. The current study sought to determine the incidence of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia, identify risk factors and their clinical significance, and provide an overview of the incidence in different Saudi Arabian cities.Methods:This retrospective analysis included all patients who underwent thyroidectomies at King Salman Armed Forces Hospital Northwestern Region, Tabuk, Saudi Arabia between 2015 and 2021 and had available pre- and post-operative calcium levels and clinical, surgical, and pathological data. Various risk factors were evaluated using binary logistic regression to determine their impact on the development of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia.Results:A total of 153 patients were enrolled, and most (n = 128, 83.7%) were women; 88 patients (57.5%) received a malignant diagnosis. The overall incidence of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia was 70.6%. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, female gender (adjusted odds ratio [ORA] = 2.9; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.0–7.6), the presence of diabetes mellitus (ORA = 4.9; 95% CI, 1.2–20.5), and the failure to identify the parathyroid glands in the operative report (ORA = 2.9; 95% CI, 1.2–6.7) significantly correlated with a higher risk of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia. Redo surgery was the sole factor significantly correlated with permanent hypocalcemia (p = 0.048).Conclusion:Post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia is a serious condition that should be evaluated at each center to provide patients with accurate risk assessments prior to surgery. Further studies are required to overcome this surgically challenging condition.

Introduction

The most prevalent endocrine disorders worldwide involve the thyroid, and therapeutic options include medical treatment and surgical intervention [1]. Indications for thyroidectomy include suspected or confirmed cancer, cosmetic concerns, and compression symptoms. Although thyroidectomy is currently regarded as a safe procedure, post-operative complications may occur [2].

Post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia is the leading cause of hospitalization after thyroid surgeries, representing a clinical challenge that adversely impacts the overall quality of life of patients [1]. This condition is thought to stem from inadvertent excision or destruction of the parathyroid glands, leading to insufficient production or an absence of parathyroid hormone [3].

An understanding of thyroid anatomy, the variations and interconnections between the thyroid and parathyroid glands, the embryology of the parathyroid glands, and their vascular supply is essential to prevent complications associated with thyroidectomy [4]. Most individuals have four elliptical parathyroid glands that are yellow to reddish-brown in color. However, fewer than four glands and supernumerary glands have been documented. Typically, the inferior thyroid artery is the principal vascular supply for the lower and upper parathyroid glands [4].

The incidence of hypocalcemia differs based on the definition used, with the overall risk ranging from 1.7% to 68% [5]. Temporary hypocalcemia, which affects 50–68% of individuals who have undergone total thyroidectomy (TT), is characterized by a post-operative reduction in calcium levels that persists for 6–12 months. In contrast, permanent hypocalcemia affects 3% of TT cases and is characterized by a decrease in calcium levels that persists beyond 12 months [6].

Post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia is influenced by various factors such as pre- and post-operative biochemical serum markers and clinical, technical, and anatomical characteristics. Identifying such risk factors may facilitate the timely detection and prevention of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia, thereby mitigating its effects, shortening hospital stays, and avoiding superfluous laboratory investigations [1].

Thus, the current study aim to estimate the incidence of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia and establish relevant risk factors for the condition, along with their clinical significance.

Methods

This study was conducted as a retrospective analysis of 153 patients who underwent thyroid surgeries at King Salman Armed Forces Hospital Northwestern Region, Tabuk, Saudi Arabia between 2015 and 2021. The patients were enrolled based on the availability of pre- and post-operative calcium levels and complete clinical, surgical, and pathological records. Only patients who received total or completion thyroidectomies were included; patients who underwent hemithyroidectomies and those with pre-operative hypocalcemia were excluded.

We collected data from patient charts and operative reports, including demographic details, the presence of diabetes mellitus (DM), pathological diagnoses, and the type of surgical procedure. Post-operative outcomes, particularly the incidence and type of hypocalcemia, were also documented. Pre- and post-operative serum calcium levels were retrieved from laboratory records and used to define hypocalcemia based on our institutional reference ranges. Normal calcium levels at our hospital range from 2.1 to 2.55 mmol/L. Hypocalcemia was characterized as a serum calcium level <2.1 mmol/L. The severity and duration were further assessed during follow-up; transient hypocalcemia was defined as calcium levels returning to normal within 12 months and permanent hypocalcemia as low calcium levels persisting beyond 12 months despite supplementation. Patients were categorized based on the presence or absence of hypocalcemia and further classified into transient or permanent hypocalcemia.

We evaluated various parameters to determine their effect on the occurrence of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia. Factors included age, gender, presence of DM or Hashimoto's thyroiditis, type of procedure, number of parathyroid glands recorded in the operative report, and pathological diagnosis.

This study complied with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and received clearance from the ethical committee, ensuring patient confidentiality and data integrity (approval number: KSAFH-RET-2025-672). Informed consent was not required owing to the retrospective design of the study.

Statistical analysis

We used IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to analyze all data. Demographic characteristics and clinical data were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD, whereas categorical variables are reported in frequency tables. The association between the study variables and occurrence of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia was assessed using Pearson's Chi-squared test. To identify potential risk factors, both crude odds ratios (ORC) and adjusted odds ratios (ORA) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using binary logistic regression analysis. Additionally, we used cross-tabulation and the chi-square test to explore the correlation between the type of post-operative hypocalcemia (transient vs. permanent) and risk factors. We considered p < 0.05 to be statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 shows the demographic features of 153 patients who underwent thyroid surgeries at our institution during the study period. Of these patients, 133 (86.9%) were under 55 years of age and the majority (n = 128, 83.7%) were female. In terms of comorbidities, 26 patients (17.0%) had DM. A total of 65 (42.5%) patients had benign conditions, and the most common diagnosis was nodular/multinodular goiter (n = 52, 34%). In contrast, 88 (57.5%) patients had malignancies, with papillary thyroid carcinoma (n = 63, 41.2%) being the most prevalent type. The parathyroid glands were not mentioned in the operative reports of 68.6% (n = 105) of the cases. Additionally, 24 (15.7%) patients had a redo surgery, and 36 (23.5%) underwent central lymph node dissection. The overall mean pre-operative calcium level was 2.3 ± 0.1 mmol/L.

Several factors were evaluated for their independent association with post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia using a multivariate logistic regression analysis. Female gender was significantly associated with an increased risk, with an ORA of 2.9 (95% CI, 1.0–7.6), indicating that women were nearly three times more likely to develop hypocalcemia than men. Similarly, DM emerged as an independent predictor, with an ORA of 4.9 (95% CI, 1.2–20.5), suggesting that patients with DM were nearly 5 times more likely to experience hypocalcemia. Furthermore, patients had a significantly increased risk of developing hypocalcemia when the parathyroid glands were not mentioned in the operative report (ORA = 2.9; 95% CI, 1.2–6.7) (Table 3).

Discussion

Thyroidectomy is a common surgical procedure performed worldwide. As with any surgical procedures, thyroidectomy may have certain complications, including hypocalcemia, nerve injury (either recurrent or superior laryngeal), hemorrhage, seroma, and hypertrophic scar development. Of these, hypocalcemia is the leading cause of hospitalization after thyroidectomy [1]. In our study population, the incidence of hypocalcemia was 70.6%, and our data showed that female gender, the presence of DM, and the failure of recording the parathyroid glands in the operative report significantly correlated with an elevated risk of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia.

The incidence of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia fluctuates according to the different definitions used for the condition. The overall incidence ranges from 1.7% to 68%. Specifically, the incidence of transient hypocalcemia falls between 50% and 68% [6], whereas that of permanent hypocalcemia is 12.1%, with a range of 0.4–33% [1]. In our study, the overall incidence was 70.6%, that of transient hypocalcemia was 63.4%, and that of permanent hypocalcemia was 7.2%. Risk factors associated with post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia may be categorized as modifiable, such as vitamin D deficiency and hypomagnesemia, or non-modifiable, such as post-operative parathyroid hormone levels and Graves' disease [1]. Identifying such factors may facilitate the swift detection and prevention of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia, thereby mitigating its repercussions, shortening hospital stays, and avoiding superfluous laboratory tests. Moreover, identifying individuals at risk of hypocalcemia facilitates prophylactic treatment, thus preventing the onset of symptomatic hypocalcemia. Modifying the technique or extent of surgical intervention may also be appropriate for high-risk patients. Alternatively, innovative methods such as fluorescence imaging may be utilized to detect or conserve the parathyroid glands [1]. Furthermore, hypocalcemia is associated with hypoparathyroidism [5], and monitoring parathyroid hormone levels throughout surgery or recovery may help guide recommendations for calcium supplementation [23].

Patients with Graves' disease have an elevated risk of developing transient hypocalcemia [423]. This is because the thyroid gland is generally larger and highly vascularized in these patients, requiring a more complex surgical procedure [24]. Conversely, some studies have found no associations between the presence of Graves' disease and the risk of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia [2526]. Unfortunately, we could not assess associations with this factor due to our limited sample size.

Of note, the association between the incidence of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia and age is controversial. Some investigators have found that the risk was higher among older patients [523], whereas others found no association [2327], which is consistent with our results.

Similar to our findings, several studies have reported a higher risk of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia in women [16232528]. This may result from genetic variations in cell signaling pathways, the influence of sex hormones on parathyroid hormone release, or physical differences which could lead to increased iatrogenic damage due to a reduced operative field [1].

Patients with DM are at an elevated risk of experiencing post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia because diabetic microangiopathy temporarily increases the vulnerability of the parathyroid glands to hypoxia, causing a delay in the restoration of normal function [2930]. This is consistent with our findings; in contrast, Del et al. [25] found no significant associations between the 2 conditions.

Thyroidectomy for cancerous nodules is deemed a high-risk procedure because of the radical excision of the gland and its posterior capsule, which increases the risk of harm to the parathyroid glands [31]. Our data showed that surgical intervention for malignant diagnoses was not a significant factor in the occurrence of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia, similar to previous report [25].

The extent of thyroidectomy is associated with parathyroid damage, ischemia, edema, infarction, and unintentional parathyroidectomy. Furthermore, dissection around the parathyroid glands, ligation of the thyroid veins, and attempts to isolate the recurrent laryngeal nerve may result in venous congestion, stasis, and edema. Thus, TT has a higher risk of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia than lobectomy, which is not reflected in our findings [25]. This may be attributed to the limited sample size and exclusion of hemithyroidectomy cases in our analysis.

The association between pre-operative calcium levels and the development of temporary hypocalcemia remains a topic of debate. One report demonstrated that temporary post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia occurs more frequently in individuals with significantly reduced pre-operative calcium levels, whereas another found no significant correlation between the two conditions [3225].

A pragmatic preventive strategy for managing probable hypocalcemia is calcium supplementation with or without calcitriol, negating the need for monitoring calcium or parathyroid hormone levels. Consistently providing oral calcium with or without calcitriol to all individuals undergoing thyroid surgery decreases the occurrence of symptomatic hypocalcemia. This technique reduces the occurrence of post-operative hypocalcemia by 10% [1].

One of the strengths of the current study is that it included the reported incidences of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia across Saudi Arabia, which provides an extensive overview of this specific post-operative complication in our country. However, the study has some limitations, including its retrospective nature and limited sample size. Furthermore, data on parathyroid hormone, pre-operative vitamin D, and magnesium levels, as well as gland size, the existence of large or retrosternal multinodular goiters, and the utilization of energy devices are lacking.

In conclusion, the incidence of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia was 70.6% in our study population. Female gender, the presence of DM, and the failure to identify the parathyroid glands in the operative report significantly correlated with an elevated risk of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia. We advocate further studies with larger sample sizes in several cities throughout the country, which may provide additional insights to enhance the identification of preventive measures for post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia.
