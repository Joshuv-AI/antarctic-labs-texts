Possible Implications for Clinical Practice and Resource Use When Applying the 2022 ESC Guidelines for Perioperative Troponin Testing After Non‐Cardiac Surgery in a Swiss Hospital: An Observational Retrospective Study
Authors: Noemi Ruetsche, Stass Danielsons, Dalibor Milijkovic, Pascal Probst, Markus K. Mueller, Nils Horn, Christian Pieper, JoEllen Welter, Alexander Dullenkopf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsMyocardial injury after non‐cardiac surgery (MINS) is a significant postoperative complication. In 2022, the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) updated its guidelines, recommending perioperative troponin monitoring for a broader surgical population. This study aimed to assess the implications of implementing these guidelines in a secondary Swiss hospital.MethodsPatients meeting ESC guideline criteria for troponin testing were identified, along with those undergoing surgeries deemed similar in risk and complexity. We retrospectively analyzed all in‐patient non‐cardiac surgeries performed in 2023 (n = 6413). Demographic data, cardiac risk factors, new cardiac diagnoses, and mortality within 3 months post‐surgery were assessed. The primary outcome was the demand for postoperative troponin testing when applying the 2022 ESC guidelines. The secondary outcome was to detect new cardiac diagnoses among patients meeting these criteria.ResultsOf the 6413 in‐patient surgeries, 1306 (20%) met the guideline's or study‐defined criteria for intermediate‐risk, high‐risk, or procedures similar regarding risk and complexity. After excluding ineligible patients, we analyzed data from 1231 patients, and found 1089 patients (88% of included patients; 17% of all patients) who would have required testing. Within 3 months postoperatively, we identified 72 patients (6%) who developed new cardiac conditions and 54 patients (4%) died. Surgeries outside regular hours comprised 17% of cases.ConclusionSubstantial postoperative troponin testing would be required to comply with the 2022 ESC guidelines, necessitating expanded organizational capacity and resources. While the value of early detection and prevention of MINS is clear, implementation in routine clinical practice may be challenging.

Introduction

Myocardial injury after non‐cardiac surgery (MINS) is becoming more widely recognized as an important complication in surgical patients [1, 2, 3]. It is characterized by elevated postoperative cardiac troponin (cTn) levels due to ischemia, with or without associated symptoms or electrocardiogram (ECG) abnormalities [4]. MINS often occurs without overt clinical signs or symptoms of myocardial infarction or cardiovascular instability. According to a recently published meta‐analysis, the estimated incidence of MINS after major non‐cardiac surgery in patients with known risk factors can reach 20% and is strongly associated with 30‐day and 1‐year mortality [5].

Cardiac biomarkers, such as cardiac troponins, play an important role in diagnosing, ruling out, and managing coronary artery disease [6]. Systematic postoperative cardiac troponin measurement can detect acute perioperative increases in troponin levels, helping to identify patients at risk for complications related to MINS. Evidence gathered by several large‐scale observational studies suggests that elevated postoperative high‐sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs‐cTn) is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular complications, poor cardiac and extra‐cardiac outcomes, and higher mortality [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

In response to growing evidence, a decade ago, the European Society of Anesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine (now ESAIC) and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) recommended monitoring cardiac biomarkers, such as cardiac troponin and B‐type natriuretic peptides, in at‐risk patients before and 48–72 h after major surgery [13]. In 2022, the ESC updated its guidelines on perioperative cardiac troponin measurement following non‐cardiac surgery [14]. In contrast to the previous, more cautious recommendations, the updated guidelines strongly advocate for assessing postoperative cardiac troponins, including preoperative measurement and 24‐ and 48‐h postoperative testing in all patients aged 65 years and older, regardless of cardiovascular risk factors, undergoing intermediate‐ and high‐risk surgery.

While the rationale for prioritizing prevention over treatment is clear, implementing these ambitious guidelines may be challenging due to their potential strain on hospital resources. This study aimed to estimate the demand for postoperative troponin testing according to the 2022 ESC guidelines, with subsequent identification of new cardiac diagnoses in these patients. We retrospectively applied these guidelines to non‐cardiac surgeries performed at a secondary hospital in Switzerland during 2023 to estimate their potential impact on healthcare resource utilization.

Materials and Methods

The setting for this retrospective study was a cantonal hospital in northeast Switzerland, where approximately 9000 anesthetics are performed annually. The main surgical departments include general surgery, orthopedics and traumatology, gynecology and obstetrics, urology, and plastic surgery. Additionally, smaller departments perform operations such as hand surgery; ear, nose, and throat surgery; and dentistry. Outpatient procedures account for approximately 30% of all cases.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The local ethics committee (Ethics Committee East Switzerland; EKOS 24/022; February 15, 2024) determined that the project met the criteria for a quality improvement exemption from research ethics review. The primary outcome was the demand for postoperative troponin testing when applying the 2022 ESC guidelines. The secondary outcome was to identify new cardiac diagnoses among patients meeting these criteria.

To assess patient eligibility, we identified all non‐cardiac, in‐patient surgeries conducted during 2023. We included only those individuals who had previously provided written informed consent (general consent form) for further use of their data for research purposes, as routinely obtained by the institution. Patients who explicitly refused general consent were excluded from the analysis. We included all patients 18 years and older. It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

We screened the list of non‐cardiac surgeries to identify patients undergoing intermediate or high‐risk surgery, according to the ESC 2022 guidelines [14], who would require troponin monitoring. According to the guidelines, testing was required for intermediate or high‐risk surgery AND patients aged 65 years or older OR any patient with cardiac risk factors and/or known cardiovascular disease. In addition, our study team created a list of surgeries considered “similar” to those surgery types stated in the guidelines (Table 1). The parameters we used to define “similar surgeries” were as follows: op duration, estimated blood loss, perceived extent of the operation, and postoperative pain. We created a “probably required testing” category that included those procedures considered similar to those outlined in the guidelines AND patients aged 65 years or older OR any patient with cardiac risk factors and/or known cardiovascular disease.

For all eligible patients, medical history data were extracted from the electronic medical records management system (KISIM; CISTEC, Zurich, Switzerland). Variables collected included gender, age, cardiac risk factors as defined in the ESC guidelines (hypertension, smoking, dyslipidemia, diabetes, family history of cardiovascular disease; excluding anemia), known cardiac diseases, and surgical department carrying out the procedure. Additionally, we recorded whether the surgery was performed during regular working hours, defined as any procedure with the initial incision occurring between 8 a.m. and 4 p.m. on workdays (Monday to Friday). Furthermore, we searched the electronic medical records for new cardiac diagnoses within 3 months post‐surgery or notification of patient death. New cardiac diagnoses were defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes (e.g., ischemic heart disease, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation). New cardiac diagnoses were classified as early (i.e., within 48 h postoperative) or late. In cases of early diagnoses, patients' medical records were screened for concomitant sepsis.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on their distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. For continuous variables, differences between groups were assessed with the Mann–Whitney U test. To identify independent predictors of new cardiac diagnoses within 3 months postoperatively, we performed a multivariate logistic regression analysis, including age, sex, and surgical risk category as covariates. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using Stata version 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). This was a convenience sample of patients treated over 1 year; therefore, no a priori power analysis was conducted to calculate the sample size.

Results

A total of 9423 surgeries were performed at our hospital in 2023, with a mean procedure time of 55 min. Of these, 6413 (68%) were in‐patient procedures. Using the 2022 ESC guidelines, we identified 744 surgeries (12%) as intermediate‐risk, 135 (2%) as high‐risk, and 427 (7%) as procedures our study team defined as similar to those listed in the ESC guidelines. Among these 1306 procedures, 75 patients had previously declined use of their data for research purposes (written refusal of consent stored in the medical records system); therefore, these cases were excluded from further assessment (Figure 1). As a result, 1231 patients with the following characteristics were included: 632 (51%) were female, and the median age of the patients at the time of surgery was 70 years (IQR 59–79, range 19–100). When we analyzed patient data for procedures specifically listed in the 2022 ESC guidelines (n = 687 intermediate risk and n = 124 high risk), we found that 669 patients (83%) would have required perioperative troponin monitoring. Expanding this assessment to include study‐defined procedures similar to those in the guidelines (n = 420), 1089 out of 1231 patients (88% of included patients; approximately 20% of all inpatient surgeries) would have met the criteria for recommended troponin monitoring, representing 17% of all in‐patient surgeries performed in 2023. Seventeen percent (n = 212) of all the assessed procedures took place outside regular working hours, with 89% (n = 188) of these patients meeting the criteria for recommended troponin monitoring.

In total, 72 patients (6% of the patients requiring testing) had a new cardiac diagnosis within 3 months after surgery that was recorded in the clinical patient data management system of our hospital. The most frequently reported diagnoses (n = 32) were in the ICD‐10 group I20–I25 (ischemic heart diseases). These included 29 cases of non‐S‐T‐segment elevation myocardial infarction, 2 cases of angina pectoris, and 1 case of acute S‐T‐segment elevation myocardial infarction. In addition, 19 patients had newly diagnosed heart failure (ICD‐10 I50), and 16 patients developed atrial fibrillation (ICD‐10 I47) or paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (ICD‐10 I48). The remaining five patients suffered from further rhythmogenic events, hypertensive emergency, or pulmonary embolism. Among the 72 patients, 30 (42%) received a new cardiac diagnosis within the first 2 postoperative days (early diagnosis). Sepsis was found at the time of diagnosis in 11 (37%) of these early cases.

Of these cases, 49 (68%) had a preexisting cardiovascular risk. The high‐risk surgical group had an increased rate of a new cardiac diagnosis than the other two risk groups (14% vs. 5% both other groups; p = 002). The median age of the patients who developed a new cardiac diagnosis was 81 years (IQR 71–85) vs. 69 years (IQR 58–78) in those who did not receive this diagnosis (p < 0.0001). Moreover, 58% of the cardiac diagnoses were in men.

In 130 patients (11%; 92 guidelines‐identified and 38 study‐identified similar procedures), the only indication for monitoring, aside the procedure itself, was being 65 years or older. Among these patients, 2 were diagnosed with a new cardiac condition, and 4 died within the first 3 months following surgery.

Using a multivariate logistic regression model controlling for age, gender, and surgical risk category, older age was a significant predictor of developing a new cardiac diagnosis within 3 months following surgery (OR = 1.073, 95% CI: 1.05–1.10, p < 0.0001). Furthermore, being in the high‐risk ESC guidelines category was strongly associated with an increased likelihood of a new cardiac diagnosis (OR = 3.21, 95% CI: 1.7–6.1, p < 0.0001). The other two risk categories were not significantly associated with the outcome.

Discussion

In this retrospective assessment of the need for perioperative troponin testing in non‐cardiac surgery patients based on the updated ESC guidelines (2022) [14], we found that a substantial proportion of our patients would have required testing, potentially leading to considerable healthcare resource utilization. Among those meeting criteria for testing, 6% had a new cardiac diagnosis documented in their medical records within 3 months postoperatively. As expected, older age and high‐risk classification according to the guidelines were associated with an increased likelihood of developing a new cardiac condition within 3 months postoperatively.

MINS can have severe consequences for patients and it can occur in approximately 20% of high‐risk patients [5, 15, 16, 17]. Mortality rates are similar between patients with overt signs and symptoms of ischemia and those without [18]. As previously shown in postoperative ECG surveillance studies done in the 1980s, most episodes of myocardial injury in the postoperative period go unrecognized. This may be partly due to patients still recovering from anesthesia and the use of pain medication for surgery‐related pain. Assessing hs‐cTn levels for elevations and dynamic changes helps to detect MINS in these circumstances [19, 20].

The value of preventing perioperative cardiac complications is undeniable. Proper screening contributes to patient safety, despite the existing knowledge gaps in interpreting MINS screening results and determining subsequent clinical actions. Key benefits to screening include ruling out type 1 myocardial infarction, managing triggers of myocardial ischemia due to oxygen supply‐demand imbalance (type 2 myocardial infarction), and identifying cardiac or extracardiac causes of acute myocardial injury. Studies have appraised the cost‐effectiveness of perioperative troponin monitoring [21]. While this type of appraisal is comprehensible, we believe this point is debatable given that costs of laboratory testing, as well as expenses of treating cardiac events vary across healthcare settings. Additionally, the cost‐benefit ratio may differ depending on the perspective—whether viewed from the standpoint of the patient, department, hospital, insurance provider, or healthcare system as a whole.

At the hospital level, such monitoring would impose considerable organizational and resource demands, and in our secondary care setting, routine implementation may only be partly feasible given current limitations in laboratory capacity, specialist availability, and workflow organization. Refining troponin testing criteria to improve efficiency may involve targeting patients at highest risk, for example by incorporating validated perioperative risk indices or biomarker pre‐screening, while prospective studies are needed to confirm the cost‐effectiveness of such approaches.

The 2022 ESC guidelines included updates for perioperative biomarker assessment [14], including the Class 1B recommendation to measure hs‐cTn T and hs‐cTn I preoperatively, as well as 24 and 48 h postoperatively. This not only increases the number of required laboratory tests but also necessitates clear coordination regarding responsibility for reviewing results and acting on elevated troponin values. By including emergency procedures in the screening programs, a 24/7 system must be in place. In our patient population, 17% of the assessed procedures occurred outside regular working hours.

Using data from 2023, we found that approximately 20% of the patients undergoing in‐patient surgery in our hospital would have qualified for perioperative troponin screening. Of the patients included in the analysis, 11% (130 of 1231) being 65 years or older would have been the only risk factor for inclusion in the screening. Comparing overall complication rates remains challenging due to variations in hospital settings, surgical procedures, inpatient vs. ambulatory selection, and observation periods across studies. In our cohort, the hospital‐wide mortality rate among patients eligible for perioperative troponin monitoring was 2%.

Apart from the ESC guidelines, various other recommendations exist for perioperative cTn screening in non‐cardiac surgery patients, including those from the Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) and locally adopted practices [22], which also set age > 65 years as an indication. In 2018, the European Society of Anesthesiology and Intensive Care (ESAIC; formerly known as the European Society of Anaesthesiology (ESA)) classified the evidence for perioperative cTn screening as moderate and issued only a weak recommendation (Grade 2B) for systematic perioperative troponin assessment in patients at high cardiovascular risk [23]. In 2021, the ESAIC initiated the development of focused guidelines addressing key questions on perioperative troponin use. In 2024, the guidelines were finally updated to differentiate between prognosis, risk prediction, and management [24]. However, no surgical procedure types were specified. While the routine use of cardiac biomarkers was still supported by moderate evidence, it received a weak recommendation (Level of Evidence B). Although a combined pre‐ and post‐operative cTn‐enhanced management was not recommended for routine practice, cTn surveillance and corresponding management algorithms were recognized as valuable and considered prudent for integration into a clinical research framework to assess their impact on patient outcomes.

Similarly, in 2014, the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) rated the usefulness of postoperative troponin screening as uncertain (Level of Evidence B) [25]. In the updated 2024 guidelines [26], MINS is recognized as a newly identified disease process with significant consequences for affected patients. The guidelines now include a Class 2b recommendation and a Level of Evidence B‐NR 2, stating that for patients with known cardiovascular disease (CVD), age ≥ 65 years, or age ≥ 45 years with symptoms suggestive of CVD undergoing elevated‐risk non‐cardiac surgery, it may be reasonable to measure cTn preoperatively to supplement perioperative risk evaluation. A similar Class 2B recommendation with a higher Level of Evidence B‐NR 1 was made for postoperative cTn surveillance at 24 and 48 h after elevated‐risk non‐cardiac surgery. As with the previous guidelines, no specific surgical procedures were listed.

As with most studies of this nature, there are limitations worth mentioning. First, we used retrospectively collected data to estimate the monitoring needs assuming we adhered to the ESC guidelines. Therefore, the outcomes are not based on actual troponin screening of our at‐risk patient population. Perioperative troponin measurements at our institution were obtained only on a case‐by‐case basis. Consequently, neither the prevalence of MINS nor the proportion of patients who underwent postoperative troponin testing could be determined, which limits the generalizability of these findings. Our aim was to estimate the potential impact of adhering to these guidelines rather than evaluating their effectiveness or advocating for or against their use. Second, since we are relying on routinely collected data, biases in completeness, reliability, and quality may exist. For example, patient outcome data following discharge may vary substantially based on the standard of care established for the procedure type and medical condition. Furthermore, cardiac morbidity treated outside our institution may not be documented in our system if patients did not have a follow‐up visit after the incident. Similarly, disease‐specific mortality and potential confounders, such as physical status or comorbidity, were not assessed. Third, we expanded our study to include patients undergoing procedures that our study team considered intermediate risk. However, the extent of the surgeries was rather conservative. We did not include any urogynecological robotic pelvic floor surgeries, gynecological intraperitoneal surgeries, or extensive plastic or transurethral urologic procedures. A further indication that including these procedures might have been justified is that the rates of new cardiac diagnoses after 3 months, hospital mortality, and 3‐month mortality were similar to those observed in our intermediate‐risk patient group.

In conclusion, application of the updated 2022 ESC guidelines for perioperative biomarker assessment may substantially increase the demand for troponin testing in non‐cardiac surgery patients. In our cohort, 6% of patients who met the guideline criteria had a new cardiac diagnosis within 3 months postoperatively reported in their medical records. These findings indicate that guideline‐based monitoring could support earlier recognition of cardiac complications, but they also suggest a potentially significant increase in laboratory workload and organizational requirements. Since such an approach to monitoring carries implications for healthcare resources, there is a need to balance early detection with feasibility and cost‐effectiveness in real‐world secondary care settings.
