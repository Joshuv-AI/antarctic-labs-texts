Severity of Cardiovascular Autonomic Neuropathy Is Linked to Increasing Levels of Circulating Proinflammatory Cytokines
Authors: Michelle Damgaard, Marietta Kokla, Rebekka H. Gerwig, Hatice Isik Mizrak, Sofie Korsgaard Hecquet, Tik Muk, Gurjeet Kaur, Birgitte Brock, Christian Stevns Hansen, Joachim Størling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsCardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (CAN) is a severe form of diabetic neuropathy affecting approximately one in five people with diabetes and is a major predictor of cardiovascular mortality. Low‐grade inflammation may contribute to CAN development, but the exact inflammatory factors associated with CAN remain unclear. Here, we examined the circulating levels of cytokines across different stages of CAN severity in individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D).Materials and MethodsFrom an initial T2D cohort with 899 participants undergoing cardiovascular autonomic reflex tests (CARTs) and measures of heart rate variability (HRV) indices, a final sub‐cohort of 249 participants was selected and classified as: no CAN (n = 83), early CAN (n = 69) or manifest CAN (n = 97), according to CARTs. One abnormal test constituted early CAN; two or three abnormal CARTs constituted manifest CAN. Ten cytokines were measured in serum samples using Meso Scale Discovery multiplex technology.ResultsIndividuals with manifest CAN exhibited subtle but significantly higher circulating levels of the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)‐6, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)‐α and interferon (IFN)‐γ compared to the no CAN group. Furthermore, TNF‐α levels tended to be upregulated already in early CAN compared to no CAN. Higher levels of IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10, IFN‐γ and TNF‐α were associated with worse autonomic function outcomes, including HRV indices and CARTs.ConclusionCAN severity is associated with increased circulating levels of IL‐6, IFN‐γ and TNF‐α in T2D. These cytokines may therefore hold promise as clinically useful biomarkers and/or therapeutic targets to mitigate CAN burden and possibly cardiovascular risk. Trial Registration: Number: NCT05043636; Diabetic Neuropathy Screening Study 1.1 + Substudy 1.2–1.3–1.4

Introduction

Cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (CAN) is a severe and frequently underdiagnosed complication of type 2 diabetes (T2D), resulting from damage to the autonomic nerves that regulate cardiovascular function [1]. While T2D currently affects over 460 million people worldwide, representing more than 90% of all diabetes cases, CAN remains one of its most clinically significant yet overlooked complications [2]. CAN is a specific form of diabetic neuropathy with reported prevalence rates from 20% to 65% [1, 3, 4, 5, 6]. CAN is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular morbidity, foot complications, kidney disease and mortality [7, 8, 9, 10]. Proper management of CAN is currently hindered by the lack of efficient screening tools for early detection and no effective treatments targeting the underlying causal mechanisms. Consequently, focusing on prevention and early detection is essential for optimised diabetes care.

The pathophysiology of CAN is complex and multifactorial, involving metabolic‐, vascular‐ and inflammatory mechanisms [11]. Several studies suggest that systemic low‐grade inflammation contributes to the progression of neuropathy [6, 12, 13, 14]. Associations between CAN indices and proinflammatory factors including interleukin‐ (IL) 6 [15, 16, 17], IL‐18 [17, 18], IL12/IL23p40 [19] and tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α) [20] have been reported in both newly diagnosed and long‐term T2D individuals. However, data are scarce and with heterogeneous results, and only a few studies have used the recommended cardiovascular autonomic reflex tests (CARTs) to assess CAN. CAN often begins with initial vagus nerve damage, leading to dysregulation of the inflammatory reflex. Studies indicate that perturbations in this reflex play a key role in the pathophysiology of CAN [3, 12]. Under normal physiological conditions, the inflammatory reflex mainly acts as a protective brake where inflammatory mediators activate the autonomic nervous system to maintain immune homeostasis. The parasympathetic system acts via the vagus nerve and activates the cholinergic anti‐inflammatory pathway, leading to suppression of cytokine release, while the sympathetic nervous system modulates immune activity through adrenergic signalling in lymphoid organs, stimulating acetylcholine‐mediated inhibition of cytokine release from innate immune cells [3, 21]. However, in T2D, chronic hyperglycaemia and low‐grade inflammation impair vagus nerve function, creating a vicious cycle where the damaged vagus nerve loses its ability to effectively dampen inflammation, leading to autonomic dysfunction and cardiovascular instability [3, 22]. Despite this knowledge, the associations and potential mechanistic roles of specific inflammatory cytokines in CAN severity in individuals with T2D remain incompletely understood. Identification of inflammatory factors associated with CAN severity may be useful as biomarkers, provide insights into disease mechanisms, and/or constitute potential therapeutic targets. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the circulating levels of specific cytokines in individuals with T2D with and without CAN and further assess whether CAN severity correlates with cytokine concentrations.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Serum samples from a cross‐sectional study that included 899 individuals with T2D followed at the outpatient clinic at Steno Diabetes Center Copenhagen (SDCC), Denmark, from 1st October 2019 to 23rd December 2022, originally deriving from the DANES cohort (NCT05043636) [1], were used for the current study approved by the Research Ethics Committee (H‐23073967). All participants gave their informed consent prior to their inclusion. Participants underwent CARTs, and 304 participants were specifically selected to provide ~100 individuals with late‐stage (manifest) CAN, sex‐ and age‐matched to ~100 individuals with early‐stage CAN and ~100 individuals with no evidence of CAN.

Categorisation of CAN

The test method for assessing CAN is based on the protocol outlined previously [5]. Participants fasted for a minimum of 2 h and abstained from caffeine and strenuous exercise for 24 h prior to testing. CAN was assessed using Cardiovascular autonomic reflex tests (CARTs), including the lying‐to‐standing test (30/15 ratio), deep breathing test (E/I ratio) and Valsalva manoeuvre, with measurements obtained via the Vagus device (Medicus Engineering, Aarhus, Denmark). CARTs results were evaluated based on age‐dependent cut‐off values [23], with two or more abnormal tests indicating manifest CAN and one abnormal test indicating early CAN.

From 5‐min resting ECG recordings R–R intervals were obtained, and heart rate variability (HRV) indices were analysed. Time domain HRV outcomes were the root mean square of the sum of the squares of differences between consecutive R–R intervals (RMSSD) and standard deviation of normal‐to‐normal intervals (SDNN). Frequency domain outcomes were low‐frequency (LF) power (0.04–0.15 Hz), high‐frequency (HF) power (0.15–0.4 Hz) and total power (≤ 0.4 Hz). RMSSD and HF power were assessed as measures of parasympathetic activity and SDNN, LF power and total power as measures of both the parasympathetic and sympathetic activity [24].

Serum Concentration of Cytokines

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from study participants, then centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min to isolate serum. The serum was aliquoted into appropriate volumes and stored at −80°C until analysis. Serum concentrations of the cytokines interferon gamma (IFN‐γ), interleukin‐ (IL) 1β, IL‐2, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐12p70, IL‐13 and tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α) were quantified using the V‐PLEX Proinflammatory Panel 1 Human kit (Meso Scale Diagnostics LLC, Rockville, Maryland, USA) and measured using a MESO QuickPlex SQ 120 instrument (Meso Scale Diagnostics) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol and as done previously [19, 25].

Statistical Analysis

Raw cytokine concentration data were pre‐processed prior to analysis. Duplicate measurements were averaged, and concentrations below the assay‐specific lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) were temporarily substituted with LLOQ × √2 to allow consistent handling of values relative to detection limits. Values outside the analytical detection range were excluded. To reduce skewness and stabilise variance, cytokine concentrations were transformed using log (concentration + 1) followed by square‐root transformation.

After applying a missingness threshold, six cytokines with < 40% missing data were retained for downstream analyses, namely IFN‐γ, IL‐10, IL‐2, IL‐6, IL‐8 and TNF‐α (Figure S1). Extreme outliers were identified and removed using the interquartile range (IQR) method. Remaining missing values reflected samples without reliable cytokine measurements after quality filtering and were retained as missing; no imputation was performed. Descriptive statistics including mean, median, standard deviation, standard error and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for cytokines and clinical variables.

Exploratory analyses included pairwise scatterplots, principal component analysis (PCA) to assess overall cytokine variance structure (Figure S2), and group‐wise error bar plots illustrating cytokine levels across CAN categories (Figure 1). Associations between cytokines and clinical, metabolic and autonomic parameters were evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation with false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Figures 2 and S3).

Group differences across CAN categories were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's post hoc comparisons. Benjamini–Hochberg (BH)‐FDR correction was applied to both overall and pairwise tests, with adjusted p‐values reported as q‐values. A q < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Multivariable linear regression models were used to assess associations between cytokines and CAN group (Table S1). In addition to analyses of individual cytokines, principal component analysis (PCA)‐derived composite inflammatory scores were generated, including PC1 based on six cytokines (PC1‐6) and PC1 based on three pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IFN‐γ and IL‐6; PC1‐3). For PC1_6, the component direction was reversed (multiplied by −1) to ensure that higher scores corresponded to higher overall cytokine levels and to facilitate interpretation of odds ratios. Three model specifications were evaluated: (1) unadjusted; (2) adjusted for core demographic covariates (sex, age, diabetes duration) and (3) adjusted for demographic covariates and diabetes‐related comorbidities including recalculated estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine albumin‐creatinine ratio (uACR) and average systolic blood pressure.

Penalised logistic regression with Firth correction was used to evaluate associations between cytokines and CAN status using pairwise group comparisons (Table 3). This approach was applied to reduce sparse‐data bias and coefficient inflation in models with relatively small subgroup sizes and correlated inflammatory markers. To improve biological interpretability and reduce scale‐related inflation of odds ratios, IQR scaling was applied prior to all penalised logistic regression analyses, including both individual cytokine models and PCA‐derived cytokine models. Following IQR scaling, odds ratios represent the effect of a typical biological increase in cytokine level within the cohort, corresponding to the difference between the 25th and 75th percentile. Both individual cytokines and PCA‐derived cytokine scores were tested, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% profile likelihood confidence intervals and BH‐FDR‐adjusted q‐values.

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.2). Data processing was conducted using dplyr and tidyr, visualisation using ggplot2, non‐parametric tests using rstatix, penalised logistic regression using logistf, estimated marginal means using emmeans and correlation analyses using Hmisc.

Results

Study Population

Initially, 304 individuals with T2D from the DANES cohort [1] were included. Fifty‐five participants were excluded due to unknown CAN status because of incomplete CARTs, primarily due to unsuccessful Valsalva manoeuvres. Of the remaining 249 individuals, 83 had no CAN, 69 early CAN and 97 manifest CAN. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics stratified by CAN status are shown in Table 1. Clinical profiles only differed significantly between the manifest CAN and no CAN groups, with the former being older and having higher BMI, HbA1c, LDL, uACR and VPT (all q < 0.05, Table 2). No significant differences were observed for sex, diabetes duration or eGFR.

CAN Is Associated With Increasing Levels of Circulating Cytokines

Circulating cytokine levels differed across CAN groups (Figure 1). Individuals with manifest CAN had modestly but statistically significant higher levels of IFN‐γ (Δ median 0.07 pg/mL, q = 0.006), IL‐6 (Δ 0.12 pg/mL, q < 0.001) and TNF‐α (Δ 0.06 pg/mL, q < 0.001) compared with those without CAN. Although not statistically significant, our results suggest a graded increase across CAN severity, with manifest CAN showing a trend towards higher levels of IL‐6 compared with early CAN (q = 0.053). Further, in early CAN, the levels of TNF‐α tended to be elevated compared with no CAN (q = 0.054). No significant differences were observed for IL‐2, IL‐8 or IL‐10 across CAN groups (Figure 1).

To account for potential confounders, we employed multivariable linear regression models. In unadjusted analyses (model 1), individuals with manifest CAN had significantly higher levels of IFN‐γ (β = 0.121, q = 0.001), IL‐6 (β = 0.111, q < 0.001) and TNF‐α (β = 0.068, q < 0.001) compared to those without CAN (Table S1). All of these associations remained significant in both adjusted models (model 2 and model 3) (Model 2: IFN‐γ β = 0.108, q = 0.005; IL‐6 β = 0.095, q = 0.005; TNF‐α β = 0.063, q = 0.001; Model 3: IFN‐γ β = 0.112, q = 0.006; IL‐6 β = 0.070, q = 0.050; TNF‐α β = 0.035, q = 0.048) (Table S1).

Circulating Cytokine Levels as a Predictor of CAN Status

In an unadjusted penalised logistic regression analysis (Model 1), higher circulating levels of IFN‐γ, IL‐6, TNF‐α and the PCA‐derived cytokine scores (3 [PC1_3] or 6 [PC1_6] cytokines) were strong predictors of manifest CAN vs. no CAN. These predictions remained significant after adjustment for age, sex and diabetes duration (Model 2). Following additional adjustment for diabetes‐related comorbidities, including eGFR, uACR and systolic blood pressure (Model 3), the prediction for manifest CAN vs. no CAN for IL‐6 and TNF‐α were no longer statistically significant. However, IFN‐γ, PC1_3 and PC1_6 remained significant (Table 3).

Correlations Between Circulating Cytokines and Autonomic Nerve Function

Spearman correlation analyses were performed in the pooled study population, i.e., irrespective of CAN category. As shown in Figure 2, higher circulating cytokine levels consistently correlated with impaired autonomic nerve function. TNF‐α and IL‐6 showed the strongest correlations with CARTs and HRV measures. TNF‐α was negatively correlated with the 30:15 response (r = −0.28, p < 0.001), Valsalva response (r = −0.22, p < 0.001), SDNN (r = −0.21, p < 0.01), RMSSD (r = −0.22, p < 0.001) and total power (r = −0.25, p < 0.001). Similarly, IL‐6 showed inverse correlations with the 30:15 response (r = −0.24, p < 0.001), Valsalva response (r = −0.28, p < 0.001), SDNN (r = −0.17, p < 0.01), RMSSD (r = −0.15, p < 0.05) and total power (r = −0.18, p < 0.01). IFN‐γ, IL‐8 and IL‐10 also demonstrated significant but weaker inverse correlations with several HRV indices, including SDNN and total power (r≈−0.13 to −0.22, p < 0.05; Figure 2 and Table S2).

A comprehensive overview of all significant correlations between cytokines and clinical, metabolic, neuropathy and autonomic parameters is provided in Figure S3 and Table S2.

Discussion

We investigated circulating inflammatory cytokine levels in individuals with T2D with and without either early or manifest CAN. Manifest CAN were associated with subtly elevated circulating levels of IFN‐γ, IL‐6 and TNF‐α compared to no CAN. Moreover, TNF‐α levels tended to be elevated already in early CAN, while IL‐6 levels appeared higher in manifest CAN compared with early CAN.

These findings were further supported by multivariable linear and penalised logistic regression analyses where IFN‐γ, IL‐6 and TNF‐α were predictors of manifest CAN vs. no CAN. Importantly, the PCA‐derived composite cytokine models demonstrated the most robust predictions of CAN, suggesting that the cumulative inflammatory burden is more informative than individual cytokines alone.

Alterations in Circulating Cytokines Based on CAN Status

It is well established that low‐grade inflammation and oxidative stress play crucial roles in the progression of T2D [26]. However, recent studies also indicate that these factors influence the severity of DN [14, 27]. Cytokines are essential regulators of inflammation and immune responses and likely contribute to the pathogenesis of CAN. However, the specific cytokines and pathways involved currently remain unclear.

Metabolic disturbances and phenotypic changes in T2D, including hyperglycaemia, adipose tissue expansion, local hypoxia and immune cell activation, promote chronic activation of inflammatory signalling pathways, leading to increased production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including TNF‐α [26, 28]. Higher serum levels of TNF‐α have been reported in patients with DN compared to individuals without diabetes [26]. In line with this, we found elevated circulating levels of TNF‐α in patients with manifest CAN compared with no CAN. Other studies likewise reported associations between TNF‐α and diminished HRV parameters, a central mechanism behind CAN [12, 19, 29].

TNF‐α may contribute directly to neuronal injury and impaired autonomic regulatory mechanisms, which are thought to underlie the development of CAN [3, 14, 21]. Further, TNF‐α and IL‐6 may amplify inflammatory responses through interconnected signalling pathways, contributing to sustained inflammatory activity [26]. This positive feedback loop might be present in our study, as we observed elevated IL‐6 levels in individuals with manifest CAN, which aligns with the longitudinal associations previously reported, suggesting that increased systemic inflammation, including increased levels of IL‐6, contributes to the progression of cardiac autonomic dysfunction in individuals with diabetes after a 5‐year follow‐up period [16].

We did not observe increased IL‐6 levels in individuals with early CAN compared with those without CAN. However, elevated IL‐6 levels were observed in individuals with manifest CAN compared to no and early CAN. This pattern may suggest that IL‐6 becomes more prominent with CAN severity. IL‐6 is recognised for its negative impact on metabolism and its link to chronic inflammation. Research has shown increased concentrations in both diabetic populations [30], and individuals with neuropathy [17, 31]. However, IL‐6 biology is complex as it exhibits a dual role by also contributing to anti‐inflammatory pathways to restore homeostasis in the inflammatory processes [26].

Manifest CAN was linked to higher IFN‐γ levels compared to no and early CAN. Previous studies in T2D individuals with CAN reported no group differences in IFN‐γ [19, 25] or excluded the cytokine due to insufficient detectability [12]. Based on data from studies in DPN, IFN‐γ likely contributes to nerve damage by stimulating reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide production through JAK/STAT signalling. Furthermore, it facilitates immune activation by upregulating MHC class II expression in macrophages and promoting T‐cell recruitment via C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 10 activation [26]. Together, these processes exacerbate the neuroinflammation and oxidative stress suspected to be primary drivers of CAN pathology [26].

In our prediction models, the circulating levels of TNF‐α, IL‐6 and IFN‐γ significantly predicted manifest CAN compared with no CAN after adjustment for core clinical covariates. Combining these cytokines into PCA‐derived composite inflammatory scores further strengthened the predictive value, reflecting a coordinated inflammatory response. Notably, both PCA‐derived cytokine scores (PC1_3 and PC1_6) remained significant across demographic‐ and comorbidity‐adjusted models. This suggests that combined inflammatory signatures may better capture the cumulative systemic inflammatory burden associated with CAN severity than isolated cytokine markers alone.

Associations Between Cytokines and Measurements of Autonomic Function

Irrespective of the presence or severity of CAN, our findings reveal significant inverse correlations between all HRV measures and IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10, IFN‐γ and TNF‐α (except for IFN‐γ and HF), reflecting an impact on overall autonomic flexibility influenced by both sympathetic and parasympathetic activity [32]. These findings suggest that systemic inflammation may be linked to impaired autonomic regulation in individuals with T2D. This aligns with previous research linking TNF‐α and IL‐6 to lower HRV parameters [12, 17, 18, 29]. Furthermore, elevated IL‐6 is associated with long‐term reductions in SDNN, RMSSD, low frequency (LF) and high frequency (HF), though it was also influenced by BMI [16]. This indicates that cytokine‐mediated inflammation affects both the overall autonomic function (SDNN and total power) and specific parasympathetic (RMSSD and HF) and sympathetic (LF) components.

Due to the cross‐sectional study design, we cannot determine whether cytokine alterations precede or result from CAN. However, cardiac autonomic dysfunction has been recognised as a primary driver of arrhythmias and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in individuals with T2D [33]. Furthermore, antioxidant treatment was shown to lower systemic inflammation with subsequent measurable improvement in cardiac nerve function [34]. Our observed HRV imbalances may thus be driven by cytokine‐mediated mechanisms. To further support this, a prospective study found that HRV measures serve as predictive markers for future vaso‐vagal syncope recurrence in individuals with T2D [35]. The associations between HRV measures and cytokine levels found in our study thus suggest that cytokines could be a central driver of CAN pathogenesis.

The traditional view of systemic inflammation causing the axonal damage seen in CAN is increasingly challenged by evidence of a bidirectional relationship mediated by the inflammatory reflex [21, 36, 37, 38]. Our data support this bidirectional relationship, as patients with manifest CAN exhibited elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines alongside significantly reduced HRV across all parameters. This loss of parasympathetic (RMSSD/HF) and sympathetic/total (SDNN/LF) regulation reflects a failure of the innate anti‐inflammatory brakes [39], creating a feedback loop where autonomic impairment hinders inflammatory regulation, further accelerating neurodegeneration. This crosstalk is also observed in rheumatoid arthritis, where vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) has been proposed to attenuate cytokine levels and enhance cardiac vagal tone [37, 38]. Conversely, a study of VNS in individuals with T2D found no significant impact on systemic inflammation [36], highlighting a need for further investigation into disease‐specific responses.

Biomarker and Target Potential of Cytokines in CAN

Several treatment strategies have been explored to dampen inflammation and oxidative stress to minimise symptoms and prevent disease progression [14, 40]. Good glycaemic control is commonly emphasised as essential for preventing complications in individuals with T2D; however, while it does provide important benefits, studies indicate that it does not necessarily prevent the development of CAN. As no definitive therapies currently exist for CAN, investigations into anti‐inflammatory agents such as NSAIDs and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) have shown that reducing inflammatory markers does not necessarily translate into improved neural function [26, 41]. While GLP‐1RAs lack established direct effects on neural function, they offer significant vasoprotective benefits in T2D, independent of weight loss and glycaemic control [42]. However, caution is warranted in patients with CAN, as recent data suggest that GLP‐1 RAs may increase HR and ventricular events [43]. This is particularly relevant given that CAN is characterised by a high resting heart rate and sympathetic‐vagal imbalance, which could potentially be exacerbated by such pharmacological effects. Glucose sodium‐cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT‐2i) have shown pleiotropic benefits on CAN and clinical outcomes [44] and combined SGLT‐2i and GLP‐1RA treatment further attenuates oxidative stress and reduces major cardiovascular events in T2DM populations compared to monotherapy [45, 46]. Due to our study design, the specific effects of anti‐diabetic agents were not directly assessed. Notably, the distribution of these medications was similar across all CAN groups. Nevertheless, considering the pleiotropic benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors reported elsewhere [44] and the additive effect of combined therapy [45, 46], it is possible that the use of these agents may have mitigated the severity or prevalence of CAN in our study population.

While the potential of TNF‐α inhibitors and IL‐6 axis antagonists, such as tocilizumab and ziltivekimab, for reducing systemic inflammation and decreasing cardiovascular risk is an area of growing interest [27, 47, 48], any clinical implications remain speculative given the observational design of the current study. Furthermore, the use of TNF‐α inhibitors remains controversial, as some evidence paradoxically reports treatment‐induced neuropathy [49, 50].

Limitations

Our study has certain limitations. Firstly, it includes only a subgroup of the overall cohort, resulting in a limited sample size hampering statistical power, and it was conducted as a cross‐sectional study, which precludes any assumptions about causality between cytokines and clinical characteristics. Secondly, we cannot inform about whether the cytokine alterations found in patients with manifest CAN are specific for CAN as most participants also have other diabetes‐related complications. Thirdly, although most cytokine associations remained significant after adjustment for covariates, the absolute differences in cytokine levels were subtle, suggesting limited utility as standalone biomarkers. Moreover, some regression models demonstrated wide confidence intervals and large odds ratios, particularly for TNF‐α, reflecting the limited subgroup sizes. We dealt with this, however, by applying penalised logistic regression with Firth correction together with IQR scaling to improve coefficient stability and biological interpretability of the odds ratios. Finally, attenuation of some cytokine associations following broader adjustment suggests the presence of residual confounding. Given that participants with manifest CAN also exhibited higher age, BMI and HbA1c levels, it remains possible that the observed inflammatory profile partly reflects a broader state of multimorbidity rather than a CAN‐specific pathology. Future longitudinal studies in larger cohorts are therefore needed to clarify the temporal relationship between systemic inflammation and the development of CAN, as well as to validate the clinical utility of composite inflammatory cytokine signatures.

Conclusion

This study presents evidence that manifest CAN is linked to significantly higher serum levels of the pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐6, IFN‐γ and TNF‐α. Additionally, TNF‐α is increased already in the early CAN state and all investigated cytokines, except for IL‐2, are inversely correlated with measures of autonomic nerve function. Importantly, a composite risk score model utilising 3 or 6 cytokines provides a more robust prediction of CAN than individual cytokines. These findings suggest that cytokines may contribute to the severity of CAN through impaired cardiac autonomic control, underscoring the importance of further exploration of the pathophysiological role of cytokines in CAN and if therapeutic interventions targeting cytokines can mitigate the burden of CAN and its associated cardiovascular risk.
