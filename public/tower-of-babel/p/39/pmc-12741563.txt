TCRdesign: an antigen-specific generative language model for de novo design of T-cell receptors
Authors: Xiaokun Li, Qiang Yang, Long Xu, Weihe Dong, Kuanquan Wang, Suyu Dong, Wei Wang, Gongning Luo, Xianyu Zhang, Tiansong Yang, Xin Gao, Guohua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractT-cell receptors (TCR), which are heterodimers of and chains that recognize foreign antigens, are of great significance to current immunotherapy. Although artificial intelligence (AI) has explosively accelerated de novo protein design, the challenge of therapeutic TCR design has been overlooked by most researchers. Existing TCR engineering relies heavily on isolating antigen-specific TCRs from tumor tissues, which requires a large amount of labor resources and wet experimental verification. To mitigate this issue, we present TCRdesign, a pretrained generative protein language model (PLM) for the de novo design of artificial TCR -chain complementarity-determining region 3 sequences conditioned on antigen-binding specificity (BS). In parallel, we develop a high-accuracy binding predictor (TCRBinder) that couples paired / chain information with antigen sequences to assess BS. Our in silico comparisons demonstrate that (i) TCRdesign surpasses state-of-the-art baselines in generating antigen-specific TCR sequences. The model leverages paired-chain coherence to refine amino-acid level interaction patterns. (ii) TCRdesign-generated TCR sequences exhibit better antigen binding capability to diverse oncogenic hotspots compared with natural counterparts. (iii) TCRdesign inherits the intrinsic properties of large PLMs, enabling effectively identify the determinant residues in TCR-antigen binding, which enhances its interpretability. These results highlight the significant capability of TCRdesign in understanding and generating TCR sequences with an antigen-specific interaction pattern, charting a versatile path toward AI-driven T-cell engineering for precision immunotherapy.Keywords: TCR sequence design, protein large language model, antigen-specific generation, pretraining technique, encoder–decoder framework, model interpretability

Introduction

T-cell engineering has advanced the therapeutic landscape in immune-oncology, contributing unprecedented clinical responses in hematologic malignancies and providing novel avenues for solid tumor immunotherapy [1–4]. Although chimeric antigen receptor T-cells demonstrate the capacity for lymphocyte-specific rewiring [5, 6], their dependence on surface antigens constrains their therapeutic scope [7, 8]. In contrast, T-cell receptors (TCRs) empower engineered lymphocytes to interrogate intracellular oncoproteins presented by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules [9, 10], dramatically expanding the repertoire of targetable tumor antigens and enabling truly personalized interventions [11, 12]. TCRs include heterodimers composed of -chains [recombined by variable (V), diversity (D), and joining (J) (VDJ) gene domains] and -chains (recombined by V and J gene domains) [13], genetically analogous to the heavy chains and light chains of antibodies [14]. Antigen specificity is principally governed by chain complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) within the V domains [15], with the hypervariable CDR3 loop occupying the central role in peptide–MHC (pMHC) recognition, thus emerging as the primary focus for sequence engineering efforts [16].

Protein language models (PLMs) operating at foundation scale treat amino acid chains as structured grammar, driving recent breakthroughs in de novo protein design [17, 18]. Models such as ESM [19], ProtTrans [20], and ProGen [21], which are pretrained on billions of natural sequences, capture evolutionary constraints, translating into accurate predictions of structures, sequences, functionality, and mutational fitness [22]. Furthermore, autoregressive (AR) variants (e.g. ProteinMPNN [23] and LigandMPNN [24]) enable sampling of novel sequences with high folding fidelity. These capabilities have catalyzed a wave of in silico discovery pipelines, from enzyme engineering to membrane protein stabilization [25]. Among therapeutic modalities, antibody sequence design has benefited most prominently from this revolution. Deep generative language models, including ProGen2 [26], IgLM [27], and SeqDesign [28], are now increasing to orders of magnitude in affinity gains within just a few laboratory cycles. Meanwhile, generating neutralizing variants against emerging viral strains far faster than traditional hybridoma or phage display workflows. Advanced docking engines (e.g. Rosetta [29] and SnugDock [30]) and structure-aware diffusion models further refine complementarity-determining region (CDR) conformations, enabling de novo antibody sequence design [31]. Although research on TCR design is not as successful as that on antibody design, there are still some breakthrough works [32–37], such as GRATCR [32], TCR-VALID [36], and CATCR [37]. GRATCR is an epitope-aware conditional generator that couples pretrained encoders with an AR decoder to produce CDR3 sequences given a pMHC context. TCR-VALID, a deep generative framework based on a variational autoencoder, aims at designing meaningful continuous representations of TCR sequences. CATCR is a predictive framework that integrates structural descriptors and sequence-based features to infer TCR sequences conditioned on antigen epitopes. Despite these advances, current TCR design lacks the biophysical information of paired-chain coherence, highlighting the urgent need for a dedicated generative PLM capable of autonomously exploring the vast TCR sequence space while tailoring receptors to intracellular tumor-specific antigens [38].

To attain the aforementioned goal, we propose a pretrained TCR generative large language model (TCRdesign), designed for understanding the TCR binding specificity (BS) with tumor antigens and generating the -chain CDR3 sequence, which plays a central role in the T-cell immune activation. To resolve the dilemma of missing full-length paired-chain datasets and boost model learning representation, Roformers [39] with masked language modeling (MLM) are pretrained on a large number of paired TCR sequences, and we then fine-tune and evaluate the model on an antigen–TCR binding dataset. Afterwards, the fine-tuned Roformer and antigen–TCR pairing data are employed to generate the artificial CDR3 of the TCR.

TCRdesign showcases the notable improvement in the realm of TCR understanding and outperforms other advanced methods in generating CDR3 sequences with antigen-BS. To assess the BS of the CDR3 generated by TCRdesign for tumor antigens, an assembly of docking and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven approaches is employed. Recent AI methods have used tertiary structure information to infer the antigen–TCR BS [40, 41]. However, such high-quality 3D crystal structures are inadequate, and its resource-intensive and time-consuming to collect these structural data through wet-lab experiments [42]. Some sequence-based methods are introduced to predict the BS of antigen–TCR pairs in a relatively simpler manner [43, 44]. Although these methods utilize multimodal characteristics or large-scale pretraining techniques to achieve more precise results, the performance is still limited to the trained data itself, because they only consider the CDR3 sequence information instead of using paired-chain sequence information with coherence. Thus, we develop the TCRBinder for accurately assessing the BS of tumor antigens to TCR. TCRBinder employs large-scale self-supervised pretrained models for sequence feature learning representation from TCR-paired chains and antigens. Subsequently, feature fusion and BS prediction are conducted employing multi-fusion convolutional neural networks (MF-CNNs) and linear probing [22]. TCRBinder fully considers the paired-chain sequence information with coherence, enabling it to realize highly confident and generalizable BS predictions for neoantigens.

TCRdesign demonstrates a remarkable capability to generate de novo TCR CDR3 sequences. These sequences not only display diverse tertiary structures closely akin to natural counterparts, but also can be customized toward specific tumors with rare mutational situations through supervised fine-tuning. This “super alignment” capability of TCRdesign highlights its potential as a programmable tool for exploring and synthesizing the vast TCR space [45]. Furthermore, TCRdesign inherits the intrinsic properties of the cross-attention mechanism in pretrained language models, enabling effectively identify the determinant residues in TCR-antigen binding, which enhances its interpretability in generating CDR3 sequences.

Results

Overview of TCRdesign and TCRBinder

The overall workflow and model visualization of TCRdesign and TCRBinder are summarized in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1. The primary objective of TCRdesign is to perform de novo generation of CDR3 sequences with both biophysical viability and high specificity to tumor antigens (Fig. 1a). Complementing this generative model, TCRBinder provides a high-accuracy assessment of the BS between the generated CDR3 motifs (coupled with cognate -chains) and target antigens. By doing so, we could close the design-evaluate loop and enable rapid in silico screening of generated TCR candidates. As depicted in Fig. 1b, TCRdesign is a transformer-based model that employs the encoder–decoder structure. The ESM-based Antigen model (a style of masked language model [46] that focuses on bidirectional contextual understanding) is utilized as the encoder. The Roformer-based TCR model (a style of general language model [47] that uses bidirectional attention and AR objective to generate protein sequences) is employed as the decoder. Inspired by the philosophy of curriculum learning, the development of TCRdesign and TCRBinder is conducted in three distinct phases. First, two Roformer models are pretrained on full-length paired TCR - and -chain sequences that obtained from the Observed TCR Space (OTS) database [48], respectively (Fig. 1c). Subsequently, the TCRBinder model is constructed based on pretrained TCR -chain Roformer (-Roformer), TCR -chain Roformer (-Roformer), and ESM2 (Fig. 1d). We train it on the paired-chain antigen–TCR binding data. Lastly, the TCRdesign model is built by employing the pretrained ESM2 and -Roformer and trained on the antigen–TCR pairs.

Pretraining learns embedding patterns for TCRdesign

In recent years, accumulated studies have demonstrated that pretrained PLMs have significant performance for characterizing learning representations [18, 21–24]. In this study, the -Roformer and -Roformer are pretrained in a self-supervised manner. We assume that the TCRdesign model can absorb potential embedding patterns of TCR sequence representations by disclosing various TCR sequences. The predictive performance of TCR -Roformer and -Roformer is 98.89% and 99.51% in terms of accuracy, respectively, manifesting perfect TCR sequence feature extraction of the pretrained model.

To assess whether the pretrained Roformers could learn the embedding patterns of TCR by clustering the antigenic epitopes, we initially employ the Antigen–TCR binding dataset (Materials and methods), which includes epitope information of antigens. Specifically, we choose five antigens that occurs most frequently in the Antigen–TCR binding dataset and cluster the TCR sequences by these antigens. Then, we separately input the -chain sequences into the randomly initialized Roformer and the pretrained -Roformer to learn the embedding representations of TCR sequences. The Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) is executed to obtain the visualization of the characteristic distribution (Fig. 2a). As shown, the TCR embeddings clustered by antigens with pretrained Roformer are divided into several regions with clear boundaries, whereas those with the initialized model are scattered. Subsequently, we evaluate the capability of the pretrained model to differentiate certain cancer types. Akin to the epitopes, we employed both the pretrained and initialized models to represent embeddings, and then utilized UMAP for visualization (Fig. 2b). Similarly, the initialized model answers scattered embeddings, and by contrast, embeddings produced by the pretrained model are excellently clustered. Notably, the ability to differentiate epitopes is not as significant as that in cancers. This observation demonstrates that the pretrained model has a better understanding of learning the representation of cancers, since the diversity and cross-reactivity of antigens lead to the dispersion of clustering. In summary, these results show that pretraining improves the performance of the model to learn correct representations, for instance, the TCR’s binding antigens.

TCRBinder accurately predicts T-cell receptor–antigen binding specificity

To evaluate the performance of TCRBinder, we compare its capability to predict Antigen–TCR BS to that of six advanced sequence-based baselines, including DeepTCR [49], NetTCR-2.2 [50], ImRex [51], TCR-AI [52], TEIM [53], and UniPMT-PT [54]. These baselines span classical motif learners, e.g. DeepTCR and TCR-AI, to recent foundation models that utilize single CDR3 segments, e.g. UniPMT. More detailed information about these models is provided (Supplementary Note 1). To make the comparison fair, TCRdesign and other advanced methods are trained on the Antigen–TCR binding dataset (see Materials and methods). We split the binding dataset into a ratio of 8:1:1 for training, validation, and testing with a random split strategy, which means we randomly sample from Antigen–TCR binding dataset by BS. The parameter settings of all compared methods are default from their published studies. We use two-sided Welch’s t-test for statistical significance. The results illustrate that TCRBinder achieves the highest scores on both discrimination and early retrieval metrics (Fig. 2c). Specifically, the average value of the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) gains a 7.2% performance improvement against the second-best model, while the average value of the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPR) increased by 17.6% relative to the strongest baseline (Supplementary Table S1). The enhancement is particularly noteworthy, since AUPR is a stringent measurement of practical utility for classifying imbalance in a binding dataset.

We further probe the generalization ability beyond the interpolation regime by introducing an Unseen Binding benchmark (Materials and methods), in which each test antigen or TCR and corresponding binding items are unseen in the training set (Fig. 2d). Therefore, predictive tools must accurately infer contact logic rather than memorize recurrent templates. TCRBinder maintains a clear performance margin under this extrapolative setting (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table S2), AUROC and AUPR gains over the second-ranked method expanded to 17.1% and 21.6%, respectively, underscoring the robustness of the learned representations.

A fine-grained epitope-wise comparison further illustrates the benefit of our design (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. S2e). Across five clinically relevant antigenic epitopes, comprising IE-1 (KLGGALQAK), EBNA-3A (FLRGRAYGL), BMLF-1 (GLCTLVAML), PP65 (MLNIPSINV), and NP (LPRRSGAAGA), TCRBinder consistently outperforms other state-of-the-art models in terms of AUROC, balanced accuracy (B.Acc), and AUPR. The per-epitope gains demonstrate that our proposed model does not rely on a single dominant motif but instead captures the spectrum of interface chemistries that mediate recognition.

We consider that there are several aspects of TCRBinder that appear central to these results. Chiefly, large-scale Roformer pretraining endows the - and -chain encoders with a nuanced grasp of immunogenetic grammar. The technique of pretraining allows the network to recognize V-gene preferences, junctional insertions, and convergent evolution patterns that are invisible to randomly initialized models. Therefore, the predictive performance of TCRBinder drops dramatically when pretraining is banned (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). Nevertheless, our proposed method remains compelling compared with these baselines (Supplementary Fig. S2). We believe that the second shining point is the paired-chain coherence, which explicitly aligns - and -embeddings in latent space, allowing TCRBinder to model the cooperative “hot-spot” architecture of the binding interface. Furthermore, the MF-CNN combines chain-specific and antigen-specific features at multiple receptive-field widths, greatly improving the learning representation for biological characteristics of BS (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). This innovative learning block mirrors the hierarchical nature of antigen recognition, whereby local CDR3-antigen interactions are modulated by global V-domain orientation. Collectively, the reasonable construction of a tailored deep-learning model could greatly facilitate the understanding of BS in the representation space and support the development of the antigen-specific TCR designer.

TCRdesign outperforms baselines in designing T-cell receptor sequences with binding specificity

To benchmark the capacity of TCRdesign to create -chain CDR3 loops, we employ a general PLM, i.e. ProGen2 [26], and two TCR-specific models, i.e. GRATCR [32] and CATCR [37] (Supplementary Note 2). To ensure the comparison of TCRBinder and other advanced methods is fair, we train them on the same datasets with identical training strategy. To balance model performance and computational cost, we choose the ProGen2-Large version. Notably, as a general model, the input of the ProgGen2-Large model is a joint sequence of an antigen sequence and a corresponding TCR sequence (the designed part of the joint sequence will be masked) when designing TCR -chains for a particular antigen. We evaluate these methods in two complementary sets of metrics, namely language-model fidelity and functional plausibility. The language-model fidelity assesses whether the artificial sequences conform to global repertoire statistics and structural priors, as well as the functional plausibility measures the binding propensity and structure surrogates.

For the language-model fidelity, the density estimated by perplexity (PPL) and self-reconstruction rate (SRR) highlights the intrinsic quality of the generated TCR. Since the limitation of space, the detailed definition of used metrics are provided in Supplementary Note 3. TCRdesign exhibits a more compelling PPL scores, namely, 4.82 versus 5.31 for ProGen2-Large (Fig. 3a), indicating that CDR sequences generated by TCRdesign are more confidently closer to natural TCR repertoire. In contrast, TCRdesign achieves an SRR of 0.891 for the generated sequences, higher than other advanced methods. Sequences produced by TCRdesign yield higher predicted template-modeling score (pTM), interface pTM (ipTM), and predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores (Fig. 3a). We also analyze the sequence similarity and diversity of artificial sequences to the natural sequence space (Supplementary Fig. S3). The comparison of TCRdesign and ProGen2-Large at different PPL ranges demonstrates the superiority of the proposed method in designing TCR sequences (two-sided Welch’s t-test, 0.001, Supplementary Fig. S4). Although the distributions of TCRdesign and ProGen2-Large look similar, our proposed method greatly improves the predictive performance. Additionally, we also compare the capability of generate other TCR -chain sequences with ProGen2-Large [26] and TCR-VALID [36]. The results demonstrate that TCRdesign can be easily extended to other parts of TCR -chain sequences and the model’s performance can be improved by co-generating other CDR loops, since only the generating CDR3 loops are highly variable in natural space (Supplementary Table S5). These statistics indicate that our proposed designer writes grammatically correct receptors.

To verify whether the generated TCR -chain sequence will affect the BS of antigens, we employ TCRBinder to predict the binding probability of generated TCR -chains with an antigen. We utilize the Levenshtein distance to compute the dissimilarity between generated TCR -chain sequences and natural TCR -chain sequences. Subsequently, we divide the dataset into subsets based on the Levenshtein distance. To ensure the comparison is fair, we employ the antigen–TCR binding pairs from STCRDab ( 315) for TCRdesign and compared methods to generate TCR -chain sequences. When artificial CDR3 loops are grafted onto their natural sequences, binding probability remains robust across increasing sequence divergence from the native loop (two-sided Welch’s t-test, , Fig. 3b). Even at a Levenshtein distance of 10, TCRdesign maintains a median probability >0.80, whereas ProGen2-Large drops <0.68 and random mutations collapse to baseline. This superiority is consistent with the decoder’s explicit exposure to antigen embeddings during training, which biases mutations toward positions that tolerate change without disrupting key contact hot spots. Additionally, structural analysis stratifies artificial sequences by backbone Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) from the parental loop (, Fig. 3c). TCRdesign exhibits high binding scores across 0.9–2.0 Å, indicating that the learned sequence-structure mapping can accommodate moderate geometric drift while preserving interface chemistry. Furthermore, we also benchmark binding probability as a function of sequence similarity to the natural TCR sequence ( 0.001, Supplementary Fig. S5). Across all similarity bins, our proposed designer shows significant improvement against the ProGen2-Large model. To compare the binding affinity of these in silico generated TCRs to high-affinity natural TCRs, we utilize Rosetta to calculate the binding energies between TCR and antigens from STCRDab, which is comprised of the experimentally verified TCR–pMHC complexes with high binding affinity. The results show that TCRs generated by TCRdesign exhibit lower binding energies compared with natural TCRs, indicating the advantages of TCRdesign in TCR design (Supplementary Fig. S6). We believe that these improvements mainly stem from our elaborate encoder–decoder architecture. By coupling an encoder with a comprehensive understanding of antigen and an AR decoder that receives antigen embeddings via cross-attention, TCRdesign learns to distribute sequence probability mass toward variants that complement peptide charge, shape, and hydrogen-bond networks.

To visualize how TCRdesign explores the immunogenic receptor landscape, we project 50 000 generated TCR sequences onto a UMAP learned from Antigen–TCR Binding pairs (Materials and methods, Fig. 4). The TCRdesign-generated TCR sequences form contiguous manifolds that bridge sparsely populated regions of natural TCR space, implying that the model can interpolate between canonical clonotypes and reach undersampled but structurally admissible zones. The structure of these TCRdesign-generated sequences is predicted by ESM3, they achieve TM-scores from 0.81 to 0.89. In conclusion, our proposed designer not only surpasses existing sequence generators in language-model metrics but also, crucially, translates those gains into higher predicted binding and structural plausibility.

TCRdesign generates structurally faithful T-cell receptors against oncogenic hotspot neoantigens

To further estimate whether TCRdesign is sensitive to clinically actionable mutations, we prompt our model with two representative oncogenic mutations from PDB database. One of the mutations is the KRAS G12-derived peptide [56], which drives various cancers, such as pancreatic, colorectal, and lung adenocarcinoma. The other mutation is tumor-testis MAGE antigen that frequently re-express in multiple solid malignancies [57]. For each neoantigen, we generate 100 artificial sequences and subsequently subject them to ESM3 to infer the structures.

Accordingly, four representative designs are visualized (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S6). For the KRAS evaluation, generated TCRs achieve BS of 0.89 and 0.72 for 7PB2 and 9IKY, respectively. These two designs almost obtain concomitant fold confidence values (pTM=0.83, pLDDT=0.95) on high-resolution experimental models. The TM-align [58] is employed to measure the structural information. 7PB2 and 9IKY yield TM-Scores of 0.956 and 0.971 and backbone RMSDs of 1.52 Å and 1.07 Å, respectively. Similar fidelity is observed for MAGE-A-derived neoantigens. These designed TCRs obtain BS values 0.99, TM-Scores 0.981, and RMSDs Å relative to natural structures 7QPJ and 5BS0. Of note, the overlap in crystals demonstrates that CDR3 loops produced by TCRdesign (colored pink or yellow) adopt nearly identical exit angles and hydrogen-bond networks at the peptide apex. Despite they differ by up to seven nonconservative substitutions from their natural counterparts. These results underline two advantages of the proposed designer. Primarily, the antigen encoder embeds the biological feature with contextual BS, enabling the AR decoder to select residue chemistries that complement hotspot side chains such as G12D Aspartate in KRAS or Tryptophan in MAGE backbones. Secondly, pretrained Roformer provides a latent prior with paired-chain coherence, ensuring that oncogenic mutations are introduced to enhance peptide contacts. We believe that the capacity of TCRdesign to generate high-fidelity receptors could accelerate the development of off-the-shelf TCR libraries, facilitating precision immunotherapy against some of the most intractable oncogenic drivers.

TCRdesign identifies determinant residues with high interpretation

We subsequently validate the interpretability of TCRdesign and its capability to identify crucial interaction residues during the learning process. The cross-attention mechanism that couples the antigen encoder to the AR decoder in TCRdesign yields a residue-level interaction map that rationalizes the resulting binding modes. Taking the complex human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A*02:01-NLSALGIFST-Mel5 (PDB ID: 7Q99) for instance [59], the attention weights from the peptide tokens are projected onto the -chain sequence (Fig. 6a). Intuitively, the cross-attention focuses on positions 91–105 in the -chain sequence, which is the CDR3 fragments (the color of magenta indicates the high-confidence attentions). When these attention weights are mapped into a 2D matrix (Fig. 6b and Table S7), the results show that the THR (Threonine) and GLU (Glutamic acid) in the CDR3 loop are more likely to bind with the antigen NLSALGIFST. The structure of the TCRdesign-generated TCR sequence is also inferred by ESM3 (Fig. 6c, TM-Score=0.924, RMSD=1.69 Å). The cross-attention visualizations of 7PB2 and 5BS0 are also exhibited (Supplementary Figs S7 and S8) An interesting observation is that the designed structure forms a hydrogen-bond triage and a -stack that clamps the peptide backbone, which is in line with the characteristic of high tumor-reactive TCRs [60]. Collectively, TCRdesign provides a transparent window into the sequence determinants of specificity. We consider the proposed designer to be a powerful tool for the safe translation of AI-generated TCRs into clinical immunotherapies.

Discussion

Here we introduce TCRdesign, an antigen-specific generative language model for TCR CDR3 sequence design, and TCRBinder, a deep learning model using antigen sequences and paired-chain TCR sequences to predict the BS between them. Based on 5.3 million paired / repertoires for self-supervised pretraining and >32 000 curated antigen–TCR pairs for fine-tuning, our method achieves three main advances. Primarily, it establishes a foundation scale representation of TCR characteristics, which can be perfectly stratified by the epitopes or cancers through pretraining techniques. The linear probes also outperform other advanced baselines on BS benchmarks. Secondly, our well-designed encoder–decoder framework translates the understanding of BS into generation. We train TCRdesign and other advanced methos in the Antigen–TCR binding dataset with identical training strategy to ensure comparison is fair. TCRdesign generates sequences with lower PPL, higher SRR and superior fold confidence than other state-of-the-art sequence generators. The generated TCR sequences retain high BS for antigens even at large sequence or backbone divergences. The reason why we focus on the design of TCR CDR3 sequences conditioned on antigen BS is that antigen-specific CDR3 sequences are of great significance to the T-cell activation and immune response. Furthermore, we conduct the generation of other parts of TCR -chain sequences, i.e. CDR1 and CDR2. The results demonstrate TCRdesign can be easily extended to all sorts of TCR -chain sequences. Lastly, the antigen-aware cross-attention highlights residue-level interpretability. With this powerful module, TCRdesign identifies determinant residues as contact “hot spots” in antigen–TCR BS, enabling programmable targeting of oncogenic neoantigens such as KRAS-G12D and MAGE-A, with structural fidelity to natural references.

We attribute the superiority of the model to three factors: (i) TCRdesign employs large-scale paired-chain pretraining to learn the representation of chain coherence. (ii) TCRdesign combines the MLM objective and GLM objective to integrate the understanding of antigenic binding specificity when generating TCR sequences. (iii) TCRBinder merges the chain-specific and antigen-specific information with multi-fusion CNN block, guiding the hierarchical nature of TCR recognition. These factors allow the model to navigate sparsely sampled regions of TCR space while preserving foldability and binding competence.

Some significant limitations remain within our methods. The first is the current encoder attends only to antigen sequence, omitting explicit MHC polymorphism and 3D context that are known to regulate specificity. Exploring the binding pattern of TCR-pMHC is also important for TCR generation task. However, it is beyond the scope of this work, and thus we will investigate how the TCR-pMHC will modulate the TCR sequence generation in the following study. Besides, the scarcity of confirmed negative binding pairs still limits the performance of BS prediction. Most datasets record positives while untested combinations are treated as negatives, which may bring label noise, prevalence bias, and spectrum bias, and can inflate apparent specificity or distort calibration. Furthermore, the sequence generation is conditioned on CDR3. The lack of larger, high-quality datasets makes it difficult to extend the generation task to full-length paired-chain TCR sequences. Additionally, only the in silico validations are conducted for TCRdesign, however, these cannot substitute for comprehensive functional assays. The potential off-target cross-reactivity and immunogenicity of TCRdesign-generated sequences must still be evaluated in wet-lab experiments. Lastly, occasional hallucinations of cysteine-rich or aggregation-prone motifs highlight the need for additional biochemical regularization. Future work should integrate structured MHC representations, exploit multimodal pretraining that unifies sequence, structure and functional annotations, like ESM3 dose. Meanwhile, coupling the model with wet-lab loops to continuously refine both the generator and the predictor.

In summary, the proposed TCRdesign integrates the ability of large-scale TCR pretraining and the effectiveness of feature fusion, resulting in excellent BS prediction performance and the superiority of designing a TCR sequence for antigen recognition. Afterwards, the direct sequence generation and high interpretation make it an insightful and robust tool for the generation of artificial TCRs into practical T-cell design.

Materials and methods

Datasets

The Observed TCR Space

The OTS database is a public source of consistently processed and annotated, full-length, paired-chain TCR sequences [49]. With the rapid development of cell handling techniques, higher-throughput single-cell sequencing is allowed. Thus, the single-cell TCR profiling data can be analyzed with higher sequencing depth relative to bulk TCR sequencing. We collected the OTS database and extracted the paired TCR -chain and -chain sequences, as well as the -chain junction sequences (usually treated as CDR3 fragments). It now includes 6 977 225 filtered TCR sequences from pan-population across over 66 studies and over at least 75 individuals (24 May 2025). For each study, we recorded the “sequence:alignment_aa” and “junction_aa” contents comprising the amino acid sequences. Subsequently, we removed all the sequences containing illegal amino acid characters or repetitive sequences in the whole dataset. By doing so, we obtained almost 5.3 million TCR paired-chain sequences for the first-time pretraining of Roformer models. Afterwards, we further refined the collected sequences into human counterparts that were only diagnosed with solid tumors when sequencing. This elaborated subset contains 968 941 paired-chain sequences and cover with most common cancers, including acute myeloid leukemia ( 15 332), basal cell carcinoma ( 44 703), breast cancer ( 39 980), colorectal cancer ( 33 012), metastatic epithelial tumor ( 185 334), gastric carcinoma (13,549), melanoma (38 188), non-small cell lung cancer ( 379 122), pancreatic cancer ( 75 193), prostate cancer ( 10 557), renal cell carcinoma (71 020), and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma ( 62 951). The resulting pan-cancer paired-chain TCR sequences are employed for the second-round pretraining of Roformer models.

Chain coherence

Recent studies have demonstrated that the light-chain coherence of the B-cell receptor plays a crucial role in correctly orienting the heavy-chain CDRs for antigen binding [61], as well as contributing to critical germline interactions through its own CDR loops. However, the coherence signal in TCRs is more likely to be balanced between chains. Because the energetics of TCR-pMHC complexes appear to be more evenly shared between the -chain and -chain than do the energetics of antibody–antigen complexes between the heavy and light chains. The TCR chain coherence can be obtained by following Raybould et al.’s method [62].

Antigen–TCR binding dataset

We largely depend on the OTS database to learn the full-length paired-chain TCR sequences with coherence information. However, the design task of antigen-specific TCR strongly requires the integrated information of both antigen and TCR sequences. Thus, beside the OTS database we also elaborately include the Antigen–TCR binding dataset. The antigen–TCR binding data are collected from the UniPMT, which is a comprehensive and well-structured database. This curated database includes 144 053 TCRs, 291 632 antigens, 208 MHCs, 593 109 TCR-pMHC binding events, 155 479 antigen–TCR binding events, and 70 112 pMHC binding events. In this study, 32 021 antigen–TCR binding pairs are obtained to train and test the TCRBinder model. To couple the full-length chain information with antigen–TCR binding pairs, CDR3 fragments are mapped into pan-cancer paired-chain TCR space. We retrieve whether the CDR3 sequences overlap with the junction part in TCR -chains. If an overleap has occurred, the paired TCR chain and antigen are regarded as a positive sample for the TCRBinder model, and the BS is labeled as 1. Thus, 38 764 hits are collected as the antigen–TCR dataset. To create the decoys, we randomly shuffle the antigen–TCR pairs and sample 10-fold disordered antigen–TCR pairs that are unseen in positive candidates by clustering in antigen specificity.

Unseen binding dataset

We also obtain 619 hits of antigen–TCR binding samples that are unseen in the antigen–TCR dataset for further evaluation [41, 54], and the same sampling strategy is employed to generate the negative samples. The unseen binding dataset is constructed by Lu et al. [41]. Specifically, they collect the 619 antigen–TCR binding samples that are experimentally verified by the TCR affinity testing, such as ELISPOT, Cytotoxic lysis by T-cell, and Flow Cytometry analysis. Training procedure

In the stage of pretraining TCR Roformer models, over 5 million paired - and -chain sequences are obtained from the OTS database [48]. Both -Roformer and -Roformer encode the relative position of each amino acid in sequences with a rotation matrix, and utilize a self-supervised pretraining manner to learn the biophysical representation patterns within a large available TCR repertoire space in the first-round of pretraining (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. S1a). Afterwards, we elaborately chose 968 941 paired-chain TCR sequences with coherence information across 12 solid tumor types. Since we want the second-round pretraining to only focus on the TCR characteristics with solid tumors, the TCR sequences extracted from influenza and viruses (e.g. COVID-19 [63]) are filtered. These selected paired TCR sequences are used for the second pretraining of -Roformer and -Roformer, respectively. The mask amino acid (MAA) strategy is applied to the pretraining phase to characterize the primary structure information of TCR paired chain sequences.

In the stage of constructing TCRBinder, the pretrained -Roformer, -Roformer, and ESM2 are employed and fine-tuned on the antigen–TCR binding task, allowing the model to extract the prior characteristics of BS to TCRs. These three feature extractors in TCRBinder serve to capture the high-level representation from the antigen, -chain, and -chain sequences. Then, each feature extractor is followed by a multi-layer MF-CNN. The -Roformer and -Roformer from the stage of pretraining TCR Roformer models are utilized to learn the sequence features of -chain and -chain, respectively. A pretrained ESM2 [64] model from previous works is used to learn the sequence feature of tumor-specific antigen. The multi-layer MF-CNN is employed to integrate the sequential feature representations from three pretraining models. We concatenate the output features from the MF-CNN and use them as the final information to infer the BS.

In the last stage, we construct a sequence-to-sequence objective via the encoder–decoder architecture to implement the TCRdesign model. The pretrained weight parameters from ESM2 are employed to initialize the encoder module. For the decoder module, we initialize the self-attention layers [65] with the pretrained weight parameters from the -Roformer. The cross-attention layers in the decoder module are trained from scratch through the fine-tuning task on the antigen–TCR pairs. This scheme capitalizes on the large-scale unlabeled TCR data exploited during Roformer pretraining while avoiding the limited training data size for full training. Specifically, the antigen encoder contains 12 consecutive antigen layers, and the TCR decoder is stacked with 12 TCR layers (a self-attention layer and a cross-attention layer are included in each TCR layer). Of note, we treat the pretrained -Roformer as the self-attention layer. For the antigen encoder, TCRdesign employs the weight parameters from the pretrained ESM2 model, enabling the powerful contextual understanding ability for antigen sequences. Interestingly, abundant evolution information is contained in the ESM2 embedding space, and thus, the antigen encoder can learn the accurate evolutionary routes of antigens for BS. For each TCR layer, the weights of the self-attention layer are inherited from pretrained -Roformer, and the weights of the cross-attention layer are randomly initialized and learned through fine-tuning from antigen–TCR pairs data. During decoding, each TCR cross-attention sublayer takes the last antigen layer as input key and value matrices, and treats the output of corresponding TCR self-attention sublayer as a query matrix. Thus, TCRdesign can realize the understanding of antigen–TCR binding patterns and the sequence-to-sequence task of generating CDR3 sequences with BS. Roformer is a variant of the classic transformer that employs rotary position embeddings rather than the absolute positional encodings used in the standard transformer structure. ESM2 model

Recently, Meta AI released ESM2 [19], a cutting-edge PLM trained with an MLM objective [46] that parallels the strategy pioneered by BERT in natural-language processing. After training on billions of evolutionarily diverse sequences, ESM2 is compelling in modeling local side-chain preferences and long-range structural constraints at atomic-resolution. This procedure yields high-fidelity latent embeddings that are remarkably superior to conventional one-hot or physicochemical encodings across a spectrum of downstream tasks, including structure prediction, function annotation, and mutational fitness estimation. For tumor-specific antigen research, ESM2’s contextual understanding is particularly advanced. ESM2 captures subtle sequence motifs that govern MHC presentation, proteasomal cleavage, and T-cell recognition. These capabilities establish ESM2 as a dual-purpose foundation: (i) predicting neoantigen immunogenicity and (ii) guiding antigen-aware TCR generation in frameworks like TCRdesign. Multi-fusion convolutional neural network

MF-CNN defines a maximal sequence length that inputs longer than this threshold are truncated, whereas shorter inputs are padded with a special token “[PAD].” This strategy allows for accommodating protein or peptide sequences of any length up to the predefined limit while maintaining fixed-size tensors for batched computation.

The pretraining strategy

During both large-scale pretraining and downstream fine-tuning, we adopted a unique amino acid (UAA) tokenizer tailored to the high mutation sensitivity of immunoglobulin sequences. Under the UAA scheme, each residues symbol occurred in the corpus is assigned with a dedicated token index, ensuring a one-to-one correspondence between sequence characters and model inputs. Each token utilizes integer value to represent a specific residue. After tokenization, every amino acid is then projected through a weighted, learnable embedding layer, yielding compact vector representations that preserve the identity of single-site mutations while allowing the network to model their positional context. This fine-grained vocabulary is critical for recognizing the subtle insertions, deletions and point substitutions that frequently govern TCR-epitope binding.

Our large-scale pretraining is driven by the MAA objective, an analog of BERT’s masked language modelling. MAA randomly masks a part of the tokens in the input text sequence and then predicts the masked tokens using the model. During optimization, 15% of amino acids are replaced with a special [mask] symbol, the model will recover the original amino acids solely from its neighbors, thereby learning both local biochemistry and long-range structural correlations in an entirely self-supervised fashion. We utilize the cross-entropy loss function for TCR representation learning.

The Roformer-base version is selected to learn the paired-chain TCR feature representations. This model is stacked with 12 Transformer blocks, each comprised of 12 attention heads, a hidden size of 768, and an intermediate feed-forward dimension of 1536. The activation function of the hidden layer is GLU. To keep both computational efficiency and precision, we employ the 32-bit floating-point format, which is robust to overflow and underflow errors. Both TCR -chain and -chain are trained on 4 NVIDIA GeForce A100 GPUs under data-parallel distribution. In the phase of first-round pretraining, over 5.3 million paired-chain TCR sequences with coherence information are employed to train the -Roformer and -Roformer models. We utilized the AdamW optimizer and a learning rate of with 0.1 warmup, as well as a batch size of 1280. The training of the model takes 15 days. In the second-round pretraining phase, 968 941 paired-chain TCR sequences of solid tumors are used to train the -Roformer and -Roformer models with the same parameters. The batch size is set to 128. This training process costs about 33 h.

Antigen–TCR binding prediction

To predict the TCR specificity to antigens, the antigen–TCR binding dataset is split into the training, validation, and testing sets in a ratio of 8:1:1. Two separate learning rates are employed for optimizing the Roformers and MF-CNN to enhance TCRBinder’s performance. Furthermore, we facilitate the training process using a linear learning rate schedule. We operate a 10-repeated training strategy with distinct random seeds and record the average performance for the proposed model and other advanced methods. The AUROC, AUPR, and balanced accuracy (B. Acc) are utilized as evaluation criteria in the BS prediction task (Supplementary Note 4). Additionally, we further evaluate the generalization ability of our model in a new dataset where all samples are unseen in the antigen–TCR binding dataset.

This sequence-to-sequence generation task is designed to be an AR generative modelling. At inference time, the model auto-regressively samples tokens so that each predicted amino acid is conditioned on the full prefix generated thus far. In this way, the AR framework synthesizes complete sequences one position at a time while respecting the learned distribution of the training data.

According to the philosophy of curriculum learning, we initiate the training of TCRdesign with pretrained ESM2-150 and -Roformer, followed by a novel cross-attention. The pretrained ESM2-150 model is initialized as the antigen layer in the encoder. Meanwhile, we stack the TCR layers to build the AR decoder. Specifically, each TCR layer contains a self-attention block and a cross-attention block, where the self-attention block is initialized by the pretrained -Roformer, as well as the cross-attention block is randomly initialized and trained from scratch in the sequence-to-sequence generation task. We train TCRdesign for 20 epochs with the antigen–CDR3 paired data from the antigen–TCR binding dataset, enabling it to generate TCR CDR3 sequences with both biophysical understanding and high specificity to tumor antigens. In the generation phase, we utilize the Beam search strategy to boost the output sequence quality and save memory consumption [66]. The Beam search is a breadth-first searching method that exhibits significant improvement against the greedy search method. The Beam width is set to 10. After the generation of CDR3 candidates, we replace the artificial sequences with natural counterparts. Subsequently, the trained TCRBinder is employed for binding prediction.
