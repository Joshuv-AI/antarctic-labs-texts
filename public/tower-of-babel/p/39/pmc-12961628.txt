Clinical Evaluation of Drug–Drug Interactions With Aficamten
Authors: Neha Maharao, Donghong Xu, Punag Divanji, Tyrell J. Simkins, Jianlin Li, Camelia Dumitrescu, Priyanka Solanki, Adrienne Griffith, Stephen B. Heitner, Stuart Kupfer, Polina German, Justin D. Lutz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAficamten is a next‐in‐class small molecule cardiac myosin inhibitor that was recently approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of symptomatic obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM). A comprehensive drug–drug interaction (DDI) evaluation of aficamten was achieved through two phase 1 studies in healthy participants. Aficamten as a victim of DDIs was studied using a moderate‐to‐strong P450 inducer (carbamazepine) and CYP inhibitors—itraconazole (strong CYP3A), paroxetine (strong CYP2D6), fluconazole (strong CYP2C19 and moderate 2C9 and 3A), and fluoxetine (strong CYP2D6 and 2C19). Aficamten's potential for P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) inhibition was assessed using dabigatran etexilate (sensitive P‐gp substrate). Minor increases in aficamten exposure (area under the curve) were observed following treatment with itraconazole (26%), paroxetine (27%), and fluoxetine (32%) compared with aficamten alone. Treatment with carbamazepine decreased aficamten exposure by 50%. Concomitant administration of aficamten with fluconazole, a multi‐P450 inhibitor, resulted in a 278% increase in aficamten exposure. Taken together, results from these studies indicate that aficamten is primarily eliminated via CYP2C9‐mediated metabolism (fraction metabolized [fm] = 54%) with contributions from CYP2D6 (fm = 21%), CYP3A (fm = 21%), and minimal metabolism via CYP2C19 (fm = 3%). A small increase (26%–27%) in dabigatran exposure was observed in the presence of aficamten; indicative of weak inhibition of P‐gp. Aficamten was well tolerated, and adverse events were generally mild. In conclusion, aficamten is metabolized by multiple P450 enzymes, limiting its DDI liability. Only weak DDIs (< 2‐fold) are likely from strong inhibition of any one pathway, and only moderate (< 5‐fold) impact on aficamten exposure is expected with strong multi‐pathway inhibitors or inducers.

Introduction

Aficamten is a next‐in‐class small molecule cardiac myosin inhibitor (CMI) that was recently approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of symptomatic obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM). Aficamten is designed to reduce the hypercontractility that underlies the pathophysiology of HCM. By targeting cardiac myosin at the sarcomere level, aficamten reduces the excessive contractile force characteristic of this disease, thereby addressing its root cause. Unlike historically used therapies for obstructive HCM (oHCM), aficamten acts directly on the molecular motor responsible for contraction, diminishing the number of active myosin heads that generate force [1].

In a phase 3, placebo controlled trial in participants with symptomatic oHCM (SEQUOIA‐HCM), echocardiography‐guided dosing of aficamten once daily (QD) led to a substantial reduction in left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) gradients, symptom relief, and enhanced exercise capacity over a 24‐week treatment period [2]. The trial also confirmed the safety and tolerability of aficamten, with a low incidence of treatment emergent adverse events (TEAEs) compared with placebo and infrequent reductions of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 50% [2]. In a recent phase 3 trial in participants with symptomatic oHCM (MAPLE‐HCM), aficamten was superior to metoprolol with respect to improvement of exercise capacity, reduction of LVOT gradients, and amelioration of heart failure symptoms [3].

In healthy participants, aficamten was administered as single doses up to 75 mg and multiple once‐daily doses of 5–10 mg [4]. Patients with oHCM received echocardiography‐guided, titrated doses of aficamten starting at 5 mg once daily [5]. Dose escalations occurred in 5 mg increments up to a maximum dose of 30 mg once daily for a maximum treatment duration of 120 weeks [5]. The drug demonstrated linear and time‐independent pharmacokinetics (PK), supporting the use of single‐dose studies to adequately characterize its multiple‐dose PK profile [4]. Results from a human mass balance study indicated that aficamten is primarily eliminated through metabolism and biliary excretion, with minimal contribution from renal elimination [6]. The principal metabolic pathway involves aficamten hydroxylation, resulting in the formation of a pharmacologically inactive enantiomeric pair of metabolites, CK‐3834282 and CK‐3834283.

In vitro studies yielded conflicting results on the exact responsibility of cytochrome P450s (CYPs) involved in aficamten metabolism, but CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 were generally implicated [7]. Therefore, aficamten was clinically evaluated as a victim of DDIs with CYP inhibitors and inducers with the goal of characterizing the metabolic pathways responsible for aficamten elimination in healthy participants. Additionally, aficamten was evaluated as a potential in vivo inhibitor of P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) mediated transport based on in vitro findings.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Two phase 1, open‐label, fixed‐sequence studies were conducted in healthy participants. Eligible participants in both studies were healthy adults aged 19–45 years, with body mass index between 18.0 and 30.0 kg/m2, LVEF ≥ 55% at screening visit, creatinine clearance ≥ 90.0 mL/min (determined via Cockcroft–Gault equation), normal clinical laboratory evaluations, normal liver biochemistry, and no significant medical history.

The studies were conducted in compliance with Good Clinical Practice principles, applicable regulatory principles, and the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Advarra Institutional Review Board. All participants provided written informed consent prior to all screening procedures.

To evaluate potential DDI risk, aficamten was assessed in clinical studies as a victim (Cohorts A through E) and as a perpetrator (Cohort F) using probe drugs listed in Table 1. The treatment periods for aficamten and probe drugs in each cohort are provided in detail in Table 1 and Figure S1. A fixed sequence design with a sufficient washout between treatments was employed in each cohort to minimize the effect of confounding intersubject variability on treatment effect (Figure S1). The dosing regimen of probe drugs and meal timings relative to aficamten and probe administration were carefully determined based on the PK profiles of the perpetrators to ensure that drug concentrations reach levels that result in near maximum inhibition or induction of the metabolic pathway being studied. The variable meal timing in this study was dictated by the administration requirements of the perpetrator drugs to achieve maximal systemic exposure and effect. This approach is justified as aficamten can be administered without regard to food. In all cohorts, participants remained in the clinical research unit until discharged. Aficamten was supplied as 5 mg tablets (Cytokinetics Inc., South San Francisco, CA, USA) while probe drugs were commercially available products sourced by the study centers. Notably, the itraconazole capsule formulation was used in Cohort A. The studies were statistically powered, and the following sample sizes were determined using historical data to detect differences in aficamten PK with and without probe drugs. In Cohorts A through E, up to 17 participants were enrolled to ensure that at least 14 participants completed the study. In Cohort F, up to 25 participants were enrolled to get at least 22 evaluable participants.

Rationale for Dose Selection

The safety, pharmacodynamics, and PK of aficamten have been extensively evaluated following administration of single (1–75 mg) and multiple QD (5, 7.5, and 10 mg) doses in healthy participants. Aficamten doses up to 30 mg QD were safe and well tolerated in HCM patients in the phase 2 studies with the 20 mg QD dose representing the highest clinical dose evaluated in the phase 3 clinical program. To assess the impact on aficamten as a victim of DDIs, an intermediate aficamten dose of 10 mg (single dose) was conservatively chosen. This approach aligns with regulatory guidance [8], as the established linear and dose‐proportional PK of aficamten ensure that the magnitude of a metabolic DDI (treatment/reference AUC ratio) remains consistent across its therapeutic dose range. The conservative 10 mg dose was selected to mitigate any potential risk to healthy volunteers from an unanticipated larger increase in exposure. A higher 20 mg dose of aficamten was selected for evaluation with an inducer carbamazepine. A single 20 mg aficamten dose was chosen to assess the potential of aficamten as a P‐gp inhibitor. The use of single dose was appropriate given that the primary site of P‐gp mediated drug–drug interactions is the intestine. The selected doses of itraconazole, carbamazepine, paroxetine, fluconazole, fluoxetine, and dabigatran etexilate represent a standard dosing regimen and have been safely used for clinical drug interaction studies in healthy participants [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

PK Sampling and Assessments

In Cohorts A–E, serial plasma PK sampling was conducted predose and up to 216 h post dose to quantify concentrations of aficamten and its metabolites CK‐3834282 and CK‐3834283. In Cohort F, PK samples for determining free and total (free plus conjugated) dabigatran concentrations were collected predose and up to 48 h post‐dose.

The PK parameters assessed included the area under the curve (AUC) from time zero to the last non‐zero concentration (AUC0‐last), AUC extrapolated from time zero to infinity (AUC0‐∞), the maximum observed concentration (C max), the time to reach C max (t max), and the apparent terminal elimination half‐life (t 1/2). All PK parameters were estimated using noncompartmental analysis with Phoenix WinNonlin (Certara, version 8.3).

The fractional contribution of the P450s to aficamten metabolism (fm) was calculated based on observed AUC ratio (AUCR) data using standard algebraic methods and following assumptions: (1) itraconazole and paroxetine cause near complete selective inhibition of CYP3A and CYP2D6, respectively (fm3A4 = 1–1/AUCRITZ; fm2D6 = 1–1/AUCRPRX). (2) Any increase in aficamten AUC by fluoxetine (a strong CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 inhibitor) that is not explained by CYP2D6 involvement (i.e., paroxetine DDI) is due to CYP2C19 inhibition (fm2C19 = 1–1/AUCRFLX—fm2D6). (3) Any remaining DDI liability (i.e., fluconazole DDI), after accounting for fraction excreted unchanged in urine (fe ≈ 0.01), can be assigned to CYP2C9 (fm2C9 = 1–fm2C19–fm2D6–fm3A–fe).

Statistical Methods

All participants who received at least one dose of aficamten were included in the safety population. All participants that complied with protocol requirements and had sufficient evaluable PK data were included in the PK analysis population. Demographics, baseline characteristics, and safety variables were summarized using descriptive statistics.

DDI evaluations were performed in the PK analysis population using natural log transformed PK parameters (for aficamten in Cohorts A–E and dabigatran extexilate in Cohort F) in a linear mixed effects model with treatment as a fixed effect and participant as a random effect. Point estimates were determined as the geometric least square mean (GLSM) of the individual ratios of each PK parameter for the test/reference treatments and expressed as percentage. GLSM and associated 90% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for the following comparisons: Aficamten + probe drugs (test) versus aficamten given alone (cohorts A–E) or standalone dabigatran (cohort F). In each Cohort, a statistical comparison of t 1/2 was performed and p‐value for the difference in t 1/2 between test and reference treatments is presented.

The point estimates (% GLSM) of C max and AUC0‐ꚙ for test versus reference treatments were used as primary determinants. While the conventional 80%–125% bounds recommended by the FDA guidance [8] were applied when interpreting the PK results, these were not used as a strict criterion for clinical interpretation. The clinical relevance of changes in PK parameters was assessed by considering all available data and determining whether these changes necessitate clinical intervention—such as avoiding coadministration, adjusting the dose or dosing schedule, or implementing additional therapeutic monitoring. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4).

Bioanalysis

Plasma concentrations of aficamten, its metabolites (CK‐3834282 and CK‐3834283), free and total (free plus conjugated) dabigatran were determined using high‐performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry methods, validated at Celerion (Lincoln, Nebraska). The analytical ranges for aficamten and its metabolites were 1.00–500 ng/mL. The analytical ranges for free and total dabigatran were 0.500–250 ng/mL and 1.00–500 ng/mL, respectively. Quantitation was performed using weighted linear regression analysis (1/concentration2) of peak area ratios between the analyte(s) and internal standard. The method for aficamten involved a protein precipitation procedure, while the metabolite method used a liquid–liquid extraction procedure. For free dabigatran, acidified plasma samples were extracted using a solid phase extraction procedure. For total dabigatran, acidified plasma samples underwent hydrolysis followed by solid phase extraction.

During sample analysis, accuracy of the quality control samples for aficamten, CK‐3834282, and CK‐3834283 ranged from −6.9% to 0.0%, −2.7% to 1.7%, and −4.0% to 1.1%, respectively. Precision for aficamten, CK‐3834282, and CK‐3834283 was within 5.5%, 5.6%, and 4.6%, respectively. For free and total dabigatran, accuracy ranged from −3.2% to −0.8% and −2.4% to −0.4%, respectively, and precision was within 4.1% and 5.0%, respectively.

Safety

Safety was assessed by monitoring TEAEs, 12‐lead ECGs, vital signs, clinical laboratory analyses, and physical examinations. All AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) version 25.1.

Results

Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

Study 1 (Cohorts A, B, and F) was conducted between October 7, 2022, and March 10, 2023, at a single study center (Daytona Beach, FL, USA). Of 59 participants enrolled, 55 participants completed the study; four participants in Cohort B discontinued due to AEs (discussed in the Safety section). Two of these four discontinued participants had insufficient PK samples and were therefore excluded from statistical analyses. Study 2 (cohorts C, D, and E) was conducted between August 8, 2023, and January 11, 2024, at a single study center (Lincoln, NB, USA). Of 51 participants enrolled, 49 completed the study; two participants (one each in Cohorts D and E) withdrew early from the study due to personal reasons and were excluded from statistical analyses. Baseline demographics for all Cohorts were consistent with those of a typical phase 1 study and are summarized in Table 2. Mean age ranged from 32 to 36 years across all Cohorts, and the mean body mass indices (BMI) ranged from 24.7 to 25.4 kg/m2.

Assessment of CYP3A Contribution

To assess the contribution of CYP3A to aficamten metabolism, Cohorts A and B evaluated the impact of a strong CYP3A inhibitor (itraconazole) and a multi‐CYP inducer (carbamazepine) on aficamten PK, respectively. Carbamazepine is a strong inducer of several P450 enzymes [15], of which strong‐to‐moderate CYP3A and weak CYP2C9 induction are relevant to aficamten metabolism. In Cohort A, itraconazole coadministration led to a 26% increase in geometric mean AUC0‐ꚙ and a 7% decrease in C max of aficamten (Figure 2, Table 3). Aficamten half‐life (t 1/2) increased from 80.6 to 91.7 h (p < 0.0001), with no significant change in metabolite‐to‐parent AUC ratios (MR AUC0‐ꚙ) for CK‐3834282 or CK‐3834283 (Table 3 and Table S1). One participant exhibited an anomalous reduction in aficamten exposure (75.0% and 82.3% decreases in AUC0‐ꚙ and C max, respectively) and was excluded from the primary analysis. However, overall PK results were consistent regardless of exclusion (Table 3) or inclusion (Table S2).

In Cohort B, carbamazepine reduced aficamten geometric mean AUC0‐ꚙ and C max by ~51% and ~31%, respectively (Figure 2, Table 3). Aficamten t 1/2 shortened from 78.9 to 52.3 h (p < 0.0001), while MR AUC0‐ꚙ values for CK‐3834282 and CK‐3834283 increased by 27% and 48%, respectively (Table 3 and Table S1).

The weak interaction between aficamten and itraconazole indicated minor CYP3A contribution to aficamten elimination. However, the weak‐to‐moderate interaction between aficamten and carbamazepine indicated possible involvement of other P450 enzymes; thereby, prompting further DDI evaluation.

Assessment of CYP2D6 Contribution

Cohort C assessed the impact of paroxetine (strong CYP2D6 inhibitor) to definitively evaluate the contribution of CYP2D6 to aficamten elimination. Coadministration of aficamten with paroxetine resulted in 27% and 20% increases in AUC0‐ꚙ and C max, respectively (Figure 2, Table 3). Median t 1/2 of aficamten was ~86 h both in presence (t 1/2 range: 63.6–129 h) and absence of paroxetine (t 1/2 range: 42.0–113 h). The statistically significant difference in t 1/2 between the two treatments could be potentially related to the wide range of t 1/2 estimates of aficamten. MR AUC0‐ꚙ values decreased by 15% (CK‐3834282) and 29% (CK‐3834283) (Table 3 and Table S1). These results suggest that CYP2D6 indeed plays a minor role in aficamten clearance.

Assessment of CYP2C19 Contribution

The contribution of CYP2C19 on top of CYP2D6 to aficamten metabolism was assessed using fluoxetine, a strong CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 inhibitor. Coadministration with fluoxetine (Cohort D) increased geometric mean AUC0‐ꚙ and C max of aficamten by approximately 32% and 55%, respectively (Figure 2, Table 3). Aficamten t 1/2 increased from 74.5 to 95.4 h (p < 0.0001), and MR AUC0‐ꚙ values decreased by 18% and 16% for CK‐3834282 and CK‐3834283, respectively (Table 3 and Table S1). When compared to the effect of paroxetine on aficamten exposure, fluoxetine DDI results suggest that CYP2C19 plays only a minor role in aficamten metabolism.

Assessment of CYP2C9 Contribution

In the absence of an available strong selective CYP2C9 inhibitor, the impact of fluconazole (a moderate CYP2C9 but strong CYP2C19 and moderate CYP3A inhibitor [15]) on aficamten PK was assessed with the goal of understanding the role of CYP2C9 in aficamten elimination on top of the earlier characterized minor roles CYP3A and CYP2C19. Additionally, fluconazole represents a worst case DDI scenario of aficamten as a victim as it inhibits multiple enzymes involved in metabolizing aficamten. Treatment with fluconazole (Cohort E) increased aficamten AUC0‐ꚙ by 278% and prolonged t 1/2 from 76.7 to 226 h (p < 0.0001). A substantial reduction in formation of CK‐3834282 (by 76%) and CK‐3834283 (by 69%) was noted in the presence of fluconazole (Table 3 and Table S1).

Safety

There were no deaths or serious TEAEs reported across all cohorts (A–E) in both Studies 1 and 2. No notable trends were observed in clinical chemistry, hematology, or urinalysis data during the study across all Cohorts.

In Study 1, a total of 5 (29.4%; Cohort A), 9 (52.9%; Cohort B), and 3 (12.0%; Cohort F) participants reported at least one treatment emergent adverse event (TEAE, Table S3). All events were of mild severity except two TEAEs of moderate severity (carbamazepine induced presyncope and syncope) in Cohort B. There were no TEAEs leading to discontinuation of participants from study in Cohorts A and F. In Cohort B, four participants discontinued from the study due to observed TEAEs: one due to COVID and three because of carbamazepine‐mediated transient elevations in alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and gamma‐glutamyl transferase levels (N = 2) or thrombocytopenia (N = 1). All TEAES resolved by the end of the study without treatment intervention except one TEAE in Cohort A (mild headache) and three TEAEs in Cohort B (mild headache, pain, and COVID) that resolved following treatment with paracetamol.

In Study 2, a total of 10 (58.8%; Cohort C), 11 (64.7%; Cohort D), and 6 (35.3%; Cohort E) participants reported at least one TEAE (Table S4). All events were of mild severity except for one TEAE (fatigue) of moderate severity. There were no TEAEs leading to discontinuation of participants from the study in all three cohorts. Two participants (one each in Cohorts D and E) withdrew from the study due to personal reasons not related to the study or study drug(s).

Discussion

The results of previous in vitro phenotyping studies indicated that aficamten was a substrate of multiple CYP enzymes including CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A. Metabolism accounts for more than 99% of aficamten's elimination, underscoring the importance of characterizing the specific P450 enzymes involved in its metabolic clearance [6]. Collectively, these data informed the design of the first clinical DDI study that investigated the impact of CYP3A inhibition (itraconazole) and CYP induction (carbamazepine) on aficamten PK. Coadministration of aficamten with itraconazole (Cohort A), a strong CYP3A inhibitor [15], resulted in a small increase in aficamten AUC (~26%) along with unchanged CK‐3834282 and CK‐3834283 metabolic ratios. These results suggest a minor contribution of CYP3A to aficamten elimination, supporting its concomitant use with CYP3A inhibitors.

Treatment with carbamazepine (Cohort B), a moderate‐to‐strong CYP3A, weak CYP2C9, and weak CYP2C19 inducer [15], markedly reduced aficamten exposure (50% decrease in AUC0‐ꚙ) compared to aficamten alone. An increase in the metabolic AUC ratios for aficamten metabolites CK‐3834282 (1.37‐fold) and CK‐3834283 (1.47‐fold) was observed. A strong CYP3A inhibitor like itraconazole did not fully reverse this effect, suggesting involvement of additional P450 pathways and prompting further DDI evaluation.

The in vitro phenotyping studies suggested involvement of CYP2D6 in aficamten metabolism [7]. In the first in human (FIH) study, no meaningful differences in aficamten PK were observed between CYP2D6 poor versus non‐poor metabolizers, contradicting the in vitro DDI data [4, 7]. Therefore, the impact of paroxetine (Cohort C), a strong CYP2D6 inhibitor [15], on aficamten PK was studied to definitively estimate the contribution of CYP2D6 to aficamten elimination. A minor increase in aficamten AUC0‐ꚙ (27%) and C max (20%) was observed in presence of paroxetine indicating that CYP2D6 plays a minor role in aficamten metabolism. The polymorphism study in the FIH trial had low statistical power to observe an effect due to cross‐cohort analysis with a small CYP2D6 PM group size (N = 7), and inherent limitations of a non‐crossover design, possibly explaining the discrepancy. Since CYP3A and CYP2D6 each accounted for only a minor portion of aficamten metabolism, a further DDI evaluation of other P450 pathways implicated in the in vitro studies was conducted.

To estimate the contribution of CYP2C19 to aficamten elimination, fluoxetine, a strong CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 inhibitor [15], was coadministered with aficamten (Cohort D). Treatment with fluoxetine showed a generally comparable increase in aficamten exposure (32% increase in AUC0‐ꚙ) to the paroxetine cohort (27% increase in AUC0‐ꚙ), suggesting minor CYP2C19 involvement in aficamten metabolism. Based on these data, aficamten may be coadministered with CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 inhibitors without dose adjustment.

Taken together, the results of cohorts A through D indicated that CYP3A, CYP2D6, and CYP2C19 collectively accounted for approximately 50% of aficamten elimination suggesting significant as of yet uncharacterized CYP2C9 contribution to aficamten elimination. However, due to the lack of a strong, selective, and clinically available CYP2C9 inhibitor, the impact of fluconazole, a strong inhibitor of CYP2C19 and a moderate inhibitor of CYP3A and CYP2C9 [15], on aficamten PK was evaluated. Fluconazole was chosen because it was the most appropriate and available “worst‐case scenario” multi‐P450 inhibitor for the specific balance of P450s involved in the elimination of aficamten. Treatment with fluconazole increased aficamten AUC0‐ꚙ by 278% with no change in C max (moderate DDI). A prolongation of aficamten t 1/2 (~3‐fold) was also observed, suggesting inhibition of systemic clearance of aficamten by fluconazole, rather than an increase in aficamten bioavailability. Considering only a small contribution of CYP3A and CYP2C19 to aficamten elimination (based on results of itraconazole, paroxetine, and fluoxetine cohorts), the increase in aficamten exposure in the presence of fluconazole was attributed primarily to CYP2C9 inhibition. Interestingly, an increase in C max of aficamten was observed in the presence of paroxetine and fluoxetine, whereas no meaningful C max change occurred following treatment with itraconazole or fluconazole. Aficamten is a highly permeable compound and not a substrate of efflux transporters, consistent with a high fraction absorbed in humans [7]. Its elimination primarily depends on relatively inefficient P450‐mediated metabolism, resulting in high bioavailability after first pass [6, 7]. Thus, a clear systemic or presystemic mechanism by which DDIs might increase aficamten absorption has not been identified. Notably, a similar C max phenomenon has been reported with another CMI, mavacamten, with the authors concluding that the mechanism explaining the observation is unknown [16].

In vitro studies evaluating aficamten as a perpetrator identified inhibition of P‐gp as the only potential DDI liability. The impact of aficamten on P‐gp was evaluated using dabigatran, a sensitive P‐gp substrate [15]. The minor increases in plasma PK exposures (C max and AUC) of free (24%–25%) and total (26%–27%) dabigatran indicate a weak interaction. As such, aficamten may be coadministered with P‐gp substrates without concerns for DDI liability.

The administration of aficamten and probe drugs was safe and well tolerated in both studies with no unexpected safety concerns or clinically significant adverse events attributed to their use.

In summary, the DDI profile of aficamten was extensively characterized through two clinical DDI studies. The results indicate that aficamten is eliminated by multiple P450s: primarily CYP2C9‐mediated with contributions from CYP2D6, CYP3A, and minimal metabolism via CYP2C19. The fm estimates determined using observed data were further refined using physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling and will be published separately. This metabolic profile means that aficamten is susceptible to only a limited number of uncommon drug interactions. Strong inhibition of any single P450 pathway is expected to result in only weak DDIs (≤ 2‐fold increases in exposure), while the use of multi‐P450 inhibitors or CYP inducers may have only a moderate effect on aficamten levels.

In phase 2 and 3 studies, the steady‐state exposure (AUCτ) of aficamten was 7570 ng·h/mL (range 4109–19,200 ng·h/mL) following a 20 mg QD dose [17]. Based on single‐dose DDI data, coadministration with CYP inhibitors except fluconazole may increase aficamten exposure only by 20%–30%, which remains within the observed interindividual variability of exposures achieved at the same 20 mg dose under unperturbed conditions. Larger increases in aficamten exposure, as seen with fluconazole, represent the less common multi‐P450 inhibition.

Multi‐P450 inhibitors can be those that inhibit a combination of CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A. The inhibition potency and aficamten fm by each CYP determine the overall effect of inhibitor coadministration on aficamten exposure. Although uncommonly used in clinical practice in HCM patients, a handful of drugs (fluconazole, fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, and voriconazole) are known to inhibit multiple CYP pathways relevant to aficamten metabolism and, hence, have greater potential to increase aficamten exposure than that expected from inhibition of a single CYP pathway. This small number of drugs represents the comprehensive list of relevant multiple CYP inhibitors that may be coadministered with aficamten in patients with HCM. The impact of fluconazole and fluoxetine on aficamten PK has been evaluated in clinical studies; a detailed discussion of these findings is provided above. Fluvoxamine is a strong CYP2C19, weak‐to‐moderate CYP3A, weak CYP2C9, and weak CYP2D6 inhibitor, and voriconazole is a strong CYP3A, moderate CYP2C19, and weak CYP2C9 inhibitor. The effect of fluvoxamine and voriconazole on aficamten disposition has not been evaluated in a clinical study, but a moderate interaction with aficamten is expected based on the mix of P450 enzymes inhibited by these drugs. The use of these drugs in the intended patient population is expected to be infrequent, thus limiting the DDI liability of aficamten.

Genetic polymorphisms in CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP2D6 can contribute to inter‐individual variability in aficamten exposure. The combined fractional metabolism for CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 is low (~24%), meaning even complete loss of both pathways would increase exposure by less than 2‐fold, consistent with the modest effects observed with paroxetine and fluoxetine. CYP2C9, the primary metabolic pathway (fm ~54%), is the only polymorphic enzyme of potential clinical relevance. However, strong CYP2C9 inhibitors are not widely used clinically; the poor metabolizer phenotype is rare (< 2%), it results only in partial not complete loss of activity [18], and the most relevant consideration is changes in aficamten exposure following addition or removal of a co‐administered drug.

Importantly, most potential interactions (due to genetics, concomitant medications or a combination of both) can be effectively managed by individualized dose titration guided by echocardiographic monitoring. Taken together, these data highlight aficamten's favorable drug interaction profile, further supporting its potential as a promising therapeutic option for treating HCM.
