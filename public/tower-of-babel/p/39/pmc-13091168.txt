A full-active phosphorus dendron-based nanomedicine alleviates ischemic stroke through multi-target immunomodulation and neuroprotection
Authors: Caiyun Zhang, Huxiao Sun, Yu Zou, Mingjuan Qiu, Yanying Li, You Ji, Mengyao Cui, Serge Mignani, Jean-Pierre Majoral, Mingwu Shen, Xiangyang Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesigning nanomedicines with full-active components to overcome the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and achieve multi-target immunomodulation and neuroprotection for effective ischemic stroke (IS) treatment still remains a great challenge. Herein, we developed bioactive per se hydroxyl-terminated phosphorus dendron (C17G1-OH) micelles to co-deliver fibronectin (FN), an anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative protein drug and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), an anti-inflammatory and neuroprotection drug. The constructed DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles with a mean size of 254.5 nm are cytocompatible, exhibit desired stability, and can be efficiently phagocytosed by brain endothelial cells and microglia. Importantly, the high density of peripheral hydroxyl groups and FN-mediated integrin recognition enable the multifunctional micelles to penetrate BBB and achieve targeted accumulation at the IS region, thus suppressing neuroinflammation by microglia M2 polarization and oxidative stress alleviation, while rescuing neuronal apoptosis by balancing mitochondrial homeostasis. In a rat IS model, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles significantly reduce the infarct area, restore neurological behavior, neutralize inflammation and attenuate neuronal damage by multi-target immunomodulation, neuroprotection and repairment of the damaged BBB through FN-promoted angiogenesis of endothelial cells. The developed full-active phosphorus dendron-based nanomedicine synergistically modulates microglia, neurons, and endothelial cells for synergistic immune modulation and neuroprotection, and may represent an advanced formulation for effective IS alleviation.Keywords: Phosphorus dendron micelles, Ischemic stroke, Microglia polarization, Revascularization, Neuroprotection

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) is a multifactorial neurological disorder caused by the transient or permanent occlusion of cerebral blood vessels, leading to cerebral infarction, neurological deficits, and eventually disability or cognitive impairment. It has emerged as one of the world's most serious public health challenges [[1], [2], [3]]. Current clinical interventions, including intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase and intra-arterial thrombectomy, aim to restore cerebral blood supply in time [4]. However, blood recanalization can lead to ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) [5], an inevitable and serious complication that frequently exacerbates neuronal damage and significantly impedes therapeutic outcomes. The IS pathophysiology is complex and multifactorial, including mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic stress, excitotoxicity, and ferroptosis, while oxidative stress and neuroinflammation are the dominant mechanisms of injury [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Although major therapeutic modalities (thrombolytic, antiplatelet agents, and neuroprotective agents) have been extensively investigated for stroke treatment, their clinical efficacy and translational potential are hampered by several critical limitations including a narrow therapeutic time window, adverse side effects, blood-brain barrier (BBB) limitation, and single therapeutic target [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. In particular, conventional neuroprotective strategies only target a single event such as oxidation, inflammation, apoptosis, or single-pathway regulation, which cannot comprehensively block the synergistic and interconnected injury cascade in IS [15]. Therefore, it is crucial to revisit the pathogenesis of IS and develop multi-target combinatorial therapeutic strategies with desired safety and efficacy.

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS) that exhibit distinct immune phenotypes in response to microenvironmental signals, serve as the first line of defense against ischemic insults [16,17]. Disruption of the redox balance in the brain leads to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and activates microglial polarization toward the M1-phenotype, which exacerbates immune-mediated neuronal damage and neurological dysfunction by secreting harmful cellular mediators such as various proteases, proinflammatory cytokines, and chemokines [18,19]. Conversely, M2-like microglia secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines including interleukin (IL)-4, IL-10, IL-13, and transforming growth factor-β [20,21], as well as neurotrophic factors such as ciliary neurotrophic factor 1, insulin-like growth factor 1, and nerve growth factor 1 [22]. They collaborate with infiltrating macrophages to clear cellular debris and damaged neurons, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory and ischemic repair effects [23]. Therefore, targeting microglial polarization is a promising therapeutic approach for IS treatment. Additionally, mitophagy, the selective autophagy of mitochondria, exerts neuroprotective effects by clearing dysfunctional mitochondria to limit oxidative stress and suppress apoptosis. It is a critical mechanism for maintaining neuronal homeostasis in the brain after stroke [24]. However, post-stroke lysosomal dysfunction and reduced expression of autophagosome-lysosome fusion-mediating proteins disrupt autophagic flux, ultimately triggering autophagic neuronal death [25,26]. Studies indicate that moderate enhancement of mitophagy alleviates ischemic brain injury [27,28]. Meanwhile, the BBB is a highly selective physical and biochemical barrier, and the integrity and normal function of brain microvascular endothelial cells are critical to maintain CNS homeostasis by preventing the infiltration of toxic substances and peripheral immune cells [29]. Unfortunately, increased BBB permeability induced by stroke promotes inflammatory cascade response and neurologic damage by facilitating the recruitment of numerous immune cells (circulating neutrophils or T cells) to the injury site [30]. Thus, developing a strategy to simultaneously modulate microglial phenotype, mitigate oxidative stress, and enhance neuronal mitophagy for neuroprotection and BBB reconstitution represents a promising approach for IS management.

Fibronectin (FN), a key non-collagen glycoprotein in the extracellular matrix, plays a pivotal role in regulating macrophage phenotype transition to an anti-inflammatory state [31,32]. Meanwhile, FN serves as an essential component of the vascular basement membrane, contributing to capillary morphogenesis and vascular homeostasis [33]. It has been demonstrated that FN can significantly promote angiogenesis and alleviate inflammation by stimulating the proliferation and migration of epidermal stem cells [34]. Importantly, the Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) sequence in FN selectively binds to α5β3 integrins overexpressed on endothelial cells within the ischemic penumbra [35], thereby regulating endothelial cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation. This receptor-ligand recognition is beneficial for vascular remodeling and immune homeostasis restoration in stroke [[36], [37], [38]]. Meanwhile, RGD modification endows drug-loaded liposomes with enhanced uptake by brain microvascular endothelial cells and improved BBB penetration ability, enabling effective brain targeting under inflammatory conditions [39].

In addition, different neuroprotective agents with desired biocompatibility such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor, arginine-rich cell penetrating peptides, and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) have emerged as promising therapeutic candidates for the treatment of IS [[40], [41], [42]]. Specifically, DHA enables damaged mitochondria removal by initiating mitophagy reprograming and induce the transcriptional activation of antioxidant enzyme to restore redox balance within neurons [[43], [44], [45]]. thereby significantly reducing cerebral infarction volume and neurological deficits in experimental IS models [46,47]. Moreover, DHA polarizes macrophage towards anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and reduces neuroinflammation by activating extracellular signal-regulated kinase and protein kinase B signaling pathways [46,[48], [49], [50]]. Taken together, the combined application of FN and DHA would be conducive to reducing inflammatory responses, alleviating oxidative stress, and exerting neuroprotective effects at stroke lesions.

However, the translational applications of FN and DHA are hindered by the poor water solubility of DHA, low cell membrane permeability, short half-life, or limited BBB penetration efficiency [48,51,52]. These intrinsic disadvantages underscore the need for advanced delivery strategies, and rationally designed nanocarriers are promising to enhance drug bioavailability, prolong circulation time, and facilitate BBB traversal. Numerous nanocarriers, such as liposomes, exosomes, inorganic nanoparticles (NPs) and cell membrane-coated NPs, have been employed for IS treatment [53]. In general, most of these nanocarriers do not possess intrinsic bioactivity such as anti-inflammatory property and some of the inorganic NPs may have certain toxicity. Recently, phosphorus dendrimers or amphiphilic phosphorus dendrons with precise molecular architecture, inherent bioactivity and satisfactory biocompatibility have been widely employed as excellent carriers for intracellular protein/drug delivery [[54], [55], [56], [57], [58], [59]]. In particular, hydroxyl-terminated phosphorus dendrimers show intrinsic anti-inflammatory bioactivity, and can inhibit nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling and promote the repolarization of macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [[60], [61], [62]]. These beneficial properties are independent of loaded drugs, thus enabling synergistic therapeutic intervention of inflammatory and CNS diseases. Importantly, hydroxyl-terminated phosphorus dendrimers or poly(amidoamine) dendrimers can penetrate the damaged BBB and accumulate at injured brain sites, thereby selectively localizing activated glial cells [[63], [64], [65]] for therapeutic interventions of Parkinson's disease [62] and Alzheimer's disease [61]. This intrinsic BBB-penetrating capability of hydroxylated dendrimers is generally considered through adsorptive-mediated transcytosis, which is driven by the interaction of the hydrophilic hydroxyl groups of the dendrimers with the luminal surface of brain endothelial cells [63,65]. Moreover, the hyperbranched nanostructure of phosphorus dendrimers further promotes transvascular migration across the damaged BBB, contributing to their excellent brain-targeting efficiency [61,62,64]. Collectively, amphiphilic phosphorus dendrons terminated with hydroxyl groups that can self-assemble into stable nanomicelles to significantly improve the bioavailability of hydrophobic drugs [66] may be developed as a multifunctional nanomedicine for BBB crossing and immunomodulation to tackle CNS disorders.

Herein, we developed hydroxyl-terminated phosphorus dendron micelles to co-deliver DHA and FN for triple cellular immunomodulation/neuroprotection to alleviate IS including microglia, neurons, and endothelial cells (Fig. 1). First, generation 1 (G1) amphiphilic phosphorus dendron bearing ten hydroxyl surface groups and a hydrophobic C17H35 alkyl chain was synthesized via a divergent method and protonated to form cationic dendron C17G1-OH (Fig. S1). The amphiphilic dendrons were self-assembled to form micelles for effective encapsulation of DHA. Subsequently, FN was complexed with the DHA-loaded micelles to form the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles via multiple physical interactions. The resulting DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles were thoroughly characterized. The targeted anti-inflammatory therapeutic efficacy, multimodal neuroprotective effect and pro-angiogenic efficacy regulated by microglia, neurons and endothelial cells were systematically investigated using in vitro oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) model and in vivo transient middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (tMCAO/R) rat model. The advantages of the multifunctional micelles are as follows: 1) to achieve efficient BBB penetration via terminal dendron hydroxyl groups and FN-mediated targeted recognition, while supporting FN-driven endothelial regulation and subsequent repair; 2) to promote the M2 polarization of microglia through synergistic actions of phosphorus dendron, DHA and FN; 3) to rescue neuronal apoptosis through upregulation of antioxidant enzyme systems and activation of protective mitophagy; and 4) to achieve multi-pronged managements of IS encompassing immune microenvironment regulation, neuroprotection, angiogenesis, and BBB reconstruction via the coordinated modulation of the above three pivotal cell types in IS.

Materials and method

Preparation of DHA@C17G1-OH micelles

DHA and C17G1-OH were dissolved in methanol and water, respectively, and the DHA solution was dropwise added to the C17G1-OH solution at a DHA/C17G1-OH mass ratio of 3: 1 while stirring overnight at room temperature to evaporate the methanol solvent. The mixture was then centrifuged (1000 rpm, 10 min) to remove the precipitate and collect the supernatant. The supernatant was lyophilized to obtain the DHA@C17G1-OH micelles.

Preparation of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles

Briefly, DHA@C17G1-OH micelles and FN were dispersed/dissolved in water, respectively, and the FN solution was dropped into the DHA@C17G1-OH solution at a DHA@C17G1-OH micelle/FN mass ratio of 2: 1 while stirring slowly at room temperature for 4 h. After that, the above mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the precipitate associated with DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles. The supernatant was also collected to detect the LC and EE of FN in DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles through a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit.

Cell culture and in vitro assays

BV2 cells, SH-SY5Y cells, and bEnd.3 cells were regularly cultured, passaged and adapted for in-vitro assays including cytotoxicity, cellular uptake, BBB crossing, while apoptosis, microglia polarization, ROS elimination, mitochondrial protection, WB, and ELISA were detected based on the in-vitro OGD/R model.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Committee on Experimental Animal Care and Use of Donghua University (approval # DHUEC-MOST-2023-07) and also following the policy of the National Ministry of Health of China. Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were used to construct a tMCAO/R model according to the literature. After intravenously administered with different formulations, the tMCAO/R rats were subjected to in-vivo BBB crossing and biodistribution evaluations, and the therapeutic efficacy was evaluated through T2W MRI and neurobehavioral tests. The rats were sacrificed to harvest brain tissues for TTC staining, histological examinations, BBB integrity assessments, immune cell infiltration, and biosafety assessments.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± SD and at least triplicate independent experiments were carried out unless otherwise noted. Statistical differences were analyzed by two-tailed paired Student's t-test for comparison between two groups and analyzed using one-way analysis of variance with a Tukey post hoc test for comparison of three or more groups using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY). A p-value of 0.05 was selected as the significance level, and the data were indicated with (ns) for no significance, (∗) for p < 0.05, (∗∗) for p < 0.01, and (∗∗∗) for p < 0.001, respectively.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles

In this work, we synthesized an amphiphilic cationic phosphorus dendron with hydrophobic alkyl chain (C17H35) as a core, rigid branched structure, and surface hydroxyl groups according to the literature [66]. The detailed synthesis steps to create C17G1-OH are shown in Figure S1, the structures of the intermediate and final dendron product were confirmed through 1H NMR, 31P NMR, and 13C NMR spectra (Figure S2-S4). The critical micelle concentration (CMC) of the dendron C17G1-OH was measured to be 318 μM using pyrene as a fluorescence probe (Fig. S5A), and transmission electronmicroscopy (TEM) imaging reveals the formation of monodispersed spherical micelles with an average size of 73 nm in aqueous solution via self-assembly (Fig. S5B). Subsequently, the dendron C17G1-OH was mixed with neuroprotective agent DHA at different mass ratios (C17G1-OH/DHA = 1: 1, 1: 2, 1: 3, and 1: 5, respectively) to fabricate drug-loaded micelles (DHA@C17G1-OH). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading content (LC) of DHA in the DHA@C17G1-OH micelles were quantified via ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy based on the characteristic absorption peak of DHA at 208 nm. The formed DHA-loaded micelles have the smallest hydrodynamic size of 218.4 nm and polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.31 at a DHA/C17G1-OH mass ratio of 3: 1, and optimal EE (76.39%) and LC (69.62%) are also achieved at this ratio (Fig. S6 and Table S1). Hence, the DHA/C17G1-OH mass ratio of 3: 1 was selected for subsequent studies.

TEM imaging reveals that the size of DHA@C17G1-OH micelles increases to 205.4 nm with a uniform spherical morphology (Fig. 2A), in consistence with the size (220.4 nm) observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Fig. 2B). The slight discrepancy should be derived from the difference of sample preparation protocols between TEM and SEM since SEM samples are usually prepared by sputter coating of thin gold film before observation. Subsequently, the DHA@C17G1-OH micelles were further complexed with FN to obtain DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles at varying DHA@C17G1-OH/FN mass ratios. As the feeding ratio increases, the hydrodynamic size of the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles gradually decreases, accompanied by a progressive increase in surface potential (Table S2 and Fig. S7). In particular, the DHA@C17G1-OH//FN micelles possess a suitable hydrodynamic diameter (254.5 nm) and excellent monodispersity, as indicated by a low PDI (0.221) at a feed ratio of 2: 1 (Table S2). Meanwhile, the EE and LC of FN complexed with the micelles reach 67.9% and 25.3% respectively, underscoring the robust protein absorption capacity of the phosphorus dendron micelles. Thus, the ratio of 2: 1 was selected as the optimal one for preparing DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles.

TEM and SEM images reveal that the morphology of the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles remains unchanged, albeit with a noticeable increase in size (234.1 nm in TEM and 244.6 nm in SEM, respectively) after FN complexation (Fig. 2C–D). Meanwhile, the FN complexation leads to increased hydrodynamic size of the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles (254.5 nm) when compared to that of DHA@C17G1-OH micelles (218.4 nm) before FN complexation (Fig. 2E), while their surface potential decreases from 54.98 to 11.98 mV (Fig. 2F). This change is attributed to the electrostatic adsorption of negatively charged FN.

Moreover, atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging further demonstrates the retention of spherical morphology of the micelles after FN complexation with a height of 54.91 nm (Fig. 2G–H). UV–vis spectra reveal that the characteristic absorption peak associated to DHA (208 nm) undergoes a blueshift for both DHA@C17G1-OH micelles and DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles, likely due to interactions between DHA and the C17G1-OH that leads to alteration of DHA's electronic transitions via a more hydrophobic microenvironment (Fig. 2I). In addition, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN shows the same electrophoretic bands as those of free FN (Fig. S8). These results collectively support the successful incorporation of both DHA and FN within the C17G1-OH micelles, yielding well-defined DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles. Notably, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles exhibit long-term storage stability, as only slight variations in hydrodynamic size are detected over a one-week period when dispersed in water, phosphate buffered saline (PBS), or Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (Fig. S9).

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was employed to investigate the interaction behavior between C17G1-OH micelles and FN (Fig. 2J). The strong affinity between the micelles and FN is indicated by the high binding constants (Ka = 6.99 × 105 M−1). This binding process is predominantly driven by hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding, as evidenced by the thermodynamic parameters with positive enthalpy change (ΔH > 0) and entropy change (ΔS > 0) [67,68]. Importantly, the complexation of C17G1-OH micelles with FN does not impair the secondary structure of FN (Fig. S10), providing an important safeguard for maintaining the intact biological activity of FN protein.

We next examined the DHA drug release profile in different buffers including those simulating acidic lysosomal (pH 5.5) and ischemic microenvironment (pH 5.5 + 1 mM H2O2) [69]. As displayed in Fig. 2K, under a slightly acidic condition (pH 6.5), the cumulative DHA release within 48 h reaches 26.0%, which is a little higher than at pH 7.4 (22.5%). This should stem from partial cleavage of the pH-sensitive hydrazino bonds in the dendron backbone under acidic environments [70,71]. Remarkably, the introduction of oxidative stress induces a pronounced acceleration of DHA release, and 46.7% of DHA is released within 48 h at pH 5.5 with 1 mM H2O2 compared to pH 5.5 without H2O2. This is presumably attributed to the additional oxidation of the hydrazino bonds in the micellar structure upon exposure to an oxidative environment for enhanced cleavage of the bonds [[72], [73], [74]]. These findings demonstrate that the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles with hydrazino bonds are sensitive to both pH and ROS for fast DHA release, which is beneficial to avoid off-target side effects.

In vitro BBB penetration and intracellular uptake

Cytotoxicity assays demonstrate that the prepared DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles exert negligible effects on the viability of microglia (BV2 cells, a mouse microglia cell line) and neurons (SH-SY5Y cells, a human neuroblastoma cell line) at a DHA concentrations up to 20 μg/mL, indicating their favorable biosafety profile (Fig. S11). Subsequently, we constructed an in vitro BBB transwell model to assess the BBB penetration ability and targeting cellular uptake behavior of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles (Fig. 3A). The Cy5.5-labeled micelles were added into the upper chamber, which was seeded with mouse brain microvascular endothelial (bEnd.3) cell monolayer to mimic the BBB endothelium for evaluation of their transendothelial penetration efficiency and uptake behavior by BV2 cells in the lower chamber. Fluorescence imaging reveals distinct signals in both upper and lower chambers, indicating that the micelles can effectively cross the endothelial cell layer and be delivered to the BV2 cells. Notably, the increased fluorescence intensity in the lower chamber with increasing Cy5.5@C17G1-OH micelle concentration indicates that the micelles themselves possess excellent BBB permeability (Fig. 3B). This is likely attributed to the abundant hydroxyl groups on the surface of phosphorus dendron micelles [64]. Interestingly, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of bEnd.3 and BV2 cells treated with Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN micelles is significantly higher than that of corresponding cells treated with Cy5.5@C17G1-OH (p < 0.05, Fig. 3C–D). Specifically, the MFI of Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN in BV2 cells is 1.31-fold higher than that of the FN-free micelles at the C17G1-OH concentration of 5 μM (Fig. 3D). This confirms that FN functionalization not only promotes the internalization of micelles by both bEnd.3 and BV2 cells but also enables the micelles to achieve enhanced BBB penetration. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images of integrins α5β3 overexpression on the surface of bEnd.3 cells provides a molecular basis for the specific interaction between FN and endothelial cells (Fig. 3E). These findings highlight that FN functionalization enhances the transendothelial transport ability of the C17G1-OH micelles, and enables the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN micelles to cross BBB through a dual mechanism involving both the hydroxyl groups on the periphery of micelles and FN-mediated integrin recognition.

To further analyze the internalization mechanisms of micelles in bEnd.3 cells, the cells were pretreated with different endocytosis inhibitors and free RGD peptide (a competitive inhibitor of integrin α5β3) before incubation of Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN. As shown in Fig. S12, pretreatments with amiloride (a micropinocytosis inhibitor) and chlorpromazine (a clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor) lead to a marked reduction in cellular uptake of Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN micelles, with uptake efficiencies decreased by 35.8% and 36.9%, respectively. Meanwhile, pretreatment with RGD peptide results in a 26.7% reduction in the uptake of Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN micelles in bEnd.3 cells. In contrast, genistein has no significant impact on micelle uptake. These results clearly indicate that the cellular uptake of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles depends on macropinocytosis and clathrin-mediated pathways, and is also regulated by integrin α5β3-mediated targeting. Additionally, incubation at low temperature (4 °C) results in a 65.6% decrease in micelle uptake by bEnd.3 cells, confirming the energy-dependent nature of the uptake process.

For effective BBB penetration, micelles must escape endo-lysosomes in bEnd.3 endothelial cells to avoid degradation and enable efficient transendothelial transportation. CLSM imaging reveals strong colocalization between Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN micelles and lysosomes in bEnd.3 cells at an incubation time of 2 h, with a peak Pearson's correlation coefficient (R) of 0.60, indicating that the micelles are enriched within the lysosomal compartment (Fig. 3F). However, the overlap between red fluorescence associated with micelles and green fluorescence associated with lysosomes significantly diminish as the incubation time extends to 4 and 6 h, with the corresponding R values decreasing to 0.3 and 0.35, respectively. This reduction in colocalization directly suggests that the micelles can achieve efficient lysosomal escape.

To further characterize the targeting uptake of Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN micelles by microglia, we quantified the fluorescence intensity of BV2 cell after incubated with different materials using flow cytometry. The uptake of Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN by BV2 cells is 1.2-fold higher than that of FN-free Cy5.5@C17G1-OH micelles after 4 h of incubation (Fig. 3G–H). This enhanced intracellular uptake effect may originate from the specific recognition of integrin on the surface of BV2 cells by the RGD sequence of FN, as supported by a 13.0% reduction in the uptake of Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN following pretreatment of BV2 cells with free RGD peptide and CLSM observation of integrin α5β3 overexpression in BV2 cells (Fig. S13). Subsequently, CLSM was further employed to visualize the intracellular localization and lysosomal escape of the micelles in BV2 cells. Consistent with the findings in bEnd.3 cells, the micelles can efficiently escape from lysosomes after internalization by BV2 cells for 6 h, with a corresponding R value of 0.45 (Fig. 3I and Fig. S14). To be similar, the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN micelles can also be efficiently taken up by SH-SY5Y cells that express integrin α5β3 (Figure S13 and Figure S15), and are evenly distributed in the cytoplasm through FN-mediated targeted recognition (Fig. 3J). Taken together, these results confirm that FN functionalization confers the micelles with targeting capability towards endothelial cells, microglia, and neurons via interaction with integrin α5β3, which is critical for BBB penetration and precise therapeutic delivery to neurological lesion site of IS.

In vitro microglial repolarization, oxidative stress alleviation, and endothelial cell angiogenesis promotion

M1-phenotype microglia aggravate neuronal damage by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines, whereas M2-phenotype microglia exert neuroprotection via releasing anti-inflammatory mediators [23]. To assess the immunomodulatory potential of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles, oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) model of BV2 cells was employed to simulate microenvironment of IS. Flow cytometry was utilized to quantify phenotypic shifts by detecting the expression of CD86 (M1 biomarker) and CD206 (M2 biomarker). OGD/R stimulation significantly upregulates CD86 expression by 2.6-fold when compared to the control group (Fig. 4A), indicating a robust shift toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. In contrast, free C17G1-OH micelles induce microglia polarization toward the M2 phenotype and exhibit inherent immunomodulatory activity, as manifested by increased CD206 expression (31.54%) and decreased CD86 expression (56.7%). Notably, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN leads to the most potent regulatory effect on microglial phenotype with the lowest proportion of CD86-positive microglia (8.37%) and the highest percentage of CD206-positive microglia among all studied materials. Additionally, the highest CD206/CD86 ratio observed in the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN group further demonstrates the outstanding M2 polarization performance of the dual drug-loaded micelles on microglia (Fig. 4B). This superior performance of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles is attributed to the synergistic effects of the inherent immunomodulatory capacity of C17G1-OH, the independent anti-inflammatory action of DHA and FN, as well as the FN-mediated targeted delivery performance. As the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) results indicated, free DHA, C17G1-OH micelle, and FN all reduce the secretion of various proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) triggered by OGD/R to a certain extent, but none exhibit efficacy comparable to that of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles (Fig. 4C–D and Fig. S16). Meanwhile, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN significantly promotes the generation of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 by improving the bioavailability of DHA and FN and synergistically utilizing the biological activity of different components (p < 0.001, Fig. 4E). Together with the phenotypic regulation observed above, these data further confirm the intrinsic immunomodulatory role of C17G1-OH independent of FN and DHA within the nanosystem.

Considering oxidative stress is a pivotal factor contributing to ischemia-reperfusion injury, intracellular ROS levels of OGD/R-primed BV2 cells after different treatments were detected through 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) staining. CLSM images show intense green fluorescence signal associated with ROS accumulation in OGD/R-induced BV2 cells. In sharp contrast, control group just show negligible green fluorescence signal (Fig. 4F). The treatment with DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles results in significantly diminished green fluorescence intensity, consistent with results of flow cytometry, where a 43.03% decrease in ROS level can be achieved when compared to the OGD/R group (Fig. 4G and Fig. S17). However, the treatments with individual component of DHA, C17G1-OH, and FN only shows moderate antioxidant effects, with ROS reductions of 29.3%, 17.3%, and 21.0%, respectively (Fig. S17). Overall, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles possess excellent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties by enhancing the bioavailability of the delivered cargos and synergistically leveraging the unique biological activities of the full-active components.

FN is known to facilitate vascular regeneration by promoting endothelial cell adhesion and angiogenesis [75], thus we next investigated the pro-angiogenic effects of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN using a tube formation assay. In Fig. S18A–B, the OGD/R treatment impairs vascular network formation in bEnd.3 cells, resulting in a sparsely scattered state of the vascular network. Notably, DHA@C17G1-OH/FN-treated cells exhibit pronounced vascular network restoration, comparable to the control group without OGD/R treatment. A similar trend can also be observed in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (Fig. S18C–D). These findings indicate that the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles not only promote microglia polarization but also enhances microvascular regeneration, a key factor in restoring post-ischemic cerebral perfusion.

Neuroprotective effects via mitochondrial stabilization and mitophagy activation

A neuronal model under IS pathological conditions was established by exposing SH-SY5Y cells to OGD/R. Firstly, Annexin V-FITC/PI staining assays were conducted to assess the neuroprotective effects of the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles against IS-induced neuronal damage. As illustrated in Fig. 5A–B, the treatment of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles leads to markedly reduced apoptosis and necrosis rates of cells from 52.57% in the OGD/R group to 4.83%. Western blot (WB) analysis further confirms the anti-apoptotic effect of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN, as evidenced by the upregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein of B-cell Lymphoma 2 Protein (Bcl-2) and downregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2-Associated X Protein (Bax) and Caspase-3 (Fig. 5C and Fig. S19). Excessive oxidative stress can lead to neuronal dysfunction and death by inducing lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage [76]. Inspired by the prominent antioxidant activity of the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles observed in BV2 cells, we further investigated their effect on ROS levels of SH-SY5Y cells induced by OGD/R. As expected, the treatment with DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles results in significantly reduced intracellular ROS level by 43.2% compared to OGD/R group, with efficacy superior to that of DHA, C17G1-OH micelle, FN, and DHA@C17G1-OH (p < 0.001, Fig. 5D). In addition to DHA's neutralizing effect on ROS, the regulatory effects of the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN on the antioxidant enzymes including glutathione (GSH) peroxidase 4 (GPX4), superoxide dismutase (SOD2), and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) were also found to be greatly upregulated (Fig. 5E and Fig. S20), which are closely associated to the antioxidant state of the neuron cells. In particular, the upregulations of GPX-4 boost endogenous antioxidant glutathione (GSH) levels, facilitating the restoration of redox homeostasis within neurons (Fig. 5F).

Mitophagy, a selective autophagic process that specifically targets damaged mitochondria, plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial quality and cellular homeostasis (Fig. 5G). Impaired mitophagy leads to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, exacerbating oxidative stress and irreversible neuronal damage [77]. Green fluorescent monodansylcadaverine (MDC) staining was employed to visualize autophagosome formation in SH-SY5Y cells. In Fig. 5H, the mitophagy flux of SH-SY5Y cells is suppressed under OGD/R treatment, as evidenced by the weak and sparse green fluorescence signals compared to the control group. The treatment with DHA or C17G1-OH micelles just leads to moderately improved green fluorescence intensity and signal distribution. Notably, the most prominent increase in autophagosome formation can be seen in the group of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles, as manifested by intense and abundant MDC-positive SH-SY5Y cells. This observation suggests efficient mitophagy activation and subsequent clearance of damaged mitochondria. Furthermore, a 1.56-fold upregulation of LC3-II/LC3-I ratio (a mitophagy marker) in DHA@C17G1-OH/FN group compared to the control group provides additional evidence for the successful activation of mitophagy (Fig. S21), which may be attributed to DHA-mediated activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway [78].

OGD/R-induced mitochondrial damage of neurons (depolarization or structural disruption) impairs mitochondria's buffering capacity against Ca2+ fluctuations via inner membrane transporters, leading to unregulated Ca2+ accumulation [79]. Excessive calcium influx into neurons is a critical pathological event in ischemic neuronal death by triggering mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and pro-apoptotic factor secretion, while also exacerbating inflammatory infiltration-mediated cerebral edema through increasing vascular permeability [80]. The treatment of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN leads to reduced fluorescence intensity of Fluo-4 acetoxymethyl ester (Fluo-4 AM), which is positively correlated with Ca2+ concentration in neuronal cells, thus restoring intracellular Ca2+ concentration to normal levels and effectively improving the intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis induced by OGR/D (Fig. 5I).

It is known that the Ca2+-ROS vicious cycle disrupts mitochondrial integrity and triggers apoptotic cascades [81]. The excellent mitophagy activation performance and Ca2+ homeostasis repair ability of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles encourage us to further explore changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) within neuronal cells after different treatments. Flow cytometry assay shows that the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN group has the highest percentage of J-aggregate-positive cells (86.1%) and the lowest level of J-monomer-positive cells (11.4%) among all treatment groups except the negative control (Fig. 5J and Fig. S22). This should be due to the fact that the targeted micellar delivery system synergizes the biological activities of DHA, C17G1-OH micelles, and FN. This change in MMP supports the claim that DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles can most effectively inhibit mitochondrial dysfunction by initiating mitophagy and ROS clearance. Ultrastructural analysis of mitochondria by biological TEM further visually corroborates the restoration of mitochondrial structural integrity and the activation of mitophagy in SH-SY5Y cells after treatment with the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles (Fig. 5K). Specifically, the OGD/R-treated cells exhibit swollen mitochondria with disrupted cristae, compromised outer membranes, and cavitation. In sharp contrast, DHA@C17G1-OH/FN-treated cells display well-preserved mitochondrial morphology accompanied by the autophagosomes that engulf damaged mitochondria, and quantitative morphometric analysis in Fig. S23 verifies that the mitochondrial length (Feret's diameter) is restored to the control level and is significantly higher than that in the OGD/R group (p < 0.001). Therefore, the developed multifunctional micelle system generates a powerful neuroprotective effect through mitochondrial function reconstruction mediated by multiple mechanisms such as ROS scavenging, mitophagy activation, and calcium homeostasis restoration.

In vivo brain targeting and biodistribution

To evaluate the in vivo targeting behavior of the designed micelles, we established a transient middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (tMCAO/R) rat model involving 2 h of ischemia followed by reperfusion. Specifically, different formulations were administered intravenously to tMCAO/R rats immediately upon reperfusion to track their in vivo distribution. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of the brain in Fig. 6A demonstrates that both Cy5.5@C17G1-OH and Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN micelles accumulate in the ischemic hemisphere at 4 h postinjection with enhanced fluorescence signal observed throughout the brain at 8 h. At 4 h, such selective accumulation is attributed to ischemia-induced BBB disruption and increased vascular permeability. At 8 h, the appearance of fluorescence in both hemispheres can be ascribed to the extended intrinsic BBB-penetrating of the phosphorus dendron micelles for enhanced brain distribution. The gradual reduction of fluorescence at 24 h is mainly caused by the normal metabolic clearance of non-targeted micelles, while FN-mediated targeting enables sustained retention in the ischemic region. Importantly, the fluorescence intensity of the ischemic hemisphere (Left) is significantly higher than that of the normal hemisphere (Right) in the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH group at 4 h postinjection (p < 0.05, Fig. 6B), whereas in the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN group, this higher fluorescence intensity in the ischemic hemisphere can be consistently observed at 4, 8, and 24 h postinjection (p < 0.05, Fig. 6C). This result directly validates that the C17G1-OH micelles possess excellent BBB permeability and the FN functionalization of the micelles further enhances their BBB permeability for targeted and prolonged localization in the ischemic regions.

Moreover, the fluorescence intensity of the brain in the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN group is consistently higher than that in the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH group across all tested time points (p < 0.05, Fig. 6D). In particular, at 24 h postinjection, the ischemic hemisphere in the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN group retains detectable fluorescent signals, whereas no fluorescent signals are observed in the brains in the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH group (Fig. 6A). This stark difference underscores the critical role of FN functionalization in enhancing the retention and sustained accumulation of micelles at the ischemic site, which is attributed to specific recognition between RGD sequence of FN and endothelial biomarker integrin [35]. Additionally, the inherent BBB cr ossing ability of phosphorus dendron micelles terminated with hydroxyl groups enables the appearance of red fluorescence associated with Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN in the cerebral cortex of the ischemic hemisphere (Fig. S24). Increased red fluorescence intensity in the cerebral sections of the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN group compared to the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH group further proves the positive role of FN modification in enhancing the BBB penetration. Next, we investigated the cellular colocalization of Cy5.5@C17G1-OH and Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN in the ischemic hemisphere. Brain sections were stained with platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (CD31) to label vascular endothelial cells and ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba-1) to label microglia. Both Cy5.5@C17G1-OH and Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN micelles show clear colocalization with CD31-positive endothelial cells (Fig. 6E) and Iba-1-positive microglia (Fig. 6F), indicating effective cellular internalization. Notably, the intracellular fluorescence in the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN group is more abundant and uniformly distributed than in the FN-free Cy5.5@C17G1-OH group. Meanwhile, the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN group shows stronger and more uniform fluorescence intensity in the hippocampus, a region highly vulnerable to IRI and rich in neurons than the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH group (Fig. 6G). At 24 h postinjection, ex-vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs of rats shows that the fluorescence signal of Cy5.5@C17G1-OH is mainly distributed in the liver and kidneys (Fig. S25). This result indicates that the micelles are primarily metabolized and cleared via the hepatic-renal pathway. In contrast, Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN shows almost invisible fluorescence in most visceral organs but distinct signal in the brain. This difference likely stems from FN-mediated brain targeting, promoting brain accumulation and reducing distribution in peripheral organs, and also contributes to the longer half-life of Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN, as the micelles remain stable in the brain instead of being rapidly cleared via the hepatic-renal pathway like Cy5.5@C17G1-OH.

In vivo pharmacokinetics was evaluated by monitoring blood fluorescence signals at the predetermined time points. Free Cy5.5 shows rapid fluorescence signal decay and fast metabolic clearance with a short half-life of 0.62 h (Fig. S26). In contrast, Cy5.5@C17G1-OH micelles exhibit a prolonged time half-life (2.81 h) due to the reduced renal clearance and phagocytosis by reticuloendothelial system (RES) conferred by structure of the micelles. Especially1, the Cy5.5@C17G1-OH/FN micelles possess the longest blood circulation time (T1/2 = 3.49 h) by reducing nonspecific protein adsorption, providing a longer temporal window for dual drug-loaded micelles to interact with the BBB and accumulate in the ischemic brain, thereby minimizing undesired off-target toxicity.

In vivo therapy

To comprehensively evaluate the potential of the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN in IS therapy, a tMCAO/R rat model was established and treated by saline, DHA, DHA@C17G1-OH, or DHA@C17G1-OH/FN with the experimental protocol outlined in Fig. 7A. At 24 h post-treatment, T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (T2W MRI) was first performed to assess brain pathological changes in tMCAO/R rats following different interventions. As shown in Fig. 7B, no infarct regions were detected in the sham group, while obvious hyperintense signals (indicative of infarcts) can be observed in the saline group. Corresponding quantitative MRI analysis reveals that the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN group exhibits a marked reduction in both infarct volume (Fig. S27A) and cerebral edema (Fig. S27B) when compared to the other treatment groups (p < 0.01). These findings were further corroborated by ex-vivo triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining of brain sections after rat euthanasia. TTC staining reveals a large infarct area (appearing white) in the saline group (Fig. 7C). In contrast, the group of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN shows significantly reduced cerebral infarct volume from 68.5% of saline group to 6.65% (Fig. 7D), demonstrating the superior efficacy over the groups of DHA (46.97%) and DHA@C17G1-OH (18.92%).

Subsequently, Evans blue dye extravasation was employed to assess the integrity of the BBB following different treatments. Under physiological conditions, Evans blue binds to plasma albumin and remains confined to the vasculature. Disruption of the BBB allows the dye-albumin complex to leak into the brain parenchyma, thus Evans blue dye can serve as a sensitive indicator of increased vascular permeability [82]. In the saline group, at 24 h post-treatment, a pronounced accumulation of the blue dye is observed in the ipsilateral hemisphere, indicating severe BBB disruption (Fig. 7E). Brain tissue from tMCAO/R rats administered with DHA@C17G1-OH micelles exhibits an effective reduction in Evans blue leakage, with efficacy significantly superior to that of free DHA (p < 0.05, Fig. 7F). This enhanced efficacy is presumably attributed to micelle-mediated BBB penetration and subsequent increased DHA accumulation at the ischemic region. Notably, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles elicit the most pronounced inhibitory effect on Evans blue leakage among all the studied materials (p < 0.01), emphasizing the pivotal role of FN in targeting injured vasculature, which is beneficial for accelerating the BBB repair. Next, immunofluorescence staining for CD31, a vascular endothelial marker, presents as a dense and continuous cerebral vascular network with well-preserved structural integrity in the sham-operated group (Fig. S28). However, the saline group shows a profound reduction in vascular density, characterized by fragmented, sparsely distributed, and truncated segments, reflecting severe damage to the architectural organization of the cerebral microvasculature. Following various therapeutic interventions, the most substantial improvements in cerebral vascular density and structural integrity can be observed in the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN group (p < 0.01), accompanied by a markedly interconnected vascular network and well-maintained vessel architecture (Figure S28 and Fig. 7G). The strong BBB repair and angiogenic effect on CNS endothelial cells may be attributed to FN-induced activation of MAP kinase signaling via α5β3 integrins [83]. Mechanistically, FN ligation to α5β3 integrins can further activate the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-AKT pathway to inhibit endothelial apoptosis [84] and modulate NF-κB activity to suppress the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules [85], collectively reinforcing endothelial integrity and facilitating functional BBB restoration after ischemia.

The functional recovery in tMCAO/R rats was further examined via behavioral tests at 24 h post-treatment. Neurological function recovery was evaluated using the Zea-Longa neurological deficit scoring, a widely adopted tool for quantifying motor function impairments in rodent focal cerebral ischemia models, and the scoring guidelines are detailed in Table S3. Unlike the saline group, where the scores are predominantly concentrated in 2 or 3, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN group exhibits a distinct shift in score distribution, with the majority of score falling at 0 or 1 (Fig. 7H). This marked reduction in neurological deficit scores directly reflects improved neurological function by enabling high-efficiency drug delivery and synergistic therapy. Motor function was systematically assessed using two well-established behavioral tests including rotarod and forelimb grip. The DHA@C17G1-OH/FN treatment extends rotarod latency by 50.55s compared to the saline group, and restores forelimb strength to 84.25% of normal rats (sham group) (Fig. 7I–J). Therefore, DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles exhibit excellent therapeutic efficacy for IS by reducing cerebral ischemic volume, repairing BBB integrity, reconstructing vascular networks, and restoring neurological function, highlighting BBB penetration capability conferred by phosphorus dendron micelles and FN, as well as synergistic effects of the incorporated full-active components.

In vivo neuroprotection effects by apoptosis suppression and antioxidant defense

Brain injury was further evaluated through hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. After tMCAO/R induction, distinct pathological differences are observed in cells within the cerebral infarcted hemisphere (IH) compared to the cerebral contralateral hemisphere (CH), including extensive nuclear pyknosis, cytoplasmic vacuolation, and widespread cytolysis (Fig. 8A). Administration of DHA or DHA@C17G1-OH shows mild alleviation of these pathological features, with a slightly reduced infarct area but still visible neuronal damage and vacuolar degeneration in the infarct core. Notably, the treatment of DHA@C17G1-OH/FN only results in a small infarct area without obvious nuclear abnormalities, vacuolar degeneration or cytolysis even within the infarction core, contrasting sharply with the extensive damage in the saline group (Fig. 8A). Immunofluorescence staining of NeuN further indicates excellent neuronal protection performance of the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN, as evidenced by the uniform expression of green fluorescence in the ischemic hemispheres (Fig. 8B).

WB analysis was used to explore the molecular mechanism underlying the neuroprotective effects of the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN. Caspase-3, a central effector of apoptosis, is identified as a key marker for neuronal cell death [86]. Compared to the saline group, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN group shows significantly suppressed expressions of pro-apoptotic proteins Caspase-3 and Bax, and upregulated anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 (Fig. 8C–D). Furthermore, the expression levels of antioxidant enzymes in the ischemic brain tissue, including SOD2, HO-1, and GPX4 are simultaneously upregulated in the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN group (Fig. 8E–F), suggesting the effective activation of endogenous antioxidant pathways for enhanced ROS clearance. These molecular changes align with the histological findings in H&E staining (reduced cell necrosis) and NeuN staining (improved neuronal survival), collectively demonstrating that DHA@C17G1-OH/FN exerts neuroprotection through the dual mechanisms of apoptosis inhibition and oxidative stress mitigation.

Neuroinflammation modulation via glial cell phenotype reprogramming

Neuroinflammation driven by excessive activation of microglia and astrocytes is a key pathological hallmark of IS, exacerbating secondary brain damage through pro-inflammatory mediator release and neuronal loss [87]. Considering the outstanding anti-inflammatory and microglia polarization properties of the micelles in vitro, we systematically evaluated the activation status of microglia cells and the level of inflammatory mediators in the ischemic brain. Iba1 and CD68 (an M1 marker) were selected as indicators of M1 phenotype microglia. In the saline group, Iba1+ microglia exhibit a typical activated morphology with shortened protrusions and intense colocalization with CD68, indicative of an activated microglial phenotype (Fig. 9A). In striking contrast, treatment with DHA@C17G1-OH/FN shifts microglial morphology toward a resting state, characterized by smaller somata and elongated protrusions, along with a decrease of CD68 red fluorescence in the microglia. Quantitative analysis reveals that the percentage of Iba1/CD68 positive cells in the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN group significantly reduces from 8.12% of saline group to 0.64% (p < 0.01, Fig. 9B), underscoring a marked suppression of M1-type microglia.

Excessive astrocyte activation induces glial scar formation and the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby amplifying neuroinflammatory cascades, particularly in the hippocampal Cornu Ammonis 1(CA1) region susceptible to ischemic damage [88]. In Fig. 9C, DHA@C17G1-OH can inhibit astrocyte activation featured by overexpression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) in the CA1 region, and suppressed characteristic morphological changes such as cell body enlargement and protrusion thickening. Meanwhile, GFAP fluorescence intensity in the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN group is lower than that in DHA@C17G1-OH group by leveraging FN's unique anti-inflammatory activity and enhanced lesion accumulation capacity, thereby achieving the strongest astrocyte activation blocking ability (Fig. 9D). Therefore, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN can calm the cytokine storm in brain tissue, manifested by the inhibition of various pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) and the production of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 9F–I), which is consistent with the in-vitro results. Collectively, the above investigations verify that DHA@C17G1-OH/FN can effectively reshapes the cerebral inflammatory microenvironment via reprogramming microglial polarization and inhibiting astrocyte activation.

Immune microenvironment modulation

Since the abrupt influx of peripheral immune cells into the ischemic brain could exacerbate brain injury [89], the infiltration of immunocytes into the cerebral IH after different treatments was analyzed by flow cytometry. CD11b+ myeloid cells were stained with an anti-CD45 antibody to identify CD45high peripheral monocytes/macrophages (Mo/MΦ). In addition, B lymphocytes (CD19+CD3−) and T lymphocytes (CD3+CD19−) in the ischemic brain were detected using specific surface markers. The post-ischemic infiltration of peripheral Mo/MΦ (CD11b+ CD45high) is significantly attenuated after treatment with DHA@C17G1-OH and DHA@C17G1-OH/FN, implying the alleviation of neuroinflammation in ischemic regions (Fig. 10A, D). In addition, DHA@C17G1-OH/FN treatment more potently inhibits the CD3+ T cells and CD19+ B cells infiltration in the brains than DHA@C17G1-OH (Fig. 10B, E-F). These results indicate that BBB restoration attributed to the micelles effectively limits the migration of peripheral immune cells across the barrier and reduce secondary inflammatory injury. Macrophages play a critical role in amplifying neuroinflammation and exacerbating neuronal injury following ischemic stroke [90]. Similar to microglia, M1-type macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines that contribute to tissue damage, whereas M2-type macrophages produce anti-inflammatory cytokines that promote tissue repair and neuroprotection [91]. As expected, treatment with DHA@C17G1-OH/FN significantly decreases the proportion of CD11b+CD86+ macrophages (M1) while increasing the proportion of CD11b+CD206+ macrophages (M2) compared to the saline group (Fig. 10C, G), facilitating successful macrophage phenotype transition from M1-type towards M2-type. These findings demonstrate that the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles reduce peripheral immune cell infiltration and reprogram macrophage phenotypes by FN-mediated BBB restoration and their multifaceted neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory actions.

Evaluation of systemic toxicity

To comprehensively evaluate the biosafety of the developed micelles, a series of histological and biochemical assessments were conducted. Histopathological examinations of major organs reveal no abnormal morphological changes or inflammatory infiltration (Fig. S29), indicating the absence of acute organ toxicity. Systemic toxicity was further assessed by measuring key serum biochemical markers. As shown in Fig. S30, no significant deviations are observed in alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels across treatment groups, suggesting no evident hepatic or renal dysfunctions induced by the micelles. Hematological analysis similarly shows no notable alterations in red blood cell (RBC) or white blood cell (WBC) counts, hemoglobin levels, or platelet indices, confirming that the micelle formulation does not disrupt hematopoietic function. A slight increase in WBC counts is observed in the saline group compared to the sham group, potentially reflecting mild inflammation driven by ischemic injury (Fig. S31). Collectively, these results indicate that the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN possesses excellent in-vivo biocompatibility, with no detectable toxic effects on major organs or systemic physiological functions within 24 h of administration.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed bioactive hydroxyl-terminated phosphorus dendron micelles with BBB penetrability to co-deliver the neuroprotective agent DHA and the inflammation-targeting protein FN (DHA@C17G1-OH/FN) for effective IS therapy through triple cellular immunomodulation and neuroprotection. The hydroxyl-terminated amphiphilic phosphorus dendrons can self-assemble into micelles in aqueous solution, enabling efficient DHA encapsulation and FN complexation with excellent stability and a pH/ROS-dependent drug release profile. The created DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles with full-active components can polarize activated microglia toward the M2 phenotype, inhibit inflammatory responses via the synergistically integrated anti-inflammatory effects of C17G1-OH, DHA and FN, and exert powerful neuroprotective effect by activating ischemia-vulnerable neuronal mitophagy, restoring mitochondrial homeostasis, balancing antioxidant system, and blocking the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins. Importantly, FN functionalization endows the micelles with enhanced BBB permeability in vitro and in vivo, prolonged blood circulation time, targeted accumulation in ischemic brain tissue and promoted endothelial angiogenesis for BBB disruption repair. In a tMCAO/R rat model, the DHA@C17G1-OH/FN micelles enable coordinated triple cellular regulations for synergistic therapeutic intervention of IS with good biosafety, including reduced infarct volume, restored BBB integrity, and normalized functional behavior. These observed therapeutic effects are attributed to the synergistic integration of multiple properties of the full-active components of dendron, DHA, and FN in the micelles, such as rescuing neuronal apoptosis, alleviating neuroinflammation, and regulating the brain's immune microenvironment, thus holding a great potential to tackle different brain diseases.
