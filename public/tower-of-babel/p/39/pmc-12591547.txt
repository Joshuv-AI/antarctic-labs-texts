Virtual CNS Tumor Board Rounds Within the Canadian Adolescent and Young Adult Neuro-Oncology Network
Authors: Biren Dave, Mikayla Machado, Robert Siddaway, Natalie Massey, Abitha Suthakaran, Aimee Chan, Cyril Li, Derek S Tsang, Sunit Das, Peter Dirks, Cynthia Hawkins, Uri Tabori, Seth Climans, Mary Jane Lim-Fat, Julie Bennett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSECNS tumors are a significant cause of death in the adolescent and young adult (AYA; age 15-39 years) population; however, these patients often lack standardized treatments.METHODSIn Canada, we have established national multidisciplinary virtual AYA CNS tumor board rounds (national rounds) to improve and standardize care. From November 2021 to June 2024, 185 AYA patients with CNS tumors were presented from centers nationwide, including 138 patients with glioma.RESULTSBefore case presentation, 5.1% of patients with glioma were taking targeted agents or were enrolled in clinical trials. However, after national rounds, 72.6% of patients with pediatric-type glioma and 45.9% of patients with adult-type glioma were recommended clinical trials and/or targeted agents. Among the 44 patients with glioma who had received radiation therapy before national rounds, only 14 were recommended further radiation. Cumulatively, 68.9% of patients analyzed received a treatment recommendation that represented a change in clinical management compared with their previous treatments. Concurrently, we performed molecular review of 174 AYA CNS tumors during the study time frame. Using TruSight, we identified gene fusions involving BRAF and FGFR1 in 17.9% and 7.4% of patients with low-grade glioma, respectively. Moreover, single-nucleotide variants in BRAF and FGFR1 were detected in 7.4% and 12.6% of patients with low-grade glioma, respectively.CONCLUSIONOur results suggest that national rounds with centralized molecular review can direct AYA patients with CNS tumors toward targeted agents and clinical trials, while deferring radiation therapy. Taken together, our work details an ongoing effort to improve and standardize care of AYA patients with CNS tumors in Canada.

INTRODUCTION

CNS tumors are the second leading cause of cancer-related death within the adolescent and young adult (AYA, age 15-39 years) population.1,2 Treatment of AYA patients with CNS tumors remains a challenge because of the lack of standardized approaches and limited participation in clinical trials.3,27 Moreover, radiation is a commonly used treatment modality for AYA CNS tumors and has been associated with various long-term complications including neurocognitive changes, risk of vasculopathy, endocrine dysfunction, and secondary malignancy.4

Gliomas are the most common type of malignant CNS tumor in AYAs.5 The 2021 WHO CNS classification has distinguished pediatric-type glioma from adult-type glioma.6 Recent molecular profiling of a large series of AYA glioma shows the overlap of pediatric-type and adult-type gliomas, with distinct patterns of tumor location and molecular features in AYAs compared with children. Indeed, up to one third of AYA gliomas are classified as pediatric-type tumors.7 Many of these pediatric-type gliomas have been found to harbor pharmacologically targetable alterations in the RAS/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway.7,8 Such patients may greatly benefit from treatment with targeted agents. RAS/MAPK inhibitors have changed treatment paradigms for RAS-/MAPK-altered low-grade and high-grade gliomas; however, trials have largely been limited to the pediatric population.9-14 In adult-type glioma, isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-mutant tumors are enriched for among AYAs. Vorasidenib, an oral IDH1/2 inhibitor, was recently shown to improve progression-free survival in previously untreated low-grade IDH-mutant glioma and is now approved for use in North America and Europe. While this approval is for age groups 12 years and above, there are limited data for use in patients younger than 18 years.7,15

Due to a paucity of evidence in AYAs,16 targeted treatment approaches are being heterogeneously implemented. In Canada, there are barriers to using this approach. First, access to comprehensive molecular testing is variable nationwide, meaning that providers may lack relevant information to inform options for targeted therapy. Second, with a lack of trials and standardized care, physicians across different institutions may use an inconsistent treatment approach, including therapies with long-term toxicities. Third, access to targeted therapy may be challenging given the lack of outcome data in AYAs.

To overcome the aforementioned challenges, multidisciplinary virtual biweekly nation-wide AYA CNS tumor board rounds (national rounds) have been established as part of the Canadian Adolescent and Young Adult Neuro-Oncology Network. These rounds allow clinicians from across Canada to present challenging AYA CNS tumor cases to an interdisciplinary board comprising neuro-oncologists, pathologists, radiation oncologists, neuroradiologists, and neurosurgeons representing both pediatric and adult care. Although most patients fall within the AYA age range, older patients with pediatric-type tumors and younger patients with adult-type tumors may be presented at the discretion of tumor board chair members. Discussion of patient cases culminates in a tumor board recommendation, allowing patients to receive the most appropriate care for their tumor and harmonizing treatment of AYA patients with CNS tumor across Canada. Alongside national rounds, centralized pathology and molecular review is available for AYAs, with access to genetic and epigenetic analyses of submitted pathologic specimens. This initiative enables comprehensive molecular diagnosis of AYA CNS tumors.

Here, we report a summation of patterns of referral and recommendations from our national program since inception, including results from centralized molecular review.

METHODS

Clinical Data From National Rounds

This data collection was approved through Clinical Trial Ontario (project ID#3794) with a waiver of consent. Here, we present data from national rounds from November 2021 through June 2024. Our cohort includes all patients presented during this timeframe. While national rounds focused on patients in the AYA age range, clinicians were permitted to present cases of adult patients with pediatric-type tumors (eg, medulloblastoma) or pediatric patients with adult-type tumors (eg, IDH-mutant glioma). Therefore, while most patients fell within the AYA age range, there were exceptions. For each patient presented, an intake sheet was completed by the treating physician, which included patient demographics, medical history, diagnosis, treatment history (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, systemic agents), and tumor board recommendation. Diagnosis was categorized according to the 2021 WHO criteria. This information was deidentified and entered into a REDCap database and organized into four data entry instruments: tumor board intake, demographics and medical history, survival and long-term follow-up, and progression.

For graphical representation, REDCap data were exported as excel spreadsheets, and parameters of interest were counted and/or computed using built-in formulas. Graphs were generated using R (version 4.2.0) software.

Molecular Sequencing From National Rounds

In a subset of cases, sequencing was performed centrally at the Hospital for Sick Children. This either occurred before presentation at rounds or after recommendation for further molecular characterization at national rounds. The results were shared with referring clinicians within 2 weeks for TruSight and 2-3 weeks for methylation. The methods below detail the sequencing techniques performed centrally.

TruSight

RNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue using the RNAstorm kit (CELLDATA, Fremont, CA) or from frozen tissue using the Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA). Libraries were prepared using the TruSight RNA Pan-Cancer Panel (Illumina, San Diego, CA) and sequenced on NextSeq-550 instruments (Illumina). Fusion genes were identified using MetaFusion.17 For single-nucleotide variants, and insertions and deletions (indels) detection, reads were first aligned to the human genome build hg19 using STAR v2.6.1a.18 Mutations were called using Strelka v2.9.919 and filtered to remove low-quality calls. Variants were annotated using the dbSNP, ClinVar, and COSMIC databases. Mutation data were visualized using R (version 4.2.0) software.

Methylation

DNA was extracted using the DNAstorm kit (CELLDATA). Samples were prepared using the Infinium MethylationEPIC kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer's recommendations (Illumina) and scanned on a NextSeq-550 (Illumina). Arrays were classified using the DKFZ CNS v12.5 classifier.20 Copy number analysis was performed using the Conumee R package.21

RESULTS

Characterization of the Patient Population Presented at National Rounds

Since inception in November 2021 through June 2024, 185 patients were presented at national rounds. Of these, 75 patients were presented more than once. This includes representation from all provinces and territories in Canada with the exceptions of Yukon, Northwest Territories, Nunavut, and Prince Edward Island (Fig 1A). Most patients presented (n = 109) were from Ontario. Patients presented at national rounds were first diagnosed with a CNS tumor at an average age of 25.02 ± 12.16 years and presented at national rounds at an average age of 29.22 ± 9.95 years (Fig 1B). Male and female patients comprised roughly equal proportions of presented patients (Fig 1C). With respect to the treatment stage, most patients were newly diagnosed (n = 96 of 185), whereas smaller subsets were presented after varying numbers of disease progressions (Fig 1D).

The clinical diagnoses of presented patients included a diverse array of CNS tumor types, with the top two most numerous diagnoses being glioma, glioneuronal and neuronal tumors (hereafter referred to as glioma) (n = 138, 74.6%), and embryonal tumor (n = 14, 77.6%). Given the relative abundance of glioma diagnoses within our cohort, we further characterized the proportions of glioma subtypes (Fig 1F). Of the total 138 patients with glioma, 37 (26.8%) had an adult-type diffuse glioma, 59 (42.8%) had a pediatric-type diffuse low-grade glioma (including pediatric-type diffuse low-grade glioma along with circumscribed astrocytic gliomas and glioneuronal/neuronal tumors as these are enriched in pediatric age groups and often harbor RAS/MAPK alterations), 14 (10.1%) had a pediatric-type diffuse high-grade glioma, and nine (6.5%) had an ependymal tumor. The remaining 19 patients had a diagnosis of glioma that could not be further classified.

AYA CNS Tumor Board Often Recommended Radiation-Sparing, Targeted Treatment

A key aspect of our national rounds is the opportunity for multidisciplinary discussion with providers from both pediatric and adult institutions. Thus, we were interested in analyzing the modalities of care patients received before their presentation at national rounds compared with recommendations from the multidisciplinary board. Since patients with glioma comprised the bulk of our cohort, we focused our analysis on them. We observed that before their presentation at national rounds, almost all patients with glioma were treated with some combination of surgery (n = 114 of 138, 82.6%), radiation (n = 44 of 138, 31.9%), or systemic chemotherapy (n = 37 of 138, 26.8%). Very few patients had previously been treated with targeted agents or enrolled in clinical trials (n = 7 of 138, 5.1%; Fig 2A). After national rounds, 72.6% (n = 53 of 73) of patients with pediatric-type glioma and 45.9% (n = 17 of 37) of patients with adult-type glioma were directed toward appropriate clinical trials and/or targeted agents (Fig 2A). One of the suggested clinical trials/targeted agents was ONC201 for patients with pediatric-type diffuse midline glioma (n = 3), including one patient who was recommended ACTION (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05580562; ONC201 in H3K27M-mutant diffuse glioma after radiotherapy). Two patients with adult-type diffuse glioma were recommended TRIDENT (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04471844; alternating electrical field therapy with temozolomide and radiation for patients with glioblastoma multiforme [GBM]). For pediatric-type diffuse low-grade glioma patients with amenable molecular alterations, BRAF and MEK inhibitors were commonly suggested although this is largely accessible through managed access programs rather than clinical trials. We measured the proportion of patients who had a recommendation from national rounds representing a change in treatment modality from previous therapies. We observed that 64.7% (n = 24 of 37) of patients with adult-type diffuse glioma, 92.9% (n = 13 of 14) of patients with pediatric-type diffuse high-grade glioma, 67.8% (n = 40 of 59) of patients with pediatric-type diffuse low-grade glioma, and 55.5% (n = 5 of 9) of patients with ependymal tumor were given such recommendations (Fig 2B). Finally, we have performed a limited follow-up on 14 patients presented at national rounds who were suggested a targeted agent or clinical trial and found that 13 of these patients did go on to receive the recommended course of treatment.

Next, we quantified the fraction of patients in each glioma subtype who had received initial radiation treatment and were subsequently suggested a radiation-sparing treatment approach by the tumor board (Fig 2C). Of the patients with glioma who had received radiation before national rounds, we observed that 2 of 11 patients with pediatric-type diffuse high-grade glioma, 4 of 11 patients with pediatric-type diffuse low-grade glioma, 4 of 16 patients with adult-type diffuse glioma, and 4 of 6 patients with ependymal tumor were recommended further radiation therapy. These data indicate that most patients who received radiation before national rounds were not recommended further radiation.

Concurrent Molecular Review Identifies Clinically Actionable Alterations in AYA Patients With CNS Tumor

In conjunction with national rounds, centralized molecular review was available for clinicians from across Canada to submit pathologic specimens for genetic and epigenetic analyses and review. In the same time span (ie, November 2021-June 2024), 173 AYA CNS tumors were molecularly profiled at SickKids (Fig 3). Most of these patients had either a pediatric-type low-grade glioma (n = 77) or H3-wild-type (WT) and IDH-WT high-grade glioma (n = 37), with other tumor types comprising the remainder (Fig 3A). Clinical and pathologic diagnosis of the tumor type was corroborated and confirmed using the DNA methylation array using the DKFZ CNS v12.5 classifier. Of note, only 14 of 26 pediatric-type low-grade gliomas were classified on methylation array testing, demonstrating the limitations of this test for this disease entity. To confirm that these patients belonged to the AYA demographic, we quantified their ages (Fig 3B).

TruSight targeted sequencing identified pathogenic alterations in all tumor types analyzed (Figs 3C and 3D). Notably, fusions involving BRAF and FGFR1 were identified in 17.9% and 7.4% of patients with low-grade glioma, respectively. The presence of such molecular events is clinically relevant as they enable treatment with targeted inhibitors.22,23 Next, we analyzed gene mutations identified by TruSight sequencing (Fig 3D). Among the high-grade gliomas, mutations in TP53 (58.1%), ATRX (35.5%), IDH1 (33.9%), and H3F3A (12.9%) were most common. In low-grade gliomas, the most common mutations identified were FGFR1 (12.6%), IDH1 (8.4%), BRAF (7.4%), and NF1 (7.4%).

Molecular profiling of AYA CNS tumors is important for confirmation of diagnosis, clinical prognostication, and selection of appropriate targeted agents. In concordance with treatment recommendations made by our tumor board, our molecular profiling efforts demonstrate that many AYA patients with CNS tumors do harbor targetable genetic alterations that would benefit from targeted treatment.

DISCUSSION

In the era of precision oncology, there have been significant challenges in establishing standards for molecular testing, therapy-matching, and clinical trial enrollment for AYA neuro-oncology patients. Here, we present the results of a national effort to improve awareness and management of AYA CNS oncology cases through a platform regrouping pediatric and adult neuro-oncology experts, with access to central molecular sequencing and clinical expertise from across Canada. We present data from approximately 3 years of biweekly AYA national rounds held between November 2021 and June 2024. Of the 185 patients presented over this period, the majority were diagnosed with pediatric-type glioma. We found that across all patients with glioma, most received clinical treatment recommendations from the tumor board that represented a change in management from their previous treatment. Importantly, these treatment recommendations tended to steer patients toward appropriate clinical trials and targeted agents, while minimizing exposure to radiation therapy. Concurrent genetic and epigenetic analysis of AYA CNS tumor specimens identified clinically targetable alterations in our patients.

The results from our rounds demonstrate the complexity and challenges of managing AYA patients with CNS tumors, particularly those with pediatric-type tumors. As these patients have excellent long-term survival,24 it is imperative to minimize morbidity and late effects, while maximizing quality of life. At present, there are limited data for use of targeted therapy in pediatric-type glioma in AYAs.16 This highlights an unmet need where more data are required for this population to provide evidence-based care that keeps pace with advancements in the field. It is noted that targeted therapy might have a profound beneficial effect. In particular, we would like to highlight the case of a 28-year-old female patient, diagnosed with KRAS-mutant pilocytic astrocytoma, who achieved symptomatic relief and objective radiographic response on treatment with trametinib, at the recommendation of national rounds.25 While radiation remains an important retreatment modality for pediatric patients with high-grade glioma, IDH-mutant glioma, and recurrent GBM, its usage should be carefully considered in patients whose tumors bear targetable molecular alterations to minimize long-term risks of neurocognitive changes, endocrine disruption, cerebrovascular events, and secondary radiation-induced malignancy.26 Targeted therapy offers the option to avoid or defer radiation and associated late effects in these cases. Our work also demonstrates the importance of focusing on disease biology rather than age in patients with CNS tumors. For example, we note the inclusion of a subset of patients older than 40 years who had pediatric-type tumors. The lack of clinical trials available to this cohort is also notable, especially given the amount of data available and clinical trial opportunities for pediatric patients with similar tumors.

To address the lack of evidence described above, future work includes collecting prospective data on patient outcomes, particularly those on targeted therapy to better understand outcomes in the AYA population. Given our national effort, we also hope to offer patients across Canada access to novel, cutting-edge diagnostic testing and access to clinical trials for eligible patients. Collecting data on the value of national rounds from a patient perspective is also an important avenue to explore. We will continue to seek periodic feedback from participants to ensure that meetings continue to provide value and identify areas of potential improvement. In addition, our future plans include engaging patients to collect patient-reported outcomes to better understand the lived experiences of AYAs with CNS tumors.

Based on the results of this study and our experiences with national rounds, we make the following recommendations for the treatment of AYA patients with CNS tumor. First, comprehensive molecular analysis for all patients. This is especially important for patients with IDH-WT glioma.7 Second, multidisciplinary review of all patients. For adult patients with pediatric-type tumors or pediatric patients with adult-type tumors, this should include both pediatric and adult providers given their distinct sets of expertise. Third, clinical trial access needs to be expanded to focus more on tumor biology rather than age to capture outcomes within different age groups. While expanding eligibility criteria is one necessary step, systemic issues remain in place that are challenging to overcome to ensure access to clinical trials, even for patients who are eligible. Fourth, there needs to be recognition of AYA patients with CNS tumors as a unique group whose clinical features intersect pediatric-type and adult-type tumors. Specific knowledge in the management of these types of tumors will help improve outcomes.

Some limitations of our work include limited representation of patients from more remote regions of Canada. A possible explanation for this is that patients from such regions may get referred to tertiary care centers in larger cities by their primary care providers. Nonetheless, efforts to engage patients and clinicians in these regions are underway. In our present work, we also lack long-term follow-up regarding the clinical outcomes of the patients presented at rounds and to whom recommendations were made. Ongoing work includes consenting these patients for prospective data collection so that their outcomes may be monitored. Accumulation of such clinical data will be valuable for forming evidence-based guidelines for AYA CNS tumor care.

In conclusion, our work establishes a forum for clinicians to seek treatment advice on their challenging AYA CNS tumor cases. Preliminary data suggest that our rounds are successful in guiding patients toward radiation-sparing targeted therapies and offering the opportunity for multidisciplinary discussion. While access and eligibility for clinical trials remain challenging for AYA patients with CNS tumor, we hope to advocate for more comprehensive inclusion criteria based on biological features. Continuous broadening of our patient cohort and long-term follow-up with previously presented patients will provide important clinical evidence for the establishment of new guidelines for optimal management of AYA patients with CNS tumors and improve patient outcomes.
