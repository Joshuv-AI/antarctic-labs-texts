Impact of physical activity on visual outcomes among persons with type 2 diabetes: A scoping review of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Thomas Hormenu, Ebenezer Oduro Antiri, Stephen Ocansey, Edward Wilson Ansah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe global burden of Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is increasing, with diabetic eye complications remaining a leading cause of irreversible blindness. Current evidence suggests that physical activity may help mitigate diabetes-related microvascular complications. This scoping review synthesized evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing the impact of physical activity interventions on visual outcomes in individuals living with T2DM.MethodsUsing the JBI framework, a search of MEDLINE, CENTRAL, SCOPUS, JSTOR, and clinical trial registries was conducted yielding 3634 records, of which 8 RCTs satisfied the inclusion criteria. The included studies were conducted across 8 countries with varied designs, intervention protocols, outcome measures, and study durations.ResultsThe findings revealed mixed outcomes regarding how physical activity affects visual outcomes. Of the 8 included RCTs, 4 reported beneficial effects (including significant decreases in central macular thickness, reduced risk of retinal microaneurysm formation, and lower diabetic retinopathy incidence or progression), 3 reported no significant effect, and 1 reported an unanticipated increase in retinopathy incidence in intervention groups. This review highlights critical gaps, including the lack of standardised visual outcome measurements, insufficient exploration of contrast sensitivity, colour vision, and visual fields, and limited or no follow-ups. Additionally, adherence to physical activity interventions may have been influenced by social determinants of health, such as socioeconomic status, cultural factors, and healthcare accessibility.ConclusionThis review highlights the need for further high-quality RCTs with standardised physical activity protocols and comprehensive visual assessments. The findings offer a basis for incorporating structured physical activity into diabetes care, especially in resource-limited settings.RegistrationThe scoping review is registered with Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/856YB).

Introduction

Over 537 million individuals suffer from type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) globally [1]. It is a public health emergency that is projected to increase to 643 million by 2030, and 783 million by 2045 [1]. Chronic hyperglycemia in T2DM causes negative ocular changes, including diabetic retinopathy, a leading cause of blindness among working-age individuals [2]. This is further compounded by the fact that diabetes-related eye conditions such as diabetic retinopathy, diabetic macular edema, and the resulting visual impairments, are a major cause of decreased quality of life and increased healthcare expenses [3], [4]. Despite significant improvements in glycemic control regimens, the incidence of vision-threatening diabetic eye diseases remains high [5], highlighting the necessity of additional measures to mitigate microvascular damage.

A growing body of research suggests that lifestyle changes, especially physical activity may be crucial for managing diabetes and its complications [6]. Physical activity can be considered to be any bodily movement that is produced by skeletal muscles resulting in significant energy expenditure [7]. Physical activity is essential for managing T2DM, since it improves glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, and cardiovascular health [8], [9]. Emerging evidence suggests that physical activity may also have protective effects on microvascular integrity, including retinal health, by reducing oxidative stress and improving endothelial function [10], [11]. However, there is still a lack of comprehensive research on the direct effects of physical activity on visual outcomes or function, including contrast sensitivity, visual acuity, colour vision, visual fields, and diabetic retinopathy progression. The current literature reveals inconsistent findings, with some studies indicating the preventive effects of physical activity on the eye [12], [13], while others show no discernible benefits and even unanticipated negative effects [14], [15].

The majority of the current reviews on T2DM management predominantly focus on metabolic outcomes [5], [16], [17], leaving a critical gap in understanding how lifestyle interventions influence ocular health [16]. The few reviews that have explored physical activity and visual outcomes in T2DM have focused exclusively on diabetic retinopathy, omitting other visual outcomes [5], [18], [19]. These reviews have also relied predominantly on case control or cross-sectional studies, limiting causal inference [5], [18], [19]. Furthermore, the role of social determinants of health like socioeconomic status, healthcare access, and cultural barriers in influencing the efficacy of physical activity interventions on visual outcomes has not been systematically mapped. Given the substantial heterogeneity in intervention protocols, outcome measures, and statistical methods across existing RCTs, a scoping review is the appropriate methodology to map this fragmented evidence base and identify research gaps [20].

This scoping review addresses these gaps by synthesizing data from RCTs assessing how physical activity interventions impact visual outcomes in individuals with T2DM. The findings have important implications for identifying evidence gaps and incorporating physical activity into multidisciplinary diabetes care, especially in areas with limited resources where access to advanced treatment is limited. This review fills a significant knowledge gap, calls for further studies, and provides a basis for evidence-based recommendations to lessen the global burden of diabetes-related visual impairment.

Methods

This scoping review followed the updated JBI framework for conducting scoping reviews [20]. This framework recommends developing research questions, finding relevant studies, selecting and including the studies, extracting and organizing data from the selected studies, and synthesizing and analyzing the results [20]. This review is registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) registry (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/856YB), and the full protocol has been published separately [21]. Moreover, the review reporting framework of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis - Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) was used to report the findings from this scoping review [22].

Research questions

The primary research question that guided this scoping review was: ‘What is the scope of evidence of the interaction between physical activity interventions and visual outcomes (visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, visual function, colour vision, diabetic retinopathy status) among persons with T2DM as reported in randomized controlled trials?’. Therefore, the specific objectives that guided the scoping review process were, to: 1. aggregate and synthesize a wide spectrum of RCTs covering different regions with different healthcare settings and sociodemographic characteristics; 2. report differences in the types of visual outcomes reported and variations in RCTs used in included studies, in relation to sample sizes, intervention protocols, measurement tools, visual outcomes; 3. explore how social determinants of health, for instance, access to healthcare, socioeconomic status, and culture, shape the outcomes of physical activity interventions; 4. ascertain the interaction between visual and other general outcomes measured in the RCTs.

Identifying relevant studies

The search for this scoping review was performed in four major databases: MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, SCOPUS, and JSTOR. MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, SCOPUS provided comprehensive coverage of the health and clinical literature, while JSTOR extended the search to interdisciplinary scholarship on lifestyle intervention and social determinants of health. Further searches were conducted in clinical trial registries: ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP). Supplementary searches in Google and Google Scholar were undertaken to identify any eligible records not indexed in the primary databases, including grey literature and recently published trials. The search in the MEDLINE database was done using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms as well as free text. These search terms were subsequently modified for the search in other databases (Appendix C). Study selection

The initial search was conducted between January 16, 2025 and February 2, 2025, and was rerun on May 13, 2025 prior to final analysis to identify any newly published eligible studies. Records identified in the rerun were screened using the same procedure described in Section 2.3, and any additional eligible studies were incorporated into the synthesis. The records were loaded into Mendeley software where the duplicates were merged or removed. The screening procedure was done in two stages, with title and abstract screening followed by a full-text screening. To ensure accuracy and reduce bias, a structured approach involving three different pairs of graduate students was used for screening the titles and abstracts of the retrieved records. Each group independently conducted an initial screening of title and abstracts. A cross-over screening approach was used, where each group double-checked the screening done by another group. Inter-rater agreement was evaluated at the title and abstract screening stage, with disagreements settled through discussion and, if they remained unresolved, by senior authors. The reference lists of selected relevant full-text records were checked for other relevant records. Moreover, the selected full-text records were screened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria by authors E.O.A. and E.W.A. Any disagreements found during this procedure were discussed and agreed via consensus; and unresolved cases were sent to the third and fourth authors (T.H. and S.O.) for resolution. Only studies that satisfied the inclusion criteria after full-text screening were chosen for data extraction. Assessing data quality

Given the variety of RCT designs across the studies included in this scoping review, the JBI critical evaluation checklist for RCTs [23] was used to assess the selected studies. For each of the selected records, two authors evaluated and came to a consensus on the validity of such studies. No study was excluded based on this critical appraisal, as this was done solely to establish the methodological quality of the records selected. The detailed description of the quality assessment conducted is found in Appendix B.

Data extraction and charting

The data extraction form for this review was designed, and piloted utilising four randomly selected studies. The piloting was conducted to refine the extraction form and ensure the consistency, validity and reliability of the extracted data, and that the data met the objectives of the review. Data extraction is an iterative process, thus, the authors updated the form when new information were found. The data from the selected full-text RCTs peer review articles were extracted independently by E.O.A. and E.W.A. and reviewed by T.H. and S.O. Details of the data extracted included authors and year, country, enrolment criteria, design of RCT, population, sample size, type of physical activity intervention, level of intensity, frequency of physical activity, study duration, supervision, remission definition, comparator intervention, and the visual outcomes assessed (see Appendix A, Table 1). Additionally, any disagreements regarding record selection and data extraction were resolved during regular meetings among the authors.

Data analysis and synthesis

The synthesis and analysis were guided by the primary research questions and objectives of this scoping review. A narrative synthesis was condcuted following an established framework [24] to identify, compare, and report the characteristics, findings, and variations across the included studies. The focus was on the origin of the selected studies (country and location), demographic characteristics of the participants included in the various studies, recommended physical activity guidelines for individuals with T2DM, and the type of physical activity interventions implemented. The synthesis also explored general and vision-specific health outcomes, as well as moderating and mediating factors. This allowed for evidence mapping across diverse contexts and to identify existing research gaps for further studies [25], [26], [27]. A thematic analysis was applied to identify recurrent themes and patterns in the literature, following an established approach [28] previously used in similar reviews [29], [30]. Two authors (E.O.A. and E.W.A) independently familiarised themselves with the extracted data and generated initial codes inductively from the charted study characteristics and findings. Codes were then collated into candidate themes through discussion until consensus was reached, with any remaining disagreements resolved by the third and fourth authors (T.H. and S.O.). Themes were derived based on variances in methods, including RCT designs, sample sizes, frequency and duration of interventions, levels of supervision, measuring instruments, and types of visual outcomes evaluated. Specific attention was given to visual outcomes such as visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, visual function, colour vision, and diabetic retinopathy status. Heterogeneity across included trials in study design, intervention protocols, outcome measures, and follow-up duration precluded statistical pooling. Thus, findings were synthesized narratively, with studies grouped by comparable features to make sources of variation explicit. Based on this, a comprehensive discussion of the findings in relation to the current literature was done.

Consultation

In conducting this review, a chartered librarian form the University of Cape Coast Sam Jonah Library was consulted through the searching and screening process. In the conduct of the review, cardiometabolic disease, vision, and trial experts were also consulted to review the review objectives and the information extracted. These consultations were done to ensure the search strategy and subsequent synthesis and analysis of the selected records were comprehensive, relevant, and methodologically sound. The experts provided guidance on refining eligibility criteria, identifying suitable outcome measures, and interpreting complex findings. Their recommendations improved the rigour, clarity, and contextual relevance of the final synthesis, but did not directly influence the results, findings and the conclusion drawn from this scoping review.

Search results

The search performed in the four major literature databases: MEDLINE (872), Cochrane Library (754), SCOPUS (1046) and JSTOR (962) produced 3634 records. Further search conducted in major clinical trial registries as well as Google and Google Scholar yielded an additional 80 records. Subsequently, 171 duplicates were removed using the Mendeley software, leaving 3543 for title and abstract screening. After preliminary title and abstract screening, 3509 records were excluded because such records failed to satisfy the inclusion criteria for the scoping review. In addition, 6 other records were obtained through consultation and reference list checking of the full-text records. Thus, a total of 40 full-text records met the eligibility criteria for full-text screening. Characteristics of included studies

The most common type of RCT observed in the 8 included studies (see Appendix A, Table 1) was the two-arm parallel-group RCTs (n = 5, 62.5%). The RCTs were conducted across 8 countries [27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34] with one study involving multiple locations [33]. The majority of the studies were conducted in Asia (50%) [31], [32], [34], [38] followed by Europe (37.5%) [35], [36], [37], and Northern America (25%) [33]. The included studies varied in duration, ranging from a minimum of 3 months [31], [32] to a maximum of 26 years [37] (including follow-up periods). Intervention durations alone ranged from 3 months [31], [32] to 8 years [38], with other interventions lasting 6 years [33] and 4 years [35], [36], [37]. Concerning the duration of follow-ups, the longest duration of follow up was 22 years [37], with no follow-ups for majority of the studies (57.1%) [31], [32], [33], [36], [38]. The largest study had 2033 participants [38], with the smallest study having 40 participants [32]. The majority of the studies were published in recent years (2023–2024) [31], [32], [33], [37], demonstrating a growing interest in the area. The participants across the included RCTs had a mean age ranging from 45.2 years [34] to 62.6 years [33], with the majority of the studies enrolling middle-aged to older adults between 35 and 80 years [32], [35], [36], [38]. Sex distribution varied across studies, with females predominating in 2 studies [32], [37], males constituting the majority in 4 studies [33], [34], [36], [38], and only one study maintained a near-equal representation [31]. One study did not report sex distribution [35]. Findings

Enrolment criteria

The studies included in this review applied different enrolment criteria based on age, diabetes diagnosis, and glucose tolerance status. Three studies [34], [35], [37] concentrated on recruiting individuals with impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) and T2DM, with their ages ranging between 25 and 64. Three other studies [31], [33], [36] focused exclusively on people with overt T2DM, with participants in these studies having a verified diagnosis of diabetes, with one concentrating on persons aged over 80 years [36]. Only two studies [32], [38] examined whether physical activity helped slow down retinal deterioration in individuals diagnosed with diabetic retinopathy.

Physical activity supervision, intensity and duration

Supervision during physical activity intervention

Most of the included RCTs used researcher-supervised physical activity interventions, but the degree of supervision varied across studies. Specifically, direct supervision by researchers was used in five studies [32], [34], [35], [36], [37], citing adherence as justification for this approach. In contrast, one RCT [33] placed all the responsibility for adherence on the participants, relying only on participant self-supervision. The remaining two RCTs employed a hybrid approach [31], [38], which combined participant self-monitoring through logbooks with remote telephone monitoring by the researchers.

Physical activity type and intensity

Majority of the included studies did not explicitly state the types of physical activity employed during the interventions. The descriptions included a general increase in habitual physical activity while sustaining a moderate level of intensity [37], leisure-time physical activity [34], recommended levels of physical activity [36], [38], and physical activity-based lifestyle interventions [33]. The remaining three studies were more specific, citing aerobic and circuit training, walking and hiking sessions [35], walking and running [31], and aerobic physical activity [32].

Although the precise criteria of intensity differ from study to study, most of the included studies described their physical activity regimen as moderate-intensity physical activity [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [37]. Only one study did not specify the intensity of the physical activity intervention implemented [36].

Physical activity frequency

Most of the included RCTs recommended three to five sessions of physical activity per week [26], [27], [28], [29], [31], [33], while the duration of the physical activity interventions varied. These included a minimum of four hours of physical activity per week [37], at least 30 min of aerobic or circuit exercise per day [35], walking or running for 30 min five days a week with 15-min increments every 4 weeks [31], at least 150 min per week [33], and three supervised 45-min aerobic sessions per week [32]. Other studies characterized the frequency of the physical activity as regular [34], meeting general recommendations [36], and pedometer-monitored regular physical activity [38], with the exact number of sessions or durations not specified.

Impact of physical activity on visual outcomes

RCTs on the impact of physical activity on visual health outcomes in individuals with T2DM have produced varied results. While some studies indicated that physical activity may have a protective effect [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], others observed no significant beneficial effect or even unexpected increases in diabetic retinopathy incidence [31], [37].

Visual functions

Research on the effects of physical activity on specific visual functions in people with T2DM is very limited. In fact, only one RCT explored the impact of physical activity on visual functions, where the study observed no significant improvement in visual acuity (logMAR) in the intervention group (0.04 ± 0.08 to 0.04 ± 0.08, p = 0.62) [31]. However, this study did not explore other visual functions like colour vision, contrast sensitivity and visual field. None of the other included studies explored any visual functions, with emphasis on diabetic retinopathy progression and retinal changes [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37].

Diabetic retinopathy progression and retinal changes

The review documented conflicting evidence about the effects of physical activity interventions on the development of diabetic retinopathy, with different studies producing varying results. After 22 years of follow-up, the Finnish Diabetes Prevention Study observed no discernible difference in the treatment group's cumulative incidence of diabetic retinopathy (8.9%) compared to the control group (7.7%) [37]. Similarly, the RCT conducted in Japan documented no significant differences between the intervention and control groups regarding the development and progression of diabetic retinopathy [38]. In contrast, the intervention group of the China Da Qing Diabetes Prevention Outcome Study reported a 47% lower incidence of severe diabetic retinopathy compared with the control group (HR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.29–0.99, p = 0.048) [34]. Similarly, diabetic retinopathy advancement rates were reported as lower in the intervention group than in the control group (p = 0.009) of the Italian Group-Based Lifestyle Intervention Study [36].

Regarding general retinal changes, a single short-duration trial reported a statistically significant reduction in central macular thickness following moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (p < 0.001) [32]. Additionally, a 52% lower odds ratio for microaneurysm formation was recorded in the intervention group (p = 0.039) of the Finnish Lifestyle Intervention Study [35].

Mechanisms underlying the relationship between physical activity and visual outcomes

The review showed that physical activity affects visual outcomes mainly through vascular and metabolic mechanisms. It has been proposed that microvascular injury results from endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress, and both are exacerbated by elevated triglycerides and chronic hyperglycemia associated with T2DM [35]. Specifically, one of the included RCTs demonstrated the importance of lipid metabolism in the development of diabetic retinopathy by associating increased triglycerides with a higher risk of microaneurysm formation. Additionally, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise increased central macular thinning in an Iranian population [32]. However, one RCT reported that even after a 22-year follow up period, physical activity did not produce a discernible vascular or metabolic effect on the eye [37].

Influence of social determinants of health on physical activity and visual outcomes

Healthcare accessibility and policy support

Where reported by the included RCTs, some social determinants appeared to relate to participation and outcomes, although these were systematically assessed. One RCT highlighted Finland's egalitarian healthcare system, which reduced inequalities in diabetes and diabetic retinopathy outcomes by providing universal access to follow-up care [37]. The case was different in the China Da Qing Study which hypothesized that negative long-term consequences might have resulted from the lack of structured diabetes management after the intervention phase [34]. In the Nepal Diabetes Study, the existence of a focused educational program may have influenced the 100% diabetic retinopathy screening uptake rate in the intervention group compared to 0% for the control group [31], which subsequently lowered mean HbA1c levels from 7.2% to 6.1% [31].

Socioeconomic and educational barriers

Adherence to a lifestyle change is significantly hampered by lower socioeconomic levels and limited education. According to the Nepal Diabetes Study, 20% of participants lacked literacy, and 45% had low health literacy, which made it challenging for participants to follow the prescribed physical activity regimen [31]. Similarly, the China Da Qing Study identified rural residence as a major barrier to receiving advanced diabetes care [34]. Other studies demonstrated varying degrees of contextual adaptation, with the Japanese study using culturally contextualised materials and methods such as Asian-specific BMI thresholds [38], and the Italian study using interactive group education to overcome socio-cultural barriers [36]. While education was included as a covariate in the RCT conducted in the US and Canada, socioeconomic status, healthcare access, and cultural influences were not directly assessed in relation to outcomes [33], indicating a larger gap in how RCTs in this area approach social determinants.

Relationship between visual outcomes and other variables

RCTs that explored other variables alongside visual outcomes established associations across several metabolic and demographic factors. The most often reported metabolic variable linked to visual outcomes is HbA1c, and in one study, cumulative HbA1c was the sole significant predictor of diabetic retinopathy (HR 1.4, 95% CI: 1.02–1.88) [37]. Higher HbA1c and fasting glucose were also associated with an increased risk of retinopathy in the China Da Qing Study, where all cases of serious retinopathy only occurred in individuals who developed diabetes [34]. Triglyceride levels also appeared to have an impact on the microvascular health of the retina with higher baseline triglyceride levels found to be significantly related with an increased risk of microaneurysm development (p = 0.003) [35]. With regards to BMI, the China Da Qing Study [34] indicated that a reduced BMI decreased the incidence of diabetes although no direct correlation between BMI and improvement of visual outcomes was observed. Similar evidence showed that a reduction in BMI among the intervention group improved metabolic control and reduced the progression of diabetic retinopathy [36]. Age also emerged as a relevant factor, with the Iranian study identifying significant correlations between older age and both higher central macular thickness (rho = 0.525, p = 0.001) and fasting blood sugar (rho = 0.457, p = 0.003), suggesting an age-mediated relationship between glycaemic status and retinal outcomes [32]. Again, higher levels of physical activity and better HbA1c were linked to a lower risk of retinopathy in the Nepal study, although this was not statistically quantified [31].

Discussion

Summary of findings

This scoping review revealed significant variations in the RCTs that have examined the relationship between physical activity and visual outcomes in people living with T2DM. Across the selected studies, the sample sizes ranged from 40 to 2033 people, while study durations were between three months to 26 years. The majority of the studies focused on individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, aged between 25 to above 80 years. Although protocols varied in frequency and duration, interventions were generally moderately intense and researcher-supervised to improve adherence. Visual outcomes were inconsistent across studies, with some studies reporting a decrease in microaneurysms, diabetic retinopathy complications, and central macular thickness, while others observed no change in the incidence of retinopathy, and even reported an increase in retinopathy incidence in the intervention group. Several studies reported associations between physical activity-related reductions in HbA1c and a lower incidence of retinopathy, while moderate aerobic exercise was hypothesized to promote retinal perfusion through enhanced endothelial function. Moreover, adherence and intervention success were influenced by factors such as geographic location, socioeconomic status, education and health literacy. Compliance to prescribed routines was hampered by low level of formal education or illiteracy, while access to long-term care was restricted in remote areas. The complex nature of diabetic eye disease and the challenges in implementing physical activity guidelines are generally reflected in the substantial variations in RCT designs, demographics, and interventions found in this review.

Discussion of main findings

The research on how physical activity affects visual outcomes in people with T2DM remains complex and evolving. This scoping review examined RCTs that explored this relationship, revealing a significant variation in study designs, participant characteristics, intervention regimens, and outcome measures. These variations across the studies highlights the complex nature of diabetic eye disease and the challenges in implementing lifestyle interventions that consistently improve clinical outcomes.

The RCTs included in this review used varying study designs. While some employed factorial [33], stepped-wedge [32], or four-arm parallel-group designs [34], the majority used two-arm parallel-group formats [31], [35], [36], [37], [38], with these variations reflecting various research objectives and contexts. Long-term studies, some of which lasted up to 26 years [34], [35], [37], provided insights on the chronic nature of the disease and its progression, and the possible contribution of physical activity in delaying retinal complications. In contrast, shorter three-month interventions were useful in the assessment of acute effects [32], although they may not have captured long-term retinal changes. Study findings were substantially influenced by the duration of the intervention and period of follow-up. Although studies with longer follow-up periods were more susceptible to confounding factors such as participant attrition and changing clinical care, they were valuable in identifying subtle changes in microvascular complications. The statistical power and generalisability of results were impacted by the vast range of sample sizes, which ranged from 40 to 2033 participants [32], [33]. This necessitates the development of standard measurement or method for such studies.

The criteria for enrolment also differed. Only one study [31] concentrated on newly diagnosed patients, whereas the majority [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37] targeted people already diagnosed with T2DM. Age ranges spanned from 25 to over 80 years, with one study specifically targeting participants over 80 years old [36]. These variations have implications both for research and clinical practice. While physical activity may only delay the progression of ocular complications in individuals with long-standing diabetes, early lifestyle interventions may prevent the actual onset of these ocular complications, promoting patient independence and quality of life. Additionally, people who were older or had lived with diabetes over a longer period of time were less likely to benefit from physical activity due to irreversible retinal changes [36].

One crucial element that emerged from the findings of this review is supervision. Five RCTs improved adherence and intervention fidelity by using researcher-supervised protocols [32], [34], [35], [36], [37]. Self-supervision was used in one study [33], which may have resulted in inconsistent intensity and decreased efficacy. A hybrid approach that combined self-monitoring and phone follow-ups was also reported [31], [38], indicating that mixed models could enhance adherence in resource-limited environments, which could improve the effects of physical activity on the visual functioning of patients with T2DM.

The majority of interventions were categorized as moderate intensity, despite definitional inconsistencies. While some studies were less specific, focusing on overall increases in habitual activity [33], [35], [36], [37], [38], others offered clear protocols, such as culturally relevant or scheduled aerobic activities [31], [32], [34]. The frequency and length of the prescribed activities also varied, with sessions taking place three to five times a week, ranging from 30 min daily to four hours weekly. These differences are noteworthy because the level of intensity and cumulative exposure to physical activity influence physiological changes in glycemic control and retinal vascular function [32]. This certainly calls for the design of a standardised protocol in developing effective interventions that are sustainable, equitable and culturally appropriate for T2DM.

Visual outcomes were among the most inconsistent findings in this review. A 52% decrease in microaneurysms [35], a 47% decrease in the incidence of severe diabetic retinopathy [34], and a decrease in central macular thickness [32] were among the protective effects reported by some of the RCTs. These imply that early retinal damage may be significantly mitigated by physical activity, though the macular thickness finding [32] should be interpreted with caution given the short three-month duration and unclear outcome assessor blinding. However, other studies produced adverse or null results. While two RCT observed no discernible change in the incidence of diabetic retinopathy over the study periods [37], [38], another documented an unanticipated rise in retinopathy cases in the intervention group [31]. These disparities could be due to confounding pharmacologic therapies, variations in adherence levels, or baseline disease severity, and may also reflect methodological differences across the trials, including absent allocation concealment in the trial reporting the unexpected rise in retinopathy [31] and baseline imbalance in the trial reporting null effects [36], whereas more methodologically robust trials [33], [37], [38] consistently point to modest, glycaemia-mediated effects. Additionally, they imply that the advantages of physical activity on diabetic eye health are inconsistent, necessitating further well-structured physical activity intervention.

Several studies indicated specific biological pathways that may explain the observed effects of physical activity on visual outcomes. A study proposed that important mechanisms in diabetic microvascular complications, including endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress were actively driven by chronic hyperglycemia [39]. While some studies reported associations between physical activity-related reductions in HbA1c and a lower risk of retinopathy [32], [34], [35], [37], another study linked increased triglycerides to the formation of microaneurysms [35]. It was also reported that moderate aerobic exercise increased retinal perfusion and decreased ischemia through improvements in endothelial function [33], although these findings require additional empirical justification. Overall, the precise mechanisms remain underexplored in the literature.

Although the RCTs were not typically designed to assess some social determinants of health, they were noted in individuals. Socioeconomic status, health literacy, and access to care had impact on adherence and efficacy, even in controlled RCT settings. One study linked high level of adherence to a robust healthcare system [35], [37], while another observed that high illiteracy rates made it difficult for participants to follow activity routines [31]. Living in remote areas made it harder to receive long-term care, which reduced the sustainability of the benefits of the intervention [34]. These observations emphasize that recommending physical activity as a stand-alone intervention without any contextual support may not be enough to prevent deteriorating of vision in persons with T2DM. Tailored interventions that consider local infrastructure, health education, and financial obstacles, such as the use of community health workers or mobile health tools may improve outcomes in resource-limited settings.

An important consideration not addressed by any of the included RCTs is the bidirectional link between visual function and physical activity. Evidence indicates that poor visual functioning itself may constitute a barrier to physical activity [40], [41], [42]. Visually impaired individuals with diabetes have consistently shown lower levels of physical activity and lower diabetes management self-efficacy than their counterparts with no visual impairment [40]. For instance, accelerometer-based studies have shown that older adults with visual impairment accumulate fewer active minutes and more fragmented activity patterns, partly due to fear of falling [41]. This sets up a potentially harmful cycle whereby diabetes-related vision deterioration lowers physical activity levels, which consequently worsens glycaemic control and accelerates further visual decline.

Despite the geographic concentration of the included RCTs in Europe and Asia, the findings of this review still hold relevance for underserved populations globally. The core mechanisms of increased glycaemic control, increased endothelial function, and decreased oxidative stress through physical activity are physiologically universal and context-independent. Furthermore, the evidence that low-cost, moderate-intensity physical activity, such as walking and habitual movement offer protective benefits for the retina suggests that eye health may be improved without specialised equipment or advanced healthcare infrastructure. The observation that adherence was improved by hybrid supervision models that combined remote follow-up and self-monitoring supports the need for adaptability in resource-limited settings. Together, these findings suggest that structured physical activity interventions may potentially reduce the burden of diabetes-related visual impairment in the populations where it is most prevalent, provided they are suitably adapted to local literacy levels, cultural norms, and healthcare realities.

Implication for policy, practice and research

While the evidence base is mixed, this scoping review highlights key tentative implications for policy development, clinical practice, and future research, particularly regarding the management of visual outcomes among individuals with T2DM.

Policy implications

The evidence in this review provides preliminary support for the consideration of structured physical activity programs within national diabetes management guidelines. This is particularly vital in LMICs, where diabetes-related visual impairment is increasing in prevalence, with preventive measures still lacking. Policies that support community-based physical activity programs, universal access to diabetic eye screening, and education campaigns on preventing diabetic eye disease can help decrease the long-term burden of T2DM on healthcare systems. Also, targeted funding is needed to support locally adaptable, sustainable and culturally relevant physical activity programs, especially in underprivileged and rural areas with limited access to healthcare.

Implications for clinical practice

The review emphasizes the importance of integrating supervised and customised physical activity programs into routine diabetes care by eye care professionals, diabetes educators, and other public health experts. However, these should be tailored to reflect literacy levels, socioeconomic conditions, and cultural norms of patients. The variances in individuals' adherence to physical activity regimen and outcomes observed necessitate multidisciplinary collaboration in designing interventions that are practical, sustainable, and context-sensitive. Additionally, utilising peer-led support systems, mobile health devices, and community health workers may improve adherence and associated visual health outcomes in resource-limited settings.

Research implications

Significant research gaps remain regarding the long-term impacts of physical activity on visual functions such as contrast sensitivity, colour vision, and visual fields. There is a need for more high-quality RCTs with extended follow-ups and standardised outcome measures. Future research could be conducted in diverse geographical areas, especially in LMICs where the prevalence of diabetes complications is highest. Additionally, incorporating social determinants of health into trial designs will offer a thorough understanding of the contextual factors that exacerbate or hinder the effectiveness of interventions. Finally, more mechanistic studies are needed to explore the biological pathways by which physical activity affects retinal and optic nerve health of people living with T2DM.

Limitations and future research

The direct comparisons between trials were limited by the significant heterogeneity in research design, intervention regimens, and outcome measures across the included studies. Although RCTs are considered gold standard research and are expected to provide high levels of evidence, the critical appraisal revealed some inherent methodological limitations such as smaller sample sizes, lack of long-term follow-up, and variances in adherence to physical activity interventions, that may likely affect the generalisability of the findings. Also, inter-rater agreement at the screening stage was assessed qualitatively through consensus discussion rather than quantified using a formal statistic. Furthermore, restricting eligibility to RCTs to align with the review's objective of mapping interventional evidence and supporting causal inference excluded observational and quasi-experimental studies that may have provided complementary insights. Also, just one RCT specifically explored visual functions beyond the progression of diabetic retinopathy, highlighting that the impact of physical activity on visual functions like contrast sensitivity, colour vision, and visual fields remains mostly unexplored. Finally, there was a conspicuous lack of RCTs from Africa, South America and Oceania, with all the included studies conducted in Europe, Asia and North America. Despite these limitations, this scoping review synthesises and analyses data from relevant RCTs conducted in various geographic and healthcare settings, identifying the existing research gaps and offering vital insights into the complex relationship between physical activity and visual outcomes in people with T2DM.

Looking ahead, studies should prioritise large-scale, multicentre RCTs with standardised physical activity protocols and comprehensive visual function assessment that extend beyond diabetic retinopathy to include contrast sensitivity, colour vision, and visual fields. These studies should be carried out in underrepresented areas, especially Africa, South America, and Oceania, where the burden of T2DM-related visual impairment is increasing the fastest, and should include extended follow-up periods to capture the cumulative effects of physical activity on retinal health. Social determinants of health, such as health literacy, socioeconomic level, and cultural acceptability, should be incorporated into future trial designs as pre-specified moderating variables to clarify who benefits from interventions and under what conditions. To clarify the biological processes behind reported effects, mechanistic studies utilising retinal imaging biomarkers such as optical coherence tomography angiography, should be incorporated into protocols. Future trials should also consider baseline visual function as a potential moderator of adherence to physical activity interventions, as none of the included RCTs accounted for this factor. Finally, pragmatic studies evaluating community-based delivery models and adherence methods supported by mobile health are needed to generate evidence that can inform routine diabetes care protocols in resource-limited settings.

This review characterises the complex and evolving evidence regarding physical activity as a modifiable factor in reducing diabetes-related visual impairment, aligning with the WHO Global Diabetes Compact and the Global Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases 2013–2030. The review reinforces the idea that structured lifestyle interventions could complement pharmacological management in achieving these global goals. Importantly, we echo the joint position of the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) and the International Agency for the Prevention of Blindness (IAPB), which calls on the WHO to integrate a specific target for diabetic retinopathy in its global eye health framework alongside current targets for cataract and refractive error, acknowledging that diabetes-related eye disease is still a leading cause of avoidable blindness. We propose that future diabetes research and practice draw on these globally accepted frameworks as basis for developing context-specific physical activity interventions for persons with T2DM.

Conclusion

The RCTs included in this review provide a wealth of varied evidence on the effects of physical activity on visual outcomes in individuals with T2DM. The mixed findings highlight the complexity of this association, ranging from null or even negative effects to significant decreases in microaneurysm development and severe diabetic retinopathy risk. These inconsistencies are likely caused by variations in study designs, intervention strategies, participant demographics, as well as contextual factors like socioeconomic status and healthcare accessibility.

Ultimately, in order to ensure that initiatives are both successful and equitable, the integration of lifestyle interventions into conventional diabetes care should be pursued with careful consideration of both individual patient characteristics and broader socioeconomic variables. Although physical activity shows potential as a modifiable factor in reducing the burden of diabetic eye disease, achieving its full potential will necessitate collaborative efforts among the clinical, public health, and research domains.
