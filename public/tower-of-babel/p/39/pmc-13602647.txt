Socioeconomic status and cognitive function in older adults: the mediating roles of social activity and loneliness in three aging cohorts
Authors: Rui Dong, Lin Gui, Dexiang Xu, Huijuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive impairment among older adults poses a substantial and growing public health burden. Despite established links between SES and cognition, the psychosocial pathways and their variation across national contexts remain unclear. This study examines the relationship between SES and cognitive function in middle-aged and older adults, and explores the mediating roles of social activity and loneliness in this association.MethodsUsing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) in the United States and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), we analyzed participants aged ≥ 50. SES was derived from latent class analysis of family income, educational level, and employment status. Cognitive function was assessed through standardized tests. Linear mixed models evaluated SES-cognition associations, and mediation analyses explored potential associative pathways.ResultsHigher SES was significantly associated with better cognitive function in all cohort studies, with the strength of the association varying cross-nationally: strongest in CHARLS (β = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.16–0.26), intermediate in HRS (β = 0.14, 0.11–0.17), and weakest in ELSA (β = 0.031, 0.003–0.058). Social activities mediated 7.28% to 12.08%, and loneliness mediated 1.89% to 18.33% of these associations. The relationships remained consistent across the subgroups.ConclusionSocioeconomic status demonstrated a robust, graded association with cognitive function in older adults, with this relationship partially mediated by social activity involvement and loneliness. The cross-national gradient in effect sizes suggests that macro-level structural and policy contexts may substantially moderate this relationship. These findings highlight the need for integrated interventions addressing both psychosocial mediators and structural determinants to reduce cognitive health disparities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28457-z.Keywords: Socioeconomic status, Cognitive function, Social activity, Loneliness, Cohort studies

Introduction

The World Health Organization predicts that by 2030, one in six people worldwide will be 60 years or older [1]. Population aging, a global demographic trend, is accompanied by age-related cognitive decline, thereby posing a substantial burden on healthcare systems [2]. The number of people living with dementia is projected to nearly triple from 57 million in 2019 to 153 million in 2050, with associated global economic costs escalating to approximately 3 trillion dollars, underscoring the urgent need for effective preventive strategies [3]. Given the absence of disease-modifying treatments, identifying modifiable risk factors in the preclinical stages of cognitive decline is crucial [4].

Socioeconomic status (SES), as defined by Mueller and Parcel [5] as “the relative position of a household or individual within a hierarchical social structure based on access to or control over wealth, prestige, and power,” is a key determinant of health disparities in later life [6], with substantial evidence indicating that lower SES is associated with poorer cognitive performance and an accelerated rate of cognitive decline [7]. However, the psychosocial mechanisms underlying this association are not fully understood. Social activities and loneliness represent two promising candidate mediators. Based on stress theory, individuals with higher SES and class identity have greater social support and fewer stressors [8]. These advantages, including access to a wider range of social resources, may in turn facilitate greater engagement in social activities [9, 10]. Previous studies also found that residents with low SES had a higher prevalence of social isolation and loneliness than did higher-SES residents [11]. The ability to form social connections is also constrained by factors in deprived neighborhoods, such as low income, poor services, fear of crime, and a transient population [12]. The Reserve Capacity Model (RCM) provides a complementary framework for examining psychosocial pathways in health disparities [13]. According to the RCM, low SES may predict enhanced emotional and physiological reactivity to stress due to a deficiency in resilient psychosocial resources (i.e., “reserve capacity”) and limited opportunities to replenish those resources [14]. Drawing on stress theory and the reserve capacity model, we posit that the association between SES and cognitive health may operate through psychosocial pathways—specifically, that higher SES facilitates social engagement while buffering against loneliness, thereby preserving cognitive function [12, 15–17]. Yet, the extent to which these factors mediate the SES-cognition relationship remains unclear.

To address this gap, this study utilizes harmonized data from three nationally representative cohort studies conducted in diverse economic and social contexts: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) in the United States, the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) in the United Kingdom, and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). We aim to examine the mediating roles of social activity and loneliness in the association between SES and cognitive function in older adults. By identifying the specific roles of modifiable factors like social engagement and loneliness, our findings can help guide public health initiatives aimed at promoting cognitive health and reducing health inequalities among the aging population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study utilized data from three nationally representative cohort studies: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [18–20]. To ensure comparability, we included participants from waves 9 to 13 for HRS (2008–2016), waves 5 to 9 for ELSA (2010–2018) and waves 1 to 4 for CHARLS (2011–2018). This study received ethical approval from the institutional review boards of the University of Michigan for HRS, the London Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee for the ELSA, and Peking University for CHARLS. Written informed consent was obtained from every participant before any data collection.

We included only participants who were aged 50 years or older at their baseline assessment with complete baseline data on socioeconomic status, social activities, loneliness, cognitive function and relevant covariates. Any participant under age 50 at baseline was excluded, ensuring a harmonized age criterion across cohorts for valid cross-national comparison of older adults. In addition, participants were required to have completed at least two follow-up assessments. We excluded those with memory-related diseases (dementia and Parkinson’s disease) or a baseline z-score of cognitive state less than 10th percentile to minimize recall bias. The participant selection process for each cohort is outlined in Figure S1 to S3 in Supplementary Material. The analysis comprised 18,798, 14,371 and 6551 participants from HRS, ELSA and CHARLS cohort studies, respectively. Detailed characteristics of participants are provided in Supplementary material: Table S1. We compared the baseline characteristics of the included and excluded samples for CHARLS (Supplementary Table S2), for HRS (Supplementary Table S3) and for ELSA (Supplementary Table S4), respectively.

Measures

Based on previous studies, three indicators were used to measure SES, including family income, educational level and employment status [21, 22]. Family income was assessed as the total income at the household level of each participant. We categorized family income into quartiles (lowest, lower, higher, and highest). Educational level was categorized into three levels, including less than upper secondary, upper secondary and tertiary education. Employment status was categorized as not working, working and retired. An overall SES variable was created using latent class analysis based on family income, education and employment status. The latent class analysis creates an unmeasured variable with a set of mutually exclusive latent classes by utilizing numerous observed categorical variables [23]. Two latent classes were identified, which respectively represented high and low SES according to the item-response probabilities. The model fit indices for latent class analysis of SES are shown in Supplementary material: Table S5 (CHARLS), Table S6 (HRS), and Table S7 (ELSA).

Cognitive function was assessed at baseline and follow-up visits in three domains: memory, orientation, and executive function. The memory function was evaluated using immediate and delayed word recall tests. The orientation function was assessed by asking participants about the year, month, day, and day of the week (and season). Executive function was measured using serial sevens test and backward counting from 20 in HRS, an animal-naming fluency test in the ELSA, and pentagon copying test and serial sevens test in the CHARLS (Supplementary material: Table S8). Higher scores indicated greater performance, and the global cognitive score in the study was defined as the sum of scores of 3 dimensions [18–20]. Standardized z scores were generated by subtracting the baseline mean and dividing by the SD. These standardized z scores served as a proxy for cognitive function in all analyses. Positive scores indicated performance above the population mean, while negative scores indicated performance below it.

Participants were classified based on their involvement in social activities: those who participated in at least one social group were coded as 1, while those with no participation in any social group were coded as 0. To explore the roles of different types of social activities, we categorized these organizations into three dimensions based on their primary function and calculated the count within each: civic and advocacy organizations, community and social organizations, and personal development organizations. The detailed classification of this variable for the HRS, ELSA, and CHARLS datasets used in our study is provided in Supplementary Material Table S9. Loneliness was assessed using the three-item revised University of California, Los Angeles Loneliness Scale (R-UCLA) [24] in both the HRS and ELSA studies. Participants were asked to report frequency of feeling left out, isolated, or lacking companionship (“hardly or never”, “some of the time”, or “often”). Scores ranged from 3 to 9, with a higher score indicating more severe loneliness. Loneliness was coded as 0 (not lonely) and 1 (scores ≥ 4). In CHARLS, loneliness was measured with a single item included in the Centre for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CESD): “how often did you feel lonely in the last week?” [25] This item measured the frequency of feeling lonely during the last week on a scale from “rarely or none (< 1 day),” “some or a little (1–2 days),” “occasionally (3–4 days),” and “most (5–7 days)”. Loneliness was coded into 0 (felt lonely rarely or none of the time) and 1 (felt lonely some or a little, occasionally or most of the time) [26, 27].

Based on prior research, covariates included demographic factors, health behaviors and clinical factors. Demographic covariates included age, sex, early-life conditions and medical insurance. Specifically, early-life conditions were constructed based on retrospective life history data collected in the surveys. In CHARLS, early-life conditions were assessed using a self-reported childhood economic status item, in which participants rated their family’s financial situation before age 17 compared to the average household in their community. In HRS, early-life conditions were derived from parental education, specifically the highest level of education attained by either parent. In ELSA, early-life conditions were captured by a binary indicator of whether the participant experienced severe financial hardship during childhood. Across all three cohorts, early-life conditions were harmonized into a binary variable contrasting “poor” versus “fair” early circumstances. Health behavior covariates included physical activity, smoking status and alcohol consumption. Clinical factors included the number of non-communicable diseases (Supplementary material: Table S9).

Statistical analysis

For continuous data, baseline attributes are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and for categorical variables, as percentages. The t-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables were used to compare baseline characteristics.

We employed linear mixed models to examine the association between socioeconomic status and cognitive function. We included participants from HRS waves 9–13 (2008–2016), ELSA waves 5–9 (2010–2018), and CHARLS waves 1–4 (2011–2018), and used linear mixed models to examine the associations of time-varying SES, social activity, loneliness and cognitive function over 7–8 years of follow-up, accounting for within-subject correlation via random effects. The following fixed effects were included: main effects of SES, time (follow-up year from baseline, as a continuous variable), interaction SES × time, and covariates. The coefficient of the SES × time interaction represents the difference in the annual rate of cognitive change between the two SES groups (SD/year), with a positive value indicating slower decline and a negative value indicating faster decline in the higher SES group relative to the lower SES group. Random effects included both random intercept and random slope for time, allowing each participant to account for the individual difference in baseline cognitive performance and longitudinal rate of cognitive aging during follow-up. To examine the robustness of our findings, we adjusted for different covariates in the models. In model 1, we used a crude model without any adjustment. In model 2, we adjusted for demographic covariates, including age, sex, early-life condition, and medical insurance; health behavior covariates including physical activity, smoking status, alcohol consumption; clinical covariate including the number of non-communicable diseases. Model 3, as a fully adjusted model, additionally adjusted for social activities (including civic and advocacy organization, community and social organization, and personal development organization) and loneliness. As a sensitivity check, we constructed an alternative continuous SES score by summing and standardizing the same three indicators (educational level, family income, and employment status).

To investigate the differences in the relationship between SES and cognitive function across various subpopulations, subgroup analysis was carried out, stratified by gender, age, alcohol consumption, current smoking, physical activity, non-communicable diseases, medical insurance coverage, social activity, civic and advocacy organization, community and social organization, personal development organization and loneliness. We conducted a mediation analysis to assess the direct and indirect associations between SES and cognitive function via social activities and loneliness. In brief, SES was used as the predictor variable (X), cognitive function as the outcome variable (Y), and two variables served as mediators (M): social activities (encompassing civic and advocacy organization, community and social organization, and personal development organization) and loneliness. For each cohort, we specified: (i) mediator models – logistic regression for social activities and loneliness; (ii) outcome model – linear regression for cognitive function. For CHARLS, SES was assessed at Wave 1 (2011), social activity and loneliness at Wave 3 (2015), and cognition at Wave 4 (2018). For ELSA, SES was assessed at Wave 5 (2010), social activity and loneliness at Wave 8 (2016), and cognition at Wave 9 (2018). For HRS, SES was assessed at Wave 9 (2008), social activity and loneliness at Wave 11 (2012), and cognition at Wave 13 (2016). As a sensitivity check for potential reverse causation, we also conducted a reverse mediation analysis with cognitive function as the predictor, the same mediators (loneliness and social activities), and later SES as the outcome. Specifically, we maintained the same temporal lags as in the primary mediation analysis but reversed the temporal ordering. Additionally, we assessed the robustness of our mediation estimates to unmeasured confounding using the medsens function in the mediation package, which evaluates how strong the correlation between the residuals of the mediator and outcome models would need to be to nullify the indirect effect.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1). The mediation analysis was performed using the causal mediation framework proposed by Imai et al. (2010) as implemented in the mediation package in R [28]. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 39,720 participants from three independent studies were included in the present analysis. The characteristics of each study population, stratified by socioeconomic status (SES), are presented separately in Table 1 (CHARLS, n = 6,551), Table 2 (HRS, n = 18,798), and Table 3 (ELSA, n = 14,371). Within each study cohort, participants were distributed between high and low SES groups. P-values test differences in the distribution of baseline characteristics between the high and low SES groups. The proportion of participants classified as high versus low SES was balanced by study design (50% high, 50% low in each cohort). Notable differences in baseline characteristics between the high and low SES groups were observed. In general, participants in the high SES group tended to be younger, had fewer chronic diseases, and exhibited more favorable health profiles compared to their low SES counterparts. High SES group also showed higher cognitive scores. Across the three cohorts, the prevalence of active social participation was 74.7% in CHARLS, 80.9% in HRS, and 69.1% in ELSA. Loneliness was reported by 30.5% of CHARLS participants, 55.8% of HRS participants, and 44.4% of ELSA participants. Detailed distributions for all measured characteristics are provided in Tables 1, 2 and 3.

The association between SES and cognitive function was analyzed within each of the three prospective cohort studies (CHARLS, HRS, ELSA). After full adjustment for covariates including demographics, health behaviors, and clinical factors (Model 3), high SES was associated with significantly higher cognitive scores: β = 0.21 (0.16–0.26) in CHARLS, β = 0.14 (0.11–0.17) in HRS and β = 0.031 (0.003–0.058) in ELSA (all p < 0.001) (Table 4). Furthermore, the SES × Time interaction was significant in the HRS cohort, suggesting that higher SES was associated with a slower rate of cognitive decline over time. However, this interaction was not statistically significant in the CHARLS or ELSA cohorts. To assess whether dichotomizing socioeconomic status (SES) via latent class analysis (LCA) might bias the observed associations, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which the binary SES variable was replaced by a continuous SES score. The results from models using the continuous SES score were highly consistent with those from our primary analysis based on the binary LCA classification (Supplementary Table S10).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to utilize large-scale data from three nationally representative cohort studies to examine the association between socioeconomic status and cognitive function in older adults and its underlying mechanisms. The analysis demonstrated that higher SES is significantly associated with better cognitive performance across all three cohorts. Notably, the analysis revealed that in HRS, higher SES was associated not only with better baseline cognition but also with slower rates of cognitive decline over time. Furthermore, exploratory analyses suggested that participation in social activities and loneliness may serve as potential mediators in this association.

The association between socioeconomic status and cognitive function was robust across all three cohorts, with the strongest association observed in CHARLS (β = 0.21), intermediate in HRS (β = 0.14), and weakest in ELSA (β = 0.031). Furthermore, the significant positive SES × Time interaction in HRS indicated that individuals with higher SES experienced slower cognitive decline over time compared to their lower SES counterparts. In contrast, this interaction was not statistically significant in CHARLS or ELSA. This cross-national variation may be attributed to differences in the maturity and scope of welfare systems across these countries [29]. The United Kingdom has a public health-care system, and although the United States provides public health care for adults aged 65 years and older through Medicare, a federal health insurance program, there are certain eligibility requirements to receive Medicare benefits [30]. Inequalities in disposable income among people aged 65 years and older are highest in China, followed by the United States, and the United Kingdom [31]. The United Kingdom and the United States have substantially higher healthcare expenditures per capita compared to China, which may help mitigate the negative association of low socioeconomic status on cognitive aging [32]. China’s rapidly evolving socioeconomic context, where structural disparities in resource allocation and access to health and social services remain pronounced, may amplify the association between SES and cognitive health [33]. The lack of a significant interaction in CHARLS, despite the largest cross-sectional effect, may reflect the relatively shorter follow-up period or the complex dynamics of a society undergoing rapid economic transition, where long-term cumulative effects of SES on cognitive decline are not yet fully manifested. These cross-national comparisons align with existing evidence on the political and structural determinants of health [34], reinforcing the hypothesis that societal-level policies play a critical role in shaping health inequalities. Building on these findings, it is plausible to hypothesize that broader investment in inclusive social welfare systems, equitable educational opportunities, and health coverage may emerge as key mechanisms for mitigating the effects of socioeconomic disadvantage.

The subgroup analyses demonstrated that the pervasive protective effect of SES on cognitive function remained statistically significant across demographic, lifestyle, and clinical subgroups. However, the strength of this association varied substantially among different populations. The association was strongest in the < 60 years group, strongly suggesting that the influence of SES is most critical around the transition into older adulthood and in the early phases of age-related cognitive change. This finding aligns with previous studies emphasizing the importance of early intervention and prevention strategies for cognitive decline, especially among individuals at risk of dementia. Identifying effective interventions to slow cognitive decline holds significant potential for enhancing quality of life in later years and reducing the healthcare costs associated with dementia care [35]. Furthermore, the beneficial association remained significant both among those who were physically inactive and those who were socially inactive, reinforcing that SES confers cognitive advantages that cannot be fully explained or replaced by any single health behavior. This pattern underscores the role of SES as a fundamental socio-structural determinant that operates through multiple pathways beyond individual lifestyle factors. These findings highlight the need for multi-level interventions spanning community-based social engagement, psychological support for loneliness, and policy reforms to reduce structural inequities—such as prioritizing health resources for disadvantaged groups, addressing unhealthy behaviors, and improving healthcare access [36].

Mediation analyses revealed that social activity and loneliness may play significant roles linking SES to cognitive function. Individuals with higher SES showed greater participation in social activities and lower levels of loneliness, both of which were associated with better cognitive outcomes. Notably, the magnitude of these indirect effects was modest, suggesting these psychosocial factors explain only a small fraction of the socioeconomic gradient in cognitive scores. As a sensitivity check for reverse causation, we conducted reverse mediation analyses. Significant indirect effects were observed only in the HRS cohort, not in CHARLS or ELSA. This cross-cohort inconsistency suggests that reverse causation is unlikely to fully account for our primary findings, although bidirectional influences—particularly in the United States context—cannot be completely ruled out. These findings, while quantitatively limited in their mediating role, fit well within the “psychosocial-biological” model. Studies are increasingly linking high levels of physiological stress mechanisms to low SES and biobehavioral variables, such as depression, chronic stress, and lower levels of social support [37]. Higher socioeconomic status and class identity are linked to greater social support and fewer stressors, as well as access to broader social networks and resources [8]. On the one hand, previous studies have shown that individuals with higher SES demonstrate greater participation in social activities, which provides a form of complex cognitive stimulation that helps maintain and enhance neural function [38–40]. On the other hand, lonely individuals tend to engage less in cognitively stimulating activities, which may limit neural resilience and recovery, ultimately leading to reduced cognitive reserve and impaired cognitive function [41–43]. Beyond the psychosocial pathways in our mediation analysis, other mechanisms may also contribute to the SES-cognition relationship and warrant further investigation. For instance, higher SES may facilitate better healthcare access and healthier lifestyles, while buffering against chronic stress that can adversely remodel brain regions such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex [44–47]. Future research should directly test these potential pathways to clarify their roles in mediating the socioeconomic gradient in cognitive health.

This study has several strengths, including using high-quality data from multiple large prospective cohort studies and applying a unified framework for mediation and subgroup analyses, which enhances the reliability of the results. However, several limitations should be noted. First, despite adjusting for numerous covariates, additional potential confounders could not be obtained from available data. Second, the measurements of SES, social activity, loneliness, and cognitive function relied on self-report, which is subject to potential measurement error. Thirdly, the absolute magnitudes of the indirect effects are modest, and these exploratory analyses suggest that loneliness and social activities may account for only a small fraction of the socioeconomic gradient in cognitive scores. Fourth, factors such as unmeasured psychosocial confounding or health status could bias the estimates in mediation analyses. For example, loneliness is closely related to depressive symptoms and other psychosocial factors that may influence both SES over the life course and cognitive outcomes. Additionally, selective mortality may play a role: individuals with lower SES and poorer health profiles are at higher risk of mortality, leading to an underrepresentation of the most disadvantaged individuals in older age groups. Consequently, the older age groups may be comprised of relatively “healthier” survivors, which could attenuate the observed SES-cognition gradient in these strata. Fifth, owing to the unavoidable missing data in this large nationwide longitudinal survey, selection bias may be created by eliminating cases without complete information in the analysis phase. Specifically, the excluded participants exhibited lower levels of social activity, higher loneliness, and lower educational attainment—factors linked to reduced research participation interest. This pattern suggests that data loss was not random, but rather driven by individuals who were less socially engaged, more isolated, and less inclined to cooperate with the survey. Consequently, our results may underrepresent these disengaged subgroups, limiting the generalizability of our findings to the most marginalized populations. Future studies should adopt targeted strategies to better capture data from such hard-to-reach individuals. Finally, this study measured socioeconomic status using only three core dimensions: family income, educational attainment and employment status. Future research could further examine more established characteristics associated with socioeconomic status.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this multinational study demonstrates that higher socioeconomic status is strongly associated with better cognitive function in older adults, mediated partially through social activity and loneliness. The association was strongest in CHARLS, intermediate in HRS, and weakest in ELSA. These findings indicate the importance of combining individual-level interventions with policy-driven strategies aimed at addressing structural inequities, such as expanding educational access, promoting physical activity, fostering community engagement to effectively reduce disparities in cognitive aging.
