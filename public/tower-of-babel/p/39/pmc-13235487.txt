From Efference Copy to Emotional State: Insular Cortex Somatostatin Neurons Buffer Adaptive Responses to Physical Stress
Authors: Max E. Joffe
Published: 2026

Trauma, physical distress, and chronic occupational strain can all contribute to the incidence and severity of psychiatric disorders. Despite these compelling links, we have a limited understanding of how interoceptive somatic signals are transmitted into long-term changes in mood and anxiety. In this issue of Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science, Adank et al. (1) address this critical gap in knowledge by investigating projections from the motor cortex to areas associated with interoceptive processing and affective behavior.

The brain constantly filters out extraneous sensory information to efficiently identify relevant stimuli, make complex decisions, and execute goal-directed motor programs. For mobile animals, filtering out one’s own movement is essential. Primary motor cortex (MOp) efferents guide this process. While the MOp drives movement through its subcortical projections, simultaneous cortico-cortical efferents provide a copy of those movement signals so that other areas may account for future movements. These “efference copy” signals, first described in the 1950s, are thought to enable higher-order brain areas to anticipate future movements and related sensory stimuli (2). As a frivolous but well-known example of this function, these motor efference copies prevent you from tickling yourself. But what happens when the planned motor action does not match the ensuing outcome? When an animal experiences trauma or undergoes a physical stressor such as immobilization, could these motor efference copies promote acute or chronic distress?

The midinsular cortex (mIC) guides emotional responses to sensations and somatic internal states. Previous studies from Luchsinger et al. (3) used brain-clearing and virus-assisted circuit mapping to identify the somatosensory cortex, piriform cortex, and MOp as the 3 most substantial inputs to the mIC. Previous work had identified mIC projections to the extended amygdala as key drivers of responses to immobilization stress and subsequent anxiety-like behavior (4). Thus, the mIC is positioned as a key site for translating efference copies into representations of negative affect. But is the mIC a simple relay station, or does local circuitry enable modulatory processes that titrate responses to stress? About 15% of neurons in cortical areas are local inhibitory interneurons that sculpt projection neuron activity through several subcellular processes. Therefore, projections from the MOp onto mIC interneurons could enable local modulation of affective processing, but the extent, or even existence, of this circuit motif had not been described. This is the exact topic that Adank et al. (1) set out to address.

To first determine whether inhibitory interneurons in the mIC receive input from the MOp, the authors delivered a high-titer AAV1-Cre virus into the MOp of Ai4 mice. High-titer AAV1 displays anterograde transmission, and cells in Ai14 mice express tdTomato red fluorescent protein when Cre is present. Therefore, cells that express tdTomato are in close proximity to inputs from the MOp. Of all tdTomato cells, more than 36% co-expressed somatostatin (SST), a robust marker for a subtype of inhibitory interneuron that targets pyramidal cell dendrites in the neocortex. In contrast, molecular markers for other abundant subtypes of inhibitory interneurons displayed virtually no overlap with tdTomato. Co-expression with SST was far greater than expected compared with the overall abundance of SST cells (5%–7% of neurons in the neocortex). Importantly, these findings were corroborated using retrograde tracing, which identified intact MOp projections to mIC SST cells that preferentially arose from superficial layers of the MOp. Next, the authors used optogenetics to confirm functional synaptic connections from MOp to mIC SST cells. More than 68% of mIC SST cells received measurable excitatory events following light stimulation of channelrhodopsin-expressing MOp terminals. Together, these data suggest that disynaptic inhibitory transmission through SST interneurons may be a key mechanism through which the MOp modulates mIC function.

Previous findings from Luchsinger et al. (3) identified that mIC outputs to the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) mediate adaptive response to stress. The authors next sought to test whether SST cells in the mIC regulate BNST-projecting neurons and whether this synapse may be affected by stress. Optogenetic stimulation of SST cells generated pronounced GABAergic (gamma-aminobutyric acidergic) currents in most BNST-projecting cells. Furthermore, a single bout of restraint stress increased the frequency of GABAergic currents in these cells, suggesting that stress enhances GABA release probability. Next, the authors used in vivo calcium imaging via fiber photometry to assess SST cell activity during restraint stress. Calcium signals within SST cells, which are putative signatures of neural activity, increased during escape behaviors. Interestingly, SST cell activity was consistent and nonadaptive across 5 consecutive days of restraint stress, drawing a clear contrast from previous work showing adaptation in mIC projection neurons. These findings suggest that mIC SST cell activity increases in response to stress and may attenuate affective behaviors. To directly test this idea, the authors expressed the chemogenetic actuator hM3Dq in mIC SST cells in a new cohort of mice. Delivery of the hM3Dq agonist clozapine-N-oxide was then used to increase calcium signaling and neural activity within these cells prior to a series of behavioral assays. SST-hM3Dq stimulation decreased 3 independent measures of anxiety-like and despair-like behavior, highlighting that inhibitory transmission within the mIC can reduce the expression of negative affective behaviors.

Neocortical SST interneurons have emerged as a key cell type mediating affective behaviors in preclinical models. Importantly, convergent findings from human postmortem studies support the translational relevance of SST cells in psychiatric disease (5). Thus far, preclinical research has primarily focused on SST interneuron function within the medial prefrontal cortex, so these new findings broaden our understanding of neocortical regions in which stressful stimuli recruit SST cell activity. The current findings are reminiscent of the effects of immobilization stress and footshock on SST interneuron activity within the medial prefrontal cortex (6,7). The convergent nature of these findings suggests that at least 1 common neuromodulator system broadly activates neocortical SST cells during stressful experiences. Catecholamine systems represent compelling candidates for this likely phenomenon.

The current findings are consistent with previous work by Centanni et al. (4) affirming that inhibition of mIC projections to the BNST can attenuate affective behaviors. Nonetheless, SST cells inhibit projection cells through several molecular mechanisms, and future studies should provide greater granularity in describing how SST cells affect mIC projection neuron activity. For example, GABA released by SST interneurons in the frontal cortex can attenuate dendritic calcium signaling and shunt excitatory transmission from discrete afferents (6). The signaling of the SST peptide itself is an underappreciated inhibitory neuromodulator. Investigating whether related mechanisms occur in the mIC and whether adaptations emerge over time is an important future direction. SST cells can also inhibit parvalbumin interneurons to disinhibit projection cells (7), but it is unknown whether this disinhibitory motif exists in the mIC. Thus, now that mIC SST cells have been identified as guiding adaptive responses to stress, there are several more nuanced mechanisms that merit further investigation.

Chronic stress and physical trauma can promote symptoms of psychiatric disorders and contribute to development of disease. The current studies provide both a basis and a blueprint for investigating long-term adaptations to mIC SST interneurons following disease-related experiences. As noted by the authors, examining effects of chronic stress on synaptic strength across these circuit elements is a clear direction for further studies. In addition, chronic drinking precipitates hyperexcitability of mIC neurons that project to the BNST (8). Considering that drinking decreases excitatory inputs onto SST cells in the frontal cortex (9), these findings raise the possibility that alcohol inhibits mIC SST cells to disinhibit BNST-projecting pyramidal cells and promote negative affective behaviors. Given the strong projections from the MOp onto mIC SST cells described here, it is also tempting to speculate that alcohol-induced changes in this system are involved in the hyperalgesia observed during drug withdrawal.

On the other hand, the current studies introduce mIC SST interneurons as a potential target for antidepressant development. SST cells in the frontal cortex have been extensively implicated in the actions of ketamine and other rapid-acting antidepressants. Interestingly, this literature suggests that acute inhibition of SST cells is necessary and sufficient to drive antidepressant-like effects (10). At face value, the current finding that hM3Dq stimulation of mIC SST cells confers antidepressant-like responses is in opposition to the literature on SST cells in the frontal cortex. The hypothesis that SST cells in the mIC and frontal cortex may exert opposite effects on behavior and display opposite responses to chronic stress may be best tested through a within-subject comparative assessment. Given their strong stimulation by MOp afferents, and the activation of the MOp during repeated movements, it is also tempting to speculate that activation of mIC SST interneurons may be involved in the antidepressant effects of exercise. Future studies into these and related areas may provide additional translational and mechanistic insight that could help to identify new treatment approaches.

In conclusion, the current studies identify an inhibitory circuit that guides adaptive responses to stress. Among inhibitory interneurons in the mIC, MOp innervation displays remarkable specificity to SST cells, and these interneurons inhibit projections to the BNST and affective behaviors. Together, this circuit is poised to integrate sensorimotor information with affective processing to buffer adaptive responses to stress.
