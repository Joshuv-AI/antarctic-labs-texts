Implementation of risk prediction and stratification approaches for ageing populations in Australian healthcare: a systematic review
Authors: Leanne Greene, Jacob Brain, Belinda Brown, Claire V Burley, Elissa Burton, Zhongyang Guan, Bronwyn Myers, Katherine Ride, Serena Sabatini, Mario Siervo, Abay Woday Tadesse, Eugene Yee Hing Tang, Blossom CM Stephan, Jennifer Dunne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDespite widespread development of prognostic risk stratification tools, their implementation in Australian healthcare remains poorly characterised. This review identified tools implemented for adults aged ≥40 years and examined their real-world use. Following PRISMA and SWiM guidelines, MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and CINAHL Plus were searched from inception to 20th February 2026. Eligible studies reported implementation of a risk stratification tool in Australian healthcare and health, functional, or implementation outcomes. The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool assessed study quality. Of 7115 records screened, 21 studies were included across primary care, hospitals, residential aged care, and community services. Interventions included electronic decision support, multifactorial assessments, structured clinical protocols, and AI-enabled screening. Studies that focused on early implementation outcomes (acceptability, feasibility, and adoption) rarely examined sustainability, penetration, and cost. Risk stratification tools are feasible and acceptable across Australian healthcare settings, but few are implemented as part of routine care.RegistrationPROSPERO—CRD420251028985.Keywords: Risk stratification, Risk assessment, Prediction model, Implementation science

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Risk stratification tools to identify individuals at risk of adverse health outcomes have proliferated globally across conditions such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, frailty, and falls. Despite this expansion, systematic reviews consistently show that most tools remain at the stages of development or statistical validation, with limited progression to routine clinical implementation and sparse evidence on feasibility, acceptability, or real-world impact from patient and clinician perspectives. In Australia, the implementation of such tools within a healthcare system characterised by universal Medicare coverage, shared federal–state governance, and a central role for general practice has remained poorly described.

Added value of this study

This systematic review is the first to comprehensively examine the implementation of risk stratification tools for mid-life and older adults (≥40 years) within the Australian healthcare context. Among 7115 records screened, we identified 21 studies reporting implementation across primary care, hospital, residential aged care, and community settings, demonstrating that most tools were feasible and acceptable but largely limited to pilot or early implementation phases with minimal evidence of sustained scale-up or long-term impact. Half of the included studies applied established implementation science frameworks, and mapping barriers and enablers to the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) highlighted challenges including IT integration, alert fatigue, resource constraints, and funding misalignment, alongside enablers such as workflow integration, leadership support, and stakeholder engagement.

Implications of all the available evidence

Combined with existing evidence, our findings indicate that the potential of risk stratification tools to improve care for ageing populations in Australia remains underutilised. Future implementation efforts should prioritise theory-informed approaches, meaningful integration into clinical workflows, and alignment with funding mechanisms and policy priorities, rather than the continued development of new prediction models. Greater emphasis is needed on evaluating existing tools for sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and patient-reported outcomes, and on addressing systemic barriers such as Medicare reimbursement gaps, IT interoperability, and inequities in rural and remote access to support equitable scale-up.

Introduction

The global population is ageing rapidly, with the number of people aged ≥65 years projected to double by 2050.1 This demographic shift is accompanied by rising rates of chronic diseases, multimorbidity, and functional decline, creating significant challenges for healthcare systems.2,3 Multimorbidity is a defining feature of age-related health burden, with many co-occurring chronic conditions sharing common origins in mid-life, making this a critical window for intervention.4,5 Early identification of at-risk individuals during mid-life creates the opportunity for timely risk management and modification to disease trajectories. Risk stratification tools, encompassing risk prediction models and risk assessment tools, have been developed to support early identification of several health conditions and outcomes (such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and falls).6, 7, 8 These tools enable healthcare providers to target interventions towards individuals at greatest risk of adverse health outcomes as they age.

Despite the growing number of risk stratification tools developed each year, most remain at the development and statistical validation stage, with limited evidence of whether they are feasible to implement in routine clinical care, or that their use holds benefits for patient outcomes.9, 10, 11 Research exploring patients and clinicians' perspectives on the acceptability, appropriateness, and utility of risk stratification tools in practice is also lacking, despite these stakeholder insights being critical for their successful adoption and implementation.12 Australia's healthcare system offers a distinctive context for this work. Universal healthcare coverage through Medicare enables general practice to routinely offer screening for chronic conditions and support for risk reduction, providing opportunities for risk stratification tool integration. In Australian primary care practice, routine screening most commonly takes the form of standardised physical (including cardiovascular risk) and mental health assessments that are offered to at-risk individuals, persons between 45 and 49 years of age, and annually to persons ≥75 years old, with these assessments funded through Medicare.13 These assessments are typically delivered by general practitioners, nurses, or allied health professionals. Risk stratification tools can be incorporated into the assessment via the electronic medical records as decision-support modules, automated alerts, or risk calculators, or administered as structured assessments outside the electronic record.14 Individuals identified at high risk are closely monitored via chronic disease management plans that may include referral to specialist medical or allied health services. However, the use of these tools is strongly influenced by workflow fit, time constraints, and interoperability with clinical software. Despite these system-level opportunities for routine health assessments, the extent to which risk stratification tools have been successfully integrated into Australian primary care, and the barriers and enablers that influence their implementation, remain poorly understood.

A systematic review is therefore necessary to identify potential context-specific barriers that risk stratification tools currently under development will need to consider when planning for implementation. As such, this review aimed to identify risk stratification tools targeted at mid-life and older adults (≥40 years) that have been implemented in the Australian healthcare system. Mid-life was operationalised as ≥40 years to reflect common life-course epidemiology definitions (approximately 40–65 years) and the decade during which cardiometabolic and vascular risk trajectories typically accelerate.15 This threshold aligns with national preventive guidelines recommending absolute cardiovascular risk assessment from mid-life and diabetes risk screening from age 40 and broadly maps onto Medicare-supported preventive activities such as the Heart Health Check.16 This review examined how risk stratification tools have been operationalised in practice, and synthesised findings on barriers and facilitators influencing their adoption, strategies to address these barriers, and the extent to which the use of these tools demonstrated patient benefit.

Methods

The review protocol was registered on PROSPERO CRD420251028985. The review was completed in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Guidelines17 and Synthesis without meta-analysis (SWiM) guidance18 (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive systematic search was undertaken, with guidance from an academic librarian, across MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and CINAHL Plus from inception to 10th April 2025, with an updated search on the 20th February 2026. The search strategy (Supplementary Table S3) incorporated terms including risk model, risk stratification, prognostic, midlife, older adults, and Australia, and was supplemented by backward citation chaining. No restrictions were applied regarding publication date.

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility criteria were structured according to the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study type)19 (Supplementary Table S4).

Population

Studies involving mid-life and older adults (≥40 years) were included. Studies with participants aged ≥18 years were eligible if health outcomes specific to those aged ≥40 years were reported.

Intervention

Eligible studies examined the implementation of risk stratification approaches, tools, or protocols used to identify or categorise individuals based on their level of health risk. For the purposes of this review, “implementation” was defined as any deliberate effort to integrate a tool into a real-world clinical setting, distinguished from purely evaluative work assessing tool accuracy without routine integration.

Implementation phase was categorised according to the EPIS framework (Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment): pre-implementation (exploration and preparation activities, including feasibility, acceptability, and pilot testing prior to rollout); implementation (active deployment within routine clinical practice); and sustainment (ongoing, embedded use at scale).

Comparison

No restriction was placed on the comparison group; studies with or without a comparator were eligible.

Outcomes

Studies reporting health and functional outcomes relevant to mid-life and older adults (such as morbidity, mortality, hospitalisation, and falls), as well as implementation outcomes (including acceptability, appropriateness, adoption, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, penetration, and sustainability) and factors that influence implementation outcomes (such as barriers and facilitators), were included. Implementation outcomes were considered as the primary outcomes of interest, consistent with how risk stratification tools are operationalised in practice. Clinical and patient health outcomes were treated as secondary outcomes, contextualising potential effectiveness.

Study type

Only peer-reviewed studies published in English were included, consistent with the Australian healthcare focus of the review. All study designs were eligible, including qualitative studies. Exclusions comprised non-human research, study protocols, reviews, editorials, commentaries, letters, conference abstracts without original data, and studies describing model development without implementation.

Effect modifiers

Potential effect modifiers included setting (primary care, hospital, residential aged care, community), type of risk stratification tool (decision support, multifactorial assessment, protocolised pathway, AI-enabled screening), and population characteristics (age, comorbidity, cultural background).

Study quality assessment

Critical appraisal was performed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT).20 The appraisal tool evaluates methodological quality using five core criteria specific to each of five study design categories: qualitative, quantitative randomised, quantitative non-randomised, quantitative descriptive, and mixed methods. Two reviewers (JB and LG) independently conducted the quality assessments, with any discrepancies in scoring addressed and resolved through critical discussion to achieve consensus. Consistent with MMAT guidance, overall summary scores were not calculated, as this approach is discouraged. Instead, the quality of the included studies is described through a concise presentation of ratings for each criterion. In accordance with the MMAT framework, studies identified as having lower methodological quality were retained in the review, as exclusion based on quality alone is not recommended.

Data extraction strategy

After removal of duplicates using the EndNote built-in function, all titles and abstracts were independently screened in Covidence by nine authors (JD, AT, JB, LG, CB, ZG, ET, SS, KR). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with conflicts resolved by two reviewers (JD, LG). Full-text screening was conducted independently by two authors (JD, LG), and disagreements were resolved by discussion.

Data were independently extracted by two authors (JD, LG) using Microsoft Excel and checked by a third author (JB). Extracted items included: study characteristics (setting, population, model domain, integration mode); implementation strategies (classified using the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change [ERIC]21); implementation outcomes (acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, penetration, sustainability, and cost, as defined by Proctor et al.22); patient and clinical outcomes; and whether implementation frameworks had been used and in what capacity (e.g. for evaluation, planning, guidance). In addition, data were mapped to the five domains of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)23: intervention characteristics, inner setting, outer setting, characteristics of individuals, and process to identify barriers and facilitators to implementation. Each domain captured specific data types: intervention characteristics (complexity, adaptability, evidence base); inner setting (leadership, workflow fit, resources); outer setting (funding structures, policy environment, community factors); characteristics of individuals (clinician attitudes, patient factors, training needs); and process (implementation strategies and engagement). Where data could be attributed to both a CFIR domain and a specific implementation outcome, it was recorded under both, with primary classification determined by the context in which it was reported.

Data synthesis and presentation

Given the heterogeneity of study designs, populations, settings, and outcome measures, a meta-analysis was not appropriate. Instead, a structured narrative synthesis approach was adopted. Interventions were categorised into three groups: (i) risk prediction models (formal algorithms generating individualised probabilistic risk estimates); (ii) screening tools (structured instruments identifying elevated risk without a quantified probability output); and (iii) protocolised pathways or decision-support systems (tools embedded within clinical workflows guiding management based on predefined criteria). Findings were organised by mapping extracted data to the CFIR domains, Proctor et al. implementation outcomes, and ERIC strategy classifications, providing a consistent conceptual structure across studies. Themes were derived inductively from the extracted data and iteratively refined through discussion between two authors (LG & JB), with any discrepancies resolved by consensus or referral to a third author (JD). Findings were integrated across studies by grouping thematically consistent evidence within each framework domain, noting patterns of convergence or divergence across settings, tool types, and population groups.

Results

The search yielded 7115 records, with 5221 titles and abstracts screened. After full-text review of 218 articles, 21 studies met inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Most were published after 2015 (90%), reflecting growing interest in implementing risk stratification tools in Australian healthcare.

Summary of findings from included papers that implemented risk stratification tools for adults aged ≥40 years in Australia.

Studies were conducted across six Australian states/territories, predominantly in Victoria (n = 7),25,26,29,31,34,40,43 and New South Wales (n = 5).27,28,33,37,44 Most were situated in primary care (n = 10)25, 26, 27, 28,30,32,38,40,43,44 or hospitals (n = 6),29,33, 34, 35, 36, 37 with fewer in mixed settings (n = 3),24,31,41 residential aged care (n = 1)39 or community (n = 1).42 Sample sizes ranged from 3925 to over 28,00033 participants. Of the 21 included studies, approximately one-third (n = 7) primarily targeted mid-life adults (typically 45–64 years),25,26,31,40, 41, 42,45 while just under half (n = 11) focused predominantly on older adults (≥65 years).27, 28, 29, 30,32,34,36, 37, 38, 39,44

Risk tools were implemented for cardiovascular disease,25,26,28,44 surgical and medical risk,33, 34, 35, 36 diabetic retinopathy,31,41 cancer risk (melanoma43 and colorectal cancer40), liver disease,24 multimorbidity,42 adverse drug reactions,30 passing a specialist on-road driving assessment,32 frailty,27 delirium,37 falls,38,39 and general hospital harm in patients with cognitive impairment.29

Across the 21 included studies, tools fell into three categories: formal risk prediction algorithms (n = 6), including perioperative mortality calculators, antithrombotic risk tools, and machine learning–based deterioration indices24,30,33,34,36,44; screening instruments (n = 10), including multifactorial falls and frailty assessments and AI-enabled retinal imaging and cardiac screening tools that identified elevated risk without a quantified probability output26, 27, 28, 29,31,38,39,41, 42, 43; and protocolised pathways or decision-support systems (n = 5), including electronic decision-support tools and structured clinical protocols embedded within primary care and emergency department workflows25,32,35,37,40 (Table 1). Most tools were implemented as electronic systems integrated within the electronic medical record with automated alerts, pop-ups, or decision-support modules,25,27,30,32, 33, 34, 35, 36,44 while others used structured, multifactorial assessment instruments completed outside the usual clinical workflow to identify risk factors before generating a risk score.24,29,37, 38, 39 A smaller number employed artificial intelligence–based systems, such as automated retinal imaging or melanoma risk algorithms, which produced immediate risk predictions at the point of care.26,31,41,43

Application of implementation frameworks

Eleven used a recognised implementation science framework. Frameworks included the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM),24,46 i-Integrated Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services (i-PARIHS),35,47 Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).23,27,31,37,40 Other studies described using the Medical Research Council complex intervention framework30,32,39,43,48 or intervention-specific frameworks (such as the Institute for a Broadband-Enabled Society telehealth framework).25

Barriers and facilitators to implementation were reported across most studies and can be understood within the domains of CFIR23 (Fig. 2). Intervention characteristics

Common barriers included complexity, information technology integration difficulties, and alert fatigue,24,32,33 alongside high costs of novel medications and technologies,44 and equipment breakdown in outreach settings, such as mobile retinal screening cameras.41 Conversely, tools perceived as evidence-based, user-friendly, or capable of providing real-time feedback were more readily adopted,26,31,41 with faster and less invasive processes highlighted as facilitators.41

Inner setting

Within the inner setting, time pressures, documentation requirements, and poor workflow fit limited uptake in some contexts,25,29 with unclear role delineation between nurses and doctors also identified as a barrier.38 By contrast, strong leadership support, visible clinical champions, and protected staff time facilitated implementation and helped embed interventions into routine practice,28,37 along with integration into existing clinical workflows35,40 and dedicated executive support and funding.27

Outer setting

Barriers in the outer setting related to structural and systemic factors, including lack of funding (such as the absence of Medicare item numbers), gaps in follow-up services, and inequities affecting rural and remote communities,29,30,41 as well as the vulnerability of equipment to geography and terrain in remote areas.41 Facilitators included strong community partnerships, culturally safe co-design processes, and outreach strategies that improved access for underserved populations.27,31,41

Individual characteristics

At the level of individuals, patients self-reported that they sometimes experienced anxiety, lack of motivation, or language barriers that hindered participation.27,43 In one study, clinicians expressed scepticism about the relevance of certain tools or uncertainty in interpreting scores.30 Conversely, patients often described reassurance and increased confidence, and clinicians reported enhanced knowledge and professional development following training, which increased their motivation to use tools,26,28,42 with engagement further strengthened through action research approaches that encouraged staff ownership of change.39

Implementation processes

Within the process domain, barriers included staff turnover, competing program priorities, and inconsistent leadership across sites,29,40 as well as attrition of project team members during implementation.40 Visible and committed leadership, clear project structures, and feedback loops, supported by multifaceted training and ongoing support, facilitated implementation.28,35,37

Strategies to overcome implementation barriers

Across studies, a wide range of implementation strategies were reported, most of which could be mapped to the ERIC taxonomy.21 The most common were training and educating through one-to-one instruction, group workshops, refresher sessions, or e-learning modules, with ongoing support provided via troubleshooting or check-ins.24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32,35,38, 39, 40,42,44 Strategies to develop stakeholder interrelationships included building collaborations across disciplines, involving opinion leaders, and fostering engagement through co-design or culturally safe community partnerships.26,28,29,31,39, 40, 41 Many studies also reported adapting interventions to context, such as tailoring content for local workflows, settings, or populations.24,26,30,32,35,37, 38, 39,41,43 Audit and feedback mechanisms were widely used to support fidelity of implementation, most often in the form of electronic dashboards, reminders, or compliance reports.25,29,31,33,35,36 The use of financial or non-financial incentives to encourage uptake was uncommon and varied across studies.

Implementation outcomes

Implementation outcomes were variably reported across the included studies. Adoption was the most frequently assessed, either through explicitly reported adoption as an implementation outcome,34,35,37,40,44 or through evidence of routine use, workflow integration, or pilot uptake.24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29,32,38,39 Feasibility was formally assessed in a small number of studies, all of which found interventions to be deliverable in routine clinical practice without major disruption.26,28,31,41,43 Several others provided indirect evidence of feasibility, describing tools as workable within existing general practice, community, or hospital workflows despite some challenges.24,27,40 Acceptability was also widely reported and typically positive. Clinicians valued structured decision support and the standardisation of risk stratification tools,24,28,34 while patients frequently expressed reassurance, confidence, and satisfaction with tools that supported their care.27,43 Appropriateness was demonstrated when interventions were successfully integrated into existing workflows and aligned with policy or funding priorities.27, 28, 29,37,41

Fidelity was less commonly measured but was generally high when assessed, with studies reporting consistent adherence to standardised protocols and feedback systems.27,35,39,40 Evidence on penetration and sustainability was rarely evaluated. Some studies described broad reach across practices, hospitals, or communities, including melanoma risk stratification and AI-based retinal screening initiatives.26,31,41,43 Others remained site-specific pilots with limited scale-up.34,40,44 Likewise, cost outcomes were rarely assessed, with a small number of studies reporting efficiency gains, including reduced wait times, lower per-patient costs, and avoided admissions.33,35,41

Critical appraisal

All studies met the MMAT screening criteria, confirming their suitability for appraisal (Supplementary Table S6). Qualitative studies demonstrated consistently high methodological quality, with clear research aims, appropriate data collection, and coherent interpretation. Quantitative studies, including both randomised and non-randomised designs, generally used appropriate methods but often lacked clarity in randomisation, confounder control, and blinding procedures. The descriptive quantitative studies were suitable for their aims, though several provided limited information on sampling and response rates. Mixed methods studies were typically strong in their individual qualitative and quantitative components; however, variation was noted in how well these strands were integrated and in the justification for using a mixed methods design.

Discussion

This review provides the first systematic review of the implementation of risk stratification tools for mid-life and older adults within the Australian healthcare context. The results highlight that interest in this area has grown markedly over the past decade, in parallel with global patterns reflecting the accelerated development of risk stratification tools.49 Despite this momentum, our review identified only 21 models or tools that had progressed from model validation to considering implementation within the Australian healthcare system. Most of these risk stratification tools had not progressed past exploring and preparing for implementation, with few models fully embedded into routine clinical practice and limited evaluation of longer-term implementation outcomes. These findings suggest a limited evidence base to support full and sustained implementation. As such, our findings suggest that rather than prioritising the creation of additional clinical prediction models, future efforts should focus on evaluating the real-world clinical impact of existing risk stratification tools and their implementation outcomes to avoid research waste and strengthen translation into practice.49, 50, 51

The diversity of implementation contexts highlights the adaptability of risk stratification approaches across the health system. Models were applied in general practice, hospitals, emergency departments, residential aged care, and community programs, suggesting that risk stratification can be flexibly integrated where it aligns with clinical priorities. Tools ranged from electronic decision-support systems embedded into medical records to structured assessment instruments and AI-based diagnostic technologies. The included studies broadly align with a tiered risk stratification approach: (i) preventive assessment in generally healthy mid-life adults, often implemented in primary care using cardiovascular or chronic disease risk algorithms; (ii) identification of individuals at elevated risk due to emerging multimorbidity or functional decline, including frailty, falls, or medication-related risk screening; and (iii) clinical subgroups within acute or specialist care, where tools support perioperative risk assessment, deterioration monitoring, or targeted disease screening. Many tools used in practice correspond with nationally recommended models (e.g., cardiovascular risk calculators and frailty screening instruments), suggesting that implementation efforts may benefit from prioritising integration and validation of existing evidence-based models rather than developing new prediction tools. These findings also echo broader implementation challenges that innovations can sometimes diffuse unevenly across systems, with context strongly shaping uptake.45

While spanning diverse clinical domains, these tools share a common purpose: identifying individuals at elevated risk of conditions that are major contributors to disability, functional decline, and hospitalisation among Australia's ageing population, thus providing a coherent rationale for examining their implementation collectively, particularly in the context of rising multimorbidity. Nonetheless, this heterogeneity also presents challenges. When risk stratification tools differ in their data requirements, scoring systems, and underlying infrastructure, integrating them across health settings or enabling consistent data sharing becomes more difficult. These demands may be particularly difficult to meet in rural and remote communities where workforce, technology, and resource constraints are more acute, raising questions about equitable access to risk-based care.52,53

While all of the studies focused on testing or integrating these tools into healthcare settings, half drew on established implementation frameworks such as CFIR,23 RE-AIM,46 and i-PARIHS.47 Implementation without a guiding framework risks ad hoc strategy selection and unclear alignment between barriers and solutions. Without theoretical grounding, interventions are less likely to be sustained beyond the pilot phase, and findings are harder to adapt or transfer across Australia's geographically and structurally diverse healthcare settings. Studies that explicitly employed implementation frameworks tended to report a broader range of implementation outcomes, greater contextual detail, and a clearer rationale for the selection of implementation strategies, whereas those without frameworks tended to focus on narrow feasibility or clinical outcomes. Even so, many studies used recognised strategies such as training, audit and feedback, and contextual tailoring. This uneven application of frameworks has also been noted internationally.15,54, 55, 56, 57 Our findings highlight an opportunity to strengthen the implementation of risk stratification tools by more explicitly drawing on implementation science theories, frameworks, and methods which can support more systematic, data-informed decisions.50

Implementation outcomes were generally positive, particularly in terms of acceptability, feasibility, and adoption. Clinicians valued structured tools that provided standardisation and decision support,58 while patients expressed reassurance and satisfaction with risk stratification processes.27,43 These findings align with existing evidence that risk tools can enhance healthcare delivery. However, most evaluations emphasised clinician perceptions of acceptability, with limited attention to service-user perspectives, a gap also highlighted in prior research.59 There was much less evidence on sustainability, penetration, and cost-effectiveness, or effectiveness, with only a small number of studies reporting efficiency gains such as reduced admissions or shorter wait times. While follow-up durations ranged from several weeks to 6–12 months in primary care pilots and feasibility trials, they were typically less than 6 months and rarely extended beyond one year. The limited reporting of sustainability, penetration, and long-term patient outcomes likely reflects the predominance of pilot and feasibility-stage studies, and the absence of robust economic evaluation and long-term follow-up makes it difficult to argue that these interventions deliver value beyond their pilot phase.60 However, it is important to distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of no effect, consequently conclusions regarding sustained effectiveness or system-level impact remain premature.

The barriers and facilitators reported by the included studies mapped closely to the CFIR domains. At the intervention level, complexity, integration challenges, and alert fatigue were recurrent obstacles, echoing global concerns around clinical decision-support tools.61 Conversely, interventions that were user-friendly, evidence-based, and capable of providing real-time feedback were more readily adopted. Within the CFIR inner setting domain, studies identified organisational factors such as leadership support, availability of protected staff time, and integration into existing workflows as critical enablers. Studies also identified factors within the outer setting that influenced implementation, including the absence of dedicated funding structures, Medicare barriers (e.g., out-of-pocket costs for patients or the absence of relevant Medicare item numbers to support reimbursement), and inequities in rural service provision constrained scalability. Individual-level factors also shaped outcomes, with some patients reporting anxiety or language barriers, while clinicians described uncertainty in interpreting scores. However, training, professional development, and supportive feedback mechanisms appeared to enhance both motivation and confidence, consistent with broader evidence on behaviour change in healthcare.62

Implications for practice and policy

While risk stratification tools hold significant promise for strengthening health service delivery in Australia, their implementation will require coordinated action across multiple levels of the health system to address the barriers identified by this review. Review findings suggest that Primary Health Networks and health services could address provider barriers related to motivation, knowledge and confidence through implementing strategies such as workforce training and cross-site learning collaboratives. At the organisational level, health services and software developers could address inner setting barriers related to interoperability and workflow fit by co-designing risk stratification tools for integration within existing electronic medical record systems and data and digital governance frameworks and infrastructure, with the aim of streamlining clinical workflows rather than creating additional burden through stand-alone applications. At the policy level, the absence of dedicated Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS) reimbursement items for validated risk stratification activities represents a key structural barrier to equitable scale-up beyond pilot implementation. Addressing this barrier will require evidence of the clinical, economic, and health system benefits of these tools that professional associations (Heart Foundation Australia and Dementia Australia), health service providers, and Australian health translation networks can use to lobby and advocate for reimbursement reform.

Beyond these technical and systemic considerations, equity must be central to how risk stratification tools are designed, validated, and deployed in the Australian context. This is particularly important given persistent disparities in access to, quality of and outcomes of healthcare among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and rural and remote communities.52,53,63 Although several studies included Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander participants or were conducted within services that include Indigenous Australians, there was inconsistent reporting on whether tools were co-designed with communities, incorporated cultural safety principles, or respected community governance, a gap that warrants attention in future implementation work.64 Risk stratification tools that are not co-designed with communities or embedded within culturally safe models of care may risk reinforcing existing inequities in communities with distinct health needs and cultural contexts. For AI-enabled tools specifically, additional implementation challenges arise around the interpretability of algorithmic outputs, as clinicians and communities may have limited ability to interrogate or contest “black box” risk scores,65 as well as data sovereignty, privacy governance, and the need for representative training data that reflects the diversity of Australian populations.66 Implementation in rural and remote settings also revealed structural barriers related to digital infrastructure, workforce shortages, and equipment maintenance, especially for AI-enabled screening technologies requiring reliable connectivity or specialised hardware. While digital health innovations, such as telehealth-supported consultations, remote monitoring, mobile AI screening units, and patient-completed digital assessments, offer opportunities to overcome geographic barriers, equitable scale-up requires investment in connectivity, digital hardware and workforce capability.67 A synthesis of the key system-level prerequisites for implementation and sustainment is presented in Box 1.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. We conducted a comprehensive, systematic search across five major databases, with backward citation chaining to maximise study capture. Our focus on the Australian healthcare context provides jurisdiction-specific insights that are critical for local implementation planning, given the unique configuration of Medicare, federal-state responsibilities, and the central role of general practice. Australia's system is characterised by a blended federal–state governance structure, universal health coverage through Medicare, and a strong reliance on general practice as the primary point of care. We assessed study quality using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT), providing a transparent quality appraisal of included models. Finally, our review explicitly examined implementation processes, outcomes, barriers, and facilitators, areas often overlooked in systematic reviews of prediction models.

However, most of the included studies were not designed as implementation science research. Our application of implementation frameworks was retrospective and may not fully reflect study intent. Implementation outcome measurement was inconsistent, and synthesis relied on heterogeneous data sources of variable quality. The predominance of pilot and feasibility studies limits our understanding of sustained implementation and scale-up. Publication bias may also have inflated positive findings, and patient and consumer perspectives were underrepresented in the included studies, limiting our ability to comprehensively assess acceptability from the end-user viewpoint. While the review was limited to English-language studies, we considered this appropriate given the Australian healthcare focus and no studies were excluded on the basis of language.

Conclusion

Overall, while risk stratification approaches are feasible, acceptable, and adaptable within the Australian healthcare system, their potential remains underutilised. Future implementation efforts should prioritise embedding tools into workflows, aligning with policy and funding structures, and ensuring sustainability and equity of access. Co-design approaches that meaningfully involve clinicians, patients (including individuals from rural and remote and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities), and policymakers will be essential to ensure tools are contextually relevant, culturally safe, and sustainable. Rigorous evaluation of cost-effectiveness, patient-reported outcomes, and long-term impacts on service use will be critical. Without such evidence, the promise of risk stratification to improve care and reduce avoidable healthcare burden will remain only partially fulfilled.
