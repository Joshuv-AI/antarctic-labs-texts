Experimental evaluation of natural material mixtures for soil resistivity reduction
Authors: Hugo Martínez Ángeles, José Gabriel Ríos Moreno, César Augusto Navarro Rubio, Margarita Guadalupe García Barajas, José Luis Reyes Araiza, Roberto Valentín Carrillo-Serrano, Mario Trejo Perea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil electrical resistivity is influenced by multiple factors, including temperature, geography, soil chemical composition, precipitation, and particularly moisture content, which exerts the most significant influence. Nevertheless, combining natural materials in varying proportions can produce distinctive physicochemical and electrical properties, relevant for the development of low-resistivity materials in engineering applications that require soil resistivity reduction. In this study, 15 mixtures were formulated using four natural materials with contrasting physicochemical characteristics. Experimental evaluations included electrical resistivity as a function of moisture content (10%-100%), pH, and electrochemical activity (ORP), the latter assessed through cyclic voltammetry. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was also applied to identify patterns among the measured variables. The results show a progressive decrease in resistivity as moisture content increases. Likewise, the relationship between pH and conductivity displayed a non-linear behavior, with the lowest resistivity occurring within the pH range of 8.50–9.50. Mixtures exhibiting higher electrochemical activity were also the most conductive. Furthermore, PCA enabled the classification of mixtures according to their electrical behavior. These findings demonstrate that an integrated approach—combining physicochemical, electrical, and electrochemical analyses—is essential for the rational design of functional natural materials intended to serve as soil resistivity reducers.Keywords: Electrical resistivity, Experimental evaluation, Natural mixtures, Physicochemical parameters, Soil resistivity reductionSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Soil electrical resistivity is influenced by multiple variables such as temperature, geography, precipitation, and soil chemical composition1. In addition, soil moisture content has a non-linear effect on resistivity, due to changes in ionic conductivity and the connectivity of water-filled pores2. Thus, soil electrical resistivity can be defined as a measure of the resistance offered by a soil layer to the flow of electrical current passing through it3.

Estimating soil electrical resistivity is a fundamental parameter in several branches of engineering, including geotechnics and geophysics, and is also of importance in other fields such as agriculture4. In electrical engineering, one of the most critical aspects during the installation of grounding systems is the surrounding soil environment and composition5. Certain soils, such as those composed mainly of rock, may exhibit resistivity values of up to 50,000 Ohm-meter (Ωm)6, resulting in a considerable increase in soil resistivity. Furthermore, the scientific literature reports high resistivity values for various types of soils and terrains7, which present extremely elevated values8.

Historically, the use of low-resistivity materials (ground enhancement materials, GEMs) derived from chemical agents has addressed the problem of high soil resistivity. Although such materials are effective in reducing soil resistivity, their application is limited due to the corrosion they may cause in ecosystems9. Common chemical materials used include sodium chloride (NaCl) and magnesium carbonate (MgCO3)10,11. Nevertheless, the appropriate selection of GEMs is essential to ensure soil resistivity reduction12.

GEMs are not exclusively chemical in origin; they can also be classified as traditional or recent alternatives. Accordingly, the literature suggests organizing GEMs into three categories based on their composition and nature: natural, chemical, and waste-derived13. Although chemical and waste-based GEMs have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing soil resistivity, studies such as those reported in14–17 have shown that natural materials can also achieve significant reductions.

A clear example of a natural GEM is bentonite18, which exhibits strong hygroscopic properties, enabling moisture retention and consequently reducing resistivity when applied to soils. Thus, GEMs have found widespread use as enhancers of soil impedance capacity. However, for a GEM to be effective, it must meet specific criteria, such as high moisture retention capacity19 and low deterioration over time20.

Although some studies have evaluated the resistivity of GEMs under variable moisture conditions21, few have simultaneously considered the influence of hydrogen potential (pH) and oxidation–reduction potential (ORP) in multicomponent mixtures. Therefore, the design and development of GEM mixtures prepared from natural materials with specific electrochemical properties may represent a viable strategy for advancing sustainable technologies22. Potential applications include soil sensors23, eco-friendly construction materials24, environmental monitoring platforms25, grounding system design5, soil improvement materials13, and other areas in which soil resistivity reduction is essential.

Based on the above, the objective of this study is to evaluate the influence of physicochemical parameters—namely moisture content, pH, and ORP—on the electrical resistivity of natural mixtures designed as potential soil enhancement materials. To this end, 15 representative mixtures were formulated from four natural materials13 with contrasting physicochemical properties: sodium bentonite, bituminous coal (marine origin), amorphous graphite, and natural ferric oxide, following a Simplex Central Centroid Design (SCCD). Each mixture was tested for electrical resistivity under controlled moisture content conditions (10%–100%), and further characterized for pH and ORP under standard conditions, to analyze the relationships between these variables and their potential applicability as soil resistivity-reducing materials in high-resistivity soils. The results are expected to establish both quantitative and qualitative relationships that will be useful for the rational design of materials with specific electrochemical functions.

On the other hand, unlike conventional low-resistivity agents such as sodium chloride (NaCl), which reduce soil resistivity primarily through high ionic solubility but may promote corrosion and long-term environmental degradation, the present study proposes the rational design of natural-material mixtures with controlled physicochemical and electrochemical behavior. The novelty of this work lies in the integrated evaluation of moisture-dependent resistivity, pH, and cyclic voltammetry response, combined with multivariate statistical analysis to identify optimized formulations with improved functional stability and environmental compatibility.

From Table 1, it can be observed that sodium bentonite, rich in SiO2 and Al2O3, is notable for its high swelling capacity, a property that promotes water retention and reduces resistivity27. Experimental design and mixtures preparations

To evaluate the combined effects of the four selected materials13 on electrical resistivity, pH, and ORP, a Simplex Centroid Central Design (SCCD) was employed, resulting in 15 different formulations. This design enables exploration of the compositional space of four components while maintaining the total proportion at 100% (weight/weight). The mixtures include all vertices (100% of a single material), edge centers (50/50 combinations), face-centered points, and the centroid.

This approach is particularly useful for studying mixture optimization, as it allows assessment of the interactions among components and their effects on the response variables. Table 2 presents the design of the studied mixtures, based on the SCCD methodology.

The preparation of the mixtures was standardized to ensure reproducibility and homogeneity across all mixtures. Each component was first oven-dried at 105 °C for 24 h to remove residual moisture, and subsequently sieved through a − 200 mesh (75 μm) to minimize particle size differences. The specified mass proportions were weighed using an analytical balance (± 0.01 g). Mixing was carried out mechanically in a rotary drum mixer at 60 rpm for 15 min, followed by manual stirring to break up potential agglomerates. The sequence of addition was fixed: sodium bentonite first, followed by bituminous coal and amorphous graphite, and finally ferric oxide, in order to prevent segregation. To further ensure uniformity, submixtures were taken from three different positions in each batch, and their resistivity was measured independently. This procedure guaranteed that the prepared mixtures were as homogeneous and representative as possible for the experimental tests.

All experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, with room temperature maintained between 22 °C and 25 °C. Temperature was monitored at the beginning and end of each measurement session to ensure stability. This control minimized thermal effects on resistivity and electrochemical responses, allowing observed variations to be attributed to the intrinsic properties of the mixtures.

Measurement of electrical resistivity

For each of the percentage combinations shown in Table 2, the corresponding mixture was prepared and the response variable (resistivity) was evaluated. The method used to measure resistivity followed the recommendations of ASTM G57-20, “Standard Test Method for Measurement of Soil Resistivity Using the Wenner Four-Electrode Method”. This procedure consists of placing four electrodes or metallic plates in a straight line connected to a soil box, where the two outer electrodes are used to inject current (C1 and C2), and the two inner electrodes are used to measure voltage (P1 and P2).

The resulting measurement represents the average resistance of a hemispherical volume of soil with a radius equal to the electrode spacing30. Figure 1 shows the schematic connection between the soil box, the two copper plates at the ends, the two central electrodes placed in the middle of the box, the four-terminal resistance meter (telurometer), and the corresponding electrical connections.

Typical four-electrode soil box and its connections.

To saturate the mixtures in amounts ranging from 10 to 100%, distilled water grade IV, as specified in ASTM D119331, was used. During the filling of the box with the saturated material, proper compaction of the mixture was ensured to eliminate voids, and this procedure was continued until the box was completely filled.

Finally, the results were recorded for each mixture studied and for each level of moisture incorporated into the mixtures.

Determination of pH in the mixtures

The pH measurement (Fig. 3) was performed using a potentiometric method with a properly calibrated pH meter. This method is based on determining the electrical potential generated by a glass electrode sensitive to hydrogen ion (H⁺) activity in solution, compared with a constant-potential reference electrode, typically calomel or silver/silver chloride.

pH measurement for each of the 15 formulations.

Before each series of measurements, the instrument was calibrated using standard buffer solutions of known pH (typically pH 4.00, 7.00, and 10.00) at room temperature (25 °C). The electrode was then immersed in the mixture, ensuring continuous stirring and avoiding bubble formation on the electrode bulb, which could interfere with the reading. Measurements were performed in triplicate, and the average value was recorded as the pH of the mixture. This procedure provides a precise quantitative determination of pH, with a typical resolution of ± 0.01 units.

Since moisture content directly affects pH measurements in soils and mixtures, standardization of water content is a recommended practice to minimize bias and obtain consistent, comparable results. Therefore, in this study, pH measurements were standardized at a single moisture content of 20%32. This decision allowed for more consistent and comparable values across the different mixtures.

Determination of redox potential in the mixtures

To complement the physicochemical and electrical characterization of the mixtures, cyclic voltammetry was applied to evaluate the electrochemical behavior of each of the 15 formulations under controlled conditions. This technique allows for the observation of the ORP response of the materials under an applied potential sweep, revealing processes such as adsorption, electron transfer, and surface charge storage capacity. In this study, a two-electrode system was employed, consisting of a working electrode and a reference electrode (Fig. 4).

Cyclic voltammetry technique.

Measurements were performed using a potentiostat controlled via software, within a defined potential range and at predefined rates. Prior to the measurements, the electrolyte solution was deoxygenated by purging with an inert gas (N2 or Ar) for at least 10 min to minimize interference from dissolved oxygen reduction.

Principal component analysis

To integrate and analyze the combined influence of the physicochemical and electrical variables evaluated in the formulated mixtures, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied. This technique reduced the dimensionality of the dataset, facilitating the visualization of clustering patterns and the identification of variables that significantly contributed to the variability observed among the mixtures.

For the PCA, the quantitative variables considered included: the percentages of the four components in each mixture (sodium bentonite, amorphous graphite, bituminous coal, and natural ferric oxide), the pH values measured under standard conditions, and the average electrical resistivity obtained across the evaluated moisture content range.

The data were normalized using Z-score standardization (zero mean and unit standard deviation) to ensure comparability among variables with different scales. Subsequently, the principal component method based on singular value decomposition (SVD) of the covariance matrix was applied, and the first two principal components were selected for two-dimensional graphical representation.

Results and discussion

This relationship was not linear and displayed distinctive characteristics depending on the composition of each mixture. It is worth noting that each measurement was validated through control repetitions, with variations of less than 5% being observed.

Effect of moisture content of mixture on resistivity.

Based on the data in Table 3, mixtures M1, M3, and M6—composed exclusively or in equal proportions of sodium bentonite and ferric oxide, both materials with low conductivity under dry conditions—exhibited the highest resistivity values at low moisture content levels, reaching maxima of 28,900 Ωm (M1) and 39,980 Ωm (M3) at 10% w. However, these mixtures also showed the greatest reductions in resistivity as moisture content increased, with decreases of more than 98% in some cases.

In contrast, mixtures M2, M4, and M9, which contained high or exclusive amounts of bituminous coal (marine origin) and amorphous graphite—materials with inherently conductive behavior—displayed low resistivity values even at minimal moisture content levels (e.g., M2: 45 Ωm at 10% w; M9: 15 Ωm at 10% w), indicating stable electronic conduction pathways33. For M4 (100% amorphous graphite), the initial decrease in resistivity at low moisture content is associated with improved ionic transport. At higher moisture levels, excess water may slightly increase interparticle spacing and weaken electronic contact between graphite particles, causing a moderate increase in resistivity. This behavior is consistent with percolation effects in conductive composites. Overall, these mixtures show lower moisture dependence than bentonite-rich systems, which may be advantageous in environments with variable water content34.

It can be observed that mixtures, such as M5 (50% bentonite, 50% bituminous coal) and M7 (50% bentonite, 50% graphite), exhibited transitional behaviors. For example, M5 started with a resistivity of 84 Ωm at 10% w but increased to nearly 400 Ωm at 100% w, indicating an inversion of the trend. This may be attributable to phase redistribution or the collapse of microstructures that inhibit efficient conduction at high moisture content levels35.

A particularly atypical case was M8 (50% bituminous coal, 50% ferric oxide), which exhibited a sustained increase in resistivity as moisture content increased, from 33 Ωm to 4,020 Ωm. This phenomenon may be related to the progressive obstruction of conductive pathways or to a loss of effective contact between conductive particles36, a process that warrants further investigation. Another possible explanation is the repulsion of ions remaining on particle surfaces, making the current path more tortuous or forcing ions apart, thereby increasing resistivity37.

Other ternary and quaternary mixtures (M11–M15), with balanced proportions of three or four materials, showed more stable behaviors. In particular, M15 (25% of each component) exhibited resistivity values between 71 Ωm and 718 Ωm, suggesting a synergistic interaction among the materials that moderates the response to changes in moisture content.

Therefore, the graphical analysis in Fig. 6 demonstrates that moisture content is a determining factor in the resistivity of the analyzed mixtures, with a non-linear effect that varies across formulations. This behavior should be taken into account when selecting materials for applications in which electrical flow control or insulation is critical under variable environmental conditions.

Qualitative electrochemical behavior of the mixtures evaluated by cyclic voltammetry

Figure 7 shows the superimposed voltammetry curves of the 15 formulated mixtures, obtained by measuring the electric current as a function of the applied voltage. These curves enable direct comparison of the intensity of ORP processes, the presence of capacitive or resistive behavior, and the influence of dominant components on the electrochemical activity of the system38.

Cyclic voltammetry curves of the 15 formulated mixtures.

It is important to note that although formal statistical replications were not conducted for all tests, each cyclic voltammetry measurement was validated through consecutive control repetitions. These repetitions demonstrated high consistency, with variations of less than 5% relative to the initial value. This low dispersion confirms the stability of the experimental system and supports confidence in the reproducibility of the reported data, even in the absence of a broader statistical analysis.

From Fig. 7, it can be observed that mixtures dominated by bituminous coal, particularly M9, exhibit a nearly linear current–potential relationship, characteristic of predominantly ohmic behavior with low polarization and efficient electronic transport. Although M2 also shows a quasi-linear trend, slight deviations from linearity are present, suggesting the coexistence of resistive and capacitive contributions, possibly associated with structural heterogeneity or localized surface charge effects. In contrast, mixtures such as M3 and M4, composed exclusively of ferric oxide and amorphous graphite, respectively, display lower current intensities and more pronounced deviations from linearity. This behavior may be attributed to limited electronic connectivity, semiconducting characteristics, or a reduced density of electroactive sites13.

Additionally, distinct cathodic features observed around − 0.75 V in Figs. 7, 8, 9 and 10 (notably in M2, M4, and M9) are attributed to surface redox processes associated with carbonaceous phases. These features were reproducible in consecutive control measurements, with variations below 5%, confirming the stability of the electrochemical response. Their selective presence suggests that the electrochemical behavior is strongly influenced by conductive phase connectivity and surface chemistry. Furthermore, the lower pH values observed in these mixtures (Fig. 11) may facilitate proton-mediated charge-transfer processes, enhancing localized redox activity within this potential range39.

Quantitative summary of cyclic voltammetry parameters (M1–M15).

The quantitative analysis of cyclic voltammetry provides key insights into the electrochemical performance of the mixtures. The peak currents (Ip, a and Ip, c) reflect the intensity of charge transfer, while the associated potentials (Ep, a and Ep, c) indicate the voltages at which oxidation and reduction processes occur42. The difference between these potentials (ΔEp) enables evaluation of reaction efficiency and reversibility, with smaller values being more favorable.

Furthermore, the enclosed area of the curve (∫IdV) is related to the materials charge storage and transport capacity: a larger area indicates greater ability to store or transfer charge43. This area was transformed into electric charge (Q, in mC) by dividing it by the scan rate of 20 millivolts per second (mV/s).

Taken together, these parameters allow for a more precise comparison of the behavior of each mixture and its relationship to electrical performance.

In addition, Table 4 shows that mixtures with higher carbon content (M2, M5, M8, M9) exhibited higher peak currents (Ip, a and Ip, c) and lower ΔEp values, suggesting a more efficient electrochemical response42. In contrast, formulations dominated by bentonite or iron oxide (M1, M3, M6, M10) displayed lower currents and greater peak separations, indicating slower kinetics and reduced reversibility. These trends are consistent with the electrical resistivity results, confirming that the electrochemical activity of the mixtures is closely related to their electrical conductivity capacity43.

Although electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was not conducted in this study, future work may incorporate EIS and equivalent circuit modeling to further decompose resistive and capacitive contributions, thereby strengthening the link between electrochemical responses and resistivity.

Relationship between electrochemical behavior and electrical resistivity

Figures 8, 9 and 10 illustrate the relationship between electrochemical behavior and electrical resistivity for selected average resistivity intervals presented in Table 4, indicating the corresponding mixture for each analyzed relationship (e.g., M2, M4, M5, etc.) through its voltammetry curves.

Voltammetry curves for mixtures with low electrical resistivity (< 500 Ωm)

Figure 8 shows the voltammetry curves of mixtures with an average electrical resistivity below 500 Ωm. These mixtures exhibit highly conductive behavior, with broad and well-defined current profiles characteristic of materials with good electronic and ionic mobility43. The curves display widened and continuous profiles, with elevated current intensities, even at the extremes of the voltage sweep.

From Fig. 8, it can be observed that mixtures M13 (Ip, a = 0.038 mA) and M15 (Ip, a = 0.033 mA), with more balanced compositions, present smooth yet active responses, with indications of multiple ORP processes or capacitive–resistive transitions. In the case of M4 (Ip, a ≈ 0.000 mA), the current is more modest but stable, reflecting slightly capacitive behavior. Mixture M12 (Ip, a = 0.044 mA) stands out for exhibiting a smooth and symmetrical curve, representing a favorable balance between conductive phases. In contrast, mixtures such as M2 (Ip, a = 0.196 mA) and M9 (Ip, a = 0.117 mA) display linear resistive behavior, indicating excellent connectivity between conductive particles and strong electronic mobility44.

Voltammetry curves for mixtures with medium electrical resistivity (500–2000 Ωm)

Figure 9 presents the voltammetry curves of mixtures with resistivities between 500 and 2000 Ωm. In this group, the curves show intermediate electrochemical activity, with less defined shapes or mild current peaks, reflecting a combination of conductive and insulating components. The profiles display weaker ORP peaks or flatter current regions, indicating a partial or interrupted electrochemical response44.

These mixtures exhibit intermediate electrochemical activity, with anodic peak currents (Ip, a) ranging from 0.020 to 0.050 mA and integrated areas (Q) of approximately 1–3 mC.

From Fig. 9, it can be observed that M6 (Ip, a = 0.024 mA) exhibits a relatively active curve, though with lower slope and intensity compared to M2 or M9 (Ip, a > 0.100 mA). M10 (Ip, a = 0.013 mA) displays a sigmoidal shape, while M11 (Ip, a = 0.035 mA) and M14 (Ip, a = 0.039 mA) show mixed responses—initially capacitive with transitions toward more resistive behavior. Increased signal noise or fluctuations were also observed, possibly due to internal heterogeneity of the mixtures or uneven distribution of active materials44.

Voltammetry curves for mixtures with high electrical resistivity (> 2000 Ωm)

Figure 10 shows the voltammetry curves of mixtures with resistivities above 2000 Ωm, characterized by very low currents (< 0.010 mA) and flat curves, typical of materials with limited electrical conductivity45. The weak or inert signals reflect restricted electrochemical activity, associated with predominantly insulating materials such as sodium bentonite and ferric oxide13. The curves in this group exhibit extremely low current intensities, in some cases barely detectable, indicating little to no electrochemical activity45.

From Fig. 10, it can be seen that M1 (Ip, a = 0.061 mA) and M3 (Ip, a = 0.008 mA) are the clearest examples of highly resistive materials, with nearly dielectric behavior, as their internal structure prevents electron mobility and the formation of effective conductive pathways43. M8 (Ip, a = 0.014 mA) reflects a combination of insulating effects, resulting in an almost flat curve.

In particular, M8—where bituminous coal acts as a conductive and highly porous component, while ferric oxide is predominantly insulating and may form compact structures upon hydration13—shows a distorted curve with limited response, suggesting structural connectivity issues or blockage of active sites by passive phases44.

Analysis of average pH values

To characterize the chemical environment of the mixtures and its potential influence on charge transport and electrical resistivity, the pH of each of the 15 formulations was measured. To ensure comparability across mixtures and avoid bias from moisture content variations, pH measurements were standardized at a single moisture content (20%)46, previously defined as representative of intermediate laboratory working conditions. This decision enabled more consistent and comparable values among the different mixtures.

Figure 11 presents the pH values obtained, which span a broad range from slightly acidic conditions (pH 5.30) to strongly basic conditions (pH 9.83), depending on the nature and proportion of the incorporated materials.

From Fig. 11, it can be observed that the pH values measured in the 15 formulated mixtures covered a wide range, reflecting the chemical diversity of the materials used. Mixtures M1, M5–M7, M11–M13, and M15, all with high sodium bentonite content, exhibited pH values above 9.00. This may be due to the alkaline nature of bentonite, which releases Na⁺ ions upon hydration, thereby increasing pH32.

In contrast, mixtures with amorphous graphite (M4) and bituminous coal (M9) as predominant components showed the lowest pH values (5.30 and 6.72, respectively), which could be attributed to the surface acidity of these materials as well as partial oxidation of associated organic or mineral compounds47. Ternary and quaternary mixtures (e.g., M10, M14, M15) displayed intermediate pH values, generally between 8.0 and 9.0, reflecting a balance between acidic and basic phases.

Thus, pH may significantly influence ionic mobility and medium conductivity, as it affects the availability of charged species in solution48. In the context of this study, mixtures with pH values close to neutrality (7.00–8.00) may provide a favorable compromise between physicochemical stability and low electrical resistivity, particularly when compatibility with neutral soil or water systems is desired.

Relationship between pH and average electrical resistivity

To explore the influence of the chemical environment on the electrical properties of the natural mixtures, the potential correlation between pH and average electrical resistivity was analyzed (Fig. 12). Reference pH values were determined at a standardized moisture content of 20%46. This fixed level was selected to ensure that comparisons with average resistivity reflected the influence of pH rather than confounding effects of variations in water content.

Relationship between pH and average electrical resistivity.

The relationship between pH and average resistivity is relevant because pH can affect ionic mobility, the solubility of species in the porous medium, and the surface chemical structure of the components—all key factors in determining electrical conduction in partially saturated media49.

From Fig. 12, the relationship between the pH of each mixture and its average resistivity can be observed. Statistical analysis revealed a very weak and non-significant positive linear correlation (r = + 0.17; p = 0.55; R² = 0.03), indicating that pH alone does not determine the overall electrical response of the mixtures. However, some relevant trends were observed: the most conductive mixtures, such as M2, M4, and M9, were located within acidic to neutral pH intervals (5.30–7.30), while several mixtures with intermediate resistivity clustered in the alkaline range of 9.00–9.50. In contrast, highly resistive mixtures such as M1 and M3 were also found within this alkaline range, demonstrating that the effect of pH is not universal but instead conditioned by mineral composition and microstructural interactions50.

Thus, the influence of pH on ionic mobility and conductivity can be explained by both ionic speciation in solution and the surface chemistry of minerals51. Under acidic conditions, proton abundance enhances conductivity due to their high mobility and ability to reduce electrostatic barriers. Conversely, at alkaline pH, hydroxylated complexes predominate; these species are bulkier and less mobile, reducing ionic transport49.

Moreover, pH affects the surface charge of clay minerals by shifting their point of zero charge (PZC), thereby altering ion adsorption–desorption equilibria and the concentration of free charge carriers in the pore solution52. This mechanistic interpretation is consistent with electrical double layer (EDL) theory and explains the nonlinear correlation observed between pH and resistivity, as previously reported in soil electrochemistry studies53.

Correlation matrix between pH and average resistivity

To explore the relationships among the measured variables, a correlation matrix was constructed using 150 observations (15 mixtures × 10 moisture content levels). The pH of each mixture was determined at a standardized moisture content of 20%46 and was kept constant for that mixture in the analysis.

Figure 13 presents the heatmap of the Pearson correlation matrix between moisture content, pH, and resistivity of the mixtures (n = 150 observations). At the global level, the correlation between pH and resistivity was weak and not statistically significant (r = + 0.10; p = 0.238), confirming the trend observed in Fig. 12.

Global statistical validation: correlation matrix.

Similarly, moisture and resistivity showed a slight negative association (r = − 0.15; p = 0.069), while pH and moisture were statistically independent (r ≈ 0; p = 1.000). An analysis by mixture revealed differentiated patterns: in five mixtures (e.g., M3), resistivity decreased with increasing moisture content (negative r, significant in M3, p = 0.028), whereas in ten mixtures (e.g., M5, M8, M10, M12, M14), resistivity increased with moisture content (positive r, several statistically significant).

According to Fig. 13, the heterogeneity suggests that the effect of moisture content on electrical response is strongly modulated by the composition and microstructure of each mixture under the evaluated laboratory conditions54. Consequently, the aggregate (weak) result does not reflect a single universal trend but rather the superposition of mixture-dependent patterns.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

To globally integrate the physicochemical and electrical information obtained for each mixture, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted using the proportions of sodium bentonite, bituminous coal, natural ferric oxide, and amorphous graphite, together with the measured values of pH and average electrical resistivity.

The PCA reduced the dimensionality of the system to two principal components (PCA1 and PCA2), which together explain a significant proportion of the total variability among the 15 formulated mixtures (Fig. 14). This representation facilitates the visualization of clustering patterns and the identification of dominant variables that structure the overall behavior.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of the natural mixtures.

The first principal component (PCA1) accounts for 40.6% of the total variance, while the second principal component (PCA2) explains 29.7%, resulting in a cumulative explained variance of 70.3%. This indicates that the two-dimensional projection captures most of the variability among the formulated mixtures. Both PCA1 and PCA2 presented eigenvalues greater than 1, satisfying the Kaiser criterion for principal component retention and confirming the statistical robustness of the dimensional reduction.

Analysis of the loadings (Fig. 15) reveals that PCA1 is positively associated with electrical resistivity and ferric oxide content, and negatively associated with graphite and carbon content, clearly separating conductive from resistive systems. PCA2 is primarily influenced by bentonite proportion and pH, reflecting the role of chemical environment and clay content in modulating electrical behavior. These quantitative results confirm that the clustering observed in Fig. 14 is statistically supported.

Loadings of the original variables on the first two principal components of the PCA.

Likewise, although sodium bentonite can exhibit reduced resistivity at high moisture contents due to enhanced ionic conduction associated with swelling and water retention, its intrinsic electronic conductivity is low. When combined with ferric oxide, which presents semiconducting characteristics, the overall conductive behavior remains strongly moisture-dependent. In contrast, mixtures containing higher proportions of carbonaceous materials exhibit more stable electronic conduction pathways. Since PCA reflects the overall electrical behavior rather than isolated high-moisture conditions, the positioning of bentonite–ferric oxide mixtures in the resistive region are consistent with their dominant conduction mechanism.

In Fig. 14, groupings consistent with the composition of the mixtures can be observed. Mixtures with higher contents of conductive materials13 (such as bituminous coal and amorphous graphite), including M2, M4, M9, and M15, tended to cluster within a specific region of the PCA plane, sharing characteristics such as low resistivity and high electrochemical activity. In contrast, mixtures dominated by sodium bentonite and ferric oxide (e.g., M1, M3, M6) were positioned in opposite regions, reflecting their highly resistive behavior, extreme pH values, and limited electrochemical response. Balanced mixtures (ternary or quaternary compositions such as M10, M12, M14) occupied intermediate positions, suggesting a functional response balanced between insulation and conductivity.

This multivariate analysis supports the results obtained in previous sections and provides a framework for classifying mixtures according to their potential application in high-resistivity soils, based on an integrated variable profile. Furthermore, the PCA highlights the importance of including parameters such as pH in this evaluation.

On the other hand, Fig. 15 shows the loadings of the original variables on the first two principal components of the PCA. The arrows indicate the relative contribution of each variable to the principal dimensions.

From Fig. 15, it can be observed that resistivity and oxide content are positively correlated with PCA1, while graphite and bituminous coal content are negatively correlated, reinforcing the separation between conductive and insulating mixtures in the multivariate space.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the electrical resistivity of natural-material mixtures composed of sodium bentonite, amorphous graphite, bituminous coal, and natural ferric oxide depends on physicochemical, structural, and electrochemical factors. The main conclusions are as follows:

Moisture content is the principal factor; as it increases, resistivity decreases significantly until stabilizing between 60% and 70%, due to pore saturation and improved hydraulic connectivity.

pH exerts an indirect influence: the lowest resistivities were observed in mixtures with moderately alkaline pH values, while extreme values increased resistivity.

ORP activity, evaluated by cyclic voltammetry, revealed that the most conductive mixtures also exhibited higher intensity and greater symmetry in their curves, indicating enhanced electronic and surface conductivity.

Certain mixtures of natural materials analyzed in this study, particularly M2, M4, and M9, exhibited the lowest resistivity values, ranging from 6 Ωm to 39 Ωm (M4) and from 5 Ωm to 32 Ωm (M9). These results are comparable to those reported in the literature, such as55, where resistivity values of 22.81 Ωm–14.83 Ωm were achieved using chemical materials.
