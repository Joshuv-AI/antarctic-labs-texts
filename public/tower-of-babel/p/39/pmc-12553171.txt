Therapeutic effects of cinnamic acid in a rat model of collagenase-induced Achilles tendinopathy
Authors: Sercan Capkin, Ali Ihsan Kilic, Saadet Ozen Akarca Dizakar, Yagmur Isin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAchilles tendinopathy is a common degenerative disorder characterized by pain, impaired function, and poor tissue regeneration. Effective treatment remains a challenge due to the tendon’s limited healing capacity. Cinnamic acid, a naturally occurring phenolic compound, has demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects in various tissue repair models. However, its therapeutic potential in tendon injury remains unexplored, and this study represents the first study to evaluate its effects in an experimental Achilles tendinopathy model.MethodsIn this experimental study, tendinopathy was induced in male rats via ultrasound-guided collagenase injection into the right Achilles tendon. Animals were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 7 each): Sham, Injury Control, vehicle control (Tendinopathy + DMSO), and three cinnamic acid treatment groups (10, 30, and 90 mg/kg). Background

Tendinopathy is a common musculoskeletal disorder that frequently affects the Achilles tendon and is characterized by pain, functional impairment, and structural degeneration [1, 2]. Despite its high prevalence, tendon healing remains a clinical challenge due to the tissue’s limited vascularization, low cellularity, and inherently poor regenerative capacity [3]. Although various conservative and surgical treatment strategies have been developed, complete restoration of tendon structure and function in Achilles tendinopathy is rarely achieved [4]. Among conservative modalities, extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT) and a variety of injectable therapies, including corticosteroids, platelet-rich plasma (PRP), and hyaluronic acid, have been investigated as potential adjuncts to promote tendon healing; however, clinical outcomes remain inconsistent and controversial [5, 6]. Given these limitations, increasing attention has been directed toward natural compounds with regenerative potential, such as caffeic acid and quercetin, which have shown promising results in preclinical models of Achilles tendinopathy [7, 8].

There is increasing interest in novel therapeutic strategies, particularly those involving antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agents, to promote tendon regeneration and accelerate recovery [9, 10]. Among these, cinnamic acid (CA), a naturally occurring phenolic compound, has attracted attention for its ability to modulate inflammation and support tissue repair [9]. Recent studies have emphasized the broad pharmacological profile of CA and its derivatives, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, neuroprotective, and anti-melanogenesis properties [9–12].

In addition to its general antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, CA has demonstrated a broad range of biologically relevant activities, including antimicrobial (antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral), anticancer, and antidiabetic effects [13–15]. Structurally related compounds, such as hydroxycinnamic acid and cinnamaldehyde, have been shown to enhance osteoblast differentiation and activity while inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption in both in vitro and in vivo models [16–19]. These findings suggest potential bone-protective effects through the modulation of bone remodeling pathways. In line with these observations, Karatas et al. reported that systemic administration of CA significantly reduced inflammation and alveolar bone loss while promoting osteoblastic activity in a rat model of experimental periodontitis, further supporting its regenerative potential in skeletal tissues [20].

In a full-thickness wound model in rabbits, topical application of low-dose CA (< 1%) significantly accelerated wound closure and improved histological healing [9]. Similarly, in a deep second-degree burn model, CA demonstrated comparable efficacy, with enhanced tissue repair observed across multiple concentrations [21]. Although the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood, CA has been shown to stimulate fibroblast proliferation and enhance the synthesis of extracellular matrix components, such as type I collagen and laminin—key contributors to tissue remodeling and wound repair [22].

As previously discussed, research on CA has expanded substantially in recent years, highlighting its diverse pharmacological profile, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Although no previous study has directly investigated the effects of CA on tendon healing, several preclinical studies have demonstrated its regenerative potential in multiple connective tissue models, including full-thickness skin wounds [9], bone remodeling [16–19], and experimental periodontitis [20]. These tissues share key regenerative mechanisms with tendon, including fibroblast activation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and resolution of inflammation. Moreover, CA has been shown to promote fibroblast proliferation and migration, stimulate type I collagen and laminin synthesis, and regulate oxidative and inflammatory pathways—biological processes that are central to tendon healing [22].

Based on this rationale, we hypothesized that CA may facilitate tendon repair and regeneration by modulating key biological pathways shared across connective tissues. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the therapeutic effects of CA in a rat model of Achilles tendinopathy and to assess its feasibility as a therapeutic agent for tendon injuries. To our knowledge, this is the first preclinical study to evaluate CA in a tendon injury model, thereby addressing an important gap in the literature regarding the regenerative effects of phenolic compounds.

Methods

Ethical approval and study design

This study was approved by the Dokuz Eylul University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (Approval No: 36/2024). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines and the principles outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. An a priori power analysis (effect size = 0.4, α = 0.05, power = 0.90) indicated a statistical power of 0.91, ensuring sufficient sensitivity to detect significant differences between groups. The study design was adapted from previously validated models of collagenase-induced Achilles tendinopathy [23, 24]. Previous studies have demonstrated that collagenase injection reliably induces tendinopathic changes, including collagen disorganization and inflammatory cell infiltration, from day 7 post-injection onwards [23]. Additionally, ultrasound-guided collagenase injection has been validated as a reproducible and reliable method in rat models [24]. Group allocation and treatment protocols were structured based on published data regarding CA dosages [25]. All animals were obtained from the Dokuz Eylul University Experimental Animal Research and Application Center (Izmir, Türkiye). Humane endpoints were predefined as a loss of more than 20% body weight, persistent immobility, severe wound infection, or signs of severe distress such as labored breathing, hunching, or unresponsiveness. However, no animals met these criteria during the experimental period, and all survived until the planned study endpoint.

Animal assignment and surgical model

Healthy adult male Wistar rats weighing 392–578 g (mean ± SD: 462.4 ± 36.6 g) were used in this study. Animals were randomly assigned to six experimental groups (n = 7 per group) using a simple randomization method. Prior to the procedures, all animals underwent a one-week acclimatization period under standard laboratory conditions. Surgical interventions were performed exclusively on the right Achilles tendon; the left tendon was not manipulated and was excluded from all experimental assessments.

Baseline model induction and allocation

Collagenase injections were performed under real-time ultrasound (USG) guidance by the same surgeon using a fixed dose (30 µL of 3 mg/mL collagenase) and an identical injection trajectory to standardize lesion induction across animals. Group allocation (n = 7 per group) was conducted by simple randomization to minimize selection bias. In accordance with the 3R principles, we did not perform additional sentinel histology or quantitative USG scoring at day 7 (pre-treatment). Instead, baseline tendinopathy development was inferred from the established validity of this model, which has been extensively characterized in previous studies demonstrating reproducible degenerative changes beginning as early as day 7 and peaking around day 14 post-injection [23, 24]. This standardized induction method and randomization were implemented to ensure comparability across groups in the absence of a separate baseline assessment.

Anesthesia and preoperative preparation

Anesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal injection of 10 mg/kg xylazine (2% Alfazyne, Rompun, Bayer AG, Germany) and 100 mg/kg ketamine (Ketalar, Pfizer, USA). The depth of anesthesia was continuously monitored, and supplemental doses were administered as needed to maintain an adequate anesthetic plane. To prevent postoperative infection, a single prophylactic dose of cefazolin sodium (30 mg/kg; Cefamezin, Sanofi, France) was administered intraperitoneally. After confirming adequate anesthesia, the surgical site (right hind limb) was shaved and disinfected with povidone-iodine solution (Batticon®, Adeka İlaç Sanayi, Turkey). All procedures were performed on a heated surgical table to maintain normothermia. To ensure technical consistency and minimize operator-related variability, all surgical interventions were conducted by the same surgeon.

Experimental groups and treatment protocols

Achilles tendinopathy was induced in all experimental groups, except the Sham group, under guidance of a portable wireless USG device (Clarius L7 Linear Ultrasound, 4–13 MHz; Clarius Mobile Health Corp., Canada) connected wirelessly to a tablet (Samsung S8 Ultra Suwon-si South Korea). A 30 µL injection of Type I collagenase (3 mg/mL; Collagenase Type I, C0130, ≥ 125 CDU/mg solid, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was administered into the medial portion of the right Achilles tendon using a 30 G needle under real-time USG guidance (S1 Video). Tendinopathy development was subsequently assessed through USG and histological analyses. Animals were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 7 per group) as follows:

Group 0 (Sham): A 30 G needle was inserted into the right Achilles tendon under USG guidance without any injection, mimicking the mechanical stimulus of the needle alone.

Assessment methods

Tendon healing and the therapeutic efficacy of CA were comprehensively evaluated using USG and histomorphological analysis. USG, a non-invasive and real-time imaging technique, was used to monitor dynamic structural changes in the tendon on days 21 and 35, allowing for longitudinal assessment of tendinopathy progression and treatment response. At the end of the 35-day experimental period, all animals were euthanized, and the right Achilles tendons were harvested for light microscopic evaluation of collagen fiber organization, cellularity, and vascularization. The use of USG allowed for repeated in vivo assessments within the same animals, thereby reducing the number of animals required, in accordance with the ethical principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement).

USG evaluation

USG evaluations were performed on day 21 (after 14 days of treatment) and day 35 (at the end of the experimental protocol) to assess treatment response and tendinopathy progression. Imaging was conducted in vivo using a portable, wireless high-resolution USG device. To minimize motion artifacts and ensure consistent image quality, rats were anesthetized before each session using intraperitoneal xylazine (10 mg/kg) and ketamine (100 mg/kg). The right Achilles tendons were scanned in both longitudinal and transverse planes under standardized imaging conditions.

USG assessment was based on three key parameters described by Lee et al. [25]: echogenicity, neovascularization, and calcification. Each parameter was scored semi-quantitatively on a scale from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe pathological changes. A total USG score was calculated by summing the three parameter scores. This scoring system, adapted from Lee et al. [25], allowed for objective quantification of tendon alterations and enabled both longitudinal and intergroup comparisons. To reduce potential bias, all ultrasound images were anonymized and randomly coded prior to evaluation, and scoring was performed by a single experienced operator blinded to group allocation. However, inter- and intra-rater reliability analyses were not performed.

Histomorphological evaluation

Histological assessment was performed only at the terminal endpoint (day 35); no interim histology (day 14) was performed to adhere to the 3R principles (particularly Reduction). At the end of the experimental period, all animals were humanely euthanized under deep anesthesia using an overdose of intraperitoneally administered pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg). Confirmation of death was established by the absence of respiratory movement, heartbeat (verified via auscultation), and corneal reflex. Following euthanasia, the right Achilles tendons were harvested en bloc and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 48 to 72 h. Standard histological processing was performed, including paraffin embedding and longitudinal sectioning at 5 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used for microscopic evaluation, which was conducted using a ZEISS Axiolab 5 light microscope at 100× and 200× magnifications. Images were digitally recorded. Histological assessments were performed by a single experienced histologist who was blinded to group allocation. All slides were anonymized and labeled with randomized alphanumeric codes prior to evaluation to ensure observer blinding and minimize assessment bias.

Tendon healing was evaluated using a semi-quantitative scoring system modified from the method described by Chen et al. [26]. This system included six parameters: collagen fiber structure, fiber alignment, nuclear morphology, cell density, inflammation, and neovascularization. Each parameter was scored on a scale from 0 (normal) to 3 (severe), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 18. This composite score enabled objective comparison of tendon healing quality across experimental groups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; although minor deviations were observed, data were considered approximately normally distributed based on skewness and kurtosis values within the acceptable range (–2 to + 2), supported by histogram and Q–Q plot inspection. Paired samples t-tests were used to compare USG scores between day 21 and day 35 within each group. Intergroup comparisons of USG and histological scores on day 35 were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Levene’s test was used to assess homogeneity of variances. Due to significant violations (p < 0.001), the Games–Howell post hoc test was applied. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Mean differences are reported as mean ± standard error (SE), along with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The correlation between total USG and histological scores on day 35 was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient due to the ordinal nature of the data and potential non-normal distribution.

Correlation between USG and histological findings

A strong and statistically significant positive correlation was found between total USG scores and histological scores on day 35 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.794, p < 0.001). This indicates that higher USG scores—reflecting greater echogenicity loss, neovascularization, and calcification—were closely associated with more severe histological degeneration, including disorganized collagen fibers, increased cellularity, and inflammation. Approximately 63% of the variability in histological scores was explained by USG scores (ρ² = 0.63), supporting a strong quantitative agreement between non-invasive USG imaging and histological evaluation in assessing tendinopathy severity.

Discussion

This study investigated the therapeutic potential of CA in a collagenase-induced rat model of Achilles tendinopathy. The results confirmed our hypothesis that CA promotes tendon regeneration, as evidenced by significant improvements in both USG and histopathological assessments. The therapeutic response was dose-dependent, with the 30 mg/kg dose demonstrating the most consistent benefits. This group showed reduced echogenicity loss and neovascularization on USG, along with improved collagen fiber organization, normalized cellular morphology, and decreased inflammation in histological evaluations. A strong positive correlation between USG and histological scores further validated the reliability of imaging-based assessments for tendon healing. Collectively, these findings suggest that CA facilitates tendon repair by modulating degenerative processes, with 30 mg/kg emerging as the most effective dose in this experimental model.

Our findings align with prior research on natural phenolic antioxidants in musculoskeletal healing. Yurteri et al. reported that intraperitoneal administration of caffeic acid significantly improved both histological and biomechanical outcomes in a rat model of Achilles tendon injury [7]. In a subsequent study, the same group showed that oral quercetin enhanced tendon repair by upregulating type I/III collagen, α-SMA, and Galectin-3 expression [8]. Compared to these studies, our model utilized enzymatically induced tendinopathy, incorporated multiple CA dosages, and combined USG and histomorphological analyses for a multidimensional assessment. These methodological distinctions strengthen the translational value of our findings, highlighting CA’s potential as a regenerative agent akin to caffeic acid and quercetin.

The dose-response relationship we observed mirrors patterns reported with other CA derivatives. Jahromi et al. demonstrated that cinnamaldehyde, a CA metabolite, improved functional and histological outcomes in a sciatic nerve injury model, with maximal efficacy at 30 mg/kg [25]. Both lower and higher doses showed diminished effects, suggesting a narrow therapeutic window. Our findings replicate this pattern, with the 30 mg/kg CA dose yielding the most robust improvements, while 90 mg/kg did not confer additional benefit. Such a non-linear response may reflect saturation of cellular uptake pathways or dose-dependent shifts in pharmacodynamics. Further mechanistic studies investigating tissue-level CA bioavailability and oxidative stress modulation are warranted.

Additional support for CA’s regenerative capacity is provided by Naghavi et al., who showed that topical CA at low concentrations (< 1%) significantly enhanced wound healing in a rabbit full-thickness skin injury model [9]. Their findings—elevated hydroxyproline content, increased neovascularization, and improved epithelial regeneration—were most pronounced at the 0.1% dose. Interestingly, 10% CA yielded reduced benefits, consistent with the dose-effect ceiling observed in our study. Similarly, Alizadeh Haghighi et al. [21] found that 1% topical CA significantly enhanced dermal regeneration, collagen synthesis, and wound contraction in a deep second-degree burn model, outperforming silver sulfadiazine. These converging observations across diverse tissue types reinforce the importance of identifying an optimal therapeutic range for CA.

CA’s regenerative effects may extend beyond its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions to include anabolic modulation of connective tissue. Multiple studies have shown that CA derivatives—such as p-hydroxycinnamic acid and cinnamaldehyde—enhance osteoblastic activity, suppress osteoclastic resorption, and promote bone matrix mineralization [16–19]. These compounds upregulate RUNX2, ALP, and COL1A1 expression and regulate the RANKL/OPG axis to inhibit osteoclastogenesis [18, 20]. Karatas et al. reported that CA reduced alveolar bone loss and inflammation in rats with periodontitis, increasing OPG while downregulating COX-2, PPAR-γ, and RANKL [20]. Since tendon healing involves extracellular matrix remodeling, fibroblast activation, and resolution of inflammation—mechanisms also central to bone repair—these molecular pathways may underlie CA’s beneficial effects in tendinopathic tissue. The pronounced histological recovery seen in the 30 mg/kg group may thus reflect activation of conserved regenerative circuits across different connective tissues.

In the study by Lee et al. [24], collagenase was administered under USG guidance to establish an Achilles tendinopathy model, and the resulting tendinopathic changes were evaluated both histologically and biomechanically. Their findings demonstrated that USG-derived parameters, such as neovascularization and echogenicity, correlated significantly with the mechanical properties of the tendon. Similarly, in our study, collagenase injection was performed under USG guidance, and tendon degeneration and subsequent regeneration were assessed using USG and histomorphological methods. Although biomechanical testing was not conducted, the strong correlation observed between USG and histological scores supports the reliability of USG in monitoring tendon healing non-invasively. In this context, our study aligns with the methodological approach of Lee et al., further confirming that USG-guided collagenase injection provides a valid and reproducible tendinopathy model, and that USG offers a practical and accurate tool for assessing therapeutic outcomes.

Nevertheless, biomechanical testing is widely considered the gold standard for confirming tendon functionality, as tendons primarily serve to bear and transmit mechanical loads [24]. While our findings provide robust structural and histological evidence of tissue regeneration, these parameters alone cannot fully establish whether functional load-bearing capacity was restored. This limitation constrains the translational interpretation of our results, and future studies should integrate biomechanical testing alongside imaging and histology to comprehensively evaluate tendon healing.

Strengths and Limitations.

A major strength of this study is the use of a well-established and reproducible collagenase-induced tendinopathy model, guided by real-time USG, which allowed precise and minimally invasive induction of tendon degeneration. The combined application of serial USG evaluation and histomorphological analysis enabled comprehensive monitoring of both structural and cellular aspects of tendon regeneration. Furthermore, the inclusion of multiple CA doses allowed characterization of its therapeutic window and dose–response profile.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the absence of biomechanical testing represents a major limitation, as functional recovery of tendons is primarily determined by their ability to withstand and transmit mechanical loads. Although USG and histology provided valuable structural and cellular insights, these modalities cannot substitute for direct biomechanical assessment. Consequently, the translational interpretation of our findings remains limited, and future studies should integrate biomechanical testing alongside imaging and histology to more comprehensively confirm tendon functionality. Second, no molecular analyses, such as gene expression profiling or signaling pathway evaluation, were performed, which limits mechanistic insight into the therapeutic effects of CA. In particular, the potential effects of CA on tenogenic differentiation of tendon-derived stem cells (TDSCs) and on macrophage polarization in an inflammatory microenvironment were not investigated, thereby restricting the mechanistic interpretation of our findings. Future studies addressing these cellular and molecular mechanisms are warranted to better elucidate the therapeutic potential of CA in tendinopathy. Third, we did not perform additional quantitative ultrasound scoring or sentinel histology at day 7 (pre-treatment) to verify baseline lesion severity across groups. Although standardized induction under real-time ultrasound guidance and randomization were implemented to minimize variability, this omission precluded explicit confirmation of baseline comparability. Future studies should incorporate pre-treatment assessments to further strengthen model validation. Fourth, another limitation of this study is the lack of interim histological evaluation at day 14, which corresponds to the peak of the inflammatory response in collagenase-induced tendinopathy models. This evaluation was not performed in order to reduce animal use in line with the 3R principles (particularly Reduction). As a result, while our findings clearly demonstrate regenerative effects at day 35, the absence of earlier histological data limits our ability to capture the dynamic inflammatory phase. Future studies should incorporate intermediate histological assessments to better characterize the temporal effects of CA on tendon healing. Fifth, USG imaging in rats posed inherent technical challenges due to the small size of the Achilles tendon and limited vascular flow, which particularly impacted the reliable detection of neovascularization. Although a high-resolution probe was used, Doppler-based assessment may have lacked the sensitivity to detect microvascular changes. To minimize potential bias, all ultrasound images were anonymized and assessed by a blinded operator; however, no inter- or intra-rater reliability analyses were performed, and a fully standardized protocol for image acquisition and interpretation was not applied. These factors may have introduced observer-related variability and may have limited the reproducibility of the USG-based assessments. Finally, as this study was conducted in an animal model, further translational and clinical research is warranted to validate the applicability of these findings to human Achilles tendinopathy. Moreover, collagenase injection represents a chemically induced model of tendinopathy, which, although well-established and widely used in preclinical research, does not fully replicate the chronic mechanical overuse that underlies most clinical cases of Achilles tendinopathy. Therefore, while this model reliably induces tendon degeneration and allows reproducible evaluation of therapeutic interventions, its translational relevance to human overuse tendinopathy should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, histological evaluation was limited to hematoxylin–eosin staining. While this provided valuable information on tendon architecture and cellularity, the absence of complementary stains such as Masson’s trichrome or Picro Sirius Red restricted a more detailed assessment of collagen remodeling.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that CA significantly promotes tendon regeneration in a collagenase-induced rat model of Achilles tendinopathy. The 30 mg/kg dose yielded the most consistent improvements in USG and histological evaluations, indicating a dose-dependent therapeutic response. The strong correlation between USG and histological scores further supports the utility of USG as a reliable, non-invasive monitoring tool. Although further molecular and clinical studies are warranted, these findings highlight CA’s potential as a promising therapeutic agent for tendinopathy.
