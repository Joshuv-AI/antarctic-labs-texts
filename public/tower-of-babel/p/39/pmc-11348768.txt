Undifferentiated embryonal sarcoma of the liver in children: our experience in four difficult cases and three-dimensional practical exploration
Authors: Wenli Xiu, Tong Li, Jie Liu, Jingli Zhang, Jingmiao Wang, Feifei Wang, Rongkun Zhu, Nan Xia, Xin Chen, Qian Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimsTo explore rare and difficult cases of undifferentiated embryonal sarcoma of the liver (UESL) in children in a single centre, summarize the diagnosis and treatment experience and analyse the role of a computer-assisted surgery system (Hisense CAS), thus providing a new global vision and three-dimensional perspective.MethodsWe retrospectively collected the clinical data including the diagnoses and treatment processes, of children with UESL confirmed by histopathological examination in our hospital from January 2009 to December 2020. The relationship between the tumour volume and important blood vessels and between the liver volume and tumour volume, as well as other three-dimensional characteristics in the reconstructed three-dimensional model were analysed using Hisense CAS. The findings from this analysis can be used to aid in surgical decision-making and preoperative planning.ResultsFour children—3 girls and 1 boy—with UESL were included in the study. The age at onset ranged from 6 to 8 years. All four children presented with symptoms of abdominal discomfort, and abdominal masses were detected during physical examination. Owing to the wishes of their parents and the possibility that the disease was benign, all four children underwent one-stage radical surgery. For patient 1, a three-dimensional reconstruction was created during the initial diagnosis, which made accurate evaluation and planning of the preoperative procedure challenging. In patient 2, the tumour was located in the middle lobe of the liver and involved the first and second hepatic hilum. For patient 3, the pathological diagnosis of the tumour after surgery was challenging, but eventually, the diagnosis was confirmed through histochemistry and consultation with higher-level hospitals. Patient 4 had a giant tumour, which had a preoperative simulated future liver remnant volume (FLV) that was 21.0% of the total volume of the liver and tumour (TLTV). According to the standard liver volume (SLV) for children, the FLV was 77.0% of the SLV, making surgery feasible. All four children underwent complete resection, and only patient 4 experienced recurrence below the diaphragm 19 months after surgery. Currently, the 3-year overall survival rate is 100%, and the 3-year event-free survival rate is 75%.ConclusionUESL in children is rare, and the key to diagnosis and treatment is complete surgical resection. Through individualized three-dimensional surgical planning, accurate and complete resection of difficult and complex UESL in children can be achieved, leading to a favourable prognosis.

Introduction

Undifferentiated embryonal sarcoma of the liver (UESL) is a rare malignant liver tumour that originates from the mesenchyme. It is more common in children between the ages of 6 and 10, with an annual incidence rate of one in one million [1]. The clinical manifestations and imaging features of UESL are often nonspecific, leading to delayed or misdiagnosed diagnoses. When it is discovered, the tumour is typically large, exceeding 10 cm in diameter, and it can compress surrounding organs or involve important blood vessels in the liver. Furthermore, the tumour capsule frequently adheres to surrounding tissues, and considering the complex anatomy of the liver and frail nature of children, surgical procedures become particularly challenging. Additionally, UESL has a high recurrence rate, with recurrence and metastatic disease being the main causes of death [2, 3].

In recent years, advancements in surgical technology and the combination of surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy have significantly improved the survival rate of children with UESL. Complete tumour resection is a crucial factor that impacts the prognosis of children [4, 5]. The computer-assisted surgery system (Hisense CAS) enables individualized three-dimensional reconstruction of traditional two-dimensional CT scans, overcoming the limitations of two-dimensional images. The ability to obtain three-dimensional spatial information and facilitate precise preoperative planning has been proven to assist in achieving complete tumour resection [6, 7]. The aim of this study is to analyse challenging cases of UESL treated at our centre, summarize our experience and evaluate the three-dimensional auxiliary role of CAS. The findings of this study can potentially contribute to improvements in the clinical diagnosis and treatment of UESL.

Patients and methods

Patients and clinical data

Children with UESL confirmed via histopathological examination at the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University between January 2009 and December 2020 were included. Patients with other malignant tumours, who were not followed up postoperatively, and were 14 years older and above were excluded. Clinical data, including initial treatment time, age, sex, symptoms, signs, laboratory and imaging manifestations, treatment, and prognosis of children with UESL, were collected. The characteristics and difficulties of clinical diagnosis and treatment were retrospectively analysed.

Three-dimensional individualized assessment and preoperative planning

Upper abdominal contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) was conducted using a CT scanner (Sensation 64; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with a slice thickness of 0.625 mm. The scanning area extended from the top of the diaphragm to the lowest point of the kidneys. The CT images were viewed using the centricity system and stored in DICOM file format. For three-dimensional reconstruction of CT images to evaluate the patient’s condition before surgery, a computer-assisted surgery system (Hisense CAS, Qingdao, China) was employed after 2013. The specific steps for this reconstruction process have been previously described [8]. In summary, the system utilized artificial intelligence and traditional image segmentation algorithms to automatically segment and generate the liver and tumour. The hepatic artery, vein, and portal vein were also automatically generated by selecting seed points and tracking blood vessels. Different organs and lesions were distinguished by different colours, and the system automatically calculated and displayed the liver volume and tumour volume. Preoperative simulated surgical resection was performed using Hisense CAS, which offered three modes: anatomical liver resection, tumour dissection, and autonomous resection. These modes allowed the selection of the best surgical plan and provided the residual liver volume.

Future liver remnant volume evaluation

The evaluation index used is the ratio of future liver remnant volume (FLV) to standard liver volume (SLV) (FLV/SLV). The Hisense CAS calculates the FLV. The SLV is calculated using the following formula: for males, SLV = 691.90×BSA1.06, and for females, SLV = 663.19×BSA1.04. The body surface area (BSA) was calculated as BSA = BW(kg)0.425×BH(cm)0.725 × 0.007184 [9].

Clinical treatment and follow-up

The treatment plan for each child was developed by a multidisciplinary team consisting of paediatric surgeons, radiologists, and paediatric oncologists. Prior to surgery, all the children underwent CECT for differential diagnosis and two-dimensional imaging evaluation. Starting in 2013, children also underwent three-dimensional surgical simulation and volume assessment. The diagnosis and treatment plan were determined based on the child’s general condition and other auxiliary examination results. Additionally, 3D images were displayed in the operating room to compare findings during the operation and provide guidance for the surgical process. Following postoperative pathological diagnosis, adjuvant chemotherapy was administered, and all patients were followed up until October 2023.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 4 children with UESL were included in the study. The detailed clinical characteristics of these children are presented in Table 1. Among the participants, there were 3 girls and 1 boy, all ranging in age from 6 to 8 years. All 4 patients reported abdominal discomfort, and abdominal masses were detected during physical examination. Three of the children also had fever. Patient 4 presented with a high fever and pain in the right upper quadrant. Physical examination revealed an obviously distended abdomen with a palpable mass, which was tender but did not exhibit rebound tenderness. A complete routine blood examination revealed a white blood cell count of 16.98 × 109/L and a CRP level of 55 mg/L, suggesting the possibility of tumour hyperthermia. Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and carbohydrate antigen 199 (CA199) levels were within the normal range for all children, whereas carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125) levels were elevated in 2 patients.

CT images and three-dimensional models

The imaging findings of the four children revealed intrahepatic cystic and solid masses. Two tumours originated from the left lobe of the liver, one tumour was located in the middle lobe, and one tumour was located in the right lobe. The maximum diameter of the tumours in the CT images ranged from 9.0 to 17.5 cm. The tumours compressed the liver, intrahepatic vessels, and surrounding tissues, causing significant changes in anatomical position. Three-dimensional reconstructions, which clearly revealed the spatial location of the tumour and its relationship with surrounding important blood vessels were created for Patients 2, 3, and 4. The blood vessels appeared deformed due to pressure rather than being violated. The tumour volumes were 345.5 cm3, 456.4 cm3, and 1392.4 cm3, and the tumour-free liver volumes were 633.4 cm3, 560.1 cm3, and 624.2 cm3. Surgical simulation using the three-dimensional model revealed that the future liver remnant volume (FLV) of the optimal solution was 632.8 cm3, 357.4 cm3, and 422.8 cm3, and the FLV/standard liver volume (SLV) ratios were 97.3%, 57.3%, and 77.0%, respectively (Table 2). Therefore, all patients were deemed suitable for one-stage surgical resection. None of the four children underwent needle biopsy or received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, as there were no urgent indications or suspicion of malignancy, and the parents expressed their preference for immediate surgery.

In patient 2, the tumour was located at the junction of the left inner and right front lobes, involved the first and second hepatic hilum, and was approximately 9.5 cm × 9.2 cm in size, as assessed in two dimensions, so it was not possible to perform one-stage surgery. However, preoperative CECT indicated the possibility of bile duct cystadenoma or mesenchymal hamartoma, and the parents’ requested surgery. The three-dimensional reconstruction revealed that the tumour did not invade the left or right portal vein, inferior vena cava, or three hepatic veins. The evaluation of three-dimensional images and multiple surgical simulations led the doctor to dissect the tumour (Fig. 1). Patient 4 underwent left hepatectomy. The FLV was determined to be 422.8 cm3, accounting for 21.0% of the total volume of liver and tumour (TLTV). Based on the SLV and calculation formula for children, the FLV accounted for 77.0% of the SLV, indicating that surgery was feasible.

Intraoperative process and postoperative results

An incision was made in the upper abdomen, below the costal margin, to access the ligaments around the liver and examine the size, location, and relationship of the tumour to nearby organs. The first porta hepatis was dissected, and then the intermittent Pringle method was used to temporarily block hepatic blood flow. Typically, the flow was obstructed for 15 to 20 min and then unobstructed for 5 min. The cutting line was determined using electrosurgery based on the preoperative simulation. During the obstructed period, the liver was dissected and separated using Cavitational Ultrasonic Surgical Aspiration (CUSA) and vascular forceps to prioritize liver parenchyma resection. Compression and electrocoagulation were used during the unobstructed period to minimize bleeding. The tumour-involved major blood vessels were carefully separated, ligated, and sutured if necessary. All four children underwent successful operations, and the postoperative pathological surgical margins were negative (R0). The patients were discharged from the hospital 15 to 20 days after surgery.

During the operation on patient 3, the tumour was observed breaching the liver capsule and partially enveloped by the greater omentum, raising concerns of tumour rupture. The operation was successful, but postoperative pathological diagnosis posed challenges. The postoperative pathology results revealed that most tumours exhibited mesenchymal differentiation, whereas a small portion exhibited epithelial differentiation. The mesenchymal component displayed numerous expanded vascular structures, with slightly atypical tumour cells loosely arranged and actively growing in the local area. Mitotic figures were also observed. The epithelial component exhibited a distribution pattern of hepatocyte nests in strip-like and sheet-like formations, along with a significant number of small bile ducts, some of which displayed immature differentiation. Based on these findings, it was difficult to completely rule out the possibility of mesenchymal hamartomas with atypical features. However, after immunohistochemistry, molecular testing, and consultation with a higher-level hospital, the tumour was finally determined to be UESL (Fig. 2).

Follow-up results

All four children started postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy one week after surgery, with alternating courses of vincristine, epirubicin, ifosfamide, actinomycin D, and etoposide. After six courses of treatment, the patient exhibited a complete response, and the medication was discontinued. The follow-up period ranged from 36 to 175 months. The 3-year overall survival rate was 100%, and the 3-year event-free survival rate was 75%, with no recurrence or metastasis observed in three children. Patient 4 underwent symptomatic treatment prior to surgery, and surgical treatment was performed once the patient’s condition stabilized according to the preoperative plan. During the operation, a large mass measuring 15 × 12 × 10 cm was discovered in the left lobe of the liver. Dense adhesions were found between the tumour, the lateral abdominal wall, and the diaphragm, which were carefully released to facilitate safe removal. Postoperative pathology revealed tumour involvement in the middle subdiaphragmatic adhesion area but no involvement of the left subdiaphragmatic tumour adhesion or the diaphragm, indicating a negative surgical margin. A PET‒CT scan conducted half a month after surgery did not reveal increased tumour metabolism. Unfortunately, one month after completing chemotherapy, Patient 4 experienced sudden abdominal pain, low-grade fever, and left shoulder pain. Blood tests revealed an increase in inflammatory markers, and ultrasound revealed a heterogeneous hypoechoic mass under the left diaphragm, suggesting the possibility of an abscess. However, considering the patient’s medical history, the possibility of UESL metastasis and recurrence could not be ruled out. PET-CT was also inconclusive in differentiating between the two possibilities, leading to the decision to perform a needle biopsy. The pathology results confirmed UESL, prompting the child to undergo another surgery. During the operation, the boundaries of the tumour’s surrounding tissue were unclear, raising suspicion of invasion into part of the diaphragm and some tissues of the lesser curvature of the stomach. However, postoperative pathology confirmed only tumour infiltration of the diaphragm (Fig. 3). Following surgery, the patient received adjuvant chemotherapy once again and has been followed up since, showing no signs of recurrence or metastasis.

Discussion

UESL, a highly aggressive primary mesenchymal malignant tumour, was first reported and named by Stocher and Ishak in 1978 [1]. It is characterized by an insidious onset, postoperative recurrence, and a poor prognosis. Complete tumour resection is crucial in the treatment of UESL but often challenging. Owing to its low incidence, most UESL cases are documented in small-scale case series or case reports. Previous studies involving the national cancer database data and reports of previous treatment experience have focused primarily on analysing patient characteristics and prognostic factors [10, 11]. This study is unique because it analyses cases treated in our centre in terms of difficulties in clinical diagnosis and treatment, particularly surgery. Our surgical exploration was based on a three-dimensional perspective and global vision.

The clinical diagnosis of UESL in children is difficult, and surgery is needed for confirmation

Children with UESL exhibit diverse and nonspecific clinical manifestations, often presenting with an upper abdominal mass and potential abdominal pain. Additional symptoms may include fever, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and dyspnoea [12]. Laboratory tests typically do not reveal specific findings, and liver function and tumour marker levels are usually within the normal range. Radiology also has limited diagnostic value, showing a solitary low-density mass in the liver that may exhibit cystic watery density or solid cystic features. These clinical characteristics make it difficult to differentiate UESL from benign lesions such as mesenchymal hamartomas, bile duct cystadenomas, and liver abscesses, as well as malignant tumours such as hepatoblastomas [13, 14]. Our cases also confirmed the challenges in clinically diagnosing UESL in children, including cases of metastatic recurrence.

The diagnosis of UESL relies primarily on histopathology. However, in some cases, it can still be difficult to make a definitive diagnosis, and surgery is often necessary to confirm the diagnosis [15]. During surgery, distinct fish-like gross pathological boundaries can be observed, which include both cystic and solid components within a grey-white colloid area. These components may or may not exhibit haemorrhage and necrosis. Microscopically, the background of the myometrium shows spindle-shaped or stellate cells with unclear cell borders, hyperchromic nuclei, inconspicuous nucleoli, eosinophilic PAS-positive and antidiarrheal cytoplasmic globules, and multinucleated giant cells. Immunohistochemistry is not highly specific for UESL, but most cases show positive staining for vimentin, α-1 antitrypsin, and CD68 [16, 17]. Among our patients, Patient 3 proved to be particularly difficult to diagnose even with postoperative pathology, and the final diagnosis was reached after consultation with other departments.

The effectiveness of chemotherapy for UESL in children is uncertain, and radical surgery is necessary for a cure

Currently, there is no specific neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimen for UESL, so chemotherapy regimens originally designed for paediatric rhabdomyosarcoma or other soft tissue sarcomas are often used. In some medical centres, preoperative chemotherapy is performed to reduce the tumour size and enable complete tumour resection without the need for PRETEXT assessment or tumour resection [18, 19]. However, unfortunately, many chemotherapy drugs have potentially serious side effects, and owing to the rarity of UESL, the effectiveness of preoperative chemotherapy remains uncertain [20]. In China, some families choose not to accept chemotherapy for various reasons. These patients may require urgent surgery or abandon treatment. For these reasons, and based on diagnostic and treatment criteria, we opted for a more aggressive approach by performing one-stage radical surgery.

A UESL diameter greater than 15 cm or tumour rupture is considered a risk factor for poor prognosis in children [2]. In this study, Patient 3 was considered to have experienced tumour rupture during surgery, but it is possible that the omentum wrapped around the tumour to prevent invasion and metastasis. The tumour was successfully removed along with the wrapped omentum, and there was no recurrence or metastasis after surgery. Patient 4 had a high fever at the time of initial diagnosis. The tumour diameter was 17.5 cm, and adhesion to the diaphragm was observed during the operation. Tumour rupture was also suspected. However, even with the removal of the potentially involved portion of the diaphragm to the greatest extent possible, the child still experienced metastasis and recurrence 19 months after surgery, despite undergoing regular and complete postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy. Nevertheless, it is undeniable that the combination of surgery and chemotherapy significantly improves the survival rate of children.

Surgical resection for UESL in children is challenging, and three-dimensional imaging is helpful for precise planning

Surgical resection is the primary treatment approach for UESL. However, UESL is highly malignant and grows rapidly, often resulting in a large tumour that compresses surrounding organs and distorts vital liver blood vessels. This poses significant challenges for surgeons during resection [21, 22]. Three-dimensional reconstruction has been proven to increase surgical safety, enable complete tumour resection, and reduce the incidence of complications such as liver failure [23]. Our three-dimensional reconstruction allows surgeons to visualize the three-dimensional position of the tumour in relation to major blood vessels and assess its resectability through simulated surgery. This technique is particularly useful for evaluating residual liver volume and planning complex liver segment resections, such as those in the middle lobe of the liver. This support enables surgeons to completely remove the tumour, protect important blood vessels, and retain sufficient residual liver volume, thus avoiding the need for liver transplantation and reducing the incidence of postoperative complications. In this study, we performed a preoperative evaluation of 3 cases of UESL using simulated FLVs and children’s SLVs. Notably, in Patient 4, the corrected residual liver volume, which was determined based on the children’s standard liver volume and calculation formula, was deemed sufficient for surgical resection. These findings provide valuable clinical insights and accurate references for diagnosis and treatment.

Additionally, in our study, we carefully considered the boundaries of the tumour. Despite the tumour coming into contact with, or even compressing, major blood vessels, it did not invade them. In the past, radical tumour resection involved ensuring that the resection margin of the liver was more than 1 cm away from the tumour boundary. However, recent large retrospective studies have shown that a negative margin status and chemotherapy can ensure a good prognosis for UESL. Postoperative chemotherapy may play a crucial role in controlling or eliminating UESL, as microscopic residual disease can still have an impact [2, 24]. We prioritized the safety of blood vessels while completely resecting the tumour, ensuring that the resection edge was as far away from the tumour as possible. Histological examination of the resected portion revealed no residual tumour cells, indicating negative resection margins. Even if there was invasion or the tumour was found to be locally ruptured during the operation, we still removed it to the greatest extent possible, achieving positive therapeutic outcomes.

Conclusion

UESL in children is clinically rare, and complete surgical resection is crucial for diagnosis and treatment. Individualized three-dimensional surgical assessment aids accurate and complete resection of UESL. This approach offers valuable spatial position and volume information, as well as surgical path simulation, providing a comprehensive and novel perspective.
