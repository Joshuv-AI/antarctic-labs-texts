Exposure to Lead (Pb) influences the outcomes of male-male competition during precopulatory intrasexual selection
Authors: Rosemary Patrick, Kim Colyvas, Andrea S. Griffin, Michael Stat, Wayne A. O’Connor, Mohammad Mahmudur Rahman, Mallavarapu Megharaj, Geoff R. MacFarlane
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Male-male competition is a component of Darwin’s theory of precopulatory intrasexual selection, where males compete for access to resources important for reproduction and successful males pass on traits that endow greater competitive ability to their offspring, thereby exaggerating the trait(s) over evolutionary time. Metals, such as lead (Pb) often occur in urban mangrove forests, where shore crabs reside, and being neurotoxic may cause sub-lethal effects on behaviour. In this study, we asked whether exposure to Pb influences the outcomes of male-male competition in the Semaphore crab, Heloecius cordiformis, thereby altering outcomes of intrasexual selection. Crabs were exposed to an environmentally relevant, and a behaviourally relevant, concentration of lead (i.e. Pb, 10 µg/L and 100 µg/L respectively) under laboratory conditions for 96 h and subsequently allowed to compete for burrow ownership in experimental arenas. Exposed crabs (100 µg/L) were less successful than control crabs in chela size-matched paired competitive interactions, spent less time in burrows and predominantly lost burrow ownership. Further, exposure to Pb contamination (both 10 µg/L and 100 µg/L) negated the chela size advantage in size-asymmetric competitive interactions. This contaminant-induced loss of size-related competitive advantage may result in relaxation in selection pressure on body size and smaller male carapace widths and especially male chela lengths in contaminated locations. Assessment of a range of locations in the wild found a consistent pattern of metal-associated size declines in more contaminated locations for males, but not for females.

Introduction

Ever since Darwin, the theory of sexual selection has provided a powerful explanation for the evolution of exaggerated traits, which at first glance seem at odds with natural selection. Precopulatory sexual selection comprises two major independent and often complimentary evolutionary processes, namely intrasexual selection (involving male-male competition) and intersexual selection (involving female-mate choice). In intrasexual selection, males compete for access to females and/or other resources important for reproduction. Males possessing morphological or behavioural traits that place them at a competitive advantage enjoy greater reproductive success with the consequence that the trait(s) becomes exaggerated over evolutionary time. In intersexual selection, females choose among candidate males for traits that they find most attractive, which results in differential reproductive success amongst males based on trait choice and a similar exaggeration of traits over evolutionary time. Although both male-male competition and female choice are precopulatory in nature, selection may also occur through postcopulatory mechanisms including sperm competition and cryptic female choice1,2.

Changes in environmental conditions can alter the strength or direction of sexual selection. Anthropogenic environmental changes such as climate change, hunting and fishing can all affect population dynamics including mate availability, costs of mate searching and the operational sex ratio, thereby leading to complex interactions and eco-evolutionary feedback loops between environmental change and sexual selection3. Further, anthropogenic changes such as noise and light pollution can affect how females perceive male traits important for intersexual selection and thus ultimately affect the evolutionary trajectory of sexually selected traits and the trait composition of populations4. Finally, exposure to environmental contaminants, such as the organophosphorous pesticide, methyl parathion, can reduce the size of sexually selected male traits5. Despite progress in measuring the impacts of anthropogenic factors on sexual selection, no study to date has examined the effect of metallic contaminants, such as lead (Pb), on sexual selection processes and associated wild population trait change in any taxa.

The Semaphore crab, Heloecius cordiformis, is an intertidal Brachyurid crab of the Ocypodoidea Family, common in mangrove swamps in south-eastern (SE) Australian estuaries and a great model to study anthropogenic impacts on the process of sexual selection. The species is sexually dimorphic, with males generally larger than females and males possessing equally exaggerated claws (chela; male chela are equal in size but disproportionally larger compared to female chela of the same body size) which may hamper male camouflage and increase predation, but are thought to be under sexual selection and beneficial in male-male competition and mate choice6. Males construct and actively defend individual burrows, which are used for protection from predation and as a resource to attract females for reproduction, as copulation occurs within the resident’s burrow. Males constantly compete for burrow ownership, and larger males with larger claws are more successful at retaining burrow ownership and holding larger territories7,8. Males also use their exaggerated chela for waving to attract females to mate, thus female choice is thought to further exaggerate claw size4. Further, experimental evidence of male-male competition and female choice has been well established in other representatives of Ocypodoidea9,10.

Much of the Semaphore crab’s current range consists of mangrove remnants in heavily urbanised estuaries in New South Wales (NSW), Australia, with inputs of metallic contaminants from current and legacy industrial activity11. Such sources include metallic smelter operations, coal-fired power generation, wastewater treatment plant effluents, urban and industrial runoff and boating activity 12. Lead (Pb), is the metal most elevated and of most ecotoxicological concern in SE Australian estuaries13. Due to mangroves’ silty, organically enriched and largely anoxic sediment, metal(loid)s are often sequestered in mangrove sediments. These sequestered metals can act subsequently as a significant source of metallic exposure to crabs due to their active bioturbation and periodic oxygenation of sediment, which modifies sediment pH and increases metal bioavailability to gill surfaces via metal-enriched porewater14. Further, Semaphore crabs are detritus feeders, making ingestion a further pathway for significant metal uptake. Pb is a non-essential metal and is generally accumulated in crabs proportionally with environmental dose11.

The effects of metallic contaminants on aggressive behaviour are rare in the literature and no prior study has examined the effects of metallic exposure on male-male competition for resources important to mating opportunities in intrasexual selection. However, there is evidence of reduced aggressive behaviour in hermit crabs exposed to copper, when fighting for ownership of shells15. Given these findings, it is reasonable to hypothesise that exposure to other metallic contaminants such as lead (Pb) may elicit similar changes in aggressive encounters when males fight for resources important for reproduction. Further, Pb is neurotoxic and may alter biogenic amine neurotransmission which is implicated in modulating aggressive behaviour, and is thus likely to have impacts on competitive ability16,17.

In the current study, we ask whether exposure to Pb impacts the male-male competition component of intrasexual selection in H. cordiformis. Firstly, we examine if exposure to an environmentally relevant concentration (10 µg/L), and a behaviourally relevant concentration (100 µg/L) of Pb, can affect the outcomes of male-male competition for chela size-matched individuals vying for an unoccupied burrow. Secondly, we examine whether exposure to these concentrations of Pb influences the asymmetric chela size advantage in contests over resources important for reproduction (an unoccupied burrow). Finally, we seek evidence for changes in the strength of sexual selection in wild populations across a metal contamination gradient in the field.

Results and discussion

In Experiment 1, we exposed male crabs to dissolved Pb in seawater in aquaria for 96 h and tested whether this exposure influenced the outcome of competitive interactions in precopulatory intrasexual contests. We constructed three types of competitive contests in an arena between chela size-matched males: (1) exposed to 10 μg/L Pb versus unexposed, (2) exposed to 100 μg/L Pb versus unexposed, and (3) exposed to 10 μg/L Pb versus 100 μg/L Pb. Unexposed control males had little Pb in their tissues (4–5 µg/g) after 96-h exposure, while the 10 µg/L Pb and the 100 µg/L Pb exposure accumulated 12–16 µg/g and 52–66 µg/g Pb in their tissues respectively (Table 1, experiment 1). Lead (Pb) exposed individuals in dyads did not differ in their time spent in movement compared to unexposed, or lower exposed individuals (Fig. 1a-c). Similarly, there were no differences in time spent in the opponent zone for Pb exposed individuals compared to unexposed, or lower exposed individuals (Fig. 1d-f). However, individuals exposed to 100 µg/L Pb were less successful in competitive interactions, spending less time in the burrow compared to unexposed individuals (Pb 0 µg/L vs 100 µg/L, (t(26) = −2.83, p = 0.01) (Fig. 1 g-i). Using linear mixed model (LMM) approaches, patterns of significance were similar in terms of pointing to a competitive impairment in high Pb-exposed male crabs. Time spent in movement exhibited a contest by concentration interaction (LMM, F(1, 34) = 5.13, p = 0.03), suggesting crabs exposed to 100 µg/L engaged in less movement when competing with controls (Table S1). No effect of contest type, exposure concentration or interaction was observed for time in opponent zone (Table S1). For time spent in burrow, there was no significant contest by concentration interaction as may be predicted from t-test findings, but nevertheless a significant main effect of concentration (LMM, F(2, 69) = 6.74, p = < 0.01), with less time in burrow for crabs exposed to 100 µg/L Pb across all contests (Table S1). For the number of chela thrusts, the generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) revealed a significant contest by concentration interaction, with crabs exposed to 100 µg/L Pb exhibiting lower thrusts when competing in 0–100 and 10–100 contests (Table S1). We found no evidence that chela size (mm) was a significant predictor of any of the modelled outcomes (Table S1).

In summary, male individuals exposed to higher concentrations of Pb at 100 µg/L were less successful in competitive interactions than lower exposures or unexposed individuals, indicating exposure to Pb negatively influences the outcomes of the male-male competition component of precopulatory intrasexual selection and exposed males are at a competitive disadvantage in contests for resources important for reproduction.

In Experiment 2, we asked whether the established size advantage in competitive interactions, a hallmark of precopulatory intrasexual selection in the species, was affected by contaminant exposure. We paired larger and smaller clawed male crabs in dyads where both members of the dyad were exposed to the same exposure concentration of Pb under three aquaria exposure scenarios (i.e. unexposed control conditions or Pb at 10 µg/L or 100 µg/L for 96 h). Although no differences in the time spent in movement (Fig. 2a-c), the time spent in the opponent zone (t(22) = 2.33, p = 0.03) was significantly greater for smaller clawed crabs compared to larger clawed crabs when both members of the dyad were exposed to 10 µg/L Pb (Fig. 2d-f). This is an unusual finding as no such similar trend was found at higher doses. More importantly, a size advantage of larger clawed males spending more time in the burrow was observed in unexposed controls as predicted (t(22) = -2.11, p = 0.05); however, this size advantage was abolished at both 10 µg/L Pb (t(22) = −1.43, p = 0.17) and 100 µg/L Pb (t(22) =−0.71, p = 0.41) (Fig. 2g-i). Further, outcomes of final burrow ownership (winner/loser) demonstrated a contaminant effect, with larger clawed males winning ownership of burrows under unexposed conditions (χ21,24 = 8.33, p = < 0.01) and 10 µg/L Pb (χ21,24 = 5.33, p = 0.02), whereas no size advantage in burrow ownership was observed at 100 µg/L Pb (χ21,24 = 0.33, p = 0.56). The absence of a size advantage under Pb exposure conditions could be due to the load of Pb accumulated on a per weight basis, as larger clawed male individuals accumulated more Pb in both the 10 µg/L (large = 16 µg/g, small = 7 µg/g Pb) and 100 µg/L (large = 63 µg/g, small = 19 µg/g Pb) treatments (Table 1, Experiment 2). There were no differences in the number of chela thrusts between large and small crabs at any Pb exposure (Fig. 2j-l). When examining the same data employing a LMM approach, there was no main effect of size, no main effect of concentration and no significant interaction for any of the dependent variables (time spent in movement, time in opponent zone and time spent in burrow). Similarly, the GLMM for the number of thrusts revealed no significant predictor effects. Similar to experiment 1, chelae size was not a significant predictor of outcomes in any of the models.

When examining chela size (mm) of winners and losers across the experiment employing LMM, there was a main effect of outcome (F(1,33) = 16.42, p = < 0.01); i.e. winners were larger in chela size than losers. There was no concentration effect, and no interaction, which contrasts to t-test findings where the size advantage was abolished at 100 µg/L Pb.

Finally, we employed a generalised linear model to assess differences in the probability of winning for large versus small individuals among exposure concentrations. There was a trend for larger crabs to be winners in controls and a more even distribution of winners as large and small crabs at 100 µg/L exposures, but the trend was not significant (Table S1).

Discrepancies between the t-test approach and modelling approaches are likely due to insufficient power attributable to small effect sizes and low sample sizes. Further, complex models with numerous factors and consequent simultaneous comparisons have lower power to detect marginally significant differences. Finally, small effect sizes are likely due to the short exposure window of 96 h with longer, chronic exposures more likely to produce greater treatment differences (Table S1). Given the chela size covariate was non-significant in all LMM and GLMM models, and score correlations between pairs were low, reliance on interpreting significance via t-test outcomes is valid. Finally, we did undertake a retrospective power analysis to determine the sample size that would have been needed to reliably detect a change in larger crab winner proportions from 0.90 at 0 ug/ml Pb concentration to 0.60 at 100 Pb ug/ml. Achieving a power of 0.80 would have required an increase from 12 to 38 pairs at both the 0 ug/ml condition and the 100 ug/ml condition, thus the experiment was considerably under-powered to detect an effect of Pb concentration that would have reflected a realistic and reasonably large effect size.

Collectively, t-tests and chi-square analyses found that the demonstrated claw size advantage in competitive interaction outcomes was abolished at environmentally relevant concentrations of Pb exposure, thus Pb exposure can be identified as a causal factor likely to influence the strength and direction of size-related precopulatory intrasexual selection in the field. Less support was found with our modelling approaches, again likely attributed to small effect sizes and low samples sizes.

Given exposure to Pb appears to negate the size advantage of larger males outcompeting smaller males in competitive encounters, we hypothesised that such contaminant-induced relaxation of selection for male size, and especially chela size, will be evident in the field and that average population-level male size and chela size should decrease across locations as metal(loid) levels and especially Pb contamination increases. Uncontaminated locations should select for increased male size and especially chela length, while at contaminated locations, such selection would be negated as there is no differential competitive size advantage.

In our field study, male crabs in seven populations across NSW estuaries were measured for carapace width and chela length. Carapace width and chela length averages were undertaken on the total population and a subset of sexually mature males (chela develops a purple colour upon sexual maturity). Sediments were also collected from each location for total metal(loid) analysis including Pb, Cu, Zn, As and Cd as these are the metal(loid)s most commonly elevated in Australian estuaries11 (Table S2). To reduce the dimensionality of the dataset, a principal components analysis was undertaken and an assessment of eigen-values and the scree plot identified two significant components. Component 1 explained 75% of variation and was positively correlated with Pb, Cu, Zn and As, while component 2 explained 21% of variation and was positively correlated with Cd (Table S3, Fig S1).

Copper ranged from 11–49 µg/g, Zinc 33–103 µg/g, Cd 0.03–0.49 µg/g and As 2–16 µg/g among locations. Lead was the only metal significantly elevated with a range of 10–212 µg/g among locations, exceeding the ANZG default sediment guideline value of 50 µg/g Pb, thus being the only metal of significant ecological concern (Table S2).

Robust negative linear relationships were found between male morphology and Pb contamination. As Pb contamination increased among locations, male carapace width (R2 = 0.715) and male chela length (R2 = 0.789) decreased as predicted. Similarly, male carapace width (R2 = 0.696) and chela length (R2 = 0.710) were negatively correlated with component 1, which represented the combined metal(loid) assemblage of Pb, Cu, Zn and As. Similar to Pb alone, relationships were stronger with mature purple males than the total population (purple carapace width R2 = 0.847; purple chela length R2 = 0.817) (Table 2, S2 and S3, Fig S1). No significant relationships were found with component 2, which was correlated with Cd (Table 2, S2 and S4).

Further, we also analysed female chela morphology in relation to Pb contamination and found no significant relationship between average female chela length versus sediment Pb across locations (β = -0.009 ± 0.004, t = -2.40, p = 0.06, R2 = 0.54) (Table S3). These findings strongly suggest that a relaxation of sexual selection on males at higher Pb loadings is responsible for size/Pb associations rather than a general toxicological response on morphology operating on both sexes. Thus, there is strong correlative evidence from the field that at locations where metal loadings are low, selection for large males, and especially large chela size is evident, while at more contaminated locations, there is reduced selection for size, likely due to the contaminant- induced abolishment of selection.

How Pb mechanistically reduces aggression in contests is largely unknown. However, amine neuron systems (serotonin and octopamine) are implicated as key physiological regulators of aggressive behaviour and social dominance in crustaceans, but experimental results are mixed on the exact roles of these neurotransmitters in increasing or decreasing aggressiveness depending on context and temporality18. Most evidence points towards serotonin inducing dominance and aggressive responses while octopamine is associated with subordinate responses19. No work to date has examined the effects of Pb on serotonin or octopamine transmission in crustaceans, though it is likely Pb may directly or indirectly affect their production, transmission or reuptake.

In summary, male crabs that accumulate more Pb, are less successful in competitive interactions, i.e. are at a competitive disadvantage when contesting burrow ownership, an essential reproductive resource. Further, the established competitive advantage of larger chelae males outcompeting smaller males for burrow ownership is abolished when individuals are exposed to levels of Pb typically found in contaminated locations in the wild. This contaminant-induced relaxation of selection on size is evident in the wild, with more contaminated locations exhibiting size reductions in terms of both male carapace width and chela length. Such impacts on the sexual selection process results in morphological impacts to populations. Such impacts could also ultimately result in population-level declines in resilience to further environmental change in the face of the Anthropocene.

Experimental method

96-h sub-lethal Pb exposure

Semaphore crabs (Heloecius cordiformis), were collected from an uncontaminated location, Swansea, Lake Macquarie, NSW, Australia (Table S2) and transported to the laboratory in plastic boxes containing sponge cubes moistened with local seawater. Morphometric parameters such as carapace width and right chela length were measured with digital vernier callipers upon arrival at the laboratory.

Test stock solutions were prepared by dissolving lead nitrate (PbNO3) into seawater. Crabs were then subjected to a sub-lethal exposure of Pb at concentrations of 0, 10, and 100 μg/L. The 10 μg/L exposure was chosen as an environmentally relevant dose, being close to the default guideline value for Pb in seawater from Australian water quality guidelines (ANZG for Pb = 12 μg/L)20. The 100 μg/L dose was chosen as a behaviourally relevant dose, being the concentration observed to elicit significant impacts on behaviour in crustaceans in past studies21.

The exposure of crabs to the Pb solutions and seawater controls were carried out in 5L glass aquaria, each containing 3L of Pb-spiked seawater. Each aquaria housed an individual crab to achieve independent replication of dose. The aquaria were set up in a temperature-controlled room held at 20 °C with an aeration system. All the experimental crabs were exposed to Pb for 96-h with no feeding during exposures.

To verify the accuracy of nominal concentrations, water was collected at the beginning of the trial with measured concentrations by ICP-MS 9.34 ± 0.7 μg/L for the 10 μg/L exposure and 113.74 ± 1.74 μg/L for the 100 μg/L.

Experiment 1 and 2: behavioural assays

After the 96-h exposure period, the crabs were held for 20 min in a behavioural test arena to habituate before the behavioural observations commenced. The test arena (30 × 25 × 25 cm) was filled with sediment from Swansea, to a depth of 6 cm and divided with a barrier into two equal zones, with an artificial burrow of 25 mm diameter to a depth of 30 mm at the centre of the arena. Two male crabs were habituated, each on either side of the partition for each behavioural test and the barrier was raised to commence the behavioural observations. Behavioural observations were recorded for 15 min using a video camera mounted above the centre of the arena (Sony DCR10 TRV). Experiment one involved chela size-matched crabs (length of right chela ranging from 12.3 – 19.3 mm) with each pairing of crabs being within 1 mm of each other to ensure similar size pairings. Chela size was chosen rather than body size as chelae are thought to be the object of selection and chela size was found to be a more reliable predictor of the outcome of pairwise fights than body size in past experiments on shore crabs22. To initiate agonistic encounters, crabs were paired based on the following conditions: 1. Chela size-matched crabs with one crab exposed to 10 μg/L versus the other crab exposed to 100 μg/L, 2. Chela size-matched crabs unexposed versus exposed to 10 μg/L, and 3. Chela size-matched crabs unexposed versus exposure to 100 μg/L (N = 14 per treatment).

In Experiment 2, chela size-asymmetric pairings were undertaken with two size categories based on chela length (10.2 to 15.5 mm of right chela for small crabs and 16.4 to 19.8 mm for large crabs) with each crab pairing having a difference in size of ~ 4–5 mm. Chela size asymmetric pairings of crabs were for both crabs exposed to 1. Seawater/unexposed controls; 2. 10 μg/L Pb; and 3. 100 μg/L Pb (N = 12 per treatment).

For both Experiments 1 and 2, each crab of the pair was scored for the total time spent in movement (min), time spent in the opponent zone (min), time spent in burrow (min) and number of chelae thrusts, all indicators of competitive ability. The final burrow ownership was assessed as the crab which remained in burrow at the end of the observation period.

Exposures and behavioural observations were carried out between September—October, 2023.

Field population study

To assess whether the morphology of crabs were related to metal contamination levels in the field, crabs were collected at seven sampling locations across NSW, Australia, during October—November 2023. Throsby Creek (-32.9095, 151.762113), Swansea (-33.096762, 151.649235), Kooragang Island (Cobban Track, -32.852106, 151.708837), Kuring gai Chase National Park (Cowan Creek, -33.611473, 151.05632), and Homebush Bay (-33.84243, 151.08025, Powells Creek, Badu Mangrove, Bicentennial Park), were sampled. These locations were chosen based on a range of metal loadings to create an environmental metal gradient and the presence and abundance of H. cordiformis.

During low tide, two transects with five quadrats spaced evenly along the intertidal gradient were used to collect crab samples. Crabs were collected from 0.25 m2 quadrats on the two parallel transect lines, perpendicular to the waterline and separated by 50 m. Low tide provided the best opportunity to collect specimens, which were gathered either from the surface or by carefully excavating using a garden trowel under the crab’s burrow and raising the sediments until the crab emerged. At each sample quadrat, a sediment sample 15 cm depth, 10 cm diameter, was hand-collected with a trowel and taken to the lab for metal analysis. In the field, crab samples were sexed and measured to the nearest 0.01 mm using digital vernier callipers for carapace width (CW) and right chela length (CHL). To characterize population structure and identify recruitment events, a modified technique by Azevedo et al.23 was used to create a size-frequency distribution for each location and sex based on carapace width (CW) in mm, with each class being 2.5 mm in length. All locations only exhibited one age/size-related cohort so calculations of average carapace width and chela length were based on the entire population and sexually mature male purple chela only (N = 30–77 individuals per location).

Metal(loid)s analysis

The analysis of the metal(loids) As, Cu, Zn, Cd, and Pb were undertaken on 500 mg samples of air-dried powdered fine sediment (< 2 mm). The EPA 3050B method24 was used to digest sediment samples in a microwave-assisted digestion chamber (CEM Mars 6, NC, USA). In a Teflon digestion tube, a sample of silt was collected, and 5 ml of aqua regia (HNO3:3HCl, trace elements grade, Merck) was added. After the acid digestion was complete, the solution was diluted with MilliQ water, filtered by a syringe filter (0.45 μm, Millipore cellulose acetate hydrophilic membrane), and 10 ml of the solution was transferred to a plastic (polypropylene) tube for metals analysis using ICP-MS (NexION 350, Perkin Elmer, USA).

From the laboratory experiments, individual dried (60 °C) semaphore crabs were homogenized using a mortar and pestle before being weighed into a Teflon digestion vessel with 0.250 g of tissue. The block digestion method was used to dissolve 250 mg crab sample using a standardized chemical mixture of concentrated nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide. Afterward, the digestion vessels were heated in a microwave digestor using the USEPA method 305225. The elements (Pb) were analysed by ICP-MS using 10 mL of the digested solution after digestion and filtration.

All samples were analysed via ICP-MS with sample blanks, duplicates, and continuing calibration verification (CCV) included in each batch throughout the elemental analysis. Additionally, certified reference materials (Montana soil, 2711a = % recoveries, Cu = 96%, Zn = 89%, As = 94%, Cd = 99%, Pb = 100% and TORT 2 lobster hepatopancreas = % recovery, Pb = 102%) were run in parallel with samples to ensure quality of analyses (n = 4 per CRM).

Statistical analysis

In Experiments 1 and 2, time spent in movement, time spent in opponent zone, time spent in the burrow, the number of chelae thrusts and chela size were all normally distributed as assessed by normal probability plots of model residuals, thus two-sided parametric independent sample t-tests were initially employed. These t-tests were undertaken to compare behaviour between crabs (different exposure concentrations in Experiment 1 and different crab chela sizes in experiment 2) for each set of contests in JMP 16. Also, chelae size between winners and losers at each exposure in experiment 2 were also assessed via t-tests. Burrow ownership at the end of trials between the two crabs was assessed via 2 × 2 Chi-square tests in JMP 16.

As absolute chela size may influence outcomes of competitive interactions, and as the two individuals within a contest pair are not truly independent from one another, we also undertook modelling to account for these influencing factors. For all continuous dependent variables (time spent in movement, time spent in opponent zone, time spent in the burrow) we employed linear mixed models (LMM) with a two-way design. For experiment 1, we modelled each continuous dependent variable as a function of two categorical variables, namely contest (three levels: 0–10, 10–100,0–100) and concentration (three levels: 0,10, 100 μg/L Pb), as well as their interaction. We included the covariate chelae size (mm) as a continuous variable and a random intercept for each contest pair (pair ID). For experiment 2, we modelled each continuous dependent variable as a function of two categorical predictor variables, concentration (three levels: 0,10, 100 μg/L Pb) and relative chela size (two levels: larger, smaller), as well as their interaction. We included the covariate chelae size (mm) as a continuous predictor and pair ID as a random factor. For experiment 2, we also modelled variation in chela size (mm) as a function of two categorical predictor variables, contest outcome (two levels: winner, loser) and concentration (three levels: 0, 10, 100 μg/L Pb), as well as their interaction. We included pair ID as a random factor.

As the number of thrusts was a count variable, we employed generalised linear mixed models (GLMM) with a log link function and a negative binomial distribution (the Poisson models were over-dispersed). For experiment 1, we modelled the number of thrusts as a function of two categorical predictors each with three levels, namely contest (0–10, 10–100,0–100) and concentration (0, 10, 100 μg/L Pb), as well as their interaction. For experiment 2, we modelled number of thrusts as a function of two categorical predictors, namely concentration (three levels: 0,10, 100 μg/L Pb) and relative chela size (two levels: larger, smaller), alongside their interaction. For both experiment 1 and 2, we included the crab’s chelae size (mm) as a continuous covariate and a random intercept for each contest pair (pair ID).

Finally, for experiment 2, we used generalised linear models with a binomial distribution to assess differences in counts of large versus small winners among exposure concentrations.

All LMMs and GLMMs were conducted in R (Version 4.4.0) using the R packages glmmTMB for the negative binomial GLMMs and lme4 for the LMMs. For the LMMs Type III ANOVA tests for model effects were conducted using the lmerTest package.

In terms of the field analyses, to reduce the dimensionality of numerous metal(loid)s (Cu, Pb, Zn, Cd, As) in field sediments a principal components analysis was undertaken in JMP 16. The number of components was chosen based on scree plots and eigen-values greater than 1 and the proportion of variability explained. Significant loadings on components were assessed via correlation coefficients > 0.7.

Relationships between metals, components and male morphology were assessed via linear regressions on raw variables in JMP 16.
