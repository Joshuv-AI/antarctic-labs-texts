Combined CHK1 and PD-L1 blockade as a novel therapeutic strategy against stemness and immunosuppression in ovarian cancer
Authors: Mengqing Chen, Lin Huang, Mengna Zhu, Jing Cai, Feiquan Ying, Lin Liu, Wenhan Li, Si Sun, Yiping Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer stem cells (CSCs) are considered the ‘seeds’ of recurrence after chemotherapy, but eliminating CSCs remains notoriously challenging. This study aims to examine whether cell cycle checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1) blockade can abrogate the stemness of ovarian cancer (OC) cells, making them easier targets of anti-tumor immunity.MethodsPrexasertib was used to block CHK1 in OC cell lines and xenografts, and its cytotoxicity was assessed in vitro and in vivo. In vitro tumor-sphere formation assays and stemness markers were used to evaluate cell stemness. PD-L1 expressions were examined via qRT-PCR, Western blot, flow cytometry, and immunohistochemistry. Prexasertib in combination with anti-PD-L1 antibody Atezolizumab was tested in immune-proficient mice bearing OC xenografts in terms of effects on tumor growth, tumor cell stemness, and tumor infiltrating lymphocytes via tumor volume monitoring, immunohistochemistry, and flow cytometry.ResultsPrexasertib effectively inhibited CHK1 phosphorylation, exhibited significant anti-tumor effects in vitro and in vivo, accompanied by decreased OC cell stemness. CHK1 was highly expressed in tumor spheres versus tumor cells cultured in 2D system, and Prexasertib treatment suppressed sphere formation and reduced the ALDH+ cell fraction. Unexpectedly, Prexasertib upregulated PD-L1 expression in tumor cells. In vivo, combining Prexasertib with Atezolizumab led to more remarkable remission of tumors, when compared with Prexasertib or Atezolizumab alone. Meanwhile, the tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells significantly increased in the combination group, while exhausted T cells decreased; the treatments did not affect CD4+ cell infiltration.ConclusionDual targeting of CHK1 and PD-L1 may improve OC treatment by simultaneously suppressing stemness and enhancing anti-tumor immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04215-9.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) ranks among the most lethal forms of malignant gynecological malignancies, constituting approximately 3.7% of all cancer instances and 4.7% of cancer-associated mortality [1]. The primary treatment protocol for OC involves adjuvant platinum-based combination chemotherapy following tumor cytoreductive surgery [2]. Although the majority of patients with advanced OC initially respond well to platinum-based therapy, therapeutic resistance and tumor relapse remain critical hurdles [1]. All such biological mechanisms have lately been proven to be related to a population of cancer cells termed cancer stem cells (CSCs) [3, 4]. CSCs, which are also recognized as tumor-initiating cells, are a relatively rare cell subpopulation endowed with infinite self-renewal and differentiation abilities, and exhibit stem cell-like and cancer cell-like properties driving tumor growth, progression, metastasis, and treatment resistance, leading to worse clinical outcomes [3–5]. Therefore, targeting tumor cell stemness may represent an effective therapeutic strategy to improve the prognosis of OC [6, 7].

Cell cycle checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1) is an indispensable part of the DNA damage response (DDR) pathway [8, 9]. CHK1 activation phosphorylates downstream effectors, leading to transcriptional regulation, cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, and cell death [10]. Our previous research revealed that CHK1 is essential in maintaining stemness and increasing cisplatin resistance in OC cells [11]. Additionally, targeting CHK1 may destroy tumor cell stemness in colorectal and non-small cell lung cancer [12, 13]. Recent studies further reveal that CHK1 inhibition induces genomic instability through the accumulation of somatic mutations and DNA fragments, thereby enhancing neoantigen presentation and potentiating anti-tumor immunity [14]. In line with this, emerging evidence indicates that CHK1 inhibitors can modulate the tumor immune microenvironment. For example, CHK1 inhibition has been shown to promote T cell infiltration in colorectal cancer, thereby enhancing the response of ARID1A-deficient colorectal cancer to radiotherapy [15]. In melanoma, inhibiting CHK1 can increase cytotoxic CD8+ T cell activity and NKT cell tumor infiltration [16], indicating a potential immunomodulatory role for CHK1 beyond its direct cytotoxic effects.

The PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint axis serves as a critical mediator of tumor immune evasion across multiple malignancies [17]. Studies have demonstrated that PD-L1 is highly expressed in various solid tumors, including lung cancer, melanoma, OC, and colon cancer, but is barely detectable in normal tissues [18]. Notably, PD-L1 shows significantly elevated expression in CSCs, such as colorectal CSCs and lung CSCs [19, 20], suggesting that CSCs may evade immune surveillance through PD-L1. Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway can reactivate T cell function, promote their proliferation, and enhance tumor cell recognition and clearance [21]. Clinical data indicate that PD-L1-positive patients show significantly better response rates to blockade therapy than negative patients [22], further highlighting the importance of this pathway in tumor immunotherapy.

In this study, we found that CHK1 was highly expressed in OC stem cells (OCSCs) and linked to poor patient outcomes. The CHK1 inhibitor Prexasertib increased PD-L1 expression, weakening local immune function. We proposed that inhibiting both CHK1 and PD-L1 could enhance treatment effects. In immunocompetent mouse models of OC, we confirmed that combining Prexasertib and anti-PD-L1 antibody reduced tumor cell stemness and increased CD8+ cytotoxic T-cell infiltration. Our study indicates that the combined application of Prexasertib and anti-PD-L1 antibody influences both the stemness and anti-tumor immune response in OC, thereby offering novel insights into therapeutic strategies for this malignancy.

Materials and methods

Source of datasets

CHK1 expression in OC and normal ovarian tissues was evaluated using GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), leveraging information from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases. Additionally, we analyzed the correlations between the expression of CHK1 and CSC-related genes, including ALDH1A1, KLF4, POU5F1B, and CD44 in OC using GEPIA and Spearman’s correlation analysis. We analyzed the effects of CHK1 expression on the overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) of OC patients using the Kaplan–Meier plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/).

Cell lines

The human OC cell lines SKOV3 (adenocarcinoma), ES2(clear cell carcinoma), and OVCAR3 (high-grade serous adenocarcinoma), along with the ID8 mouse OC cell line, were procured from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC, Wuhan University, China) [23, 24]. Their STR identification has been carried out by Biowing Biotechnology. To ensure the absence of mycoplasma contamination, all cell lines were routinely tested using the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector (D101, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Adherent cell culture

For adherent cell culture, DMEM/F12 medium (L310KJ, basalmedia, Shanghai, China) was used and supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin (S110JV, basalmedia) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 7E602L2, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2, under controlled temperature and gas conditions.

Tumorsphere culture

Single-cell suspensions of SKOV3, ES2, and OVCAR3 cells were plated on ultralow attachment six-well plates (Corning, New York, United States) at a density of 1000 cells per well. The tumorsphere culture system was maintained in DMEM/F12 medium without FBS supplemented with 2% B27 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, United States), epidermal growth factor (EGF, 20 ng/mL, Peprotech, Rocky Hill, New Jersey), fibroblast growth factor (FGF, 20 ng/mL, Peprotech, Rocky Hill, New Jersey), leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF, 10 ng/mL, Peprotech, Rocky Hill, New Jersey), and insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, United States). After two weeks, tumorspheres with a diameter of 50 μm or greater were enumerated under a microscope.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

The VAMNE Magnetic Cell/Tissue Total RNA Kit (#RMA101-C2-P1, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and an automatic nucleic acid extractor (#VNP-32P, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) were used to extract cell total RNA. Subsequently, reverse transcription was performed using HiScript IV RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The qTOWER3 Real-Time PCR System (Analytik Jena) was used to perform quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). For this purpose, Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (#Q712, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was employed. Subsequently, the relative expression levels were ascertained employing the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH being designated as the internal control. The sequences of primers used are in Table S1. Each sample was tested with three biological replicates and three technical replicates.

Western blotting

The RIPA lysis buffer was prepared with a 1% phosphatase inhibitor (G2007, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (HY-K0010, MedChemExpress, Rocky Hill, New Jersey). Cells were then added to this buffer and disrupted on ice for 30 min to extract total protein from OC cells. The above mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 125,000 × g for 15 min, with the temperature maintained at 4 ℃. Using the bicinchoninic acid assay (P0011, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), the protein concentration was measured. After that, the carefully prepared protein extracts were thoroughly mixed with an appropriate amount of loading buffer in a clean container and were precisely boiled at 95 ℃ for exactly 10 min to ensure proper denaturation of the proteins. Following this, the proteins were separated on an SDS-PAGE gel and subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The PVDF membrane was then blocked using TBST containing 5% BSA. This blocking process was carried out at room temperature for 2 h. Next, the membrane was placed in a shaker and kept in incubation together with the primary antibody overnight at 4 ℃. The antibodies used in this study consisted of rabbit anti-CHK1 (25,887-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 1:1000), rabbit anti-phospho CHK1 (Ser296) (ab79758, Abcam, Cambridge, UK, 1:1000), rabbit anti-PD-L1 antibody (MH68942, Abmart, Shanghai, China, 1:1000), and rabbit anti-GAPDH (A19056, ABclonal, Wuhan, China, 1:5000) antibodies. After washes with TBST, the membrane was incubated with an anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase: HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (AS014, ABclonal,Wuhan, China, 1:5000). The protein strips were made visible by using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent (E411-03/04/05, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Imaging was performed and analyzed using the Molecular Imager® ChemiDoc™ XRS + and Image Lab™ Software (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Colony formation assay

The human OC cell lines SKOV3, ES2, and OVCAR3 were cultivated in six-well plates, and every well of the six-well plate contained 1000 cells. The strains were cultured in media supplemented with 0, 0.1, or 0.5 μM Prexasertib for 14 days. After being washed with PBS, they were then fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Subsequently, to visualize the colonies, the cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (C0428, TCI, Shanghai, China) for 1 h. More than 50 cells per colony were photographed and counted. This experiment included three independent biological replicates.

Aldefluor assay

The Aldefluor Assay Kit (01700, Stemcell Technologies, Canada) was used to measure ALDH activity in OC cells. To conduct the Aldefluor assay, first collect the cells subjected to various treatments and resuspend them in 1 mL of Aldefluor assay buffer. Prepare a control tube by adding 5 µL of DEAB to an empty 1.5 mL tube and label it as the control tube. Subsequently, add 5 µL of the ALDH substrate to the cell suspension, ensuring thorough mixing. Transfer 500 µL of this mixture into the control tube, mix again, and incubate the samples at 37 ℃ for 45 min. Following incubation, wash the samples twice with Aldefluor assay buffer and resuspend them. An ID7000 spectral cell analyzer was used for detection. The proportion of ALDH+ cells was analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.8.1. Each experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Fresh OC tissue from mouse subcutaneous tumors was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and embedded in paraffin. An IHC analysis of paraffin-embedded tumor sections was performed. Then, the tumor tissue sections were deparaffinized to remove the paraffin used for embedding. Next, antigen retrieval was carried out on these sections to expose the antigens that might have been masked during the embedding process. After that, the endogenous peroxidases were blocked to prevent false-positive results during the subsequent immunohistochemical staining. Finally, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃ to allow the antibodies to specifically bind to their corresponding antigens. Afterward, the positive signal was determined by diaminobenzidine and stained with hematoxylin. The immunohistochemistry kit was purchased from ZSGB-BIO (PV-9000, Beijing, China). Positive expression was indicated by the presence of brownish-yellow particles observable under a microscope (Olympus IX71, Japan) at a magnification of 200 × . The IHC analysis results (PD-L1, Cleaved-Caspase3, ALDH1A1, and GZMB) were evaluated according to the staining intensity (0, negative; 1, faint; 2, moderate; and 3, strong) and percentage of the stained area (1, ≤ 25%; 2, 26–50%; 3, 51–75%; and 4, > 75%). The two values were multiplied to obtain the final score for each sample. The scoring standard for Ki67, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells was the percentage of DAB-positive cells to hematoxylin-positive cells in each visual field. The following antibodies were used: PD-L1 (A19135, 1:200, ABclonal, Wuhan, China); ALDH1A1 (15,910–1-AP, 1:200, Proteintech, Wuhan, China); Ki67 (34330SF, 1:3000, CST, Massachusetts, United States); cleaved-caspase3 (9664S, 1:10, CST); CD8 (ab237709, 1:2000, Abcam); CD4 (ab183685, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK); and GZMB (A2557, 1:200, ABclonal, Wuhan, Chinzza). The results were independently evaluated by two researchers who were blinded to the sample characteristics.

Apoptosis analysis

For the detection of apoptosis of SKOV3, ES2, and OVCAR3 cells, an Annexin-V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was obtained. Briefly, 1 × 106 cells were exposed to 0, 0.1, or 0.5 μM Prexasertib for 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed with cold PBS to remove any unwanted substances or residues. After that, they were resuspended in 100 μL of Annexin V binding buffer, except the blank group, which was used as a negative control. Next, 5 μL of FITC Annexin V and 5 μL of PI were added to the cell suspension. Once these reagents were added, the cell suspension was incubated at room temperature for 15 min away from light to allow the necessary reactions to take place. Finally, the cells were detected using an ID7000 Spectral Cell Analyzer (Sony Biotechnology) to obtain the relevant data regarding their apoptotic status. FlowJo software version 10.8.1 was used to analyze the results. Annexin V-FITC + /PI- cells represented early apoptotic cells, and annexin V-FITC + /PI + cells represented late apoptotic cells. The sum of the two populations was the total percentage of apoptotic cells (where “ + ” indicates positive staining and “ − ” indicates negative staining).

Cell cycle analysis

The cell cycle analysis was carried out utilizing a cell cycle assay kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). SKOV3, ES2, and OVCAR3 cells were exposed to 0, 0.1, or 0.5 μM Prexasertib for 48 h. A total of 1 × 106 cells underwent a gentle washing procedure twice and were stored in anhydrous ethanol at − 20 ℃ overnight. After resuspension in RNase A reagent and incubation in a water bath set at 37 ℃ for 30 min. With the added PI reagent, the samples were incubated at 4 ℃ in a lightless area for 30 min. Detection was conducted using an ID7000 Spectral Cell Analyzer (Sony Biotechnology). The results were analyzed with FlowJo software version 10.8.1.

Animal models

For monotherapy study, 1 × 107 ID8 cells were resuspended in 200 μL PBS and injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of 4 week-old female C57BL/6N mice (Beijing Vital River, China). Four weeks later, mice were randomly assigned to receive either: 1) vehicle (n = 6); 2) Prexasertib (10 mg/kg, 2/7 days, BID, Days 1 and 2, i.e., n = 6).

For combination study, 1 × 107 ID8 cells were resuspended in 200 μL of PBS. The cell suspension was subsequently injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of female C57BL/6N mice (Beijing Vital River, China) aged 4 weeks. After 4 weeks, the mice were randomly received one of the following treatments: 1) vehicle (n = 6); 2) Prexasertib (10 mg/kg, 2/7 days, BID, Days 1 and 2, i.e., n = 6) [25]; 3) anti-PD-L1 antibody (1 mg/kg, QOD, i.e., n = 6); and 4) the combination of anti-PD-L1 antibody and Prexasertib (n = 6).

Body weight and tumor volume were measured, and their values were recorded every 3 days following the administration of the drug. We observed the health of the mice daily throughout the experiment. The formula for calculating tumor volume was length × width2. To minimize bias, the investigator responsible for tumor measurement was blinded to the group allocation throughout the experiment.

The mice were sacrificed after three complete cycles of treatment. The tumors were isolated and weighed. Portions of the tumors were analyzed by RT‒qPCR and multicolor flow cytometry, while the remainder was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for immunohistochemical analysis. All animal procedures conformed to the ethical standards approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Wuhan Youdu Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Flow cytometry

The mouse subcutaneous tumor tissues were dissociated with instruments and digested with a mixture of collagenase I, II, and IV (1 mg/ml; Biosharp, Beijing, China) for 1 h at 37 ℃. Afterward, a single-cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm filter to obtain a refined suspension. Next, tumor cells were obtained after the single-cell suspension was centrifuged at a speed of 500 × g for 10 min. For cell surface marker staining, 1 × 106 cells were incubated with the appropriate antibodies at 4 ℃ for 30 min. Next, the staining buffer was used to wash the samples twice after blocking. The stained cells were then examined using an ID7000 Spectral Cell Analyzer (Sony Biotechnology), and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.8.1. The following antibodies were used: PD-L1 (563,738, BD, New Jersey, United States); CD45 (552,848, BD, New Jersey, United States); CD3 (100,218, Biolegend, San Diego, California); CD8 (100,708, Biolegend, San Diego, California); CD4 (100,516, Biolegend, San Diego, California); PD1 (562,584, BD, New Jersey, United States); and TIM3 (747,624, BD, New Jersey, United States) antibodies.

Statistical analysis

For all in vitro experiments, three independent biological replicates were performed. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test, and equality of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test before applying parametric methods. When data were normally distributed and variances were equal, comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The effects of two factors on experimental outcomes were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships between CHK1 expression and the expression of CSC-related genes in OC tissues. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05. Significance levels are denoted as follows: ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.

Results

CHK1 inhibitor exerts an anti-tumor effect in vivo and in vitro

The results revealed significantly higher CHK1 mRNA expression in tumor tissues than in normal ovarian tissues (Fig. 1A). Among clinically relevant CHK1 inhibitors, Prexasertib has shown promise in multiple trials [26, 27]. To evaluate the inhibitory effects of Prexasertib in OC, we exposed the OC cell lines SKOV3, ES2, and OVCAR3 to 0, 0.1, and 0.5 μM Prexasertib for 48 h. Western blotting demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in CHK1 phosphorylation (Fig. 1C), confirming the effective targeting of CHK1 signaling by Prexasertib. Cell cycle assays showed that Prexasertib led to a rise in the proportion of G2/M phase tumor cells (Fig. 1D), which is consistent with previous findings [8, 28]. To confirm the impacts of Prexasertib on apoptosis and proliferation of OC cells, we treated SKOV3, ES2, and OVCAR3 cells with 0, 0.1, or 0.5 μM Prexasertib. As shown in the results, the apoptotic OC cells increased, and the number of OC cell colonies decreased in a concentration-related manner (Fig. 1E–H).

To validate these findings in vivo, we established a subcutaneous tumor model in immunocompetent C57BL/6N mice using mouse-derived ID8 cells. Following tumor formation, the mice were randomly assigned to the vehicle group (n = 4) and Prexasertib group (10 mg/kg, 2/7 days, BID, Days 1 and 2, i.e., n = 4). Notably, Prexasertib treatment markedly reduced tumor growth (Fig. S1A–C) without inducing significant weight loss (Fig. S1D), indicating favorable tolerability. Collectively, these data demonstrate that Prexasertib triggers apoptosis, suppresses cell proliferation, and elicits cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase in OC cells, highlighting its therapeutic potential in OC.

CHK1 inhibitor plays a vital role in decreasing the stemness of OC in vitro

Our previous study and other scientist’s research on CHK1 suggested that CHK1 inhibition may suppress OC stemness [11, 29]. Initially, a positive association was identified between the expression of CHK1 and that of CSC-related genes, such as ALDH1A1, KLF4, POU5F1B, and CD44, in the TCGA database via GEPIA (Fig. 2A) [30–32]. Additionally, we evaluated CHK1 expression in OCSCs using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and western blotting. The results indicated significantly higher CHK1 expression in OC tumorspheres compared to monolayer-cultured cells (Fig. 2B, C). To examine the influence of CHK1 inhibition on OC cell stemness, SKOV3, ES2, OVCAR3 and ID8 cells were treated with 0.5 μM Prexasertib. We observed that the mRNA expression levels of CSC-related genes, including ALDH1A1, KLF4, POU5F1B, and CD44 were reduced in OC cells treated with Prexasertib (Fig. 2D and S2A). Given that cancer stem cells (CSCs) possess self-renewal capacity—a property for which tumorsphere formation serves as a functional indicator [33], we first confirmed that tumorspheres from SKOV3, ES2, OVCAR3, and ID8 OC cells exhibited significantly elevated expression of the stemness-related genes ALDH1A1, POU5F1, KLF4, and CD44 compared to their monolayer counterparts (Fig. S2B). To functionally assess the impact of Prexasertib on this self-renewal capability, we conducted tumorsphere formation assays. The results demonstrated that Prexasertib treatment effectively reduced both the number and size of the tumorspheres (Fig. 2E, F, S2C and S2D). An in vitro limiting dilution assay revealed that Prexasertib significantly impaired OC cells’ self-renewal (Fig.S2E). ALDH+ cells possess stem cell-like properties [34]. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a substantial decrease in the ALDH+ cell population following Prexasertib treatment compared to DMSO treatment (Fig. 2G, H). Moreover, we observed that treatment with Prexasertib notably decreased the percentage of CD133+ cells, another known CSC marker, in both SKOV3 and ES2 cells (Fig. S2F and S2G). CHK1 inhibitor elevates PD-L1 expression in vitro and in vivo

Increased PD-L1 expression in tumor cells has been evidenced to promote tumor cell immune escape through the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling axis [35]. To investigate whether CHK1 inhibition modulates this immune checkpoint, we treated monolayer SKOV3, ES2, OVCAR3 and ID8 cells with increasing concentrations of Prexasertib (0–0.5 μM). Both qRT-PCR and Western blotting revealed a dose-dependent upregulation of PD-L1 at mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3A, B and S3A). Flow cytometric analysis further confirmed a significant concentration-dependent increase in cell surface PD-L1 expression across all the cell lines (Fig. 3C, D and S3B–S3D). Consistent with the findings in monolayer cells, flow cytometric analysis further demonstrated that Prexasertib treatment also upregulates the surface expression of PD-L1 on SKOV3, ES2, OVCAR3, and ID8 spheres (Fig. S3E and S3F). It has been reported that membrane-bound PD-L1 can mediate immune suppression by interacting with T cells [36]. To further characterize the in vivo effects of CHK1 inhibition on PD-L1 expression, we performed IHC analysis on subcutaneous tumors derived from vehicle- and Prexasertib-treated mice. The results indicated that, compared with the vehicle group, PD-L1 expression was significantly increased in the Prexasertib group (Fig. 3E, F). Discussion

Recent studies indicates that CSCs play a crucial role in chemotherapy resistance, recurrence, and metastasis across various cancer types. Therefore, identifying novel targets that regulate tumor stemness is the foundation for developing effective therapies and ultimately improving cancer treatment outcomes. This study provides the first demonstration that CHK1 serves as a critical regulatory target of cancer stemness in OC. We observed significant upregulation of CHK1 expression in OC tumorspheres, where it plays an essential role in maintaining stemness characteristics, including spheroid-forming capacity and ALDH activity. These findings are consistent with previous reports documenting CHK1 overexpression in CSCs across multiple malignancies [12, 29].

As a pivotal kinase in the DDR pathway, CHK1 plays critical roles in both DNA damage repair and cell cycle regulation, particularly during S phase and G2/M phase [37]. Studies have shown that CHK1 promotes tumor cell survival and proliferation by repairing therapy-induced DNA damage, making it an important therapeutic target [38]. For instance, Prexasertib attenuates NOTCH signaling and sensitizes head and neck squamous cell carcinoma to cisplatin and radiation both in vitro and in vivo, supporting the therapeutic potential of combination strategies [39]. Similarly, the CHK1 inhibitor MK-8776 enhances radiosensitivity in triple-negative breast cancer through autophagy suppression, highlighting its utility as a radiosensitizer [40]. Notably, our study extends the potential value of CHK1 inhibitors-Prexasertib not only potentiates DNA-damaging therapies such as PARP inhibitors and platinum-based agents [41, 42], but also directly targets the tumor stemness. Building upon the recent research, it provided preliminary evidence of the treatment effect on cell stemness, which was only suggested by the decreased fraction of ALDH-positive cells [43]. In our study, we provided rather solid evidence of reduced stemness markers and self-renewal property of tumor cells undergoing Prexasertib treatment. The major novelty of our study lies in the discovery of the paradox effect of Prexasertib on CSCs, diminishing their stemness and enhancing their immune escape through elevating PD-L1 expression, which provides a rationale for combining blockades of CHK1 and PD-L1 as a strategy targeting CSCs.

CHK1 inhibitors not only suppress tumor stemness but also promote the accumulation of somatic mutations and intracellular DNA fragments, leading to increased neoantigen generation and enhanced anti-tumor immune responses [14]. Previous studies using Western blot, co-immunoprecipitation, and immunofluorescence have demonstrated that Prexasertib activates STING, which in turn enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy [43]. Our work revealed a different mechanism involving PD-L1 immune check point with a focus on CSCs. Through flow cytometry, tumor-sphere formation, and syngeneic models, we directly link CHK1 inhibition to the ablation of ovarian CSCs and the remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. Based on established literature, we propose that this effect is mediated by compromised DNA damage repair upon CHK1 inhibition. The subsequent accumulation of DNA damage activates the cGAS-STING pathway, which in turn stimulates the IRF1/STAT1 transcription factors through a STING-dependent type I interferon signaling cascade, ultimately driving the change of tumor immune microenvironment [25].

Our preclinical study and the clinical trial by Do et al. mutually corroborate and provide important complementary insights [44]. The clinical trial demonstrated the preliminary efficacy and manageable safety of Prexasertib combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy in CCNE1-amplified OC. Our research elucidates a novel mechanism underlying the combination of CHK1 inhibition and PD-L1 blockade, which involves targeting tumor stemness and restoring anti-tumor immunity, highlighting its significant translational potential. In immunocompetent mouse models, the combined treatment resulted in significantly greater tumor suppression than either monotherapy and increased the infiltration of CD8+GZMB+ T cells and CD8+CCR7+ T cells into tumor tissues. These findings provide a strong theoretical foundation for clinical translation and suggest that future clinical trials should specifically focus on evaluating the therapy’s impact on CSCs, different subtypes of T-cell infiltration, and long-term survival outcomes.
