Mental well-being, occupational stress, and caring behaviours among paediatric and psychiatric nurses in Slovenia
Authors: Leona Cilar Budler, Tamara Trajbarič, Klavdija Čuček Trifkovič
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNursing is a demanding profession associated with elevated levels of occupational stress, which can negatively affect nurses’ mental well-being, job satisfaction, and capacity for caring behaviour. Paediatric and psychiatric nurses face distinct stressors related to their work environments and patient populations. This study aimed to compare occupational stress, mental well-being, and caring behaviours among nurses working in paediatric and psychiatric settings in Slovenia, where limited research has examined how work environment characteristics influence nurses’ psychological well-being.MethodsA cross-sectional single-centre study using convenience sampling was conducted between September 2024 and February 2025 in a public teaching hospital in Slovenia. A total of 105 nurses (66 psychiatric, 39 paediatric) completed a structured questionnaire including the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale, Nurses’ Stress Scale, and Caring Dimensions Inventory. Due to non-normal data distribution, non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis) were used alongside linear regression analysis to examine group differences and associations between variables. The study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were found in stress levels or caring behaviours between paediatric and psychiatric nurses. However, psychiatric nurses reported higher mental well-being, though the difference was non-significant (p = 0.050). Significant associations were observed between mental well-being and self-rated general health (p = 0.008), education level (p = 0.011), and marital status (p = 0.048). Linear regression indicated that more years of work experience were associated with slightly lower stress levels (p = 0.013), although the explained variance was modest (R2 = 0.059).ConclusionsThe findings suggest that individual and contextual factors, such as perceived health, education, and marital status, may have a stronger influence on nurses’ mental well-being than the specific clinical setting. While stress and caring behaviours were relatively stable across groups, targeted strategies to support nurses’ psychological health should consider broader personal and occupational characteristics. Understanding how demographic and psychosocial variables affect nurses’ mental well-being is essential for developing effective interventions. Promoting resilience and mental health in nursing requires a multidimensional approach that addresses both workplace factors and individual circumstances.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Nursing is a demanding profession that often involves high levels of stress due to various factors, such as heavy workloads, staffing shortages, emotional labour, and exposure to challenging patient situations. Workplace stress can significantly impact nurses’ quality of life, mental well-being, and their ability to provide caring behaviour. Understanding stress and its effects on nurses across different fields, such as paediatric and psychiatric nursing, is essential for developing targeted interventions and support systems [1]. Work-related stress refers to an employee’s response to workplace demands that are perceived as mentally and physically challenging [2]. It has been shown to negatively impact job satisfaction [2], contribute to burnout, and reduce organisational commitment [3].

In paediatric nursing, caring for children and adolescents with complex medical needs can be emotionally exhausting. Paediatric nurses frequently navigate sensitive family dynamics, manage the suffering of young patients, and handle uncertainty regarding their conditions. This unique combination of responsibilities can lead to increased levels of stress, affecting their mental well-being [4]. Recent research additionally highlights that paediatric nurses frequently experience burnout, compassion fatigue, and moral distress due to prolonged exposure to critically ill children and demanding family interactions, which may further compromise their mental well-being and professional functioning [5]. Similarly, evidence suggests that paediatric nurses often report moderate to high occupational stress and reduced resilience, which may negatively affect both staff mental well-being and patient safety [6]. Similarly, psychiatric nursing presents its own set of challenges. Nurses in psychiatric settings provide care for individuals with mental health disorders, often managing crisis situations, addressing behavioural challenges, and delivering therapeutic support. The unpredictable nature of psychiatric care, combined with the stigma associated with mental illness, can introduce additional stressors that impact the mental well-being and job satisfaction of psychiatric nurses. Research in mental health nursing further shows that exposure to workplace stressors is associated with higher levels of psychological distress and may negatively influence nurses’ therapeutic engagement and caring behaviours [7, 8]). Moreover, psychological resilience has been identified as an important protective factor that may buffer the negative effects of occupational stress on mental wellbeing among psychiatric nurses [9].

A comparative analysis of paediatric and psychiatric nurses offers valuable insights into how different work environments and patient populations influence work-related stress and its consequences. By examining factors such as workload, emotional demands, coping mechanisms, and organisational support, we can identify patterns and disparities that inform targeted interventions to reduce stress and improve the mental well-being of nurses across all specialisations. Previous studies suggest that organisational support and resilience-focused interventions may play an important role in strengthening nurses’ mental well-being and reducing the negative effects of occupational stress [6].

Furthermore, understanding how workplace stress affects nurses’ caring behaviour is crucial for maintaining high-quality patient care. Research suggests that elevated stress levels may compromise nurses’ ability to demonstrate empathy, communicate effectively, and provide holistic patient care. Empirical studies in mental health nursing confirm that higher workplace stress is associated with lower mental well-being and reduced caring behaviours, indicating that nurses’ mental well-being represents an important prerequisite for maintaining therapeutic relationships and quality nursing care [10]. By investigating the impact of stress on caring behaviour among paediatric and psychiatric nurses, we can identify areas for improvement and implement strategies to foster a supportive work environment that benefits both nurses’ mental well-being and patient outcomes.

This study aims to assess work-related stress, quality of life, mental well-being, and caring behaviour among nurses employed in paediatric and psychiatric settings. By synthesising existing literature and conducting comparative analyses, we aim to deepen understanding of the unique challenges nurses face in these fields and propose recommendations to strengthen support systems and enhance resilience in nursing practice.

Theoretical framework

This study is conceptually grounded in the biopsychosocial model and ecological systems theory, which together provide a comprehensive framework for understanding the complex interaction between occupational stress, mental well-being, and caring behaviours in nursing practice. These perspectives emphasise that mental health outcomes are shaped by the interaction of individual, interpersonal, organisational, and broader contextual factors rather than by single determinants. The biopsychosocial model proposes that mental well-being is influenced by the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors [11]. Within the nursing context, biological factors may include fatigue and physical workload, psychological factors include coping strategies and resilience, and social factors include workplace relationships, organisational support, and work environment characteristics. This multidimensional perspective is particularly relevant in nursing research, as occupational stress and well-being are rarely determined by a single factor but rather emerge from the interaction between personal characteristics and workplace demands [7]. In parallel, ecological systems theory provides an additional explanatory framework by conceptualising nurses’ experiences within multiple nested systems, ranging from individual characteristics to organisational and societal influences [12]. At the microsystem level, factors such as individual resilience, professional experience, and perceived health may influence mental well-being. At the mesosystem level, workplace relationships and team dynamics may shape stress experiences and caring behaviours. At the exosystem level, organisational policies, staffing levels, and workload distribution may indirectly influence nurses’ psychological functioning. Finally, the macrosystem includes broader healthcare system expectations and professional norms that shape the nursing role.

Recent research using participatory and ecological approaches has further highlighted the importance of understanding mental health within real-world contexts. For example, studies using Online Photovoice methodology have demonstrated that professional well-being is shaped by structural facilitators and barriers within educational and organisational environments [13, 14]. Similarly, research based on community-based participatory research (CBPR) approaches emphasises that mental well-being should be understood within the broader social and organisational context in which individuals operate [15, 16]. Although the present study used a quantitative design, these perspectives support the interpretation that nurses’ well-being is influenced by multiple contextual and demographic factors rather than solely by clinical specialty.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design to examine nurses working in paediatric and psychiatric settings. Data collection was conducted using a structured survey questionnaire comprising several validated instruments described below.

Participant and setting

A convenience sampling approach was used to recruit nurses employed in paediatric and psychiatric units at a public university clinical centre in Slovenia. This hospital was selected based on accessibility and the institution’s willingness to participate, enabling the inclusion of nurses from both paediatric and psychiatric departments within the same organisational environment. Eligible participants were registered nurses employed in paediatric or psychiatric units during the study period. The inclusion criteria were: (1) employment in a paediatric or psychiatric clinical setting, (2) active involvement in direct patient care, and (3) willingness to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included nurses in managerial positions without direct patient contact, nurses on long-term leave during the data collection period, and incomplete questionnaire responses. Participation was voluntary. All eligible nurses in the selected departments were invited to participate through internal communication channels. Nurses who agreed to participate provided informed consent prior to data collection. The final sample size was determined by the number of nurses available and willing to participate during the data collection period, which is consistent with the principles of convenience sampling. The study was conducted between September 2024 and February 2025.

Measurements

Three standardised instruments were used to assess the main study variables: mental well-being, occupational stress, and caring behaviours. In addition, a demographic questionnaire was used to collect background characteristics. Internal consistency was assessed for each scale in the current sample using Cronbach’s alpha.

Mental well-being was assessed using the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (WEMWBS), developed to measure positive mental health and psychological functioning over the previous two weeks [17]. The scale consists of 14 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“none of the time”) to 5 (“all of the time”). Total scores range from 14 to 70, with higher scores indicating better mental well-being. In this study, a validated Slovenian translation [18] was used, and permission for use was registered in accordance with the official guidelines. Based on previous recommendations, scores may be interpreted as indicating low (14–41), moderate (42–59), or high (60 and above) mental well-being [19]. In the present sample, the internal consistency of the WEMWBS was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.77).

Occupational stress was assessed using the Nurses’ Stress Scale (NSS) developed by Gray-Toft and Anderson [20]. This instrument measures the frequency of stressors encountered in nursing practice. It contains 34 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (“never”) to 3 (“very often”). Total scores range from 0 to 102, with higher scores indicating greater occupational stress. In the present sample, the internal consistency of the NSS was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.94).

Caring behaviours were assessed using the Caring Dimensions Inventory (CDI-25) [21]. This instrument measures nurses’ perceptions of caring in professional practice. It includes 25 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Higher total scores indicate stronger endorsement of caring behaviours and caring-related professional values. The instrument covers several dimensions of caring, including psychosocial, physical-technical, professional, unnecessary, and inappropriate aspects. Items 3 and 16 are reverse-scored. In the present sample, the internal consistency of the CDI-25 was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.94).

The demographic questionnaire was used to collect participants’ personal and professional characteristics. These variables included gender, age, years of professional experience, work setting (pediatrics/psychiatry), education level, marital status, parental status (yes/no), and self-rated general health (very good, good, satisfied, bad). These variables were included to describe the sample and to explore their associations with mental well-being, occupational stress, and caring behaviours.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Version 31. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were used to summarise the demographic and professional characteristics of the sample and the main study variables. Mental well-being (WEMWBS total score), occupational stress (NSS total score), and caring behaviours (CDI-25 total score) were treated as dependent variables. Independent variables included work setting (paediatric/psychiatric), years of work experience, education level, marital status, and self-rated general health. Before inferential analysis, the distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Because some variables deviated from normality, non-parametric tests were used for group comparisons. Differences between two independent groups were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test, while differences across multiple groups were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. A simple linear regression analysis was additionally conducted to examine whether years of work experience predicted stress levels. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Study procedure

The study followed a structured recruitment and data collection process. First, ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the University of Maribor, Faculty of Health Sciences. After that, institutional permission was obtained from the nursing management service of the participating hospital, followed by approval from the heads of the paediatric and psychiatric departments. Data were collected using printed questionnaires. The questionnaires were prepared in paper form and delivered to the ward heads, who distributed them to eligible nurses in their respective units. Nurses were approached through their departments and invited to participate voluntarily. Before completing the questionnaire, participants were informed of the study’s purpose, the anonymity of their participation, and their right to decline or withdraw without consequences. Completed questionnaires were returned anonymously in accordance with the hospital’s agreed procedure. Data collection took place between September 2024 and February 2025. After data collection, the data analysis phase began, during which statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Version 31 (released in June 2025), incorporating both descriptive and inferential statistics to interpret the findings. Finally, the results were analysed, interpreted, and compiled into a comprehensive report that included key findings, graphical representations, and relevant implications.

Ethical considerations

The ethical committee of the University of Maribor, Faculty of Health Sciences (Maribor, Slovenia), gave a positive opinion on the proposed research (038/2024/1486–2/900). Prior to participation, all potential respondents were provided with detailed written information about the study’s purpose, objectives, and voluntary nature. They were explicitly informed that their participation was entirely optional and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. Informed consent was implied through the completion and return of the questionnaire. No personal identifiers (e.g., names, contact details, or IP addresses) were collected, and no sensitive personal data were recorded, ensuring participants’ complete anonymity. The questionnaire was designed so that no responses could be traced back to individual participants, and all data were handled with strict confidentiality. Participants were informed that the data would be used solely for research and presented in aggregated form, so that individual responses could not be identified. The researchers adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and followed national regulations and institutional guidelines for conducting research involving human participants. No financial or material incentives were provided.

The study included 105 participants: 66 psychiatric nurses and 39 paediatric nurses. Among them, 73 (69.5%) were male, and 32 (30.5%) were female, with the majority holding either a high school diploma (51.4%) or a registered nurse (RN) qualification (41.9%). A smaller proportion of participants (6.7%) completed a master’s degree.

Discussion

Nurses working in psychiatry had higher mean ranks for mental well-being than paediatric nurses, although the difference was only borderline statistically significant (p = 0.050). Existing literature indicates a complex interplay between occupational stress and mental health for psychiatric nurses, which may suggest that the psychiatric environment fosters resilience under certain conditions. However, it should be noted that psychiatric nurses have been found to experience lower mental well-being compared to other nursing populations [9]. Oates et al. [22] reported that mental well-being among psychiatric nurses was generally lower than anticipated, possibly reflecting stigma and professional challenges faced by psychiatric nurses. The emotional challenges in psychiatric care can also lead to a sense of accomplishment and duty, contributing to resilience and mental well-being [10].

Our findings indicated statistically significant overall differences in mental well-being according to self-rated general health (p = 0.008), education level (p = 0.011), and marital status (p = 0.048). However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. First, higher WEMWBS scores indicate better mental well-being, and the direction of the observed mean-rank differences must therefore be interpreted accordingly. Second, some subgroup sizes were very small, particularly for participants with a master’s degree (n = 7), widowed participants (n = 2), and those in the “other” marital-status category (n = 2). Third, the Kruskal–Wallis test indicates an overall difference across groups but does not identify which specific groups differ from one another in the absence of post-hoc testing. Taken together, these findings suggest that mental well-being may vary across individual and social characteristics, but firm subgroup-specific conclusions should be avoided. Consistent with these findings, Ersin et al. [23] documented that nurses’ perceptions of their general health significantly correlated with their mental well-being during the COVID-19 pandemic, noting that those who perceived their health negatively exhibited lower levels of mental well-being and social support. This supports the notion that self-rated health serves as a critical predictor of mental health outcomes in nursing populations. Furthermore, research indicates that self-efficacy and a sense of social connectedness can enhance mental well-being in nursing roles, reinforcing the critical connection between personal health evaluations and psychological outcomes, though direct support from Zhou et al. [24] is not yet established. Moreover, the relationship between educational attainment and mental well-being is well-documented in the existing literature. According to Chen et al. [9], higher education in nursing may be linked to increased expectations and stressors in professional roles, leading to a paradoxical effect in which greater educational achievement correlates with lower mental well-being. This phenomenon can be attributed to the increased responsibilities and pressures that often accompany advanced nursing degrees. Findings also highlight that mental well-being in nurses is significantly influenced by education, emphasising that the quality of care and patient support depend heavily on the mental health of the providers [25]. In terms of marital status, our study aligns with the findings of Kalpakjian et al. [26], which emphasise that marital transitions and stability are integral to overall well-being. Divorced or widowed individuals generally experience heightened levels of stress and emotional distress, adversely affecting their mental health. This supports our observation that nurses with such marital statuses reported lower mental well-being compared to their unmarried counterparts, corroborating broader literature indicating the significant impact of social support systems and relational dynamics on mental health outcomes, although Hadi and Mohammed [27] also linked marital status and quality of life, which further underpins these assertions.

Our study indicated no significant differences in stress levels between psychiatric and paediatric nurses. Work experience was a significant predictor of stress (p = 0.013): more years of experience were associated with lower stress levels (β = −0.357). However, the explained variance was low (R2 = 0.059). Complementary studies have provided insights that both affirm and expand upon our findings. For instance, Ashraf et al. [28] reported that early-career nurses, specifically those with less than 6 years of experience, reported higher stress levels, with female nurses 1.5 times more likely to experience stress than their male counterparts. Furthermore, Duasin et al. [29] focused on job stress connected to working conditions, suggesting that variations in the work environment significantly influence nurses’ stress experiences. This emphasises that factors such as workplace dynamics, support systems, and organisational culture play critical roles in shaping the stress levels of nursing professionals. Moreover, Pekurinen et al. [30] examined stress levels concerning patient aggression in both psychiatric and non-psychiatric settings. Their findings suggested that psychiatric nurses experience specific stressors, such as dealing with aggressive patients, which may elevate their stress levels compared to paediatric nurses. Additionally, Chen et al. [9] noted that high levels of occupational stress in psychiatric nurses could stem from the complexity of the patient populations they serve and the challenging work environments typical in psychiatric settings, which can adversely affect their overall mental health. This aligns with our observations regarding the potential influences of experience but also highlights the unique challenges faced in psychiatric nursing that may not fully correlate with years of experience alone. Our findings resonate with Kassa’s [31] work, which reported that greater work experience among nurses was associated with lower stress levels, reinforcing the notion that familiarity with the role can mitigate immediate stressors in healthcare environments.

No statistically significant differences in caring behaviour scores were found across any of the analysed variables. However, trends suggested that perceived caring may increase with education level and better self-rated health, although these associations were not significant. This finding aligns with research conducted by Ben Natan et al. [32], who examined the relationship between education and caring behaviours in nursing students, indicating that those with higher academic qualifications tend to exhibit more pronounced caring behaviours. Their study emphasised that educational experiences that emphasise empathy and caring behaviour can enhance nursing students’ understanding and practice of caring behaviours. The relationship between self-rated health and professionalism in nursing is supported by numerous studies showing that nurses’ perceptions of their own health significantly influence their engagement and empathy in patient care. Research by Hensel [33], demonstrates that caring is a key aspect of nurses’ professional self-concept. This suggests that nurses who rate their health positively are more likely to exhibit positive, empathetic behaviours toward patients. Furthermore, a systematic review by Juanamasta et al. [34] supports this claim, asserting that nurses with robust educational backgrounds tend to report higher levels of caring behaviours. This finding emphasises the importance of continuous education and training in nursing, which are significant in nurturing skills that contribute to caring behaviour. In examining potential variance outcomes across different demographics, the research by Duarte et al. [35] is particularly illuminating. Although statistical differences may not always be present, qualitative assessments frequently indicate a perceived increase in caring behaviours, correlated with both educational attainment and mental well-being. This aligns with findings in the literature that link professional self-concept to clinical performance, where a well-developed self-concept supports higher levels of empathy and care, thereby enhancing overall patient interactions [34, 35].

Implications

The findings of this study have several important implications for nursing practice, education, and research. From a clinical perspective, the results suggest that nurses’ mental well-being may be influenced more by personal and contextual factors than by the specific clinical setting. This highlights the importance of implementing workplace mental health promotion programs that address stress management, resilience, and mental health support across all nursing departments rather than focusing only on high-risk specialities. From an educational perspective, the association between education level and mental well-being suggests the need to better prepare nurses for the psychological demands associated with advanced professional roles. Educational programs may therefore benefit from incorporating content related to coping strategies, emotional regulation, and mental health self-care. From an organisational perspective, healthcare administrators should consider developing supportive work environments that promote work–life balance, mentorship, and access to mental health resources. Since work experience was associated with lower stress levels, structured mentorship programs could help less experienced nurses adapt more effectively to workplace demands. Finally, the study also has implications for future research. Further studies should explore organisational and psychosocial factors influencing nurses’ mental well-being using longitudinal and mixed-method approaches. Expanding research to multiple institutions and healthcare systems could further strengthen the understanding of how workplace environments influence nurse well-being and caring behaviours.

Study limitations

While this study offers valuable insights into the mental well-being, stress levels, and caring behaviours of nurses working in paediatric and psychiatric settings, several limitations must be acknowledged to contextualise the findings and inform future research. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to infer causality between variables. For instance, although associations between general health, marital status, and mental well-being were identified, it is not possible to determine the direction or temporal order of these relationships. Moreover, the study was conducted at a single teaching hospital in Slovenia, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although the total sample size (n = 105) was adequate for non-parametric testing, some subgroups were very small, including nurses with a master’s degree (n = 7), widowed participants (n = 2), and those in the “Other” marital status category (n = 2). Findings related to these categories should therefore be interpreted with caution, as results based on such small subgroup sizes are statistically unstable and may not reliably reflect broader patterns. The limited representation of these groups reduced the statistical power to detect meaningful differences and may have increased variability in subgroup analyses. All data were collected through self-report questionnaires, which may be subject to several biases, including social desirability bias and recall bias. Several scales (especially WEMWBS and CDI) violated the normality assumption, requiring nonparametric tests that are less powerful than their parametric counterparts. This may have reduced the ability to detect smaller, yet potentially meaningful, differences between groups. The study relied solely on quantitative instruments, limiting its understanding of the personal and contextual factors influencing nurses’ experiences. Finally, participation was voluntary, raising the possibility of selection bias. Nurses experiencing higher stress or lower mental well-being might have been either more or less likely to participate, potentially skewing the results. These limitations suggest the need for more comprehensive, mixed-method, and longitudinal research to better understand the complex interplay between work-related stress, mental health, and caring behaviour among nurses. Future studies should also consider broader institutional and psychosocial variables to develop more targeted and effective interventions.

Conclusion

This study compared mental well-being, occupational stress, and caring behaviours among paediatric and psychiatric nurses and found no significant differences between the two clinical settings. Instead, individual factors such as self-rated health, education level, and marital status were more strongly associated with mental well-being. Work experience showed a small but significant negative association with stress levels. These findings highlight the importance of considering both personal and occupational factors when developing strategies to support nurses’ mental health. Interventions aimed at improving psychological well-being should therefore address not only workplace conditions but also individual support needs. Future research should further explore these relationships in larger and more diverse samples and include qualitative approaches to better understand nurses’ experiences.

Future research could benefit from complementing quantitative approaches with qualitative and participatory methodologies to better understand nurses lived experiences of occupational stress and mental well-being. Approaches such as Online Photovoice (OPV), which allows participants to document and reflect on their experiences through visual narratives, may provide deeper insights into the contextual factors shaping nurses’ well-being [8, 12]. Similarly, Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) approaches may help develop more contextually grounded interventions by actively involving nurses in the research process and in the development of support strategies [15, 16]. In addition, qualitative approaches such as Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis could further help capture the subjective experiences, meanings, and coping processes associated with nursing work. Combining quantitative and qualitative approaches may therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of the complex relationship between occupational stress, mental well-being, and caring behaviours and support the development of more effective organisational and psychological support interventions.
