Venture-Backed Maternal Health Startups and the Maternal Health Crisis
Authors: Madeline F. Perry, Kristan Scott, Diana Montoya-Williams, Leah Blum, Sindhu Srinivas, David Grande, Scott A. Lorch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionHow many venture capital-backed maternal health startups exist in the US and what are their characteristics and purpose?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 172 venture capital–backed maternal health startups identified from 2014 to 2022, $977.5 million was collectively raised during the study period and the health care category raising the most capital was maternal and fetal health diagnostics ($520.1 million). The most common company type was virtual or hybrid wraparound pregnancy care and few startups mentioned health equity or maternal mortality or accepted Medicaid.MeaningResults of this study suggest that venture capital–backed startups are filling gaps in pregnancy care delivery and while startups have the potential to facilitate needed innovation, few focus on low-income populations or health equity.

Introduction

In the US, the maternal mortality rate is the highest rate among other high-income countries, and Black birthing people are 2.6 times more likely than White birthing people to die of pregnancy-related causes.1 Adverse pregnancy outcomes in the US, such as hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, preterm birth, and low birthweight, are increasing.2 These adverse pregnancy outcomes increase the risk for future pregnancy complications and chronic health conditions.2 Beyond birth outcomes, 1 in 5 birthing people reports experiencing mistreatment and nearly a third report discrimination during pregnancy care, with higher rates reported among racially minoritized birthing people and people with low incomes.3,4,5 In spite of worsening pregnancy outcomes and experiences, there have been minimal changes in prenatal care for decades.6

In the setting of the current state of maternal health in the US, there has been a heightened focus on pregnancy and postpartum care and experiences. Maternal health has been the focus of national news media, the Surgeon General’s Call to Action, and White House reports.7,8,9 Along with media attention, maternal health has had more financial investment from government sources. The Biden-Harris administration allocated over $558 million to improve maternal health and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention announced $118.5 million to develop the infrastructure in states to better identify and prevent pregnancy-related deaths.10

Alongside this media and governmental focus on maternal health, there has also been private sector investment in women’s health, with many firms increasing their focus on maternal health.11,12,13,14,15 While the driving force for these investments, such as commitment to public health, recognized consumer interest, perceived investment opportunity, may be different for each firm, this focus to some extent is likely driven by perceived market opportunity.13,14,15 Investment in women’s health by venture capital (VC) has not been rigorously evaluated. VC is a form of private sector investment. In VC, a firm provides capital to an early or expanding company, often termed a startup, with an innovative idea, product, or service that has potential for growth. These investments are higher risk; many companies receiving VC funds will fail and it may take over 10 years for a firm to see a return on investment.16 Yet VC firms are substantial sources of innovation, research, and development.16,17

Research on the scope and impact of VC in health care needs to be expanded. Several studies have described the scope of VC-backed companies in cardiology, oncology, ophthalmology, and orthopedic surgery among other specialties.17,18,19,20 The trends in investment vary, with cardiology and oncology outraising other specialties on the order of billions.17,18 Pharmaceuticals and devices have been a mainstay of VC investment in health care, but in the past 5 years, there have been notable increases in digital health funding.17 There is limited peer-reviewed research that has used financial databases to perform a descriptive analysis of the current state of VC-backed maternal health companies. While a number of financial and consulting firms have evaluated the landscape of maternal health and women’s health, their goal typically is to demonstrate investment opportunities in FemTech, a consumer category including tech-driven products, services, and software targeted toward addressing women's health and wellness.11,21 Therefore, we aimed to describe the landscape of VC-backed maternal health startups, how these companies compare with VC investment in other specialties, and whether maternal health startups are addressing recognized problems in maternal health.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional content analysis of VC-backed pregnancy startups. This observational study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline and was deemed exempt from review by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board as it utilizes publicly available, nonclinical data. We used financial databases Pitchbook (MorningStar Inc) and Crunchbase (Crunchbase Inc) to identify pregnancy startups and extract descriptive company data. Company websites were used for content analysis. Companies were eligible for inclusion if they were headquartered in the US, were founded between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2022, and were focused mainly on maternal health (including both pregnancy and postpartum health). Companies founded in 2023 and 2024 were excluded because of concern for newly developed companies not being identifiable due to stealth mode (eMethods in Supplement 1). Data were analyzed March to June 2025.

We defined a maternal health startup as a company receiving VC funds whose focus is on the health of the birthing person during the pregnancy, delivery, and/or postpartum period. We excluded non–health care companies, companies focused on the prepregnancy period, companies focused on neonatal or infant health, companies using technology from pregnancy genetics screening to diagnose cancer in nonpregnant populations, companies whose target population was people outside of the US, and companies where maternal health was only one of many services provided (eMethods in Supplement 1). Our inclusion and exclusion criteria are developed based on Cwalina et al20 and included filtering our dataset to exclude non-VC, non–health care, and non-US based companies and subsequently reviewing identified VC-backed companies for false positives (companies using key search terms in a context unrelated to maternal health). Many existing studies on VC in other specialties do not describe how included companies were evaluated for false positives19,22,23,24 and others do not describe firm identification methods.25 We additionally used expert consensus opinion to develop a maternal health startup definition with inclusion and exclusion criteria. These criteria were iteratively developed by the study team, who include experts in maternal fetal medicine (M.F.P., S.S.), neonatology (K.S., D.M.W., S.A.L.), and health policy (M.F.P., K.S., D.M.W., S.S., D.G., S.A.L.). Market maps developed in the business sector were reviewed but not replicated because of a focus on FemTech (rather than only maternal health) and a lack of methodologic information.26 All companies that did not clearly meet inclusion or exclusion criteria were adjudicated by discussion with the research team.

Pitchbook and Crunchbase were selected as the data sources for this study because of their frequent use in medical literature on VC.17,19,24,27 While most prior studies typically use a single financial database, we performed searches in 2 databases to expand the total number of companies identified. Pitchbook and Crunchbase use web crawling algorithms, which review company websites, news media, regulatory data, and partnerships with investment funds to generate their database.28,29 Iterative searches were performed with keywords related to maternal health in both Pitchbook and Crunchbase until no new companies were identified. Final list of search terms is detailed in eMethods in Supplement 1. Searches were limited to companies that were currently or formerly backed by VC. Searches were performed in December 2024 and January 2025.

Descriptive characteristics of included companies were extracted from financial databases and included year founded, total capital raised in US dollars, number and financial size of deals, last funding type, and headquarter location. We categorized the last funding round that a company went through as preseed/seed (including preseed, seed, accelerator, incubator, and angel funding), early venture (including series A and series B rounds), late venture (including series C and subsequent series), other (all other types of funding including buyout, convertible note, corporate round, debt, debt financing, debt refinancing, equity crowdfunding, grant, mezzanine, nonequity assistance, private equity, product crowdfunding, secondary transaction, series unknown).28 Companies that had exited, meaning they had an initial public offering or merger and acquisition, were identified but not included in the primary analysis because they were post VC, as is consistent with existing literature evaluating VC in health care (eMethods in Supplement 1).19,20,22,23 Headquarter location was defined by state of headquarters and grouped by US census region categories: Northeast, South, Midwest, West.

Content analysis was performed with included companies based on publicly available data through May 2025. Through analysis of company website and financial database summaries, the following variables were analyzed: operating status, financial model, mention of health equity, mention of maternal mortality, coverage by insurance, coverage by Medicaid, health care category of company. With the exception of health care category, variables were selected a priori and deductively defined by the lead author (M.F.P.) and the second coder (K.S.). Health care category was deductively defined based on industry literature and inductively developed in an iterative fashion during the process of coding.26 Both the first and second author coded 55 companies and had a high interrater reliability. The remainder of the companies were coded by the first author. Results of content analysis are reported for companies that are actively in business as limited data exists for companies that are inactive or that have an unknown operating status.

Companies were defined as active, inactive, or unknown operative status. A company was determined to be active if it was listed as active on either Pitchbook or Crunchbase and had a functioning website. A company was inactive if it met 2 of the following criteria: listed as closed on Pitchbook, listed as closed on Crunchbase, no website, or news article/social media post stating company is closed. Companies that were not defined as active or inactive were unknown. Financial model was defined as business to business, business to consumer, both business to business and business to consumer, or not applicable when companies were not yet selling their product or service (eMethods in Supplement 1). Mention of health equity was a yes/no variable where “yes” was defined as having any mention of health equity, disparities, unequal rates of maternal morbidity and/or mortality, or the experiences of minoritized or medical marginalized populations in their public facing websites. Mention of maternal mortality was a yes/no variable where “yes” was defined as any mention of maternal mortality in the US in their public facing websites. Covered by insurance was coded as one of the following options: not applicable if the company was not providing a product or service that would reasonably be covered by insurance (eg, an electronic health record platform) or if a company’s product or service was not yet available for purchase, “yes” if the product or service was covered by insurance, “no” if the product or service was not covered by insurance but could reasonably be covered by insurance, and unknown if it was not clear if the product or service is covered by insurance. Covered by Medicaid was similarly coded as not applicable, yes, no, unknown. For companies with an unknown insurance or Medicaid coverage status, the company was emailed to clarify coverage. Descriptive statistics were performed using Stata/BE 18.0 (StataCorp LLC) and RStudio 2025.05.1 build 513 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

We initially identified 439 companies through searches in Pitchbook and Crunchbase. After evaluation of companies for meeting inclusion criteria and exclusion of duplicates, 183 companies were included in our analysis (Figure 1). Eight health care categories were identified: virtual and hybrid pregnancy care, virtual and hybrid wraparound care, maternal and fetal diagnostics, maternal and fetal treatment, maternity classes and community, breastfeeding technology, prenatal vitamins, and other (which included electronic health records, parental leave platforms, and other companies). Category definitions can be found in eTable 1 in Supplement 1.

During the study period, the total amount of capital raised by all companies was $977.5 million. Maternal and fetal diagnostics raised the most capital during the study period with $520.2 million raised, but this was driven by 2 companies that raised $281.2 and $90.3 million. The distribution of total capital raised per company was skewed with only 3 companies each raising more than $90 million (e Figure 1 in Supplement 1). The median capital raised per company was $0.3 million (IQR 0-2.0 million). The amount of funds raised and number of deals per year increased annually with a notable increase in funding raised for maternal health startups starting in 2019 (Figure 2; eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). After removing the 3 companies that accounted for 52.8% ($515.8 million) investments, the most substantial growth in investments was among companies classified as virtual and hybrid wraparound care, maternity classes and community, and virtual and hybrid prenatal care (Figure 3).

Most maternal health startups had preseed/seed funding as their most recent type of funding. Startups were spread throughout regions of the US, with less than 10% in the Midwest and most identified startups were actively operating (Table 1). Of actively operating companies, most advertised directly to the consumer either through a business to consumer model (40%) or a combined business to consumer and business to business model (24.8%) (Table 2). Only 17.3% of companies had any mention of health equity on their website, and only 18.1% of companies had any mention of maternal mortality on their website. Among all active companies providing a product or service reasonably covered by insurance, 46.2% were covered by any insurance and 33.8% were covered by Medicaid. Annually, there was a 63% increase in startups founded, with the greatest annual increase among virtual and hybrid wraparound care companies (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1).

Virtual and hybrid wraparound care represented the most common health care category among included companies (46 of 133 companies, 34.6%). Of these companies, 87% either advertised directly to consumers or to both consumers and businesses (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Still less than one-third of companies addressed health equity (26.1%) or maternal mortality (15.2%). Of companies reasonably covered by insurance, 53% were covered by any insurance and 39.0% were covered by Medicaid (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this content analysis using industry financial databases and startup websites, we identified 172 actively operating and currently venture-backed maternal health companies that collectively raised $977.5 million during the study period. Most companies had raised small amounts of money with the median capital raised of $0.3 million. However, a small number raised amounts in excess of $90 million. The number and total amount invested in maternal health startups is increasing annually. Many startups we identified leveraged digital technology to address pregnancy care delivery and education through virtual and hybrid wraparound care and digital pregnancy classes and community. Few companies mentioned health equity or disparities in pregnancy outcomes in the US or maternal mortality. To our knowledge, this is the one of the first studies to evaluate the role of VC-backed startups in the pregnancy sector.

The type of startup we most identified (virtual and hybrid wraparound care) and startups that were growing the fastest (virtual and hybrid wraparound care, maternity classes and community) do not represent the typical health care company in a venture fund’s portfolio. Biotech and pharmaceutical represent the largest portion of startups in health care.30 Within health tech, telehealth services are the highest funded, but health coaching and wellness, the category that pregnancy classes and community would be most aligned with, is the least funded.31 The total amount of capital raised by maternal health startups was similar to investments in other specialties. On a per-year basis, orthopedic surgery and otolaryngological devices have raised $190 and $120 million, respectively.20,24 This is in comparison with $107 million raised by maternal health startups annualized over the 9-year study period. However, maternal health startups have raised significantly less than other fields, such as cardiology which has received approximately $3.5 billion per year.32 In comparison with specialties that are more similar in the total capital raised, the rate of capital raised per year in maternal health startups continued to increase throughout the study, whereas other specialties have noted a leveling off or decrease in funding.19,20 While the most common health care category type was virtual and hybrid wraparound care, the health care category raising the most funds was maternal and fetal health diagnostics.18,33 Technology-enabled health care provision, such as virtual and hybrid pregnancy care, does not cost as much to develop as a new drug, for example, lowering the barrier for companies to enter this market.34,35,36

VC in health care has traditionally been directed toward pharmaceuticals and devices.18,33 However, in this study, we identify pregnancy care delivery, largely through startups providing virtual and hybrid wraparound care which often relies on digital health technologies, to be the most common health care category among maternal health startups. Advancing digital health in perinatal care has been a goal of patient, the public, and government, especially during the current maternal health crisis.8,9,37 Digital health has been seen as a potential lever to better the health of marginalized populations.4 Digital health companies, such as those that provide virtual wraparound and pregnancy care or connect birthing people to educational communities and classes, have features that could make high-quality health care more accessible.38 Telehealth services can help people who otherwise may not be able to seek care.38 However, although many companies we identified offered digital health service, only 46.1% of them were covered by any insurance with fewer being covered by Medicaid, which reduces their accessibility for patients with highest need.

Many birthing people, especially Black birthing individuals and people with low incomes, find that the current prenatal care model does not meet their medical and psychosocial needs.39 They report barriers to care worsened by inequities over medicalized care that does not focus on their emotional and social needs, and the desire for more touchpoints with their care team in between scheduled visits.39,40,41 They also advocate for more education both in the prenatal and postpartum period.42,43,44 We identify an increasing number of companies focused on virtual and hybrid wraparound care as well as maternity classes and community that could be developing in response to this desire for more patient-centered and comprehensive prenatal care and education. Companies providing wraparound care often focus on the time in between prenatal visits, connecting pregnant and postpartum people with additional services such as care coordinators, social workers, lactation consultants, registered dieticians, and coaches. Maternity classes and community companies aim to connect birthing people to resources for perinatal education. Maternal health startups have the opportunity to fill in gaps in prenatal and postpartum care perceived by birthing people.

Discrimination and mistreatment are commonly reported in pregnancy care and many birthing people report that they feel as if they are not heard.5,45 A reported 30% of Black, Hispanic, and multiracial people and people with low incomes disclosed that they experienced mistreatment during pregnancy.3 In this content analysis, we found that less than one-fourth of startups included any mention of disparities in perinatal health or advocated for maternal health equity. Maternal health startups have an opportunity to address inequities in perinatal care and outcomes, which have been a significant focus of public health agencies, the news media and even the federal government.8,9 Importantly, only a mention of health equity or maternal mortality is an insufficient assessment of a company’s ability to impact perinatal health outcomes. There may be companies with no mention of health equity or maternal mortality that may be doing more to improve outcomes. Consumer marketing has historically leveraged public interest in social causes such as gender equity, breast cancer, and environmentalism to bolster profitability and promote an overly positive belief about the organization’s impact on a social cause.46,47 Thus, an actual evaluation of VC-backed startup client demographics, clinical characteristics, and relevant perinatal outcomes is needed to assess for meaningful contribution to a reduction of health disparities and maternal mortality.

Strengths and Limitations

This study is novel in its objective to measure the growth and focus of VC in the maternal health sector. We include a detailed method and use 2 financial databases to identify companies rather than a single database.19,20,22,23,24,27,48,49 Most existing studies evaluating VC do not describe inclusion and exclusion criteria to account for false positives in searches. This study also includes a content analysis to assess for relevant company features. While we include a mention of health equity and maternal mortality in the content analysis, we acknowledge that only a mention of these topics does not necessarily translate to the company contributing meaningful work to reducing perinatal disparities or maternal mortality. However, in the absence of client demographics or outcomes data from startups, there are limited methods to assess meaningful focus on health equity or maternal mortality. There is no standardized research method for use of private databases for evaluation of VC in health care and therefore comparing VC investment between different specialties should be interpreted with caution. As our search for VC-backed companies was limited to Pitchbook and Crunchbase, it is possible that there were companies that were not captured, but these companies are the 2 most used financial databases for VC-backed companies. We minimize selection bias by combining searches from both Pitchbook and Crunchbase.

The use of Pitchbook and Crunchbase are biased toward companies that are actively functioning (ie, not out of business). It is possible that companies out of business, especially those in the earlier years of our study period, may not have been identified by Pitchbook and Crunchbase because there is limited information about these companies available. Our content analysis relied on startup websites which can rapidly change. The information for the variables we analyzed on company websites was up to date at the time of the content analysis, but startups can rapidly pivot their messaging and even product. We did not include companies founded between 2023 and 2025 because there was limited information for these companies with no website and limited if any information available on financial databases. Finally, additional studies are needed to measure outcomes of the interventions developed by the companies included in this analysis and whether investments in these interventions represent opportunities to address challenges and disparities in maternal health.

Conclusions

VC-backed startups are increasing in number and funding in maternal health. Results of this study suggest that these companies are filling gaps in pregnancy care delivery rather than pharmaceuticals and devices as is most common in VC. Startups have the potential to contribute needed innovation in maternal health, but few focus on low-income populations or health equity. Importantly, rigorous evaluation of startup outcomes is needed.
