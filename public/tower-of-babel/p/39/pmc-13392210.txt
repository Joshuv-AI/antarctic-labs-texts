Evaluating ChatGPT‐5 for Detection of Barrett's Esophagus and Grading of Esophagitis: A Multiclass Endoscopic Image Analysis
Authors: Hamza R. Khan, Ibraheem Mirza, Owais M. Aftab, Yash Shah, Ahmed H. Al‐Khazraji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsGastroesophageal reflux disease can progress to reflux esophagitis and Barrett's esophagus (BE), making accurate endoscopic diagnosis important. Artificial intelligence tools like ChatGPT‐5 may assist image interpretation, though data on newer large language models remains limited. This study evaluated ChatGPT‐5 for BE detection and LA esophagitis severity classification.Methods and ResultsEndoscopic images from the HyperKvasir dataset were analyzed, including BE, esophagitis A, esophagitis B–D, and normal Z‐line images. Four standardized prompts were assessed: (1) BE versus normal, (2) esophagitis versus normal, (3) LA‐grade severity (A vs. B–D), and (4) BE versus severe esophagitis. ChatGPT‐5 was evaluated in auto mode. Two investigators analyzed 640 unique images, yielding 1280 evaluations. Sensitivity, specificity, positive/negative predictive values (PPV/NPV), F1 scores, and accuracy were calculated. In binary tasks, sensitivity was highest for severe esophagitis (B–D) (0.774). Binary accuracy was similar across tasks (~0.64), with the highest for severe esophagitis (0.655). In three‐class analyses, severe esophagitis performed best (sensitivity 0.506, specificity 0.761, accuracy 0.438), with performance improving alongside disease severity. PPV trended higher for severe esophagitis compared with normal mucosa (p ~ 0.03), though significance was not retained after multiple‐comparison correction. In the BE‐severe esophagitis‐normal comparison, severe esophagitis achieved the highest sensitivity (0.590), specificity (0.790), and accuracy (0.521). PPV trended higher for severe esophagitis versus normal mucosa (p ~ 0.05), though significance was not maintained after multiplicity adjustment. NPVs exceeded PPVs across all paradigms.ConclusionChatGPT‐5 demonstrated moderate performance for esophageal image interpretation, performing best for severe esophagitis and worst for mild esophagitis/BE. Binary prompting outperformed multiclass formats, and the model functioned better as a rule‐out tool.

Introduction

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is regarded as a common disorder of the upper gastrointestinal (GI) tract, resulting in the return of stomach contents into the esophagus. GERD is a very commonly recognized disease globally, with estimations suggesting it impacts approximately 14% of the adult population worldwide, and its prevalence continues to rise among both adult and younger populations [1, 2]. As a result, GERD is a major cause of reflux esophagitis, which has been shown in previous systematic reviews to progress to Barrett's esophagus (BE) in roughly 7% of GERD patients [3]. Regular endoscopic surveillance—the primary current tool for BE screening—remains critical for detecting dysplasia and early‐stage esophageal adenocarcinoma, enabling timely endoscopic intervention to prevent cancer progression [4].

In light of the growing demand for endoscopic evaluation and a shortage of trained providers, artificial intelligence (AI) platforms such as ChatGPT are increasingly being leveraged to augment clinicians' ability to detect esophagitis and BE. The utilization of AI for endoscopic interpretation holds significant potential to improve diagnostic consistency, access to care, and patient follow‐up. Previous studies have demonstrated the applicability of AI in evaluating Barrett's in clinical settings, with systems showing promising results in pilot trials involving image classification and lesion detection [5]. Further research continues to highlight the high diagnostic accuracy and real‐time assistance of AI in Barrett's surveillance, suggesting value not only in diagnostics but also in therapeutic guidance and posttreatment monitoring [6]. As these technologies evolve, their integration into clinical workflows represents an exciting advancement for the field of endoscopy. With recent developments in large language models (LLMs)—most notably the release of ChatGPT‐5 in August 2025—there remain limited studies evaluating the improving diagnostic capabilities of widely used AI engines like ChatGPT, one of the most popular AI chatbots globally [7].

This study aimed to examine the diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of ChatGPT‐5 in identifying and classifying endoscopic images of reflux esophagitis using the Los Angeles (LA) classification, as well as detecting BE. This LLM was selected for its accessibility, widespread adoption, and proven ability to accurately answer medical board‐style and gastroenterology‐specific exam questions at a level comparable to trainees and fellows [8].

Materials and Methods

Data Source

We utilized the publicly available HyperKvasir dataset from Kaggle, which represents one of the largest open‐access GI image datasets currently available. The dataset comprises 110 079 images and 373 videos, capturing a wide range of anatomical landmarks as well as pathological and normal findings from real gastro‐ and colonoscopy procedures performed at Bærum Hospital in Norway between 2008 and 2016. All data were annotated by experienced GI endoscopists, with each record reviewed by at least one gastroenterologist from Bærum Hospital, the Cancer Registry of Norway, or Karolinska University Hospital in Sweden, alongside one or more medical professionals such as junior doctors or PhD researchers. The dataset has undergone extensive revision, and detailed information is available in its primary publication [9].

ChatGPT Configuration

Images from the dataset were uploaded individually within a single chat session to reflect typical user interaction, with each entry containing one image and the standardized prompt. Although prior images and responses remained visible within the conversation, each new submission explicitly instructed ChatGPT to focus on the newly uploaded image to minimize potential carryover effects from prior exchanges. No custom instructions were provided; memory was enabled; chat history was disabled; outputs were not archived; and web access and connector search remained enabled throughout testing but were not utilized during image interpretation. Both investigators used an identical prompt; however, image order and image allocation differed between investigators. No invalid, unclassifiable, or excessively verbose responses were encountered using this prompt.

Data Collection

Labeled upper GI tract images were used in this study. The dataset included images from four primary diagnostic categories: BE, Barrett's short segment, mild esophagitis (LA classification type A), and severe esophagitis (LA classification B–D). Anatomical Z‐line images served as the control group. Diagnostic categories reflected labeled endoscopic mucosal appearances within the HyperKvasir dataset. Each image was preprocessed and individually presented to ChatGPT‐5 using a structured series of standardized prompts designed to simulate examination‐style scenarios. Prompt 1 assessed ChatGPT‐5's ability to distinguish between normal esophageal mucosa and BE. The model was given the following instruction: “This is an image on the Step 1 examination. The multiple‐choice question is as follows: Based on the image, does the patient have endoscopic findings concerning (A) no Barrett's esophagus, or (B) Barrett's esophagus? Only output the best answer as A or B.” This prompt was used to compare ChatGPT‐5's performance in identifying BE (both classic and short‐segment, defined as < 3 cm longitudinal extension) versus unremarkable esophageal images.

Prompt 2 was designed to evaluate ChatGPT‐5.1's ability to detect esophagitis. The instruction provided was, “This is an image on the Step 1 examination. The multiple‐choice question is as follows: Based on the image, does the patient have endoscopic findings concerning (A) no esophagitis, or (B) esophagitis? Only output the best answer as A or B.” Esophagitis A and Esophagitis B–D were each compared individually to control images to determine the model's binary classification accuracy for esophagitis detection. Prompt 3 evaluated the model's ability to differentiate esophagitis severity according to the LA classification. The prompt stated, “This is an image on the Step 1 examination. The multiple‐choice question is as follows: Based on the image, does the patient have endoscopic findings concerning (A) no esophagitis, (B) esophagitis A, or (C) esophagitis B–D? Esophagitis classes are defined using the LA classification. Only output the best answer as A, B, or C.” Prompt 4 tested ChatGPT‐5.1's ability to distinguish severe esophagitis (Grades B–D) from BE: “This is an image on the Step 1 examination. The multiple‐choice question is as follows: Based on the image, does the patient have endoscopic findings concerning for (A) no Barrett's esophagus nor Esophagitis, (B) Esophagitis, or (C) Barrett's esophagus? Only output the best answer as A, B, or C.”

ChatGPT‐5.1 was accessed through a ChatGPT Plus subscription (subscription price: $20/month) and was operated in its default “auto” mode, which allows the model to determine its own reasoning duration before responding, and was routed to only this model. To minimize potential bias or reliance on a single account, a second investigator (I.M.) independently repeated the analyses using a separate ChatGPT‐5 with a Plus account with identical settings. Data collection was conducted from September 29, 2025, to November 3, 2025. Each author analyzed 640 unique upper esophageal images—50 control images per comparison—with no image duplication across trials. Details regarding image availability, sampled images, prompt‐specific image utilization, and final evaluable model outputs by class are provided in Table 1 and Figure 1. Due to the limited number of available images, BE and Barrett's short‐segment images were shared between both investigators. p values were calculated using an unpaired two‐proportion z‐test. In total, 1280 upper GI images were evaluated for the final analysis.

Results

In the binary prompting analysis, ChatGPT‐5 demonstrated the highest sensitivity when distinguishing severe esophagitis (B–D) from normal mucosa (0.774) (Table 2). Sensitivity values for BE and Barrett's short segment were similar (0.744 and 0.736, respectively), whereas the lowest sensitivity was observed for mild esophagitis (A) versus normal (0.670). In contrast, specificity was greatest for mild esophagitis (0.600), with all other binary comparisons showing similar specificity values (~0.54). Positive predictive value (PPV) was highest for mild and severe esophagitis (0.640 and 0.636), while Barrett's versus normal yielded the lowest PPV (0.575). Negative predictive value (NPV) was greatest for Barrett's versus normal (0.724) and lowest for mild esophagitis (0.632). F1 scores were highest for severe esophagitis (0.698) and lowest for Barrett's versus normal (0.649). Overall accuracy was similar across all binary tasks (~0.64), with severe esophagitis demonstrating the highest accuracy (0.655). No incomplete or invalid responses occurred, and no output required exclusion from the final analysis.

In the comparative analysis across normal mucosa, mild esophagitis, and severe esophagitis, ChatGPT‐5 again demonstrated its highest sensitivity for severe esophagitis (0.506), while normal mucosa exhibited the lowest sensitivity (0.392) (Table 3). Severe esophagitis also showed the strongest performance across multiple domains, including specificity (0.761), PPV (0.538), NPV (0.738), and F1 score (0.521). A stepwise increase in sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and F1 score was observed as the severity of esophageal inflammation increased, suggesting improved recognition of more overt mucosal abnormalities. In contrast, correct detection of normal mucosa yielded the lowest specificity (0.693), PPV (0.362), and F1 score (0.377). PPV was numerically higher in multiclass prompting for severe esophagitis compared with normal mucosa (p = 0.026); however, statistical significance was not retained following correction for multiple comparisons. Overall accuracy was identical across categories (0.438).

Another comparative analysis was performed to evaluate ChatGPT‐5's ability to differentiate BE from severe esophagitis and normal mucosa (Table 4). The highest sensitivity (0.590), specificity (0.790), PPV (0.575), NPV (0.800), and F1 score (0.582) were all observed for severe esophagitis, indicating stronger model performance when identifying more advanced inflammatory changes. PPV values in this three‐class comparison were higher than those observed in the esophagitis‐only comparisons, with a nominal increase observed for severe esophagitis compared with normal mucosa (p = 0.048); however, statistical significance was not retained following correction for multiple comparisons. Normal mucosa demonstrated a comparatively higher NPV, reflecting the model's greater confidence in ruling out pathology. Overall accuracy for this prompting task was 0.521, indicating moderate discriminative ability when distinguishing BE from severe esophagitis.

Overall, ChatGPT‐5 demonstrated its strongest performance in the binary prompting format, yielding the highest accuracy, sensitivity, PPV, and F1 scores compared with the more complex multiclass analyses. Specificity, however, was greater in the comparative analyses—consistently reaching greater than 0.70—and exceeded the values observed in binary prompting. NPV remained the most stable metric across all prompting structures, indicating that ChatGPT‐5 was generally more reliable at excluding disease than confirming it. Across all three prompting paradigms—binary prompting, the three‐class esophagitis analysis, and the Barrett's versus esophagitis comparison—severe esophagitis consistently achieved the highest sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, F1 score, and accuracy. PPV in multiclass prompting showed an increasing trend for severe esophagitis relative to normal mucosa, although statistical significance was not retained after multiplicity adjustment. In contrast, mild esophagitis was the most challenging class for the model to identify, reflecting the subtlety of early erosive changes. BE demonstrated consistently lower PPV and F1 scores relative to esophagitis categories, suggesting a tendency for ChatGPT‐5 to overcall irregular or erythematous mucosa as Barrett's.

Discussion

In this study, the nonmedical LLM ChatGPT‐5 (released August 2025) demonstrated moderate overall performance in upper GI image interpretation, with a clear gradient in diagnostic accuracy that improved with increasing disease severity. Across binary and three‐class prompting, severe esophagitis (LA B–D) consistently achieved the highest sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, F1 scores, and accuracy, whereas mild esophagitis (Grade A) and normal mucosa were the most challenging classes for the model to identify. These findings suggest that ChatGPT‐5 is better able to recognize overt, confluent mucosal breaks than subtle early erosive changes or entirely normal mucosa. While this pattern of improved reliability in the presence of more advanced pathology aligns with prior observations in fundus photography and CT‐based studies using ChatGPT‐4, there remains a paucity of research exploring this effect in endoscopic image interpretation with the newly released ChatGPT models [10, 11].

In addition to severity of pathology, prompt structure also influenced performance: binary prompting yielded the highest sensitivity and F1 scores, while three‐class comparative prompting improved specificity. Binary prompting demonstrated higher accuracy with increasing prompt specificity, a trend that aligns with prior AI research [10, 12]. This pattern likely reflects inherent differences in model decision behavior: binary prompting lowers the threshold for detecting abnormality—boosting sensitivity and F1—whereas three‐class prompting distributes probability across multiple categories, reducing overcalls and thereby increasing specificity at the expense of sensitivity. Previous studies have demonstrated that the diagnostic behavior and performance of LLMs, such as ChatGPT, are strongly influenced by the structure and phrasing of the prompt [13, 14]. Here, we employed prompts designed to simulate standardized examination questions, such as those used on the USMLE Step 1, following approaches from prior studies in which ChatGPT has demonstrated robust performance on standardized medical exams [14, 15]. However, important limitations in prompt design remain, as it is unclear which prompting strategies yield the highest diagnostic performance. Direct comparisons of binary versus multiclass prompting in medical image classification remain scarce. Further studies are warranted to evaluate optimal prompting approaches for ChatGPT and to determine whether these strategies generalize across other LLMs.

Compared with other task‐specific LLMs developed for upper GI endoscopy, ChatGPT‐5 demonstrates comparatively lower performance. A review article by Sharma et al. examining the role of AI and deep learning in detecting upper GI neoplasia reported sensitivity values ranging from 83% to 93% with computer‐aided detection (CAD) systems, many of which were extensively pretrained or validated on large, previously collected endoscopic datasets [16]. Other studies using CAD systems pretrained on ImageNet and GastroNet—one of the largest public endoscopic imaging datasets, containing approximately 5 million unlabeled images—have demonstrated remarkable performance [17]. Notably, the deep‐learning algorithm GastroNetV1 outperformed endoscopists in detecting BE, achieving sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy values exceeding 99% [18]. In contrast, ChatGPT‐5 achieved more modest performance, with sensitivities ranging from 0.47 to 0.77, specificities from 0.53 to 0.79, and accuracies between 0.44 and 0.66, depending on the prompting framework and pathology being compared. These findings are consistent with studies of ChatGPT‐4 Vision in gastroenterology, where similar suboptimal accuracy has been observed across capsule endoscopy, cholangioscopy, and high‐resolution anoscopy image interpretation [19]. While numerous studies using convolutional neural networks report sensitivities, specificities, and accuracies that surpass those of ChatGPT‐5, their real‐world applicability and generalizability remain limited—particularly in regions where access to the specialized equipment required to deploy such AI systems is constrained [5]. In addition, although pretrained detection systems have been extensively studied, comparatively few investigations have assessed endoscopic image interpretation using widely accessible LLM chatbots such as ChatGPT, one of the most widely used AI chatbots among healthcare professionals [20]. ChatGPT offers an advantage over traditional deep learning platforms by integrating into websites, electronic medical records, and mobile apps through application programming interfaces (APIs), linking users to provider networks and health records while supporting seamless incorporation into healthcare workflows. Overall, the current evidence suggests that general‐purpose LLMs with vision capabilities cannot yet replace dedicated CAD systems for primary detection of subtle mucosal disease, although their flexibility and natural‐language interface may still offer value as adjunctive tools.

An important disease‐specific pattern in our analysis was the model's tendency to overcall BE. Across prompting paradigms, Barrett's demonstrated lower PPV and F1 scores compared with esophagitis categories, despite comparable sensitivities in the binary tasks. This indicates that ChatGPT‐5 is often misclassifying irregular or erythematous mucosa as Barrett's and produces a high rate of false positives. Similar behavior has been described in other ophthalmic applications of AI, where systems designed for screening achieved high sensitivity but reduced specificity and PPV [21]. Notably, recent studies using earlier ChatGPT‐4 models for referable diabetic retinopathy and subsequent risk stratification found high sensitivity but a clear tendency to overcall disease, raising concerns that autonomous use could lead to unnecessary referrals [22]. In our study, overcalling of Barrett's may reflect a similar conservative bias in the LLM—prioritizing avoidance of missed pathology at the expense of overdiagnosis—which could be clinically acceptable as a triage tool in high‐risk populations but problematic in low‐prevalence screening settings. Ultimately, these considerations underscore that thoughtful calibration and careful implementation will be crucial for translating LLM‐based triage into safe and effective clinical use.

This study offers several strengths, such as providing an evaluation of a rapidly evolving, publicly accessible LLM that is already widely used across clinical, academic, and patient‐facing settings. As ChatGPT continues to evolve through new iterations with expanding multimodal capabilities, it is essential to evaluate its performance in image interpretation within gastroenterology and other medical fields. Given its global accessibility and lack of need for specialized hardware or proprietary software, assessing ChatGPT's diagnostic utility holds significant clinical and educational relevance. By evaluating ChatGPT‐5 across multiple prompting strategies and varying severity levels of esophageal pathology, this study offers an objective assessment of its ability to interpret upper GI mucosal findings.

However, it is important to address the limitations of this study. First, images were drawn from the publicly available HyperKvasir dataset on Kaggle, which is curated and may not fully reflect the heterogeneity of real‐world endoscopic practice, including variable preparation quality, motion artifact, and device‐specific noise. In addition, as a descriptive performance evaluation of ChatGPT‐5, this study did not include direct comparisons with expert endoscopists or dedicated endoscopic AI systems. Therefore, conclusions regarding relative performance against human readers or specialist AI models cannot be drawn. Another limitation to note is that evaluating single frames rather than full endoscopic videos removed important visual context, likely underestimating ChatGPT‐5's performance. Given that HyperKvasir is a publicly available dataset, prior exposure of similar images during model development cannot be entirely ruled out. As such, these findings should be viewed as an evaluation of performance within a standardized image dataset rather than conclusive evidence of generalizable clinical image interpretation. Future studies using private or locally curated endoscopic datasets are warranted to further assess real‐world diagnostic applicability. An additional limitation relates to image availability, as the limited number of BE and short‐segment Barrett's images necessitated use of the same image set by both investigators. This may have introduced a degree of overlap in image interpretation and could have influenced model performance estimates for these classifications. Findings related to BE and short‐segment Barrett's should be interpreted with caution, and future studies using larger, independent image sets are warranted to validate these observations. Interpretation of PPV and NPV is limited by their dependence on disease prevalence and class balance. Although PPV demonstrated an increasing trend with greater disease severity, the reported values reflect the sampled dataset and may not directly translate to real‐world screening or surveillance populations. While two separate ChatGPT‐5 accounts were used to reduce variability, LLM outputs can inherently vary across runs, and the extent of these fluctuations was not formally assessed. Finally, our prompts followed a Step‐1 style format without clinical context such as age, symptoms, or risk factors, and providing this information might have altered the model's performance. Further studies are warranted to optimize prompt engineering strategies and better define the most effective prompting approaches for LLM performance in endoscopic image interpretation.

Regarding the dataset, limitations within the Kaggle repository are worth noting. As differentiating mild esophagitis from normal mucosa is subject to observer variability, Kaggle minimized classification bias by consolidating categories with high disagreement into broader groups, leading to LA grade B–D esophagitis merging into a single category [9]. In addition, because the dataset includes a substantial number of unlabeled images, only labeled samples were incorporated into our study to ensure meaningful interpretation of the model's performance. Furthermore, limitations related to the ChatGPT model should be acknowledged. This study used the default “auto” setting, yet performance may differ across other versions such as ChatGPT‐5 Instant or ChatGPT‐5 Thinking. ChatGPT also carries well‐recognized limitations, including an inability to learn from prior cases, occasional unreliable or fabricated outputs (“hallucinations”), susceptibility to disinformation, and potential privacy or cybersecurity risks when handling protected health information [23].

Our findings, together with recent evaluations of ChatGPT‐4 on capsule endoscopy, cholangioscopy, and colonoscopy images, suggest that current LLMs can generate immediate, human‐readable impressions during upper endoscopy. Nevertheless, the present performance of ChatGPT‐5 remains insufficient to support autonomous clinical use and instead supports a role as an educational or adjunctive decision‐support tool used alongside established CAD systems and clinician expertise, pending further validation and comparative studies against endoscopic experts. In the future, integrating multimodal LLMs into the endoscopy suite could enable real‐time flagging of suspicious mucosa, standardized reporting, and point‐of‐care teaching for trainees, while maintaining the endoscopist as the final decision‐maker. Prospective studies using nonpublic image databases, continuous video input, and assessments of their effect on clinical decisions and workflow will be essential to ensure safe implementation and to determine whether ChatGPT‐based systems can complement specialized deep‐learning tools in routine upper GI practice. Furthermore, comparative studies involving board‐certified gastroenterologists and dedicated endoscopic AI systems are needed to better contextualize model performance and define the potential role of general‐purpose LLMs in endoscopic image interpretation. This framework also supports future extensions, such as evaluating the model's ability to inform subsequent clinical decisions or differentiate stages of BE using the Prague classification. Together, these strengths highlight the importance of early benchmarking studies as LLMs become increasingly incorporated into clinical decision‐support systems.
