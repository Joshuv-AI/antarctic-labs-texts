Research trends and prospects of Circulating tumor DNA associated with colorectal cancer: a bibliometric analysis
Authors: Shusen Xia, Lixin Liu, Yi Zhou, Yanbin Zhu, Qian Dai, Hong Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent research has highlighted ctDNA as a crucial tool for screening, monitoring, and treating colorectal cancer, gaining significant attention. This study aims to map the research landscape via bibliometric analysis, identifying future trends and key areas of interest. We collected articles and reviews on ctDNA and colorectal cancer from the Web of Science Core Collection and Scopus, conducting bibliometric analysis and visualization using VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and R packages. A total of 1,730 publications were included in the bibliometric analysis. Of these, 325 were unique to WOS and 639 were unique to SCOPUS. The United States and China led in publication volume with 555 and 306 papers, respectively, while the United States and Italy had the highest citation counts, with 39,142 and 21,417 citations, respectively. The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center was the most prolific institution. The journals Cancers and Frontiers in Oncology were the most frequently published. Bardelli A stood out as the most prolific and cited author. Cluster analysis revealed trending topics such as “minimal residual disease,” “adjuvant chemotherapy,” and “recurrence.” This bibliometric analysis highlights significant advancements and emerging trends in the clinical application of ctDNA for colorectal cancer. Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a deadly disease, ranking third in incidence and second in mortality worldwide [1]. Although its mortality rate remains high, early detection significantly improves survival rates. Therefore, before CRC progresses to advanced stages, there is a pressing need for novel and more effective non-invasive biomarkers for early detection, as well as prognostic biomarkers that can continuously monitor its recurrence or progression during treatment. In recent years, liquid biopsy, a minimally invasive blood sampling and analysis technique that can be repeated over time, has emerged as a promising approach for biomarker detection. Meanwhile, traditional protein-based biomarkers exhibit low sensitivity in early recurrence detection and treatment efficacy monitoring [2]. Hence, the quest for biomarkers suitable for CRC diagnosis, treatment efficacy, prognosis, recurrence, and metastasis remain the focus of future research.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) serves as a blood biomarker originating from tumor cells or the tumor microenvironment, released during cell apoptosis and necrosis, with a short half-life of approximately 2 h [3]. Various studies have confirmed a high level of concordance between genomic alterations detected in plasma ctDNA and those found in tumor tissues [4, 5]. As ctDNA only represents a very tiny fraction of the total circulating DNA in plasma, sometimes less than 0.01%, the reliable detection of such mutant DNA fragments was challenging in the past [6]. With the advancement of molecular techniques enabling sensitive detection and monitoring of various genetic and epigenetic alterations, the utilization of ctDNA as a noninvasive diagnostic tool for cancer has increased significantly [6, 7]. In recent years, methods for isolating and analyzing ctDNA have rapidly advanced, offering detailed insights into tumor characteristics like progression, staging, and heterogeneity [7, 8]. By detecting specific gene mutations unique to CRC, such as mutations in the KRAS and PIK3CA genes [9], ctDNA analysis can help identify CRC early, even before clinical symptoms appear. After curative surgery for cancer, the presence of ctDNA highly predicts early disease recurrence, while early reduction of ctDNA after the start of treatment indicates a good prognosis in metastatic cancer [10, 11, 12]. Compared to protein biomarkers, ctDNA demonstrates higher cancer specificity and sensitivity in early recurrence/metastatic disease detection, surpassing carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) in detecting residual lesions and early recurrence in surgically resected CRC patients [5].Bibliometrics has been widely employed for quantitative publication assessment in specific fields [13, 14, 15]. In the field of liquid biopsy for tumors, some bibliometric analyses have been published [16]. Particularly in breast cancer, liquid biopsies based on ctDNA [17] or exosomes [18] are considered important future directions.

This study introduces several pioneering contributions to the bibliometric analysis of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in colorectal cancer (CRC).First, we present the first multi-platform analysis integrating both the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) and Scopus databases, capturing 639 Scopus-exclusive publications to mitigate inherent database biases and ensure a more comprehensive representation of global research output. Second, through dynamic trend visualizations—including overlay maps and choropleth diagrams—we decode the thematic evolution of the field, revealing a distinct shift from early focus areas such as KRAS mutation profiling (2013–2018) to emerging priorities like minimal residual disease (MRD) detection (2020–2024). Third, our analysis bridges academic research and clinical practice by linking bibliometric hotspots to unmet needs in CRC recurrence monitoring, offering actionable insights for translating ctDNA advancements into real-world oncology workflows.

Methodologically, this work emphasizes rigor through its dual-database framework and extended temporal scope (2011–2024), surpassing prior studies limited to single platforms or shorter timeframes. Innovative visual analytics, such as authorship network mapping and keyword cluster timelines, provide a granular view of collaborative patterns and citation dynamics, establishing a foundational roadmap for prioritizing future research in ctDNA-driven precision oncology. By synthesizing these elements, our study not only addresses a critical gap in existing literature but also equips policymakers and researchers with evidence-based strategies to navigate the evolving landscape of CRC diagnostics and therapeutics.

This integrative approach underscores the translational relevance of ctDNA research while advancing methodologies in bibliometric science, setting a new benchmark for interdisciplinary cancer studies.

Results

Analysis of annual publication trends

Initially, topic searches in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) and Scopus database, employing the keywords (“Colorectal Neoplasm*” OR “Colorectal Tumor*” OR “Colorectal Cancer*” OR “Colorectal Carcinoma*”) AND (“ctDNA” OR “circulating tumor DNA” OR “cell free tumor DNA”), yielded 1,536 and 1,730 results respectively. Following the exclusion of non-articles and reviews, non-English full texts, studies published in 2025, those without a DOI number, and duplicates, a total of 1,106 articles from WoSCC and 1,420 from Scopus were obtained. After removing 781 duplicates between the two databases, we were decided to exclude articles published before 2011 from the analysis, as the number of articles prior to 2011 was low (n = 15). A total of 1,730 publications were included in the bibliometric analysis. Of these, 325 were unique to WOS and 639 were unique to SCOPUS. These publications had a total of 37,113 citations, with an average of 46.16 citations per document. The flowchart of data collection and filtering is shown n Fig. 1.

The number of publications exhibited a substantial increase, rising from a mere 2 in 2011 to 261 in 2024. This upward trajectory was marked by a pronounced escalation, with an average annual growth rate of 42.05%. The statistical analysis further consolidated this trend, revealing a strong correlation (R² = 0.95, p < 0.001). Despite a slight deceleration in the growth rate in 2021, there was a swift rebound in 2023 and 2024, culminating in a peak of 261 publications in 2024. Analysis of keywords

To analyze the focal points in this field, a keyword co-occurrence network map was generated using VOSviewer. Out of a total of 2045 keywords, 85 were selected based on an occurrence frequency threshold of more than 20. Among these, the top 5 most frequently occurring keywords, excluding the search terms, were “liquid biopsy” (n = 255), “acquired-resistance” (n = 162), “plasma” (n = 136), “mutations” (n = 109), and “kras” (n = 92). Using VOSviewer’s clustering function, all keywords were categorized into 5 clusters represented by different colors. In Fig. 7a, the three main clusters of keywords in this field were highlighted.

(a) The network visualization of co-occurrence analysis of keywords. (b) The overlay visualization of co-occurrence analysis of keywords. (c) Top 40 keywords ith the strongest citation bursts by CiteSpace

Cluster 1 comprised 33 keywords, including “liquid biopsy”, “circulating tumor cells”, “plasma DNA”, “droplet digital PCR”, “DNA methylation” and “diagnosis” among others. Cluster 1 mainly focused on ctDNA as a method of liquid biopsy, which can diagnose through DNA in plasma, and some research also compared it with similar methods such as CTCs (circulating tumor cells) [5, 21]. With the advancement of technology, new techniques were used for ctDNA detection. On one hand, the new PCR technique, ddPCR (droplet digital PCR), enabled absolute quantification of targets, thereby significantly enhancing the sensitivity and accuracy of detection [22, 23]. On the other hand, ctDNA methylation modification, rather than the sequence of ctDNA itself, has been proposed as an alternative biomarker due to its reliability and effectiveness in detecting molecular residual disease and recurrence [24].

Cluster 2 contained 20 keywords, such as “metastatic colorectal cancer”, “acquired-resistance”, “heterogeneity”, “mutations”, “ras”, “braf” and “egfr blockade”. Cluster 2 primarily focused on identifying resistance mutations that emerged when patients with CRC initially responded to therapy and then showed progression. These patients had metastatic CRC resistant to EGFR blockade due to acquired mutations in KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF [5]. In contrast to the analysis of tumor biopsy samples, which were invasive to obtain and often did not fully capture tumor heterogeneity and evolution, the analysis of ctDNA provided a non-invasive method to the need for molecular diagnosis at baseline, particularly in patients who lacked obtainable tumor tissue [25, 26].

Cluster 3 consisted of 29 items, such as “minimal residual disease”, “adjuvant chemotherapy”, “surgery”, “recurrence”, “early detection”, “survival”, and “prognosis”. Cluster 3 focused on the role of ctDNA in indicating the presence of micro-metastases after definitive surgical resection, which could act as an early warning for persistent and or recurrent malignancy before it became clinically apparent. MRD, minimal residual disease, persisted after primary malignant tumor treatment and was considered the origin of tumor recurrence. Due to the small number of tumor cells, these minute lesions were difficult to detect through traditional imaging and laboratory methods. With a short half-life and high detection sensitivity, ctDNA may indicate micro-metastases undetectable by imaging. The postsurgical status of ctDNA emerged as a highly significant prognostic biomarker compared to the high-risk clinicopathological features commonly utilized. It had the potential to predict the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy, thus avoiding unnecessary chemotherapy exposure for patients. Studies indicate that ALU-based cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is a promising non-invasive tool for colorectal cancer (CRC) diagnosis and prognosis [27]. Moreover, ctDNA detection can be linked to surrogate endpoints such as Overall Survival (OS), Disease-Free Survival (DFS), Cancer-Specific Survival (CSS), and Recurrence-Free Survival (RFS) outlined in the DATECAN initiative’s standardized definitions for time-to-event (TTE) endpoints in colorectal cancer (CRC). By incorporating these endpoints, the role of ctDNA in the context of CRC treatment and prognosis can be better understood and evaluated. Given the critical importance of survival and prognosis in CRC patient management, the focus on metrics like OS, DFS, CSS, and RFS is essential for improving patient outcomes.

Furthermore, an overlay visualization map illustrated the years in which each keyword appeared. Figure 7b revealed that Cluster 3 encompassed the most recent keywords, notably “adjuvant chemotherapy” and “recurrence”. Despite the unequivocal establishment of the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy for patients with stage III colon cancer, its efficacy for patients with stage II disease remained a subject of debate. Recent studies revealed that a ctDNA-guided approach to treating stage II colon cancer reduced the utilization of adjuvant chemotherapy without compromising recurrence-free survival [10, 28]. Additionally, 40 keywords with significant citation bursts were identified using CiteSpace in Fig. 7c. Keywords related to acquired resistance and heterogeneity analysis, such as “kras mutations” and “egfr blockade,” were prominently featured at the outset of this field and were the focal points from 2013 to 2018. However, after 2020, attention shifted towards ctDNA as a significant risk factor for recurrence. The field has transitioned from exploratory studies to clinical practice, where ctDNA guides decision-making.

Discussion

CRC, a disease with high mortality worldwide, is increasingly affecting young populations [29].With expanding therapeutic options, interest in personalized treatment grows, driving the need for molecular profiling in precision medicine. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in cancer patients’ plasma or serum offers a non-invasive way to obtain tumor DNA samples. This “liquid biopsy” enables analysis of DNA quantity, chromosomal alterations, sequence mutations, and epigenetic changes, serving as a tool for disease burden assessment and genomic analysis [30]. Thus, ctDNA analysis serves as a tool for quantitative disease burden assessment and genomic analysis. In CRC, ctDNA has emerged as a promising non-invasive biomarker used throughout the disease management process. It enables longitudinal tumor assessment, including early diagnosis, minimally invasive molecular profiling, identification of resistance mutations, detection of minimal residual disease, and monitoring of recurrence.

This bibliometric analysis examines ctDNA and CRC research in the WoSCC and SCOPUS databases (2011–2024). Publications were scarce until 2015, with only 1–2 in 2011–2012 and fewer than 10 annually before 2015. After 2015, there was a significant surge, reaching 261 publications in 2024—a twentyfold increase. By 2024-end, 1,730 publications had accumulated 37,113 citations (46.16 per paper), reflecting substantial academic impact. Researchers from 62 countries contributed, with the US leading in publications and citations. China recently overtook Italy in publications and the US in 2023, but still lags in citations behind Italy, Australia, and Germany. The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (US) and IRCCS (Italy) are the top two institutions in output and collaboration. Other institutions like Fudan University (China), National Cancer Center Hospital East (Japan), and the University of Melbourne (Australia) are less interconnected but linked to the top two. Greater collaboration among institutions in China, Australia, Japan, and elsewhere is needed to advance ctDNA research in CRC.

The top 7 most productive authors in this field include four from Italy. Bardeli A had the highest h-index, his first publication on this topic as a corresponding author was in 2013. This review published in the Nature Reviews Clinical Oncology provides an overview of how technological advancements have facilitated the detection of genetic and epigenetic abnormalities in ctDNA, and discusses the clinical implications of utilizing tumor-associated mutations identified in blood samples following diagnosis [31]. Just a year later, he released another review with Siena S and Sarote-Bianchi A, highlighting that ctDNA has become a valuable asset for monitoring resistance to anti-EGFR therapy in patients and for evaluating combination therapies designed to overcome or reverse resistance [25]. Moreover, a collaborative effort in 2015 among the Italian researchers, including Bardeli A, Siravegna G, Siena S, and Sarote-Bianchi A, resulted in the successful identification of ctDNA alterations in patients exhibiting primary or acquired resistance to EGFR blockade in various genes, including KRAS, NRAS, MET, ERBB2, FLT3, EGFR, and MAP2K1 [20].

Highly cited papers emphasize ctDNA’s role in early CRC detection, residual lesion identification, recurrence monitoring, and tumor genotyping. Bettegowda et al.‘s 2014 study in Science Translational Medicine, the most cited with 327 local citations, showed ctDNA’s broad applicability as a sensitive and specific biomarker across cancer types, linking ctDNA concentration to survival in metastatic CRC patients and distinguishing ctDNA from CTCs [5]. Tie J’s 2016 paper on stage II colon cancer patients gained increasing attention, surpassing Bettegowda’s in citations by 2023. It demonstrated that post-operative ctDNA detection predicted poor recurrence-free survival [12]. Two 2019 studies in JAMA Oncology further highlighted the association between post-surgical and post-chemotherapy ctDNA detection and recurrence risk [32, 33]. Despite ctDNA’s value in predicting early recurrence, questions remain about testing frequency, guiding treatment plans, and improving survival. More prospective studies are needed to validate ctDNA’s clinical value and facilitate its translation into clinical practice.

Keyword analysis serves as a valuable tool for unveiling emerging topics and forecasting future research trends within a particular field. The exploration of ctDNA in CRC can be divided into three distinct stages based on keyword analysis. During the initial stage, researchers focus on establishing fundamental theories and techniques, thus laying a robust groundwork for subsequent investigations. In the early years, pioneering scholars like Mouliere F uncovered that ctDNA extracted from cancer patients exhibited a distinct size distribution profile and significantly greater fragmentation compared to ctDNA isolated from healthy individuals [34, 35]. Additionally, scholars demonstrated the clinical utility of digital PCR (dPCR) in simultaneously screening multiple mutations in circulating DNA acquired through noninvasive blood collection [36].

In the subsequent research phase, researchers examined ctDNA’s roles in precision oncology, including diagnosis, prognosis, therapy, and drug resistance. They found that ctDNA presence correlates with poor outcomes and tumor relapse in CRC patients [12]. It also enables real-time monitoring of tumor progression and EGFR blockade resistance [20].In metastatic CRC patients, mutations in KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF detected in plasma ctDNA contribute to primary resistance to anti-EGFR therapies [37]. A blinded prospective multi-center study by Thierry et al. showed high specificity and sensitivity of ctDNA analysis for BRAF V600E and KRAS mutations compared to tumor-section analysis [26]. Liquid biopsy via ctDNA analysis can replace traditional methods and enhance personalized medicine. Unlike traditional biopsies, liquid biopsy offers non-invasive, real-time monitoring and better analysis of tumor heterogeneity. It reflects the genomic structure of a patient’s cancer more comprehensively, avoiding biases in personalized medicine selection and effectiveness. Multi-region sequencing studies have shown significant differences in mutation spectra between different tumor regions and between primary and metastatic sites [38]. Repeated biopsies to address heterogeneity are neither feasible nor desirable. ctDNA, originating from multiple tumor regions, provides a more comprehensive reflection of tumor heterogeneity. Additionally, epigenetic alterations like ctDNA methylation patterns have been identified as potential CRC biomarkers [39, 40].

Recent research has focused on using ctDNA to detect minimal residual disease (MRD) and predict outcomes after curative treatments like surgery and chemoradiotherapy. Surgical resection is standard for stage II/III CRC patients, followed by adjuvant chemotherapy based on clinicopathological risk factors [41]. While most stage II CRC patients did not receive adjuvant chemotherapy, about 10–15% of them had residual disease post-surgery [42]. In contrast, the majority of stage III CRC patients underwent adjuvant chemotherapy, even though surgery alone cured more than 50% of them [43].Relying solely on staging for treatment decisions risks under-or overtreatment. Post-surgical ctDNA detection is a more efficient and accurate way to indicate MRD and spot patients with a higher chance of recurrence. In the prospective study conducted by Tie J in 2016 [12], ctDNA-positive patients had a 0% 3-year recurrence-free survival rate versus 90% for ctDNA-negative patients. Using ctDNA for risk stratification can help target adjuvant therapy to those who will benefit most, sparing low-risk patients from unnecessary side effects. The timing of ctDNA sampling is critical, with post-chemotherapy testing potentially offering greater prognostic value [33]. Additionally, next-generation sequencing (NGS) has expanded ctDNA analysis beyond PCR-based methods, enabling the detection of a broader range of genetic variants [44, 45].

Using VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and R’s Bibliometrix, we visualized ctDNA CRC-related literature to identify trends and hotspots. However, limitations exist: only English articles and reviews were included, which might have introduced selection bias and overlooked high-impact non-English or other-type articles. The analysis was based on the Web of Science Core Collection and Scopus, possibly excluding influential publications from other databases like PubMed. We didn’t examine full texts, so some important information might have been missed. Also, only 2011–2024 publications were included, omitting current-year influential studies. Conclusions must be interpreted within these limitations. Future research should address them by including non-English studies and latest publications for a more comprehensive understanding. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the field’s evolving research foci and trends, beyond traditional reviews.

Methods

Data source and search strategy

The Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) and Scopus were searched on March, 2025, with no limitation on publication date. The search utilized the keywords: (“Colorectal Neoplasm*” OR “Colorectal Tumor*” OR “Colorectal Cancer*” OR “Colorectal Carcinoma*”) AND (“ctDNA” OR “circulating tumor DNA” OR “cell free tumor DNA”). To ensure accuracy, duplicates, non-English publications, and records that were not articles or reviews were excluded. Two groups of reviewers underwent standard selection training and independently screened titles and abstracts (with full texts reviewed if necessary), with disagreements resolved through discussion. Ultimately, a total of 1,730 publications were included in the bibliometric analysis. Of these, 325 were unique to Web of Science (WOS) and 639 were unique to SCOPUS and filtering was depicted in Fig. 1.

Data analysis

All results were downloaded with the record content of “Full Record and Cited References” from WoSCC and exported as plain text. Bibliometric analyses were conducted using CiteSpace v.6.3.R1 (Drexel University, United States), VOS viewer v.1.6.20 (Leiden University, Netherlands), and the “bibliometrix package v.4.1.4” of R software (v.4.3.2). Burst detection of references in CiteSpace was utilized to identify records with abrupt changes in frequency over a certain period, indicating hot points in the field. The parameters of CiteSpace were set as follows: time span (2011–2023), year of slice (1), selection criteria g-index (k = 25), selection criteria (Top 50), link retaining factor (LRF = 3), look back years (LBY = 5), and pruning (minimum spanning tree, pruning sliced networks). VOSviewer calculated the data and ranked published records for different countries, institutions, and authors. Detailed information, such as total citations or total link strength among different countries and organizations, was gathered and calculated using this software. The bibliometrix package was utilized to analyze the bibliographic information of retrieved literature. R packages, including ggplot2, ggpubr, gt, and gtExtras in R software, were used for descriptive analysis and plotting graphs. The H-index, characterizing the cumulative impact of an author or journal by defining the maximum value of h such that the given author or journal has published h papers that have received at least h citations, was employed in the analysis.
