Analysis of electrohysterogram signals for predicting obstetric outcome using machine learning methods: a scoping review
Authors: Rubana H. Chowdhury, Roma Sultana, Mithila Arman, Yasir Rahman, Quazi D. Hossain, Mohiuddin Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeEfficiently detecting obstetric outcomes, such as preterm birth or mode of delivery, is crucial for enhancing mother and newborn health. Proactive screening, identification, and prevention in asymptomatic pregnant women exhibiting risk factors for preterm birth or c-section delivery can mitigate incidence and fatality rates.MethodsThis study thoroughly reviewed prediction models for obstetric outcomes, described the EHG signal acquisition protocols, pre-processing methods, feature extraction from EHG, and model properties, and compared their quality to establish the most effective prediction model for clinical decision-making. For this study, the biomedical databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase) of published publications were searched from December 2000 to February 2025. In addition to electrohysterography, other search terms to consider are electrohysterogram, uterine electromyography, Term-preterm labor, birth delivery mode, and EHG in machine learning.ResultsBased on a literature review, the prevailing recording technique for acquiring EHG signals across various applications, including pregnancy monitoring, preterm risk evaluation, and birth delivery mode detection, commonly employs four bipolar electrodes. A bandpass filter of minimum bandwidth of 0.1 to 4 Hz is most commonly used for pre-process the EHG signal. High discriminative performance was reported in 5 studies, with an Area Under the Curve (AUC) ranging from 0.93 to 0.99. A single classifier may suffice for predicting obstetric outcomes using an EHG signal, eliminating the need for a combined classifier. A total of 98.5% of the studies exhibited a high risk of bias in the analysis domain, primarily due to the limited sample size and the absence of external validation.ConclusionThis review will familiarize academics and obstetricians with the comprehensive EHG analytical process and its prospective uses in clinical decision support systems.

Introduction

Labor, the physiological procedure through which a fetus is expelled from the uterus, is characterized by regular UCs, cervical effacement, and dilation. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines preterm birth as the start of labor before 37 weeks of pregnancy have passed, between weeks 37-42 term, and post-term when a baby has been born after a gestation period exceeding forty-two [1]. Experiencing complications during pregnancy can lead to premature birth, which consistently increases the risk of the physical condition of very preterm children and parental mental health problems [1, 2]. UC is a vital diagnostic tool for monitoring uterine activity during pregnancy and labor [3]. During pregnancy, UC evaluates the advancement of labor and delivery substantially.

Previously, researchers had paid close attention to fetal heart rate (FHR) patterns when interpreting the cardiotocogram (CTG). Continuous monitoring of uterine contractions and FHR is a vital aspect of risk management during the third trimester or during labor labor and delivery [4]. Generally, two techniques, external tocodynamometry (TOCO) and Intrauterine Pressure Catheters (IUPC), have been used for monitoring UC. In the TOCO method, a pressure transducer was placed on the patient’s abdomen, and contractions were measured. The intrauterine pressure significantly changes as a result of the uterine UCs. Moreover, it can be affected by subcutaneous fat content and is subject to movement artifacts. The intrauterine pressure catheter (IUPC) directly detects the intrauterine pressure changes brought on by UCs; however, its invasiveness poses a risk of infection and membrane rupture. Moreover, TOCO records interpretation requires a high level of staff training. It is heavily reliant on the inspectors’ subjective experience as well as the sensor position [5]. This interpretation has low inter- and intra-observer agreement, and even expert interpretations of the same records can differ [6]. Researchers have been exploring different techniques and trying to find safe and accurate alternatives. Recently, EHG has been considered a noninvasive technique for UC monitoring, which is a specific form of electromyography (EMG).

During pregnancy, electrophysiological monitoring of uterine muscle activity, also known as an electrohysterogram (EHG) which, is necessary to allow for prompt treatment. In this technique, electrodes are placed on the abdominal surface of pregnant women, which represents the electrical activity of the uterine muscle.

The uterine electromyogram or electrohyterogram (EHG) has recently gained popularity as a method for assessing and detecting obstetric-related issues and labor outcomes (term-preterm delivery, spontaneous vaginal, cesarean, and induced vaginal delivery). EHG monitoring systems have become more prevalent in the research field because of the possible detection of many obstetric problems that are life-threatening for pregnant women and their babies.

Uterine electromyography, or Electrohysterography (EHG), has recently gained prominence due to its noninvasive characteristics and enhanced sensitivity and specificity for lower gestational ages, surpassing the two other established uterine monitoring methods [7, 8]. Therefore, Researchers are persistently concentrating on the use of EHG for long-term uterine contraction (UC) monitoring and obstetric outcome prediction. The temporal, spectral, and non-linear features of the EHG signal offer supplementary insights into uterine dynamics beyond what TOCO signals provide [9]. Kirsten M.J. Thijssen et al. [10] experimented on pregnant women and simultaneously used three different techniques (TOCO, IUPC, and EHG) for uterine contraction monitoring. In the case of IUPC recordings, the experiment showed the best result regarding quality. As this technique is invasive, it has not been used recently due to safety concerns. EHG is a recently validated technology for uterine activity monitoring that has better sensitivity (89.5%) than TOCO (65.3%) in identifying uterine contraction [11]. The application of artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms utilizing EHG signals to enhance decision-making and diagnostic processes has seen significant growth in recent decades within the field of obstetrics [12–14]. For instance, Mas-Cabo et al. [15] predicted impending labor in women showing signs of preterm labor by analyzing the characteristics of uterine EHG signals. Moreover, EHG signals were used for predicting the labor onset type (spontaneous vaginal or induced vaginal) [16], the success of labor induction [17], and assessing the risk of postpartum hemorrhage following both spontaneous vaginal and cesarean deliveries [18]. However, the predictive outcomes of obstetric machine learning algorithms are significantly influenced by the features derived from signals obtained from pregnant women, which are contingent upon maternal anthropometric and obstetric variables. Some research exists concerning the correlation between commonly utilized time domain and frequency domain EHG features and maternal anthropometric variables, including maternal age, weeks before delivery [19], pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), and abdominal circumference [20], gestational age [21, 22], placental position [22], gravidity, abortion history, and cesarean section history [23]. Diaz-Martinez et al. [18] extracted several non-linear features from the EHG signal for post-partum hemorrhage prediction and observed significant differences in post-partum myometrial activity between women who delivered spontaneously and those who underwent elective cesarean sections. These works are just some examples of how the EHG-based method has recently contributed to diagnostic precision across various areas within the field of obstetrics. For predicting the obstetric outcome, some researchers focused on only contraction bursts from the EHG signal, and some researchers focused on the whole EHG signal, which includes contraction and non-contraction segments. In uterine bursts, the low-frequency component of the signal carries more information about the propagation than the high-frequency component.

Nevertheless, several researchers suggest that there are two frequency content ranges specified in the EHG signal, which are the slow wave and the fast wave in the frequency bands 0.03–0.1 Hz and 0.3–3.0 Hz, respectively. The fast wave is further divided into the Fast Wave Low (FWL) and Fast Wave High (FWH). The frequency distribution in the FWL is 0.2 to 0.45 Hz, which comprises any uterine electrical recording, and FWH is 0.8–3 Hz, which contains the information of efficient labor contractions according to [24, 25] or 0.1–1.2 Hz and 1.2–4.7 Hz according to [26]. Therefore, most of the researchers focused on FWH and used the wider frequency such as 0.1–3 Hz [27, 28], 0.3–3 Hz [29–31], 0.3–4 Hz [29, 32], and 0.2–8 Hz [33], and so on. It is important to know the frequency band before extracting effective features from EHG signals, which is a key step for predicting obstetric outcomes.

In this article, we address the following research question: How have EHG signal acquisition configurations, signal processing and feature extraction techniques, and machine learning models been utilized to predict obstetric outcomes—particularly preterm birth and mode of delivery—and what methodological gaps limit their applicability in clinical decision-support systems? This article provides an overview of the research focused on forecasting obstetric outcomes based on uterine contraction bursts from the EHG signal and provides an overview of the research focused on predicting obstetric outcomes by entire EHG signal analysis. In both parts, we examined how academics employ machine learning algorithms for prediction, with special emphasis on feature extraction, predictive performance, and unresolved challenges.

This scoping review aims to:

(i)Identify and summarize the methods used for EHG signal acquisition, pre-processing, feature extraction, and classification in predicting obstetric outcomes.(ii)Compare the performance of machine learning algorithms across different EHG configurations and signal segments (e.g., full signal vs. contraction bursts).(iii)Highlight existing gaps in the current literature regarding EHG-based prediction of preterm birth and delivery mode (vaginal vs. cesarean).(iv)Propose future directions for improving the diagnostic utility and clinical translation of EHG-based decision-support tools.

Method

Search strategy

The PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines provide a standardized framework for conducting and reporting scoping reviews. They ensure transparency, replicability, and quality of such reviews. Data extraction was carried out by two separate reviewers (QDH and RHC), who also cross-checked all of the material gathered. We examine the deficiencies revealed in the evaluated work and propose strategies to resolve these unresolved issues. To undertake this review procedure, 102 publications were included through the successive identification, screening, and eligibility testing processes. Assessment of risk of bias

Five reviewers (RHC, Sultana R, Arman M, and Rahman Y) independently evaluated the risk of bias in the included papers utilizing the PROBAST tool. The PROBAST tool has 20 evaluative questions distributed across four domains: participants, predictors, outcomes, and analysis. Responses to signaling questions included yes, probably yes, no, probably no, or no information. The risk of bias in each domain was assessed as low, high, or unclear. The comprehensive evaluation of bias risk was classified as low risk if all domains were assessed as low risk, high risk if any domain was assessed as high risk, unclear risk if any domain was deemed unclear danger, and low risk for all other domains.

Recording protocol and signal acquisition configuration

A large EHG signal database will aid in improving techniques for predicting the mode of birth delivery and preterm birth. This makes it vital to collect a lot of EHG signals from pregnant women of various gestational ages.

However, the deployment of current EHG technologies in clinical settings is challenging due to the lack of a standardized acquisition protocols and electrode placement strategies. Some researchers provided their dataset through the Research Resource for Complex Physiological Signals (PhysioNet) so that other researchers might use it for their pilot study because collecting bio-signals necessitates time and effort [34–36]. Early EHG studies demonstrated that uterine electrical activity can be detected using a minimal number of electrodes, with some reports showing that two strategically placed abdominal electrodes can capture uterine contractions, which are represented in Fig. 2(a) [16]. Given that uterine electrical activity is spatially heterogeneous and propagates dynamically across the abdominal surface during pregnancy and labor, these limited configurations may fail to represent global uterine coordination. As a result, reliance on single-sensor or sparse electrode setups can reduce the robustness and physiological interpretability of predictions related to preterm birth and labor onset.

To overcome these limitations, some authors employ multiple electrodes for examining the characteristics of complex EHG signals. Four electrodes were positioned on the abdominal wall over the uterine muscle to capture signals, which the researchers utilized to form a square with a side of seven cm. Figure 2(b) shows the four electrode placement protocols [37]. To get a strong EHG recording signal, they also advise positioning the ground electrode on the right side of the hip and the reference electrode on the left. Authors should have obtained written consent from the subjects, and after that skin needs to be prepared using an abrasive paste and alcoholic solution before electrode placement. To ensure optimal reproducibility of EHG, recordings have been done using disposable adhesive Ag/AgCl electrodes, which will be arranged properly on the lower abdomen of the pregnant women. For long-term monitoring of UCs, interference component removal from the measured EHG signals, and real-time analysis of EHG signals, several studies used a variety of electrode configuration protocols.

Following a thorough review of the electrode placement literature, Jossou TR et al. [37] suggest that the minimum allowed inter-electrode spacing should be between 17.5 and 25 mm when placed in the median axis (see Fig. 2(d)).

They evaluated it according to a 16-electrode placement methodology. Furthermore, they recommend it for optimising EHG signal quality; nevertheless, they do not address which configuration is beneficial for various obstetric outcome prediction models. The type and quantity of electrodes may differ based on the specific aims of the EHG investigation. Consequently, the influence of electrode arrangement on the recognition of obstetric outcomes must be examined. Recent research has increasingly shifted toward multichannel EHG systems, leveraging spatial propagation, synchrony, and connectivity analysis to better capture global uterine dynamics and improve prediction of preterm labor and delivery mode. In the early phases, the majority of EHG studies used 2 to 5 electrodes, focusing on activity in a specific region of the uterus [38–40]. Based on this exploratory research, Iceland’s Landspitali University Hospital, Akureyri Hospital, and Akureyri Primary Health Care Centre recorded pregnancies and labor using a 4 by 4 grid electrode system, which is shown in Fig. 2(d). Between 2008 and 2010, 122 EHG recordings were collected into a database [41]. Xiaoxiao Song et al. [42] acquired EHG and TOCO signals using 8-channel configurations from 54 pregnant women at Beijing Union Medical College Hospital in China, shown in Fig. 2(c).

Very often, there are two types of electrodes used for EHG signal acquisition, which are monopolar and bipolar electrode techniques. The efficiency of data processing is affected by the type of electrode configuration utilized for EHG recording. A bipolar electrode configuration has the advantage of minimizing volume conducted potentials since the differential amplifier attenuates common signal components from distant bioelectric sources.

A configuration of monopolar electrodes that is more susceptible to common mode signals results in a worse signal-to-noise ratio [43–46]. Moreover, the frequency content for bipolar recordings was higher than the monopolar configuration [47]. The monopolar electrode placement technique is not recommended due to the lower spatial resolution and local sensitivity when acquiring the EHG signal [48]. The monopolar and bipolar Ag/AgCl electrode structures are shown in Fig. 3. Figure 4 displays a sample of the filtered EHG and TOCO signals that were obtained from the Physionet database (https://physionet.org/physiobank/database/tpehgt/). In this figure, the y-axis shows the amplitude in µV. The group and position of the electrodes, the space between them, and the skin impedance of a particular patient can all have an impact on the EHG’s amplitude [48, 49]. Recently, researchers have been hard at work developing flexible electrodes that improve signal quality and spatial resolution [50].

Pre-processing method

The EHG recordings not only contained the electrical activity of the uterus but also a sequence of physiological interference parameters (abdominal muscle activity, baseline fluctuations, and maternal-fetal ECG) and motion artifacts. The motion artifact can distort the power spectral density (PSD) of EHG, leading to misinterpreted results. Therefore, it is crucial to eliminate the best possible noise before applying the feature extraction method. Skeletal electromyography, electrocardiograms of the fetus and the expecting mother, electrode movement artifacts, and noises from electronic instruments adversely affect the EHG signals. Therefore, before an in-depth analysis of the signal, it is required to eliminate noise from the recordings. It is necessary to understand the distinction between artifacts and uterine contractions before signal processing. The digital filter selection and electrode placement choice are significant before signal processing. Most of the researchers focused on the high wave band during the filtering process due to proper contraction characterization, because most of the uterine activity is concentrated in this bandwidth (BW) [51, 52]. Fele-Zˇorz et al. [29] tested three band-pass Butterworth filters with frequency bands 0.08-4, 0.3-4, and 0.3–3 Hz. They acquired data by placing electrodes approximately 3.5 cm below the navel and 7 cm apart from each other. In their experiment, they used 300 EHG records and divided them into four groups according to gestational age. They showed that median frequency and sample entropy techniques achieve a satisfactory result for separating early-term and preterm delivery when using a filter of range 0.3–3 Hz. Hence, a digital filter with a range of 0 to 3 was first used to preprocess the EHG signal.

Processing the EHG signal

Different methods for recognizing uterine contractions (UCs)

Several researchers have been processing EHG recordings during pregnancy to identify uterine contractions for predicting obstetric outcomes and monitoring pregnancy. Uterine contractions (UCs) are the result of uterine activity in the form of an action potential, which is a significant tool for clinical decision-making. Many researchers have been working on EHG signals to identify uterine and non-uterine contractions. In addition, EHG bursts associated with UCs are essential for obstetricians to determine the health condition of the fetus since UC increases fetal heart rate variation [53]. Huber et al. [54] have reviewed the nature of brief uterine contraction.

Moreover, several studies have been performed to extract UCs in the EHG, which naturally last from thirty seconds to a few minutes. Few researchers have been concentrating on the characteristics of EHG bursts, such as the number of contractions, the duration of the contractions, the amplitude, and the force of the contraction, rather than the classification of contractions for determining the progression of labor [42, 55]. Therefore, it is recommended to incorporate these parameters with certain classifications. Xiao Song et al. [42] modulated the EHG signal using the power of zero-crossing rate (ZCR) and highlighted the EHG bursts. Then they recognized UC by extracting the envelope from that modulated EHG signal and the TOCO signal as well. They showed that EHG is more capable of detecting a large number of UCs than TOCO. They observed that the uterus is relatively silent during the first 30 weeks of pregnancy, and after 30 weeks, contractions progressively become more frequent and intense. In the future, EHG can be used to give obstetric doctors an accurate, noninvasive approach to monitor uterine contractions on their own during delivery. Some studies have obtained promising results in predicting preterm birth or analyzing the different conditions in pregnancy (such as labor conditions and antepartum conditions) by isolating contractions or bursts from the EHG records [5, 9, 28, 35, 39, 56–58].

While they concentrate on the characteristics of contraction segments from the EHG signal, some manually retrieved contraction bursts from a 30-minute recording of EHG using TOCO [5, 56, 57].

Feature extraction from the contraction segments of the EHG signal

Some researchers extracted features from the contraction regions of the EHG signal, utilising them as inputs for classifiers to detect preterm birth and mode of delivery. Extraction of efficient features such as linear, non-linear, and spectral features increases the classifier’s performance and reduces computational costs. The temporal parameters evaluate the strength of contraction intervals during pregnancy, while the spectral parameters assess changes in shifts and amplitude within the power spectrum [59]. It is essential to select the pertinent feature set for designing the classifier due as the use of an inadequate number of features or their redundancy could adversely affect the classifier’s accuracy. Otherwise, information will be missing, or data may be over-trained, which affects the classifier’s performance.

Feature extraction from the whole EHG signal

Numerous researchers retrieved various aspects from the entire EHG signals, which included contraction and non-contraction segments. Recently, several academics have concentrated on entropy-based indices as a means of evaluating and quantifying the complexity of EHG time series [60–64]. Most of the researchers suggested that for predicting preterm labor and assessing the progress of labor efficiently, it would be better to choose sample entropy and median frequency as potential features.

Results

Characteristics of acquisition protocol

Although extensive research endeavours to improve the automated computerised analysis of EHG signals, a definitive gold standard has not been established. Numerous researchers have employed diverse recording parameters to examine the EHG signal. The primary differences pertain to electrode placement methodologies, filter cut-off frequencies, feature extraction processes, and classifier selection. The protocol for electrode installation significantly influences model’s performance and classification outcomes. Furthermore, suitable pre-segmentation and filtering as pre-processing measures significantly improve the capacity to differentiate between signals of pregnancy and labor-induced contractions [65]. This review study has addressed the options for augmenting the quantity and positioning of electrodes on a pregnant woman’s abdomen to get an EHG signal. The quantity of source electrodes employed differs among authors, as does the inter-electrode spacing for example, Dongmei H [66]. demonstrated that four electrodes in the upper left uterine quadrant yielded the highest CNN-based classification accuracy for uterine contraction detection.

While some studies achieved adequate signals using only two electrodes [67], increasing electrode density enables better analysis of signal propagation and potentially improves predictive power. Tables 1 and 2 indicates that the majority of studies on obstetric outcome detection employed a four-electrode placement approach. The four electrodes located in the upper left quadrant of the uterus had the highest efficacy in classifying EHG signals.

Characteristics of the pre-processing method

Pre-processing methods are a crucial component of signal processing as well. According to Table 1, many researchers reported improved performance when using a 4th-order Butterworth filter with a 0.1–3 Hz band-pass range for predicting obstetric outcomes using contraction segments only. The EHG contains two signals: a slow wave and a fast wave. The slow wave ranges from 0.005 to 0.03 Hz, with an amplitude between 0.5 mV and 15 mV. The range of the fast wave part of the EHG signal is between 0.1 and 3 Hz, with amplitudes ranging from 0.02 mV to 0.5 mV [16, 25]. This high-frequency wave part of the physiological EHG signal effectively captures the dominant uterine electrical activity associated with contraction bursts while suppressing low-frequency motion artifacts, maternal respiration (MR-EMG) and higher-frequency noise such as External electromagnetic noise, EMG noise. For classifying EHG, the features retrieved from the frequency bands above and below 1.0 Hz are similarly essential and useful [79]. A minimum bandwidth of 0.1 to 4 Hz is recommended for authors using the Butterworth bandpass filter.

Similarly, according to Table 2 for studies analysing the whole EHG signal, a 4th-order Butterworth filter with a wider band-pass range (typically within 0–5 Hz) was frequently adopted. This broader frequency range preserves both contraction-related components and baseline uterine electrical activity, which are relevant when modeling long-term uterine dynamics across different labor outcomes.

Characteristics of prediction models

EHG analysis approaches can be broadly categorized into contraction-only and whole-signal methods. Tables 1 and 2 highlight the filtering method, feature extraction, electrode placement configuration, and model performance utilising contraction segments and the entire EHG signal, respectively. Upon comparing Tables 1 and 2, it is evident that models predicting preterm birth predominantly utilized the complete EHG signal (n = 17), achieving SVM-based AUCs between 75% and 99.7%. In contrast, birth delivery mode prediction largely relied on contraction bursts (n = 6), with RF-based AUCs exceeding 0.90. The seventh model yielded favourable results; nevertheless, none of the researchers disclosed the evaluation parameter AUC. Both the accuracy and AUC of these models were greater than 0.95 [36, 67, 76] for predicting preterm birth, and the AUC of the 2 predictive models was greater than 0.90 for identifying the mode of birth delivery. Risk of bias assessment

The PROBAST-based assessment revealed that all studies maintained low bias in participant, predictor, and outcome domains. A total of 98.5% of studies (n = 20) exhibited high risk in the analysis domain, primarily due to limited sample sizes and lack of external validation. Additionally, 52% of studies (n = 12) selected predictors solely via univariable analyses, and 21.7% (n = 5) exhibited a significant risk of bias, as the predictors were selected exclusively based on the outcomes of univariable (one-variable-at-a-time) analyses. 21.74% of studies (n = 5) exhibited an unclear risk of bias in the predictor domain due to a lack of information regarding the treatment of various predictor categories (features).

Discussion

IUPC, TOCO, and EHG are the three different uterine monitoring techniques; comparison among them is very difficult because of their different approach and natures. Few studies worked on their comparison and showed that electrohysterography is substantially more sensitive than TOCO at detecting uterine contractions during term labor (90-93). Since, both EHG and TOCO are noninvasive, recent research efforts have increasingly focused on improving these modalities, with particular attention given to EHG due to its ability to directly reflect the electrical activity of myometrial muscle cells. As EHG represents the whole electrical activity of the myometrial muscle cells, researchers continuously try to understand the pattern of this signal for identifying informative biomarkers. Feature types (linear, non-linear, propagation-related features) and numbers might vary depending on the specific goals of an EHG study; however, entropy-based features and RMS are the most frequently utilized features. Recently, Vinothini et al. [73] emphasized the shape and geometry of the underlying data in order to extract topological features such as area, perimeter, circularity, convexity, ellipse variance, and bending energy. They observed the distinct difference between term and preterm EHG components with an AUC of about 99%. Similarly, Chowdhury et al. [71] reported strong performance detecting the birth delivery mode using EHG- based topological features with an AUC of 93%.

Therefore, RMS, entropy-based features, and topological features might be used as potential biomarkers for the early detection of obstetric outcomes and risks. For final classification, much research work has been done using machine learning algorithms (MLA) because, compared with traditional statistical techniques, MLA is more recommended for reliable medical decision support systems. Few researchers show that combined ensemble learning, particularly stacking, outperforms individual models by improving accuracy, robustness, and generalizability for clinical decision system [79–81]. In case of detecting preterm births Fergus P. et al. [82] proposed that combining classifiers provides an improved result rather than using a single classifier. In their studies, they used seven different artificial neural networks and showed that the combination classifier achieved the best. They used the SMOTE technique for data balancing and extracted temporal and spectral features. However, reviewing many articles, we concluded that a single classifier might be a good solution for classifying obstetric outcomes and detecting obstetric risks using EHG. Although, the drawback of our review is that we only searched English-language publications. Table 1 shows that EHG contraction bursts attributes can predict preterm delivery using a single classifier that has a sensitivity of 94.14%, a specificity of 98.47%, an accuracy of 96.33%, and an AUC of 99.44%. In this instance, the disparity arises from authors manually extracting contraction segments, which is highly subjective. From Table 2, we see that whole EHG (including contraction and non-contraction segments) revealed a high sensitivity in detecting uterine contractions and predicting preterm delivery. The single-classifier SVM using a Gaussian and polynomial kernel function had a sensitivity, specificity, and total accuracy of over 99%. In this predicting test, a single SVM classifier with a polynomial kernel function showed good sensitivity (99.5%), specificity (99.7%), and total accuracy (99.7%). The SVM with Gaussian kernel function gives very similar results, with a sensitivity of 99.54%, a specificity of 99.93%, an accuracy of 99.74%, and an F1 score of 99.744%. In the case of predicting birth delivery mode topological features (Area, Perimeter, Circularity, Variance, Bending Energy) from EHG bursts give highest performance. The literature suggests that the SVM and RF classifiers may be the most effective and efficient choices for predicting preterm birth and mode of birth delivery, respectively. They potentially simplify the computational complexity compared to using multiple classifiers. In this study, the four-electrode placement protocol consistently demonstrated superior performance in predicting both birth delivery mode and preterm birth compared to alternative configurations. This finding suggests that limited multichannel configurations can offer a meaningful improvement over single-electrode EHG by capturing a broader spatial representation of uterine electrical activity, while avoiding the increased complexity and susceptibility to noise often associated with high-density electrode arrays. However, given the inherently spatially heterogeneous and propagative nature of uterine electrophysiology, no single electrode configuration can be considered universally optimal for all obstetric outcome prediction tasks. Rather, the four-electrode protocol appears to represent a practical and clinically feasible compromise, providing sufficient spatial information to enhance prediction robustness while maintaining ease of deployment in clinical environments. This review recommends that the four-electrode protocol might be used as the standard for EHG acquisition. Table 1 and 2 show that the topological features achieve the highest predictive performance for both preterm birth and mode of delivery. These features quantify the global geometric and connectivity properties of EHG signal trajectories rather than local point-wise variations. Features such as area, perimeter, bending energy, circularity, and variance are computed from reconstructed signal manifolds and are therefore robust to small temporal shifts and amplitude perturbations arising from noise, electrode displacement, and inter-subject variability. Notably, studies forecasting preterm birth generally demonstrate enhanced performance by examining the complete EHG signal, while research predicting the manner of delivery typically yield improved outcomes when concentrating solely on contraction bursts. Preterm labor is not solely characterized by the presence of strong or frequent contractions, but also by subtle changes in uterine excitability, synchronization, and baseline electrical activity that may occur outside clinically visible contraction events. Non-contraction segments contain valuable information related to myometrial readiness, and long-term uterine activation patterns. In contrast, predicting the mode of birth delivery especially the differentiation between vaginal and cesarean deliveries appears to benefit more from contraction-burst-based analysis. The mode of delivery is closely linked to the effectiveness, coordination, and strength of uterine contractions during active labor. Features derived from contraction segments, including amplitude-related measures, entropy-based indices, and topological features, are particularly effective in characterizing contractile organization and mechanical efficiency. As a result, contraction-focused EHG analysis is better suited to distinguishing between effective labor leading to vaginal delivery and dysfunctional contraction patterns that may necessitate cesarean intervention. Based on the results, 4th-order Butterworth filter is recommended as a robust and physiologically meaningful choice for EHG preprocessing. Specifically, a 0.1–3 Hz band-pass range is more suitable for contraction-only analysis, whereas a wider 0–5 Hz range is preferable when analyzing the full EHG signal to retain comprehensive uterine electrical information.

In comparison to preterm birth detection, the review shows that relatively little research has been done to differentiate between vaginal and cesarean deliveries after labor induction. It would be interesting to see more studies on the ability to discern between vaginal births and cesarean births after labor induction. Tables 3 and 4 summarize machine learning-based prediction studies using EHG and alternative modalities, including ultrasound, cardiotocography, and biochemical biomarkers. Across these comparisons, EHG-based features generally demonstrated superior predictive performance, reinforcing their potential clinical utility. In this review, we observe that autoencoders have been widely used for EHG feature learning and dimensionality reduction; therefore, future work should extend this review to emerging deep learning techniques applied to uterine contraction monitoring. Various practical implications of the study’s findings for the practicing obstetrician in the clinical scenario could be highlighted. First of all, from the viewpoint of antenatal care, the potentially important role of the EHG method may lie in the early detection of the risk of preterm deliveries. Finally, from the viewpoint of the clinical scenario of the intrapartum period of childbirth itself, the potentially important role of the information provided by the methods of the study may lie in the detection of the dysfunction of the patterns of efficient labor itself, reducing unplanned cesarean delivery. With respect to the induction of labor itself, the potentially important role of the information provided by the methods of the study may lie in the early prediction of the success of induction procedures.

Conclulsion

There is a great potential for the timely and automated prediction of adverse obstetric outcomes to reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity significantly. In this regard, the review emphasizes EHG as one of the most promising modalities for obstetric monitoring, providing unparalleled and direct insight into uterine electrical activity in a non-invasive and patient-friendly way. This paper comprehensively reviewed existing EHG-based studies that targeted clinically relevant outcomes, namely term-preterm birth prediction, mode of delivery, and labor induction. Across this application, this review has shown that EHG already performed better compared to conventional techniques like intrauterine pressure catheters and tocodynamometry. Advantages and limitations of acquisition protocols, preprocessing strategies, feature extraction methods, and classification models were critically analyzed and are structured into an overview of current methodological trends and gaps. To advance the field, future research should focus on building adequately powered, standardized, and multi-center multichannel EHG datasets to address sample size limitations and inter-subject variability.

Rigorous external validation using independent and heterogeneous cohorts should be adopted as a standard evaluation strategy to ensure model robustness and clinical generalizability. In addition, researchers are encouraged to adhere to established reporting and bias assessment frameworks, such as TRIPOD and PROBAST, to improve transparency and reproducibility. Integrating EHG-derived features with relevant obstetric and maternal clinical parameters should be prioritized to develop robust multimodal prediction models with higher clinical relevance.
