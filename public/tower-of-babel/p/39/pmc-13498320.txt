ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute ischaemic stroke and primary intracerebral haemorrhage are complicated by oropharyngeal dysphagia in more than 50% of patients. Currently, there are no definitive treatments for post-stroke dysphagia (PSD). Pharyngeal electrical stimulation (PES) can drive long-term beneficial changes in the cortical control of swallowing with functionally relevant reorganisation of the swallowing cortex correlating with reduced dysphagia.MethodsThe pharyngeal electrical stimulation for acute stroke dysphagia trial (PhEAST) is an international prospective randomised open-label blinded-endpoint parallel-group phase 4 superiority effectiveness trial. A total of 638 patients with PSD and dependency on nasogastric tube feeding are being recruited within 2–31 days of ictus. Participants are recruited from the UK and Austria and randomised to 6 days of optimised PES (given for 10 min each day) vs. no PES on top of standard care. The primary outcome is the dysphagia severity rating scale, an assessment of swallowing impairment, measured locally on day 14 and centrally at day 90 by trained observers masked to treatment and analysed using repeated measures regression analysis. Secondary outcomes include: functional oral intake scale, feeding status scale, international dysphagia diet standardisation initiative scale, presence of a feeding tube, pneumonia, antibiotic use, health-related quality of life, dependency, disability, frailty, mood, cognition, global impact and health economics at days 14, 90, 180, and 365. Safety measures include death and serious adverse events.ConclusionPhEAST has recruited more than 574 participants from 45 hospitals in the UK and Austria; recruitment will be complete at the end of October 2026. The attached Supplement summarises the protocol and follows the EQUATOR SPIRIT guideline.

Introduction

Acute stroke is common (UK 140 K pa) and complicated by dysphagia (swallowing problems) in >50% of patients, many of whom remain dysphagic a year later [1, 2]. Post-stroke dysphagia (PSD) is an independent predictor of poor outcome associated with aspiration of material into the lungs, pneumonia, malnutrition, and death [2, 3]. Patients often need feeding through an enteral tube, initially via a nasogastric tube and later via a percutaneous endoscopically introduced gastrostomy tube or radiologically inserted gastrostomy. People with PSD have prolonged hospital stay and many end up in long-term institutional care [2, 4]. Hence, PSD negatively impacts participant and carer quality of life and its care is expensive and may be a tipping point for admission to residential or nursing care.

Treatment of PSD

Although PSD may be treated using a number of physical and behavioural techniques, the evidence base is controversial, and there are no definitive treatments [2, 5]. Besides behavioural approaches as used routinely by Speech and Language Therapists, a number of stimulation techniques have been assessed in randomised trials. Central stimulation approaches include transcranial direct current stimulation and transcranial magnetic stimulation. Peripheral stimulation includes acupuncture and neuromuscular electrical stimulation. Although each technique was associated with reduced swallowing impairment and dysphagia, key problems were present throughout. First, most approaches were based on little mechanistic data describing how they might work; second, most trials were small involving less than 100 participants; third, their results were very heterogeneous; fourth, they tended to be of low quality when assessed using GRADE criteria; fifth, most require patients to be awake although early dysphagia is often associated with reduced levels of consciousness (such that treatment needs to be delayed); sixth, some approaches require intensive and prolonged treatment over several weeks (which makes the intervention complex and expensive to deliver). Last and of critical importance, systematic reviews suggest that publication bias is present (manifest as asymmetrical funnel plots) such that efficacy may disappear if apparently missing trials were added. Another intervention is pharyngeal electrical stimulation (PES), which is the focus of this paper.

Pharyngeal Electrical Stimulation

In contrast to some of the earlier listed stimulation techniques, PES has a robust theoretical base and a well-defined treatment paradigm, including requiring only 10 min of treatment per day over 3–6 days. Human swallowing has bilateral representation in the brain with a “dominant” cortex, which may be left- or right-sided and so is unrelated to handedness [6]. So, by chance, dysphagia follows a stroke which affects the dominant swallowing cortex or the non-dominant hemisphere if the dominant cortex or subcortical circuits are already lesioned by a previous stroke or silent strokes; as a result, the risk of dysphagia is increased in recurrent strokes. Swallowing is dependent on afferent feedback via bulbar cranial nerves innervating the pharynx. Increased sensory input from the pharynx, delivered as PES, has been shown to drive long-term beneficial changes in the cortical control of swallowing [7] with reorganisation of the swallowing cortex or expansion of the cortical area that is active during swallowing [7–9]. PES also increases the frequency of spontaneous involuntary swallowing, possibly by activating centres in the brainstem.

PES was initially developed academically by Prof. Shaheen Hamdy at the University of Manchester and then commercially by Phagenesis Ltd., initially a University of Manchester spin-out company. Key studies involving non-ventilated stroke patients are described here and summarised in Table 1. More recently, studies have recruited patients who have been ventilated, required a tracheostomy, and then required decannulation in the presence of dysphagia; these studies are not relevant to the pharyngeal electrical stimulation for acute stroke dysphagia trial (PhEAST) population of non-ventilated stroke patients and so are not discussed further here.

Phase 1 Study

A phase 1 study in human volunteers suggested that PES should be delivered at 5 Hz for 10 min, a paradigm maximising the effect on brain excitability [19, 20].

Phase 2 Trials, Non-Ventilated Stroke

Three UK-based non-commercial phase 2 trials have been completed and published (Table 1) [10, 11, 21]. In a randomised dose-comparison phase 2a trial in patients with subacute stroke, PES reduced radiological aspiration (lower penetration aspiration score, PAS) on videofluoroscopy [10]. PES also reduced swallowing impairment (assessed using the dysphagia severity rating scale, DSRS) and length of stay in hospital in patients with PSD in a sham-controlled parallel group phase 2a trial [10]. In an NIHR RfPB-funded multicentre phase 2b randomised sham-controlled trial, the study was feasible in respect of recruitment, compliance, and retention; PES non-significantly reduced clinical dysphagia and length of stay in hospital [21]. A small single-centre trial done in Egypt suggested that PES improved both FOIS and PAS at day 14 (Table 1) [13].

Phase 3 Trials, Non-Ventilated Stroke

An international randomised single-blind phase 3 trial in 162 patients with a recent ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke and radiological penetration-aspiration (PAS >=3) found that the study was feasible (recruitment, compliance, and retention) and PES was safe (Table 1) [11]. However, PES was not associated with reductions in penetration-aspiration (PAS score) or swallowing impairment (DSRS) relative to sham. A number of possible explanations could account for this outcome; first, stimulation levels were low relative to subsequent studies, e.g., mean 14.8 (standard deviation 7.9) mA as compared to levels exceeding 20 mA (Table 1); second, sham participants received some stimulation during assessment of threshold and tolerance levels at the beginning of each sham treatment session which may have been therapeutic and contributed to improvement in the sham group seen in the trial.

Summary of Available Data

An individual patient data meta-analysis of the first three phase 2ab trials (n = 73) found that PES significantly reduced penetration-aspiration (PAS) and swallowing impairment (DSRS) and was safe and well tolerated [12]. This meta-analysis was updated with the inclusion of the Youssef and STEPS trials and continues to show significant overall beneficial effects of PES on reducing PAS (Fig. 1) and swallowing impairment (Fig. 2) [11, 13]. However, the updated meta-analysis for PAS is confounded by significant heterogeneity (primarily introduced by the neutral phase 3 STEPS trial, Fig. 1), whilst none of the included studies were significant individually for effects on swallow impairment when analysed by comparing change scores (Fig. 2).

Approvals

PES delivered using the Phagenesis Ltd. Phagenyx® system has had a CE Mark since 2012 and FDA approval since 2022. However, in the UK, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) (effectively the gate-keeper of what interventions may be used in the National Health Service) has said that “more research is needed” and that the “procedure should only be done as part of a formal research study and a research ethics committee needs to have approved its use” [22]. This statement and the reality that whilst PES is supported by positive phase 2 studies, the only phase 3 trial was neutral, means that a large definitive randomised controlled trial is urgently needed. The PhEAST trial serves this purpose.

Guidelines vary in their recommendation for PES. In the UK, the Royal College of Physicians guidelines say: “Patients with tracheostomy and severe dysphagia after stroke may be considered for pharyngeal electrical stimulation to aid decannulation where the device is available and it can be delivered by a trained healthcare professional” [23]. However, there is no comment about non-ventilated patients with PSD. In contrast, the American Stroke Association has issued guidance for this group: “In patients with stroke with dysphagia, treatment with pharyngeal electrical stimulation (PES), can be beneficial to reduce dysphagia severity and decrease the risk of aspiration” [24]. These inconsistencies again support the need for a large definitive trial in non-ventilated stroke patients with dysphagia.

Rationale

Although PES has a CE Mark and FDA approval, its clinical use is limited in Europe and the USA and it is unavailable elsewhere. The UK's NICE have recommended that more research is needed on PES for people with neurogenic dysphagia after stroke who do not have a tracheostomy [22]. Here we describe the protocol for PhEAST, a large randomised controlled trial that intends to provide definitive information on the safety and effectiveness of PES. We follow the EQUATOR SPIRIT 2013 guidelines for reporting a protocol for a randomised controlled trial [25].

Methods

PhEAST Trial

Here we describe the protocol for the PhEAST in the attached online supplement (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553643). PhEAST is currently actively recruiting participants, and a few sites have yet to start recruiting. Recruitment will cease at the end of October 2026, with day 90 follow-up completed in early January 2027. We intend to publish a statistical analysis plan in quarter 2/3 2026 and information on baseline characteristics in early 2027. We hope to present the main results at the European Stroke Organisation Conference in May 2027, ideally with parallel publication. The final follow-up at 1 year will complete in October 2027 with presentation of these longer-term data in 2028.

Discussion

PhEAST is designed to be much larger than current PSD trials and aims to provide a definitive result on the effectiveness and safety of PES. If positive, it should influence guidelines and practice worldwide.

Summary

PhEAST is assessing the safety and effectiveness of PES in PSD. The large sample size of 638 patients (exceeding the size of hemicraniectomy and thrombectomy trials) means that a moderate-to-high clinical effect can be detected with high statistical power (90%). The trial has currently recruited more than 574 participants, will finish recruitment at the end of October 2026 and is expected to present the main results in May 2027.
