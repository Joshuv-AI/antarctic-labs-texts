Surgical management of petrous apex trigeminal nerve herniation causing trigeminal neuralgia with long-term outcome: illustrative case
Authors: Jonathan A Tangsrivimol, Guilherme Finger, Matthieu D Weber, Kyle C Wu, Daniel M Prevedello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDPetrous apex cephaloceles (PACs) are infrequent lesions mostly diagnosed as incidental findings. Rare cases may manifest as trigeminal neuralgia (TN).OBSERVATIONSA 51-year-old female presented with a 19-year history of TN. Investigative imaging revealed herniation of the trigeminal nerve into Meckel’s cave through a right-sided petrous apex defect. The patient underwent a right preauricular middle cranial fossa craniotomy and subtemporal extradural approach to access Meckel’s cave. The nerve was released and repositioned, and a fat graft was used to obliterate the petrous apex defect to prevent recurrence. The procedure successfully relieved the patient’s severe pain. At the 6-week postoperative follow-up visit, the patient was free from TN symptoms with no surgical complications. At 18 months, the patient was still free from TN symptoms.LESSONSTo the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are few reported cases of TN caused by PACs in the literature. This study highlights the importance of considering PACs as a rare but potentially reversible cause of TN, especially in patients with refractory symptoms whose imaging demonstrates no neurovascular conflict. The petrous crest is a distinctive pyramid-shaped structure nestled within the central region of the temporal bone. It is positioned between the temporal and occipital bones and terminates at the foramen lacerum. The petrous crest earned the moniker “no man’s land” in reference to the challenging nature of surgical access. This region is associated with various pathological lesions1 that pose a considerable diagnostic and therapeutic challenge for both radiologists and neurosurgeons.2

Petrous apex cephaloceles (PACs) represent a rare class of lesions characterized by their appearance on radiographic imaging, extending from the posterior lateral region of Meckel’s cave into the superomedial portion of the petrous apex.3,4 The differential diagnosis for PAC is broad, encompassing cholesterol granuloma, petrous apex effusions, cholesteatoma, apical petrositis, and mucoceles.5,6 Even though there are different pathologies that may occur in this location, most of the cases are found as incidental findings since they are typically asymptomatic, regardless of if they occur unilaterally or bilaterally. It is worth noting that the term “cephalocele” refers to the broader categorization of four subtypes of herniation.7 We use the term “cephalocele” here in accordance with naming conventions found in the literature for these lesions;3 however, the lesion in this case can be better described as the meningocele subtype with trigeminal nerve herniation.

Considering the rare occurrence of PACs associated with trigeminal neuralgia (TN), this article explores the clinical presentation and surgical management of a 51-year-old female diagnosed with this rare condition. By shedding light on this unique case, this study contributes to a better understanding of PACs, aiding in improved patient care and treatment outcomes.

Illustrative Case

A 51-year-old female with a history of chronic neck pain and bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome presented with 19 years of right-sided sharp shooting electrical pain on her right hemiface at the level of the maxilla. She had been diagnosed with TN previously, and pain management was kept under control with gabapentin and baclofen. However, she stated that her pain had been more frequent and intense during the 3 months prior to her initial neurosurgical visit. A review of records revealed that the patient had undergone brain MRI 26 years earlier to evaluate for multiple sclerosis after presenting with facial numbness; the study was unremarkable. A subsequent MRI study, obtained 13 years later for evaluation of facial pain, first demonstrated a lesion consistent with a suspected PAC.

At presentation, she denied any numbness on the face, facial palsy, tinnitus, vertigo, or hearing loss. She described an almost constant and intense sharp shooting electrical pain in the topography of her right maxillary nerve (V2) distribution with absence of symptoms in the ophthalmic or mandibular divisions. Her tactile triggers included brushing her teeth, wind, hair brushing her face, and washing her face.

Initially, CT was performed and revealed a right petrous bone defect (Fig. 1A and C). Preoperative T2-weighted MRI revealed an extension of the right Meckel’s cistern into the petrous bone, which was associated with abnormal thinning of the trigeminal nerve. Imaging did not reveal any other abnormality, compression at the entry zone, or suspect areas of demyelination within the pons. Although not necessary for the diagnosis of classical TN, we did not note any flow voids distorting the trigeminal nerve along its course, including the entry zone. From this imaging evidence, we suspected that part of the trigeminal nerve was herniating into the petrous bone defect, resulting in tension on and mechanical trauma to the nerve (Fig. 2).

Axial (A–C) and sagittal (D–F) T2-weighted MR images demonstrating a PAC with inferior displacement and herniation of the right trigeminal nerve (arrows).

The patient elected to undergo a preauricular middle cranial fossa craniotomy with a right subtemporal approach to the extradural component of Meckel’s cave. The surgical aims included empiric extradural nerve decompression and buttressing of the herniating component with interposition of a fat graft within the bony defect, with the goal of immediate postoperative pain relief.

Surgical Technique

The patient was positioned in the lateral position with the right side up and the sagittal axis of the head parallel to the floor. A linear incision anterior to the tragus was performed, extending from the zygomatic process to the superior temporal line. Soft tissue dissection and temporalis detachment from the skull were performed, preserving the innervation and vascular supply of the muscle while optimizing exposure of the zygoma and temporal bone.8

Once the skull was exposed and the landmarks identified, a M8 drill bit was used to perform a craniotomy extending down to the level of the middle fossa floor. A Penfield no. 1 was then used to carefully peel the dura over the right temporal lobe. This maneuver was performed in an extradural fashion starting at the middle fossa floor until the foramen spinosum and middle meningeal artery (MMA) were visualized. At this point, the operative microscope was introduced into the field.

The MMA was coagulated with bipolar forceps and cut sharply at its point of entry within the foramen spinosum. The foramen ovale was then identified, located just medial and anterior to the foramen spinosum. A blunt hook insertion into the foramen was used to confirm the location of this anatomical landmark. Subsequently, a Kerrison instrument was utilized to expand the foramen and allow a comprehensive view of the foramen ovale.

A nerve stimulator was then used to identify the location of the greater superior petrosal nerve (GSPN). The GSPN was maintained along the skull base while working in a plane superiorly (Video 1). As the dissection progressed toward the anterior petrous apex, the internal carotid artery was identified along with an area of bony dehiscence, which was located medial and posterior to the foramen ovale.

At this point, a small meningocele with the trigeminal nerve herniating through the bony defect was encountered. Neuronavigational assistance was used to confirm the location. Using a combination of neuronavigation and microscopic inspection, we were able to confirm that the V2 division was partially herniating into the bony defect (Fig. 3A). Further dissection revealed a dural defect of the inferomedial component of Meckel’s cave. The trigeminal nerve, surrounded by arachnoid adhesions, was visualized forming a loop through the defect. Tension on the nerve created by herniation was hypothesized as the cause of TN in this patient. Additionally, adhesions were found between the meninges covering the meningocele and the surrounding bone. Using a microscopic Penfield no. 4 and blunt-tipped instruments, we carefully reduced the meningocele and dissected V2 away from the bony defect (Video 1). During this process, the nerve was freed from surrounding adhesions, facilitating restoration into proper anatomical position (Figs. 3B and 4A–C). To prevent future herniation and buttress the repositioned nerve, harvested fat from the preauricular region was introduced into the vacated cavity. This was then secured in place using Surgicel and Gelfoam. To reinforce dural integrity, a collagen matrix graft was applied over the inferior lateral aspect of the temporal lobe, addressing pinpoint dehiscence in the dura. The cranial bone flap was then repositioned and secured using titanium plates, followed by standard multilayered closure of the soft tissues. Somatosensory evoked potentials remained stable throughout the procedure.

At the 6-week postoperative visit, the patient was free from TN symptoms and had no postoperative complications. She did have mild postoperative facial numbness, which resolved completely by her 6-month follow-up visit. The patient remained on gabapentin under the management of her primary care team, which was reduced postoperatively to one-third of the preoperative amount, as her pain had resolved. She continued at this dose to treat chronic neck pain and bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome predating surgery. Fourteen months postoperatively, she developed headaches and was diagnosed with cervicogenic headaches by her neurology team, prompting an increase in her gabapentin dose. At that time, she was not experiencing TN pain and was still pain free at 18 months.

Discussion

In most cases, classical TN arises from neurovascular conflict with arteries or veins at the nerve’s root entry zone. The presence of vascular compression may be discernible by findings on MRI examinations;9,10 however, instances may arise where no vascular compression is evident. Even though neurovascular conflict is still the most common cause, it is imperative to broaden diagnostic considerations that encompass the approximately 5%–10% of alternative etiologies.11,12 These include multiple sclerosis, lesions of the petrous apex, and cerebellopontine angle tumors that exert pressure on the nerve as it extends from the pons. This case adds to the limited literature describing mechanical entrapment of the trigeminal nerve by a PAC as a surgically reversible cause of TN.

The etiology of PACs remains uncertain; the prevailing theory is that they arise due to alterations in CSF dynamics, resulting in increased intracranial pressure within the congenitally thinned petrous apex bone.3,4 Meckel’s apex is typically smooth and unaffected; however, increased intracranial pressure can potentially cause erosion in parts of the skull where the bone is thinner. In the progressive development of PACs, the posterior edge of Meckel’s cave is most susceptible.13 As it relates to TN, the trigeminal nerve itself courses from the prepontine cistern through Meckel’s cave, located superior to the petrous apex, and subsequently traverses the porus trigeminus and the trigeminal cistern along the posterior border of the cavern. Therefore, bony erosion in this area can render PACs potential sources of CSF leaks, otitis media, vertigo, meningitis, and TN secondary to nerve entrapment.14 We highlight 15 cases of different petrous apex lesions in the literature presenting with trigeminal nerve symptoms: 12 with TN, 2 with CN V palsy, and 1 with trigeminal neuropathic pain (Table 1).3,14–22

Observations

Historically, several investigators have advocated for conservative management when addressing PACs, despite symptomatic presentations. This is primarily due to the intricate and challenging nature of accessing the petrous apex site. However, patients harboring CSF leaks or intractable TN secondary to nerve entrapment should be surgically treated. Currently, diverse approaches including transcranial and endoscopic techniques have emerged for accessing the petrous apex. We chose the preauricular transcranial route primarily to maximize extradural exposure, facilitate nerve repositioning, and effectively obliterate the defect.

For individuals with TN secondary to a PAC, nerve repositioning emerges as a nerve-sparing and potentially curative intervention. In contrast, approaches such as balloon compression, glycerol rhizotomy, radiofrequency thermocoagulation, and Gamma Knife radiosurgery have been described in the context of neurovascular conflict and not nerve entrapment.23 While less invasive, they trade damage to the nerve for a chance at pain relief. These modalities, while minimally invasive, have been shown to provide less durable long-term pain relief outcomes than nerve-sparing, albeit more invasive, interventions such as microvascular decompression. In this context, it is important to recognize that nerve manipulation at surgery increases the risk of injury. Our patient had temporary mild postoperative numbness, which resolved completely by her 6-month follow-up. She did not have any new atypical neuropathic pain at her follow-up visits. Therefore, we believe this temporary numbness likely represented neuropraxia injury from repositioning rather than a true rhizotomy effect experienced by patients who undergo neurolysis or other rhizotomy procedures.

Lessons

This study underscores the importance of considering PACs as a rare although potentially reversible cause of TN. The surgical intervention described in this report involved careful release with repositioning of the herniated trigeminal nerve and fat graft to address the petrous apex defect. We show a favorable outcome with this nerve-sparing technique and hope this case contributes to the understanding and management of PACs associated with TN while emphasizing broader diagnostic considerations for this condition.
