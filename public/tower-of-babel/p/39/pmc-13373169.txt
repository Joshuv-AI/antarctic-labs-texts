Efficient and scalable photochemical coupling of pinacol and aza-pinacol in continuous-flow
Authors: Antonella Ilenia Alfano, Luigi Maria Moreno Tufano, Vincenzo Summa, Margherita Brindisi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotochemistry has experienced a renaissance in organic synthesis as a sustainable approach to chemical transformations. However, batch processes are often limited by the Beer–Lambert law, which reduces light penetration and scalability. Continuous-flow technology overcomes these limitations through uniform irradiation and improved control over reaction conditions. Herein, we developed highly efficient, scalable, and photocatalyst-free continuous-flow protocol for the reductive coupling of carbonyls and imines. Exploiting the intrinsic photoactivity of the substrates and utilizing N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) as a simple sacrificial electron donor, the protocol enables the direct synthesis of vicinal 1,2-diols and 1,2-diamines, crucial structural motifs in medicinal chemistry. The continuous-flow pinacol coupling of aromatic aldehydes afforded 1,2-diols in excellent yields (up to 98%) within a residence time of just 10 minutes, demonstrating a ~ 6.9-fold increase in productivity compared to batch conditions. Similarly, the imino-pinacol coupling provided 1,2-diamines in high yields in 30 minutes without the need for strict deaeration. Further investigations successfully integrated both the imine condensation and the photochemical coupling into a single, fully continuous setup, yielding the target diamine in an 80% yield. Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Photochemistry, Sustainability, Flow chemistry

Introduction

Ketyl radicals are versatile intermediates generated through the reductive activation of carbonyl compounds and play a central role in pinacol coupling reactions, where radical-radical homocoupling leas to the formation of vicinal 1,2-diols1–3. By converting simple functional groups into highly reactive intermediates, they enable the construction of complex molecular architectures via elegant and non-traditional pathways, allowing transformations such as pinacol coupling, Barbier reaction, reductive arylation, allylation, and addition to π-systems4–10. In these processes, the concentration and lifetime of the intermediates are the ultimate gatekeepers, governing the balance between high selectivity and unwanted side products11. The pinacol coupling reaction stands out as a fundamental transformation for synthesizing vicinal 1,2-diols from carbonyls and 1,2-diamines from imines via ketyl or aminyl radical intermediates12–19. Both classes of compounds are essential building blocks: 1,2-diols are prevalent in natural products, pharmaceuticals, and fine chemicals, serving as versatile precursors to epoxides, aminoalcohols, ketons or protected intermediates like acetals (Scheme 1a)20–29. Similarly, vicinal diamines are key precursors for heterocycles and unsymmetrical imines, and they serve as vital ligands in transition metal catalysis (Scheme 1b)30–34. In both cases, their rigid hydrogen-bonding networks are crucial in defining conformational assets and facilitating biological recognition.

Historically, these couplings relied on stoichiometric low-valent metals (e.g., Mg, Zn, Ti, Sm), which often require preactivation and generate significant metallic waste, raising economic and environmental concerns (Scheme 2a)15,17,35–38. To address these drawbacks, more sustainable activation methods have emerged, employing photons or electrons as “traceless” reagents (Scheme 2b)4,39–46,47,48. Driven by energy-efficient light-emitting diodes (LEDs), photochemistry has undergone a renaissance, enabling chemoselective protocols under mild conditions49–52. In 2023, a photocatalyst-free protocol for pinacol coupling marked a significant advance in this quest53. Despite these advances, conventional batch photochemistry remains hindered by physical bottlenecks. Due to the Beer-Lambert Law, limited light penetration restricts efficient irradiation to the outer edges of the vessel. Consequently, scaling up these batch protocols requires extended reaction times, which inherently trigger side reactions from overexposure and product degradation. These limitations persist despite the generally good performance and broad substrate scope reported for batch protocols, indicating that irradiation control rather than reactivity remains a key bottleneck. From a process perspective, it is notable that the pinacol coupling has not yet been widely explored in continuous-flow photochemical reactors, even though flow platforms are particularly well suited to improve irradiation uniformity and photon management54,55.

Traditional batch methods (a, b) and our continuous flow strategy (c) for pinacol and aza-pinacol coupling reactions.

This gap opens a glimmer for future development toward more scalable and controllable light-driven pinacol coupling processes. By miniaturizing reactor channels and providing narrow path lengths, continuous-flow platforms ensure efficient irradiation, improved mass and heat transfer, and safer handling of reactive intermediates55–59. Furthermore, flow photochemistry offers reproducibility, scalability, and the possibility of automation, making it increasingly attractive for both academic and industrial applications60,61. Herein, we report a photocatalyst-free pinacol and imino-pinacol coupling performed under continuous flow conditions (Scheme 2c). By merging the intrinsic photochemical reactivity of carbonyl and imino compounds with the advantages of flow chemistry, we establish a practical, scalable, sustainable and efficient strategy for the direct synthesis of 1,2-diols and 1,2-diamines. Compared to the batch implementation, the reaction under flow conditions shows higher productivity and shorter reaction times, along with improved scalability and sustainability, highlighting the practical advantages of continuous-flow photochemistry.

Results and discussion

Diols

In our quest toward the development of robust flow conditions for the reductive arylation, we initially employed readily available and inexpensive benzaldehyde (€ 60.40/L) as our model substrate. As summarized in Table 1, a thorough systematic exploration of key parameters allowed the identification of the optimum residence time, stoichiometry, solvent, concentration, wavelength and wattage. The flow set-up consisted of a Vapourtec R-series reactor with its UV-150 photo-module equipped with different light sources (i.e., LEDs), stainless steel pumps, a SF-10 laboratory pump as Back Pressure Regulator (BPR) and a tubular reactor coil (PFA, ID: 1/16-inch, V: 10 mL). The optimal conditions were identified as 365 nm, 20 W, 0.5 M MeCN/H2O mixture (3:1), 10 min of residence time, without N2 bubbling, affording the desired product in 95% yield (Entry 1, Table 1). Prior to determining the optimal conditions, we performed an extensive optimization of key reaction variables. Compared to the same batch procedure, the total processing time was reduced from 3 h to 1 h, operating at the minimum flow rate achievable with our pumping system, without modifying any other parameters (Entry 2, Table 1). The corresponding diol 2a product was obtained with 97% yield in the presence of DIPEA under the irradiation of 390 nm light, N2, 0.1 M of concentration. Building on this, and with the aim of further improving the sustainability metrics and the productivity of the process, additional reaction conditions were explored. In an initial attempt to enhance sustainability, the solvent was changed to an EtOH/H₂O mixture (3:1), allowing the reaction time to be reduced to 30 min; however, under these conditions, a moderate 78% yield was achieved (Entry 3, Table 1). Reverting to MeCN, which proved to be the most effective solvent, and avoiding the use of water, resulted in an improved yield of 92% (Entry 4, Table 1). To evaluate the effect on productivity, the substrate concentration was increased to 0.8 M; however, a reduced yield of 81% was observed due to incomplete benzaldehyde conversion under these conditions (Entry 5, Table 1). Following the sustainability common thread, a further attempt was performed interrogating the outcome of halving the amount of DIPEA to 1.0 equivalent. As partially expected, these experimental conditions led to a decreased 70% yield (Entry 6, Table 1). Prior to selecting 20 W as the optimal irradiation power, a higher power setting of 45 W was also tested, affording a comparable yield of 94% (Entry 7, Table 1).

In addition, a switch-off experiment (see Supporting Information, Fig. S1) was performed to evaluate the minimum residence time required for efficient product formation. The results showed that a significant portion of the product (60% yield) is already formed within the first 3 min of irradiation, indicating that near-maximum conversion is achieved at very short irradiation times under the applied conditions (Entry 8, Table 1). To confirm the essential role of the light, control experiments conducted in the dark confirmed the photochemical nature of the transformation, as no product formation was detected in the standard reaction conditions (Entry 9, Table 1). Additional optimization and control experiments were performed and are reported in the Supporting Information (Tables S1, S2 and S3).

Having the optimized parameters in hand (MeCN/H2O (3:1), 0.5 M substrate concentration, 2.0 equiv. of DIPEA, 28 °C and a 10-min residence time), a packed-bed column containing Amberlyst-15 (4.7 mmol/g) was integrated downstream of the photoreactor to sequester the DIPEA used in the photochemical step. Notably, this inline quenching protocol circumvented the need for conventional purification, providing the target compound in high purity directly from the flow stream. The scalability of the continuous-flow process was subsequently evaluated using model compound 2a. An initial scale-up from 0.14 to 1.0 mmol proceeded smoothly, affording the product in 88% yield. Further expansion to a 3.0 mmol scale maintained high efficiency, delivering the target compound in 86% yield with negligible loss in performance. Overall, these results demonstrate that the reaction was successfully scaled up, achieving approximately 7-fold and 21-fold increases while maintaining high yields. This highlights the excellent scalability and robustness of the developed flow protocol.

Next, we explored the scope of our continuous-flow protocol by investigating a variety of substituted aromatic aldehydes and a ketone (Scheme 3). Initially, 3,5-dimethylbenzaldehyde afforded the diol 2b in an excellent yield (98%). Notably, the method is compatible with halogen substituents (–Cl and –F) at the para and meta positions, which may serve as versatile synthetic handles for further transformations. These substrates provided diol products 2c, 2d, and 2e in high yields. Using electron-donating groups, such as methoxy, benzyloxy and thiomethyl groups, afforded the corresponding diols 2f, 2g and 2h in excellent yields (>98%). Interestingly, the introduction of electron-withdrawing groups led to different outcomes, strongly relying upon substituent nature. While a para-trifluoromethyl group afforded compound 2i with an excellent 98% yield, the presence of a nitrile group in para position provided compound 2j with a moderate 75% yield. A benzaldehyde with a para-tetrazole substituent also gave the product 2k in an high yield (>90%). To further prove the versatility of our protocol and the compatibility with classically employed protecting groups, we interrogated more complex, pharmacologically relevant structural templates. Reaction of a para-Boc-protected piperazinyl benzaldehyde afforded diol 2l in 88% yield. This substitution pattern is highly advantageous for developing bioactive pharmaceutical scaffolds, as the nitrogen can be further functionalized following the removal of the Boc protecting group. However, using a para-Boc-protected methylamine resulted in a slightly lower yield of 62% for compound 2m. Heteroaromatic aldehydes, like furfural, gave the diol 2n in a 92% yield. Additionally, to further expand our protocol, we tested as proof of concept one aryl ketone; gratifyingly, this attempt produced the highly substituted pinacol product (2o) in a 98% yield.

The continuous flow reaction significantly outperforms the previous corresponding batch process. While the batch reaction at 0.1 M required 3 h to afford 0.392 mmol of product (98% yield), the flow reaction at 0.5 M reached 0.475 mmol of product in only 1 min (96% yield). This corresponds to a productivity of 28.8 mmol/h and a molar throughput increased to 0.900 mmol/h in flow compared to only 0.131 mmol/h in batch. These results highlight that continuous flow enables drastic reductions in reaction time while massively increasing efficiency, demonstrating its potential for high-throughput and scalable syntheses. Furthermore, the continuous flow synthesis for compound 2a achieves an E-factor of ~20, compared to ~986 in the corresponding batch process, with no need for work-up or chromatographic purification, reflecting a dramatic reduction in waste and a substantial improvement in the environmental footprint of the reaction.

Diamines

Following the successful development of the continuous-flow protocol for the synthesis of 1,2-diols, we applied a similar approach for the preparation of vicinal 1,2-diamines via an imino-pinacol coupling. To establish the optimal reaction conditions for this transformation, we selected the reaction between the benzaldehyde 1a and an affordable benzylamine 3a (95.20 €/L) as our model compounds. The transformation follows a two-step sequence. Initially, we optimized the imine 4a condensation in batch to ensure rapid, quantitative intermediate formation (see Supporting Information, Table S4). By replacing the toxic DCM solvent with the greener MeCN, increasing the substrate concentration, and reducing the quantity of molecular sieves as a dehydrating agent, the reaction time was shortened from 3 h to 15 min. Subsequently, the photochemical coupling was systematically investigated and optimized under continuous-flow conditions (Table 2).

As summarized in Table 2 (Entry 1), optimal conditions were established using our imine (0.5 M) in MeCN/H2O (3:1) with 2 equiv. of DIPEA, 365 nm light (60 W), and a 30-min residence time, affording product 5a in 95% yield. Initially, decreasing the concentration to 0.1 M and the temperature to 33 °C under nitrogen reduced efficiency to 82% (Entry 2, Table 2). However, restoring the temperature to 38 °C at 0.1 M allowed the yield to recover to 93% (Entry 3, Table 2), highlighting the importance of fine thermal control within the microfluidic reactor. Notably, the MeCN/H2O system at 0.1 M yielded 95% even without nitrogen deaeration. Further tests showed that nitrogen bubbling does not significantly impact efficiency at 0.5 M, demonstrating the robustness of our protocol and further enhancing operational simplicity (Entry 4, Table 2). Operating without deaeration offers a practical advantage by eliminating gas sparging and preventing potential material loss. Conversely, to further improve the productivity, we increased the concentration to 0.65 M, but a drop of yield was observed (Entry 5, Table 2), most likely ascribable to solubility issues that destabilized the flow; on the contrary, the use of acetonitrile alone at 0.5 M afforded the product in 90% yield (Entry 6, Table 2). Finally, reducing the residence time to 10 min caused the yield to drop to 35% (Entry 7, Table 2), confirming that 30 min is required for full conversion. Also in this case, a control experiment confirmed that the process is strictly photochemically driven (Entry 8, Table 2).

With the optimal continuous-flow parameters in hand, we explored the substrate scope, examining the effect of substituents on the benzylamine and benzaldehyde rings on imine formation and subsequent vicinal diamine synthesis (Scheme 4). Evaluation of different benzylamines revealed broad electronic tolerance, with both isomers formed in most cases. An electron-donating substituent (e.g., para-methoxy) facilitated the smooth formation of the diamine, as observed for compound 5b (93%). Strong electron-withdrawing groups were also well-tolerated, as demonstrated by the addition of para-trifluoromethyl or 3,5-bistrifluoromethyl derivatives, which afforded the products 5c and 5d with 95% and 93% yields. In the last two attempts, unexpectedly, only one isomer was observed, indicating that the trifluoromethyl substituent can direct the reaction toward the formation of a single isomer. The introduction of a halogen, such as a chlorine atom at the para position, useful for further modification, led to a slightly lower but still synthetically useful yield of 70% for compound 5e. Systematic modifications of the benzaldehyde ring were then performed while keeping the benzylamine fragment unsubstituted. To evaluate the impact of increased steric bulk, para-phenylbenzylamine was employed as the amine source, affording compound 5f in 80% yield.

After establishing the formation of diamine by exploring the benzylamine moiety, we sought to expand our methodology. Systematic modifications of the benzaldehyde ring were performed while keeping the benzylamine fragment unsubstituted. 3,5-dimethyl group provided an excellent yield of 92% for compound 5g. When testing halogen-substituted benzaldehydes, the para-chloro derivative yielded 95% of the desired product 5h, whereas the para-fluoro analogue unexpectedly led to a much lower yield of 38% for compound 5i. Replacement of benzaldehyde with an extended aromatic system, such as 2-naphthaldehyde, or an electron-donating benzyloxy-substituted benzaldehyde, afforded products 5j and 5k, both in 90% yield. To further demonstrate the robustness of the methodology, we evaluated crossed combinations using the highly reactive para-trifluoromethyl-benzylamine, selected because it had previously afforded only a single isomer with benzaldehyde, coupled with different benzaldehydes. These mixed systems performed exceptionally well: the reaction with 3,5-dimethylbenzaldehyde gave a 94% yield for compound 5l, the combination with para-chlorobenzaldehyde provided 5m in 88%, and the coupling with 2- naphthaldehyde resulted in a 95% yield for diamine 5n. In contrast to the previous observations, the presence of substituents on the benzaldehyde led to the formation of two isomers. In the imino-pinacol coupling, the high diastereoselectivity observed with CF₃-substituted benzylamines is not intrinsic to the CF₃ group itself, but arises from a sterically enforced preorganization of the transition state, which can be lost when increased steric demand on the aldehyde partner restores competing coupling geometries. Limitations in the substrate scope were attributed principally to incomplete conversion of the imine intermediate or the limited solubility of specific precursors in the reaction medium (see Supporting Information, Fig. S2).

To fully leverage the potential of our microfluidic setup, the imine condensation and photochemical reductive coupling were integrated into a unified, telescoped continuous-flow process (Scheme 5). To streamline the procedure, we incorporated a packed-bed reactor for the initial condensation step. Although an aqueous acetonitrile mixture was utilized in our original protocol for the substrate scope, our preliminary optimization revealed that acetonitrile (MeCN) alone provided nearly optimal results, with only a marginal 3–4% decrease in yield compared to the MeCN/H2O mixture. Consequently, H2O was omitted to establish a unified solvent system, facilitating a seamless transition between the packed-bed and photochemical modules in a single continuous-flow sequence. To this end, we performed several preliminary tests to verify whether the presence of the DIPEA donor (in the 2.0 equivalents required for the second step) could influence the initial imine formation. Having confirmed experimentally that the base does not interfere with the condensation process, a single acetonitrile solution containing benzaldehyde 1a, benzylamine 3a, and DIPEA was pumped directly through an in-line packed-bed reactor (Scheme 5) containing 4 Å molecular sieves to promote the continuous generation of the imine intermediate 4a. During this phase, we tested various column sizes and different amounts of molecular sieves, sometimes mixed with inert materials like cotton wool or sand to improve the packing of the column. The results of this screening are reported in the Supporting Information (Table S8). The exiting stream, containing the formed imine and the already present DIPEA, was then directly introduced into the photochemical reactor under the previously optimized conditions (365 nm LED, 60 W, 38 °C, 30 min residence time). The best configuration of this fully continuous setup afforded the desired 1,2-diamine 5a in 80% yield.

Telescoped approach towards the synthesis of diamine 5a.

The batch reaction provides the product in two distinct steps: first the imine formation using toxic dichloromethane for 3 h and the photochemical step that requires 10 h in, affording an overall 89% yield, corresponding to 0.172 mmol of product. In contrast, under our flow conditions, the reaction proceeds in two combined steps requiring only 2 min and 30 min, respectively (total residence time 32 min), affording 80% yield, corresponding to 0.06 mmol of product. The flow process significantly enhanced reaction efficiency, achieving a molar throughput of 0.281 mmol/h and a productivity of 0.113 mmol/h, which represents a nearly ninefold increase over the batch system’s performance. The continuous flow synthesis of the diamine drastically reduces waste compared to the batch process. The batch reaction, requiring work-up and column purification, has an E-factor of ~1227, whereas the flow process achieves high yield with minimal purification and an E-factor of ~147, representing a nearly sevenfold reduction in waste and a clear improvement in sustainability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we report an efficient flow approach to access 1,2-diols and 1,2-diamine from cheap and readily available starting materials. The versatility of this approach was demonstrated through the synthesis of a diverse library of aromatic and heteroaromatic benzaldehydes for the diols, alongside the streamlined production of 1,2-diamines. Our continuous flow process is characterized by high efficiency as well as reproducibility. By employing a UV photoreactor and a packed column for in-line quenching, we achieve high throughput, high yields, and short residence time, which render this approach suitable for further industrial development.

The proposed protocol, due to the mildness of the conditions employed (in terms of temperature, residence time and light source potency), and to its versatility and chemoselectivity, holds significant potential for a variety of applications, ranging from organic synthesis to catalyst preparation, as well as medicinal chemistry synthetic protocols, including efficient scaffold derivatization and late-stage functionalization.

Methods

Unless otherwise stated, all solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. Also, unless otherwise stated, all substrates and reagents were purchased from Fluorochem or Sigma-Aldrich and used as received.

1H-NMR13,C and 19F spectra were recorded on 400 MHz instrument and are reported relative to the residual solvents: CDCl3 (δ7.26 ppm) and DMSO-d6 (δ2.50 ppm). A weighed amount of analyte (about 5.0–10.0 mg) was dissolved in 600 μL of deuterated chloroform or dimethyl sulfoxide (CDCl3 or DMSO-d6). The mixture was transferred into a 5 mm NMR tube, and the spectra were acquired on a Bruker Advance 400 MHz spectrometer. Splitting patterns are described as singlet (s), doublet (d), triplet (t), quartet (q) and broad (br); the values of chemical shifts (δ) are given in ppm and coupling constants (J) in Hertz (Hz). NMR data were processed with MestreNova (Version 8.1.1, Mestrelab Research).

Continuous flow experiments were performed on a Vapourtec R-series system equipped with stainless steel pumps and an SF-10 laboratory pump as Back Pressure Regulator (BPR) (1 bar, Vapourtec). For photochemical experiments, the UV-150 module was used in combination with a high-power LED (365 nm) regulated between 20 and 70 W and cooled to 25–38 °C by passing a stream of compressed air through the reactor unit. Reactor coils were made of PFA tubing (i.d. 1/16 inch) with a volume of 10 mL.

TLC was performed on Merck pre-coated Silica gel 60 F254 aluminium plates with realisation by UV irradiation at 254 nm, KMnO4 and ninhydrin stain. Column chromatography was performed using 230–400 mesh, 60 Å pore diameter silica gel. ESI-MS spectra analysis was carried out on a mass spectrometer LTQ-XL.
