Investigating apoptosis in peripheral blood mononuclear cells among the elderly in the post-COVID-19 era
Authors: Elaheh Abiri, Najmeh Hemmatian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimThe COVID-19 pandemic has left a lasting imprint on immune function, particularly in the elderly—a population already vulnerable to immunosenescence. While acute and long-COVID immune responses have been widely studied, the long-term apoptotic behavior of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) remains underexplored. This study aims to investigate the legacy of SARS-CoV-2 on PBMC apoptosis in elderly individuals during the post-COVID era, shedding light on potential persistent immune dysregulation.Materials and methodsIn this cross-sectional study, PBMCs were isolated from peripheral blood samples of elderly individuals (> 65 years old) with a documented history of COVID-19 infection at least six months prior. Using multiparametric flow cytometry, we quantified early and late apoptosis markers (Annexin V/PI), mitochondrial membrane potential disruption (ΔΨm), and expression of pro-apoptotic (Bax, Caspase-3) and anti-apoptotic (Bcl-2) proteins. Statistical analyses were performed to assess intergroup differences and correlations with clinical history. This study was conducted in 2025.ResultsElderly post-COVID individuals exhibited a significantly elevated proportion of apoptotic PBMCs compared to controls (p < 0.01), particularly within the CD4 + and CD8 + T-cell subsets. Mitochondrial depolarization and increased Bax/Bcl-2 ratios indicated a shift toward intrinsic apoptotic pathways. Caspase-3 activation was also heightened in the post-COVID group. Notably, apoptotic burden correlated with time since infection and severity of initial illness.DiscussionOur findings suggest a prolonged apoptotic signature in the immune cells of elderly individuals following recovery from COVID-19. These alterations may reflect a sustained immune exhaustion or maladaptive remodeling of lymphocyte populations, potentially contributing to impaired immunosurveillance and increased vulnerability to secondary infections or chronic inflammatory conditions.ConclusionCOVID-19 may cast a long immunological shadow in the elderly, with persistent PBMC apoptosis representing a novel facet of post-viral immune dysregulation. Flow cytometry reveals a unique apoptotic phenotype that could serve as a biomarker for long-term immune health and guide post-pandemic clinical management strategies for aging populations.Keywords: Post-COVID syndrome, Elderly immunity, Apoptosis, PBMCs, Flow cytometry

Background and aim

The COVID-19 pandemic, while largely transitioning into an endemic phase, has left behind more than clinical memories—it has potentially rewritten immune landscapes, especially in the elderly. Aging is intrinsically associated with immunosenescence and altered apoptotic responses, rendering older individuals more susceptible not only to severe SARS-CoV-2 infection but also to long-term immune dysfunction [15-]. While acute-phase immune alterations in COVID-19 have been well characterized, the long-term apoptotic behavior of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in elderly survivors remains a largely uncharted territory [1, 2]. Emerging evidence suggests that viral infections, particularly those as systemic as COVID-19, may induce sustained immune reprogramming, including persistent activation or exhaustion of immune cells. Yet, it is unclear whether this leads to an enhanced apoptotic tendency in key immune populations months after recovery—potentially weakening the immune defense or contributing to post-viral syndromes [3, 4]. This study aims to explore the enduring impact of COVID-19 on PBMC apoptosis in elderly individuals during the post-pandemic era. By employing high-resolution flow cytometry, we assess not only the presence of apoptosis but its qualitative and quantitative characteristics across immune cell subsets. Our objective is to uncover whether SARS-CoV-2 leaves a pro-apoptotic footprint in aged immune systems—and if so, to what extent this could inform future strategies for monitoring immune resilience or dysfunction in the elderly post-COVID population. The COVID-19 pandemic has fundamentally altered global health, but its most enduring impact may be on the human immune system—particularly in the older individuals. Older adults, already burdened by immunosenescence, were disproportionately affected during the pandemic, experiencing higher morbidity and mortality rates. Although many have since recovered, there is growing concern that SARS-CoV-2 may leave lasting imprints on the immune system, even after the virus is no longer detectable. The post-COVID era presents a critical opportunity to investigate these potential long-term effects and to understand how immune function in elderly individuals may have been irreversibly shaped by the pandemic [5–8]. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)—which include lymphocytes and monocytes—play a central role in orchestrating immune responses. Apoptosis, or programmed cell death, is a crucial regulatory mechanism that maintains immune homeostasis. Within the context of the immune system, lymphocytes play a vital role as they are responsible for identifying and eliminating pathogens. However, a misfit arises when apoptosis becomes excessive or dysregulated among lymphocyte populations. This dysregulation can lead to significant consequences, such as compromising overall immunity. As a result, individuals may experience chronic inflammation, an increased susceptibility to infections, or the development of autoimmune disorders, where the immune system mistakenly attacks the body’s own tissues. Understanding these dynamics is essential for addressing the impacts of aberrant apoptosis on immune health. In elderly individuals recovering from COVID-19, such disruptions may be amplified due to age-related vulnerabilities, yet this area remains underexplored [9–16]. Most current studies on post-COVID immunology focus on antibody persistence or cytokine profiles, with relatively little attention paid to cellular apoptosis. Moreover, few have specifically targeted the elderly—a demographic that not only bore the brunt of the pandemic but may now face hidden consequences in the form of subtle immune decay. Understanding whether COVID-19 has initiated a sustained apoptotic phenotype in PBMCs could reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism of immune deterioration in aging populations. To address this knowledge gap, the present study investigates the apoptotic behavior of PBMCs in elderly individuals with a history of COVID-19, compared to age-matched uninfected controls. Utilizing advanced flow cytometry techniques, we assess key markers of apoptosis, including Annexin V binding, propidium iodide uptake, mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), and the expression of pro- and anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bax, Bcl-2, and Caspase-3. By mapping these apoptotic patterns, we aim to identify whether COVID-19 acts as a long-term modulator of immune cell fate and function in the elderly. The central aim of this study is to uncover whether a legacy of enhanced or altered apoptosis persists in the immune systems of elderly individuals post-COVID-19, and to determine the potential implications for immune resilience, vulnerability to future infections, and age-related immune health.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional observational study aimed to evaluate the apoptotic profiles of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in elderly individuals with a prior history of COVID-19 infection. Participants were recruited from outpatient clinics and community health centers in Damghan, Iran, between [2024] and [2025]. Two groups were included: Post-COVID Group: Elderly individuals (≥ 65 years) who had laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection at least six months prior to enrollment and had fully recovered. Control Group: Age- and sex-matched elderly individuals with no history of COVID-19, confirmed via negative serology and absence of symptoms or PCR confirmation during the pandemic. Exclusion criteria included current infections, autoimmune diseases, malignancies, immunosuppressive therapy, or recent vaccinations (within the last 3 months). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee [IR.IAU.DAMGHAN.REC.1400.005], with all procedures performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Vaccination status of donors

Vaccination records were obtained from official health documentation and participant interviews. For each donor, the following data were recorded: vaccination status (vaccinated or unvaccinated), vaccine type (Sinopharm [BBIBP-CorV], AstraZeneca [ChAdOx1 nCoV-19], or Sputnik V), number of doses received (1–3), and the interval between last vaccination and blood sampling. Sample collection and PBMC isolation

Peripheral blood samples (10 mL) were collected in EDTA-coated tubes. PBMCs were isolated within 2 h of collection using density gradient centrifugation with Ficoll-Paque™ PLUS (GE Healthcare, Cat# 17–1440-03). Briefly, blood samples were diluted 1:1 with PBS and carefully layered over Ficoll in a 15 mL conical tube, followed by centrifugation at 400 g for 30 min at room temperature with the brake off. After centrifugation, the distinct PBMC layer (buffy coat) located at the plasma–Ficoll interface was carefully aspirated using a sterile Pasteur pipette. The harvested cells were transferred to a new tube, washed three times with PBS (300 g, 10 min each) to remove platelets and Ficoll residues, and finally resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Cat# 11875-093) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat# 10082147). Cell viability was assessed using Trypan Blue (Sigma, Cat# T8154) exclusion, and viable cells were counted using a hemocytometer.

Apoptosis assessment by flow cytometry

Early and late apoptosis were measured using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (BioLegend, Cat# 640914) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Specifically, 1 × 10^6 PBMCs were stained with Annexin V-FITC (Clone AV38) and propidium iodide (PI, Sigma Cat# P4170), then analyzed within 1 h. Mitochondrial depolarization was assessed with JC-1 dye (Thermo Fisher, Cat# T3168). For intracellular apoptotic markers, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Cytofix/Cytoperm™ kit (BD Biosciences, Cat# 554714) and stained with: Anti-Bax (Clone 2D2, BD Biosciences, Cat# 556467, PE-conjugated). Anti-Bcl-2 (Clone 100, BD Biosciences, Cat# 556357, FITC-conjugated). Anti-active Caspase-3 (Clone C92-605, BD Biosciences, Cat# 550821, Alexa Fluor® 647-conjugated).

Flow cytometry acquisition and analysis

Samples were run on a BD FACSCanto™ II. At least 10,000 events were acquired per sample. Compensation controls and fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were applied. Data were analyzed using FlowJo™ v10. Apoptosis was quantified as the percentage of Annexin V⁺/PI⁻ (early) and Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ (late) cells. Median fluorescence intensity (MFI) was used for Bax, Bcl-2, and Caspase-3.

Gating strategy for flow cytometry analysis

Specifically, CD3, CD4, and CD8 antibodies were utilized to identify T cell populations. Initially, live cells were gated using a viability dye to exclude dead cells from the analysis. Following this, CD3 + populations were selected to distinguish T cells, with subsequent gating on CD4 + and CD8 + T cells for functional assays. Dead cells were excluded using a fixable viability dye (BioLegend, Cat# 423107). CD3 (Clone UCHT1, BioLegend Cat# 300415), CD4 (Clone RPA-T4, BioLegend Cat# 300507), and CD8 (Clone RPA-T8, BioLegend Cat# 301007) were used to identify T cell subsets.

Statistical analysis

Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson or Spearman methods based on data distribution. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Graphical representation

Graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism software. Bar graphs were constructed to illustrate the differences in apoptotic profiles between groups, with individual data points overlaid on each bar to represent the variability among participants (one dot per patient).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 60 elderly participants were enrolled, including 30 post-COVID individuals and 30 age- and sex-matched controls. The mean age was 72.4 ± 4.8 years, with a male-to-female ratio of 1:1.1. There were no statistically significant differences in baseline demographics (age, sex, BMI, comorbidities) between the two groups (p > 0.05), ensuring comparability.

Participant vaccination characteristics

Overall vaccination coverage was high across both groups; unvaccinated donors were present in both the post-COVID and control cohorts. Vaccine platforms were predominantly inactivated (BBIBP-CorV) with additional use of adenoviral vector vaccines (ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 and Sputnik V). The interval between last vaccination and sampling was ≥ 4 months for all vaccinated donors. Full per-donor details (platform, doses, and interval) are reported in Table 1.

Immunological and comorbidity profiles

The most common comorbidities across both groups were hypertension (42% overall) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (35% overall). A smaller proportion of participants reported ischemic heart disease (18%) or chronic respiratory disease (10%). The prevalence of comorbidities did not differ significantly between the post-COVID and control groups (p > 0.05), suggesting appropriate matching.

Apoptosis in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)

Flow cytometric analysis revealed a significantly higher percentage of apoptotic PBMCs in the post-COVID group compared to controls. Early apoptosis (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻) was observed in 22.6% ± 6.1% of PBMCs in post-COVID individuals vs. 11.3% ± 4.2% in controls (p < 0.05). Late apoptosis (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺) was elevated at 18.9% ± 5.8% in post-COVID subjects vs. 9.4% ± 3.7% in controls (p < 0.05). When stratified by cell subset, CD4⁺ T cells and CD8⁺ T cells showed significantly increased apoptosis in the post-COVID group (both p < 0.05), suggesting a lymphocyte-specific vulnerability. Mitochondrial dysfunction in PBMCs

Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential using JC-1 dye demonstrated increased mitochondrial depolarization in the post-COVID group: The green/red fluorescence ratio (indicative of loss of ΔΨm) was significantly higher in post-COVID individuals (2.4 ± 0.5) compared to controls (1.3 ± 0.3, p < 0.05), confirming activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway.

Expression of Apoptosis-Related proteins

Intracellular staining showed altered expression of key apoptotic regulators in PBMCs from post-COVID participants: Bax expression (pro-apoptotic): Elevated MFI in post-COVID group (1,825 ± 240) vs. controls (1,130 ± 180, p < 0.05). Bcl-2 expression (anti-apoptotic): Decreased in post-COVID group (790 ± 150) compared to controls (1,220 ± 200, p < 0.05). Bax/Bcl-2 ratio was significantly higher in post-COVID subjects, suggesting a strong pro-apoptotic shift. Active Caspase-3 levels were also significantly increased in the post-COVID group (p < 0.05), indicating execution-phase apoptosis.

Correlation with COVID-19 severity and time since infection

Among post-COVID participants, higher apoptotic indices were positively correlated with: Severity of initial infection (mild, moderate, severe): r = 0.61, p < 0.05. Shorter time since recovery (< 9 months): r = − 0.45, p = 0.03, suggesting partial normalization over time in some individuals. These findings indicate a persistently altered apoptotic profile in PBMCs of elderly individuals who have recovered from COVID-19, especially those who experienced moderate to severe illness. Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the enduring impact of COVID-19 on the immune systems of elderly individuals, revealing a sustained pro-apoptotic phenotype in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) months after viral recovery. A key strength of our study is the deliberate exclusion of individuals with ongoing long COVID symptoms. This distinction is essential because immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation have been reported in symptomatic long COVID cohorts, which could confound interpretation of apoptosis-related findings. By focusing on elderly donors who had clinically recovered from COVID-19 and remained asymptomatic for at least six months, our results demonstrate that a sustained pro-apoptotic phenotype in PBMCs can persist even in the absence of long COVID. Thus, our findings highlight that COVID-19 may induce durable immunological “scars” in the elderly, independent of ongoing symptomatic sequelae. By employing multiparametric flow cytometry, we demonstrated that elderly post-COVID individuals exhibit significantly increased levels of apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and altered expression of key apoptotic regulators compared to age-matched controls. These findings unveil a previously underappreciated layer of immune dysregulation that may contribute to the complex post-COVID syndrome and immunosenescence in aging populations [16]. Our observations of elevated early and late apoptosis in PBMCs, particularly within CD4 + and CD8 + T lymphocytes, reinforce conclusions drawn in prior studies, indicating that SARS-CoV-2 infection leaves a lasting imprint on the adaptive immune system. Increased apoptosis may underlie impaired T cell function and reduced immune surveillance capacity, potentially explaining the heightened susceptibility of elderly post-COVID patients to secondary infections and vaccine hyporesponsiveness [17–22]. The marked mitochondrial depolarization and increased Bax/Bcl-2 ratio further indicate activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, reinforcing the concept of mitochondrial involvement in long-term immune cell dysfunction following COVID-19, consistent with findings in other respiratory infections [22–26]. Our results extend previous research focused primarily on acute immune responses and cytokine storms by revealing long-term alterations in cellular fate. The positive correlation between levels of apoptosis and initial COVID-19 severity aligns with clinical observations regarding profound immune exhaustion in severe cases [27–31]. Additionally, the inverse relationship with time since infection suggests some level of partial recovery; however, it also raises concerns about persistent immune vulnerability in certain individuals [32–34]. The urgency for longitudinal monitoring of immune cell viability and function in elderly COVID-19 survivors is underscored by our findings. Persistent apoptosis could serve as a biomarker for identifying patients at risk of immune decline, providing vital information to guide interventions such as immunomodulatory therapies or tailored vaccination strategies [35–38]. Furthermore, this apoptotic signature may reflect broader mechanisms of age-related immune remodeling accelerated by viral insult, thus opening avenues for targeted research into therapies aimed at restoring mitochondrial health and apoptotic balance [36]. While this study primarily focuses on the elderly, the implications for other vulnerable groups and chronic viral infections warrant further investigation. Future studies should explore the mechanistic links between persistent apoptosis, inflammation, and clinical outcomes in post-COVID populations, reinforcing the need for a more comprehensive literature review encompassing recent studies on the long-term effects of COVID-19 on immunity. Our findings contribute a crucial piece to the evolving puzzle of COVID-19’s long-term immunological legacy and highlight the importance of safeguarding immune resilience in aging societies. In our study, we primarily focused on the intrinsic apoptotic pathway to characterize the persistent apoptosis observed in T cells (CD4 + and CD8+) among elderly individuals following COVID-19 recovery. This focus is justified given the established role of mitochondrial dysfunction and the dysregulation of pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic proteins in the intrinsic pathway, which has been shown to significantly impact T cell functionality and survival. However, the extrinsic apoptotic pathway, activated through death receptors such as Fas (CD95) and TRAIL receptors, also plays a crucial role in regulating apoptosis in immune cells, including T lymphocytes. The extrinsic pathway is particularly significant in the context of viral infections, where immune cell activation and subsequent apoptosis are necessary to resolve infection and maintain immune homeostasis [36–38]. While our study did not directly assess the extrinsic apoptotic pathway, the findings of increased apoptosis in T cells could suggest its involvement, especially considering the immune dysregulation associated with COVID-19. The release of inflammatory cytokines and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) during severe infection could lead to upregulation of death receptors on T cells, potentially triggering extrinsic apoptosis as a response to heightened immune activation or infection-induced cell stress [17, 26]. Previous studies have identified increased expression of death receptors in T cells following viral infections, which may provide indirect evidence supporting the hypothesis that the extrinsic pathway contributes to the enhanced apoptosis noted in our findings [15, 30]. In future studies, incorporating additional flow cytometry panels or functional assays to quantitate the expression levels of relevant death receptors and ligands will be critical for elucidating the potential role of the extrinsic pathway in T cell apoptosis. This inclusive approach will enhance our understanding of immune dysregulation in post-viral syndromes and inform the development of targeted interventions aimed at restoring effective immune responses in affected populations. Through this broadened perspective, we aim to contribute to the evolving field of immunology concerning COVID-19 and its long-term impacts on immune function, particularly in vulnerable populations such as the elderly.

Limitations of the study

The study involved a relatively small sample size, which may limit statistical power and the ability to generalize findings to a broader population. Larger studies are necessary to confirm the observed effects and to enhance the robustness of the conclusions drawn. Although rigorous techniques for FACS analysis and data interpretation were employed, the exclusive focus on T cell populations may not fully capture the complexities of the immune response during and after COVID-19 infection. Future studies should consider a more comprehensive approach that includes other immune cell types to better understand the interplay among various immune populations. Participants in this study were predominantly elderly individuals who had recovered from COVID-19. Consequently, the findings may not be fully applicable to younger populations or those with different comorbidities. Further research in diverse cohorts is essential to validate the implications of these results across different demographic groups and clinical backgrounds.

Conclusion

This study reveals that COVID-19 leaves a lasting pro-apoptotic footprint on peripheral blood mononuclear cells in elderly individuals, characterized by enhanced lymphocyte apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and dysregulated apoptotic protein expression. These persistent changes suggest that SARS-CoV-2 infection accelerates immune cell aging and compromises immune resilience long after clinical recovery. Monitoring apoptosis in post-COVID elderly patients could serve as a valuable biomarker for immune health and guide personalized interventions to mitigate long-term immune dysfunction. Our findings highlight the urgent need for focused strategies to restore immune homeostasis in aging populations affected by the pandemic’s legacy.
