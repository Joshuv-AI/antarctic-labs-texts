Uncertainty-aware genomic classification of Alzheimer’s disease: a transformer-based ensemble approach with Monte Carlo dropout
Authors: Taeho Jo, Eun Hye Lee, for the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) and the Alzheimer’s Disease Sequencing Project (ADSP)
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) has complex genetic factors that make accurate prediction from genomic data challenging. Current methods lack confidence estimates for their predictions. Our objective is to develop and evaluate an uncertainty-aware deep learning framework for AD prediction that can identify which predictions are reliable. We developed Transformer-based, Uncertainty-aware, Ensemble Network (TrUE-Net), a deep learning framework that combines transformer and random forest models to predict AD from whole-genome sequencing data. The key innovation is using Monte Carlo Dropout to estimate prediction confidence, allowing the model to identify cases where it is uncertain. We analyzed 1050 individuals (607 AD, 443 controls) from Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative cohort. On 525 test samples, accuracy of TrUE-Net without uncertainty threshold was 65.1% with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.664. Using uncertainty thresholds, the model classified 75.4% of samples (n = 396) as ‘uncertain’ and 24.6% (n = 129) as ‘certain’. The uncertain group showed accuracy of 62.6% and F1 of 0.584, while the certain group showed accuracy of 72.9% and F1 of 0.821. Uncertainty quantification through Monte Carlo Dropout provides a framework for assessing prediction reliability in genomic AD classification, potentially allowing more informed interpretation of model outputs.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a complex, progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia in the elderly. It represents a significant public health concern as populations age, with tens of millions affected worldwide. From a genetic standpoint, AD has a highly polygenic architecture: while rare familial early onset AD may result from single-gene mutations (e.g. in APP, PSEN1, PSEN2) the more common late-onset AD involves dozens of risk-associated genetic variants, each conferring a modest effect [1, 2]. Twin studies estimate AD heritability at ~70% [3, 4], yet genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have collectively identified over 80 loci (including more than 100 independent variants) that influence AD risk [5–8]. This genetic complexity, along with gene–gene and gene–environment interactions, makes genomic variant classification for AD prediction challenging. Individual variants typically have limited predictive power, and there is substantial ‘missing heritability,’ indicating that many genetic factors and their effects remain uncertain.

Traditional statistical models and polygenic risk scores have had limited success in AD prediction due to the high-dimensional, often noisy nature of genomic data [9]. Recently, researchers have turned to deep learning to capture nonlinear interactions among variants and improve predictive accuracy. For example, fully connected neural networks have been applied to AD genomics data, reporting moderate accuracy (~68%–70%) in distinguishing AD dementia from controls [10, 11]. Transformers, a deep learning architecture originally developed for natural language processing, have shown notable ability to model long-range dependencies in sequential data and have been adapted to genomic sequences [12].

While deep learning can yield improved accuracy, the reliability of predictions remains a major concern in medical applications. Black-box models often provide outputs without clearly indicating confidence levels, and can exhibit overconfidence even when they are incorrect [13]. This issue is particularly critical in clinical genomics, where misclassification of a patient’s risk may lead to inappropriate interventions without further validation and reduce user trust [14, 15]. However, current deep learning methods for AD genomic classification typically do not provide uncertainty estimates, making it difficult to assess prediction reliability.

To address these challenges, we propose TrUE-Net (Transformer-based, Uncertainty-aware, Ensemble Network), an uncertainty-aware deep learning framework for AD genomic variant classification that leverages Monte Carlo Dropout. Monte Carlo Dropout is a Bayesian approximation technique that keeps dropout active at inference time to generate multiple stochastic forward passes, enabling an estimation of predictive variance. A notable aspect of the proposed method is its integration of a transformer-based genotype classifier with a concurrently trained random forest model. Their respective probability outputs are aggregated through a learned weighting factor, thereby producing a single combined probability estimate. By merging the transformer’s dropout-induced variance with the random forest’s estimator variance, the framework yields a comprehensive measure of predictive uncertainty for each sample. This unified uncertainty metric facilitates the identification of low-confidence predictions that warrant additional scrutiny, thereby enhancing overall reliability.

In this study, we aimed to: (1) combine transformer and random forest models for complementary pattern recognition, (2) implement Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty estimation, and (3) demonstrate that filtering high-uncertainty predictions improves classification performance on retained samples. Empirical evaluations indicate that excluding high-variance predictions can significantly improve classification performance on the remaining, more confidently assessed subset, providing a practical strategy for mitigating diagnostic risk in Alzheimer’s disease genomics.

Methods

Dataset and genomic preprocessing

We evaluated our approach using whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from a total of 1566 participants, drawn from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). Of these, 516 individuals diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) were excluded to focus on definitive diagnostic categories, resulting in a final case–control set of 443 cognitively normal (CN) participants and 607 with clinically confirmed AD dementia (Table 1). Diagnostic criteria for ADNI participants are provided on the ADNI website (http://www.adni-info.org). Sequencing was performed on Illumina platforms with either 100-bp or 150-bp paired-end reads aligned to the GRCh38 (hg38) reference genome using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner-Maximal Exact Match (BWA-MEM), [16] followed by PCR duplicate marking, local realignment, and base quality score recalibration with the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK). Joint variant calling proceeded via GATK HaplotypeCaller, and the Genome Center for Alzheimer’s Disease (GCAD) applied its Variant Calling Pipeline for standardized quality control, encompassing checks of single-nucleotide variant (SNV) concordance, sex mismatches, relatedness, and contamination.

Sample-level quality control removed individuals exhibiting sex inconsistencies, call rates below 95%, or duplicated genetic profiles, and excluded those with high relatedness (Pihat >0.4). Variant-level filtering discarded SNPs with call rates under 95% [17], Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium p-values less than 1 × 10−6 [18], or minor allele frequencies (MAF) below 1% [9]. Variants that appeared monomorphic, multi-allelic, or had poor genotype quality (GQ < 20), low read depth (DP < 10), or missingness exceeding 20% were also set aside, following standard quality control practices in genomic studies. In parallel, population structure was implicitly managed because both ADNI and ADSP-FUS1-ADNI-WGS-2 primarily included participants of European ancestry, and outliers had already been removed during each study’s enrollment. We then applied a k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) imputation method [12] to address residual missing genotypes following these QC steps.

After these procedures, 14 094 common variants in the APOE 50 kb region were retained for the final analyses. Each variant was encoded as an integer genotype (0, 1, or 2). We split the dataset into equally sized training and test sets, ensuring stratification by diagnostic label. We partitioned the dataset into training and test subsets, using a 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set to monitor variability and validate the chosen hyperparameters. Final assessments of generalization were subsequently carried out on the hold-out test set.

An ensemble approach with window-based and flattened genomic representations

We constructed a transformer-based classification model within the proposed TrUE-Net framework to distinguish AD dementia cases from CN controls by translating each individual’s genomic data into a structured sequence. In this approach, we segmented each individual’s array of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) into non-overlapping tokens of length 100 to balance resolution and computational feasibility. These token-level representations were subsequently projected into a feature dimension of 128 through a linear embedding layer, followed by ReLU activation and dropout at a rate of 0.2.

Subsequently, a stack of two transformer encoder layers processed the embedded windows in order to capture relevant dependencies among the segments. Each encoder layer implemented four attention heads and employed a feed-forward sublayer of dimension 512, allowing the model to attend to both local interactions and more distant correlations across the genotypic windows. After the transformer operations, the representations of all windows for a given individual were averaged to form a single feature vector. This pooled vector was then fed into a linear layer with two output units, whose logits were trained to discriminate AD dementia from CN controls. We minimized the cross-entropy loss function to fit this model, and we applied a softmax function during inference to interpret the logits as class probabilities. Throughout this pipeline, Monte Carlo Dropout remained active, enabling multiple stochastic forward passes for each sample at test time and facilitating the estimation of predictive variance.

In parallel, we trained a random forest classifier to provide an alternative perspective on the same genomic data. Instead of preserving any sequential arrangement of the SNP windows, this second model operated on a concatenated vector comprising the entire genotype profile, effectively flattening all windows into a single feature array. This arrangement treated each variant position as an independent predictor, disregarding explicit local ordering but potentially capturing broad, global patterns through the aggregate of decision trees. To coordinate these two complementary classification strategies, we ensured that both models were trained and evaluated on the same set of participants, which allowed subsequent ensemble weighting.

Within the training set, we carried out a five-fold cross-validation procedure to identify optimal hyperparameters, including the weighting factor that merged outputs from the transformer and random forest models, along with a variance threshold to distinguish uncertain predictions. Specifically, we averaged the best weighting factors and threshold levels discovered across the five folds to arrive at the final parameter settings. To address the moderate class imbalance (AD/CN ratio = 1.37), we evaluated the need for class balancing techniques during cross-validation. Given the relatively modest imbalance and confirmed balanced performance across both classes in validation experiments, we proceeded without explicit class weighting. Post-hoc analysis of the confusion matrix confirmed no systematic prediction bias toward either class. We subsequently retrained both the transformer-based model and the random forest on the entire training set, using an AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 and a batch size of 32 over five epochs. Throughout the training, the dropout-driven variance was monitored to encourage stable latent representations while preserving the stochasticity needed for uncertainty estimation.

Lastly, we applied the combined models to the test set to gauge the final predictive performance. For each individual in the test set, we computed a weighted average of the transformer’s probabilistic output and the random forest’s prediction, using the previously determined combination weight. Similarly, we merged the corresponding variance estimates to assign each sample either to an uncertain or a more confidently assessed category, depending on whether its variance exceeded or fell below the threshold observed in cross-validation. This procedure enabled us to calculate separate metrics for the uncertain and more certain groups and provided insight into the reliability of the resulting ensemble model. By segregating test samples in this manner, we aimed to highlight the potential benefits of uncertainty quantification in identifying cases that may require additional clinical follow-up or expert review.

The implementation used Python 3.12.9, PyTorch 2.0.1, Scikit-learn 1.3.0, NumPy 1.24.3, and Pandas 2.0.3. Training was performed on a high-memory server with dual Intel Xeon Gold 6126 processors (48 logical cores total) and 1.5 TB RAM running Linux. The source code is available at https://github.com/taehojo/TrUE-Net, with an interactive demonstration at http://www.jolab.ai/truenet.

Results

A total of 525 test samples were used after training the TrUE-Net model on the remaining data. The final predictive run across these 525 samples, referred to here as the All group, achieved an overall accuracy of 0.651, an area under the ROC curve of 0.664, and an F1-score of 0.668. Discussion

In this study, we developed an uncertainty-aware genomic classification framework for AD by combining transformer and random forest models with Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty estimation. This model provided quantified uncertainty for each classification result, thus allowing a focus on reliably classified samples. Using this classification approach, TrUE-Net with an uncertainty threshold achieved higher performance, outperforming all 11 baseline models tested, while TrUE-Net without the uncertainty threshold showed performance comparable to state-of-the-art ensemble methods. This new framework can practically aid in distinguishing reliable cases from ambiguous ones, thereby helping to identify which cases may require additional diagnostic steps.

Our major finding is that incorporating predictive variance presented differences in performance between subsets of samples with high variance and those with low variance. TrUE-Net approach set variance-based thresholds to single out test samples associated with higher predictive variance, termed uncertain, from those deemed more stable, termed certain. The final prediction results of All group presented moderate performance, and it was comparable to state-of-the-art ensemble methods such as Gradient Boosting and Random Forest. This indicated that an all-or-nothing scenario does not fully exploit the advantages of variance-based filtering. However, the certain subset achieved markedly higher performance, surpassing all other baseline models. In contrast, the uncertain group’s lower accuracy illustrated the complexity introduced by higher predictive variance. Probability estimates for these samples often hover near intermediate values, making the classification boundary less clear. This supports earlier work suggesting that a relatively small percentage of ambiguous genotypes can contribute disproportionately to overall errors, and that removing them from the main classification pipeline can yield marked gains in core metrics [19–21] , though with the trade-off of excluding 75.4% of samples from confident classification.

Beyond the technical performance metrics, these stratification results have practical implications for clinical implementation. The method allows for more nuanced output than a simple binary label by providing an estimate of confidence through variance. With extensive clinical validation, this capacity could be valuable in clinical contexts where errors carry weighty consequences, since higher-variance cases can receive extensive diagnostic evaluations including neuroimaging or blood biomarker testing. Conversely, lower-variance classifications may be handled with more confidence, potentially increasing the efficiency of clinical workflows. In addition, these uncertain samples are distributed across both CN and AD. This indicates that the model’s predictive variance is not restricted to a single group. Therefore, TrUE-Net could potentially help clinicians or researchers allocate additional diagnostic evaluations, potentially reducing diagnostic errors, regardless of specific cognitive stages.

Nevertheless, several limitations constrain the generalizability and clinical applicability of our findings. First, the dataset was primarily composed of 1050 individuals of European descent, which constrains generalization to other ancestral groups. APOE ε4 penetrance varies significantly across populations, potentially affecting both the baseline risk architecture and the applicability of uncertainty-based filtering. Second, we excluded mild cognitive impairment cases (n = 516) due to their heterogeneous clinical trajectories. Meta-analyses indicate that MCI outcomes vary considerably, with 5%–10% converting to dementia annually [22], 8%–25% reverting to normal cognition depending on study setting [23], and ~40%–50% remaining stable over 2–3 years. This heterogeneity would introduce substantial label noise into our uncertainty quantification framework. While this design choice enabled clearer validation of our approach, it limited the analysis to a binary comparison that does not capture subtle genetic features linked to early or transitional disease stages. Third, the model architecture did not address rare variants that may significantly affect disease risk. Current sequencing depth and variant calling approaches may miss rare variants with large effect sizes, and our focus on common variants (MAF > 1%) excludes potentially important low-frequency alleles. Fourth, although uncertainty-based filtering improved performance on the certain subset, the current approach provides confident predictions for only 24.6% of samples. This coverage limitation means that the majority of samples would require additional clinical evaluation or alternative assessment methods. Finally, our analysis lacks external validation on independent datasets from different institutions, sequencing platforms, or geographic regions. The generalizability of uncertainty quantification remains unvalidated outside the ADNI cohort.

Further efforts may tackle these challenges by recruiting more diverse populations and including mild or preclinical states, enabling a broader evaluation of how variance-based methods perform across different stages of Alzheimer’s disease. It would also be instructive to incorporate additional data types, such as transcriptomics, proteomics, or metabolomics, in order to capture a richer biological background. Methods that dynamically tune the variance threshold could reduce the proportion of excluded samples while sustaining performance gains. Taken together, these avenues can refine transformer-based models for genomic risk prediction and strengthen confidence in their utility in clinical scenarios. It is important to emphasize that TrUE-Net represents a research tool that requires validation in independent cohorts from multiple institutions and diverse ancestries before any practical implementation. All described clinical applications are speculative and require extensive validation studies.

Conclusion

TrUE-Net suggests the potential value of uncertainty-aware prediction in genomic medicine—acknowledging when predictions can be trusted rather than claiming superior absolute performance. While the certain subset shows improved performance (F1 = 0.821), this represents only 25% of test samples, highlighting the fundamental trade-off between coverage and accuracy in uncertainty-aware classification. Three important developments are needed for potential clinical translation: First, validation across diverse populations to ensure uncertainty quantification generalizes beyond European ancestry cohorts. Second, integration with clinical data streams (neuroimaging, biomarkers, cognitive assessments) to reduce the proportion of uncertain classifications while maintaining confidence. Third, methods to improve coverage while maintaining confidence, potentially through ensemble approaches or adaptive thresholds. Future genomic medicine tools should prioritize transparent uncertainty quantification alongside performance optimization. This approach acknowledges the inherent complexity of polygenic disorders while providing guidance for clinical decision-making, though substantial validation across diverse populations and clinical settings remains essential before any practical implementation.
