Modified Refixation Technique of Distal Deep and Superficial Medial Collateral Ligament Injuries
Authors: Grégoire Thürig, Elmar Herbst, Adrian Deichsel, Peter Behrendt, Michael J Raschke, Christoph Kittl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnteromedial knee instability is a complex clinical problem, especially when combined with valgus instability. This type of instability often occurs with injuries to the superficial (sMCL) and deep (dMCL) medial collateral ligaments. In 55%‐70% of cases, there is a combined dMCL lesion with distal sMCL lesion. These ligaments are the primary stabilizers against valgus stress and anteromedial rotational forces. Inadequately treating both the sMCL and the dMCL can lead to persistent instability and an increased risk of anterior cruciate ligament graft failure. Biomechanical studies confirm that restoring the anatomy of the sMCL and dMCL is necessary to control anteromedial rotational instability. Standard posteromedial techniques may not sufficiently treat external rotational laxity. Anteromedial reconstruction techniques, in particular, more accurately reproduce the natural kinematics of the knee. The refixation technique presented here uses 3 anchor points at the sMCL, dMCL, and anteromedial complex. Especially retensioning of the sMCL through a “fan out” effect is achieved. This method directly treats anteromedial and valgus instability. It can be performed as an isolated procedure or in combination with anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction. Current literature shows that anteromedial reconstructions, whether combined or isolated, reduce instability and protect anterior cruciate ligament grafts. Further long‐term clinical studies are needed to optimize the technique and indications.

Medial collateral ligament (MCL) injuries are common and often occur alongside anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) ruptures, 1 , 2 typically due to a valgus‐external rotation injury mechanism. 3 Isolated Grade I and II MCL injuries generally respond well to conservative treatment, 4 , 5 but combined or Grade III injuries, particularly those involving ligament dislocation over the pes anserinus, may require surgical intervention to restore stability and prevent chronic instability. 4 , 5

Recent biomechanical research has emphasized the critical role of the superficial MCL (sMCL) and deep MCL (dMCL) as primary stabilizers against external tibial rotation and anteromedial translation. 6 , 7 , 8 These findings have led to changes in surgical strategies for reconstruction, 9 , 10 , 11 especially in cases involving combined ACL and MCL injuries. In these cases, restoring anteromedial stability is essential to protect the ACL graft from excessive loading and potential failure. 12 , 13

Anatomic repair in the acute phase usually avoids the need for grafts, whereas chronic reconstruction can be more complex and requires grafting. 14 , 15 Several acute repair techniques have been described in the literature, but they predominantly focus on direct sMCL fixation, with or without augmentation.

The technique described here not only incorporates the direct refixation of the dMCL at its anatomic insertion but restores the deep medial stabilizing structure and enables the sMCL fibers to fan out anatomically. This preserves their physiological alignment and function. 7 , 16 , 17 This dual‐layered anatomic repair aims to restore medial knee stability more anatomically than traditional methods that address the sMCL in isolation.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Indications

This surgical technique is used to treat acute injuries involving significant disruption of the distal tibial insertions of both the dMCL and the sMCL. Indications are established through clinical evaluation, including a positive valgus stress test at 20° of knee flexion, as well as positivity on either the anteromedial drawer test or the anterior external rotation (dial) test. However, valgus instability in full extension is considered a relative contraindication due to likely posterior oblique ligament involvement, which necessitates alternative surgical management.

Positioning

The patient is placed in a supine position with the affected knee slightly flexed (Video 1). This allows sufficient flexibility to perform a diagnostic arthroscopy, if necessary. The leg should also be easily moved to the side over the edge of the operating table to assess medial laxity and confirm reduction of the medial compartment. A thigh tourniquet is applied, and a full range of motion from 0° to 120° of knee flexion must be achievable. To optimize access to the medial side, position the operative leg in a slight figure‐of‐4 over the contralateral limb.

Reconstruction Principle

The reconstruction involves a double, triple‐crossing suture technique to repair the sMCL. The sMCL is secured using a screw and washer at its anatomical tibial footprint, which is located approximately 6 cm distal to the joint line. 18 Additional fixation is achieved with suture anchors to reestablish the anatomical tibial insertion of the dMCL, the anteromedial complex, and the proximal tibial insertion of the sMCL (Figure 1).

Refixation

A longitudinal skin incision ranging from 6 to 8 cm is made along the medial aspect of the proximal tibia. The sartorius fascia and hamstring tendons are identified, and a longitudinal incision is made in the sartorius fascia proximal to the hamstrings. Then, the torn tibial insertions of the sMCL and dMCL are identified and debrided of scar tissue, along with the underlying bony surface of the proximal tibia.

The sMCL is whipstitched using 2 nonabsorbable sutures (No. 2 FiberWire; Arthrex, Naples, FL) with a triple‐crossing technique applied separately to its anterior and posterior fibers (Video). To reestablish the anatomic proximal tibial insertion of the sMCL, a 1.8‐mm suture anchor (Q‐Fix Mini, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is placed 10‐15 mm below the joint line. The sMCL is secured to the tibia inside‐out at this level.

Depending on the extent of the lesion, a 3.5‐mm cortical screw with a washer (Synthes GmbH, Zuchwil, Switzerland) is placed approximately 5‐6 cm distal to the joint line, in the center of the distal tibial sMCL footprint. The screw is initially left untightened to allow for controlled tensioning of the sutures.

To restore the anatomic tibial insertion of the dMCL, a second 1.8‐mm Q‐Fix mini suture anchor (Smith & Nephew) is placed 10 mm below the joint line at the anteromedial tubercle innominatum (about 15 mm anterior to the anterior fibers of the sMCL). The dMCL is sutured using a modified Mason‐Allen technique 19 that incorporates the anterior border of the sMCL's anterior fibers. Tensioning of the dMCL suture is performed at 0° of knee extension in neutral rotation.

Next, the anterior sMCL suture is secured under the washer at 30° of flexion with the knee in neutral rotation and varus stress. The posterior suture is then tensioned similarly at 20° of flexion. Once both sutures are secured, the screw is finally tightened. dMCL suture is retensioned if needed in full extension. Finally, the anteromedial retinaculum and sartorius fascia are separately sutured using absorbable running sutures. Technical pearls and pitfalls are presented in Table 2.

Postoperative Care

Postoperative care includes using a hinged knee brace locked in full extension for the first week. Thereafter, knee flexion up to 60° is gradually permitted for 4 weeks. After this period, a full range of motion is allowed while still wearing the brace. Mobilization begins with partial weight bearing of 15 to 20 kg for the first 6 weeks, followed by progression to full weight bearing as tolerated. An early appointment at 2‐3 weeks postoperatively can help guide the patient properly.

A structured physical therapy program is essential to restoring full range of motion and achieving adequate muscular rehabilitation. Because of the initial period of immobilization and limited motion, targeted neuromuscular training, especially for the quadriceps, is important to prevent muscle inhibition and promote functional recovery.

DISCUSSION

Recent advancements in anatomical and biomechanical understanding of the medial knee structures, particularly the dMCL and the anterior fibers of the sMCL, have highlighted their crucial role in resisting external tibial rotation and stabilizing against anteromedial rotatory instability. 7 , 8 , 10 , 16 These insights are the basis for restoring native anatomy in the acute setting rather than relying solely on reconstructive techniques.

The technique presented here emphasizes anatomical repair of both the sMCL and the dMCL at their native tibial insertion sites, which is particularly indicated for acute tibial‐sided avulsions. Nonoperative treatment is often associated with poor outcomes in these cases, especially when meniscal coronary ligament involvement is present. Repairing the ligaments in the acute phase preserves native tissue, which contributes to proprioception and potentially improves functional outcomes. Patients undergoing ACL reconstruction with MCL refixation or reconstruction were compared. Those managed with MCL repair had significantly lower odds of ACL revision surgery than those who underwent MCL reconstruction (odds ratio 0.50, 95% confidence interval 0.27‐0.91, P = .024). 20 The sMCL is secured with a triple‐crossing suture technique and fixed anatomically to the tibia using a combination of a suture anchor and a screw with a washer for fixation. The dMCL is refixed at its true anatomical insertion on the anteromedial tibial tubercle using a modified Mason‐Allen stitch technique. 19 Reestablishing the deep insertion facilitates fan‐like spreading of the sMCL fibers, restoring native ligament anatomy and addressing rotational instability.

Another benefit of this repair technique is the ability to perform concurrent ACL reconstruction in cases of multiligamentous injury, thereby shortening recovery time and eliminating the delay associated with conservative MCL management. 21 Early mobilization is encouraged to reduce stiffness and prevent quadricep atrophy. Care should be taken to avoid overtensioning the sutures, as this can result in overconstraint. This can be avoided by sequentially tensioning at 30° and 20° of flexion under gentle varus stress.

There remains a lack of clinical studies to validate the efficacy of this approach. Further clinical studies are needed to evaluate long‐term outcomes compared with nonoperative treatment and delayed reconstruction techniques.
