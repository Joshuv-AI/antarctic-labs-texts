Bayesian clustering of spatially distributed compositional data with application to the Great Barrier Reef
Authors: Luiza Piancastelli, Nial Friel, Julie Vercelloni, Kerrie Mengersen, Antonietta Mira
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The relative abundance of groups of species is often used in ecological surveys to estimate community composition, a metric that reflects patterns of commonness and rarity of biological assemblages. The focus of this paper is measurements of the abundances of four benthic groups (that live on the seafloor) at several reefs on Australia’s Great Barrier Reef (GBR) gathered between 2012 and 2017. In this paper we develop a statistical model to find clusters of locations with similar composition. We examine the changes in clusters during a period impacted by an unprecedented sequence of extreme environmental disturbances. To achieve this, we propose a model that incorporates the geographical location of the data, accounting for the possibility that nearby reefs are similar in composition. This is accomplished with a Dirichlet mixture model and a Potts distribution on the cluster assignments. Non-availability of the normalised Potts distribution makes Bayesian inference a doubly-intractable task. To circumvent this additional inferential challenge, an approximate exchange algorithm is specified. The analysis of the 2012 data, collected before the weather disturbances, reveals four clusters. The four groups highlight the primary habitat patterns in the 2012 GBR, each with distinct ecological characteristics: (1) areas with above-average soft coral abundance, (2) sand-dominated regions commonly found in the central part, (3) southern reefs with a more balanced distribution of species, and (4) habitats dominated by algae and hard corals. Compared to subsequent surveys conducted after disturbances, there is evidence of a decline in the number of clusters and a simplification of reef composition at the regional scale.

Introduction

The relative abundance of individuals is a key component of biodiversity. Patterns of commonness and rarity are fundamental characteristics of biological assemblages used by ecologists to compare different communities and predict future developments1. In this work, we deal with composition of algae, hard corals, soft corals and sand in local coral communities of Australia’s Great Barrier Reef (GBR) surveyed in four years (2012, 2014, 2016 and 2017)2. In community ecology, several ecological questions rely on the multivariate analysis of compositional data, including inference about environmental effects and species interactions3.

The focus of this paper is on the development of statistical methodology for clustering compositional data from such ecological surveys. This goal is pursued with the purpose of identifying reefs with similar community patterns and assessing how these changed as disturbances occurred. During 2014–2017, the GBR was impacted by a series of tropical cyclones and heat stress periods that led to back-to-back mass coral bleaching4,5. This unprecedented series of disturbances resulted in the lowest regional hard coral cover on record, shift in coral community composition and declines in coral recruitment6,7. While their impacts on hard corals have been well-documented, responses of other benthic communities and compositional reef structures to these declines are not well-understood. Once it is reasonable to assume that ecological systems similar in composition were subject to common environmental conditions, grouping the reefs could play an important role in understanding the impacts of disturbances on reef community patterns across large spatial scales. We believe that the pursuit of a robust clustering methodology is key to an improved understanding of reef changes following climate change impacts. Firstly, by revealing the main patterns of reef diversity supports ecologists in benchmarking how reef compositions are organised throughout the GBR while accounting for the spatial variability of various communities and disturbance regimes. Secondly, the reporting of clusters’ predominance across regions is an intuitive metric for decision-making enabling one to summarize complex multidimensional changes in an easy way. The access to such information will greatly benefit reef management and conservation.

Although the analysis of proportions or relative abundance is of interest in many scientific fields, statistical methodologies for this type of data most often rely on transformations. Early advancements in compositional data analysis can be traced back to8 and9 which introduced the idea of removing the unit simplex constraint. In this context, different probability distributions (or mixture components), can be interpreted as corresponding to a cluster and the fitted model explains the latent structure that generated the data. Although DMs are widespread in statistics, they are commonly employed to fulfill different tasks. For example, as prior distributions date back to19, but their application to the clustering task is less explored.

Within each survey, our goal is to investigate: i how many clusters are there and ii what is the probability that any reef belongs to a given cluster? The identification of clusters can play a role in understanding the main drivers of reef community patterns at large spatial scales along the GBR. We adopt a Bayesian analysis to answer these questions, which allows to efficiently extract information and properly quantify the uncertainty behind the inference arising from small data sets. This is crucial in our context where collecting the data is time consuming and expensive. In addition, we would like to compare the results across the ecological surveys. Although this is limited because complete follow up is not available for all observations, it could provide some insights into the effect of environmental disturbances on reef community patterns. This is possible because the 2012 survey took place in an environment that had not been recently affected by disturbances. In contrast, in the subsequent ecological surveys there had been at least one stress condition. The GBR was hit by heavy winds and precipitations due to category 4 and 5 tropical cyclones in 2014 and 20155,25. And in 2016 and 2017, the GBR was subject to back-to-back coral bleaching events7. Coral bleaching is a phenomenon caused by marine heat waves, which causes corals to stress and weaken, and become subject to mortality6. Hence, clustering the 2012 reef compositions can be seen to reflect undisturbed environmental conditions and serve as a baseline for comparisons to the composition patterns in 2014, 2016, and 2017.

The paper is organized as follows. In Methods, a neighborhood structure for the data is created using a Voronoi tesselation and the spatial clustering model is introduced. Algorithms to collect samples from the posterior distribution of model parameters and latent variables are then carefully developed. This is followed by the simplified model where allocations are independent and some model aspects such as the selection of k. Section Data analysis presents the results obtained by applying the proposed methodology to our motivating data sets. In this section, we explore the 2012 ecological survey in detail and compare its results to the surveys affected by disturbances. We conclude in Discussion with the insights obtained from clustering GBR reef compositions and some future research possibilities.

Methods

Ecological surveys were conducted at 23 coral reefs along the GBR between 2012–20172 (Fig. 1a). Within each reef, between 1–3 transects were surveyed using a sampling methodology developed as part of the XL Seaview Catlin Survey26 as illustrated in Fig. 1b. A total of 58 transects were surveyed in 2012, 33 transects in 2014, 49 in 2016, and 35 in 2017. Transects to resurvey were selected to capture coral reef community responses across a combination of different exposures to cyclones and coral bleaching events that impacted these reef locations between 2012 and 20175. Relative abundances of coral reef communities were estimated from images of the seafloor along 2 km geo-located transects of the outer reef slopes, at 10 m depth27,28. A convolutional neural network was used to automatically classify reef communities26 from a total of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 145,000$$\end{document}∼145,000 images that were taken between 2012 and 20172,5. Reef communities were then classified into four groups, namely, hard coral, soft coral, algae, and sand and were expressed as relative coverage for each image. Neighborhood

A data-driven way to determine \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {N}(j)$$\end{document}N(j) for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j=1,\dots ,n$$\end{document}j=1,⋯,n from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\underline{c}}$$\end{document}c_ is with a Voronoi tesselation. A Voronoi tesselation, or diagram, is a partition of an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}^2$$\end{document}R2 plane into regions determined by seeds. Each point in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}^2$$\end{document}R2 is allocated to the region whose seed it is the closest to, according to a given distance. A tesselation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}^2$$\end{document}R2 is determined by the inputs: (i) seeds, or points in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}^2$$\end{document}R2, that will outline polygons and (ii) a distance function. In our context, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}^2$$\end{document}R2 is equivalent to the minimal Great Barrier Reef area encompassing the observed data in a particular year and (i), the seeds, are the longitude and latitude coordinates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\underline{\varvec{c}}$$\end{document}c_. In choosing (ii), the distance, it is natural to consider the Geodesic metric that describes the shortest path between two points in an ellipsoid. However, under our small study area (in relation to the Earth’s surface), tesselations drawn with the Geodesic or Euclidian distances are indistinguishable.

From the partitioned space, different concepts of neighborhood are possible but the simplest rule is to stipulate that two regions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_1, R_2$$\end{document}R1,R2 are neighbors if they share an edge. In other words, if the Voronoi regions generated by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{c}_1$$\end{document}c1 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{c}_2$$\end{document}c2 share a border, then transects 1 and 2 are neighbors. This means that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \in \mathcal {N}(2)$$\end{document}1∈N(2) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2 \in \mathcal {N}(1)$$\end{document}2∈N(1), ensuring that neighbourhoods are symmetric in the sense described above. Although this rule is natural and appealing, a careful assessment reveals that the near to linear placement of transects in the space causes some unnatural neighbors. This is illustrated in Fig. 2, where the original tesselation is shown in the left-side panel. An example of geographically distant reefs deemed neighbors from elongation of their respective polygons is marked with a blue edge. We correct for this behaviour with the addition of artificial seeds that do not change the observed data but rather ensure a sensible \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {N}$$\end{document}N. To this end, a polynomial regression fit to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\underline{c}}$$\end{document}c_ is shifted longitudinally to the left and to the right, creating sideways borders. An illustration of such margins is provided in Section S1 of the Supplementary Material. In addition, we choose to view the data as sampled within three blocks, which we call upper, center and lower GBR, and these regions are also created with artificial seeds. This is appropriate to avoid connectivity between the upper-center and center-bottom edges, which are further apart in space. Region-wise separations are simply a linear horizontal demarcations, also displayed in the Supplementary Material. Defining neighbors to be polygons that share an edge and do not arise from artificial seeds yield the GBR neighborhood concept that will be used throughout. The latter is illustrated on the center panel of Fig. 2 where dot colors indicate the transect GBR region (upper in yellow, center in pink and lower in red) or that the point is an artificial seed, in grey. Histograms on the right-side panel show the distribution of the number of neighbours per transect, according to its region. The bottom GBR is the one with the largest number of neighboring pairs since more transects (31 in total) are in this area. Although with less data points (9 transects), the center region involves a considerable number of interactions. This is because in comparison to the upper and lower parts, locations in the center are more spread in terms of longitude, creating extra neighboring pairs in the horizontal direction. Regarding geographical distances, neighbouring locations are, on average, located 13.6 km apart. Region-specific averages are: Upper: 16.6 km, Center: 14.2 km, and Lower: 12.7 km.

It is important to recognise that our approach to partitioning the surveyed area into north, center, and south requires some level of subjectivity, however it is justified and informed by the ecological characteristics of the Great Barrier Reef associated with its size and also the sampling design of the monitoring program. Firstly, environmental factors such as ocean currents, temperature and marine biodiversity vary with reef latitude. Secondly, regions experienced different weather disturbances events. For example, the 2016 coral bleaching was mainly concentrated in the northern GBR section whereas outbreaks of coral-eating seastars more often in the southern sections29. As a result, it is common that regions of the GBR are treated independently in the published literature29–31. In our approach, region partitioning avoids reefs that are further apart to be deemed neighbors, as upper-center and center-bottom edges are more distant. By adopting this separation, we prohibt reefs that possibly experienced different conditions to be neighbors of one another. Naturally, other applications of the model presented here may not benefit from any such region separation or the addition of artificial seeds. Model aspects

The algorithms just described provide means to sample the posterior distribution of our two approaches for clustering compositions. We will refer to these as the spatial and independent models. Posterior inference is then the result of collecting draws from the desired model, after running its algorithm for a sufficient number of iterations so that stationarity is achieved. Simulated values are treated as random realisations from the model’s posterior distribution and can be used to obtain summaries of interest.

Prior to that, the chains must be processed to account for label-switching. Label-switching is a well-known phenomena in the Bayesian estimation of finite mixture or hidden Markov models32–34 that is due to invariance of the mixture distribution with respect to permutations of labels. Before \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{\rho }_1, \ldots , \varvec{\rho }_k$$\end{document}ρ1,…,ρk draws from different iterations are combined, we need to ensure labels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{1,\ldots ,k\}$$\end{document}{1,…,k} correspond across MCMC iterations. For that we employ the methodology developed by35, an algorithm applicable to Gibbs sampling strategies that uses the classification probabilities Expression (7) for label permutation. More specifically, a full-sweep at iteration t renders \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{m}_1^{(t)},\ldots , \varvec{m}_n^{(t)}$$\end{document}m1(t),…,mn(t) denoted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M^{(t)}$$\end{document}M(t), which is a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(n \times k)$$\end{document}(n×k) classification probabilities matrix. Data analysis

A full Bayesian analysis of the reef data sets is now carried out with the proposed methodology. The spatial and independent models developed in the previous sections will be investigated, starting with the analysis of the 2012 data. As mentioned previously, the 2012 survey took place in an environment not recently affected by weather disturbances. The analysis of 2014, 2016, and 2017 data that were hit at least by one tropical cyclone or coral bleaching event follows with the main focus of understanding the extent to which the clusters have changed.

We now pursue an investigation of the indicated changes in the number of clusters. This comparison shall be made under the remark that sampled reef locations were not the same across surveys. While the 2012 survey sees \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=58$$\end{document}n=58 transects, the sample sizes are respectively \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=33, 49, 35$$\end{document}n=33,49,35 for 2014, 2016, and 2017. Nevertheless, exploring clusters alongside weather disturbances can provide insights onto their impact in the composition of reefs.

As mentioned previously, the GBR was impacted by a series of disturbances between 2014-2016. In total, two tropical cyclones and two bleaching events were recorded in this period. In contrast, the 2012 survey recorded compositions under non-disturbed circumstances. The two types of disturbances affected the GBR in different ways. Cyclones cause strong winds and powerful waves which in turn break reef-building hard corals. Usually, reefs recover naturally, but this process takes between 5-10 years on the GBR. Coral bleaching originates from an increased temperature of ocean waters. During a period of heat stress, hard corals expel their symbiotic algae and become weaker and subject to mortality after a few weeks. Table 4 summarises the frequency of these events at the reef locations surveyed in 2014, 2016 and 2017. The relative frequency of reef locations impacted by cyclones is given in each cell and these varied from zero cyclones to two cyclones. Cyclones are more localized events and do not necessarily affect equally all samples in a particular year. In contrast, coral bleaching impacted a larger section of the GBR, hence characterising the survey. This is indicated in the top row of Table 4. A possible interpretation of the clustering dynamics that can be made jointly with the disturbances events is the following. In 2014 most samples were affected by a tropical cyclone, leading to a first decline in the variety of reef compositions. In such environments recently hit with heavy winds and rainfall, there is a dominance of Algae/HC characteristics. Subsequently, the 2016 and 2017 surveys take place in a GBR that sees the interaction between the two types of disturbance: coral bleaching takes place in 2016, and almost every reef is by then hit by a cyclone. In the continuation of stress conditions, there seems to be a consolidation of either mainly Algae/HC or HC mix environments.

Discussion

In this article we have analysed compositional data arising from ecological surveys conducted in different locations in the GBR between 2012 and 2017. Two substantive questions of interest were explored. First, identifying the main reef habitat patterns available throughout the GBR was pursued via clustering. To do this, we developed a hidden spatial model which assumes that the compositional pattern at nearby reef locations are spatially dependent through the introduction of a Potts model. We also present an alternative model which assumes that the compositions at each reef location are independent of one another.

Our methodological contribution in this work is pioneering the integration of a mixture model for clustering compositional data with a spatial dependency structure. Some recent related works that also apply the Potts distribution in a spatial clustering setting are45,46. However, this is pursued for univariate data and in a different fashion. Overall, modeling spatial dependency with non-Gaussian and/or multivariate responses is a complex task. While Gaussian processes (GPs) are well-established in spatial statistics, non-Normal responses pose additional challenges to this approach. Our work offers a viable and parsimonious solution to spatial dependency in the clustering setting and opens possibilities for new research in this area. For example, when additional information is available as location-wise covariates, the Distance Nearest Neighbors47 is an alternative way of setting a neighborhood structure. This consists of deeming all locations as neighbors and modeling their strength of connectivity according to the similarity between their covariate vectors.

Algorithms to fit the models under a Bayesian framework and Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling were carefully outlined. This is a non-trivial task for the spatial model due to the fact that the normalisation constant of the Potts distribution is not amenable to exact calculation. This in turn implies that the interaction parameters in the Potts model are not possible to infer using standard MCMC algorithms. We overcame this challenge with an approximate exchange algorithm that relies on draws from the Potts model to achieve the cancellation of intractable terms in the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probabilities. The proposed methodology was completed by handling label switching of mixture components, uncertainty quantification of clusters via entropy, and model choice.

Our second point of interest was whether and how these patterns varied across the reefs in the presence of disturbances. This is particularly important over the course of the study as there was an unprecedented sequence of tropical cyclones and back-to-back bleaching events. It is therefore of immediate interest to assess how disturbances impacted the compositional patterns at reef locations. Special attention was given to ecologically interpreting the clustering results for the 2012 reef data. This survey was conducted before a series of unprecedented weather disturbances occurred, hence reflecting different conditions compared to the subsequent data from 2014 to 2017. The presence of four clusters with spatial dependency was identified in 2012 and their main features were explored using MCMC samples from the model parameters. Groups were interpreted as follows. The Algae/HC/SC and HC/Sand clusters contained locations with above-average soft coral and sand abundance, respectively. Algae/HC grouped algae and hard coral-dominated environments, and HC mix was a cluster where the four species groups were reasonably well represented.

Clustering the 2014, 2016, and 2017 surveys alongside following up on the disturbances exposed a potential homogenization of GBR community composition after coral bleaching and tropical cyclones. Our analysis indicated the number of clusters to have reduced to two with discontinuation of the Algae/HC/SC and HC/Sand characteristics identified in 2012. In addition to this decline in patterns, there is evidence of consolidation of HC mix and Algae/HC and less support to local spatial interaction. Exploring clustering changes in the context of disturbances motivates future research on the development of dynamic clustering models for compositions. This would consist of allowing the initial groups to change over time, offering an outlook on the clustering transition probabilities which can answer questions such as What is the probability that a reef in the Algae/HC/SC group becomes an Algae/HC member after experiencing certain disturbance?

Other possible extensions of our approaches are the following. If location-wise covariate information is available, it can be considered in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{z}$$\end{document}z distribution via the Potts potential or a mixture of experts model48. It might also be of interest to incorporate exogenous information on the proportions. This is a straightforward extension that can be achieved with logarithm links on the mixture parameters. By letting regression effects change across clusters, practitioners can investigate if their impact varies with groups. Finally, another possible direction is to extend the Potts model for pair-specific relations between clusters, which would bring further flexibility in modeling the interactions.
