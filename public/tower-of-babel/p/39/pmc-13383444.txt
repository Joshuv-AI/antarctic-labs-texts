Understanding the dynamics: nexus between servant leadership, symbiotic diversity, identity related process and personnel behaviors
Authors: Sheikh Raheel Manzoor, Ahmad Adnan, Muhammad Zada, Nicolás Contreras-Barraza, Guido Salazar-Sepúlveda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBased on the social exchange theory, this study examines how servant leadership influences the nursing staff’s job-related outcomes by fostering perceived member status and interpersonal coordination. Precisely, the study investigates the mediating mechanism of perceived member status and interpersonal coordination in the relationship between servant leadership and job-related outcomes, i.e., job execution, psychological safety, and institutional identification. The study further explores the moderating role of a symbiotic diversity setting (SDS) between servant leadership, perceived member status, and interpersonal coordination. The SDS refers to the workplace environment in which employees from diverse backgrounds collaborate via mutual support. Furthermore, to synthesize the existing literature and validate the link between variables, the study conducted a meta-analysis.MethodsWe used a multi-source survey design for data collection. Validated measurement scales, i.e., questionnaires, were distributed among the nursing staff and healthcare managers working in the leading hospitals of Pakistan. For estimating the hierarchical nature of data, multilevel modeling was used to evaluate within-group, between-group, and cross-level relationships. In addition, meta-analytic procedures were used to synthesize and validate the relationship between the study variables. For data analysis, we used Python (v3.12) and JASP version 0.16.4.0.ResultsThe findings indicate two key process mechanisms, i.e., perceived member status and interpersonal coordination, that partially mediate the link between servant leadership and job-related outcomes. Moreover, a symbiotic diversity setting is found to be a significant boundary condition that strengthens the relationships linking servant leadership with perceived member status and interpersonal coordination.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that the effectiveness of servant leadership in healthcare is not solely direct but operates via vital relational mechanisms and contextual conditions. The findings indicate that servant leadership improves employee outcomes by fostering social exchange processes, emphasizing the critical role of leadership-driven relational dynamics in shaping workplace functioning.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Servant leadership, Psychological safety, Symbiotic diversity setting, Nursing staff

Introduction

Effective leadership is a vital determinant of nursing staff productivity, engagement, and service quality [1]. In this regard, servant leadership has gained significant scholarly attention because servant leaders strongly emphasize employees’ well-being, growth, and empowerment [2, 3]. In the healthcare sector, where relational function and coordination are essential for service delivery, servant leadership is precisely pertinent because it nurtures collaboration, ethical conduct, and trust, which eventually affects organizational success [4]. Servant leadership is conceptualized via core facets, i.e., wisdom, development, relationship building, emotional healing, pioneering, empowerment, and ethical behavior [5, 6]. Research studies link servant leadership with higher levels of performance [7], satisfaction [8], and engagement [9]. Servant leadership is also positively linked with workplace diversity and promotes a collaborative working environment, which is critical for team effectiveness and stronger problem-solving abilities with a higher level of creativity [10, 11]. Despite the growing body of literature on servant leadership, the current body of literature is fragmented in clarifying the mechanisms and boundary conditions via which servant leadership affects employee outcomes. Earlier studies extensively examined direct relationships, offering limited insight into the cognitive and relational processes that translate leadership behaviors into job-related attitudes and behaviors, i.e., job execution, institutional identification, and psychological safety [12–15]. Eventually, a substantial gap exists in understanding how and under what conditions the servant leadership produces its effects [16–18].

Based on social exchange theory, this study posits that servant leadership influences employee job-related outcomes through two core mediating relational mechanisms: perceived member status—an employee’s perception of their value and standing within the organization—and interpersonal coordination—active alignment of communication, collaboration, and task execution among members [19]. These mechanisms mediate the relationship between servant leadership and outcomes such as job execution, institutional identification, and psychological safety. However, the effectiveness of these mechanisms is subject to contextual conditions. Previous studies have overlooked such boundary conditions in servant leadership frameworks. To address this gap, the current study introduces the concept of symbiotic diversity setting (SDS), defined as a workplace environment characterized by collaboration, inclusion, shared value creation, and mutual interdependence [20, 21]. From a social exchange perspective, SDS enhances cooperation and relational reciprocity, thereby strengthening the mediating effects of servant leadership on perceived member status and interpersonal coordination.

Methodologically, earlier studies are partial with context-specific analysis, inconsistent findings, multilevel modeling, and limited generalizability [22]. Therefore, to mitigate the methodological limitations, this study integrates systematic review and meta-analysis with multilevel empirical analysis. Meta-analysis and systematic review synthesize the existing empirical evidence and offer a holistic integration of empirical findings. This would eventually establish the consistency and generalizability of relationships between factors across different contexts. This understanding is vital for detecting the evidence gaps for adjusting the conceptual framework. However, Meta-analysis is not enough to address the multilevel relationships within the organizational-specific context. Therefore, the multilevel analysis examines the cross-level interactions of servant leadership and employee job-related outcomes via process mechanism (mediation) and boundary condition (moderator) within hierarchical organizational data structures. This integrated framework enhances the effectiveness and quality of research design. In this context, the study addresses the following research question.

How does servant leadership affect personnel job-related outcomes, and what role do perceived member status and interpersonal coordination (as a mediating process mechanism) and symbiotic diversity setting (as a boundary condition) play in this relationship?

To answer the study question, the objectives of the study are first, to conduct a comprehensive meta-analysis and systematic review related to servant leadership and employees’ job-related outcomes. Second, to measure the direct effect of servant leadership on perceived member status, interpersonal coordination, institutional identification, job execution, and psychological safety. Third, to examine the mediating effect of perceived member status and interpersonal coordination between servant leadership and job-related outcomes, i.e., job execution, psychological safety, and institutional identification. Lastly, to examine the moderating role of a symbiotic diversity setting between servant leadership and mediating factors, i.e., perceived member status and interpersonal coordination. This study makes several vital contributions. First, the study enhances the theoretical understanding of the process mechanism via which servant leadership influences employee job-related outcomes. Second, the study conceptualizes the symbiotic diversity setting as a novel boundary condition, extending diversity literature beyond outdated opinions. Third, by employing meta-analysis and systematic review, the study provides robust and generalized evidence on the efficacy of servant leadership. Lastly, the study offers practical implications for healthcare organizations seeking to foster leadership effectiveness, relational coordination, and enhance employee outcomes.

Meta-analysis

To foster the theoretical basis and empirically synthesize the relationships among the factors, a holistic meta-analysis was conducted ensuing the PRISMA 2020 standard [23]. Meta-analysis is a quantitative synthesis method that integrates the effect sizes of independent studies to estimate relationships, evaluate heterogeneity, identify patterns, and provide generalized and reliable conclusions beyond individual studies [24]. A random-effects model was used because this model assumes true effect sizes differ across studies and provides generalizable estimates.

Search strategy

A systematic literature review was conducted to ensure holistic coverage; the publication type (journal article, dissertation, or conference paper) was included from the database sources (i.e., Google Scholar, Web of Science, Scopus, Emerald Insight, and Research Gate). We used Boolean keywords for search strategy such as: “servant leadership and job performance”, or “psychological safety”, or “job execution”, or “institutional identification”, or “interpersonal coordination”, or “perceived member status”. Forward, backward, and manual citation tracking were performed to identify eligible studies. The search was limited to English-language empirical studies published between January 2010 and January 2025. To mitigate publication bias, dissertations, peer-reviewed articles, working papers, and conference papers were included.

Study selection

To assure transparent review and systematic process, we used preferred reporting item for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 standard model for study selection [23]. The PRISMA workflow consisted of study identification, study screening, and study inclusion. Only those studies were included, (a) if they observed the link between at least one construct, (b) specified empirical quantitative outcomes, (c) provided adequate statistical evidences and (d) used independent samples. The excluded studies were: (a) qualitative or review-based, (b) didn’t report effect sizes, (c) contained duplicate datasets, and (d) were inaccessible in full text. After screening, 68 studies with a sample of (n = 20,127) participants were included. Figure 1 demonstrates the PRISMA flow chart.

Coding procedure

We adopted standard coding protocols and processes for coding variables to assure reproducibility and transparency. Two independent coders extracted data, e.g., (a) authors and year of publication, (b) sample size, (c) country/industry context, (d) measurement instruments, and (e) correlation among factors. Inter-coder inconsistency rectified via consensus and discussion. To maintain consistency, each variables assigned coding labels, i.e., (A-G).

Meta-analytic procedure

Pearson correlation coefficients (r) are used to measure the effect sizes. Fisher’s z-transformation (z = tanh⁻¹(r)) technique was used to stabilize the variability and correct the non-normal distribution of correlations before aggregation [24, 25]. Corrected correlations (ρ) calculated by adjusting for measurement error attenuation using obtainable reliability coefficients, providing estimates of true-score links between factors. The heterogeneity was evaluated via Cochran’s Q, I2, between-study variability (τ²), and the standard deviation of corrected correlations (SDρ). The I2 was inferred using traditional standards (25% = low heterogeneity, 50% = moderate heterogeneity, and 75% = high heterogeneity). All analysis conducted under a random-effects model. We used funnel plot symmetry, Egger’s regression test, and inclusion of unpublished studies to examine the publication bias. No significant indication of publication bias was identified; however, minor asymmetry might persist because of heterogeneity in the samples. We used JASP (v0.16.4.0) to compute the pooled effect sizes, heterogeneity indices, publication bias diagnostics, and confidence intervals, to ensure transparency in line with PRISMA 2020 standard guidelines [23].

Literature review & hypothesis

Servant leadership & job related outcomes

Servant leadership has been extensively described as a relational leadership approach that prioritizes ethics and developing followers. Based on social identity theory, this leadership style enhances the psychological attachment towards the organization by fostering trust, respect, and belongingness [26]. Instead of operating as a direct transactional mechanism, the servant leadership functions via socio-psychological processes that influence the employees’ understanding of their role inside organization. Empirical evidence proposes that servant leadership is positively linked with employees’ attitudes and performance [27, 28]. However, studies increasingly accentuate its psychological pathways. The genuine concerns of the leaders can create a psychologically secure climate for employees because they feel secure in their jobs. Similarly, their relationship with leaders enhances their performance quality [29, 30]. This exhibits that servant leadership is a contextual enabler instead of a behavioral influencer. In addition, servant leaders nurture and enhance employee perceived values, coordination, and performance outcomes [28, 31]. Employees working under servant leaders are often more proactive and enthusiastic, which motivates them to produce higher-quality work [5]. Consequently, engaged employees are more proactive in their work [32–34], and are dynamic, productive, and responsive to new information [29]. Furthermore [35], revealed that a servant leader produces energy that produces favorable work behavior. By assisting employees to find purpose and implications in their job, the servant leadership boosts intrinsic motivation and work engagement [33, 36, 37]. This motivational instrument is vital because employees feel valued and demonstrate their high level of performance [5, 38, 39]. Therefore, servant leadership is expected to form interrelated outcomes including job execution, institutional identification, psychological safety, perceived member status, and interpersonal coordination [40, 41]. Based on the discussion, the following are proposed hypotheses.

H1

Servant leadership positively predicts perceived member status, interpersonal coordination, and job-related outcomes (i.e., institutional identification, job execution, and psychological safety).

Mediating effect of perceived member status

Perceived member status is a subjective assessment by employees about their respect, inclusion, and recognition in the organizational structure. According to the social identity perspective, leadership behaviors that indicate value and inclusion can improve employees’ sense of belonging, which ultimately shapes employees’ positive job attitudes and behaviors [42, 43]. However, earlier studies have considered perceived member status as an outcome of leadership. The recent study suggested that perceived member status plays a vital mediating role in shaping behavioral responses [44, 45]. Servant leadership positively influences the perceived member status by establishing a supportive workplace environment, which further fosters a psychological connection between employees and their organization [13]. Once employees feel valued, they are likely to reciprocate via positive work behaviors, such as better performance, sense of identification, and psychological safety [44, 46, 47]. Perceived member status functions as a psychological mechanism that translates leadership influences into the behavioral outcomes [48]. Earlier studies confirm the mediating role of perceived member status between inclusive leadership, CSR-related behavioral mechanism and employee outcomes [49, 50]. Research study also revealed that perceived member status intervene the coordination and collaborative behaviors within the organizations [51]. However, the role of perceived member status in servant leadership contexts is still underdeveloped, especially the multidimensional outcomes of identification, execution, and psychological safety. Based on the discussion, the following are proposed hypotheses.

H2

Perceived member status positively predicts job-related outcomes, i.e., institutional identification, job execution, and psychological safety.

H3

Perceived member status mediates between servant leadership and job-related outcomes (i.e., institutional identification, job execution, and psychological safety).

Mediating effect of interpersonal coordination

Interpersonal coordination is defined as the extent to which employees align, cooperate, and share their understanding with other staff members for task execution [52, 53]. Interpersonal coordination is structured relational teamwork with an emphasis on communication, respect, and task interdependence [54]. Servant leadership improves interpersonal coordination as it fosters openness, trust, and transparent communication [52, 55]. According to the social identity perspective, leaders who are aware of the needs of their followers increase the collective identity, which helps the coordination between employees [54]. This exhibits that interpersonal coordination is not only a structural product, but also a socially created process that is shaped by leadership behavior. This eventually enhances interposal coordination, which further mitigates miscommunication and fosters psychological safety, leading to better performance outcomes [47, 56]. In addition, coordination allows employees to combine different skills and thoughts together and enhances the quality of problem-solving and execution [57]. Empirical evidences demonstrate that a participative environment enhances coordination, which eventually leads to better identification and better performance [58]. Earlier studies suggested that interpersonal coordination is a mediating process mechanism linking workplace diversity with performance outcomes [35]. However, the role of interpersonal coordination as a mediating mechanism in the servant leadership framework is underdeveloped or insufficiently theorized, particularly concerning psychological and behavioral job outcomes. Based on the discussion, the following are proposed hypotheses.

H4

Interpersonal coordination positively predicts job-related outcomes (i.e., institutional identification, job execution, and psychological safety).

H5

Interpersonal coordination mediates between servant leadership and job-related outcomes (i.e., institutional identification, job execution, and psychological safety).

Symbiotic diversity as a moderator

The term ‘diversity’, examined in earlier studies from the perspective of equality and discrimination, few studies evaluated the term ‘diversity’ from the viewpoint of synergistic or symbiotic form [59]. The symbiotic diversity setting is the workplace environment in which employees from diverse backgrounds collaborate via mutual support, inclusion, and use diverse skills to attain shared organizational goals. This enhances the employee’s coordination and inclusion [60, 61]. Symbiotic diversity focuses on the balance between employees, enabling them to bring together different perspectives into productive teamwork. According to the social identity perspective, workplace diversity develops synergy, which can foster fairness perceptions and belongingness, and this eventually increases group identification and cooperation [62]. Such a workplace environment reduces the uncertainty and builds trust, which further fortifies the effectiveness of servant leadership behaviors in shaping their followers’ interpersonal coordination and psychological outcomes [63–65]. Earlier studies revealed that the diversity climate might serve as boundary conditions strengthening the leadership outcome link [66]. In this regard, symbiotic diversity is projected to amplify the impact of servant leadership when it serves to enhance employees’ sense of belonging and teamwork orientation [67, 68]. Thus, the higher the symbiotic diversity, the more employees tend to communicate openly, coordinate, and integrate tasks. Despite its theoretical significance, symbiotic diversity has received little consideration as a moderating variable in servant leadership studies. Methodology

Research design

This study used a quantitative, multilevel, time-lagged multisource survey design to evaluate the direct, indirect, and cross-level relationships among the study variables. This design was adequate because the proposed conceptual model involved a hierarchical nature of data, with nursing employees nested within healthcare managers, requiring the simultaneous evaluation of both individual and organizational level effects. In addition, the time-lagged multisource survey design assist to mitigate common method bias, strengthening causal inference, and enhancing the validity of mediation and moderated mediation analysis by temporal separation between predictor and outcome factors.

Sample and procedure

The study was conducted in public and private healthcare institutions of Pakistan. These healthcare institutions operate in highly competitive and culturally diverse settings, which makes them ideal for examining the study variables. The study has adopted a multilevel research design in which healthcare managers are represented at the organization level (Level-2), whereas nursing staff are represented at the individual level (Level-1). In line with the recommendation of [69], a sufficient sample size for the multilevel modeling requires 50–100 observations at Level-2 and 300–500 observations at Level-1. In this study, the sample size at Level-2 was comprised of 360 healthcare managers, whereas Level-1 comprised 405 nursing employees. The sample size assures adequate statistical power and robustness for multilevel modeling [69]. We used a multistage stratified random sampling method to ensure the representativeness across different geographical regions and departments within organizations [70]. Subsequently, the data structure was hierarchical with nursing employees (Level-1) being nested within their respective healthcare manager (Level-2), enabling the evaluation of both within-group and cross-level relationships.

Levels aggregation

For aggregation processes, we used the referent-shift approach. This approach guarantees that within-group homogeneity and between-group heterogeneity are valid at a higher level of aggregation. To validate and evaluate the aggregation, within-group agreement, and between-group reliability, we used in-group interrater reliability (rWG (J)), and interclass correlation coefficients (ICC-1), and (ICC-2). The values of rWG (J) exceed the recommended threshold score, i.e., 0.70, which indicates high within-group variance. In addition, the value of ICC (1) revealed substantial between-group variance, whereas the value of ICC (2) exceeded the recommended threshold score, i.e., 0.70, indicating acceptable reliability of group-level means. These statistics supported the individual responses (aggregation) to the managerial level and justified the use of hierarchical linear modeling (HLM).

Measurements

All the measurement items used in this study were adapted from previously validated and developed scales. The questionnaire was adapted by integrating pre-established measurement instruments to examine the study factors, with minor modifications to ensure contextual relevance for the current research setting. The survey was administered in English, and all items were evaluated on a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). We measured the servant leadership style by using a 21-item scale adapted from [71–74]. Perceived Member Status (PMS) was measured with the modified four-item scale adapted from [75]. Institutional identification (II) was measured by using a modified three-item scale adapted from [76]. Interpersonal coordination was assessed using three items adapted from [53]. Job execution refers to workers meeting organizational goals by completing assigned tasks correctly and efficiently [77]. The job execution was measured using three items adapted from [78]. Psychological safety refers to the environment in which team members trust that they can take personal risks without worrying about embarrassment, rejection, or punishment [79]. Psychological safety was measured using five items adapted from [80]. The symbiotic diversity setting was measured by three items adapted from [81]. We used two control variables to mitigate the confounding effects. First, the team size (TS) is measured by counting the total number of individual team members working together as a team. Second, respondents’ ages were measured via dummy coded, i.e., 1 = 20–29, 2 = 30–39, 3 = 40–49, and 4 = 50 and above years. These variables were included to enrich the reliability of our findings.

Analytical approach

Before hypothesis testing, the data were screened for missing values, normality, and outliers. Descriptive statistics and correlation were examined. The internal consistency via Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability was examined, with all values found in apt threshold, i.e., 0.70 [82]. Because of the nested structure of the data, we used a multilevel modeling technique for evaluating the hierarchical structured data. At Level 2, the servant leadership and symbiotic diversity setting were specified, whereas at Level 1, perceived member status, interpersonal coordination, job execution, psychological safety, and institutional identification were modeled. Based on the established guidelines of [83], the Level 1 factors were group mean-centered, whereas the Level 2 factors were grand-mean centered. This method enhances parameter estimation, mitigates multicollinearity, and enables understanding of the cross-level relationships. To estimate the model parameters, we used maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) using Statsmodel package in Python. To evaluate the indirect, moderated mediation effects, we used the Monte Carlo resampling technique to assess 95% confidence intervals excluding zero. For all the data analysis, we used Python (v3.12). The data management was performed by using NumPy (v 2.1.3). Multilevel models were estimated by using Statsmodels (v 0.14.4), and the graphical representation was produced by Matplotlib (v 3.10.0).

Data collection

Before the main survey, the pilot study was conducted on 50 participants to check the clarity and relevance of the questionnaire items. Feedback acquired from participants and subject experts was incorporated to refine the measurement instrument. The ethical approval for data collection was obtained from the institutional review board. The participation was voluntary, and informed consent was taken from all the participants. We assured the anonymity, privacy, and confidentiality of the participants’ information. To minimize common method bias, we used a two-wave, multisource design with a one-week time lag between each wave. In the first wave, we collect the data from healthcare managers about the servant leadership and symbiotic diversity setting. Seven professional enumerators distributed 360 questionnaires, resulting in 351 usable responses, i.e., (97.5%), over a period of two-weeks. During the second wave, we collected the data from nursing employees about perceived member status, interpersonal coordination, job execution, psychological safety, and institutional identification. Eight professional enumerators distributed 405 questionnaires, resulting in 354 usable responses, i.e., (87.4%), over a period of three weeks. Table 2 shows the participants’ demographics. At Level 1, there were 77% male and 23% female participants, whereas at Level 2, there were 71% male and 29% female participants, respectively. At Level-1, the participants were between the ages of 20 and 45 years, whereas at Level-2, the participants were between the ages of 25 and 56 years, respectively. Regarding participants’ education, at Level-1, 30% of the participants had graduate-level education, and 70% of the participants had master-level education, whereas, at Level-2, 43% of the participants had graduate-level education, and 57% of the participants had master-level education, respectively.

Direct effects

The direct effect of servant leadership (SL) on perceived member status (PMS) (β = 0.61, p<.01, R2 = 0.52), interpersonal coordination (IC) (β = 0.57, p<.01, R2 = 0.49), institutional identification (II) (β = 0.33, p<.01, R2 = 0.55), job execution (JE) (β = 0.20, p<.01, R2 = 0.47) and psychological safety (PS) (β = 0.35, p<.01, R2 = 0.58) were found positive significant. Resultantly, H1 is accepted. Furthermore, the direct effect of PMS on II (β = 0.47, p<.01), JE (β = 0.41, p<.01) and PS (β = 0.66, p<.01), was found positive significant. Resultantly, H2 is accepted. The direct effect of IC on II (β = 0.65, p<.01), JE (β = 0.38, p<.01) and PS (β = 0.44, p<.01), was also found significant positive. Eventually, H4 is accepted.

Indirect effects

The mediating effect of PMS in the relationship between SL and II (β = 0.16, p<.01), JE (β = 0.23, p<.01), and PS (β = 0.37, p<.01) was estimated and found significant. Resultantly, H3 is accepted. Furthermore, the mediating effect of IC in the relationship between SL and II (β = 0.39, p<.01), JE (β = 0.15, p<.01), and PS (β = 0.16, p<.01) was estimated and found significant. Resultantly, H5 is accepted. This indicates partial mediation because, although the indirect effects of SL on PS, JE, and II via PMS and IC were significant, the direct effects of SL on these outcome variables remained significant after the inclusion of the mediators. Thus, PMS and IC account for part, but not all, of the effect of SL on employee outcomes.

Moderating effects

The moderating effect of symbiotic diversity setting (SDS) in the relationship between SL and PMS (β = 0.11, p<.01) and SL and IC (β = 0.22, p<.01) was found to be significantly positive. Ultimately, H6 is established.

Path analysis results support the proposed structural model. Servant leadership has a substantial direct impact on PMS, IC, PS, JE, and II, highlighting its importance for relational and performance aspects. The results also show meaningful indirect influences of servant leadership on employee attitudes and outcomes, with PMS and IC serving as key mediators. The findings suggest a moderated mediation model where relational factors mediate the link between servant leadership and employee outcomes, and diversity factors moderate this process.

Discussion

The study examines the association between servant leadership and job-related outcomes, i.e., job execution, psychological safety, and institutional identification. Precisely, the study investigates the mediating mechanism of perceived member status and interpersonal coordination in the relationship between servant leadership and job-related outcomes. The study further explores the moderating role of a symbiotic diversity setting, in between servant leadership, perceived member status, and interpersonal coordination. Based on social identity theory, the study projected that servant leadership is favorably associated with inclusion and coordination, precisely within a diversified workplace environment. The findings of the study revealed that servant leadership was positively associated with nursing employees’ interpersonal coordination, perceived member status, job execution, psychological safety, and institutional identification. These results are consistent with earlier studies showing that servant leaders cultivate a supportive work climate that improves employee well-being, performance, and engagement [12, 89]. The consistency might be due to similar methodological approaches used in the previous studies, using a survey design and self-reported leadership scales with employee perceptions of leader support. However, in the present study, the relationships between the variables seem to be relatively high, which could be due to the nature of the health care setting. Nursing involves a high degree of interdependence, emotional sensitivity, and collaboration with others. Therefore, leadership styles that focus on empathy, support, and service may have a greater impact on nursing job-related attitudes and behaviors. This might be attributed to how servant leadership is valued in different cultures, where collectivist cultures may place more emphasis on relational leadership and group harmony, thereby strengthening the impact of servant leadership on employee attitudes and behaviors. These findings further support Social Identity Theory, which argues that leaders who act humbly and compassionate and include others in their behavior, increase employees’ identification and sense of belonging [26, 90]. Past research has primarily focused on the direct relationship between leadership and employee outcomes, and the current results offer further evidence that identity-related processes are important in understanding how servant leadership can lead to positive outcomes. This builds upon previous research on leadership that specifically emphasizes how servant leadership has the potential to affect performance, but also impacts employees’ social identity in the organization [90]. Additionally, the study found that perceived member status and interpersonal coordination significantly mediated the relationship between servant leadership and job-related outcomes. The results are consistent with previous studies that have found employee respect and value to be positively associated with open communication, knowledge sharing, collaboration, and mutual support [80, 89]. Unlike previous studies that have looked at mediators individually, the present study looked at psychological and relational mediators together. This methodological difference may be a reason why the indirect effects were found to be stronger in the present study, as both internal identity processes and external collaborative behaviors were measured. These two mechanisms can be particularly relevant in the healthcare context, where staff performance relies on confident individuals as well as teamwork. An important finding of the study is the moderating effect of the context, SDS. Results indicated that SDS significantly enhanced the relationship between servant leadership and perceived member status and interpersonal coordination. These findings align with theoretical frameworks that indicate that diversity-supportive climates can improve the effectiveness of inclusive leadership practices [90]. The results of the present study, however, are important extensions of previous studies. Previous research has typically conceptualized diversity as a simple additive predictor and has argued that diversity climates have a direct impact on employee outcomes [89]. Our findings, on the contrary, indicate diversity is not simply a standalone precursor, but rather a boundary condition that interacts with the effectiveness of the leadership process. This finding is a significant extension of the additive model. The present study provides support for an interaction-based or contingency perspective on diversity climate, such that the impact of leadership behaviors is contingent on the diversity climate. From a practical perspective, servant leadership principles may not necessarily result in positive employee outcomes if the work environment does not foster diversity, inclusion, and mutual respect. Therefore, the additive model can be extended to include contextual moderators (e.g., SDS) because the effectiveness of leadership is dependent on the environment. Additionally, the study showed that the indirect paths were significantly stronger when the SDS was high compared to low SDS. There was a significant indirect path from servant leadership to job-related outcomes via perceived member status and interpersonal coordination. This is different from previous research, where diversity has been mainly viewed as a direct predictor, not a moderator. Part of the reason is due to the differences in measurement. Previous studies have tended to rely on general diversity climate measures, measuring diversity either in representation or in diversity policies, whereas SDS measures the quality of symbiotic relationships between diverse workers. This more nuanced measurement may help support this notion that diversity is an amplifier of relational mechanisms; that is, it does not have a direct effect on the outcomes. The results support the general literature on the positive impact of servant leadership on well-being, commitment, and performance of employees [12, 73, 89]. The study contributes to the field of leadership by showing that the concept of servant leadership has an impact on a sense of belonging, on interpersonal coordination, and on the performance-related results via psychological and relational mechanisms. It also contributes to diversity literature, demonstrating that such a symbiotic diversity environment supports these mechanisms. The study brings together the concepts of servant leadership, social identity process, and diversity climate in a moderated mediation model to provide a more comprehensive explanation of the relationship between inclusive leadership and employee effectiveness in complex and diverse healthcare organizations.

Theoretical implications

Theoretically, this study contributes to servant leadership and diversity management literature. First, the study integrates servant leadership theory with social identity theory to explain how servant leadership is linked with constructive employee job-related outcomes among nursing employees. Precisely, the perceived member status and interpersonal coordination were found to be important mediating process mechanisms linking servant leadership with nursing staff job-related outcomes. Second, this study contributes to the diversity management literature by empirically supporting the idea of symbiotic diversity setting (SDS) as a contextual moderator. In contrast to the traditional diversity perspective, the SDS focuses on mutual value creation, which goes beyond mere representation of diversity and promotes a sense of mutual interdependence and inclusion that may indeed be modified when servant leadership behavior is likely to best be related to positive outcomes. This framing is in contrast to additive models that treat diversity and leadership as separate contributors, and shows that there can be a complementary relationship between an inclusive leadership style (SL) and an enabling organizational environment. Third, in the context of multilevel leadership research, the study makes a further contribution by connecting factors at the managerial levels, i.e., (SL and SDS), with the employee level outcomes, i.e., (member status, coordination, job execution, psychological safety, and institutional identification). This is a substantial cross-level specification because the effects of leadership are studied within the context of the overall social system instead of isolation. Lastly, concentrating on the healthcare sector of Pakistan, this study contributes to the sparse range of studies conducted in non-Western environments, to enhance the overall applicability and cultural appropriateness of the servant leadership theory.

Practical implications

The current findings carry several practical implications for healthcare organizations. First, the positive link between servant leadership and nursing staff job-related outcomes highlights the value of investing in leadership development programs that cultivate servant-oriented behaviors, such as ethical stewardship, commitment to followers’ growth, and empathetic listening. Healthcare organizations might benefit from implementing such capabilities into the performance appraisal process, monitoring programs, and selection process for nursing employees. Second, the moderating role of symbiotic diversity setting (SDS) proposes that leadership development initiatives are likely to be more impactful when instigated alongside the organizational efforts to nurture a holistic environment. Diversity training programs must step out for awareness raising and focus on interdependence, cross-functional association, and the concrete value of diverse perspectives in patient care. Building structural conditions, such as comprehensive team standards and leadership commitment to diversity, might help to trigger the relational benefits linked with servant leadership. Third, the link involving interpersonal coordination as a mediating factor proposed that organizations could support nursing employees’ well-being and performance by vigorously facilitating teamwork structure. Such teamwork structure includes open communication, interdisciplinary briefings, shared health record access, and protocols that mitigate ambiguity about responsibilities. Fourth, the findings related to the perceived member status highlight that efforts to improve employees’ sense of inclusion might be associated with enhanced psychological safety and institutional identification. HR professionals and healthcare managers consider integrating metrics to guide targeted interventions. Lastly, when selecting and promoting individuals into leadership positions, healthcare organizations might benefit from evaluating servant leadership capabilities alongside administrative and technical skills. Leaders with such qualities have higher chances of establishing inclusive and high-performing work environments.

Limitations and future research domains

There are some limitations of the current study. The sample was taken from healthcare institutions of Pakistan, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future researchers replicate this study in different cultural and institutional contexts, precisely in terms of deeper-level diversity. Second, our study focused on a limited set of nursing staff job-related outcomes. Future research should explore other possible job-related outcomes, such as collaboration, societal integration, and creative behavior, to provide a more holistic understanding. Third, the study evaluated only two mediating mechanisms. Future studies should consider other relevant contextual mediating mechanism factors that affect servant leadership and job-related outcomes. Fourth, the cross-sectional time-lagged design of the study might limit the causal inference. Future studies should focus on the mixed-method technique to capture the contextual and dynamic nature of leadership and diversity relations more efficiently.

Conclusion

This study examined the relationship between servant leadership and outcomes for nursing staff, focusing on job performance, psychological safety, and identity-related processes in various healthcare settings. Based on the proposed theoretical framework, the results show that the effect of servant leadership on employee outcomes is significant, with perceived member status and interpersonal coordination serving as the mediating mechanisms. In particular, the findings reveal that servant leadership increases the perception of member status and interpersonal coordination among nurses when they perceive their supervisors as exhibiting high levels of servant leadership. In turn, these mechanisms help to execute better jobs and increase psychological safety. The results also substantiate that symbiotic diversity plays an important contextual role in these relationships, and therefore, that the effectiveness of servant leadership in diverse nursing teams is reinforced. The findings of this study statistically support the proposed model by linking clearly defined psychological and relational pathways from servant leadership to performance-related outcomes, as well as identity-related outcomes. It does so by filling an important gap in the literature on diversity-sensitive leadership in explaining how and through which mechanisms servant leadership functions in complex healthcare settings.
