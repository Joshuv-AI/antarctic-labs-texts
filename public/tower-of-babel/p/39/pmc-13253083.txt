Aberrant protein S-nitrosylation disrupts axonal development and metabolic homeostasis in a Cntnap2 mouse model of autism
Authors: Maryam Kartawy, Shashank Kumar Ojha, Mallikarjuna Nimgampalle, Haitham Amal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein S-nitrosylation (SNO) is a nitric oxide (·NO)-dependent post-translational modification that regulates various biological functions. Dysregulated SNO is involved in several brain disorders; however, its role in autism spectrum disorder (ASD) remains largely unexplored. This could be due to the transient nature and low abundance of SNO-modified proteins, which complicates their detection. Here, we employed SNOTRAP, a high-throughput mass spectrometry–based approach that captures, labels, and quantifies S-nitrosylated peptides at specific cysteines, to profile the cortical SNO-proteome in the Cntnap2 mouse model of ASD. Quantitative analysis identified significant alterations in S-nitrosylation across 76 peptides from 69 proteins. Among these, MAP1B, a key regulator of microtubule dynamics during axon formation, emerged as a prominent target, exhibiting exclusive S-nitrosylation at Cys1305 in the Cntnap2−/− cortex but not in WT. Consistently, Cntnap2−/− neurons displayed shortened axons, a phenotype that was rescued by pharmacological inhibition of ·NO signaling, linking aberrant S-nitrosylation to structural neuronal deficits. Beyond cytoskeletal regulation, pathway enrichment analysis revealed a significant influence of altered S-nitrosylation on metabolic networks, including glycolysis, the TCA cycle, pyruvate metabolism, and amino acid biosynthesis. Remarkably, many metabolic enzymes exhibited reduced or absent S-nitrosylation at specific cysteine residues, indicating a pathological shift in the SNO landscape. Consistent with these molecular changes, the Cntnap2−/− cortex exhibited an elevated NADH/NAD+ ratio, reflecting altered redox balance and thus dysregulated cellular metabolism, which was normalized upon inhibition of ·NO. Together, these findings highlight S-nitrosylation as a convergent regulatory mechanism acting across multiple neuronal domains, ultimately impacting synaptic function and ASD-related phenotypes.Keywords: Nitric oxide, S-nitrosylation, SNOTRAP, Proteomics, Autism spectrum disorder, Synaptic dysfunction, Metabolism dysregulation, Cntnap2

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder associated with impaired social skills, restricted/repetitive behaviors, anxiety, and communication deficits [1] [2]. Genetic research has revealed the involvement of numerous genes in ASD [3]; among these genes is the CNTNAP2 (contactin-associated protein-like 2). This gene encodes a transmembrane protein member of the neurexin superfamily, involved in neuron-glia interactions, clustering of potassium channels in myelinated axons [4,5], and synapse formation and stabilization [6]. Cntnap2 knockout (KO) mice showed social and communication deficits, anxiety, repetitive behaviors, and hyperactivity [7,8]. Moreover, Cntnap2 deficiency leads to a reduced stability in newly formed dendritic spines, impairments in cortical neuron migration, GABAergic dysfunction, and neuronal asynchrony [6,7]. However, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these abnormalities remain largely undefined. Among the molecular signaling pathways implicated in synaptic development and function, nitric oxide (·NO) signaling has emerged as a key regulator [8,9].

·NO is a small gasotransmitter produced in various organs and tissues, including the central and peripheral nervous systems [9]. Under normal physiological conditions, ·NO production and degradation are balanced, playing a vital role in cell signaling and regulating numerous physiological functions [10]. However, at elevated concentrations, ·NO becomes toxic, leading to abnormal cell signaling and cell death [8,9]. Disruptions in ·NO levels have been implicated in various neurodevelopmental, neuropsychiatric, and neurodegenerative disorders [9]. Elevated levels of ·NO and nitrosative stress markers, including nitrite, nitrate, and 3-nitrotyrosine, have been reported in ASD patients and experimental models, suggesting dysregulated ·NO production in the autistic brains [8,11,12]. Recently, we showed that elevated cortical ·NO levels in the Cntnap2 mouse model contribute to molecular and structural synaptic abnormalities and behavioral phenotypes. Importantly, inhibition of ·NO production via neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) reversed these ASD-like features, highlighting ·NO signaling as a central pathological factor and a potential therapeutic target in the Cntnap2 mouse model [8]. However, the molecular pathways mediating these ·NO driven effects remain undefined.

One of the key mechanisms by which ·NO regulates cell signaling is S-nitrosylation, a post-translational modification (PTM) of proteins in which a nitroso group (+NO) is incorporated into a reactive protein cysteine thiolate residue (PS−) to form a protein nitrosothiol (PSNO) [13].

Although nitric oxide reacts rapidly and preferentially with heme/metal centers (e.g., soluble guanylate cyclase, cytochrome c oxidase) [14] and with reactive oxygen species such as superoxide, which converts ·NO to peroxynitrite (ONOO−), the ·NO radical itself reacts very slowly with cysteine thiols and is therefore unlikely to mediate S-nitrosylation under physiological conditions directly. Instead, S-nitrosylation is thought to occur mainly through ·NO-related species with nitrosonium (NO+)-like character, often generated via interaction of ·NO with transition metals. This NO+-like intermediate reacts with nucleophilic cysteine thiolates (RS−) on target proteins, leading to the formation of S-nitrosothiols (SNO-proteins) [15]. Moreover, the efficiency and selectivity of S-nitrosylation are strongly influenced by the cellular redox environment. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) can modulate thiol reactivity and promote the formation of ·NO-derived nitrosating intermediates that facilitate S-nitrosothiol formation [16,17]. Thus, fluctuations in ROS levels may regulate both the stability and specificity of protein S-nitrosylation events.

In the brain, SNO contributes to essential processes such as synaptogenesis, vesicle trafficking, neuronal migration, synaptic plasticity, and protein stability [9,18]. Although SNO is a biologically significant PTM, its in vivo specificity and origins remain poorly understood [19,20]. Evidence suggests that SNO is not uniformly distributed across all cysteine (Cys) residues or Cys-containing proteins, indicating a selective process. While numerous reports highlight the occurrence of S-nitrosylation, the precise residues affected on many proteins remain unidentified, making it challenging to fully understand their functional implications [19,20]. Identifying specific SNO residues typically requires a labor-intensive combination of mutagenesis and MS-based approaches. The biotin-switch technique (BST) is the most widely used method for detecting SNO proteins. However, BST has limitations, including potential false positives caused by incomplete blocking of free Cys-thiols, which can complicate detecting true SNO-Cys residues [19,20]. Thus, to improve the accurate detection and mapping of SNO proteins and SNO-Cys residues, a group of researchers has developed a method called SNOTRAP (SNO trapping by triaryl phosphine), which uses chemical probes to tag S-nitrosylated proteins, allowing global, high-throughput enrichment and identification of SNO proteins and their corresponding SNO-sites [[19], [20], [21]].

Using this method, we mapped changes in the S-nitrosylation pattern in the brains of healthy and diseased mice across distinct biological contexts [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26]] as well as clinical samples from children with ASD [8]. The current study was designed to explore the role of protein S-nitrosylation in the Cntnap2−/− brain. Applying SNOTRAP to the Cntnap2−/− enabled the first systematic characterization of disorder-associated alterations in the cortical S-nitroso-proteome. Using this platform in combination with bioinformatic analyses, we identified significant changes in S-nitrosylation intensities of 76 SNO-peptides from 69 unique proteins in the Cntnap2−/− cortex. Enrichment analysis revealed neuronal and metabolic processes affected by aberrant S-nitrosylation. Follow-up biochemical studies showed that axonal length in cultured Cntnap2−/− neurons was shortened compared to WT neurons and was restored upon inhibition of nNOS. At the molecular level, this rescue was accompanied by increased SNO-MAP1B, a cytoskeletal regulator. In parallel, metabolic profiling revealed an elevated NADH/NAD + ratio in the Cntnap2−/− cortex, indicative of metabolic stress, which was significantly normalized following inhibition of ·NO signaling. Together, these findings extend the utility of SNOTRAP by revealing how disrupted ·NO signaling reshapes protein S-nitrosylation, provides mechanistic insights into disease states, and offers a powerful tool for identifying novel therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Materials: Primary antibodies, anti-MAP1B (58595S), Tau (4019S), anti-β-actin (#3700) and secondary antibodies, Anti-Rabbit Alexa fluor 594 (#8889), Anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (#4408), ProLong gold Antifade with DAPI (#8961), and protease phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (#5872) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Streptavidin Alexa Flour™ 488 (S32354) were purchased from Invitrogen Thermo Fisher Scientific. High-capacity streptavidin (20361) was purchased from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, Massachusetts, US). Amicon ultra-10 centrifugal filter units (UFC801096) were purchased from Merck (US). For MS, acetonitrile (ACN), protease inhibitors cocktail (P-8340), and distilled water were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Sequencing-grade modified trypsin (V5111) was purchased from Promega (Wisconsin, USA). The liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) solvents were HPLC grade. 7-Nitro-1H-indazole (Cat. No.: L07970.06) from thermo-scientific, SNAP S-nitroso-N-acetylpenicillamine (ID: AG0063E3) from Angene. Other general chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and Bio-Rad Laboratories (Haifa, Israel).

Animals: All animal experiments were conducted under the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines and the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (IACUC- MD-20-16049-3). Cntnap2−/− (Strain:01 7482) and C57BL/6J (Strain #:000664) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Farmington, CT, USA). We used Cntnap2−/− homozygous mating pairs for breeding. C57BL/6J is a control group for Cntnap2−/−. The animals were kept at a room temperature (RT) of 23 °C in a 12-h light/dark cycle and fed ad libitum with standard mouse chow and water. Only male mice were included in this study.

SNOTRAP sample preparation for SNO proteins and cysteine detection: The whole cerebral cortex was isolated from the 6-week-old mice Cntnap2−/− and WT. The cortex tissue was homogenized in the lysis buffer (pH = 7.7) prepared with 100 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM neocuproine, 20 mM Iodoacetamide, 1% Triton-X100, 1% SDS and 1% protease cocktail. The samples were centrifuged at 17,000g for 45 min at 4 ° C. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentrations were estimated using the BCA (Bicinchoninic Acid) protein assay (Sigma-Aldrich). We proceeded further with 12 mg of protein in the experiment. We washed the samples with 50 mM HEPES in a 10K MWCO. We washed three times at 5000g for 30min at 10° C. The samples were labeled with 1.5 mM SNOTRAP dissolved in acetonitrile and HEPES (50:50) for 2h in the dark. SNOTRAP labeled samples were washed 3 times with 50 mM HEPES at 10°C for 30min in 10K MWCO tubes. The samples were then digested with trypsin at 37°C overnight. The SNO peptides were pulled down with streptavidin beads. Beads (150 μl) were washed three times with 1 ml of 100 mM ABC (Ammonium bicarbonate) containing 150 mM NaCl and 0.05% SDS, followed by three washes with 50 mM ABC only (600 g for 2 min each). The samples were then added to the beads, and pull-down was carried out for 2 h at room temperature under slow rotational mixing. The beads were then washed with 5 different buffers (100 mM ABC +150 mM NaCl +1 mM EDTA+ 0.05% SDS + 0.1% tritox-100 [pH 7.4], 2) 100 mM ABC + −150 mM NaCl+1 mM EDTA+0.1% SDS. 3) 100 mM ABC +150 mM NaCl +0.05% SDS. 4) 100 mM ABC, 5). 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.7) two times each. The proteins were eluted from the beads with 10 mM TCEP prepared in 50 mM ABC at 37°C. The samples were then alkylated with 100 mM NEM prepared in 50 mM HEPES for 2h at room temperature.

Mass-spectrometry: The samples were desalted on a C-18 stage tip and then SCX and analyzed by LC-MS/MS on the Exploris 480 (Thermo) and identified by MaxQuant 2.1.1.0 against the mouse Uniprot DB. In this analysis, the peptide spectrum match-PSMs FDR is set to 0.05, and the protein FDR set is to 1. Additional statistics analysis was done in Perseus 1.6.15.0.

Bioinformatics and statistics: Initially, a two-tailed t-test was performed to identify proteins that were significantly altered in the knockout samples. Only proteins with a p-value less than 0.05 were included in the analysis. The search tool for the interacting proteins (STRING, version 12.0) was used to dissect the biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), molecular functions (MF), the mammalian phenotype ontology (Monarch), and pathways analysis, and to perform K-means clustering analysis (http://string-db.org) [27]. The Protoemaps were generated using a graphical tool available at (www.proteomaps.net). Cytoscape V3.3.0 software was used for the visualization of the protein-protein interaction [28] and for the use of the CytoHubba plugin. In CytoHubba, we selected the top 10 nodes from each of the following five algorithms [29]. These algorithms include maximal clique centrality (MCC) [30], maximum neighborhood component (MNC) [31], degree centrality [32], Edge Percolated Component (EPC) [33], and betweenness centrality [34]. GraphPad Prism v.10 software was used to generate bar graphs, violin plots, a volcano plot, and heatmaps. GOChords, Enrichment bubble plots, enrichment plots with gradient colors, sanky and dot plot, cluster heatmap, and balloon plots were all generated using SRplot [35]. The information on ASD high-risk genes was acquired from the SFARI gene database [36]. DISGENET database [37] was used to apply gene-disease association analysis. Illustrations were created using Biorender software (www.biorender.com).

Primary cortical culture: Primary cortical neuronal cultures were derived from embryonic day 16.5-17.5 fetal mouse brains. The cortex isolated from embryonic mouse brains was placed in HBSS (Gibco) and dissociated mechanically with a 19G needle/syringe 2 times. Then, dissociated tissue was treated with trypsin-EDTA solution 0.25% (Sigma-Aldrich-T4049) at 37 °C for 5 min. Trypsin activities were neutralized with FBS, filtered with 70 μm nylon membrane, and then centrifuged at 400 x g for 10 min. The cells were seeded on poly-d-lysine (P1024, Sigma-Aldrich) coated glass or plastic-bottom 22-mm coverslip placed in 6-well plates (cell density was ∼1,50,000-2,00,000) for microscopic observation. Cells were cultured in neurobasal medium [neurobasal medium (Gibco), 2% B27 supplement, 2 mM l-glutamine, 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S), and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 95% air and 5% CO2. The cells were grown for 14 days. The treatment of SNAP (200μM)/7-NI (100μM) was done 24h before the fixation of cells.

Confocal microscopy of cultured cells and neurons: Primary cells cultured on 22mm/19 mm coverslips were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. The fixed cells were washed 3 times for 10 min each with PBS and incubated in chilled methanol for 5 min at room temperature. After washing the cells three times with PBS, they were blocked with blocking buffer (PBS containing 1% BSA, 1% goat serum, and 0.1% triton-X100) for 1h at room temperature and then incubated with the respective primary antibodies prepared in the blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Next day the cells were washed 3 times with PBS for 10 min each and cells were then incubated with 300 nM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, 23030) in PBS-Triton ×100 (0.3% v/v) at 37 °C for 30 min to block the free sulfhydryl groups on proteins. The NEM solution was freshly prepared. First, it was dissolved in a small amount (10% of total volume) of methanol, and then further dissolved in PBS-Triton ×100 (0.3% v/v). Cells were then washed 3 × 5 min using 1× PBS and incubated with biotin-conjugated SNOTRAP (250 μM) in acetonitrile-PBS-Triton (50%: 50% v/v) at room temperature (R.T.) for 1 h to allow binding of SNOTRAP with –SNO groups on proteins was used, cells were washed 5 × 10 min after SNOTRAP treatment, then further incubated with streptavidin-DyLight 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, 21832) diluted 1/500 with PBS-Triton × 100 (0.3% v/v), incubated at R.T. for 1 h and afterwards washed 5 × 5 min. Then the cells were incubated with anti-rabbit Alexa fluor 594 (1:1000) secondary antibodies for 1 h, washed with PBS 4 times, and mounted with the anti-fade DAPI mounting media. Finally, the cells were observed under at 40×/60×/100× using a Nikon confocal microscope.

Axon length quantification: Primary cortical neurons were fixed at DIV4 with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. After blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against MAP1B and Tau (enriched in developing axons), followed by appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Nikon confocal microscope equipped with a 60× objective, under identical exposure settings for all experimental conditions.

The axon was defined as the Tau-positive neurite that was clearly emerging from the soma and whose length was at least twice that of the second-longest neurite. This definition is based on established morphological criteria for early-stage neurons, where Tau is enriched in the developing axon but is not exclusively axon-specific. Axon length was quantified using FIJI/ImageJ. After spatial calibration with the microscope scale, the axon was traced from the soma to the distal growth cone using the segmented line tool, and the length (μm) was obtained with the “Measure” function. For each condition, 50-100 neurons were analyzed, across 4 independent neuronal cultures.

Western blotting: Equal amounts of protein were mixed with Laemmli sample buffer (1:1), boiled at 95 °C for 5 min, and separated by SDS-PAGE. Proteins were then transferred to a PVDF or nitrocellulose membrane, which was blocked with 5% non-fat milk or BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was incubated with the appropriate primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by washing with TBST and incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After three washes, protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and visualized with an imaging system. Band intensities were quantified and normalized to a loading control such as β-actin.

NADH/NAD analysis: NAD+ and NADH levels were analyzed using the NAD/NADH colorimetric assay kit from Abcam (ab65348) as described briefly. Tissue was lysed with NAD extraction buffer. The lysate was centrifuged at top speed for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and filtered with a 10 kDa spin column tube. NAD+ was decomposed by heating the samples at 60 °C for 30 min, followed by immediate cooling on ice. The reaction mix was prepared by mixing the NAD cycling buffer and NAD cycling enzyme mix from the kit. The plate was incubated at RT, and NAD+ and NADH levels were measured using the microplate reader (Biotek 800S from Agilent) at 2h intervals at OD 450 nm.

Statistics for biochemical studies: The mean and Standard Error of the mean (SEM) were calculated for immunofluorescence and NADH/NAD+. A two-tailed t-test or one‐way ANOVA, along with Tukey's multiple comparison test, were used to determine statistical significance. Statistical details and methods used in each experiment can be found in the figure legends.

Results

Differential protein S-nitrosylation in the Cntnap2−/− cortex

To characterize disease-associated changes in protein S-nitrosylation, cortical samples from WT and Cntnap2−/− (KO) mice were labeled with SNOTRAP and analyzed by MS. This approach enabled the identification and quantification of SNO peptides modified by the NEM moiety. This was followed by integrative systems biology and bioinformatics analysis to gain a comprehensive, systems-level understanding of SNO-protein functions (Fig. 1A). Proteomic analysis using the SNOTRAP-based MS platform identified 828 SNO-peptides corresponding to 537 unique proteins. Among these, 76 SNO-peptides from 69 unique proteins exhibited significant changes in SNO intensities, either increased or decreased, across different cysteine residues in the KO cortex (Fig. 1B and C). To better visualize this pattern of change, we generated a volcano plot in which the upper right side of the plot shows the peptides with a significantly increased SNO abundance, and the upper left side shows the proteins with significantly decreased SNO abundance across different peptides (Fig. 1C). A detailed list of the SNO proteins, along with their corresponding cysteines, is presented in Supplementary Table 1 for both groups.

To investigate and interpret the biological significance of the altered SNO proteome in the KO cortex, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis examining biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC). The GO analysis revealed the enrichment of several terms related to metabolism (these include glycolysis, TCA cycle, amino acid metabolism, and others), genetic information processing (these include ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, ribosomes, chaperone and folding catalysts, peptidases, and others) and cellular processes (Such as apoptosis, cell cycle, peroxisome, and others) (Fig. 1D). MF analysis indicated that the SNO proteins are involved in various biochemical activities, including enzyme binding, cytoskeletal protein binding, ATP binding, and Oxidoreductase binding. CC analysis showed that the SNO proteins are localized to the cytosol, synapse, mitochondrion, axons, and others. All the GO analysis is included in Supplementary Table 2.

Next, we sought to determine whether upregulated and downregulated SNO proteins were associated with distinct biological processes. In the KO cortex, we found a total of 57 cysteines belonging to 52 proteins with a significant decrease in the S-nitrosylation pattern; these include 20 unique sites. While 19 cysteines belonging to 19 different proteins with an increased S-nitrosylation pattern, and 9 of them were uniquely SNOed. Unique sites refer to proteins that were S-nitrosylated exclusively in either Cntnap2−/− or WT samples (Fig. 1E). To further visualize the quantitative differences in the relative abundance of these proteins in Cntnap2−/− cortices, we conducted a heat map analysis, showing the top upregulated and downregulated SNO-peptides (Fig. 1F). Interestingly, enriched GO annotations such as response to extracellular stimuli (FDR = 0.0268), negative regulation of cytoskeleton organization (FDR = 0.0369), and regulation of supramolecular fiber organization (FDR = 0.0369) were exclusively identified among the upregulated SNO-proteins (Fig. 1F and G). In contrast, the downregulated GO categories revealed significant enrichment of metabolic process (FDR = 0.0183), glucose metabolic process (FDR = 0.0046), and pyruvate metabolism (FDR = 1.24e-05), and neuronal-related processes such as nervous system development (FDR = 0.0208), neurogenesis (FDR = 0.0205), and others (Fig. 1F and G). These findings suggest that in the Cntnap2−/− cortex, SNO selectively targets distinct proteins associated with specific functional categories, with upregulated SNO-proteins predominantly linked to cytoskeletal processes and downregulated SNO-proteins largely involved in metabolic and neuronal processes. All the GO terms are listed in Supplementary Table 3. It is also important to consider whether altered S-nitrosylation patterns in the Cntnap2−/− cortex may reflect underlying changes in protein abundance. To address this possibility, we compared the S-nitrosoproteome with our global proteomic dataset, which identified 183 significantly up- or down-regulated proteins between WT and KO samples (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Notably, only a limited subset of proteins (N = 5) overlapped between the global and S-nitrosylation datasets (Supplementary Fig. 1B), suggesting that most S-nitrosylation changes occur independently of alterations in total protein abundance. These observations could support the notion that dysregulated S-nitrosylation represents a regulatory modification rather than a simple consequence of differential protein expression.

Differential S-nitrosylation of ASD high-risk candidate proteins was observed in the Cntnap2−/− cortex

To date, the Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative (SFARI Gene) has cataloged over 1000 genes associated with ASD. In this study, we aimed to investigate whether the differentially S-nitrosylated proteins in the Cntnap2−/− cortex significantly overlap with the human high-risk encoded genes curated by SFARI. To achieve this, a Venn diagram analysis was performed to compare the SFARI catalog of ASD high-risk candidate proteins with the list of significant S-nitrosylated proteins. Our analysis identified 10 proteins that overlapped between the S-nitrosylated proteome and the SFARI gene database. These proteins are SNO-CSMD1, SNO-LRP1, SNO-NPTN, SNO-MAP1B, SNO-STXBP1, SNO-PPP3CA, SNO-GNAI1, SNO-GSTM1, SNO-YWHAE, SNO-FABP5 (Fig. 2A and C). These SNO proteins are ranked with varying scores according to the SFARI database, reflecting their different levels of association with autism. 1- high confidence risk gene, 2- strong candidate, and 3-suggestive evidence (Fig. 2B). 9 out of 10 proteins showed a significantly decreased SNO abundance on different cysteines (Fig. 2D-panels 1-9), except for MAP1B, which showed a significantly increased SNO abundance on Cys1305 in the Cntnap2−/− (KO) compared to the WT (Fig. 2D-panel 10). This pattern of change may disrupt protein function and interactions, and lead to dysregulated cellular signaling.

To examine which biological processes can be impacted by this pattern of change in the SNO signaling, BP analysis was performed on these ASD-related proteins. The analysis revealed enrichment of several biological processes known to be involved in autism, including regulation of neurogenesis (FDR = 0.0301), axonogenesis (FDR = 0.0193), transport across the blood-brain barrier (FDR = 0.0313), modulation of chemical synaptic transmission (FDR = 0.0074), regulation of transport (FDR = 0.0474), and others (Fig. 2E). This overlap with established ASD risk genes/proteins highlights the translational relevance of dysregulated protein S-nitrosylation in ASD pathophysiology.

Protein-protein interaction network and clustering analysis of the SNO-proteome

We applied K-means clustering analysis to efficiently organize our dataset and extract meaningful patterns among the differentially expressed SNO proteins. This analysis, combined with functional annotations, enabled us to gain deeper insights into the coordinated roles of SNO-proteins in specific biological processes (Fig. 3).

The differentially expressed SNO-proteins in the Cntnap2−/− cortex were able to form a distinct network with a total of 149 interactions between the nodes. To simplify the network, we clustered the SNO-proteins into 3 clusters using the STRING K-means clustering algorithm along with the GO annotations associated with each cluster (Fig. 3A–D).

BP analysis of the blue-node cluster (SNO-NCAN, SNO-GNAI1, SNO-CRMP1, SNO-CNTN1, SNO-LSAMP, SNO-CLSTN1, among others) revealed that these SNO-proteins are involved in cell adhesion (FDR = 0.0096). The mammalian phenotype ontology (Monarch) analysis revealed enrichment of abnormal long-term potentiation (FDR = 0.0065), and abnormal CNS synaptic transmission (FDR = 0.0135). CC analysis revealed that these SNO proteins are localized to the plasma membrane (FDR = 0.0332) (Fig. 3B).

The green-node cluster (SNO-CORO1B, SNO-YWHAE, SNO-PPP3CA, SNO-MAP1B, SNO-WDR1, SNO-ATP1B1, and others) showed significant enrichment of several biological processes, including positive regulation of dendritic spine morphogenesis (FDR = 0.00091), nervous system development (FDR = 0.00029), axonogenesis (FDR = 0.0292), response to stress (FDR = 0.0322), and others. Monarch analysis showed enrichment of autism-related terms such as abnormal fear/anxiety-related behavior (FDR = 0.0490), abnormal Purkinje cell morphology (FDR = 0.0060), abnormal limbic system morphology (FDR = 0.0179), and others (Fig. 3C).

The red-nodes (SNO-PKM, SNO-PFKM, SNO-IDH3A, SNO-TPI1, SNO-HSP90AB1, SNO-OGDHL, SNO-PRDX2, and others) were enriched among various metabolism-related processes such as ATP metabolic process (FDR = 3.22e-06), Pyruvate metabolic process (FDR = 3.99e-10), NADH metabolic process (FDR = 4.52e-05), oxidation-reduction process (FDR = 0.00071), removal of superoxide radicals (FDR = 0.0360), and others. MF analysis revealed significant enrichment of NAD binding (FDR = 0.00044), ATP binding (FDR = 0.0014), and Oxidoreductase activity (FDR = 0.00070) (Fig. 3D). Metabolism-related proteins as key hub players in the Cntnap2−/− SNO network

S-nitrosylation can modify multiple proteins simultaneously and potentially reshape protein interaction networks [38], therefore, identifying the key nodes that organize these networks is essential for understanding how .NO signaling may propagate across cellular pathways. To address this, we performed a centrality analysis of the S-nitrosylated protein network using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape to identify proteins that may function as key regulatory hubs. These algorithms include maximal clique centrality (MCC), maximum neighborhood component (MNC), Edge Percolated Component (EPC), degree centrality, and betweenness centrality. Each algorithm offers a unique output on the importance of nodes within the network.

The MCC algorithm identifies proteins in highly interconnected regions, highlighting proteins that maintain critical interactions in cohesive sub-networks. According to MCC, we identified 10 hub proteins such as SNO-TPI1, SNO-LDHA, SNO-PKM, SNO-HSP90AB1, SNO-OGDH, SNO-PFKM, and others (Fig. 4A). The MNC algorithm, which detects proteins that are key for local network cohesion, identified SNO–Me1, SNO-PFKM, SNO-TPI, SNO-OGDH, SNO-LDHA, SNO-PRDX1, and others as key hub proteins (Fig. 4B). The EPC, which identifies nodes that are critical for maintaining the network's connectivity when edges are randomly removed, also showed the highest ranking for SNO-TPI1, SNO-LDHA, SNO-PKM, SNO-HSP90AB1, SNO-OGDH, and others (Fig. 4C). Betweenness centrality, which identifies proteins that bridge distinct network regions, exhibited SNO-TPI, SNO-LDHA, SNO-HSP90AA1, SNO-PKM, SNO-ACTR2, and others to have the highest scores among others (Fig. 4D). Lastly, the degree of centrality, which ranks nodes based on their number of direct interactions, highlighting highly connected proteins with potential regulatory roles, showed that proteins with the highest number of connections include SNO-CAPZB, SNO-PRDX2, SNO-TPI1, SNO-LDHA, SNO-PKM, SNO-HSP90AB1, SNO-OGDH, and others (Fig. 4E). Interestingly, the SNO-proteins identified across all five centrality algorithms are mostly part of the metabolism-related red-cluster #3 shown in Fig. 3A and D. This suggests that pathological shifts in S-nitrosylation in the Cntnap2−/− cortex preferentially target central metabolic hubs, which may influence their network connectivity as key regulators integrating signaling pathways with metabolic functions.

SNO-modified proteins have been frequently linked to neurological disorders. To further explore this connection, we conducted a Gene-Disease Association analysis. Applying this analysis to our differentially SNO-metabolism proteins dataset, we identified strong correlations with major neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders, including Autistic disorder, Mood disorders, anxiety, Schizophrenia, Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, and Mental depression (Fig. 5D). These findings may suggest that dysregulated SNO modifications may contribute to metabolic-synaptic crosstalk dysfunction, potentially driving anxiety phenotypes in the Cntnap2−/− mouse model of autism.

Biochemical validation of the SNOTRAP method and functional assessment of nitric oxide signaling modulation in Cntnap2−/− mice

We next performed biochemical validation of our mass spectrometry findings to confirm reproducible changes in S-nitrosylated proteins and to examine the functional consequences of ·NO signaling modulation using the nNOS inhibitor 7-nitroindazole (7-NI) and the ·NO donor, SNAP. To capture both molecular and functional outcomes of altered ·NO signaling, we focused on two readouts: (i) Microtubule-associated protein 1 B (MAP1B), a cytoskeletal protein identified by MS as selectively S-nitrosylated in the KO cortex, and (ii) NADH/NAD+ metabolic profiling to assess metabolic stress and redox capacity under conditions of altered ·NO.

MAP1B was selected for biochemical validation, as it was the only ASD-associated protein that exhibited increased S-nitrosylation in the KO cortex according to the MS analysis. Immunofluorescence imaging and analysis confirmed the increased SNO-MAP1B in the KO cortical neurons, while total MAP1B fluorescence intensity remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 2A), indicating regulation at the post-translational rather than expression level. Pharmacological inhibition of nNOS with 7-NI prevented the increase in MAP1B S-nitrosylation in Cntnap2−/− neurons (Fig. 6B and C). These findings were further validated by Western blot analysis, which confirmed an increase in SNO-MAP1B levels in the KO cortex compared with WT, while treatment with 7-NI significantly reduced SNO-MAP1B without altering total MAP1B protein levels (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. 2B and C). Conversely, treatment of wild-type (WT) cortical neurons with the NO donor SNAP induced S-nitrosylation of MAP1B, confirming that MAP1B is directly responsive to ·NO signaling (Fig. 6E and F). MAP1B is known to regulate microtubule dynamics during axon formation, and previous reports indicate that SNO can promote axonal retraction [39]. To investigate the functional consequence of MAP1B S-nitrosylation, we quantified axon length in wild-type (WT) and Cntnap2−/− (KO) cortical neurons at day in vitro 4 (DIV4). KO neurons exhibited pronounced axonal shortening and retraction, which were recovered upon inhibition of nNOS (Fig. 6G and H). This effect may be driven by the exclusive S-nitrosylation of MAP1B at Cys1305, as detected by our MS analysis (Fig. 2D). Further studies using site-directed mutagenesis will be required to directly test the causal role of this previously uncharacterized modification site.

Our analysis revealed significant enrichment of several metabolic pathways influenced by S-nitrosylation in the Cntnap2−/− brain. To assess how ·NO modulation affects cellular redox balance and metabolic stress globally, we measured NAD+ and NADH levels and their ratio in brain samples from WT, KO, and KO+7NI. The NAD+/NADH ratio is a well-established indicator of cellular redox state and metabolic activity, reflecting the functional status of key pathways, including glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation [40]. Analysis of cortical lysates revealed a significant increase in the NADH/NAD+ ratio in KO mice compared to WT controls, indicating a shift toward a reduced redox state. This increase was significantly attenuated following nNOS inhibition with 7-NI, restoring the NADH/NAD+ ratio toward WT levels (Fig. 6I and J). Integrating these findings with the bioinformatics identification of SNO-targeted metabolic hubs suggests ·NO/S-nitrosylation as a key driver of the redox and metabolic dysregulation in the Cntnap2−/− brain.

Discussion

Nitric oxide (·NO) plays essential roles in neuronal development, redox regulation, and synaptic plasticity; however, its role in ASD has not been well explored. We showed that elevated ·NO in the Cntnap2−/− cortex is causally linked to ASD-related phenotypes, as nNOS inhibition rescued synaptic and behavioral deficits, whereas ·NO induction in WT mice using SNAP was sufficient to reproduce these abnormalities [8]. CNTNAP2 deficiency may alter neuronal activity and synaptic homeostasis [41]. Given that ·NO synthesis is tightly coupled to neuronal activity and calcium signaling [42], disrupted circuit function in Cntnap2 knockout mice may contribute to the altered ·NO/S-nitrosylation phenotypes observed. We therefore investigated whether dysregulated S-nitrosylation underlies the ·NO-dependent pathology in the Cntnap2−/− brain. To address this, we applied a refined SNOTRAP-based MS approach that substitutes the bulky SNOTRAP tag with N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) to detect SNO sites with enhanced precision directly [[19], [20], [21]]. By combining this approach with advanced bioinformatics, we identified dysregulated pathways in the Cntnap2−/− cortex spanning metabolic, neuronal, cytoskeletal processes, and others.

Importantly, our analysis also identified several differentially S-nitrosylated proteins encoded by ASD according to the SFARI database [36], underscoring the translational relevance of these findings. Six of these ASD candidate proteins are located in the synapse; these include SNO-STXBP1, SNO-MAP1B, SNO-NPTN, SNO-YWHAE, SNO-FABP5, and SNO-PPP3CA. S-nitrosylation is involved in various important functions at the synapse [8,9,43,44], including promoting synaptic plasticity [43,45], maintaining synaptic stability [8,9,43], and supporting the process of synaptic pruning [8,9,43]. These findings suggest that synaptic deficits underlying the core ASD-phenotypes in Cntnap2−/− mice may be partially driven by altered S-nitrosylation of synaptic proteins.

Notably, MAP1B was the only ASD-related protein showing increased S-nitrosylation in the Cntnap2−/− cortex. MAP1B, a member of the microtubule-binding protein family, is crucial for axonal growth and synapse maturation during brain development [46,47]. It is predominantly expressed in the neuronal soma, dendrites, and axons, with a developmental enrichment in the axonal growth cone [47,48]. De novo variants in the MAP1B gene have been identified in individuals clinically diagnosed with ASD [49]. CNTNAP2 is a cell-adhesion molecule that plays critical roles in neuronal migration, axonal organization, and synaptic stabilization during brain development [7,[50], [51], [52]]. Disruption of CNTNAP2 has been shown to alter neuronal connectivity and axonal architecture [51], processes that depend heavily on microtubule-associated proteins. Thus, dysregulation of MAP1B through aberrant S-nitrosylation may provide a mechanistic link between CNTNAP2 deficiency and the structural neuronal abnormalities observed in this model.

MAP1B undergoes proteolytic processing to produce a heavy chain (HC) comprising the first 2200 amino acids, and a light chain (LC1) which corresponds to the C-terminal 250 amino acids. Both subunits assemble into a functional complex MAP1B-HC/LC1 [39,53], where HC is the regulatory subunit, and the LC1 is the active one [46,54]. nNOS interacts physically with LC1, but not HC [53]. Activation of nNOS induces S-nitrosylation of MAP1B at Cys2457 on LC1, triggering a conformational change of LC1 that enhances microtubule binding of the HC/LC1 complex [53]. This modification inhibits the motor function of dynein, resulting in reduced extension forces and ultimately axonal retraction [39,53]. Additionally, in MAP1B-deficient dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, ·NO-induced axon retraction was abolished, indicating that nNOS exerts its effects on the axonal cytoskeleton, at least in part, through S-nitrosylation of MAP1B [39].

Here, we identified an additional S-nitrosylation site on MAP1B at Cys1305, which resides within the heavy chain. Notably, S-nitrosylation of MAP1B was detected exclusively in the Cntnap2−/− cortex and was absent in wild-type mice, highlighting its potential involvement in specific pathological conditions (Fig. 2D). Cntnap2−/− cultured neurons exhibited significantly reduced axon length compared to wild-type controls, suggesting an enhanced axonal retraction or impaired extension. Pharmacological inhibition of nNOS with 7-NI restored axon length to levels comparable to wild-type neurons (Fig. 6E and F). These structural effects were accompanied by increased SNO-MAP1B, which was similarly normalized upon nNOS inhibition (Fig. 6B and F). These findings support a mechanistic link between MAP1B S-nitrosylation and axonal retraction, positioning dysregulated ·NO/MAP1B signaling as a mechanistic contributor to axonal pathology in the Cntnap2−/− model. Future studies (e.g., site-directed mutagenesis of C1305) could determine whether S-nitrosylation of this residue in the MAP1B heavy chain alters the HC-LC1 complex conformation, localization, and function in Cntnap2−/− neurons. Additionally, electrophysiological analyses will clarify how MAP1B S-nitrosylation-driven cytoskeletal changes affect neuronal excitability, synaptic transmission, and network connectivity.

Another key finding of this study is the identification of S-nitrosylated proteins involved in metabolic pathways, highlighting metabolism as a major target of ·NO signaling in the Cntnap2−/− cortex (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). The brain demands approximately 20% of the body's daily energy supply [55,56]. Glucose is the primary brain fuel, metabolized through glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation to produce ATP [56]. Synapses represent primary sites of ATP consumption, tightly coupling neuronal metabolism to synaptic function through energy-demanding processes such as neurotransmitter release and synaptic plasticity [57,58]. During stress and higher cognitive functions, cerebral energy requirements further increase, as reflected by elevated glucose oxidation and oxygen consumption [59]. Consequently, efficient mitochondrial respiration is essential to sustain brain activity, and even subtle perturbations in metabolic processes can profoundly impair neural function, increasing vulnerability to brain disorders [60].

S-nitrosylation has emerged as a direct regulatory mechanism by which ·NO affects cellular metabolism [61]. This regulation occurs through S-nitrosylation of core metabolic enzymes, key components of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), and proteins involved in regulating mitochondrial dynamics. Consequently, S-nitrosylation directly impairs mitochondrial function and bioenergetic homeostasis [[62], [63], [64]]. The role of mitochondrial dysfunctions in ASD is reviewed in [65]. S-nitrosylation also targets enzymes of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Six of the eight TCA cycle enzymes, including aconitase, citrate synthase, succinyl-CoA synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and succinate dehydrogenase, have been identified as S-nitrosylation substrates [62]. In neurodegenerative conditions, aberrant S-nitrosylation of TCA cycle enzymes has been observed, contributing to metabolic dysfunction and synaptic loss [66,67]. Isocitrate dehydrogenase 3 (IDH3) is a mitochondrial NAD+-dependent enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of isocitrate to α-ketoglutarate, a key regulatory step of the TCA cycle. Recent studies using hiPSC models of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease with Lewy body dementia (PD/LBD) revealed inhibition of TCA cycle flux at the IDH step, which was partially reversed by the nitric oxide synthase inhibitor l-NAME, implicating nitrosative stress as a causal mechanism [[68], [69], [70]]. Consistently, S-nitrosoproteomic analyses of human AD brain tissue identified elevated S-nitrosylation of IDH3A at Cys331, linking this modification to impaired mitochondrial metabolism [70]. In line with these findings, our SNOTRAP analysis revealed increased S-nitrosylation of IDH3A in the Cntnap2−/− cortex at Cys351 and Cys359 (Fig. 5C), suggesting that aberrant S-nitrosylation of IDH3A may similarly impair its enzymatic activity and contribute to TCA cycle dysfunction and metabolic stress in our model.

α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (αKGDH/OGDH) has been identified as an S-nitrosylated protein in human postmortem brains from Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease [66,67]. In these conditions, S-nitrosylation of the OGDH/E1 subunit is increased at multiple cysteine residues, including Cys487, which lies within the TPP-binding domain and has been implicated in enzymatic inhibition [70]. In contrast, our SNOTRAP analysis revealed a significant reduction in S-nitrosylation at OGDH/E1 Cys487 in the Cntnap2−/− cortex (Fig. 5C). This decrease may reflect disease-specific remodeling of nitrosative signaling or a compensatory adaptation aimed at preserving TCA cycle flux under metabolic stress, a possibility that will require direct functional assessment in future studies.

S-nitrosylation can also regulate the function of some enzymes involved in glycolysis [66,67,71]. NOS1-induced S-nitrosylation of phosphofructokinase (PFKM), a central rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis, has been shown to promote metabolic reprogramming. PFKM S-nitrosation at Cys351 stabilizes the tetramer of PFK1 and increases its activity [72]. In our dataset, S-nitrosylation of PFKM at Cys114 was not detected in the Cntnap2−/− cortex. The functional relevance of this site is unknown; however, a similar reduction at this site was previously reported in SH-SY5Y neuron-like cells exposed to short-term nitrosative stress induced by the NO donor SNAP [73]. These findings raise the possibility that protein de-nitrosylation pathways are activated under nitrosative stress, leading to the removal of SNO from regulatory cysteine residues.

Some of the metabolism-related SNO proteins identified in this study localize to synapses and are linked to anxiety-related phenotypes (Fig. 5E and F), supporting a functional interplay between S-nitrosylation, metabolism, and synaptic signaling in ASD. This is consistent with the enrichment of mitochondria at pre- and post-synaptic compartments, where they provide ATP and buffer calcium to sustain high-frequency neurotransmission and plasticity [70,74]. The identification of three SNO proteins: SNO-ATP1A2, SNO-GLUL, and SNO-ATP6V1B2, shared across metabolic, synaptic, and anxiety-related categories (Fig. 5E and F) provides a molecular basis for understanding how disturbed SNO may translate into behavioral phenotypes. While such outcomes could in principle reflect the data-driven nature of the ontology-based analyses, our previous experimental findings support a biological link between nitric oxide signaling and anxiety-related behaviors in this model [8]. Specifically, inhibition of nNOS normalized anxiety-like behaviors in Cntnap2−/− mice, whereas pharmacological induction of ·NO signaling in WT mice using SNAP was sufficient to reproduce similar behavioral alterations [8]. In addition, nNOS inhibition improved sociability and repetitive behaviors in this model [8], indicating that dysregulated ·NO signaling may also influence core ASD-related domains [8] These bidirectional behavioral effects suggest that dysregulated ·NO signaling contributes functionally to ASD phenotypes rather than representing a purely informatics-driven association. Moreover, nitric oxide signaling is known to modulate cortico-limbic circuits involved in emotional regulation, social behavior, and cognitive control [75,76], because these circuits substantially overlap with networks implicated in ASD, dysregulated ·NO/S-nitrosylation signaling in the Cntnap2−/− cortex may influence behavioral outcomes through modulation of these cortico-limbic pathways. The Na+/K+-ATPase α2 isoform (ATP1A2) is predominantly expressed in astrocytes, where it maintains Na+ and K+ gradients essential for neuronal excitability, neurotransmitter uptake, and Ca2+ signaling, and its dysfunction has been linked to anxiety [77,78]. Although ATP1A2 has not previously been characterized as a target of S-nitrosylation, we observed exclusive S-nitrosylation at Cys457 in the Cntnap2−/− cortex but not in WT, suggesting ·NO-dependent regulation of this pump under pathological conditions. In parallel, we identified altered S-nitrosylation of the V-type H+-ATPase subunit B2 (ATP6V1B2), a proton pump required for synaptic vesicle neurotransmitter loading and previously implicated in anxiety [79,80]. While the functional consequences of SNO-ATP6V1B2 remain unclear, the observed decrease in S-nitrosylation at Cys425 in the Cntnap2−/− cortex may potentially impair synaptic efficacy.

Glutamine synthetase (GS; GLUL) is expressed predominantly in astrocytes, where it maintains glutamatergic neurotransmission by sustaining the glutamate-glutamine cycle. Disruption of glutamine homeostasis impairs excitatory signaling and has been linked to anxiety and depression [81]. A previous study showed that ·NO inhibits GS activity through reversible covalent modifications, including nitrosylation and nitration, which were restored upon nNOS inhibition [82]. Here, our SNOTRAP analysis revealed increased S-nitrosylation of GS at Cys99 in the Cntnap2−/− cortex (Fig. 5C), suggesting that S-nitrosylation at this site may represent an inhibitory modification that could influence astrocyte-mediated glutamine supply to neurons, thereby contributing to altered glutamatergic signaling and anxiety-related phenotypes. However, direct functional testing will be required to determine its specific impact. Collectively, these findings raise the possibility that altered NO·/SNO signaling in Cntnap2−/− mice drives a maladaptive metabolic–synaptic crosstalk that contributes to behavioral abnormalities. This framework further motivates future studies employing site-directed mutagenesis of specific cysteine residues to assess the functional impact of S-nitrosylation on these proteins directly and to determine whether such manipulations can rescue anxiety-related phenotypes in vivo. The prominent enrichment of metabolic pathways among S-nitrosylated proteins suggested that ·NO-dependent signaling might extend beyond individual targets to influence global metabolic homeostasis. We therefore examined the NADH/NAD+ ratio as a biochemical readout of cellular metabolic state (Fig. 6G and H). NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is a central coenzyme that regulates cellular energy metabolism across several metabolic pathways, including the TCA cycle, glycolysis, and fatty acid oxidation [40]. Within cells, NAD exists in two forms: the oxidized NAD+ and the reduced NADH. The NAD+/NADH ratio is central to mitochondrial metabolism, where NAD+ is reduced to NADH in the TCA cycle and subsequently oxidized to NAD+ in the ETC for ATP generation [40,83]. An imbalance in this ratio can impair flux through these pathways' reactions, resulting in dysregulated cellular metabolism, which is associated with metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative disorders, and psychiatric disorders [40]. Depletion of the NAD+ pool is associated with impaired synaptic plasticity, as it plays a crucial role in maintaining the structural and functional plasticity of neuronal circuits [84]. Increasing evidence has shown the efficacy of boosting NAD+ levels using NAD+ precursors in various diseases, including ASD and AD [[84], [85], [86]]. Here, we found an elevated NADH/NAD + ratio in the Cntnap2−/− cortex, which indicates a shift toward a more reduced state, often associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic stress [40,87]. This shift in NADH/NAD+ ratio was corrected upon nNOS inhibition with 7-NI (Fig. 6H), suggesting ·NO as a driver of this redox imbalance. This aligns with our bioinformatics identification of SNO-altered metabolic hubs (glycolysis, TCA cycle), where pathological SNO patterns at key cysteines of metabolic enzymes likely impair NAD+ regeneration. Importantly, these findings reflect tissue-level metabolic alterations measured in cortical lysates and do not resolve cell-autonomous mechanisms; future in vitro studies will be required to test whether these NADH/NAD+ changes and their rescue can be recapitulated at the cellular level.

In conclusion, these findings position dysregulated S-nitrosylation as a potential mechanism through which ·NO contributes to synaptic and behavioral abnormalities in the Cntnap2−/− model. We showed that Cntnap2 deficiency leads to aberrant S-nitrosylation signaling that converges on metabolic enzymes, synaptic proteins, and cytoskeletal regulators. This altered SNO landscape disrupts energy metabolism, redox equilibrium, and axonal architecture, providing a molecular basis for synaptic dysfunction and anxiety-like phenotypes observed in this model. Remodeling of the S-nitrosoproteome has also been reported in other ASD models. For example, Shank3 mutant mice exhibit widespread changes in S-nitrosylated proteins affecting synaptic, mitochondrial, and metabolic pathways [23,88]. These findings suggest that dysregulated ·NO/S-nitrosylation signaling could represent a convergent mechanism across different ASD models. An important implication of this work is that targeted modulation of ·NO/SNO signaling, whether via SNO-site-specific interventions or strategies aimed at restoring redox balance, may offer a strategy to correct metabolic and structural abnormalities in ASD-related contexts. Given that several identified targets (e.g., ATP1A2, GLUL) are enriched in astrocytes, while others are predominantly neuronal, these alterations likely reflect dysregulation across both neuronal and glial compartments, consistent with the emerging role of neuron–glia metabolic coupling in ASD-related circuit dysfunction [89]. Accordingly, future studies should extend these observations by dissecting cell-type-specific SNO signatures (e.g., sorted populations or conditional models). In addition, age- and sex-dependent analyses across developmental stages will be necessary to determine whether ·NO signaling alterations represent an early developmental driver or a later pathological consequence in a sex-dependent manner. Lastly, this work underscores the utility of SNOTRAP as a robust platform for resolving cysteine-specific S-nitrosylation changes in the brain, which enables a system-level understanding of molecular alterations in disease states and may facilitate the identification of new therapeutic targets. The findings highlight S-nitrosylation as a convergent regulatory mechanism acting across multiple metabolic and neuronal domains, ultimately impacting synaptic function and ASD-related phenotypes.
