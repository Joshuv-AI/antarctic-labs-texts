Lipid Metabolic Disorders: a Pivotal Driver in Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus Pathogenesis
Authors: Junqi Wu, Huabin Wang, Miao Fu, Meili Zhang, Guigfang Zhao, Beiwei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewThis review aims to synthesize recent evidence to clarify the causal and mechanistic roles of lipid metabolism disorders as core drivers of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and to summarize emerging therapeutic advances targeting key nodes within pathophysiological cascade, thereby providing an integrated, translational framework for the precision management of T2DM.Recent FindingsNormoglycemic obese individuals with insulin resistance (IR) frequently exhibit elevated plasma free fatty acids (FFAs), triglycerides (TGs), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), alongside reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). These observations indicate that dyslipidemia is not merely a comorbidity but an important early driver of T2DM progression. Lipid metabolism disorders initiate a unifying pathological cascade: ectopic lipid spillover induces systemic IR in the liver and skeletal muscle; lipotoxic stress directly compromises pancreatic β-cell function; and progressive disruption of inter-organ axes, including the gut–adipose–liver axis and brain–periphery axis, further exacerbates systemic metabolic deterioration. Recent evidence supports emerging therapeutic strategies, including traditional medicine-based approaches and interventions targeting lipotoxicity and the gut–brain–liver–fat axis to restore metabolic health.SummaryLipid metabolism disorders are not merely complications of T2DM, but critical drivers of disease progression. Adipose dysfunction acts as the initiating event, triggering a pathological cascade that induces systemic IR, impairs pancreatic β-cell function via lipotoxic intermediates, and disrupts inter-organ communication networks. Targeted interventions along this axis therefore represent a promising strategy for precision therapy.

Introduction

Diabetes has emerged as a major global public health challenge. According to the 11th Edition of the Diabetes Atlas (2024) released by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), the global adult population living with diabetes has risen to approximately 589 million, with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) accounting for over 90% of all diagnoses [1–3]. Epidemiological investigations confirm that T2DM onset is shifting toward younger age groups, a trend closely correlated with escalating obesity prevalence and evolving lifestyle patterns. T2DM is frequently accompanied by a cluster of metabolic comorbidities, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease [4, 5]. As the most prevalent form of diabetes worldwide, T2DM exerts a growing burden on public health, highlighting the need for intensified prevention and treatment strategies.

Current consensus defines T2DM as a heterogeneous metabolic disorders characterized by insulin resistance (IR) and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction as core pathological mechanisms [6]. Beyond simple hyperglycemia, T2DM represents a multisystem metabolic disturbance involving impaired homeostasis of carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins. Notably, normoglycemic obese individuals with IR frequently exhibit elevated plasma free fatty acids (FFAs), triglycerides (TGs), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), together with reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). These observations indicate that dyslipidemia is not a mere comorbidity but a critical early driver of T2DM progression [7].

Lipid metabolism is maintained through the dynamic equilibrium of lipid synthesis, catabolism, and inter-organ transport [8]. Adipose tissue, the primary energy reservoir, regulates this equilibrium via controlled TGs storage and fatty acids (FAs) release, while the liver—acting as the central metabolic hub—orchestrates fatty acid oxidation (FAO), de novo lipogenesis (DNL), and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) secretion. Caloric excess and obesity, driven by unhealthy lifestyles including poor dietary patterns, physical inactivity, current smoking, and heavy drinking, trigger lipid metabolism imbalances that disrupt systemic energy homeostasis [9]. Lipid metabolism disorders drive the pathogenesis of T2DM via a pathogenic triad: amplified inflammation and dysfunction of adipose tissue, the accumulation of lipotoxic intermediates and multi-organ signaling imbalance. These processes concertedly impair insulin signaling, β-cell function, and inter-organ crosstalk, supporting this axis as the key upstream driver of T2DM progression.

Centered on this core axis, this review systematically dissects the regulatory roles of lipid metabolic disorders in IR, β-cell dysfunction, and inter-organ crosstalk, integrates the latest research advances, and identifies potential therapeutic targets, providing a solid scientific basis for precise prevention and management of T2DM.

Lipid Metabolism in Adipose Tissue

The core balancing mechanisms of lipid metabolism in healthy individuals are shown in Fig. 1. Under states of increased energy demand (e.g., starvation or exercise), hormones such as catecholamines activate lipolytic enzymes in adipose tissue. These enzymes sequentially catalyze the hydrolysis of TGs and release FAs molecule. The liberated FAs enter the circulation as FFAs to supply energy via oxidation in peripheral tissues (e.g., muscle and liver) [10]. Concurrently, adipocytes can reuptake a subset of FFAs and re-esterify them with glycerol to form stored TG. This dynamic equilibrium between lipolysis and re-esterification enables adipose tissue to sensitively respond to fluctuations in energy status while maintaining stable long-term energy storage.

In the liver, ingested FFAs primarily enter mitochondria for β-oxidation, generating acetyl-CoA that subsequently enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to produce ATP [11]. This pathway is also critical for the clearance of excess lipids; impaired lipid metabolism drives hepatic lipid accumulation, thereby facilitating the development of MASLD and IR [12]. Additionally, acetyl-CoA can be derived from glucose via glycolysis and subsequent reactions, serving as a substrate for DNL [13]. Hepatically synthesized TG can either be stored as lipid droplets or assembled with cholesterol esters, phospholipids, and apolipoproteins into VLDL particles for secretion into the bloodstream. Following extensive TG hydrolysis by lipoprotein lipase (LPL) in the circulation, VLDL is converted into low-density lipoprotein (LDL), which transports cholesterol to peripheral tissues for utilization. Concurrently, nascent high-density lipoprotein (HDL) secreted by the liver and small intestine binds to free cholesterol released by peripheral tissues; upon maturation, HDL transports cholesterol back to the liver for metabolic clearance via the reverse cholesterol transport pathway [14].

Adipose Tissue Dysfunction and IR

Physiologically, insulin binds to the insulin receptor (INSR) on target cells, activating the receptor’s tyrosine kinase domain. This initiates a series of cascading reactions, including the phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) and the subsequent activation of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) signaling pathway [15]. One of the key metabolic functions of this pathway is the translocation of glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) vesicles to the plasma membrane [16]. Activated Akt promotes the translocation of GLUT4 from intracellular storage vesicles to the cell membrane, thereby mediating glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue to maintain systemic glucose homeostasis. Studies have shown that impaired transport of intracellular GSVs (GLUT4 storage vesicles) leads to reduced insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, which is associated with IR in muscle and adipose tissue [17]. The metabolites of chronic overnutrition, such as hyperglycaemia and non-esterified FAs (NEFAs), disrupt with the activation of the INSR and its downstream IRS-1/PI3K/Akt2 pathway. This dysfunction at the INSR/IRS-1/PI3K/Akt-GLUT4 level leads to weakened insulin signaling and reduced glucose uptake in peripheral tissues, which representing the primary molecular mechanism underlying IR [18].

Adipose Tissue Dysfunction and Inflammation

As the primary site of lipid storage and metabolism, adipose tissue dysfunction represents a key initiating event in lipid metabolism disorders and subsequent IR [19]. Research indicates that chronic caloric excess promotes lipid accumulation in adipose tissue, which expands via adipocyte hypertrophy and hyperplasia to accommodate increased lipid storage. Adipocyte size and the number of infiltrating macrophages is closely associated with IR and the severity of adipose tissue inflammation, and inflammation contributes to the initiation of IR [20]. Excessive adipocyte hypertrophy triggers adipocyte death, thereby recruiting macrophages to adipose tissue to clear cellular debris [21]. These infiltrating macrophages tend to polarize a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and secrete pro-inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). These factors subsequently activate stress kinases including c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and IκB kinase β (IKKβ), which can induce inhibitory serine phosphorylation of IRS-1 at Ser307 (human; Ser302 in rodents) and contribute to disrupted insulin signaling [22, 23]. In addition to soluble cytokines, polarized macrophages also release extracellular vesicles, including exosomes enriched in miR-155. These exosomes can enter neighboring adipocytes and exacerbate IR through post-transcriptional regulation [24]. Importantly, this inflammatory milieu also compromises the adaptive expandability of adipose tissue. Recent studies further indicate that macrophage-derived galectin-3 may exacerbates adipose tissue inflammation and impairs glucose tolerance by inhibiting adipocyte differentiation and maturation [25]. This creates a critical pathological bottleneck: on the one hand, undergo hypertrophic stress, which promotes chronic low-grade inflammation; on the other hand, adipose tissue fails to generate new, functional adipocytes to accommodate excess caloric load [26]. This impaired ability of adipose tissue to undergo adaptive expansion and lipid storage represents a key hallmark of adipose tissue dysfunction.

Ectopic Lipid Deposition

Under physiological conditions, lipids are preferentially stored in white adipose tissue (WAT). Under sustained metabolic overload, WAT exhibits impaired metabolic plasticity, which promotes depot-dependent efflux of FFAs into the circulation. FFAs released from intra-abdominal visceral adipose tissue (VAT) directly drain into the portal venous system and accumulate in the liver, whereas FFAs liberated from subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) enter the systemic circulation and predominantly accumulate in skeletal muscle, heart and other peripheral tissues [27, 28]. This differential lipid redistribution contributes to widespread ectopic lipid deposition, an early hallmark of lipotoxicity and a key contributor of IR in target organs [29]. Research confirms that elevated FFAs levels can specifically activate protein kinase C (PKC) subtypes, including PKC-δ/ε/β/θ, which mediate inhibitory serine phosphorylation of IRS-1 at the key residues (Ser-307/632/1101 in rodents; Ser312/636/1101 in humans) [30, 31]. This impairs normal tyrosine phosphorylation and downstream activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, ultimately reducing GLUT4 translocation and glucose uptake, thereby contributing to the development of IR [32, 33]. Liver-specific PKCε knockout mice fed a high-fat diet exhibited approximately 67% reduction in IR, whereas cellular studies confirmed that PKCθ activation is associated with impaired insulin signaling in skeletal muscle [34, 35].

Notably, multiple studies support a clear dissociation between TGs accumulation and IR. Intrahepatic and intramyocellular TG accumulation have been reported in the absence of IR, and hepatic IR has been observed independently of TGs accumulation [36, 37]. This is likely explained by the role of neutral TGs as a protective lipid buffer. When this buffering capacity is exceeded and unable to compensate for excessive FFAs uptake, the formation and buildup of bioactive lipotoxic intermediates contribute to IR [38]. Consistently, numerous preclinical and clinical studies have confirmed that accumulation of DAGs and ceramides impairs insulin signaling across multiple metabolically active tissues [39, 40]. These findings suggest that neutral TGs deposition is not a direct driver of IR, but rather a surrogate marker of underlying lipotoxic intermediate accumulation. Accumulation of lipotoxic intermediate impairs hepatic insulin signaling, weakening insulin’s ability to suppress hepatic glucose production (HGP)-a major contributor to fasting hyperglycemia in T2DM. Dysfunctional livers continuously secrete triglyceride-rich VLDL, exacerbating systemic lipid metabolism disorders. Concurrently, excess circulating FFAs released from SAT that infiltrate skeletal muscle can trigger tissue inflammation and metabolic dysfunction, further propagating systemic IR [41].

In agreement, studies have shown that increased intramyocellular triglyceride (IMTG) is frequently associated with IR [42]. However, as observed in the liver, this association reflects lipotoxic intermediate accumulation rather than IMTG itself. Specifically, intermuscular and intramyocellular fat (IMCF) infiltration, together with lipotoxic intermediate buildup, markedly reduces insulin-stimulated glucose uptake via PKCθ activation, identifying this pathway as a major contributor to systemic IR [43]. Furthermore, under sustained lipid overload, pancreatic ectopic lipid deposition may develop [44]. Clinical studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated that high intrapancreatic fat deposition (IPFD), or fatty pancreas disease (FPD), is closely associated with IR and T2DM [45, 46].

Dysregulation of Adipokine Secretion

Adipose tissue functions as a key endocrine organ, secreting a variety of adipokines (e.g., leptin, adiponectin) that exert important regulatory effects on systemic metabolism and insulin sensitivity [47].

Obesity is consistently associated with hyperleptinemia, characterized by a marked elevation in circulating leptin levels that correlates with fat mass. Leptin, one of the first adipokines, is produced according to systemic nutritional status and acts primarily on the hypothalamus to regulate appetite and energy homeostasis [48]. Chronic hyperleptinemia contributes to the development of leptin resistance, which is manifested by reduced or impaired leptin receptor signaling in the hypothalamus and peripheral tissues (e.g., liver, skeletal muscle). This attenuate leptin’s core physiological functions—suppressing appetite, enhancing energy expenditure, and improving peripheral insulin sensitivity—thereby exacerbating positive energy balance and metabolic dysfunction [49, 50].

Concurrently, states of obesity and IR are frequently associated with reduced circulating adiponectin levels. Adiponectin, the most abundant adipokine secreted by adipose tissue, is recognized as one of the most potent insulin-sensitizing adipokine to date. It promotes FAO and glucose utilization, suppresses FA synthesis, and exerts anti-inflammatory effects [51, 52]. Adiponectin deficiency reduces insulin sensitivity and is closely linked to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Hypoadiponectinemia confers an increased risk of T2DM and cardiovascular diseases [53].

Studies have demonstrated that adiponectin primarily exerts its effects via adiponectin receptor 1 (AdipoR1) and AdipoR2, which activate the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) α signaling pathways. These pathways promote FA oxidation and glucose uptake in skeletal muscle while inhibiting hepatic gluconeogenesis [54]. Thus, strategies to increase adiponectin levels or enhance its signaling activity may represent promising therapeutic strategy for T2DM. A recent study has shown that targeting central adiponectin signaling with the AdipoR1 activator (AdipoRon), an oral adiponectin receptor agonist, exerts beneficial effects against diabetes-associated cognitive impairment (DACI), which is a significant neurological complication of T2DM. This finding further supports the therapeutic potential of adiponectin-related pathways [55].

Overall, adipose tissue dysfunction and inflammation represent key events of IR, contributing to abnormal accumulation of lipotoxic intermediates, ectopic lipid deposition in non-adipose tissues, and dysregulated adipokine secretion, while these pathological changes collectively form the multidimensional pathogenic network depicted in Fig. 2 and act synergistically to induce the development and progression of IR.

Lipotoxicity and Pancreatic β-Cell Dysfunction

Oxidative Stress

Although pancreatic β-cells primarily rely on glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS), they also highly express FA transporters (e.g., cluster of differentiation 36 [CD36], fatty acid transporter 1–6 [FATP1-6]) and exhibit marked sensitivity to circulating FFAs [56, 57]. In high-FFAs environments, β-cells excessively take up FAs, which overloads mitochondrial oxidative capacity and leads to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Accumulated ROS can damage mitochondrial protein complexes, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), and lipid bilayer structures, thereby impairing oxidative phosphorylation efficiency and reducing ATP production [58]. Furthermore, elevated ROS can activate the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, suppressing the expression of key transcription factors (e.g., pancreatic and duodenal homeobox 1 [PDX1], Musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma oncogene homolog A [MAFA]) and disrupting insulin gene transcription, mRNA stability, and protein synthesis [59, 60]. Persistent oxidative stress can ultimately overwhelm antioxidant defense systems (e.g., nuclear factor E2 related factor 2 [Nrf2]/antioxidant response element [ARE] pathway), thereby exacerbating cellular damage [61]. In addition, as a well-documented upstream trigger, oxidative stress promotes the activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, which contributes to β-cell injury [62]. The study has shown that genetic ablation of NLRP3 in mice confers significant protection against oxidative stress-mediated β-cell apoptosis and preserves islet insulin secretory function, as evidenced by improved glucose homeostasis in diabetic models [63].

Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress

Pancreatic β-cells possess a highly developed endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to precisely regulate insulin synthesis and secretion, adapting to metabolic demands [64, 65]. When misfolded or unfolded proteins accumulate in the ER lumen, this accumulation leads to a pathological state known as endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS). In response to this condition, β cells activate an adaptive mechanism, the unfolded protein response (UPR) which is often used interchangeably with the term “ER stress response”, to restore ER homeostasis [66, 67]. The UPR coordinates three interrelated adaptive processes: attenuating protein translation and degrading ER‑localized mRNA to reduce ER protein load; upregulating lipid synthesis and molecular chaperones to enhance folding capacity; and activating ER‑associated degradation (ERAD) and ER‑phagy to eliminate misfolded proteins [68]. During mild or transient stress, the UPR promotes proper protein folding and export; however, severe or prolonged stress can shift the UPR toward pro-apoptotic signaling [69].

Accumulating evidence indicates that elevated levels of IR and circulating FFAs—particularly palmitate—in T2DM contribute to β-cell ERS through pathways including inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α)/X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1), protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK)/stimulates eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 subunit-α (eIF2α), activating transcription factor-6 (ATF6), and thyroid adenoma associated gene (THADA) [70]. Specifically, the endonuclease activity of IRE1α can non-specifically degrade mRNA near the ER membrane (including INS mRNA), thereby inhibiting insulin synthesis [71]. Activation of the PERK/eIF2α pathway suppresses global protein translation, reduces proinsulin synthesis, and induces the expression of pro-apoptotic transcription factors ATF4 and C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) [72, 73]. In vitro studies have confirmed that palmitic acid strongly activates both PERK and IRE1 branches and promotes apoptosis, whereas oleic acid induces a milder UPR response with lower cytotoxicity [74]. Studies in mouse models have shown that lipotoxicity upregulates THADA expression in islets, reduces INS-1/2 transcription, and impairs insulin secretion. THADA also contributes to β-cell apoptosis by activating the DR5/FADD/caspase-8 pathway under ERS [75].

Ceramide Accumulation Activates Death Signaling

Ceramides, particularly long-chain saturated species (e.g., C16:0 and C18:0 ceramide), are widely recognized as key executors of lipotoxicity in β-cells, with their levels increased via DNL under high FFAs conditions [76]. Substantial evidence indicates that ceramides contribute to pancreatic β-cell function through the following mechanisms.

At the transcriptional level, ceramides inhibit the nuclear translocation of key transcription factors such as PDX-1 and MAF BZIP transcription factor A (MAFA) and their DNA binding to the insulin promoter, thereby attenuating insulin gene expression [77]. In terms of apoptosis regulation, ceramides contribute to β-cell death through multiple pathways: (1) Promoting the recruitment and oligomerization of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax at mitochondria, increasing outer mitochondrial membrane permeability and facilitating cytochrome c release [78]. (2) Enhancing ROS production by suppressing mitochondrial complex III or activating reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase [79, 80]. (3) Inducing pore formation in the mitochondrial membrane to increase permeability to cytochrome c [81].

Beyond animal models, large‑scale prospective human studies support the pathological role of ceramides in T2DM. A six‑year cohort study including 1,974 Chinese adults demonstrated that elevated levels of specific ceramide species (e.g., d18:1/20:1) independently predict incident T2DM. Mediation analysis further confirmed that β-cell dysfunction (assessed by HOMA-B) accounted for 11%–42% of these adverse associations, supporting ceramides as important mediators of human β-cell failure [82].

Adipose tissue dysfunction and inflammation lead to excessive lipolysis and release of FFAs and lipotoxic intermediates such as ceramides into the circulation. These toxic lipids specifically target pancreatic β-cells, inducing oxidative stress, ERS, and activation of pro-death signaling pathways, which collectively impair insulin synthesis and secretion in β-cells and even trigger their dedifferentiation or apoptosis. Lipid Metabolism Disorders and Multi-organ Crosstalk

Gut-Adipose-Liver Axis

In recent years, the gut has emerged as a key regulator of systemic metabolism. Gut microbiota and their metabolites exert important regulatory effects on whole-body lipid metabolism and energy balance by directly modulating Bile acids (BAs) metabolism, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) production, and intestinal barrier integrity [3, 83]. This regulatory network is illustrated in Fig. 4.

BAs, derived from cholesterol, serve essential roles in nutrient absorption but also serve as key signaling molecules in metabolic and inflammatory regulation [84]. In the liver, cholesterol is converted into primary BAs, such as cholic acids (CAs) and chenodeoxycholic acids (CDCAs), which are subsequently conjugated with glycine or taurine [85]. In the intestine, BAs exert their regulatory functions by activating multiple receptors, including G protein-coupled receptors (e.g., the Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5)/G protein-coupled bile acid receptor 1 (GPBAR1) and nuclear receptors (e.g., farnesoid X receptor [FXR], liver X receptor [LXR]) [86]. TGR5 primarily mediates rapid non-genomic signaling effects, which contributes to enhanced energy expenditure, improving insulin sensitivity, and regulating glucose and lipid metabolism [87]. The beneficial effects of BAs are largely attributed to GPBAR1 and Gs signaling pathways, including protection against diabetes, promotion of WAT browning, and alleviation of hepatic steatosis [86].

Notably, FXR, the primary endogenous receptor for CDCAs, may exerts detrimental effects on glucose homeostasis and energy expenditure in the context of obesity [88]. Obese mice with global FXR deficiency exhibit reduced body weight, improved glycemic control, and enhanced insulin sensitivity [89]. Furthermore, chronic administration of the synthetic FXR agonist GW4064 exacerbates weight gain, dyslipidemia, and glucose intolerance in obese, insulin-resistant mice [90]. These data collectively suggest that targeted inhibition of intestinal FXR signaling represents a promising therapeutic strategy for hyperglycemia. However, inconsistent results have been reported regarding intestinal FXR modulation. While most studies support the metabolic benefits of intestinal FXR inhibition, the intestine-specific agonist fexaramine reduces weight gain and improves IR in obese mice [91]. Additional mechanisms of intestinal FXR signaling and its outcomes merit further studies.

SCFAs (e.g., acetate, propionate, butyrate), generated via microbial fermentation of dietary fiber, exert beneficial anti-inflammatory effects, enhance insulin sensitivity, and modulate whole-body energy expenditure [92]. For example, propionate upregulates intestinal free fatty acid receptor 2 (FFAR2), thereby inhibiting hepatic phosphoenolpyruvate carboxy kinase (PEPCK) expression and suppressing gluconeogenesis [84]. Butyrate may improve insulin sensitivity by approximately 40% through the inhibition of histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity [93]. Furthermore, reduced SCFAs availability synergizes with lipid stress to compromise intestinal mucosal barrier integrity, facilitating lipopolysaccharide (LPS) translocation and subsequent activation of the toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling cascade. This mechanism is supported by preclinical evidence demonstrating that HFD-induced depletion of Bifidobacterium exacerbates IR partially via endotoxemia [94, 95].

Brain-Peripheral Axis

Recent human clinical studies have shown that biliverdin reductase A (BVR-A) levels are significantly reduced in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from obese individuals, correlating with early insulin signaling defects, visceral adipose inflammation, and metabolic syndrome [96]. BVR-A, a key heme degradation reductase and serine/threonine/tyrosine kinase that modulates insulin signaling, has emerged as a potential mediator of the brain-periphery axis in T2DM [97]. In obese and diabetic rodent models, BVR-A downregulation impairs insulin signaling and mitochondrial function, which contribute to brain IR (BIR) and cognitive decline [98]. Dysregulated lipid metabolism also represents a key early pathogenic mechanism in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), potentially explaining the pathological link through which T2DM accelerates its progression.

Beyond circulating mediators, the hypothalamus functions as the primary lipid-sensing hub that detects and responds to lipid overload in the brain–peripheral axis. Elevated circulating FFAs reach hypothalamic sensing circuits by activating cell-surface lipid sensors such as GPR120 expressed on the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and adjacent neurons, thereby modulating peripheral metabolism via the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) [99]. Under lipotoxic conditions, compromised BBB integrity and disrupted central leptin/insulin feedback lead to aberrant activation of neuropeptide Y (NPY)/agouti-related peptide (AgRP) neurons in the arcuate nucleus (ARC) [100]. Sustained NPY/AgRP signaling antagonizes melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R) activity, promoting hyperphagia and reduced energy expenditure, and altering sympathetic outflow to peripheral tissues [101].

In the short term, SNS activation mobilizes WAT lipolysis to supply FFAs as oxidative fuel, transiently diverting lipid away from hepatic storage overload. However, chronic lipid-induced hypothalamic dysregulation triggers persistent NPY/AgRP overactivity, which drives uncontrolled lipolysis and chronic FFAs oversupply to the liver, exacerbating ectopic lipid deposition and systemic IR [102, 103]. Therapeutic Strategies Targeting Lipid Metabolism

Lifestyle Interventions

Lifestyle modification serves as the primary first-line strategy for managing T2DM and lipid metabolic disorders, exerting broad-spectrum and multi-targeted beneficial effects on disturbed lipid metabolism [104]. The landmark Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) study confirmed that intensive lifestyle intervention reduced the risk of T2DM onset by 58% in high-risk populations, a preventive effect significantly superior to that of metformin monotherapy (31%) [105].

Dietary Intervention

Dietary adjustment improves metabolic status mainly by reducing exogenous lipid intake, reshaping gut microbiota homeostasis, and regulating key pathways of lipid metabolism [106]. Extensive clinical and preclinical evidence indicates that the Mediterranean diet, low-carbohydrate diet and intermittent fasting effectively improve the lipid profile, reduce circulating FFAs levels, and mitigate hepatic steatosis [107, 108]. The Mediterranean diet, which is high in monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) and dietary fiber, promotes FAO by activating the PPARα pathway, thereby reducing the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in patients with T2DM [109]. A high-fiber diet selectively increases the abundance of beneficial gut bacteria, promotes the production of SCFAs, improves intestinal barrier function, and alleviates metabolic endotoxemia [106]. Notably, there is currently no universal dietary pattern applicable to all T2DM patients. In clinical practice, individualized regimens should be formulated based on the patient’s metabolic characteristics, cultural background, and dietary preferences. For example, multiple clinical studies have shown that a low-calorie, high-protein diet yields better outcomes in adolescents with T2DM, while elderly patients need to focus on the maintenance and protection of muscle mass to prevent sarcopenia [110].

Exercise Intervention

Regular exercise can ameliorate systemic lipid metabolic disorders by inducing adaptive remodeling of adipose tissue, enhancing skeletal muscle mitochondrial biogenesis and function, and facilitating lipid oxidation, while increasing skeletal muscle glucose uptake and improving systemic insulin sensitivity [111]. Clinical findings have indicated that moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (≥ 30 min per session, 3–5 times per week) can effectively elevate circulating irisin and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels in most individuals, and alleviate chronic low-grade inflammation [112]. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) induces a significant skeletal muscle-derived IL-6 and IL-15 secretory response, enhances systemic lipid oxidation capacity, and preferentially reduces visceral fat accumulation [113]. Several recent clinical studies have demonstrated that in obese T2DM patients, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) performed in the evening may confer greater improvements in insulin sensitivity and T2DM prevention compared to morning exercise [114]. In addition, intelligent management models based on digital health applications and telemedicine technology can significantly improve patients’ long-term exercise adherence, thereby further amplifying the clinical benefits of lifestyle interventions.

Pharmacotherapies Targeting Cellular Lipotoxicity

Irreversible damage to pancreatic β-cells and hepatocytes caused by lipotoxicity is a core link in the progression of T2DM. Multiple commonly used clinical hypoglycemic agents can alleviate lipotoxicity and protect target organ function through distinct mechanisms.

Glucagon-like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) Receptor Agonists

GLP-1 receptor agonists are among the most extensively studied hypoglycemic drugs with potent lipid-regulating effects. Multiple phase Ⅲ clinical trials have confirmed that these agents significantly reduce serum TGs and LDL-C levels [115, 116]. Specifically, semaglutide treatment for 24 weeks reduced in mean TGs by 28% and LDL-C by 15% [117]. Its mechanisms include: directly inhibiting adipose tissue lipolysis, promoting WAT browning, and reducing food intake and body weight via central nervous system effects. In addition, GLP-1 receptor agonists exert protective effects on pancreatic β-cells by promoting proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis, and alleviate lipotoxicity-induced oxidative stress and ERS [115].

Metformin

As the first-line cornerstone drug for T2DM, metformin, in addition to its hypoglycemic effect, promotes FFAs re-esterification, inhibits adipose tissue lipolysis, and reduces circulating lipid levels by activating the AMPK pathway [118]. Studies have shown that metformin improves hepatocyte mitochondrial function, reduces hepatic lipid synthesis and ectopic deposition, and has demonstrated benefits in T2DM patients with metabolic MASLD [119]. In Zucker diabetic fatty (ZDF) rat models of diabetic cardiomyopathy, metformin significantly reduces cardiac TG content and upregulates cardiac adiponectin expression [120]. Additionally, the DMCM-AHEAD (Diabetes and Lipid Accumulation and Heart Transplant) prospective ongoing study has shown that lipotoxic factors are elevated in diabetic versus nondiabetic heart transplant recipients, and metformin use is associated with lower levels of these factors [121]. These findings provide evidence supporting the potential utility of metformin in treating diabetic cardiovascular complications. However, studies in ZDF rats have also shown that metformin does not reduce cardiac fibrosis [120]. Further studies are needed to clarify these mechanisms and their translational relevance to human patients.

PPAR Agonists

Thiazolidinediones (TZDs) promote adipocyte differentiation at the transcriptional level, increase subcutaneous fat storage capacity, and reduce ectopic lipid spillover to the liver and pancreas [122]. Although their clinical use is limited by side effects such as weight gain, edema, and heart failure risk, their mechanisms for improving IR and lipid metabolism are well-characterized [123]. New-generation derivatives such as lobeglitazone have significantly reduced the risk of adverse events reaction risks while maintaining efficacy [124].

Interventional Studies Targeting Multi-organ Communication Axes

The brain-gut-liver-adipose axis is the key integrative network through which lipid metabolism disorders drive the progression of T2DM. Targeting this axis represents an important therapeutic direction based on lipid metabolism mechanisms.

Gut Microbiota Modulation

Probiotics are among the most extensively studied intervention for this axis. Multiple studies have shown that supplementation with strains such as Akkermansia muciniphila and Lactobacillus reuteri improves intestinal barrier function and increases SCFAs production [125]. SCFAs can not only directly inhibit hepatic DNL but also stimulate intestinal L cells to secrete GLP-1 and peptide YY (PYY), regulating central appetite and energy expenditure via the vagus nerve pathway [125, 126]. The recently discovered caseinolytic peptidase B protein homolog (ClpB)-MC4R pathway represents a novel mechanism for probiotic action: bacterial ClpB protein can activate MC4R through molecular mimicry, exerting appetite-suppressing and lipid-regulating effects [127]. A 12-week randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial showed that supplementation with Hafnia alvei HA4597, a probiotic strain expressing ClpB, significantly increased the proportion of overweight adults losing at least 3% and even 4% of baseline body weight while following a hypocaloric diet. And expert guidelines recognize 3–5% body weight loss as a clinically meaningful target for overweight individuals, as it correlates with improved glycemia and reduced T2DM and cardiovascular risks [128]. Prebiotics such as inulin and fructo-oligosaccharides selectively promote the growth of SCFAs-producing bacteria, enhancing the beneficial effects of gut microbiota. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can partially restore the function of the brain-gut-liver-adipose axis by reconstructing a more balanced gut microbiota composition, and has shown efficacy in improving IR and lipid metabolism in some clinical trials [92].

GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) are currently among the most representative multi-targeted intervention drugs. Clinical studies have found that GLP-1RAs treatment improves gut microbiota abundance, Shannon index, and observed species number in T2DM patients, suggesting that it may prevent cognitive dysfunction in T2DM patients by correcting brain-gut axis dysregulation [129].

Novel Therapeutic Strategies Targeting Lipid Metabolism

Recent years have witnessed significant advances in the development of novel drugs targeting key pathways of lipid metabolism. Based on their mechanisms of action, these agents are commonly categorized into three primary classes: (1) Key enzyme inhibitors: By targeting rate-limiting enzymes in lipid synthesis and metabolism, these agents reduce lipid synthesis, and indirectly promote lipid breakdown in some cases, thereby ameliorating dyslipidemia. And most preclinical models and early clinical trials have shown that these agents improving IR. Most of these agents have well-characterized mechanisms of action, with examples including ketohexokinase (KHK), fatty acid synthase (FASN), diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), and stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) inhibitors; (2) Energy metabolism modulators: They modulate lipid metabolism by enhancing mitochondrial function, promoting FAO, and improving systemic energy homeostasis, which have shown promising efficacy in ameliorating IR and multiple features of metabolic syndrome in preclinical and clinical studies. Examples include mitochondrial uncouplers, thyroid hormone receptor β (THRβ) agonists, fibroblast growth factor (FGF) analogues, and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) precursors; (3) Other key signaling pathways and molecular targeted drugs: These agents act on critical genes or proteins regulating lipid and glucose uptake, transport, and metabolism, can improve energy substrate utilization and insulin sensitivity by restoring dysregulated signaling networks. Conclusions and Outlook

Most current research generally regards dyslipidemia as a concomitant pathological feature of T2DM. However, this review emphasizes that lipid metabolism disorders is not merely a downstream metabolic consequence, but rather a core early driver and central pathological basis underlying the initiation and progression of T2DM. This shift in understanding has significant clinical implications: the remodeling and dynamic alterations of specific lipid species, such as ceramides, have emerged as promising high-dimensional, mechanistically informative biomarkers that can reflect organ-specific IR and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction. This goes beyond the descriptive level of traditional lipid profiles, and may provide more precise potential targets for early screening, disease stratification and preventive interventions.

Notably, lipids act as essential signaling molecules that play a critical central role in metabolic regulatory networks. Through diverse classes, including FAs, phospholipids, and cholesterol, they mediate “organ-to-organ crosstalk” among metabolically relevant organs (e.g., adipose tissue, liver, skeletal muscle, gut, and brain), constructing an intricate metabolic regulatory network. The key mechanisms underlying inter-organ crosstalk include: (1) FFAs and adipokines (e.g., leptin, adiponectin) released by adipose tissue regulate hepatic gluconeogenesis and insulin sensitivity. (2) Adipose tissue dysfunction secondary to chronic lipid overload leads to ectopic lipid deposition and further facilitates the accumulation of lipotoxic intermediates (e.g., DAGs, ceramides), which can disrupt insulin signaling in other metabolic organs. (3) SCFAs derived from gut microbiota influence systemic inflammatory status and insulin sensitivity. (4) The brain is a key regulator of lipid metabolism and energy balance in peripheral tissues via the SNS. Such dynamic multi-organ dialogue is critical for maintaining systemic metabolic homeostasis.

Due to the article layout limitation, we did not specifically address the role of lipid dysregulation in diabetic complications or strategies for targeting it. Future research should further integrate lipidomics with multi-omics technologies, leveraging artificial intelligence and machine learning techniques, to identify robust predictive signatures from high-dimensional lipid species datasets and establish their dynamic associations with clinical phenotypes, pathological mechanisms and treatment responses, so that we can ultimately achieve precise prevention and control of lipid metabolism disorders and T2DM.
