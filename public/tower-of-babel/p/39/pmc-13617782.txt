Periodontitis prevalence and effects on postoperative complications in gastric surgery: a prospective observational study
Authors: Emil A Pettersson, Tiina Vuorela, Johanna M Louhimo, Jaana Hagström, Caj H Haglund, Timo Sorsa, Hanna E Seppänen, Kethe M Hermunen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontitis, a progressive inflammatory disease of the tooth-supporting structures, has direct adverse effects on systemic health. Links between periodontitis and gastrointestinal tract malignancies have also emerged in numerous studies. The aim of our study was to uncover the prevalence of periodontitis and its effects on postoperative complications among patients undergoing gastric surgery.MethodsThis prospective study took place between May 2017 and June 2019 at the Helsinki University Hospital, with data from patients undergoing gastric surgery. Background

Being a progressive disease of the tooth-supporting structures, and involving chronic bacteria-caused inflammation, periodontitis leads to inflammation-mediated tissue destruction [1]. It first manifests as inflamed, recessed, and bleeding gums, but in its earlier stages, periodontitis often progresses with an absence of severe symptoms. Periodontitis thus often goes undiagnosed in its earlier stages, which is when preventive treatment proves most effective [2]. Importantly, periodontitis contributes to low-grade systemic inflammation and has adverse effects on general health [3]. This presents the possibility for this disease to exert a significant and insidious impact on surgical outcomes. Periodontal disease offers a range of severity, with its mild stage vastly different from its severe one. When severe, it is characterized by profound gum inflammation and recession as well as advanced and irreversible degeneration of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, and a much more pronounced systemic impact [4].

The prevalence of periodontitis is high, estimated at 45% to 50% worldwide, with its severe form affecting 10% to 15% of the population [5]. A recent meta-analysis from 2023, one looking at 55 epidemiological studies, suggested an approximate periodontitis prevalence of around 52% [6]. The National Public Health Institute of Finland estimated the periodontitis prevalence among adults over age 30 to be around 64% [7]. Periodontitis is conventionally diagnosed through clinical periodontal examination, with probing pocket depth (PPD) and clinical attachment loss (CAL) serving as major markers of disease severity [8].

Periodontitis, especially in its severe form, has serious systemic effects and is associated with a plethora of systemic diseases including cardiovascular, chronic kidney, respiratory, and metabolic disease [4]. Janati et al. showed that periodontitis has direct links to colorectal malignancies even when their study controlled for numerous confounders [9]. That study suggests that periodontal disease may be more common in patients with gastrointestinal (GI)-tract malignancies. Despite the high prevalence of periodontitis, its effects on postoperative complications have not received thorough study [4, 5].

In recent years, a few groups have studied the link between periodontitis and postoperative complications in certain surgical operations [10–12]. A historical cohort study looked at patients undergoing radical surgery for GI cancer including gastrectomy, colorectal resection, and pancreatoduodenectomy [13], aiming at investigating the relationship between the oral environment and postoperative infections. They found periodontal disease to be an independent risk factor for postoperative infections [11]. In cardiac surgery, periodontitis has been linked to an increased risk for postoperative infections and prolonged hospital stay [12, 14]. Our own study on patients undergoing pancreatic surgery showed periodontitis to be an independent risk factor for both postoperative thrombotic events and hemorrhage [10].

Periodontitis screening has historically relied primarily on clinical examination, but tests utilizing biomarkers in oral fluids have recently emerged. The active matrix metalloproteinase-8 (aMMP-8) point-of-care test (POCT), developed by Sorsa et al., has been an effective means of diagnostics and screening for periodontal diseases [15, 16]. This approach allows for consistent and standardized screening, something that is in practice difficult to achieve with individual clinical examinations.

The aim of our study was to prospectively study the prevalence of periodontitis and the effects of periodontitis on postoperative complications for patients undergoing gastric surgery.

Methods

This prospective study was conducted at Helsinki University Hospital between May 2017 and June 2019. During this period, we gathered data from patients undergoing both gastric and pancreatic surgery. For our results on the pancreatic surgery patients see reference 10. Patients were recruited through consecutive sampling, and eligible patients were screened preoperatively for periodontitis. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, elective gastric surgery, ability to provide informed consent, and completion of the preoperative aMMP-8 screening test. No patients were excluded based on systemic diseases or medications. Patient information regarded age, gender, BMI (Body Mass Index), smoking status, diabetes, tumour histology, and type of surgical procedure. BMI groups were categorized as < 30 and ≥ 30 according to World Health Organization (WHO) obesity thresholds; no patients in our cohort had a BMI > 40. Tumour histology was categorized into adenocarcinoma, GIST, NET, and benign conditions to reflect biologically distinct groups. Complications were recorded and classified based on the Clavien–Dindo classification [17].

For periodontitis screening, we utilized the aMMP-8-POCT mouthrinse immunotest, which assesses elevated levels (> 20 ng/ml) of active MMP-8 components in oral fluids. Clinical periodontal probing, radiographic examination, or periodontal charting were not performed. This test has a reported sensitivity of 75% to 85% and specificity of 80% to 90%, and elevated aMMP-8 levels have been shown to correlate with clinical signs of periodontal inflammation and active tissue breakdown [15]. The aMMP-8-POCT test is an established method for periodontitis screening and diagnostics. The test was performed by a trained nurse in accordance with manufacturer’s instructions. Actim Ltd, Espoo, Finland, and Dentognostics GmbH, Jena, Germany, the manufacturers of the test kits, were not involved in this study.

Our statistical analysis was SPSS version 28, determining whether or not statistically significant differences existed in outcomes between groups. The Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test allowed analysis of associations between categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, using a two-sided Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test.

Results

We included 59 patients, of whom 29 (49%) tested positive for periodontitis. Adenocarcinoma was the most common diagnosis, occurring in 42 patients (71%). Prevalence of adenocarcinoma was similar in the periodontitis group (72%) and in the non-periodontitis group (70%). Periodontitis prevalence did not differ significantly between adenocarcinoma and benign cases (p = 0.762). Adenocarcinoma grade was predominantly high (G3 in 85.7%), with smaller proportions of G2 (11.9%) and G1 (2.4%) tumours. Among the adenocarcinoma cases, most tumours presented at an advanced local stage, with T3–T4 disease comprising the majority, and approximately half had nodal involvement, and distant metastases were uncommon (M1 in 7 cases). We found that periodontitis did not associate with an increased number of total complications (p = 0.879), nor with severe types of complications (p = 0.417). A total of 13 postoperative infections occurred. Infection rates did not differ between the periodontitis and non-periodontitis groups (p = 0.701), and no significant difference was found for surgical site infections specifically (p = 0.149). Periodontitis did not associate with a longer hospital stay (p = 0.497) nor with an increased frequency of reoperations (p = 0.209).

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no statistically significant difference between the periodontitis and the non-periodontitis group regarding overall survival (p = 0.089). The median follow-up time was 72 months (range: 2–94 months).

Finally, we conducted a sub-analysis looking at the 24 patients who underwent total gastrectomy, focusing specifically on infective events that occurred in this group (Table 4). Considering all postoperative infective events no statistically significant association with periodontitis appeared (p = 0.237).

Discussion

Our study found a periodontitis prevalence of 49% among patients undergoing gastric surgery. This result does not differ notably from the prevalence of periodontitis among the general population [6, 7], although differing screening techniques make our results difficult to compare.

Our findings regarding overall periodontitis prevalence were, no doubt, at least in part influenced by our use of the aMMP-8 point-of-care test, with the true amount of disease likely being slightly higher. Sorsa et al. explored the compatibility between the aMMP-8 point-of-care test (cut-off: 20 ng/ml) and clinical examinations that used the new periodontitis classification from 2018 involving 150 Greek patients. The aMMP-8 test detected 98 among the 119 patients with periodontitis, an overall sensitivity of 82%, with higher stages and grades of periodontitis exhibiting even higher sensitivity and specificity [15]. It seems that for the specific purpose of uncovering the prevalence of periodontitis, the aMMP-8 POCT test can provide a sufficiently accurate measure of how much periodontitis exists within a given population; this test can thus be utilized as a screening test for periodontitis prevalence, but its attributes need close consideration in interpretation of results.

Periodontitis has for a long time been epidemiologically linked to gastric cancer, a link that has been clearly demonstrated in various parts of the world [8, 13, 18, 19]. The mechanism by which periodontitis can cause carcinogenesis remains, however, unclear; some of the proposed mechanisms include its contribution to GI-tract inflammation, and its disruption of gut microbiota [20, 21].

Recently, a nested case-control study utilizing data from a large Korean National Health Insurance Service database comprising 10 174 patients with gastric cancer and 40 696 controls, showed an increased likelihood of developing gastric cancer among individuals with a history of periodontitis [22]. In the literature, a documented trend links periodontitis with gastric cancer [8, 13, 18, 19]. In our study, periodontitis was more common among patients with a malignant diagnosis, but this was not statistically significant. Earlier epidemiological studies have typically defined oral health exposure using cumulative indicators such as tooth loss [8, 13, 18, 19], which reflect long-term oral disease burden accrued over many years. In contrast, the aMMP-8 POCT used in our study identifies current enzymatic and proteolytic inflammatory activity and provides a binary result, without quantifying cumulative exposure or distinguishing between mild and advanced disease. Although the test shows good overall sensitivity and performs increasingly well in more advanced stages of periodontitis [15], a binary positive/negative categorization inevitably groups together patients with very different periodontal histories. The recent Korean NHIS study [22] defined periodontitis using ICD-10 dental diagnoses recorded in treatment claims. While this approach identifies clinically recognized disease, it does not provide information on disease severity or lifetime cumulative periodontal burden. This study, along with others, examined gastric cancer risk in large population cohorts, whereas our study represents a comparatively small surgical population with established gastric pathology. Periodontitis prevalence was high in both the adenocarcinoma and benign groups, reducing exposure contrast in our dataset. Moreover, earlier literature focused specifically on adenocarcinoma [8, 13, 18, 19], while our overall surgical cohort included a broader range of gastric diagnoses. Differences in population structure, exposure measurement, and sample size likely contribute to the discrepancy between our findings and earlier reports.

We found no significant association between periodontitis and postoperative infections. When the focus was on surgical infections, we observed that most of these complications occurred in the periodontitis-positive group (p = 0.149). However, when it came to overall infective complications, there appeared to be no difference between groups (p = 0.701). When we focused solely on infective complications in only the subgroup of 24 patients undergoing total gastrectomy, we also found no statistically significant differences between groups, although a similar trend was evident to indicate that the presence of periodontitis may be associated with a higher risk for infective events (p = 0.237). In terms of absolute differences in infectious complications between groups, our results are somewhat comparable to the results of Nishikawa et al. with periodontitis patients, showing 10% to 25% higher rates of infectious complications than in their counterparts who had a relatively healthier periodontal status [11].

Our results may also support the conclusion that periodontitis may not represent a significant risk factor for postoperative complications regarding gastric surgery (p = 0.879). However, the relatively small sample size warrants cautious interpretation of these findings, and further research is needed.

The mechanism through which periodontitis could cause postoperative infective complications (POIC) is unknown. One theory is that the systemic chronic inflammation as well as the local tissue degradation plays a role in predisposing patients to POICs via direct contamination as well as by bloodstream infection. The positive effects of perioperative oral management on postoperative pneumonia have been demonstrated in studies considering various types of major cancer surgery [23, 24], findings which support the direct contamination theory.

Furthermore, we also looked at postoperative thrombotic events and found no difference between groups (p = 0.513). Others have found associations between periodontitis and infections [11, 23, 25], and one study of periodontitis in patients undergoing pancreatic surgery found periodontitis to be a risk factor for thrombotic events and hemorrhage [10]. These results may indicate that there exists no significant increase in the risk for thrombotic events among periodontitis patients when it comes to gastric surgery. More studies are needed to properly examine this area.

Our study was prospectively conducted, and the surgeons were blinded to the aMMP-8 POCT results: one of the strengths of our study. Nevertheless, several notable limitations must be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and single-center study design may limit statistical power and generalizability of the findings. In addition, although baseline characteristics such as smoking, diabetes, and BMI were assessed, other patient-related factors including comorbidities and medication may also have influenced postoperative outcomes. Furthermore, although previous studies have demonstrated associations between aMMP-8 levels and periodontitis stage and grade [15], its use as a binary positive/negative screening tool in our study limited evaluation of disease severity, as no full clinical periodontal examination or radiographic staging was performed. Further research on periodontitis prevalence by means of point-of-care testing technology could make it easier to compare results between studies and encourage more prospective research concerning periodontitis in various groups. Future comprehensive studies are necessary to confirm the possible effect of periodontitis on postoperative complications in gastric surgery. It would also be crucial to investigate whether perioperative periodontitis treatment could reduce postoperative infectious and other complications in gastrointestinal surgery in general.

Conclusions

The prevalence of periodontitis among patients undergoing gastric surgery was comparable to the general population. No statistically significant association was found between periodontitis and postoperative complications, including infective or thrombotic events. While a trend toward higher complication rates in periodontitis-positive groups was noted, it did not reach statistical significance. These findings could suggest that periodontitis may not constitute a major risk factor for adverse postoperative outcomes in gastric surgery. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm or refute this potential association.
