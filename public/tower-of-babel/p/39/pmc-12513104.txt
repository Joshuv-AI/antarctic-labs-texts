Sex-based disparities in asthma prevalence in Sierra Leone
Authors: Augustus Osborne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsthma is a major public health concern globally, with notable disparities in prevalence across regions and demographic groups. In Sierra Leone, a country with limited healthcare infrastructure, asthma is a challenge. Sex-based disparities in asthma prevalence have been documented globally, yet their impact in Sierra Leone remains poorly understood. This study aims to provide age-standardized estimates of asthma prevalence in 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2019, focusing on trends and inequalities by sex.MethodsThe study utilized data from the World Health Organization equity data repository to estimate age-standardized asthma prevalence in Sierra Leone. Sex-disaggregated analyses was conducted to assess disparities in prevalence rates. Inequality measures, including absolute difference (D), prevalence ratio (R), population attributable fraction (PAF), and population attributable risk (PAR), were calculated to quantify sex-related disparities in asthma prevalence.Results In 2000, the age-standardized asthma prevalence in Sierra Leone was 2,807.2 per 100,000, increasing slightly to 2,855.5 per 100,000 in 2005, followed by a decline to 2,682.7 per 100,000 in 2010. Prevalence rose again to 2,709.8 per 100,000 in 2015 and reached 2,768.1 per 100,000 in 2019. Across all years, females consistently had higher asthma prevalence rates than males. In 2019, female asthma prevalence was 3,104.7 per 100,000 compared to 2,427.8 per 100,000 for males, with an absolute difference of 677.0 per 100,000 and a prevalence ratio of 1.3. Inequality measures revealed a gap over time, with negative PAF values (-5.9% in 2000 to -12.3% in 2019) and negative PAR values (-164.7 per 100,000 in 2000 to -340.3 per 100,000 in 2019). ConclusionThe study points out the burden of asthma in Sierra Leone and the sex-based disparities in prevalence. The disproportionate burden among females underscores the need for interventions to address biological, behavioural, and environmental factors contributing to these inequalities. Policymakers should prioritize sex-disaggregated surveillance and implement sex-sensitive strategies to reduce asthma prevalence. Future research should investigate the underlying causes of sex-based disparities, including the role of environmental exposures and healthcare access, to inform more effective and equitable asthma control measures in Sierra Leone.

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic respiratory disease characterized by inflammation and narrowing of the airways, leading to recurrent episodes of wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, and coughing [1]. It is a major global health concern, affecting an estimated 262 million people worldwide in 2019 and causing more than 455,000 deaths annually, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. The burden of asthma is particularly severe in low- and middle-income countries, where access to healthcare services and effective management strategies is often limited [2]. In sub-Saharan Africa, including Sierra Leone, asthma is a major public health challenge due to environmental factors, socioeconomic disparities, and limited healthcare infrastructure [3]. Despite its high prevalence and impact on quality of life, asthma remains underdiagnosed and undertreated in many resource-constrained settings [4]. Understanding the prevalence of asthma and identifying disparities in disease burden, particularly between sexes, is critical for informing targeted interventions and reducing health inequities.

Previous studies have explored the prevalence of asthma in sub-Saharan Africa, but these efforts have been fragmented and often lacked comprehensive, standardized data [5, 6]. Many studies have focused on isolated populations or specific regions, limiting their generalizability to the broader population of Sierra Leone [7]. Furthermore, research on asthma prevalence has historically overlooked sex-disaggregated analyses, which are crucial for understanding gender-specific disparities in disease burden and access to care [8]. Evidence suggests that biological, behavioural, and environmental factors contribute to differences in asthma prevalence and severity between males and females, yet these factors remain poorly studied in the context of Sierra Leone [9]. Additionally, while global estimates of asthma prevalence have been produced by organizations such as the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) initiative, these estimates often lack the granularity needed to address local health inequities and guide policy decisions [10]. As a result, there is a pressing need for reliable, up-to-date, and geographically complete estimates of asthma prevalence that account for sex-based disparities and are specific to Sierra Leone.

This study addresses the critical gaps in asthma research by providing sex-disaggregated, age-standardized prevalence estimates of asthma in Sierra Leone over a two-decade period (2000, 2005, 2010, 2015 and 2019). Using data from the WHO equity data repository, which integrates high-quality, standardized health data from multiple sources, this study offers a comprehensive analysis of asthma prevalence trends and inequalities [11]. By focusing on sex-based disparities, the study showcases the differential burden of asthma between males and females, providing insights into potential biological, social, and environmental factors that contribute to these disparities. Furthermore, the study leverages robust methodologies for calculating age-standardized rates, ensuring comparability across time and populations. The findings of this study not only contribute to the understanding of asthma prevalence in Sierra Leone but also provide critical evidence to inform public health policies and interventions aimed at reducing health inequities. In doing so, this study fills an important gap in the literature and serves as a foundational resource for addressing asthma as a public health priority in Sierra Leone.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study employed a cross-sectional design to analyze the prevalence of asthma in Sierra Leone over a two-decade period, with a specific focus on sex-disaggregated trends and inequalities. All data were sourced from the WHO equity data repository, a global database that provides standardized, high-quality health-related data for monitoring health inequities across countries.

Reliability and plausibility of data

The WHO equity data repository compiles data from multiple sources, including Demographic and Health Surveys, national censuses, administrative health records, and other large-scale population-based surveys. For Sierra Leone, the primary data sources typically include the Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Surveys, national health information systems, and periodic health surveys coordinated by the Ministry of Health and Sanitation in collaboration with international partners. The WHO applies rigorous data quality assessment and harmonization procedures, including validation against international standards, to ensure comparability and plausibility of estimates across countries and over time. While the estimates are considered plausible and widely used for global health monitoring, some limitations may exist due to the quality and completeness of primary data sources, especially in low-resource settings.

Asthma diagnosis and data collection in Sierra Leone

Asthma diagnosis in Sierra Leone is primarily based on clinical symptoms reported during health surveys or recorded in health facility data. The use of spirometry, the gold standard for confirming asthma diagnosis, remains limited in Sierra Leone due to resource constraints and a lack of widespread availability of diagnostic equipment and trained personnel. As a result, the reported prevalence figures may reflect both diagnosed and self-reported cases, and there may be some degree of underdiagnosis or misclassification, particularly in rural or underserved areas. The WHO’s methodology for estimating asthma prevalence aims to account for such limitations by integrating multiple data sources and applying statistical adjustments.

Outcome measure and dimension of inequality

The primary outcome measure in this study was the age-standardized prevalence of asthma, expressed as the number of cases per 100,000 population. Asthma prevalence was defined according to the WHO International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), which categorizes asthma as a chronic respiratory condition characterized by airway inflammation and obstruction [12]. The prevalence estimates were analysed separately for males and females to examine sex-based differences in the burden of asthma.

The dimension of inequality assessed in this study was sex, a critical demographic variable for understanding disparities in health outcomes. Inequality measures were calculated to quantify the magnitude and persistence of sex-based differences in asthma prevalence. Specifically, the absolute difference (D) and prevalence ratio (R) were used to compare the burden of asthma between males and females. Additionally, the population attributable fraction (PAF) and population attributable risk (PAR) were calculated to assess the population-level impact of sex-based differences on the overall burden of asthma.

Data analysis

The analysis for this study was conducted using the WHO Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT), a publicly available software designed to facilitate the analysis of health inequality data. The HEAT tool provides pre-processed results based on data from the WHO equity data repository, enabling users to explore and download inequality measures and trends over time.

For this study, the HEAT tool was used to extract sex-disaggregated prevalence estimates of asthma (per 100,000 population) in Sierra Leone for the period 2000–2019. The tool automatically calculates inequality measures, including absolute differences and prevalence ratios, to quantify disparities between males and females. Furthermore, the HEAT tool computes population-level impact measures, such as the population attributable fraction and population attributable risk, to assess the contribution of sex-based disparities to the overall burden of asthma.

The downloaded results from the HEAT tool included age-standardized prevalence estimates for males and females, as well as all inequality measures. These results were presented in tabular and graphical formats, providing a clear visualization of trends and disparities over time. Since the HEAT tool performs all statistical analyses and ensures the accuracy and comparability of results, no independent statistical analysis was conducted for this study. The results were directly interpreted and reported as provided by the HEAT tool.

Results

Prevalence of asthma in Sierra Leone

Figure 1 shows the age-standardized prevalence of asthma (per 100,000 population) in Sierra Leone from 2000 to 2019. The prevalence shows a fluctuating trend over the years. In 2000, the prevalence was 2,807.2 per 100,000, which increased slightly to 2,855.5 in 2005. However, a noticeable decline occurred by 2010, with the prevalence dropping to 2,682.7 per 100,000. Following this decrease, the prevalence began to rise again, reaching 2,709.8 per 100,000 in 2015 and further increasing to 2,768.1 per 100,000 in 2019.

Trends in prevalence by sex (Table 1)

The trends in asthma prevalence from 2000 to 2019, as shown in Table 1, reveal consistent differences between females and males. Across all years, females had higher prevalence rates compared to males. In 2000, the prevalence for females was 2,945.1 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2,628.6–3,361.3), while for males, it was 2,642.5 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2,278.7–3,098.9). By 2005, female prevalence increased to 3,083.4 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2,733.9–3,515.3), while male prevalence rose to 2,598.7 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2,237.8–3,096.4). In 2010, female prevalence decreased slightly to 2,952.5 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2,605.5–3,389.2), while male prevalence was 2,391.9 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2,026.9–2,827.3). By 2015, female prevalence increased again to 3,035.2 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2,695.4–3,444.0), while male prevalence was 2,373.0 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2,006.2–2,858.3). Finally, in 2019, female prevalence reached 3,104.7 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2,730.4–3,609.2), while male prevalence was 2,427.8 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2,011.8–2,960.5).

Inequality measures by sex (Table 2)

The inequality measures associated with asthma prevalence by sex, as shown in Table 2, provide further insights into the disparities. The absolute difference between females and males increased over time, from 302.6 per 100,000 in 2000 to 677.0 per 100,000 in 2019, indicating that the inequality between sexes has widened. The ratio of prevalence between females and males also increased slightly, from 1.1 in 2000 to 1.3 in 2019, further underscoring the disproportionate burden of asthma among females. The population attributable fraction, which represents the proportion of asthma prevalence attributable to sex differences, remained negative across all years, ranging from -5.9% in 2000 to -12.3% in 2019. This suggests that sex differences do not contribute positively to the overall prevalence. Similarly, the population attributable risk, reflecting the absolute reduction in prevalence if sex differences were eliminated, became more negative over time, from -164.7 per 100,000 in 2000 to -340.3 per 100,000 in 2019.

Discussion

This study provides age-standardized estimates of asthma prevalence in Sierra Leone from 2000 to 2019, highlighting fluctuating trends over time and significant sex-based disparities. The prevalence of asthma showed an overall increase, rising from 2,807.2 per 100,000 in 2000 to 2,768.1 per 100,000 in 2019, with fluctuations observed in intermediate years. Females consistently had higher prevalence rates compared to males across all years, with the absolute difference between sexes increasing over time. In 2000, the difference in prevalence between females and males was 302.6 per 100,000, which increased to 677.0 per 100,000 by 2019. Similarly, the prevalence ratio between females and males rose from 1.1 in 2000 to 1.3 in 2019.

The consistent observation of higher asthma prevalence among females aligns with global evidence suggesting sex-related differences in asthma burden [13, 14]. Studies have shown that biological factors, such as hormonal influences and differences in airway anatomy, may contribute to the increased susceptibility of females to asthma, particularly during adolescence and adulthood [15, 16]. Behavioural and environmental factors, including differences in exposure to indoor and outdoor air pollution, occupational hazards, and healthcare-seeking behaviours, may also exacerbate disparities [17]. In the context of Sierra Leone, these disparities are likely amplified by the country’s unique environmental and socio-economic challenges. For instance, the widespread use of biomass fuels for cooking, a common practice in Sierra Leone, disproportionately affects women due to their increased exposure to indoor air pollution, which is a known risk factor for asthma [18]. The gap in asthma prevalence between sexes, as evidenced by the increasing absolute difference and prevalence ratio, underscores the need for targeted interventions to address these inequities.

The negative values of population attributable fraction and population attributable risk observed across all years further emphasize the impact of sex differences on asthma prevalence. While these measures indicate that sex-related disparities do not contribute positively to the overall prevalence, they suggest that eliminating these disparities could lead to substantial reductions in asthma burden. For example, the population attributable risk in 2019 was -340.3 per 100,000, indicating that addressing sex-related inequalities could potentially reduce asthma prevalence by this amount. In Sierra Leone, where gender inequalities are pervasive, this finding underscores the importance of incorporating sex-disaggregated analyses into public health strategies and policies aimed at reducing the burden of asthma. The observed sex differences in asthma prevalence are likely multifactorial. Biological factors, such as hormonal influences and differences in airway anatomy, may contribute to the increased susceptibility of females to asthma, particularly during adolescence and adulthood. Behavioral and environmental factors, including greater exposure to indoor air pollution due to the widespread use of biomass fuels for cooking, may also play a significant role, especially among women in Sierra Leone. While differential access to health services between males and females is an important consideration in understanding asthma outcomes such as mortality and disease control, it is less likely to be a primary driver of asthma prevalence, which is the focus of this study. Rather, health service access more directly affects asthma management and long-term morbidity. Sex-sensitive interventions that address environmental exposures and behavioural risk factors may therefore have a meaningful impact on reducing asthma prevalence among females in Sierra Leone.

The fluctuating trends in asthma prevalence observed in Sierra Leone reflect the complex interplay of factors influencing the burden of asthma in the country. The initial increase in prevalence from 2000 to 2005, followed by a decline in 2010 and subsequent rise in 2015 and 2019, may be indicative of changes in environmental exposures, and diagnostic practices over time. For example, the decrease in prevalence observed in 2010 may correspond to improvements in healthcare infrastructure or public health interventions aimed at reducing respiratory diseases [18, 19]. This period aligns with post-civil war recovery efforts, during which Sierra Leone made strides in rebuilding its healthcare system. However, the subsequent rise in prevalence may reflect increasing exposure to risk factors such as air pollution, urbanization, and climate change, which have been linked to higher rates of asthma globally [20]. In Sierra Leone, rapid urbanization, particularly in cities like Freetown, has led to increased air pollution from vehicle emissions and industrial activities, likely contributing to the observed rise in asthma prevalence.

Additionally, socio-economic factors in Sierra Leone, including poverty and limited access to healthcare, likely exacerbate the burden of asthma. Poverty restricts access to preventive measures, timely diagnosis, and effective treatment, which may explain the overall high prevalence rates observed in this study. Women, in particular, face additional socio-economic barriers, including lower educational attainment and reduced autonomy in healthcare decision-making, which may contribute to the widening sex-based disparities in asthma prevalence. Cultural norms and behavioural patterns further compound these challenges. For instance, women in Sierra Leone may be more likely to seek healthcare for asthma symptoms, leading to higher diagnostic rates, while men may underreport symptoms or delay seeking care. These factors underscore the need for sex-sensitive public health strategies to address asthma burden in Sierra Leone.

The findings of this study contribute to a better understanding of asthma prevalence within the broader context of Sierra Leone’s socio-economic, environmental, and healthcare landscape. While this analysis is descriptive and ecological in nature and cannot establish causality, the observed patterns underscore areas where further research and public health attention may be warranted. Addressing the burden of asthma in Sierra Leone likely requires multi-faceted strategies that are sensitive to the country’s unique challenges. For example, environmental interventions such as promoting cleaner cooking technologies and reducing air pollution may help mitigate asthma risk, though their effectiveness in this context should be further explored. Similarly, healthcare improvements, including expanding access to diagnostic tools such as spirometry, ensuring a consistent supply of essential asthma medications, training healthcare professionals, and increasing public awareness, may help address some of the identified gaps. These findings also suggest the potential value of gender-sensitive approaches, such as empowering women through education and improved healthcare access, in addressing sex-related disparities in asthma prevalence. However, as this study is based on secondary, ecological data, these recommendations should be interpreted as areas for further investigation rather than direct policy prescriptions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the reliance on secondary data from the WHO Equity Data repository may introduce biases related to data quality and completeness. While the repository provides standardized estimates, the accuracy of these estimates depends on the quality of the underlying data sources, which may vary across years and regions. Second, the study did not account for potential confounding factors, such as socioeconomic status, urban versus rural residence, and access to healthcare, which may influence asthma prevalence and contribute to sex-related disparities. Third, the use of age-standardized prevalence estimates, while essential for comparability, may obscure important age-specific trends and differences in asthma burden. Also, the availability of spirometry the gold standard for confirming asthma diagnosis is currently very limited in Sierra Leone due to resource constraints, a lack of equipment, and a shortage of trained healthcare professionals. As a result, most cases of asthma reflected in national data are based on clinical judgment rather than objective lung function testing. Readers are advised to interpret prevalence estimates with caution, as some degree of underdiagnosis or misclassification may occur, particularly in rural and underserved areas. Finally, the study did not explore the potential effects of environmental risk factors, such as air pollution and climate change, which are known to influence asthma prevalence and severity.

Future directions

Building on the findings of this study, future research should focus on identifying the specific factors contributing to sex-related disparities in asthma prevalence in Sierra Leone. Longitudinal studies examining the role of hormonal, behavioural, and environmental influences on asthma burden could provide valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms driving these inequalities. Additionally, research exploring the impact of targeted interventions, such as air quality improvement programs and education campaigns, on reducing asthma prevalence among females could inform evidence-based policy decisions. Expanding the scope of analysis to include other dimensions of inequality, such as socioeconomic status and geographic location, would also enhance understanding of the broader determinants of asthma burden in Sierra Leone.

Conclusion

The study spots the fluctuating trends in asthma prevalence in Sierra Leone from 2000 to 2019 and underscores the persistent sex-based disparities in disease burden. Females consistently had higher prevalence rates compared to males, with the absolute difference and prevalence ratio between sexes increasing over time. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to address sex-related inequalities and reduce the burden of asthma in Sierra Leone. By providing reliable, up-to-date, and geographically complete estimates of asthma prevalence, this study contributes to the growing body of evidence on respiratory health in low-resource settings and serves as a foundation for future research and policy development. Addressing the disparities identified in this study could lead to substantial reductions in asthma burden and improve health equity in Sierra Leone.
