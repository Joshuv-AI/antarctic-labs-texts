Knowledge mapping of burnout among nursing professionals: A bibliometric study
Authors: Simin Chi, Weiyi Wang, Mujun Wan, Sijia Xu, Fang He, Linyuan Zhang, Shanbo Hu, Xiwen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOccupational burnout is defined as a state of physical and mental exhaustion resulting from prolonged exposure to work-related stress, which is particularly evident among healthcare workers. With the changing global health situation, nurses are experiencing increased work pressures, leading to a growing incidence of occupational burnout. This affects not only the physical and mental well-being of nurses but also the quality of nursing care provided. Therefore, research on occupational burnout among nurses is of great significance. Our objective was to review systematically the current research status, identify key hotspots, and analyze trends in this field through bibliometric analysis, thereby offering a reference for future research.MethodsIn this study, literature related to occupational burnout among nurses published between January 2004 and April 2024 in the Web of Science database was retrieved. For trend comparisons, 2024 publication volume was annualized by multiplying Jan-Apr counts by 3. A total of 977 articles were included for analysis. Tools such as the "bibliometrix" package in R software, Citespace 6.3.R1, and VOSviewer were used to conduct statistical analyses on the publication year, authors, journals, keywords, etc. of the literature.ResultsFrom our results, we demonstrated that the number of studies in this field had risen steadily, with a marked surge following the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. In terms of research output, the United States (US) (204 articles) China (185 articles), and Spain (77 articles) were in the leading positions. Meanwhile, strong collaborative relationships were found between the US and China, as well as between Spain and Chile. Leading institutions included Universidad de Granada (22 articles), the University of Pennsylvania (20 articles), and Shandong University (17 articles). We identified two authors as having made foundational contributions, while Nursing and Public Health were core journals. Keyword evolution revealed three research phases: early emphasis on mental health correlations (2004–2010), mid-phase focus on job satisfaction predictors (2013–2019), and post-2020 systemic analyses addressing COVID- 19′s impact on intensive care unit burnout. Protective factors, such as structural empowerment, gained prominence after 2015, signaling a shift toward research on preventive approaches.ConclusionThrough bibliometric analysis, we have presented stress, job satisfaction, and caregiving as enduring focal themes in research on burnout among nursing professionals. Recent trends have highlighted an escalated focus on pandemic-amplified factors. We have presented comprehensively the research status and trends in the field of occupational burnout among nurses, providing a reference basis for in-depth research. However, the study has limitations, such as data sources and language restrictions. Future researchers can further broaden the data sources and pay attention to the issue of occupational burnout in different cultural backgrounds.

Introduction

Occupational burnout is defined as a chronic syndrome marked by physical, mental, and emotional exhaustion arising from prolonged workplace stress, disproportionately affecting service sectors—most notably healthcare professionals (Nobre et al., 2019). The World Health Organization (2024) has highlighted ongoing uncertainty in global health due to emerging infectious diseases, of which COVID-19 has caused 4.1 million (2020) and 6.6 million (2021) deaths (Hetherington et al., 2024). These crises have exacerbated demand for nurses while increasing patient-nurse conflicts (Lee and Kang, 2019). Nurses now face stress compounded by escalating workloads, disease exposure risks, and insufficient systemic support, creating conditions ripe for burnout (Carvalho et al., 2019).

Recently, occupational burnout among nursing professionals has emerged as a systemic issue, requiring urgent resolution in the healthcare industry globally, reflecting the multidimensional pressures and structural contradictions persistently endured by this workforce (Bey et al., 2023). Recent years have witnessed a steady rise in burnout prevalence among nurses, with researchers indicating that over half of Indonesian clinical nurses exhibited varying degrees of burnout symptoms, while half reported severe burnout effects (Rahayu et al., 2024). Furthermore, one group of researchers conducted a systematic review that found that nearly 90 % of nurses experienced work-related burnout (Slusarz et al., 2022), a phenomenon supported by meta-analytic evidence demonstrating significantly higher burnout rates among intensive care unit nurses compared to other healthcare professionals, such as physicians and technicians (Papazian et al., 2023).

This burnout manifests not only through heightened emotional exhaustion and depersonalisation tendencies, but also through a significant decrease in perceived personal achievement. This was observed in a survey of 412 clinical nurses from ten tertiary hospitals in South Korea (Jun et al., 2021). The underlying causes stem from the dual challenges faced by nurses in high-pressure environments, such as excessive workloads characterised by rotating shifts, frequent overtime and disrupted circadian rhythms due to flexible scheduling. This was observed among 287 intensive care unit nurses from five urban hospitals in Pennsylvania, USA (Imes and Chasens, 2019). While simultaneously navigating trust crises in clinician-patient relationships, including verbal aggression from anxious patient families and unrealistic therapeutic expectations, based on a sample of 514 nurses working in eight public hospitals in Israel (Davidov et al., 2023). Additionally, the cumulative effects of occupational hazards and psychological stressors can exacerbate burnout. For example, infectious disease nurses experience dual physical and mental strain when confronting occupational exposure risks. These findings are based on a study involving 196 nurses from six infectious disease centres in Lombardy, Italy (Ferrandino et al., 2023).

Of greater concern is that the primary symptoms of nursing job burnout manifest across three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (the core indicator), depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment (Baldonedo-Mosteiro et al., 2019). Carvalho et al. (2019) revealed that burnout correlates with psychosomatic symptoms, such as pain, insomnia, and gastrointestinal disorders, while also undermining work performance—a combination that perpetuates a vicious cycle of declining health and job dissatisfaction.

Researchers have identified workload intensity, workplace autonomy, and organizational support as key predictors of burnout among nursing professionals in Sub-Saharan Africa (Owuor et al., 2020), with dose- response relationships observed between workload magnitude and burnout severity. These effects have transformed burnout from an individual psychological issue into a systemic risk, possibly leading to deteriorating nurse-patient communication, increased care defects, and even threats to patient safety.

Occupational burnout syndrome may compromise care quality, elevate medical error rates (Abdel-Azeem et al., 2024), and weaken team collaboration—a challenge exacerbated by the unprecedented pressures placed on healthcare systems during the COVID-19 pandemic (Hatukay et al., 2024)

The negative impacts of workload on burnout not only expose deep-rooted issues, like imbalanced healthcare resource allocation and insufficient societal support, but also signal an urgent need for comprehensive reforms in nursing management systems—encompassing institutional optimization, psychological interventions, and enhanced social recognition (Chen et al., 2024).

Bibliometric research serves as a critical method for analyzing the developmental trajectory, research hotspots, and future trends of job burnout (Judijanto, 2024). By leveraging scientific knowledge mapping tools like CiteSpace, it enables visual, analysis of knowledge evolution in nursing staff burnout, helping researchers quickly identify core issues, such as intervention heterogeneity and measurement tool standardization, while providing data support for interdisciplinary policy-making (Ezugwu et al., 2023). However, while this approach demonstrates significant advantages in macro-trend analysis, its cultural sensitivity remains limited. For example, despite the surge in global attention to nursing staff burnout following the COVID-19 pandemic (Musker and Othman, 2024), existing bibliometric studies have rarely systematically explored cross-cultural the differences.

This discrepancy underscores two significant contradictions within the domain of nursing staff burnout. On the one hand, tools such as Maslach Burnout Inventory have been found reliable in 89% of studies (de Beer and Bianchi, 2019). On the other hand, Asian researchers consistently report disproportionately high scores in the personal accomplishment dimension, suggesting that cultural contexts may distort measurement validity (Singh et al., 2020). To address this contradiction, we aimed to achieve three objectives: (a) identifying thematic clusters in burnout among nursing staff research; (b) evaluating geographic variations in conceptual frameworks; and (c) highlighting underexplored areas for future inquiry.

Our objective was to review systematically the current research status, identify key hotspots, and analyze trends in this field through bibliometric analysis, thereby offering a reference for future research.

Materials and methods

We performed a systematic literature search in May 2024 using the Web of Science Core Collection database. The search strategy employed the following keywords with Boolean operators: ("nursing staff burnout" OR "male nurse burnout" OR "military nursing staff burnout" OR "nursing student burnout"). The publication timeframe was restricted to January 2004 to April 2024, with language filters set to English.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion: (a) Articles focusing on nursing staff burnout or closely related themes. (b) Publications within the specified timeframe (January 2004 to April 2024). (c) Studies with empirical data or theoretical frameworks relevant to nursing populations.

Exclusion: (a) Non-academic publications or studies unrelated to nursing staff burnout (e.g., burnout in non-healthcare professions, editorials, conference abstracts). (b) Duplicate records or incomplete metadata.

Data extraction and visualization

We analysed the retrieved data using the following tools: (a) Bibliometric Analysis: R software (v4.2.2) (R Core Team, 2024) with the "bibliometrix" package for trend analysis and metadata extraction. (b) CiteSpace (v6.3.R1, 64-bit) (Chen, 2024) for co-citation network visualization and burst detection. (c) Visualization: VOSviewer (v1.6.20) (Van et al., 2024) for keyword co-occurrence mapping and density-based hotspot analysis. (d) Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365, 2024) was used for chart integration, and frequency calculations. (f) Noteexpress (v4.1.0.10030) (Aegean, 2023) was used to manage and deduplicate data.

The workflow included: converting citation data into compatible formats (e.g., plain text, Research Information Systems) for importing into analytical tools; generating influence relationship diagrams, geographical distributions, and temporal trend visualizations; and performing cluster analysis using modularity-based algorithms and validating results through density visualization (Subarkah and Iskandar, 2024).

Annual growth trend of publications

The publication volume and citation rate of articles can visually reflect the development trends in the research field (Fanfan et al., 2023). To gain a clearer understanding of the publication trends in nursing staff burnout research, a statistical analysis of the annual publication volume was performed.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the publication trend from 2004 to 2024 can be divided into three distinct phases. The first phase, from 2004 to 2010, recorded the publication of 87 articles, reflecting a 31 %annual growth rate, with a peak of 25 articles published in 2010. The second phase, spanning from 2011 to 2016, saw an increase to 168 articles, corresponding to an 18 % annual growth rate. Although the growth rate was less pronounced during this period, the annual publication counts consistently exceeded those of the previous phase, culminating in a second peak of 42 articles in 2016. The final phase (2017–2024) showed a surge, with Jan-Apr 2024 data extrapolated to project an annual volume of 124 articles. To clarify global authorship and co-citation trends in nursing staff burnout research, a co-occurrence heatmap analysis was applied to a dataset of 3665 authors and 17,061 co-citations (see Supplenentary Materials for details). High-density areas highlight prolific contributors, with de la Fuente, G. A. and Aiken, L. These authors held considerable academic influence and status, with their published research possibly aiding scholars in more accurately identifying and understanding the frontiers and knowledge structures within the burnout of nursing staff domain (Castanha et al., 2024).

Journals analysis

We conducted a collinearity analysis and cluster analysis on 357 journals (see Supplementary Materials for details). The top three journals ranked by the number of publications were Nursing (565 articles), Public Health (113 articles), and Archives of Environmental & Occupational Health (66 articles). Cluster analysis revealed distinct research focuses: patient-perpetrated workplace violence (Cluster 0), oncology professionals (Cluster 1), transplant nurses (Cluster 2), health-related quality (Cluster 3), electronic health record usability (Cluster 4), and oncology professional (Cluster 5) as key research directions.

Analysis of co-cited references

The bibliometric analysis encompassed 23,804 co-cited references, with network visualization optimized by displaying the top 1 % most significant connections. Three seminal works emerged as foundational contributors: Maslach and Leiter (2016) led with 67 citations, followed by Woo et al. (2020) and Dall'Ora et al. (2020) with 63 and 58 citations respectively, indicating their central role in shaping current theoretical frameworks. We further revealed a notable concentration of influential publications between 2004 and 2024, with Galanis's 2021 study demonstrating exceptional impact (emergence intensity score = 19.28; a bibliometric metric quantifying citation burst intensity via tools like CiteSpace, where scores >10 indicate significant disciplinary influence) (Galanis et al., 2021). These citation patterns collectively highlighted key milestones in the field's recent theoretical advancement, demonstrating both established theoretical foundations and emerging research directions frontiers.

Discussion

Publication status

Articles on nursing staff burnout show three stages: a stable period, a period of moderate growth, and a period of accelerated growth. The peak was reached in 2016. This trend reflected an increasing recognition of burnout as a systemic workforce crisis, further exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, which intensified various stressors faced by nurses, such as workload surges and safety concerns (Adriana et al., 2023). Consequently, there was a notable increase in scholarly output post-2020. Fluctuations in citations, particularly notable peaks in 2005 and 2016, likely arose from evolving methodological paradigms. Initially, the field was dominated by descriptive studies, but it later shifted towards intervention-focused research. This shift may also reflect a variable alignment with global health priorities.

Geographically, contributions were made by 81 countries, yet output was predominantly concentrated in the U.S. (204 articles), China (185 articles), and Spain (77 articles). This concentration can be attributed to robust funding infrastructures and well-established academic networks (Costello et al., 2017). Collaborative efforts, particularly between the U.S. and China, as well as Spain and Chile, emerged from shared research agendas and funding incentives.

Moreover, Brazil's productivity surge during the 2020s may have been linked to its ongoing nurse attrition crisis, suggesting a complex interplay between labor dynamics and healthcare system pressures (Schlosser et al., 2022). However, there remains a persistent underrepresentation of low- and middle-income countries (Lagarde, 2020), especially African nations, which accounted for only 2 % of publications. This underrepresentation may serve as a reflection of structural inequities in research capacity and funding accessibility, even though burnout is highly-prevalent in resource-limited settings.

Institutionally, 58 out of 1527 institutions met the productivity thresholds of at least five publications, with Universidad de Granada leading with 22 articles, and the University of Pennsylvania, Shandong University, and Universidad de Almería following closely with 20 and 17. Shandong University’s increased prominence post-2020 aligned with China’s strategic investments in healthcare workforce research following the pandemic (Fu et al., 2022). Strong collaborations between Spain and Chile reflected shared linguistic and cultural affinities, while the limited scope of broader international partnerships suggested ongoing barriers to cross-regional data sharing.

Analysis of author networks highlighted Cañadas-de la Fuente and Aiken as central figures, each contributing 13 articles. Their influence was bolstered by sustained institutional support and interdisciplinary collaboration. Foundational citations of Maslach (1483) and Schaufeli (32) underscored the enduring relevance of burnout frameworks, such as the Maslach Burnout Inventory (Gürsel and Turunç, 2022). However, sparse co-citation linkages indicated a fragmented theoretical integration across studies.

Journals such as Nursing (565 articles) and Environmental Sciences, which showed an emergence intensity of 8.72 post-2020, have contributed significantly to this field. This reflected an evolving focus from individual stressors to systemic drivers, including workplace violence (Laeeque et al., 2017) and environmental pressures (Hu et al., 2023). The emergence of interdisciplinary journals signifies a paradigm shift towards comprehensive solutions. This development indicates that research on nursing staff burnout is extending beyond the nursing field to explore multidimensional interaction mechanisms, thereby broadening the research perspective towards systemic and integrated management. Such an approach may have facilitated the establishment of interdisciplinary platforms, providing a solid research foundation for more effectively addressing pertinent research issues.

Thematic clusters in burnout research

Thematic analysis of 1466 keywords delineated seven major clusters that underpinned nursing staff burnout research. High-frequency terms, such as stress (263 occurrences), job satisfaction (211 occurrences), and care (181 occurrences), dominated the discourse, reflecting persistent concerns about workplace dynamics and caregiver well-being (Dobson et al., 2018). Temporal trends revealed a clear evolution: early studies (2004–2010) emphasized associations between mental health and patient safety, while mid-phase research (2013–2019) shifted toward predictive models of turnover intention and structural empowerment. Recent investigators (2020–2024) focused on systemic challenges, including hospital care quality and resource allocation, with intensified attention to intensive care and patient mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic (Baker et al., 2020).

Initial search terms included military nursing staff burnout and male nurse burnout. These themes were later grouped under broader categories, like workload impacts, indicating these populations required targeted analysis. Cluster analysis further highlighted empathic satisfaction and emotional exhaustion as interconnected themes, illustrating the dual role of psychological resilience and systemic stressors. Notably, keyword bursts in hardiness and psychiatric nurses (2018–2022) underscored emerging interest in individualized coping mechanisms and specialized nursing contexts. These clusters collectively mapped the field’s progression from descriptive symptomology to intervention-oriented frameworks (Safavi et al., 2023), emphasizing the need for holistic strategies that integrate individual (Xie et al., 2020), organizational, and systemic perspectives (Zaeri et al., 2020).

Our findings align with systematic reviews (Ladino et al., 2023) that note a shift from descriptive burnout studies to intervention-focused frameworks. However, unlike prior meta-analyses emphasizing emotional exhaustion (Zhou et al., 2018), we highlighted hardiness as an emerging protective factor, particularly in psychiatric nursing contexts. The surge in Brazilian publications parallels Lohmann et al. (2023), who reported on burnout crises in low- and middle-income countries, while contrasting with their findings on European burnout trends.

Geographic variations in conceptual frameworks

Global research efforts spanned 81 countries, with the U.S. (23 % of total publications), China (18 %), and Spain (12 %) leading in output. Collaborative networks revealed a dominant axis connecting these nations, supported by institutional partnerships, such as Spain’s Universidad de Granada, and in the U.S., the University of Pennsylvania. This finding aligns with the observation that countries with higher research productivity tend to establish more robust international collaborations, potentially reflecting greater recognition of the value of cooperative research in this field (Cai et al., 2023).

Geographic disparities in research foci were pronounced. Brazil’s recent surge in publications (highest intensity between 2020 and 2024) correlated with escalating nurse resignation rates linked to burnout (Du Plooy et al., 2010), while European studies prioritized structural empowerment and predictive models of occupational stress (Van Bogaert, 2023).

In contrast, Asian research, exemplified by China’s Shandong University (surge in publications since 2020), emphasized workload impacts and hospital care quality but exhibited limited international collaboration. Cluster analysis identified five region-specific conceptual frameworks: North America–patient safety and turnover intention; Europe–compassion satisfaction integrated into organizational interventions; Latin America–resource scarcity; Asia–psychiatric nursing challenges; and Australia–environmental stressors. Geographic disparities in research output reflected inequities in resource allocation, echoing the World Health Organization’s advocacy for low- and middle-income country-focused studies. Those variations reflected divergent healthcare infrastructures, cultural norms, and policy priorities, necessitating context-specific interventions to address burnout’s multifaceted drivers.

Burnout for the future

Despite significant advancements, critical gaps continue to persist. First, although compassion satisfaction and hardiness were acknowledged as protective factors (Ahmed and Amit, 2024), their interaction with systemic stressors—such as pandemic-induced workloads and staffing shortages—remains insufficiently explored. Second, low- and middle-income countries, particularly in Africa and South Asia, were notably underrepresented in the literature, despite evidence indicating severe burnout in resource-constrained settings (Poku et al., 2021). For instance, few publications originated from African institutions, despite high nurse attrition rates in regions like Sub-Saharan Africa (Workneh et al., 2020) Third, although interdisciplinary linkages, such as the role of environmental sciences in occupational health, remain in their infancy, their growing significance is evident in the increase in publications in Environmental Sciences Journals (emergence intensity: 8.72, 2020–2024). This trend highlights the potential benefits of incorporating environmental factors into occupational health frameworks to address systemic stressors more effectively.

Institutional analysis further reveals a reliance on Western frameworks, with limited integration of culturally- adaptive models. Future research should prioritize: (1) longitudinal studies on intervention efficacy, particularly in low- and middle-income countries; (2) cross-regional comparative analyses to disentangle cultural and structural influences; and (3) integration of emerging fields, like environmental psychology, to address burnout’s multifactorial nature. Strengthening partnerships with underrepresented regions and fostering open-access data platforms could democratize knowledge production and accelerate innovative solutions to this global challenge.

Strengths and limitations

We employed a variety of bibliometric analysis software to conduct a comprehensive examination of research on nursing staff burnout from a global perspective over the past 20 years. This analysis can assist researchers in understanding the development process and future trends in this field.

However, we have noted certain limitations, including: (a) Data. The data source for this study was relatively narrow, relying solely on the Web of Science and neglecting research results from other larger databases; (b) Language. In filtering these data, the language was restricted to English, thereby overlooking other of languages. Nonetheless, since most current high-quality journals require research results to be published in English, the impact of this limitation on the research findings is considered to be minimal; (c) Temporal scope: Our objective was completed in April 2024. To minimize potential bias arising from incomplete annual data, the analysis relied on datasets from January to April 2024, with annual totals projected by multiplying the collected data by three. Additionally, to enable a more comprehensive assessment of trends, a direct comparison of January–April data from the previous four years was conducted.

Conclusion

In summary, from our analysis of nursing staff burnout literature, we identified stress, job satisfaction, and caregiving as central research themes. In a recent trend (2019–2024), we highlighted increased focus on depression, anxiety, and workplace violence, underscoring evolving healthcare challenges. Future research priorities include longitudinal studies in underrepresented regions to contextualize burnout drivers, interdisciplinary approaches (e.g., environmental psychology) for holistic solutions, and role-specific investigations (e.g., emergency/intensive care nurses) to address subgroup stressors. These efforts are critical to advancing workforce resilience and patient outcomes.
