CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4 and SIRT7 gene expression in human cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma development and pre-clinical diagnosis
Authors: Karzan G Khidhir, Dana K Sabir, Abbas Salihi, Benjamin H Miranda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) is the second most frequent cause of skin cancer death after melanoma. An improved understanding of molecular mechanisms will likely contribute to the development of improved diagnostic and treatment strategies. We investigated changes in the expression of CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 in human cSCC tumor tissue versus matched normal skin, and assessed their potential for use as diagnostic biomarkers.MethodsStage T2 (AJCC-8) cSCC tumor and normal tissue were collected (n = 26), qPCR was performed to detect relative target gene expression, and ROC analysis was undertaken to investigate diagnostic power of these genes. The GENT2 and DepMap were used to compare target gene expression profiles in different human cancer tissues and cell lines, and a gene-gene interaction network was generated using GeneMANIA.ResultsThe qPCR indicated an increase in ITGA3 (2.2-fold) and SIRT7 (1.5 fold), and a decrease in CDKN2A (-1.1 fold) and SMAD4 (-0.75) expression, in cSCC versus matched normal skin. The gene-gene interaction network showed co-expression of CDKN2A and SMAD4 and physical interactions between ITGA3 and SIRT7. ROC analysis indicated that target gene expression could efficiently discriminate cSCC from normal skin, supporting their potential use as diagnostic molecular biomarkers.ConclusionsCDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4 and SIRT7 genes are involved in human cSCC development and have the potential for use as diagnostic molecular biomarkers; these findings should contribute to the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15918-2.Keywords: Molecular Biomarker, CDKN2A, cSCC, ITGA3, SMAD4, SIRT7

Introduction

Skin cancer is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy by organ type, with an estimated 2–3 million non-melanoma and 155,000 melanoma cases reported annually worldwide [1]. Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) represents approximately 20% of the global skin cancer burden, with more than one million new cases diagnosed each year in the United States alone [2, 3]. As a keratinocyte-derived carcinoma, cSCC is the second most common form of non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) following basal cell carcinoma (BCC) [4]. The global incidence of primary cSCC has increased by nearly 300% over the past three decades and is projected to double across Europe by 2030 [5, 6]. Early-stage, low-risk cSCCs are typically associated with excellent prognoses, with 5-year disease-free survival rates ranging from 95% to 99%. In contrast, high-risk cSCCs exhibit markedly poorer outcomes, with survival rates below 15%, making cSCC the second leading cause of skin cancer–related mortality after melanoma [7, 8].

The etiopathogenesis of cSCC involves multiple risk factors, including ultraviolet radiation exposure, chronic photoaging, male sex, fair skin phenotype, immunosuppression, smoking, and genetic predisposition [4, 9]. Various histopathological subtypes of cSCC have been characterized, each with distinct prognostic implications. Advances in non-invasive imaging techniques, such as reflectance confocal microscopy and dermoscopy, have significantly enhanced diagnostic accuracy [10]. The management of cSCC should be multidisciplinary and guided by evidence-based recommendations, such as those outlined by the British Association of Dermatologists [11]. Surgical excision with histologically confirmed disease-free margins remains the first-line treatment for resectable cSCC [11, 12]. Additional therapeutic modalities include Mohs micrographic surgery, radiotherapy (as primary, postoperative, or adjuvant therapy), and curettage with cautery [11]. For advanced, metastatic, or non-resectable disease, systemic chemotherapy may be considered; however, a growing understanding of cSCC molecular biology has facilitated the development of targeted therapies, such as the FDA-approved anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody, cemiplimab [13–16]. As treatment delays are strongly associated with disease progression, the establishment of effective strategies for early detection and timely intervention is crucial to improving patient outcomes [17].

Several molecular signaling pathways have been implicated in the pathogenesis of cSCC, which is recognized as one of the most highly mutated human cancers [18–21]. The accumulation of somatic mutations affects multiple signaling cascades, including activation of the NF-κB, MAPK, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways [22–24]. Mutations in the tumor suppressor gene TP53, primarily induced by ultraviolet radiation, represent early molecular alterations in cSCC development [25, 26]. Additional genetic aberrations involve other tumor suppressor genes, such as NOTCH and CDKN2A, as well as oncogenes including RAS [27–32].

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is clinically distinct from cSCC, as it arises from mucosal epithelium in the oral cavity, pharynx, and larynx, and is associated with risk factors such as exposure to tobacco-derived carcinogens and excessive alcohol consumption [33]. In recurrent oral SCC, loss of CDKN2A protein expression and downregulation of its gene expression have been reported [34]. The ITGA3 gene, which encodes the integrin alpha-3 subunit, has also been implicated in HNSCC development; inactivation of ITGA3 in experimental models suppresses cancer cell migration and invasion, supporting its functional relevance in tumor progression [35].

Moreover, SMAD4-mediated signaling has been shown to suppress skin tumorigenesis in mouse models through its interaction with the TGFβ/BMP and PTEN pathways [36, 37]. Loss of SMAD4 contributes to genomic instability and has been linked to the pathogenesis of several malignancies, including HNSCC [38, 39]. The TGFβ–SMAD signaling axis is therefore frequently investigated for mutations in its components during the carcinogenesis of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) [40, 41]. Additionally, the SIRT7 gene, which promotes SMAD4 deacetylation, has been found to be highly expressed in HNSCC, where it inhibits epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in oral SCC metastasis; however, its role in cSCC remains to be elucidated [42]. Recent genomic studies have identified several key driver mutations involved in the pathogenesis of cSCC. Among the most frequently altered genes are TP53, NOTCH1, NOTCH2, FAT1, and HRAS, which regulate critical processes such as cell cycle control, differentiation, and epidermal homeostasis [43].

The genes CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 were selected for this study because they represent key pathways involved in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) pathogenesis - cell cycle regulation, cell adhesion and invasion, intracellular signaling, and epigenetic control [27–42]. The CDKN2A gene encodes the tumour suppressor proteins p16INK4a and p14ARF, which together regulate cell-cycle progression via inhibition of CDK4/6 and stabilization of p53; loss of CDKN2A function disrupts these checkpoints and promotes uncontrolled keratinocyte proliferation [44]. ITGA3 encodes the α3 subunit of the α3β1 integrin complex, which mediates keratinocyte adhesion to the basement membrane and extracellular matrix; its dysregulation disrupts epithelial integrity and enhances tumour cell invasion and metastatic potential [45]. SMAD4 serves as a critical mediator of TGF-β signaling, enforcing growth arrest and maintaining genomic stability in epithelial cells; loss of SMAD4 impairs these tumor-suppressive functions and promotes tumor progression and chromosomal instability [46]. SIRT7, an NAD⁺-dependent histone deacetylase, regulates chromatin structure and transcriptional control, and its overexpression in squamous cell carcinomas has been associated with enhanced tumor cell survival and proliferative capacity [47].

Although several key genomic alterations in cSCC have been identified, the specific roles and clinical significance of CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 in cSCC development and early diagnosis remain poorly understood. A clearer understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving cSCC initiation and progression is therefore essential for identifying novel biomarkers and improving early detection and therapeutic strategies. The present study aims to examine the expression levels of CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 in human cSCC tumor tissue compared with normal skin. Additional objectives include exploring their expression across different cancerous tissues and cell lines, and evaluating their potential as diagnostic biomarkers for cSCC.

Methods

Tissue collection and ethical approval

This study was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the College of Science, University of Sulaimani (Approval No. 121 N), and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. cSCC tumor tissue and matched tumor-free margin samples from the temporal region were collected from 26 patients with histopathologically confirmed cSCC who underwent surgical excision. The cohort included 17 male patients (median age: 73 years) and 9 female patients (median age: 76 years).

Inclusion criteria comprised histopathological confirmation by a certified pathologist, sufficient biopsy material for analysis, and absence of concurrent dermatological or systemic malignancies. Based on clinical and pathological evaluation, all tumor specimens were classified as stage T2 according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Eighth Edition. The tumors were poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinomas measuring 2.0–3.5 cm in diameter, with no evidence of perineural or lymphovascular invasion.

All specimens were immediately immersed in RNAlater® stabilization solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Gillingham, UK) to prevent RNA degradation and subsequently stored at − 20 °C until processing.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Disruption and homogenisation of frozen cSCC and matched normal skin samples was performed using the Qiagen Tissue Lyser system, followed by RNA extraction using the RNeasy Plus Universal Mini Kit (Qiagen, Crawley, UK). Sample RNA concentrations were measured using a spectrophotometric NanoDrop instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Massachusetts, USA); RNA samples with A260/280 ratios greater than 1.8 and an A260/230 ratio range between 2.0 and 2.2, were selected for gene expression investigations to ensure that nucleic acids were pure and free from unwanted organic compounds [48]. RNA quality and integrity were confirmed via gel electrophoresis (1.5% agarose gel) and the appearance of two clear 18 S and 28 S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) bands. To remove any contaminating genomic DNA, RNA samples were treated with DNase I (Invitrogen, Paisley, UK), and complementary DNAs (cDNAs) were immediately synthesised using a QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, Crawley, UK). All gene expression studies were performed as per MIQE guidelines [49].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Gene-specific primers for CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 (Table 1) were newly designed based on the human gene sequences. The Ensembl Genome Browser was used to identify the specific genomic regions of each target gene, and primer pairs were selected from sequences spanning two consecutive exons to ensure amplification from complementary DNA (cDNA) rather than residual genomic DNA (CDKN2A: ENSG00000147889; ITGA3: ENSG00000005884; SMAD4: ENSG00000141646; SIRT7: ENSG00000187531). All primers were synthesised by Qiagen (Crawley, UK).

To confirm specificity, melt curve analysis was performed following each amplification, consistently yielding single, well-defined peaks for all primer sets. Primer efficiency was assessed by generating standard curves using serial cDNA dilutions; all efficiencies were within the acceptable range of 90–110%. These measures ensured the reliability and reproducibility of the qPCR data obtained in this study.

To determine the relative expression levels of CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4 and SIRT7 in cSCC tissue, qPCR was carried out according to MIQE Guidelines using a QuantiTect SYBR Green PCR kit (Qiagen, Crawley, UK; Cat. No. 204143) and real-time cycler instrument (Applied Biosystems Fast 7500; CA, USA). All reactions were performed in triplicate in 96-well plates. Each reaction mix was prepared in a 25 µl reaction volume containing 2x QuantiTect SYBR Green Master Mix (1x final concentration), 0.5µM forward and reverse primers (Table 1), 0.5 µl QuantiTect RT Mix and cDNA (< 500ng/reaction). The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: one initial activation cycle at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 15 s, annealing 50–60 °C for 30 s and extension 72 °C for 30 s. After each PCR cycle, fluorescence data were collected to generate an amplification plot to determine the Ct value (the number of cycles required for the fluorescent signal to cross the threshold) [50]. The delta-delta-CT (∆∆CT) method was used to determine relative expression of each gene [51].

Bioinformatics analysis of CDKNA, ITAG3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 in human cancer tissues

Gene expression analysis of CDKNA, ITAG3, SMAD4, SIRT7 in multiple human cancer tissues compared to normal tissues using GENT2

Expression patterns of CDKNA, ITAG3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 transcripts (mRNA) across a variety of normal and cancer human tissues were analysed using the Gene Expression database of Normal and Tumor tissues 2 (GENT2) [52]. GENT2 provides a search platform for gene expression patterns across different normal and cancer tissues gathered from public gene expression databases [53]. GENT2 employs Affymetrix microarray data that has been processed through MAS5 normalization. All expression values obtained from GENT2 are normalized and presented as log2-transformed intensities. GENT2 lacks specific data on cutaneous SCC; however, it provides expression profiles for various pan-cancer squamous carcinoma tissues, such as those from the esophagus, lung, and cervix, along with their corresponding normal tissues. GENT2 incorporates data from multiple GEO datasets; however, individual accession numbers cannot be explicitly specified due to the platform’s output of integrated results.

Relative expression of CDKNA, ITAG3, SMAD4, SIRT7 in different cancer-derived cell lines

Relative expression levels of CDKNA, ITAG3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 mRNA in different human cell lines derived from cancer tissues were compared using the DepMap portal [54]. The DepMap project uses a genome-wide pooled RNA library to identify gene essentiality across hundreds of genomically characterised cancer cell lines (A375, SK-MEL-28, HSC-1, HSC-5, SCC-25, SCC-4, COLO-16, A431, WM266-4) from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) project [55, 56].

Relative expression of CDKNA, ITAG3, SMAD4, SIRT7 in human skin cancer-derived cell lines

Relative mRNA expression levels of CDKNA, ITAG3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 in different skin cancer-derived cell lines, including melanoma, merkel cell carcinoma and SCC, were compared using the DepMap portal [54]. DepMap expression data (CCLE_RNAseq) is presented as log2(TPM + 1), which has been normalized, with no further transformation applied.

Analysis of gene-gene interaction using gene MANIA

The GeneMANIA server predicts gene function and generates data including gene co-expression, co-localisation, involved pathways and shared protein domains, was used to construct a gene-gene interaction network of CDKNA, ITAG3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 [57, 58]. The gene-gene interaction network results were then generated for CDKNA, ITAG3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 using human genome data.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, California, USA) and SPSS version [28] (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Gene expression levels were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between cSCC and matched normal skin samples, the paired t-test was used for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-parametric data. Multiple group comparisons, where applicable, were analysed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test.

Relative quantification (RQ) of mRNA expression was calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and data were obtained from independent biological replicates (n = 26).

The diagnostic performance of CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 expression levels in distinguishing cSCC from non-tumorous controls was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. For each gene, the area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to assess diagnostic accuracy [59]. The Youden Index was used to determine optimal cutoff points by maximizing the sum of sensitivity and specificity [60].

Due to the limited sample size, cross-validation and multiple testing corrections were not applied. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Relative expression of CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, SIRT7 in cSCC tissue using qPCR analysis

CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4 and SIRT7 gene expression levels were investigated in human cSCC tissue and matched normal tissue from the tumour-free margin using qPCR analysis. There were statistically significant differences in CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4 and SIRT7 relative gene expression in cSCC tissue versus matched normal tissue from the tumour-free margin. CDKN2A and SMAD4 relative gene expression were significantly down-regulated in cSCC tissue versus matched normal tissue (CDKN2A 95% CI, 0.382–0.518; P = 0.01; SMAD4 95% CI, 0.489–0.774, P = 0.04; Fig. 1A), with a relative fold gene expression (log2) of-1.1 fold and − 0.75 fold, respectively (Fig. 1B). ITGA3 and SIRT7 relative gene expression were significantly up-regulated in cSCC tissue versus normal tissue (ITGA3 95% CI, 2.643–5.871,P = 0.01; SIRT7 95% CI, 2.052–3.897, P = 0.03; Fig. 1A), with a relative fold gene expression (log2) of 2.2 fold and 1.5 fold respectively (Fig. 1B).

Diagnostic values of CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, SIRT7mRNA expression in cSCC

The diagnostic potential of CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4 and SIRT7 mRNA expression for cSCC was assessed by ROC analysis. The ROC curve illustrated that CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4 and SIRT7 mRNA expression could efficiently discriminate cSCC from normal skin tissue (CDKN2A AUC = 0.913; ITGA3 AUC = 0.910; SMAD4 AUC = 0.905; SIRT7 AUC = 0.900; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). ROC analysis demonstrated optimal diagnostic cut-off values as follows: CDKN2A = 0.7692RQU, ITGA3 = 0.6538RQU, SMAD4 = 0.7308RQU and SIRT7 = 0.6923RQU; associated sensitivities for these cut-off values were 96%, 98%, 77%, 92%, and specificities were 80%, 65%, 93%, 77%, for CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4 and SIRT7 respectively.

Discussion

Several genes and signaling pathways have been implicated in the development of human cSCC [20, 21, 26, 63, 64]. Mutations in tumor suppressor genes such as TP53, CDKN2A, NOTCH, p16INK4a, and p14ARF, as well as oncogenic activation of RAS, and upregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin and EGFR pathways, have been reported [21, 65]. Understanding the molecular basis of cSCC is essential for identifying novel therapeutic and diagnostic strategies, as has been achieved in other malignancies such as head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) through molecularly targeted therapies [66–68].

In this study, we analyzed the expression of CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 in human cSCC compared with matched normal skin to assess their potential as diagnostic biomarkers. Quantitative real-time PCR revealed that ITGA3 and SIRT7 were significantly upregulated, while CDKN2A and SMAD4 were significantly downregulated in cSCC. Among these, ITGA3 exhibited the highest relative expression, followed by SIRT7. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the expression levels of these genes could reliably distinguish cSCC from normal skin with high sensitivity and specificity, supporting their potential as candidate biomarkers for early detection.

A functional association network revealed co-expression of CDKN2A and SMAD4, both downregulated in cSCC, and a physical interaction between ITGA3 and SIRT7, both upregulated, suggesting shared regulatory or signaling pathways. These findings are consistent with expression data from GENT2 and DepMap, where ITGA3 and SIRT7 were elevated across several human cancer tissues, while CDKN2A and SMAD4 showed reduced levels, further reinforcing their possible roles in tumorigenesis.

Mechanistically, CDKN2A regulates the cell cycle by encoding p16INK4a and p14ARF, key inhibitors of cyclin-dependent kinases, thereby preventing uncontrolled keratinocyte proliferation and promoting senescence. Loss or inactivation of CDKN2A through promoter hypermethylation, deletion, or mutation has been widely associated with tumor development, including in cSCC [69–74]. The observed downregulation in our study is consistent with such mechanisms and may reflect epigenetic silencing.

SMAD4, a central mediator of the TGF-β signaling pathway, plays an essential role in epithelial homeostasis and growth inhibition. Loss of SMAD4 disrupts TGF-β–mediated transcriptional control, contributing to uncontrolled proliferation, migration, and chemoresistance. Previous studies have demonstrated recurrent TGFBR1 and TGFBR2 mutations, SMAD2 copy number alterations, and frequent loss of SMAD4 protein in cSCC [36, 39, 62, 63, 75, 76]. The downregulation of SMAD4 in our dataset supports its tumor suppressor role and may contribute to cSCC pathogenesis by promoting epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and invasion.

ITGA3 encodes integrin α3, a transmembrane receptor involved in cell adhesion, migration, and extracellular matrix interactions. Aberrant expression of ITGA3 has been reported in various epithelial cancers and is often associated with poor prognosis and enhanced metastatic potential [37, 77]. Its significant upregulation in cSCC in our study suggests a role in tumor invasion and progression. Future studies should investigate whether ITGA3 overexpression correlates with cSCC metastasis and clinical outcomes.

SIRT7 belongs to the sirtuin family of NAD⁺- dependent deacetylases and regulates chromatin remodeling, rRNA transcription, and stress responses. Altered SIRT7 expression has been observed in several cancers, including HNSCC [42, 78, 79]. Interestingly, while SIRT7 downregulation has been noted in metastatic oral SCC, our findings indicate its upregulation in cSCC, which may reflect its involvement in early tumor development or UV-induced DNA damage responses. Further mechanistic studies are needed to clarify its precise role in cSCC biology.

Limitations and future works

The ROC analysis indicates that CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 exhibit promising sensitivity and specificity for distinguishing cSCC from normal skin. However, the modest sample size (n = 26) limits statistical power and generalizability. Cross-validation and multiple testing corrections were not applied, which is acknowledged as a limitation. Inclusion of confidence intervals for AUC values provides a measure of precision, yet validation in larger, independent cohorts is required to confirm diagnostic accuracy. We also acknowledge the absence of protein-level validation (e.g., immunohistochemistry or Western blot) and the lack of an independent cohort due to limited sample availability. Future studies integrating transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, as well as functional assays, would help validate the expression patterns observed and strengthen the diagnostic significance of these genes.

Analyses using publicly available datasets such as GENT2 and DepMap offer valuable context but are subject to inherent limitations, including sample heterogeneity and batch effects. We minimized these issues by using normalized, log₂-transformed data and restricting comparisons to skin SCC and normal skin where possible. Nevertheless, GENT2 integrates data from various SCC subtypes and tissue origins; thus, findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future work employing cSCC-specific, well-annotated transcriptomic–proteomic datasets will further refine these insights.

Overall, our findings suggest that altered expression of CDKN2A, ITGA3, SMAD4, and SIRT7 may serve as early molecular indicators of malignant transformation in keratinocytes. Given their roles in cell cycle control, adhesion, signal transduction, and chromatin remodeling, these genes may contribute to key processes in cSCC tumorigenesis. From a diagnostic perspective, they hold potential for distinguishing cSCC from benign or premalignant lesions, thereby enhancing early detection and patient stratification. Future studies incorporating larger, longitudinal cohorts and integrative molecular analyses are warranted to validate these preliminary findings and explore their prognostic and therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that ITGA3 and SIRT7 are significantly up-regulated in human cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC), whereas CDKN2A and SMAD4 are down-regulated. The mRNA expression profiles of these genes show potential as molecular biomarkers for the pre-clinical diagnosis of cSCC. Observations from previous studies in other cancers, including the clinically distinct entity HNSCC, are consistent with our findings, supporting the notion that these genes contribute to cSCC pathogenesis and progression.

By revealing distinct expression patterns in early-stage, non-metastatic cSCC, our results suggest that these genes may be involved in the initial stages of tumor development. Similar to advances in targeted therapies in HNSCC- such as EGFR inhibitors (Cetuximab, Panitumumab, Zalutumumab), EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (Gefitinib, Erlotinib), and VEGFR inhibitors (Bevacizumab, Vandetanib) [66, 67, 80–85] - our findings enhance understanding of the genetic basis of cSCC. They also provide a foundation for the development of improved diagnostic, preventive, and therapeutic strategies for this prevalent skin cancer.
