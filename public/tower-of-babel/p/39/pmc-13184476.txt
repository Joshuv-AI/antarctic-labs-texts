Author's reply to the Letters regarding “Failure analysis and design improvement of retrieved plates from revision surgery”
Authors: Ning-Ze Zhang, Ling Qin
Published: 2026

We thank Dr. Lee for his patient reading and insightful comments on our study regarding the design improvement of retrieved plates [1] from the clinical and translational perspectives. He raised methodological questions, particularly concerning the ability of the Magnesium (Mg) block's potential to prevent implant failure prior to clinical translation. These questions pertain to the biomechanical performance of the proposed plate and the biological aspects of Mg degradation.

In our published work [1], plate fracture was the definitive outcome for all 49 plates retrieved from revision surgery. As is well understood, plate fracture is ultimately caused by crack propagation under mechanical loading, although many indirect factors may also contribute. Therefore, the focus of our study is on the biomechanical analysis and improvement of the failed plates. A common traditional plate (TP) (Naton, Beijing, China) was reconstructed to serve as the control group. The central enhancement plate (CEP) is another commercial design that augments structural strength near the bone fracture line. While effective, this design exacerbates stress concentrations, particularly around screw holes, creating potential fracture initiation sites under both compression and bending. This study induced the Mg block with a thickness of 1.5 mm to plate (Mg-CEP-1.5 mm) to improve the biomechanical performance of the CEP by decreasing the stress and stiffness. The Mg-CEP-1.5 mm showed stress reductions of 3.24%, 3.73%, 3.27%, and 16.29% under 600 N compression, 1500 N compression, rotation, and bending, respectively, compared to the CEP. Its construct stiffness was also reduced by 7.54%. Compared to the traditional plate (TP), the maximum stress increased by 1.13% and 0.52% under loads of 600 N and 1500 N, respectively, while decreasing by 4.95% and 10.41% during rotation and bending, demonstrating the non-inferiority of Mg-CEP-1.5 mm to the commercial plate.

Referring to the S–N curve for titanium alloy fatigue, reducing stress directly extends fatigue life. Thus, the mechanical enhancements in Mg-CEP-1.5 mm could prolong in-service performance, allowing more time for bone healing compared with CEP. As Dr. Lee noted, classical interpretations of Perren's strain theory suggest that optimal fracture healing occurs with 2-10% interfragmentary strain, although bone union can also be achieved with strains exceeding 10%. This study did not calculate the actual interfragmentary displacement, and the lower stiffness of Mg-CEP-1.5 mm compared to the commercial CEP design suggests that it may induce higher strain, which could promote fracture healing. Furthermore, it is known that interfragmentary motion is smaller on the side of the bone beneath the plate [2]. The degradation of Mg first affects the injured bone beneath the plate and may compensate for the discrepancy caused by uneven strain. For the clinical translation of Mg-CEP-1.5 mm, fatigue testing will be conducted in the future to provide solid evidence.

The biological mechanism of Mg degradation, which promotes bone regeneration, has been validated in our series of studies [[3], [4], [5]]. In our previous study, a Mg rod (99.99 % purity) similar to that discussed in the current paper was inserted into a hollow intramedullary nail (IMN). Mg degradation products, including hydrogen gas (H2), Mg2+ ions, and OH− ions, were predominantly localized around the fracture gap during the early healing phase (within 15 days post-fracture), a period corresponding to soft callus formation [6]. Furthermore, by 6 weeks, the Mg group exhibited greater bone formation in the fracture gap compared to the control group at 8 weeks, indicating an accelerated progression through the callus formation and remodeling phases [4]. The H2 was detectable from day 3 to 12 post-fracture, with no signal present by day 15. The pH dynamics in the Mg-IMN group mirrored H2 release, with peaks at pH 8.5 on day 3 and 9, and a return to neutrality thereafter [6]. Local alkalization causes Ca2+ and PO43− in body fluids to precipitate, forming a hydroxyapatite-like corrosion layer on the metal surface [7], that may act as a protective barrier to slow degradation. This finding is supported by our involvement in a related in vivo human trial using pure Mg screws for fixation of the bone flap in patients with osteonecrosis of the femoral head [8], in which the proposed adverse effects were also not observed. In this study, a portion of the titanium alloys at the hole's position in the plate was replaced by a pure Mg block to enhance the biomechanical performance of plates by reducing stiffness and to potentially stimulate a biological response via Mg block degradation. The fundamental concept is that the Mg block serves as a non-load-bearing component of the plate, meaning that its degradation does not affect the plate's stability. We found that the primary cause of plate fracture in clinical practice is fatigue failure due to delayed union or nonunion. This highlights the core challenge: achieving bone union is paramount. Selecting a stronger, non-bioactive material, such as Ti6Al4V, just provides a greater safety margin and longer in-service time for healing.

We appreciate the recommendation from Dr. Lee. We will provide more robust evidence to support the clinical translation and regulatory approval of this promising concept in our future work. Meanwhile, collective efforts towards future clinical translation are essential to validate our compelling preclinical discoveries and innovations [9].
