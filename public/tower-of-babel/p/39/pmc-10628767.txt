Cystic echinococcosis in the abdominal wall
Authors: Niccolò Ronzoni, Nadia Galizzi, Francesca Tamarozzi
Published: 2023

Introduction

Cystic echinococcosis (CE) is a zoonosis caused by the larval stage of Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato.1 Human infection is prevalent in livestock-raising areas, especially in the Mediterranean area, North and East Africa, Latin America, Central Asia and West China.2 Infection mainly involves the liver, but any organ and tissue can be affected.3 Solitary extrahepatic localization is uncommon and probably underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed.3

Case report

A 34-year-old man, born and living in North Macedonia until 2014, came to the attention of the Infectious Diseases (ID) unit of ‘Papa Giovanni XXIII Hospital’ in Bergamo, Italy, in June 2022.

In December 2021 he complained of a painless swelling of the left flank developed after a physical exertion. He was afebrile and without other clinical remarks. An magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Figure 1A) described three masses involving the left psoas and iliac muscles, the largest of 19 × 10 cm, with ‘proteinaceous-mucinous’ content, ‘consistent with a mesenchymal mass’. He was referred to an oncological hospital in April 2022, where the mass was biopsied. After the procedure, he reported weeks-long leakage from the cutaneous fistula of liquid/pseudo-solid material, sometimes with solid components having ‘the consistency of rubber bands’.

Imaging of the lesion at diagnosis and follow-up. (A) T2-weighted MRI obtained at diagnosis, showing two of the three masses with hyperintense content, serpiginous hypointense internal structures (ball-of-wool pattern formed by the detached parasitic layers) and small strongly hyperintense round areas (daughter cyst; arrow). (B) Ultrasonography obtained after the repeated drainages in the absence of albendazole, showing a hypoechoic mass superficial to the psoas muscle, containing multiple sub-centimetric round anechoic structures (daughter cysts; arrow). (C) Follow-up ultrasound after 1 month of treatment with albendazole, showing the hypoechoic mass superficial to the psoas muscle without daughter cysts. (D) T2-weighted MRI obtained after the repeated drainage at the start of albendazole treatment, showing a smaller mass (arrow) with hyperintense content and serpiginous hypointense internal structures (ball-of-wool pattern) formed by the detached and folded parasitic layers. No daughter cyst was visible, nor signs of dissemination.

The histological exam ruled out malignancies, and the patient was referred to the ID unit of ‘Papa Giovanni XXIII Hospital’ in Bergamo, on the suspicion of abscess. Percutaneous aspirations were carried out in July and October 2022. Cultures (bacteria, mycobacteria, fungi), microscopy and Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) for Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, and QuantiFERON-TB, were negative. The patient kept being afebrile and with normal blood cell count and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels. Serology for echinococcosis was requested, resulting positive (CLIA [Vircell], Western Blot [LDBIO] and Immunochromatography [Vircell]).

The patient was referred to the Department of Infectious-Tropical Diseases and Microbiology of Istituto di Ricerca e Cura a Carattere Scientifico (IRCCS) Sacro Cuore Don Calabria Hospital, Negrar, Verona, Italy. A re-evaluation of the initial MRI images evidenced, in T2-weighted sequences, intra-lesional sub-centimetric hyperintense round areas and hypointense ‘ball of wool’-like4 pattern (Figure 1A). On ultrasound, the remaining hypoechoic lesion contained a few centimetric, round, anechoic images (Figure 1B). Since no other localizations were present, a diagnosis of primary CE of the psoas muscle in CE3b stage (World Health Organization - Informal Working Groups on Echinococcosis [WHO-IWGE] classification4) was made. Treatment with albendazole 400 mg twice daily was started, scheduled continuously for 12 months. After 1 month, on US the daughter cysts had regressed (Figure 1C) and an MRI ruled out abdominal and osseous secondary CE (Figure 1D). The patient is still on treatment and is undergoing regular follow-up. This conservative approach was chosen to evaluate dissemination, prior to envisaging surgery. This will be considered in case of reactivation of the psoas cyst and no dissemination.

Conclusion

Although imaging signs of CE were described for liver cysts,4 they should raise the suspicion of CE when visualized in extra-hepatic cysts. In our case, since primary CE of retroperitoneum/psoas is rare,5 prioritized diagnoses were neoplasm and tubercular abscess, despite the CE-typical aspect on MRI. Serology is often negative in unruptured extra-hepatic CE,3 and is therefore unreliable to rule-out infection; therefore, in patients from CE-endemic areas, diagnostic biopsies must be performed under albendazole intake, to prevent CE dissemination, representing a potentially devastating complication of invasive procedures without correct prophylaxis.
