M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes ameliorate temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis via autophagy
Authors: Guoliang Sa, Zhongyang Zou, Haoyu Zhou, Guodong Sa, Jing Zhou, Hanlin Tu, Yingliang Shi, Yuyan He, Yi Guo, Zhan Liu, Xuewen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe interaction between synovial cells and chondrocytes plays a pivotal role in the progression of osteoarthritis. However, the contribution of migrasomes, recently identified vesicular organelles mediating intercellular communication, remains inadequately explored in this context. Here, we first confirmed the presence of migrasomes in both synovial tissues and synovial fluid samples from patients with temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJ-OA). Integrative analyses, combining single-cell transcriptomics with in vitro validation, identified infiltrating M1 macrophages as one of the major sources of synovial migrasomes. Notably, M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes alleviated TNF-α-induced chondrocyte apoptosis, reduced proteoglycan depletion, and suppressed the expression of catabolic enzymes. Further analyses revealed that these migrasomes were enriched in autophagy-related proteins, implicating a link between migrasomes and autophagy. Using a synovium-cartilage organoids co-culture model (“Mini-TMJ”), we observed that blocking migrasome transfer from the synovial to the cartilage organoid led to reduced autophagy activation in the cartilage organoid under monosodium iodoacetate-induced inflammatory conditions. Conversely, exogenous addition of M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes into the cartilage organoid restored autophagy activity. In a rat TMJ-OA model, intra-articular injection of M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes effectively attenuated cartilage degradation and promoted autophagy activation. Collectively, our findings indicate that M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes ameliorate TMJ-OA progression by enhancing autophagy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04248-x.Keywords: Migrasome, M1 macrophage, Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis, Synovium-cartilage organoids, Autophagy

Introduction

TMJ-OA is a chronic degenerative joint disorder characterized by synovitis, cartilage degeneration, and subchondral bone remodeling [1]. With global prevalence estimates ranging from 8% to 16%, TMJ-OA exhibits a marked female predominance and tends to progress with age [2, 3]. Clinically, this condition manifests as joint and muscle pain, masticatory dysfunction and limited mouth opening, all of which severely impair patients’ quality of life and impose a significant socioeconomic burden [4]. While current therapeutic approaches primarily aim to suppress inflammation and delay structural degradation of the joint complex, they remain insufficient in promoting regenerative repair of the damage [5, 6]. Therefore, understanding the regulatory mechanisms underlying TMJ-OA pathogenesis is crucial for identifying potential therapeutic targets that can effectively halt disease progression.

Current research has firmly established that the progression of OA is driven by the synergistic crosstalk between synovial macrophages and fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs) [7]. Under inflammatory conditions, these cells create a catabolic microenvironment by secreting soluble factors, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), which directly degrade the cartilage matrix [8]. Concurrently, extracellular vesicle (EV)-mediated communication plays a pivotal role [9]. For instance, exosomes derived from activated FLSs have been shown to exacerbate inflammation by enhancing macrophage glycolysis via HIF-1α accumulation [10], while M1 macrophage-derived EVs deliver specific cargoes, such as miR-1246, to trigger chondrocyte dysfunction [11].

However, current research has predominantly focused on soluble cytokines and classical extracellular vesicles (such as exosomes), which originate from multivesicular bodies or plasma membrane budding. Given that synovitis involves the extensive recruitment and active migration of immune cells [12], this focus may overlook critical signaling events that are specifically coupled to cellular motility. Migrasomes are newly discovered organelles that address this gap. Mechanistically distinct from the aforementioned exosomes and microvesicles, migrasomes form specifically at the tips or intersections of retraction fibers at the rear of migrating cells [13, 14]. With diameters ranging from 0.5 to 3 μm, they differ significantly from small EVs in both biogenesis and physical characteristics. Importantly, proteomic evidence has confirmed their molecular distinctiveness. For instance, macrophage-derived migrasomes share only ~ 27% of their protein content with exosomes, indicating that they represent a unique class of vesicles capable of delivering distinct bioactive cargo [15].

Functionally, migrasomes act as critical carriers for bioactive materials, facilitating intercellular communication by delivering their contents to recipient cells [16, 17]. Unlike exosomes that diffuse rapidly in biofluids, migrasomes can adhere to the extracellular matrix or be taken up by recipient cells, facilitating spatially precise communication [18]. Emerging evidence has highlighted their roles in various physiological and pathological processes. For instance, in the context of tissue regeneration, macrophage-derived migrasomes were shown to enrich CXCL12 to recruit mesenchymal stem cells [19]. Notably, migrasomes also assist parent cells in maintaining cellular quality control through “mitocytosis,” a process where damaged mitochondria are ejected via migrasomes to preserve metabolic homeostasis [20, 21]. This unique capacity to encapsulate complex cargo, ranging from cytosolic proteins to entire organelles, suggests that migrasomes may exert potent regulatory effects on recipient cell metabolism and fate. Despite the established link between synovitis and the extensive migration of immune cells, the specific contribution of migrasomes to osteoarthritis remains unexplored. In this study, we hypothesized that the migratory behavior of M1 macrophages in the inflamed synovium leads to the generation of migrasomes, which subsequently serve as key mediators in regulating chondrocyte fate.

Materials and methods

Transmission electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy was employed to examine ultra-thin sections of synovial tissue and negatively stained migrasomes. Synovial specimens were first trimmed into 1 mm × 1 mm × 3 mm blocks using ophthalmic scissors and immediately fixed in electron microscopy fixative (G1102, Servicebio, CN). The samples were subsequently post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (18456, Ted Pella Inc, USA) at room temperature for 2 h, followed by stepwise dehydration through an ethanol gradient (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 95%, and two 100% rinses). Dehydrated specimens were embedded in Epon 812 resin, followed by polymerization at 60 °C for 48 h. 70-nm ultra-thin sections were collected on 150-mesh copper grids (WFHM-150, Servicebio, CN) for TEM analysis. For migrasome visualization, purified samples were adsorbed onto 200-mesh copper grids for 5–10 min. After removing excess liquid with filter paper, specimens were counterstained with 2% uranyl acetate solution. All observations were conducted using a Hitachi HT7700 transmission electron microscope (Japan).

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data processing and analysis

The scRNA-seq dataset was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE267942 [22]. This dataset includes TMJ tissues from healthy control mice and mice with Complete Freund’s Adjuvant (CFA)-induced arthritis. Downstream analysis was performed using the Seurat R package (version 5.3.1). For quality control, stringent filtering criteria were applied to remove low-quality cells and potential doublets. Cells expressing fewer than 300 genes were excluded from the analysis. The filtered data were normalized and variance-stabilized using the SCTransform method. To correct for batch effects and integrate the Control and CFA datasets, the Harmony functions in Seurat were employed. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the integrated dataset. The top 13 principal components (PCs) were selected for dimensionality reduction using the Uniform Manifold. Cell clustering was performed using the FindClusters function with a resolution parameter of 0.8. Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function with a log-fold change (logFC) threshold of 0.25 and a minimum percentage (min.pct) cutoff of 0.25 (genes expressed in at least 25% of cells in the cluster). Cell types were annotated based on the expression of canonical marker genes: Endothelial cells (Flt1, Kdr, Pecam1), Fibroblasts (Dcn, Col3a1, Igfbp6), Erythrocytes (Hba-a1, Car2, Slc4a1), Neutrophils (S100a8, Retnlg, Mmp8), Schwann cells (Plp1, Mbp, Mpz), Osteocytes (Col1a1, Bglap, Runx2), B cells (Cd79a, Vpreb3, Ighm), Muscle cells (Myh11, Acta2, Tpm2), Chondrocytes (Col2a1, Acan, Thbs4), Macrophages (C1qa, C1qb, C1qc), and Lymphatic endothelial cells (Lyve1, Prox1, Pdpn) [22].

To evaluate the migrasome-forming potential across different cell populations, a “Migrasome Score” was calculated for each cell using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA). The gene signature was defined based on key genes essential for migrasome biogenesis: Tspan4, Cpq, Ndst1, Pigk, Eogt, Itgb1, and Itga5. The distribution of Migrasome Scores was visualized on UMAP plots to compare the migrasome-generating capacity between the Control and CFA groups.

THP-1 cell culture and macrophage differentiation

THP-1 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (GiBco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, C04002, Vivacell, Germany), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P1400, Solarbio, CN) at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. For differentiation into M0 macrophages, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well in 6-well plates (approximately, 1 × 105 cells/cm2) and treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, HY-18738, MCE, USA) for 48 h; adherent cells were then gently washed twice with PBS and incubated in fresh complete medium for an additional 24 h. M1 polarization was achieved by stimulating M0 macrophages with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, HY-D1056, MCE, USA) and 20 ng/mL interferon-gamma (IFN-γ, HY-P7025, MCE, USA) for 48 h.

Isolation and culture of primary human fibroblast-like synoviocytes

Synovial tissues were aseptically harvested from surgical specimens of patients with condylar process hypertrophy, minced into 1–2 mm³ fragments in ice-cold PBS containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and digested with 0.2% type I collagenase (BS163, Biosharp, CN) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, C11995500BT, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C until the tissue fragments were fully dispersed and the solution became cloudy with single cells, leaving no macroscopic clumps. After centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min) and PBS washing, tissue fragments were evenly distributed in culture dishes, covered with a minimal volume of DMEM medium containing 20% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Upon partial adherence (4–6 h), additional medium was gently added to fully cover the fragments. FLSs outgrowth was monitored for 7–10 days, with medium replenishment every 2–3 days. At 80% confluence, cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, USA), and FLS were purified over 3–5 passages. To assess contamination from other synovial cell types, cell purity was evaluated at Passage 1 via immunofluorescence staining. Cells were identified as FLSs based on positive expression of Vimentin and negative expression of the macrophage marker CD68. Only cultures exhibiting > 95% purity were utilized for subsequent experiments.

Migrasome internalization assay

To investigate migrasome internalization, migrasomes were first labeled with WGA (green, 5 µg/ml) in a total volume of 200 µL at 37 °C for 15 min. The labeled migrasomes were then co-cultured with chondrocytes for 4 h to allow for cellular uptake. Following incubation, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, and then stained with phalloidin (for cytoskeleton) and DAPI (for nuclei). Samples were washed three times with PBS and observed using a confocal microscope (FV1200, Olympus, Japan) to analyze migrasome distribution in the chondrocyte cytoplasm.

TNF-α-induced in vitro TMJ-OA model

As previously described [23], the rat mandibular condylar cartilage was isolated from neonatal SD rats (within 24 h post-birth) at the condylar bone-cartilage junction under stereomicroscope guidance. Tissues were digested with 0.2% type II collagenase (BS164, Biosharp, CN) at 37 °C for 3–4 h, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer (Biosharp, Corning) to remove undigested debris, and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min to terminate digestion. The pellet was resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS and seeded into T25 flasks. Primary cells (P0) were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. At 80% confluence, P0 cells were subcultured at a 1:2 ratio, with P2-P6 cells utilized for subsequent experiments.

Rat chondrocytes were treated with TNF-α at a concentration of 100 ng/mL for 24 h. After inflammatory stimulation, the treatment group cells were serum-starved in 2% FBS for 2 h, followed by treatment with 10 µg/mL of M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes or exosomes for 24 h. In the control group, chondrocytes were left untreated.

Animal experiments

All Sprague-Dawley rats were purchased from the Hubei Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention. As previously described [24], 12 rats (male, aged 3-4weeks) were randomly assigned to 3 experimental groups and administered bilateral intra-articular injections into TMJ as follows: Control group (4 rats): 50 µl PBS per joint, MIA group (4 rats): 0.5 mg MIA dissolved in 50 µl PBS per joint, MIA + Mig group (4 rats): 0.5 mg MIA combined with 50 µg migrasomes co-suspended in 50 µl PBS per joint. The animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions, with a temperature of 22 ± 1 °C, humidity maintained at 55 ± 5%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Standard rodent chow and water were provided ad libitum throughout the study. Before injection, all intra-articular injections were performed under general anesthesia to minimize pain and ensure accurate needle placement. Specifically, rats were anesthetized using 2% isoflurane inhalation and intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). After injection, animals were monitored weekly for the presence and severity of TMJ swelling, appetite loss, and changes in body weight. All animals were euthanized 56 days after intra-articular injection. Following euthanasia, micro-CT imaging was performed, and TMJ tissues were subsequently collected for further analysis.

Isolation of migrasomes from M1 macrophages

THP-1 cells were seeded into T-225 flasks, stepwise induced to differentiate into M1 macrophages, and then cultured for an additional 48 h. As previous described [13, 25], cells and migrasomes were released by digestion with 0.25% trypsin, collected into 50 mL tubes, and all subsequent steps were performed at 4 °C. Sequential centrifugations were performed to isolate crude migrasomes: 1000 × g for 10 min (removing cells/debris), 4000 × g for 20 min, and 20,000 × g for 45 min, followed by two PBS washes. Crude migrasomes were purified via Optiprep density gradient centrifugation (LYSISO1, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 150,000 × g for 4 h at 4 °C using an SW32 Ti rotor (Beckman). Fractions (1 mL each) were collected, mixed with PBS (1:1), and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min. Pellets were washed twice with PBS, quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010S, Beyotime, CN), and stored at -80 °C. Migrasome identity was validated by TEM, Western blot and NTA.

Isolation of exosomes from M1 Macrophages

THP-1 cells were seeded into T-225 flasks and stepwise induced to differentiate into M1 macrophages. The medium was replaced with exosome-depleted serum-free RPMI-1640, followed by an additional 48 h of culture. Conditioned medium was collected after 48 h and sequentially centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min (removing cells), 2,000 × g for 15 min (eliminating large debris), and 20,000 × g for 30 min (clarifying medium). Exosomes were pelleted via ultracentrifugation (Optima L-100XP, Beckman Coulter, USA) at 120,000 × g for 120 min, washed twice with PBS by repeating ultracentrifugation at 120,000 × g for 120 min, and resuspended in sterile PBS. Exosome concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010S, Beyotime, CN) and nanoparticle tracking analysis. Purified exosomes were stored at -80 °C for downstream use.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

The numbers and sizes of M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes and exosomes were measured using nanoparticle tracking analysis with a ZetaView system (PMX-120, Particle Metrix, Germany). The instrument was calibrated with 100 nm polystyrene beads. The batch process in the NTA software was employed to determine the nanoparticle concentrations, expressed as particles per mL.

Construction of synovial organoid

96-well plates were washed three times with PBS. Molecular biology-grade 2% (w/v) agarose (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in PBS was boiled and pipetted (100 µL/well) prior to solidification. Plates were tilted to coat walls with agarose, inverted to remove excess, and air-dried. Fibroblast-like synoviocytes and THP-1 cells were pre-incubated in synovial medium (DMEM/GlutaMAX with 20% FBS, 1% NEAA, 1% ITS+, 100 ng/mL PMA, and 40 µg/mL L-proline). A mixture of 2 × 10⁶ fibroblast-like synoviocytes and 0.5 × 10⁶ THP-1 cells in 200 µL medium was seeded into agarose-coated wells. Media (400 µL total) was replenished twice daily (200 µL per change).

Construction of cartilage organoid

The ESC-H9 was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). ESC-H9 cells were maintained in mTeSR1 medium (Stem cell) on Matrigel-coated plates at 37 °C/5% CO₂, with daily medium changes and passaging using 0.5 mM EDTA at 80% confluency. Colonies were dissociated with Accutase, resuspended in EB medium (DMEM/F12, 20% FBS, 1% NEAA, 1% GlutaMAX, 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol), and seeded into AggreWell plates (1 × 10⁴ cells/mL; 1,200 wells/plate). Aggregates were centrifuged (100× g, 3 min) and cultured for 4 days with medium replacement every 48 h. Embryoid bodies (EBs) were transferred to chondrogenic medium (DMEM-high glucose, 1% ITS, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 10⁻⁷ M dexamethasone, 10 ng/mL TGF-β3, 10 ng/mL BMP-4) in ultra-low attachment 6-well plates, with medium refreshed every 3 days over 21 days.

ESC-H9-derived chondrocytes were suspended in chondrogenic medium. To ensure consistent organoid diameter and uniformity across experiments, a total of 2.5 × 10⁶ chondrocytes in 200 µL chondrogenic medium were seeded per standardized agarose-coated well [23]. Over 7–10 days, cells within these defined geometrical confines self-assembled into scaffold-free condylar cartilage organoid constructs at the well bottom. The medium was replenished twice daily with 200 µL fresh medium per exchange (total 400 µL/day).

Co-culture of synovial organoid and cartilage organoid

Biocompatible polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) cast into a 12-well plate-compatible frame. A central vertical slot houses interchangeable filtration membranes (3-µm and 0.2-µm pore sizes), dividing the well into two symmetrical chambers with a total height matching the 12-well plate. Synovial organoid and cartilage organoid were seeded in opposing chambers. Other procedures are as mentioned above. Purified M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes were added at a protein equivalent concentration of 10 µg/mL.

Label-free quantitative mass spectrometry

After extracting the M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes and exosomes using the previously mentioned method, add 300 µL of 8 M urea (Amresco, M123-1KG) for sonication. Centrifuge at 14,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, then use a Bradford protein assay kit to quantify the protein in the supernatant. Take 100 µg of protein and add dithiothreitol (DTT, Amresco, M109-5G) to a final concentration of 10 mM. Incubate at 37 °C for 1 h, then return to room temperature. Add iodoacetamide (IAM, Amresco, M216-30G) to a final concentration of 40 mM, and incubate in the dark at room temperature for 45 min. Dilute the sample with ammonium bicarbonate (Sigma-Aldrich, A6141-500G), adjust the pH to 8, and add trypsin (Promega, V5280/100ug) at a protein-to-trypsin ratio of 50:1. Incubate overnight at 37 °C. The next day, add 50 µL of 0.1% formic acid (FA, Sigma-Aldrich, T79708) to stop the reaction. Desalt the samples using a C18 desalting column. First, activate the column with 100% acetonitrile (ACN), then equilibrate with 0.1% FA. Load the sample onto the column, then wash with 0.1% FA to remove impurities. Finally, elute with 70% ACN (J.T.Baker, 34851 MSDS) and collect the flow-through, then lyophilize. Run the mass spectrometer to obtain raw data. The DEGs were screened with the BMKCloud platform (www.biocloud.net). The ratio of the average of all biological replicate quantitative values for each protein in a comparison sample pair is used as the Fold Change (FC). To determine the significance of the difference, the relative quantitative values of each protein in the two comparison samples were subjected to a T-test, and the corresponding P-value was calculated as the significance indicator. In this project, a protein was considered to be upregulated when FC ≥ 2 and P-value ≤ 0.05. A protein was considered to be downregulated when FC ≤ 0.5 and P-value ≤ 0.05. The upregulated and downregulated proteins were screened based on these criteria. For data visualization, hierarchical clustering was performed on the identified differentially expressed proteins. The quantitative protein abundance data were normalized using the Z-score method (row scaling). The heatmap was constructed using the BMKCloud platform, employing Euclidean distance for similarity measurement and complete linkage for clustering.

Western blot

Samples (cells, exosomes, and migrasomes) were lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) supplemented with 1% Phenylmethanesulfonylfluoride (PMSF, ST506, Beyotime, CN) and denatured at 95 °C for 10 min. Protein quantification was performed using a BCA Protein Assay Kit, and 5 µg of protein per sample was resolved on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST (TBS + 0.05% Tween-20) for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with diluted primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence Odyssey Fc Imaging System ((LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Detailed information regarding the antibodies used for Western blot are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses

After overnight fixation in 10% paraformaldehyde (PFA), the joint specimens were decalcified in 10% EDTA (E1171, Solarbio, CN) solution for 4 weeks. Following decalcification, the specimens were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subsequently stained. The histological sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Safranin O/Fast Green, according to standard protocols. Immunohistochemistry was performed using the IHC Detect Kit for Rabbit Primary Antibodies (KIT-9706, MXB biotechnologies, CN). Briefly, after routine deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval, and endogenous peroxidase blocking, the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with diluted primary antibodies. Staining was developed using DAB (DAB; Sigma) and counterstained with hematoxylin. All sections were photographed using a confocal microscope (SP8, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

To systematically evaluate the severity of articular cartilage degeneration, a Modified Mankin Scoring System was established based on H&E and Safranin O/Fast Green staining images [26]. The system evaluates four parameters: cartilage structure (0–6), cellular abnormalities (0–3), matrix staining intensity (0–4), and tidemark integrity (0–1). The total score ranges from 0 (normal) to 14 (severe degeneration). Scoring was performed by three independent observers blinded to the experimental grouping to minimize bias.

For synovial and cartilage organoids, the organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution at 4 °C for 24 h. After fixation, the organoids were embedded in paraffin, and 4-µm sagittal sections were prepared using a Leica RM2245 microtome (Wetzlar, Germany). The subsequent steps were carried out as described above. Detailed information regarding the antibodies used for immunohistochemical analyses are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence staining

The samples (cells or organoids tissue sections) were initially fixed at room temperature using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min. After fixation, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Permeabilization was carried out using 0.5% Triton X-100 (P0096, Beyotime, CN) at room temperature for 10 min, followed by three PBS washes. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (ST023, Beyotime, CN) at 37 °C for 1 h, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with diluted primary antibodies. After 40 min of staining with fluorescent secondary antibodies, the nuclei were stained with DAPI. Specifically, images were acquired using an Olympus FV1200 confocal microscope equipped with 60× oil immersion objectives (for subcellular details) and 20× objectives (for tissue overview). Excitation/Emission wavelengths were set as follows: 405 nm/430–470 nm for DAPI, 488 nm/505–540 nm for green fluorescence (WGA/Alexa 488), and 559 nm/575–620 nm for red fluorescence (Phalloidin/Alexa 594). Laser power, gain, and offset were optimized to avoid signal saturation and maintained constant across all samples within the same experiment to ensure semi-quantitative comparability. Detailed information regarding the antibodies used for Immunofluorescence staining are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Normality and homogeneity of variances were confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. For data violating these assumptions, non-parametric tests were applied: the Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was used for multiple group comparisons. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired t-tests, while multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Results

M1 Macrophages identify as one of the major synovial migrasomes in the inflammatory microenvironment of TMJ-OA

To confirm the presence of migrasomes within the inflammatory microenvironment of TMJ-OA, synovial tissue samples from clinically diagnosed patients were analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Our analyses identified typical pomegranate-like structures in these samples, with diameters ranging from 0.5 to 3 μm (Fig. 1A). These structures also contained multiple intraluminal vesicles, consistent with the migrasome morphology described in previous reports [13].

To comprehensively screen the cellular source of migrasomes within the complex synovial microenvironment, we analyzed a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE267942) derived from TMJ tissues of both healthy control mice and mice with CFA-induced arthritis [22]. We annotated major cell populations, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and macrophages (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, we calculated a “Migrasome Score” for each cluster based on the aggregate expression of key biogenesis genes (Tspan4, Cpq, Ndst1, Pigk, Eogt, Itgb1, Itga5) [15, 27]. Our analysis revealed a contrast in cellular composition between health and disease. Resident populations, such as fibroblasts and endothelial cells, maintained consistent Migrasome Scores across both groups, while the macrophage cluster exhibited a distinctive, infiltration-dependent pattern (Fig. 1C). Specifically, this Cd86-high pro-inflammatory macrophage cluster was virtually absent in the control group but prominently infiltrated the TMJ tissue in the CFA-induced group, coincident with exceptionally high Migrasome Scores (Fig. S1A). This indicates that M1 macrophages constitute one of the major sources of migrasomes under inflammatory conditions.

To further validate these bioinformatic findings in situ, we employed multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) to investigate the spatial relationship between migrasomes and specific cell types in human TMJ-OA synovial tissues. We observed that TSPAN4 and PIGK, markers for migrasomes [13], were colocalized with CD80, a specific marker for M1 macrophages [28]. Conversely, there was minimal co-localization between TSPAN4/PIGK and vimentin, a marker for fibroblasts or CD31, a marker for endothelial cells. (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1B).

To confirm the migrasome-generating capacity of M1 macrophages in vitro, we differentiated THP-1 monocytes into M1 macrophages using PMA, followed by stimulation with LPS and IFN-γ [29]. Successful M1 polarization was confirmed via CD80 immunofluorescence staining (Fig. S1C). Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining analysis revealed that these polarized M1 macrophages exhibited robust migrasome formation (Fig. 1E). In parallel, we isolated primary FLSs from TMJ-OA patients and generated M0 macrophages from THP-1 monocytes for comparison. Quantitative analysis of WGA staining demonstrated that the migrasome production in M1 macrophages was significantly higher than that in FLSs, whereas M0 macrophages exhibited negligible formation (Fig. 1E-F, Fig. S1D). To verify whether these migrasomes can be released into synovial fluid, we collected synovial fluid from TMJ-OA patients and isolated vesicles using density gradient centrifugation. TEM analysis of these vesicles revealed a morphology consistent with that of synovial tissue migrasomes (Fig. 1G).

Collectively, these findings, supported by both transcriptomic analysis and in vitro assays, suggest that M1 macrophages constitute one of the major sources of synovial migrasomes under inflammatory conditions, emphasizing their potential role in mediating crosstalk between M1 macrophages and cartilage.

M1 Macrophage-derived migrasomes alleviate TNF-α-induced chondrocyte apoptosis

Given the strong migrasome-generating capacity of M1 macrophages and the presence of these vesicles in synovial fluid, we hypothesized that migrasomes facilitate intercellular communication between macrophages and chondrocytes in the inflammatory environment of TMJ-OA. To test this hypothesis, we first purified M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes using density gradient centrifugation [13] (Fig. S2A). The purified vesicles exhibited a characteristic pomegranate-like morphology (Fig. 2A). Western blot analysis demonstrated that the purified migrasomes were significantly enriched in key migrasome-related markers, including TSPAN4, EOGT, and PIGK, in the purified migrasomes (Fig. 2B). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed that the diameters of these migrasomes ranged from 0.3 to 0.5 μm (Fig. S2B). For comparison, M1 macrophage-derived exosomes were isolated, with a smaller diameter ranging from 100 nm to 150 nm (Fig. 2A and Fig. S2A, C). Next, we introduced WGA-labeled migrasomes to primary rat chondrocytes. Confocal microscopy revealed that the migrasomes were internalized by the chondrocytes and dispersed throughout the cytoplasm (Fig. 2C), indicating that chondrocytes can effectively take up migrasomes.

To replicate the inflammatory conditions of TMJ-OA in vitro, we treated chondrocytes with TNF-α, a well-known pro-inflammatory cytokine [30]. Our results demonstrated that a 24-hour treatment with 100 ng/mL TNF-α significantly reduced cell viability, as indicated by Neutral Red staining, and caused proteoglycan depletion, as shown by Alcian Blue staining, compared to the untreated group. Notably, the subsequent addition of 10 µg/mL migrasomes for an additional 24 h significantly rescued chondrocytes, alleviating both TNF-α-induced cell death and proteoglycans loss. In contrast, subsequent addition of exosomes (10 µg/mL) under the same conditions exerted negligible effects on both apoptosis and proteoglycan levels (Fig. 2D, E and Fig. S2D).

Furthermore, we found that migrasomes counteracted the catabolic effects of TNF-α. Western blot analysis revealed that TNF-α treatment upregulated the protein levels of ADAMTS5 and MMP13. However, exogenous addition of migrasomes resulted in a downregulation of both ADAMTS5 and MMP13 compared to TNF-α treatment alone, while exosomes exerted negligible effects (Fig. 2F, G).

Taken together, our findings suggest that M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes alleviate TNF-α-induced chondrocyte apoptosis and suppress the expression of catabolic enzymes, highlighting their potential protective role in the inflammatory microenvironment of TMJ-OA.

Establishment and characterization of a temporomandibular joint organoids co-culture model

Conventional two-dimensional cultures have inherent limitations in replicating the complex three-dimensional cellular organization, accurately mimicking intercellular interactions, and reflecting cell-to-extracellular matrix relationships [31–33]. To overcome these challenges, we engineered a “Mini-TMJ” model, a synovium-cartilage organoids co-culture model that mimics the stratified architecture and functional microenvironment of the native TMJ (Fig. 3A and Fig. S3).

Chondrocytes were derived from H9 embryonic stem cells using a staged chondrogenic induction protocol and subsequently self-assembled into cartilage organoids within an agarose matrix [34]. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining revealed a layered structure in the cartilage organoid, with distinct superficial and proliferative zones, closely resembling the native condylar cartilage organization. Safranin O/Toluidine blue staining showed robust aggrecan deposition in the cartilage organoid (Fig. 3B), while immunohistochemical staining confirmed positive expression of type II collagen (COL2A1) (Fig. 3C).

The synovial organoid was established by co-culturing primary human FLSs with THP-1 cells within a 3D microenvironment. To replicate the TMJ-OA condition, we introduced 5 µM monosodium iodoacetate (MIA) as an inflammatory agent to mimic the inflammatory environment typical of osteoarthritis [35]. Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) analysis showed that CD68+ M0 macrophages and CD68+/CD80+ M1 macrophages were predominantly localized in the superficial layer, while Vimentin+ FLSs were found in the deeper sublining layer, recapitulating the native synovial architecture in inflammatory conditions (Fig. 3D).

Further immunofluorescence analysis revealed significant pathological changes in MIA-treated organoids after 48 h, including elevated MMP13 protein levels and reduced SOX9 expression compared to the control groups (Fig. 3E and F). These cellular compositions and molecular changes were characteristic of osteoarthritis, confirming the successful establishment of a TMJ-OA organoid model. Additionally, we collected the culture media from synovial organoids and isolated vesicles through density gradient centrifugation. TEM analysis of these vesicles revealed a morphology consistent with migrasomes (Fig. S4).

M1 Macrophage-derived migrasomes activate chondrocyte autophagy within inflammatory microenvironments

To investigate the mechanism by which migrasomes alleviate chondrocyte apoptosis, we performed mass-spectrometric profiling of exosomes and migrasomes derived from M1 macrophages. The proteomic analysis revealed marked differences in their protein cargo (Fig. 4A, B). Notably, migrasomes were significantly enriched in ATG8 family proteins compared to exosomes (Fig. 4C). Given that LC3 is the most widely studied ATG8 ortholog in mammals, with a pivotal role in autophagosome biogenesis and substrate selection [36, 37], we employed high-resolution confocal microscopy to investigate the spatial relationship between migrasomes and autophagy-related proteins. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed spatial overlap between LC3B-positive puncta and migrasomes (Fig. 4D). Western blot analysis further confirmed the significant enrichment of the canonical autophagy markers Beclin1 and LC3B in M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes compared to M1 macrophage-derived exosomes (Fig. 4E). These findings are consistent with emerging evidence suggesting that migrasomes act as specialized carriers for autophagosomes [38], implying that M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes may exert chondroprotective effects by regulating autophagy.

To test this hypothesis, we incorporated size-selective filters (3-µm and 0.2-µm pore sizes) into the TMJ-OA organoid model to modulate migrasome-mediated crosstalk (Fig. 4F and Fig. S5). The 3-µm filter allowed unrestricted migrasome trafficking, while the 0.2-µm filter completely blocked migrasome transport between synovial and cartilage organoids. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that blocking migrasome transport with the 0.2-µm filter decreased LC3B and Beclin1 protein levels, while promoting the accumulation of p62 protein in the cartilage organoid. Notably, exogenous addition of M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes restored autophagic activity, consistent with the results observed in the 3-µm filter group, where unrestricted migrasome trafficking was permitted (Fig. 4G, I and Fig. S6). We further validated these findings in TNF-α-stimulated chondrocytes. Western blot analyses showed that chondrocytes cultured with M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes exhibited enhanced autophagic activity, evidenced by increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratios and elevated Beclin1 protein levels. Notably, this was accompanied by suppressed protein levels of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 compared to TNF-α alone (Fig. 4H). To confirm whether this protective effect was autophagy-dependent, we utilized the inhibitor 3-Methyladenine (3-MA) [39]. The results showed that 3-MA significantly abrogated these effects; specifically, the migrasome-mediated upregulation of autophagy markers was partially blocked, and the protein levels of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 were reversed back to high levels despite the presence of migrasomes. These results collectively demonstrate that M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes activate chondrocyte autophagy and inhibit their degradation within inflammatory microenvironments.

Intra-articular injection of M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes attenuates cartilage degradation in rat TMJ-OA via autophagy

Building on the demonstrated inhibitory effect of M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes on TNF-α-induced chondrocyte apoptosis in vitro, we next investigated whether these migrasomes exert chondroprotective effects in an osteoarthritis model. To this end, we established a rat model of TMJ-OA, with the control group receiving intra-articular PBS injections, the MIA group receiving intra-articular MIA injections, and the MIA + MIG group receiving intra-articular MIA injections combined with M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes (Fig. 5A).

Eight weeks post-injection, micro-CT analysis and 3D reconstruction were performed to assess the morphology of the condylar process. Our results revealed significant trabecular resorption in the anterior condylar slope of the MIA group, along with a notable decrease in the bone volume to total volume ratio (BV/TV) and an increase in the bone surface to bone volume (BS/BV), compared to the control group. In contrast, treatment with migrasomes in the MIA + MIG group effectively preserved trabecular bone microarchitectural integrity relative to the MIA group (Fig. 5B–D). Cartilage samples from all three groups were collected and subjected to histological and immunohistochemical analysis. H&E staining confirmed the preservation of cartilage structure in the control group, characterized by a smooth surface, distinct stratification, regular cell morphology, and an orderly cellular distribution. In contrast, the MIA group exhibited typical degenerative changes, including a rough surface, indistinct layers, reduced cellularity, and superficial fissures. These osteoarthritic alterations were significantly attenuated in the MIA + MIG group (Fig. 5E). Histological assessment using Safranin O/Fast Green staining showed a marked recovery of glycosaminoglycan content in the MIA + MIG group compared to the MIA group (Fig. 5F). This protective effect on the extracellular matrix was further corroborated by COL2A1 immunohistochemistry, which exhibited enhanced type II collagen deposition in the treatment group (Fig. S7A-B).

To semi-quantitatively evaluate the protective effect of migrasomes, we employed the Modified Mankin Scoring System [26, 40]. The Control group exhibited pristine cartilage structure with a minimal mean score of 0.67 ± 0.38. In contrast, the MIA group showed severe osteoarthritic changes, including structural disorganization and hypocellularity, resulting in a significantly elevated Mankin score of 10.83 ± 0.81 (P < 0.001 vs. Control. Notably, intra-articular injection of M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes (MIA + MIG group) significantly mitigated these degenerative features, reducing the Mankin score to 6.00 ± 0.79 (P < 0.05 vs. MIA). These results confirm that migrasome treatment effectively preserves cartilage integrity in vivo. (Fig. S7C)

Immunohistochemical analysis of the MIA group showed a significant increase in MMP13 levels and a marked decrease in SOX9 expression compared to the control group. The MIA + MIG group effectively mitigated the MIA-induced increase in MMP13 and the decrease in SOX9, demonstrating strong protective effects against osteoarthritis progression (Fig. 6A, B). Additionally, immunohistochemical analysis showed that the MIA + MIG group exhibited increased protein levels of LC3B and Beclin1 compared to both the control and MIA groups (Fig. 6C, D). These results demonstrate that injection of M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes significantly promotes autophagy, thereby suppressing cartilage degradation in rat TMJ cartiliage.

Discussion

Migrasomes, membrane-bound vesicles generated during cellular migration, serve a range of functions across various physiological environments [16]. The identification of migrasomes in both synovial tissues and synovial fluid of TMJ-OA patients suggests their potential role as mediators in the progression of osteoarthritis. Our mIHC results and in vitro experiments further indicate that M1 macrophages are the primary cellular source of synovial migrasomes in TMJ-OA. Considering that migrasome biogenesis is closely linked to cellular motility, the pronounced migratory activity of M1 macrophages likely accounts for their role as the main cellular source of migrasomes in osteoarthritis [41].

To overcome the limitations of conventional 2D culture systems, particularly their inability to replicate 3D tissue architecture and intercellular communication [31–33], we engineered the “Mini-TMJ” model by co-culturing synovial and cartilage organoids. Both synovial and cartilage organoids exhibited a layered structure similar to that of natural tissue [23, 42]. Importantly, MIA-induced inflammatory responses within the Mini-TMJ accurately recapitulated key TMJ-OA hallmarks, including the cellular composition of synovial organoid and the dysregulation of MMP13 and SOX9 in cartilage organoid. This Mini-TMJ allowed for mechanistic investigation through size-selective filters (3-µm and 0.2-µm). Using 0.2-µm filter to block migrasome transfer from synovial organoid to cartilage organoid, we observed a significant suppression of autophagic activity in the cartilage organoid, as evidenced by decreased LC3B and Beclin1 protein levels, along with elevated P62 expression. In contrast, the exogenous addition of purified M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes to the cartilage organoid effectively enhanced autophagic activity, confirming their functional role in modulating chondrocyte autophagy and contributing to the regulation of cartilage homeostasis. The chondroprotective efficacy of M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes in the TMJ organoid model was also confirmed in a rat TMJ-OA model. Correspondingly, the Mini-TMJ exhibited a refined structure, and was highly applicable for closely mimicking the conditions of TMJ-OA.

In the TNF-α-induced TMJ-OA model in vitro, M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes exhibited chondroprotective properties, including the suppression of catabolic enzymes and a reduction in proteoglycan depletion. This contrasts with the typical pro-inflammatory role of M1 macrophages in osteoarthritis, where they exacerbate cartilage degradation by secreting TNF-α and IL-1β. These results suggest a functional dissociation between the pro-inflammatory effects of the parent M1 macrophages and the protective actions mediated by their daughter extracellular vesicles. M1 macrophages operate under high glycolytic flux and oxidative stress [43]. To maintain cellular viability and a robust inflammatory phenotype, these cells must actively eject damaged mitochondria and accumulated protein aggregates—a quality control process mediated by migrasomes [20]. Consequently, the enrichment of autophagy-related proteins (such as LC3B and Beclin1) observed in M1-migrasomes likely represents the “disposal” of the parent cell’s autophagic machinery. Critically, while this cargo represents metabolic waste for the M1 macrophage, it constitutes a functional “resource package” for OA chondrocytes. Since OA chondrocytes are characterized by defective autophagy and lysosomal dysfunction [44], the uptake of these LC3B-rich vesicles may provide the necessary structural components to restart stalled autophagic flux. This hypothesis offers a plausible explanation for how “harmful” parent cells can generate “protective” vesicles: harmful M1 macrophages expel material that is taken up by cartilage cells, facilitating their repair and unexpectedly producing a therapeutic effect. Our Western blot analysis supported this hypothesis, showing an enrichment of autophagy-related proteins in migrasomes. Additionally, fluorescence imaging demonstrated co-localization of LC3B with migrasomes. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that glioma-derived migrasomes, enriched in LC3B-positive autophagosomes, mitigate endoplasmic reticulum stress and suppress tumor cell death [38].

While our study provides novel insights into the role of migrasomes in TMJ-OA, several limitations and translational avenues warrant further discussion. First, regarding the molecular mechanism, the specific cargo (e.g., proteins, miRNAs) driving autophagy activation remains to be fully elucidated. Future studies should employ comparative high-throughput omics to identify these key molecules and validate the consistency of protein expression profiles between cellular models and target tissues, thereby improving therapeutic prediction. Second, regarding experimental modeling, developing more sophisticated in vitro systems, such as microfluidic joint-on-a-chip models that incorporate mechanical loading, will allow for better correlation with in vivo responses and align with the 3R principles. Third, significant biological hurdles remain for clinical translation. Given the pro-inflammatory origin of M1 macrophages, the long-term safety and immunogenicity of these vesicles must be rigorously evaluated. Additionally, the scalability of production poses a bottleneck, necessitating the development of high-yield bioreactor systems. Finally, regarding delivery, while direct injection proved effective in this study, engineering biomimetic delivery vehicles (e.g., hydrogels or surface-modified migrasomes) to achieve sustained release and precise targeting will be crucial for maximizing long-term outcomes in clinical settings [45].

Conclusion

In summary, we identified the presence of migrasomes in TMJ-OA and demonstrated that M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes ameliorate TMJ-OA through autophagy in both the Mini-TMJ co-culture model and a rat TMJ-OA model. Our findings suggest that M1 macrophage-derived migrasomes may serve as a novel therapeutic strategy for TMJ-OA.
