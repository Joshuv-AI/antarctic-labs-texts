Association between upper limb motor function and balance in patients after stroke: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Qiong Han, Xiaoli Xu, Jiaying Lin, Jie Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using cross-sectional data from 26 general hospitals in China that treated stroke patients in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine, the aim of this research is to explore the potential association between upper limb motor function and balance function after stroke. We conducted a prospective cross-sectional study involving 1573 stroke patients hospitalized in the rehabilitation medicine departments of 26 hospitals across China. Upper limb motor function and balance were evaluated using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) and the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), respectively. Linear regression analysis and smoothing curve fitting were performed to examine the relationship between upper limb motor and balance functions. This study included 1573 non-pregnant adults aged 18 years or older, with a mean age of 61.2 ± 12.5 years, of whom 69.0% were male. After adjusting for potential confounders, a significant positive association was observed between upper limb motor function and balance function after stroke (β = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.44–0.51, p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses indicated that this association was more pronounced in the acute phase (≤ 7 days) compared with the subacute and recovery phases. No significant interactions were detected across other subgroups (p > 0.05 for all interactions). Smooth curve fitting revealed distinct saturation effects for FMA-UE and BBS, with inflection points at 25 and 45, respectively. Sensitivity analyses further confirmed these results, supporting the robustness of the findings. This study identified a significant positive association between upper limb motor function and balance function following stroke, highlighting the potential role of upper limb motor recovery in predicting balance outcomes. Further large-scale and multidimensional studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms linking upper limb motor control and post-stroke balance function.

Introduction

In 2021, stroke ranked as the third leading Level 3 cause of death in the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) classification, following ischemic heart disease and COVID-19, and was the fourth leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)1, with a disproportionate burden on low- and middle-income countries2. Stroke is a leading cause of long-term physical disability, particularly motor impairment. The majority of stroke survivors experience varying degrees of motor dysfunction, resulting in deficits in upper limb function, mobility, and balance. Due to these physical limitations, many patients struggle to perform daily activities, resulting in a substantial decline in overall quality of life3.

Upper limb motor recovery is often more difficult to achieve than lower limb recovery after stroke4. Approximately 55–75% of stroke survivors experience upper limb motor impairment5. and only 20–30% regain functional use of the affected limb6, while more than half continue to experience residual deficits six months post-stroke7. Impaired upper limb function significantly limits independence and participation in daily and social activities7,8. In addition to its direct impact on motor performance, upper limb dysfunction may also compromise postural stability, as effective balance control relies on coordinated upper limb movement9,10, sensory feedback11, and trunk stabilization12.

Balance impairment is another prevalent consequence of stroke, affecting up to 83% of patients with residual deficits13. Damage to the central nervous system often disrupts the integration of sensory and motor systems required for postural control, resulting in balance disorders that increase the risk of falls and hinder functional recovery14,15. These disturbances further exacerbate dependence in daily activities and reduce quality of life.

Previous studies have begun to explore the relationship between post-stroke upper limb motor function and balance. In a cross-sectional study, Rafsten et al. reported that FMA-UE scores were significantly correlated with BBS and TUG performance, and that upper limb motor impairment was independently associated with balance deficits in logistic regression analyses10. In chronic stroke populations, posturography-based studies have further demonstrated relationships between upper limb motor ability and standing stability, particularly mediolateral COP sway, indicating a potential influence of upper limb control on postural regulation16. In addition, some studies have interpreted this association from the perspective of trunk control. Akkurt et al. reported that trunk control was strongly correlated with sitting balance in individuals with subacute stroke and moderately correlated with FMA-UE scores, suggesting that trunk control may partially mediate the relationship between upper limb motor function and balance17.

However, important limitations remain. Study populations differ in stroke phase, severity, and inclusion criteria, limiting generalizability and contributing to inconsistent findings. Balance has been assessed using heterogeneous constructs and measures, complicating comparisons across studies. Moreover, many studies rely on unadjusted or minimally adjusted analyses, leaving the association between upper limb motor function and balance potentially confounded by demographic, socioeconomic, clinical, cognitive, and time-since-stroke factors.

Therefore, this multicenter cross-sectional study aimed to examine the association between upper limb motor function (FMA-UE) and balance (BBS) in hospitalized stroke patients within one year of onset. To better characterize their independent relationship, we performed multivariable regression analyses adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, clinical, cognitive (MoCA), and time-since-stroke factors. Clarifying this association may improve prognostic assessment and inform integrated rehabilitation strategies targeting both upper limb recovery and postural stability.

Methods

Study population

This study was a multicenter, cross-sectional investigation conducted between October 2022 and December 2024. Patients experiencing a first-ever stroke within 1 year post-onset were recruited from 26 hospital systems spanning eight provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities across China. This time criterion was applied to control for stage-related variability in motor and balance recovery and to minimize the influence of long-term compensatory strategies typically observed in chronic stroke survivors. The primary objective of the study was to elucidate the relationship between upper limb motor function and balance performance in stroke patients. A total of 2600 participants were initially enrolled. Individuals were excluded if they were younger than 18 years (n = 41), had missing FMA-UE or BBS data (n = 102), had an onset time greater than 1 year (n = 234), or had missing values for other covariates (n = 650), the final analytical cohort comprised 1573 participants (Fig. 1). Approval for this study was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (Approval No.: Min Medical University Affiliated Ethics [2022] No. 280), and informed consent was obtained from the participants or their legal representatives.Fig. 1The flowchart of participants selection.

The participating institutions include: The First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, Huashan Hospital of Fudan University, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University, Beijing Tiantan Hospital of Capital Medical University, General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University, Longhua District Central Hospital of Shenzhen, The Third Clinical Hospital of Changchun University of Chinese Medicine, Pudong Hospital of Fudan University, Jing’an District Central Hospital of Shanghai, Shanghai Third Rehabilitation Hospital, Fuding Hospital, Quanzhou First Hospital, Fujian Provincial Hospital, Longyan Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital, Zhongshan Hospital Xiamen Branch of Fudan University, Xiamen Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital, The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanping of Fujian Medical University, Xiamen Guomao Taihe Rehabilitation Hospital, Sanming Second Hospital, Changle District Hospital of Fuzhou, Yongchun County Hospital, Dehua County Hospital, Jianyang First Hospital of Nanping, Putian University Affiliated Hospital, and Fuzhou First Hospital.

Assessment of upper limb motor function and balance function

We used the the Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Upper Extremity (FMA-UE)18 to evaluate upper limb motor function after stroke. The FMA-UE consisting of 33 assessment items with a total score of 66 points. A higher FMA-UE score indicates better motor function. The Berg Balance Scale (BBS)19 was used to assess balance function, comprising 14 items with a scoring range of 0–56 points. A higher BBS score indicates better balance ability.

Study design and measurement of covariant

We used a self-designed data collection form to uniformly investigate and record baseline information of patients through interviews and reviewing electronic medical records. This information includes gender, age, educational level, handedness, underlying comorbidities (such as hypertension, coronary heart disease), lifestyle habits (smoking status, alcohol consumption), and disease duration. Smoking history was defined as smoking for ≥ 3 years with an average daily consumption of ≥ 3 cigarettes, while alcohol history was defined as an average daily alcohol consumption of ≥ 2 standard drinks. The overall cognitive function is assessed through the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scale20. The MoCA scale consists of 12 tests, mainly covering seven dimensions, including visual-spatial and executive function, attention, calculation, language, abstract thinking, memory, and orientation. The total score of the MoCA scale is 30 points, with higher scores indicating better cognitive abilities, and scores below 26 indicating cognitive impairment21.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted for all participants. For the presentation of baseline characteristics, the continuous FMA-UE scores were categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4). This categorical approach was chosen to facilitate the clinical observation of data distributions and to identify potential non-linear trends of demographic and clinical covariates across varying severities of upper limb impairment, without assuming a priori linear relationships. Categorical data were presented as numbers (percentages), while continuous data were presented as means ± standard deviations or medians (interquartile range) as appropriate. Differences in categorical variables across the quartiles were detected using appropriate chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied as appropriate to assess continuous variables across the four groups.

To evaluate the independent associations between FMA-UE and BBS, as well as between FMA-UE and its components, multivariable linear regression analysis (β, 95% confidence interval [CI]) was conducted using FMA-UE as a continuous variable to maximize statistical power. Three models were applied in the regression analysis: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for Gender, Age, and Race; Model 3 was adjusted for Model 2 covariates plus Educational level, marital status, PIR, Region, Work status, drink, Smoke, Hypertension, CHD, BMI, Onset time, and MoCA.

Furthermore, to explore potential dose–response and non-linear associations between FMA-UE and BBS, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression model was applied. The inflection points for subsequent piecewise linear regression were determined based on the empirical trend of the RCS trajectory and further validated by established clinical severity thresholds of the FMA-UE. Subgroup analyses were performed to validate the stability of the results, and likelihood ratio tests were used to test interactions between subgroups. All analyses were performed using the statistical software package R (http://www.R-project.org, R Foundation) and Free Statistics Software version 2.3. Two-tailed tests with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant in our study.

Discussion

This study performed a cross-sectional analysis of data on upper limb motor function and balance function in stroke inpatients across 26 hospitals located in 8 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities. A significant observation in this large-scale analysis was the nonlinear, dose–response relationship between FMA-UE and BBS. Unlike previous studies that generally assumed a linear correlation, our findings reveal a more complex, stage-dependent association. This robust observation provides profound biomechanical and neurophysiological insights into how upper limb recovery interacts with postural control, which has important implications for developing stage-specific clinical rehabilitation guidelines.

Upper limb motor dysfunction after stroke is commonly characterized by reduced strength23, abnormal muscle tone (either hypertonia24 or hypotonia25), impaired motor coordination, and limited joint range of motion, which collectively alter movement strategies. Balance dysfunction after stroke is multifactorial and has been linked to deficits in strength, joint mobility, tone regulation, coordination, sensory integration, and cognition26. Most balance research in stroke has focused on trunk function27 and lower-limb performance or proprioception28, leaving the dynamic role of the upper limb less explored. Extensive research in healthy populations has established that unrestricted arm movements are critical for maintaining postural stability and counteracting center of gravity (COG) shifts29–31. While some studies, such as the 2014 research by Indian scholars32, demonstrated a moderate positive correlation between upper limb motor control and balance in stroke patients, their linear analytical models may have masked the intricate, stage-specific fluctuations observed in our study.

Our nonlinear RCS analysis specifically identified three distinct phases in the relationship between FMA-UE and BBS: an initial steep positive correlation (0–25 points), a distinct plateau phase (25–45 points), and a subsequent positive re-emergence (45–66 points). We hypothesize that this “S-shaped” trajectory is driven by the evolution of post-stroke motor control strategies and the biomechanical consequences of compensatory movements.

Phase I: The “Stabilization” Effect (FMA-UE 0–25) The rapid improvement in BBS associated with early increases in FMA-UE scores likely reflects the recovery of proximal joint stability and basal muscle tone. In the early post-stroke flaccid stage, patients often suffer from shoulder subluxation and hypotonia, causing the affected arm to act as a biomechanical “dead weight” that continuously pulls the body’s COG downwards and laterally. Previous studies have suggested that restricting upper limb activity with a sling can transiently enhance balance by mitigating this early subluxation33,34. Therefore, as patients progress from 0 to 25 on the FMA-UE, the return of proximal shoulder control and the reduction of flaccidity effectively eliminate this destabilizing drag, leading to rapid foundational balance gains.

Phase II: The “Compensation Trap” Plateau (FMA-UE 25–45) The most striking finding of our study is the plateau phase between FMA-UE scores of 25 and 45, where upper limb functional improvements showed no substantial impact on BBS. This phase typically corresponds to the emergence of synergistic movement patterns, where patients can initiate movement but lack isolated joint control. We postulate that this plateau is strongly driven by excessive trunk compensation. As demonstrated in classical kinematic studies by Cirstea and Levin12, stroke patients frequently employ excessive trunk anterior displacement or lateral flexion to compensate for deficits in shoulder flexion and elbow extension during reaching tasks. While these trunk compensatory strategies might enable the patient to complete goal-directed tasks and slightly increase their FMA-UE score35–38, they continuously and severely perturb the COG39. The frequent anterior–posterior and medial–lateral shifts of the trunk destabilize posture, effectively neutralizing the potential balance benefits gained from partial arm recovery. Garland et al. also noted that hemiplegic patients exhibit greater trunk COG shifts to maintain balance during upper limb movements40. Thus, the kinematic cost of arm movement in this synergy stage offsets overall balance performance.

Phase III: The “Dynamic Regulator” Effect (FMA-UE 45–66) As FMA-UE scores increase beyond 45, a robust positive association with BBS re-emerges. At this advanced stage, patients gradually break free from synergy patterns, regaining isolated motor control and independent distal joint movements. The upper limb transforms from a potential destabilizing burden into an active “dynamic balance regulator”. The restored ability to perform unrestricted, rapid, and fractionated arm movements enables patients to effectively counteract postural perturbations, significantly enhancing advanced dynamic balance, aligning with the physiological mechanisms observed in healthy individuals29–31.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it represents the first multicenter cross-sectional investigation in China to examine the relationship between upper limb motor function and balance function after stroke. The extended study period and large sample size enhance the robustness of the findings and underscore the need for further research in this area. In addition, a comprehensive review of the existing literature and careful adjustment for potential confounding factors improved the reliability and accuracy of the results.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the assessments of upper limb motor function and balance function inevitably involve subjective elements, which may introduce measurement bias. Second, the analysis was restricted to adult stroke patients within one year after their first stroke, thereby excluding minors and certain special populations. Third, although adjustments were made for known confounders, unmeasured factors cannot be completely ruled out. Most notably, post-stroke motor recovery is rarely an isolated phenomenon. It is highly likely that the observed robust association between FMA-UE and BBS is intrinsically linked to the concurrent, spontaneous recovery of trunk control and lower limb function. Neuroplasticity following stroke often drives a generalized pattern of motor restitution; thus, parallel improvements in core stability and leg strength—variables not explicitly measured in our dataset—may act as crucial confounding factors.

Furthermore, our assessment relied on a single scale for balance (BBS). While the BBS is widely validated, it primarily captures static and basic dynamic balance and may exhibit ceiling effects in higher-functioning patients. It is highly plausible that similar, or perhaps even more nuanced, nonlinear associations might be observed using instruments that capture advanced dynamic balance, functional mobility, and gait, such as the mini-Balance Evaluation Systems Test (mini-BESTest), the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, or the Functional Ambulation Category (FAC). Future prospective cohort studies incorporating these comprehensive functional instruments, alongside the Trunk Impairment Scale (TIS) and the lower extremity portion of the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA-LE), are warranted to adjust for these concurrent recovery variables, definitively isolate the independent contribution of the upper limb, and better align the results with real-world physiological observations.

Conclusions

In summary, this cross-sectional study, based on data from first-time stroke patients admitted to the rehabilitation departments of 26 hospitals in China, revealed a nonlinear positive association between upper limb motor function and balance function after adjusting for potential confounders. However, these findings are preliminary and warrant further confirmation in large-scale, multicenter longitudinal studies. Such evidence would be of great importance for guiding rehabilitation strategies and predicting functional outcomes in stroke patients.
