Assessment of Tell-Read-Do Technique in Comparison with Ask-Tell-Ask Technique and Conventional Tell-Show-Do Technique in Management of Anxious Pediatric Dental Patients Aged 7–10 Years: An Interventional Clinical Study

Introduction

Dental anxiety among children is a significant and ongoing concern in pediatric dentistry, as it could be a key obstacle for children to accept dental treatment.1 Dental treatment must not only focus on the prevention of caries but also on the psychological impact that it may have on the child, thereby aiding in the prevention of traumatic experiences.2

Pediatric dentists employ a variety of strategies, including nonpharmacological techniques and pharmacological techniques, to reduce anxiety and ensure that children feel comfortable and supported throughout their dental experiences. By managing dental anxiety early on, dental professionals can help foster positive attitudes toward oral care and prevent the development of long-term oral health problems.3

The recommendations from the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry suggest that more focus should be on nonpharmacological interventions.4

The majority of nonpharmacological behavior guidance approaches are based on communication and the tell-show-do technique, which is a classic model of communication, along with the addition of sensory demonstration cues (visual, auditory, touch, and sometimes proprioception). It is the most accepted behavior guidance technique and is considered the cornerstone of pediatric dentistry.5

Tell-show-do is the most effective technique, but due to changing situations of the current era and evolving cognitive abilities of children, several modifications of the tell-show-do approach have been developed, which include tell-play-do,6 tell-show-play-do,7 tell-play-do with smart phone dentist game,8 and ask-tell-ask.9

All these techniques are based on the principle of self-regulation, which states that having knowledge of what will happen will make the condition less traumatic. This can reassure the child and psychologically prepare them for the treatment in a playful and less invasive manner, which also increases the acceptance rate of the treatment.10

These modified techniques are mainly based on the play component and are more effective for younger children. However, as cognitive development occurs, the children tend to think more logically. They are less influenced by play therapy and are more curious about knowledge and reasoning.11

A new modification of the tell-show-do technique–tell-read-do, which emphasizes theme-based posters intertwined with cartoon characters, which includes a complete description of the treatment procedure in a sequential manner, using euphemisms, allows children to engage with age-appropriate reading material related to the procedure. It is designed to appeal to their natural curiosity and support their need for logical explanation, making it potentially more effective for older children.

Thus, this study aimed to assess the efficiency of the “tell-read-do” technique compared with the conventional “tell-show-do” technique and “ask-tell-ask” technique in managing anxious kids aged 7–10-years during restorative dental treatment.

Materials and Methods

This randomized, interventional, clinical study was conducted in the Pediatric Dentistry Department from August 2024 to September 2025. An ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (DYPDCH/109/2024) before the initiation of the study. Ninety children aged 7–10 years whose parents provided consent to contribute to research were included based on the inclusion criteria. The sample size of the research was estimated using: n=[(Z1-α-Zβ)6]2δ;95% confidence interval, δ 80% power

Children aged 7–10 years who can read and understand English, visiting the department of Pediatric Dentistry for the first time for restorative treatment, were incorporated in the research. Children having complaints of pain, children who are compromised medically, and children with special health care needs were excluded from the research.

The chosen sample was then randomly assigned into three groups, encompassing 30 children in each group, using SNOSE (sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelope technique).

Group I: Tell-show-do.

Group II: Ask-tell-ask.

Group III: Tell-read-do.

Assessment was done using three parameters: physiologic assessment was done using pulse rate, subjective evaluation was done by means of facial image scale, and objective evaluation was done by means of Venham anxiety scale.

After allocation of group, the kid was calmed in the waiting area, and preoperative readings of all three parameters were taken, and was then taken to the operatory for the restorative procedure. The procedure was then introduced to the child as per the allocated group. After which the treatment was completed, followed by postoperative readings of all three parameters. Evaluation was done by a single investigator who was blinded to the technique used to avoid bias.

Tell-Show-Do Group

A detailed step-by-step vocal explanation was given to the children about the restorative procedure, followed by a demonstration of the sensory aspects of the procedure, instruments, and materials required for restoration. The kid was explained about the working of the airotor, suction and the three-way syringe. After which, the restorative treatment was initiated (Fig. 1).

Tell-show-do group

Ask-Tell-Ask Group

First, the kid was enquired about his concerns regarding the dental treatment, after which the concerns of the child were addressed, which included providing information about the treatment procedure in a systematic manner using simple language based on the cognitive development of the child. Once the kid was satisfied and ready for the treatment, the treatment was initiated (Fig. 2).

Ask-tell-ask group

Tell-Read-Do Group

A complete explanation was given to the child regarding the restorative treatment. After which, a cartoon theme poster was given to the child to read. The poster consisted of a detailed step-by-step restorative procedure in story format and was made using euphemism, helping children familiarize themselves with treatment processes and equipment in a playful and less intimidating manner before their appointment. Once the child completed reading the poster, the restorative procedure was initiated (Fig. 3).

Tell-read-do group

Data recorded was entered on a Microsoft Office Excel, and analysis was done utilizing SPSS v 26.0, IBM. Demographic evaluation was done using parametric tests. Inter-group comparison was done utilizing one-way ANOVA. The association of frequencies of categories of variables with groups was done with the chi-square test. The Shapiro–Wilk test was done to evaluate normality, and it was seen normal curve was not followed, hence nonparametric tests were used for comparison. Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA was used for intergroup comparison, and the Wilcoxon Signed Rank test was done for intragroup comparison. For all the statistical tests, p < 0.05 was considered significant, with alpha error at 5% and beta error at 20%, giving a power to the research as 80%.

Discussion

Anxiety is a feeling generally stated as fear of the unknown. Anxiety related to dental treatment is ranked the fifth most common cause of anxiety.12 Alleviating a child's apprehension regarding dental treatment is significant not only in mitigating the fear but also in avoiding anxiety that can continue even in adulthood.13

In the literature, various research about the best techniques that effectively guide the behavior of a child in a dental clinic are being conducted. This is the first study to assess the efficiency of the novel approach, the tell-read-do technique, based on cognitive development of children for behavior guidance in pediatric dental care.

According to Piaget's classification, children of the age group 7–10 are in the concrete operational stage. Logical thinking and understanding abilities in this period are sign of willingness for the treatment and communications.11 Hence, this age group was selected for this study.

Heart rate was taken as physiologic parameter for assessment of anxiety, as it is considered a reliable tool for measurement of anxiety.14 In our study, the heart rate decreased remarkably in the tell-read-do group from preoperative to postoperative, whereas it increased in the ask-tell-ask group. The decrease in heart rate postoperatively for the tell-show-do group was very minimal. Similar results were seen by Elicherla et al.1 and Lekhwani et al.,15 where they found an increase in mean pulse rate, after treatment for the ask-tell-ask group.

The subjective evaluation was done utilizing the facial image scale. Use of the projective picture technique, in which the child can respond nonverbally, should be considered for assessment of anxiety as stated by Newton and Buck.16 Facial image scale assessment is done by the patient; another objective scale, in which the dentist can do the assessment, is recommended; hence Venham's anxiety scale was used as an objective evaluation parameter.

In our study, there was a reduction in readings of the facial image scale from preoperative to postoperative for the tell-show-do group. For readings of Venham's anxiety scale, there was a significant reduction from preoperative to postoperative for the tell-show-do group and tell-read-do group. Similar results were seen by Deshpande et al.,17 where they found a significant difference in Venham's anxiety score, facial image scale score, and pulse rate from preoperative to postoperative in group with a self-designed dental story book when compared to the group without a story book.

In this study, overall reduced anxiety was seen with the tell-read-do group. This may be because reading about the procedure in a story format with the help of euphemisms will help the to reassure the child and psychologically prepare them for the treatment in a playful and less invasive manner. It is constructed on the self-regulation principle, which states that knowing what will happen will make the condition less traumatic.18

Limitations

The present study evaluated the effectiveness of the Tell-Read-Do technique limited to restorative dental procedures. Future research may develop and evaluate procedure-specific Tell-Read-Do cards tailored to different dental treatments. Additionally, the study included only children who were able to read and understand English, future research should validate multilingual versions of the Tell-Read-Do technique to enhance their applicability across diverse population.

Conclusion

This study concludes that the tell-read-do technique can be effectively used to reduce anxiety in children prior to dental treatment. Although the tell-show-do is the most recognized method, contemporary shifts in children's learning preferences may enhance the significance of the tell-read-do technique. The encouraging results obtained suggest that integrating a reading component offers an innovative and potentially advantageous modification to conventional behavior guidance strategies.
