High-quality whole genome data of Hippotion velox
Authors: ChaoTang, Yuan-tong Zhang, Bo Liu, Guang-chang Ma, Wan-qiang Qian, Zhi Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe sequenced and assembled the whole genome of Hippotion velox using the Cyclone platform. The initial assembly generated by Canu was subsequently processed with Purge_haplogs to eliminated redundancies, producing a final genome size of 816.16 Mb and a contig N50 of 46.,41 kb. For genome annotation, we integrated evidence from transcriptome data, homology-based comparisons, and de novo gene prediction methods, resulting in a comprehensive set of protein-coding gene models. This complete genomic dataset, including both the assembly and annotation, has been deposited in the China National GeneBank DataBase (CNGBdb) under accession number CNA0505689. The corresponding raw sequencing data are accessible under accession CNP0007417. The availability of the Hippotion velox genome offers a crucial resource for evolutionary comparisons within the Sphingidae family and broader studies on Lepidoptera genome biology.Keywords: De novo assembly, Lepidoptera, Cyclone sequencing, Repetitive elements, Island and reef

Background

Hippotion velox is a species of Sphingidae (Lepidoptera) moth widely distributed in Sri Lanka, India, Southeast Asia, Australia, and some Pacific islands [2]. It has also been recorded in multiple regions of China, including Yunnan, Guizhou, Sichuan, southern China, Taiwan, and Hainan [3]. This insect has a relatively narrow host range, primarily infesting a limited number of plants such as Pisonia grandis [4]. In the Xisha Islands, it feeds exclusively on Pisonia grandis without attacking other co-occurring plant species, and is therefore generally regarded as a neutral insect in most contexts. However, on some islands and reefs where Pisonia grandis serves as the single dominant plant species, Hippotion velox has gradually emerged as a major local pest, posing a significant threat to the vegetation ecosystem.

In recent years, global warming has particularly affected tropical regions [5], potentially disrupting the synchrony between the generational cycle of Hippotion velox and the phenology of its host plants [6,7], which may lead to population outbreaks. Systematic research on its outbreak mechanisms remains limited, especially regarding the role of climate change in driving its transition toward becoming a pest. Whole-genome analysis has been widely employed to elucidate the adaptive evolution and outbreak mechanisms of pests [8], such as detoxification gene family expansion in Bursaphelenchus xylophilus, symbiont-mediated reproduction regulation in Bemisia tabaci [9], and adaptive evolution of thermal response-related genes [10]. Therefore, conducting a whole-genome sequencing of Hippotion velox will contribute to a molecular-level understanding of its ecological adaptability, host specificity, and climate response mechanisms, thereby providing a scientific basis for the integrated management of this insect.

Data Description

Sequencing, assembly and annotation of the hippotion velox

The Hippotion velox genome was sequenced using a combination of Illumina short-read and Cyclone long-read technologies, generatiing 1087,672,360 paired-end short reads and 32,980,438 long reads for the subsequent assembly. Based on a k-mer analysis, the genome size of Hippotion velox was initially predicted to be approximately 773 Mb (Fig. 1). Canu was employed for error correction and initial assembly, after which Purge Haplotigs removed redundant sequences, resulting in a final genome assembly of 816.16 Mb. This assembly consists of 30,502 contigs, with the largest one reaching 420,837 bp in length (Table 1). To evaluate assembly completeness, BUSCO analysis against a set of 5286 conserved Lepidopteran genes showed a high completeness rate of 96.1 %, with only 2.1 % fragmented and 1.8 % absent. Furthermore, alignment with a conserved insect gene library demonstrated that 96.8 % of these core genes were intact in the assembly.

Inferred genomic features of Hippotion velox derived from Cyclone sequencing data, analyzed via a k-mer frequency spectrum (k = 23). The genome was predicted to be approximately 773 Mb in length, with a repeat content of 0.65 % and a heterozygous site proportion of 1.22 %.

Repetitive sequences

Repetitive sequences constitute a substantial fraction (34.04 %) of the Hippotion velox genome (Fig. 3; Table 3). These sequences were primarily composed of transposons, which we classified into the two canonical categories. The most prevalent were Class I retrotransposons, which replicate through an RNA intermediate that is reverse-transcribed, leading to a copy number increase. Detailed characterization further divided these retrotransposons into Long Interspersed Nuclear Elements (LINEs), the most abundant superfamily at 7.44 % of the genome, Short Interspersed Nuclear Elements (SINEs, 6.04 %), and elements containing Long Terminal Repeats (LTRs, 1.45 %) (Fig. 3; Table 2). Conversely, Class Ⅱ DNA transposons, defined by terminal inverted repeats and mobilized by a transposase-mediated ‘cut-and-paste’ mechanism, accounted for 3.35 % of the genomic sequence. Within this class, specific families like hAT-Charlie and TcMar-Tigger represented 0.45 % and 0.08 % of the genome, respectively (Table 2). A considerable portion (15.91 %) of the repetitive content could not be assigned to a known category. This high level of unclassified, repeats aligns with findings in Manduca sexta, which shares a comparable overall repetitive landscape (33.91 %) and an unclassified fraction of 11.88 % [11]. Expanding our comparative analysis to other Sphingidae species, (Mimas tiliae, Laothoe populi, Deilephila porcellus, H. euphorbiae, and Hemaris fuciformis) comfirmed that repetitive content within this family is highly variable, ranging from 27.5 % to 47.7 %, consistent with prior reports (Table S2) [[12], [13], [14]]. In total, all identified repetitive sequences were distributed across 59 known clades or families (Fig. 4).

Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

Sample acquisitionand sequencing

A male specmen of Hippotion velox was selected for genome sequencing. The mothoriginated from an inbred strain, which was initially collected from Xisha in 2023 and has been continuously maintained in a laboratory in Haikou. Larvae were raised in a controlled environment at 26 °C with a 14 h : 10 h light : dark cycle and fed a standard wheat germ-based semi-artificial diet. We isolated high-quality genomic DNA from the specimen with the Qiagen Genomic DNA Kit (Cat. No 13,323) . The integrity and concentration of the purified DNA were determined by employing a NanoDrop One UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen), respectively. Following library construction, its quality was confirmed via Qubit quantification before being subjected to sequencing on the CycloneSEQ platform (BGI Genomics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China),adhering to the manufacturer’s recommended protocols. The accompanying CycloneMaster software facilitated the entire sequencing workflow, encompassing real-time data acquisition, instrument control,and base calling.

Data preprocessing and genome assembly

The initial quality of the raw sequencing data was assessed with FastQC (v0.11.6). Subsequently, adapter contamination and low-quality sequences were removed using fastp (v0.12.4) [15] to generate high-quality clean data. For K-mer based analysis, we employed Jellyfish (v2.3.0) [16] to count k-mers, and the resulting k-mer frequency distribution was profiled with GenomeScope2 (v2.0) [17] to estimate genomic characteristics. De novo assembly of the corrected third-generation sequencing reads was conducted with Canu (v2.2) [18]. To reduce redundancy and heterozygosity in the initial assembly, the purge_haplotigs (v1.1.3) [19] pipeline was applied. Finally, to benchmark the completeness of the purged genome assembly, we assessed it against the Insecta (odb10) and Lepidoptera (odb10) databases Using BUSCO (v5.3.2) [20].

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from range of tissues dissected from adult Hippotion velox specimens, namely the antenna, heart, wings, abdomen, legs, and genitalia, for transcriptomic analysis. Subsequently, we constructed the RNA-seq libraries with the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit (USA), adhering strictly to the provided protocol. These libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system at Novogene Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), which produced 150 bp paired-end reads. After quality control and filtering, the sequencing effort yielded approximately 41.15 Gb of clean data.

Repetitive sequence prediction

The identification of repetitive sequences was conducted through a de novo approach. First, a custom repeat library was built and self-trained using RepeatModeler[21]. This library was subsequently utilized as the primary input for RepeatMasker [22] to comprehensively detect and mask all repetitive regions throughout the genome.

Gene structure prediction

Gene structure prediction was performed on the repeat-masked genome. The process involved three complementary approaches. First, de novo prediction was conducted using AUGUSTUS (v 3.5.0) [23], wherein gene models were trained to predict gene structures. Second, RNA-seq–based prediction was carried out using second-generation transcriptome data: hisat2 (v 2.2.1) [24] was used for read alignment, followed by transcript assembly with stringtie (v 2.2.1) [25]. Coding regions were then predicted using TransDecoder (v 5.7.1) (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder) to generate gene models. Third, homology-based prediction was performed using protein sequences from four closely related sphingid species: Cephonodes hylas, Deilephila porcellus, Manduca sexta, and Theresa japonica. Miniprot (v 0.13) [26] was used for protein-to-genome alignment, and the resulting predictions were converted to GFF3 format using gffread (v 0.12.7) [27]. Finally, the predictions from all three methods were integrated into a consolidated annotation using EVidenceModeler (v 1.1.1) [28] to produce the final gene annotation for Hippotion velox.

Limitations

Single-individual sequencing: The present genome assembly was derived from a single inbred male, a strategy that dose not encompass the full spectrum of genomic variation within the species. This constraint has direct implications for downstrem application. In comparative genomic analyses, for instance, it becomes challenging to distinguish genuine assessment of population-level genetic diversity or the identification of polymorphic loci. To overcome these caveats, subsequent research initiatives should incorporate population-scale resequencing data.

Uncharacterized repetitive elements: Our analysis revealed that a substantial fraction (15.91 %) of the repetitive sequences defied classification, highlighting a significant, uncharacterized component of the genome. Elucidating the origins and biological roles of these elements will require dedicated efforts, potentially employing systematic manual curation and long-read RNA-seq technologies.

Limited functional annotation depth: While the initial functional annotations relied on well-established databases the precise roles of genes in adaptation and pest behavior remain to be fully elucidated. Empirical validation through transcriptomic profiling under defined stimuli—such as host plant exposure or thermal stress – is essential to enhance the accuracy of these functional predictions.
