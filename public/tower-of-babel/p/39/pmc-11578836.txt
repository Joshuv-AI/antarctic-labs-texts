Overview of a national endoscopy database: The Trans.IT database and its impact on data registration quality
Authors: F. Theunissen, P. C. J. ter Borg, R. J. T. Ouwendijk, M. J. Bruno, P. D. Siersema, the Trans.IT foundation study group
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Trans.IT database is a national gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy database developed in 2012. It automatically collects anonymous data from GI endoscopy procedures in a centralized database. All endoscopists use a structured reporting tool for uniform data collection. In this study, we aim to provide an overview of the database and to evaluate its impact on data registration quality.MethodsWe used all ERCPs, colonoscopies and colorectal cancer (CRC)‐screening colonoscopies performed between 2016 and 2020. We excluded centers joining after 2016 and patients below age 18. Data registration quality for ERCPs included completeness of data for: intention of ERCP, Schutz score, ASA classification, papillary status (virgin or previous sphincterotomy), cannulation (success or failure to cannulate the desired duct) and procedural success. For colonoscopies: indication, ASA‐classification, Boston Bowel Preparation Score (BBPS), cecal intubation, polyp detection rate (PDR). For CRC‐screening colonoscopies, ASA‐classification, BBPS, cecal intubation, PDR and adenoma detection rate (ADR).ResultsA total of 14,156 ERCPs, 150,962 colonoscopies and 37,199 colorectal cancer screening colonoscopies were included in our analysis. For ERCPs, registration of procedural intention, Schutz score, ASA classification, papillary status, cannulation and procedural success improved from 34.9%, 32.7%, 72.6%, 36.5%, 34.6%, 27.2% in 2016, to 86.4%, 84.6%, 97.4%, 86.4%, 82.1%, 84.0%, respectively, in 2020. For non‐screening colonoscopies, registration of indication, ASA classification, BBPS, cecal intubation and PDR improved from 40.4%, 60.5%, 47.6%, 69.8% and 32.3% in 2016 to 90.3%, 88.9%, 59.8%, 79.1% and 39.1%, respectively, in 2020. For CRC‐cancer screening colonoscopy registration equaled outcome, PDR and ADR changed from 74.7% to 63.6% in 2016 to 66.3% and 53.8% in 2020, respectively.ConclusionsThe quality of endoscopy data registration has consistently improved over the years by using the Trans.IT database. This is most likely the result of feedback to performing endoscopists to review performance in real‐time online and progressive awareness of quality of data registration.

INTRODUCTION

Gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy is an important diagnostic and therapeutic tool for GI diseases. In the Netherlands, approximately half a million GI endoscopies are performed annually and approximately 100 units perform GI endoscopy. 1

As endoscopic technologies advance and medical practices evolve, quality requirements for endoscopic procedures also become more important. High‐quality endoscopy provides better health outcomes, a better patient experience, and fewer repeat endoscopies. To set a standard of care, the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) and the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) developed performance measures for GI endoscopy. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The unit's performance measures are to be regularly audited. Auditing performance measures provides individual endoscopists with feedback about their performance. It also allows endoscopists to compare themselves with colleagues and compare performance across different endoscopy units. 6 Previous studies have already shown that the quality of endoscopy varies significantly across different endoscopy units. 7 , 8 , 9

Auditing performance measures, however, can be time‐consuming and difficult when different registration types are used in endoscopy units. Uniform reporting across endoscopy units and central documentation of data could potentially be the key to effectively audit performance measures on a national level and maybe in the future also on an international level. To facilitate uniform reporting and central documentation, the Trans.IT database was developed and introduced in the Netherlands.

The Trans.IT Foundation (Rotterdam, The Netherlands) is a collaboration of Dutch hospitals which started in 1998 and aimed to improve the quality of GI endoscopy reporting. The foundation developed a structured reporting tool with standard reports and text blocks to create uniform GI endoscopy reports. The structured reporting tool was incorporated into the report system Endobase (Olympus Europe).

The foundation developed in 2012 the Trans.IT database, a multicenter GI endoscopy database, which automatically collects anonymous data from each GI endoscopy procedure of ´Trans.IT’ hospitals in a centralized database. All participating sites use the structured reporting tool to allow uniform data collection.

The database was developed for the following reasons:To improve the quality of endoscopy—The Trans.IT database allows the automatic calculation of performance measures for endoscopy, such as cecal intubation rate (CIR), polyp detection rate (PDR) and Boston bowel preparation scale (BBPS). The performance measures can be used by individual endoscopists or hospitals to review their performance and improve upon it. The database can also compare performance measures with the aggregated results from other hospitals.For research purposes—By documenting all performed endoscopy procedures and findings, a comprehensive database was created, which gives valuable insight into the epidemiology of various diseases, endoscopic findings and therapeutic interventions.To reduce the administrative burden—The database is connected to the national ERCP quality registry and the national colorectal screening (CRC) registry. When an ERCP or CRC‐screening colonoscopy is reported, it is automatically uploaded through the database to the corresponding quality registry and thus, endoscopic findings need to be reported only once.To provide easy insight into the workload—The database gives an accurate overview of workload and production numbers, giving meaningful insight for staff, hospital board, health insurers and the government.

The aim of this study was to give an overview of the set‐up of the Trans.IT database and to evaluate the impact of the Trans.IT database on data registration quality of performed GI endoscopy procedures.

METHODS

The structured reporting tool

All hospitals participating in the Trans.IT database use a structured reporting tool developed by the Trans.IT foundation to generate endoscopy reports. Endoscopists create reports immediately after the endoscopy. Within the structured reporting tool, there are three methods to create an endoscopy report.

First, a report can be created using a standard report. This is a complete report based on one or more diagnoses, which can be selected (Figure 1). This method is mostly used for common diagnoses or when no abnormalities are found during endoscopy.

Schematic overview of a standard report.

Second, a report can be composed of text blocks. In this case, the GI tract is divided into sections according to the main organs examined during endoscopy (e.g. during gastroscopy the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum). Text blocks are selected for each section and in each text block, a diagnosis or finding is described in one or more sentences. By selecting a combination of text blocks, a complete report is generated (Figure 2).

Schematic overview of a structured report.

Third, structured input can be used. This method is an extended version of the Minimal standard terminology for GI terminology developed by the ESGE and the ASGE. 10 This method organizes all terms into a hierarchy, and for each finding, a site, a term, and one or more describing attributes are selected. This method is particularly detailed and is mostly used to report rare findings during endoscopy.

The content and quality of the standard reports and text blocks are maintained by the Trans.IT workgroup, which includes at least one gastroenterologist from each of the participating hospitals. The workgroup reviews and discusses the content of the standard reports and text blocks. When modifications are collectively decided, these are remotely added to the software in all Trans.IT hospitals to ensure uniformity in all hospitals.

Furthermore, Endobase communicates with the hospital information system. As a result, patient data, such as gender and date of birth, are automatically extracted from the hospital information system when a report is generated. Additionally, endoscopy examination data, such as type of endoscopy, endoscopist name, type of sedation, and indication of the procedure, are documented by the endoscopist.

Coding of terminology

To improve the consistency and comparability of the endoscopic reports, an extensive WHO‐approved code system for GI endoscopic terminology was developed. 11 This code system was based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) and the ICD‐10 clinical modification (ICD‐10‐CM) and expanded to include all GI endoscopic terms. The codes were expanded by preserving the first 4 characters of an existing ICD‐10 code and adding the characters behind it.

The newly expanded code system was named GI Endoscopic Terminology Coding (GET‐C) and is incorporated into the structured reporting tool of Endobase. An example of the GET‐C coding is shown in Table 1. All standard reports, text blocks and examination data are linked to GET‐C and when an endoscopy report is created, the corresponding GET‐C codes are selected in the background to be recorded in the Central database.

The central database

The Central database automatically collects the data of all endoscopic procedures performed in the participating hospitals. To enable communication between the participating hospitals and the database, an export tool was developed to automatically export the data to the database. The export tool is embedded in the structured reporting tool. The export process involves three stages.

First, from each endoscopy report, the patient data, examination data and GET‐C codes of endoscopic findings are locally extracted by the export tool and put together into an Extensible Markup Language (XML) File. Second, during the export process, all traceable patient data are anonymized according to the NEN 7524 standard, which is the Dutch standard for anonymizing patient data. In brief, direct identifiers (such as, names, address, citizen service numbers) are removed or blinded with the use of hashing and encryption to ensure that data cannot be traced back to individuals. Indirect identifiers (such as, dates of birth, locations) are generalized or suppressed. For example, exact dates can be converted to a broader range and locations to a larger area. Anonymization is an automatic process. Additionally, the original patient ID is then anonymized into a hashed patient ID. This method allows maintaining the privacy of the patient while consecutive procedures in the same patient can be stored together in the database.

Third, the XML file is then encrypted as an additional security measure and uploaded to the database. The database collects all XML files of the participating hospitals daily and stores them in the data warehouse. A schematic overview of the data input in the Trans.IT Database is depicted in Figure S1.

The database can be accessed through a secure website at https://kpionline.bitam.com/english/memberlogin.php. Each connected hospital has a unique login code and password to access its respective data. The homepage of the website presents the user with a set of predefined dashboards and datasets (Figure 3). These dashboards display calculated performance measures, such as the CIR and PDR, alongside the total production numbers of the hospital and the aggregated results from all connected hospitals. The dashboards are calculated in real‐time with the most recent available data. Furthermore, the database empowers users to create custom reports and statistics with the built‐in business intelligence tool. Using this tool, statistics can be created at the endoscopist or hospital level, allowing the evaluation of trends. To ensure privacy, hospitals can only access the data of their respective endoscopists.

Overview of the homepage of the Trans.IT database.

For research purposes, data can be exported from the database as comma separated values (CSV) Files. The CSV files can then be analyzed by specialized statistical software such as SPSS.

Dataset

We analyzed all ERCPs, colonoscopies and colorectal cancer screening colonoscopies performed between January 2016 and December 2020 in this study. Data recording achieved consistency across all hospitals starting from 2016 due to ongoing developments and refinements of the database, the structured reporting tool and GET‐C coding. To ensure continuity and comparability of the data, we only included hospitals that participated for at least five consecutive years from 2016 to 2020. We excluded all procedures performed in patients under the age of 18 years in accordance with the Dutch general data protection regulation law.

Outcomes and definitions

The main outcome of this study was to evaluate data registration quality. For ERCPs, the quality indicators were the percentage in which the following measures were registered: intention of ERCP, Schutz score, ASA classification, papilla status (virgin or previous pre‐cut), cannulation (success or failure of the common bile duct (CBD) or pancreatic duct) and procedural success. For CRC‐screening colonoscopy, this included the registration of ASA‐classification, Boston Bowel Preparation Score (BBPS), cecal intubation, PDR and adenoma detection rate (ADR). For the other colonoscopy, this included the registration of indication, ASA‐classification, BBPS, cecal intubation, PDR.

Secondly, we analyzed the procedural outcomes of the ERCPs, CRC‐screening colonoscopies and other colonoscopies from 2016 to 2020. These analyses were based on registered data only, ignoring missing values. For ECRPs, this included overall cannulation rate (biliary or pancreatic), successful CBD cannulation rate in patients with a virgin papillary anatomy and overall procedural success rate. For CRC‐screening colonoscopy, this included adequate bowel preparation rate, meaning a BBPS of at least six, CIR, PDR and ADR and for other colonoscopy, this included adequate bowel preparation rate, CIR and PDR.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistical software package version 28 (Armonk, NY). Data were exported from the Trans.IT database in comma‐separated files and imported in SPSS statistical software. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (%) and non‐parametric data as medians with interquartile ranges.

Ethical considerations

The collection of patient data in the Trans.IT database was approved by the privacy officer of the Erasmus Medical Center under the Dutch Personal Data Protection Act. All patient data are anonymously stored in a secure environment and are therefore exempted from ethics approval. All hospitals included in this study provided written consent for participation.

RESULTS

The Trans.IT database collected data from 20 Dutch hospitals, comprising 3 academic hospitals and 17 non‐academic hospitals over the period January 2012 to December 2020. The database initially included 6 hospitals in 2012 and expanded to include 20 hospitals by the end of 2019. Throughout 2012–2020, the database recorded a total of 633.111 GE examinations, comprising 253,955 colonoscopies, 49,057 colorectal cancer screening colonoscopies, 261,448 upper GI endoscopies, 24,516 ERCPs and 44.135 sigmoidoscopies.

Twelve Dutch hospitals (one academic and 11 non‐academic) participated from 2016 (or before) to 2020 and were included in the analysis. Between January 2016 and December 2020, these hospitals performed 14,197 ERCPs, 37,203 colorectal cancer screening coloscopies and 151,709 other colonoscopies. After excluding 41, 4 and 747 procedures, respectively, due to patient age below 18 years, we included 14,156 ERCPs, 37,199 colorectal cancer screening coloscopies and 150,962 other colonoscopies in our analysis.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we evaluated the impact of the Trans.IT database on data registration quality for ERCPs, colonoscopies, and CRC‐screening colonoscopies, as well as its effect on endoscopy performance. We found that the quality of data registration improved significantly over 5 years for ERCPs and colonoscopies. Additionally, we found that the overall quality of endoscopy was high, with cannulation rates for ERCP and cecal intubation rates for CRC‐screening colonoscopy above 90%. Our study was designed to eliminate variables that might influence the comparability of hospitals over the years, such as hospitals using different and non‐updated versions of GET‐C codes or hospitals participating for less than 5 years.

Data in the endoscopy reports could be missing because data fields in the reporting software were left blank. These fields are not mandatory to fill out, which highlights a flaw in the reporting software for which no easy‐to‐apply solution was available. The completeness of data was therefore dependent on the compliance of the endoscopist. Furthermore, we chose not to analyze upper GI endoscopy because we could not identify useful and objective performance outcomes to describe trends throughout the years. For CRC‐screening and other colonoscopies and ERCPs, there were more and better‐defined objective outcomes.

Our results show that the quality of data for CRC‐screening colonoscopy was already high in 2016 and remained consistently high throughout the years. This is likely due to the fact that Trans.IT is used as an intermediary to register CRC‐screening colonoscopy to the national screening program registry. The outcomes we analyzed are also mandatory to register in the national screening program registry since 2012. Endoscopists were likely to be trained in correctly documenting these outcomes. However, for the other colonoscopies, the outcomes were not mandatory to register and the increase in the quality of data (registration) we observed is probably an increase in compliance of endoscopists.

Despite the improved quality of data registration over the years, we did not see a direct improvement in the performance of endoscopy. This could mean that either the quality of endoscopy was already high, but less of it was registered by the endoscopists in the report, or that the quality of endoscopy did improve over the years by using Trans.IT as the reporting system was seen as an easy to use and time‐saving tool.

By using Trans.IT, endoscopists were able to monitor their performance in real‐time through online dashboards and receive regular performance updates. This feedback mechanism allowed endoscopists to identify shortcomings in the quality of the procedure and stimulated them to improve their performance. Furthermore, it has previously been demonstrated that providing endoscopists with feedback may improve their endoscopic performance. 6 , 12 , 13 Notably, In the case of ERCPs, this has even led some endoscopists to no longer perform ERCP because of continuously shown sub‐par performance. 14 A national database with continuous examiner‐traceable performance measures such as intubation rates, ADR and PDR can significantly improve the monitoring of endoscopists compared with periodic audits of performance measures. This system allows real‐time feedback, contributing to improved overall performance and patient outcomes. However, a downside of continuously monitoring is that it leaves the system vulnerable to incorrect reporting, as it could lead to an increased performance pressure on endoscopists. The Trans.IT database circumvents this vulnerability by directly deriving data from the patient reports produced with the structured reporting tool. Incorrect reporting in the database would also lead to incorrect endoscopy reports in the electronic patient record (EPR). Consequently, it is unlikely for endoscopists to report incorrect outcomes as this would lead to pollution in the EPR, incorrect insurance claims based on the outcomes in the report and incorrect reports sent to the general practitioner of the patient. Moreover, it is not possible to create separate reports for the database and EPR.

A notable observation in our study was the gradual decrease in PDR and ADR over the years in CRC screening colonoscopy (Figure 6). These findings are in line with the findings of the national CRC screening program. 15 The decrease in ADR and PDR is a consequence of the decreasing average age of the participants in the national screening program over the years as it originally started with inviting the oldest age groups of 65–75 years. Less abnormalities are observed in younger patients than in older patients and therefore the average PDR and ADR decrease as well. 15

Our study contributes to the understanding of the benefits of a national endoscopy database (NED). While other endoscopy databases exist worldwide, this is one of the first studies that reports on the impact of such a database on the reporting behavior of endoscopists. Other national GI endoscopy databases include the NED in the UK and the Japan Endoscopy Database (JED) in Japan. 16 , 17 NED was founded in 2013 and includes around 56% of annually performed endoscopic procedures in the UK (vs. approximately 30% for Trans.IT). Shiha et al. have previously reported on the impact of the NED on colonoscopy withdrawal time, reporting a positive effect of NED on colonoscopy withdrawal time. 18 JED was founded in 2015 and is currently still in development. 17 , 19

The limitations of this study need to be addressed as well. First, for the variables cecal intubation, ADR and PDR, we could not differentiate whether the outcome was achieved but had a negative outcome or the outcome was not registered. Unfortunately, the software has no option for recording a negative outcome. As an example, when cecal intubation is achieved, the data field is filled in; however, if it is not achieved or the endoscopist ignores registering cecal intubation, the field remains blank. Consequently, when more data are missing, the CIR, ADR and PDR automatically appear lower because the percentage is calculated based on all colonoscopies performed.

Second, two hospitals chose not to register any outcomes for ERCPs, meaning that they had 100% missing data for analyzed ERCP outcomes. This drastically impacted the overall outcomes of the study. However, we could not exclude those hospitals because they met the inclusion criteria.

Third, there is no complication registry in the central database. Complication registration is an important part of quality assessment. It would have been valuable information if we could assess whether regular feedback on performance had reduced the number of compilations.

In conclusion, the quality of endoscopy data improved consistently over the years using Trans.IT. This is most likely the result of frequent feedback and the ability to view performance in real‐time online. This study highlights the benefits of a centralized NED. Future versions of a GI endoscopy database should include a complication registry and the inability to leave data fields blank.
