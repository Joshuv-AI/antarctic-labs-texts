Graphene scaffolds entrapped polyacrylonitrile electrospun nanofibrous separator for aqueous zinc-ion storage application
Authors: Shanmugasundaram Lakshana, Mani Ulaganathan, Mani Karthega
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeparators are crucial passive components in batteries, ensuring electrical isolation and enhanced processability for energy storage applications. The shortcomings of the conventional separators remain a key challenge for industrial-scale applications. The advanced separators that enable fast ion transport and uniform zinc deposition are essential for improving the stability and cycle life of aqueous zinc-ion batteries (AZIBs). Hence, this work involves the fabrication of pure polyacrylonitrile (PAN) polymer (PP) and graphene scaffold (1wt.%, 3wt.%, and 5wt.%) incorporated PAN (PG1, PG3, and PG5) membrane and the prepared membranes were subjected to various physical, chemical, thermal, mechanical, and electrochemical studies to investigate their extensive characteristics and their performance as a separator. The graphene-incorporated PG1 membrane exhibits enhanced thermal stability, mechanical strength, ionic conductivity, and electrochemical performance compared with other membranes. In particular, the cell with a PG1 membrane as the separator exhibits a stable cycle life of approximately 1000 cycles at 1 mA and displays a superior battery capacity of 287 mAh g− 1 at 0.3 mA, which is higher than that of the PP separator. Notably, the graphene additive strategy directly demonstrates improved electrochemical characteristics, highlighting the safe, enhanced performance and reliability of AZIBs and their promising potential for large-scale energy storage applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51039-3.Keywords: Aqueous Zinc-ion battery, Electrospinning, Polyacrylonitrile, Separator, Specific capacitySubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The rising demand for carbon reduction, driven by the increasing need for renewable sources such as solar and wind, is the most effective choice and has garnered tremendous attention; however, the inherent intermittency of these sources makes storage crucial. Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are a better replacement and dominate the market compared to other rechargeable batteries due to their lightweight, high power, and high energy density. However, despite these advantages, LIBs face specific challenges, such as high cost and, in particular, the risk of fire and explosion when applied to large-scale systems like stationary grid storage. In recent years, Aqueous Zinc-ion batteries (AZIBs) have emerged as a promising energy storage system due to their safety, eco-friendliness, and cost-effectiveness, which is facilitated by their intrinsic availability, particularly for large-scale energy storage systems in the future1,2. Owing to its high capacity and non-toxicity, zinc is considered as a promising anode material for aqueous batteries, as the operation is governed by the mobility of Zn2+ ions between the cathode and the zinc anode3–6.

But in AZIBs, the foremost challenge is dendrite growth at the Zn metal anode, resulting from irregular and uncontrollable Zn deposition during plating, which is less detrimental than in Li-ion batteries but still impedes electrochemical performance. The dendrite growth is a result of the accumulation of more Zn2+ ions at the localized sites, depicting the formation of an uneven electric field7–11. This is a significant issue, which culminates in low cycle life and short circuits, which hinder the application of AZIBs as energy storage devices12–14. It also alters the pH, promoting the formation of byproducts like Zinc Hydrogen Sulphate (ZHS) at the electrode/electrolyte interface15–17.

To address these problems, numerous studies have been conducted over the last few decades. Various frameworks have been adopted to suppress dendrite growth and improve the performance of Zn-anodes in neutral electrolytes, including highly concentrated electrolytes, artificial Zn2+-conductive organic and inorganic layers on the electrode surface18–20, electrolyte additives, and separator modification21,22. One of the effective strategies is the separator architecture, which enhances the performance of the cell, serving as a protective interface between the electrodes (anode and cathode), functioning as a cornerstone in regulating Zn stripping/plating behaviour, and an efficient transport of the ions by the considerable amount of electrolyte that is retained by the separators, which acts as a reservoir23–25. Conventional separators like filter paper or glass fibres (GF) are the most commonly used separators in AZIBs, demonstrate good compatibility with the aqueous electrolyte, and are afflicted with a very frail nature, uneven pore distribution, and very low mechanical strength, easily form zinc dendrites, and brittleness render it amenable to dendrite penetration, causing internal short circuits26,27. The uneven pore distribution in traditional separators leads to irregular absorption of electrolyte wetting across the foil surfaces, rendering it unsuitable for evenly distributed zinc deposition28,29. Recent research has focused on enhancing the structural stability of the separator to achieve a uniform distribution of Zn2+ ions, thereby improving the battery’s electrochemical performance. The two main strategies (i) The first involves the addition of a high zincophilic functional group or the modifications with GF separators (ii) to replace the GF separator with polymer-based materials with a zincophilic group30,31 have reported to improve the cell performance. Wu et al.32 prepared a PANI-modified layer on GF as a separator in a Zn//MnO2 cell, with the synthesized MnO2 as the cathode, which delivers a stable cycle life of 300 cycles. The high cost of commercial separators and the preparation procedures required for separator modifications limit their widespread adoption in AZIBs. It is crucial to explore the development of low-cost separators with high mechanical strength, high thermal stability, suppress the shuttling effect, corrosion-related side reactions, and comparable thickness improved electrolyte affinity33. The ease of fabricating microstructures and the low cost of non-woven separators make them more promising as replacements for conventional separators. Various methods have been adapted to fabricate a fibrous membrane as a separator, such as solution extrusion via a spinning jet, electrospinning, melt blowing, and the paper-making method. The electrospinning is an emerging and versatile technique that facilitates a simple manufacturing process, fabricating continuous non-woven fibres with diameters ranging from nanometres to a few microns34–37. Wang et al.38 fabricated polytetrafluoroethylene/PVA (polyvinyl alcohol) derived nanofibers, and concluded that the prepared separator exhibits uniform zinc deposition due to its zincophilic groups37. Tanalue et al.39 fabricated a ZIF-8-filled polyacrylonitrile bio-based Polyurethane nanofibrous membrane, and the membrane exhibits an excellent cyclic stability up to 1000 h at 0.1 Ag− 1 with NVO layered cathode. In recent years, electrospun nanofiber separators have attracted significant attention due to several advantages, including tailorable structure, high aspect ratio, high ionic conductivity owing to uniform porosity, easy wettability by aqueous electrolytes, and unique network structure32,40,41. A polymer-based nonwoven nanofibrous membrane made of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), cellulose acetate, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), polyacrylonitrile (PAN), etc., fabricated via electrospinning, is the best alternative among recent advances in separators42. Endowed with the outstanding mechanical and thermal properties of PAN polymer nanofibers, they make these nanofibers ideal candidates for AZIBs to achieve high battery performance and stable cycle life. However, there are only subtle reports available on the encapsulation of graphene into a PAN polymer matrix to improve the mechanical, thermal, and electrochemical performance. Hence, in this work, we focused on commercial graphene reinforced at various concentrations (1 wt.%, 3 wt.%, and 5 wt.%) in PAN polymer, and the nanofibrous membranes were fabricated via electrospinning. The fabricated membranes were subjected to various characterizations to understand their morphology, structure, mechanical, and thermal properties, with the aim of utilizing these membranes as separators in AZIBs. The optimal graphene content was determined, and the separators performance was evaluated to assess the practical viability of the as-prepared separators. Zn-ion cells (coin 2032R type) have been prepared using commercial MnO2 as the cathode, Zn metal foil as anode material, 1 M ZnSO4 + 0.2 M MnSO4 as electrolyte with PP and PG membrane as separators. The as-fabricated cells (Zn//PP or PG//MnO2) with only PP and PG1 separators were subjected to various electrochemical characterization, including cyclic voltammetry, galvanostatic charge discharge, and electrochemical impedance analysis. Furthermore, the separators have been investigated after prolonged cycling to demonstrate their stability.

Experimental section

Materials

Polyacrylonitrile (PAN) (MW = 150,000, Otto Kemi, India), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), and Graphene (carbon horse) are used in the synthesis of the nanofibrous membrane. Zinc Sulphate Heptahydrate (ZnSO4.7H2O), Manganese Sulphate Monohydrate (MnSO4.H2O), Carbon cloth, Manganese (IV) oxide (MnO2), Acetylene Black, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, P8814, Otto Kemi, India), and 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used in this study.

Preparation of graphene scaffolds reinforced PAN electrospun nanofibrous separator

The graphene-scaffold-entrapped PAN nanofibrous separators were prepared by electrospinning, as shown in Fig. 1 (a, b). Initially, PAN solution was prepared by dissolving 3.6 g of PAN in 30 mL of DMF. To this prepared solution of PAN, varying concentrations of graphene (1 wt.%, 3 wt.%, and 5 wt.%) were added, and the mixture was ultrasonicated for 15 min. Later, the as-prepared 30 mL solution was transferred into a plastic syringe fitted with a 0.80 × 0.25 mm stainless steel needle, and a high-voltage source was connected to the needle. The electrospinning was carried out at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/h, a voltage of 15 kV, and a distance of 15 cm between the needle tip and the thin aluminium sheet collector. Four electrospun samples (PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5) of uniform thickness were prepared and subjected to various characterizations.

a Schematic representation of the fabrication of graphene scaffold reinforced PAN nanofibrous membrane via the electrospinning technique, and b diagrammatic representation of few-layer graphene in the PAN polymer matrix.

Preparation of the electrode

The cathode was composed of MnO2 (active substance), acetylene black (conductive agent), and PVDF (binder), which were mixed at a weight ratio of 85:10:5 wt%. First, the mixture of MnO2, acetylene black, and PVDF was uniformly ground in a mortar. A few drops of NMP solution were then added to form a homogeneous slurry. The slurry was cast onto a carbon cloth (0.75 cm x 0.75 cm current collector). Finally, the resulting electrodes were dried at 60 °C under vacuum for 12 h, then assembled into a coin cell. The assembly of the standard coin cell (CR2032) was fabricated by sandwiching the prepared membranes entrapped with the liquid electrolyte between the zinc foil and the MnO2 cathode with a mass loading of 3.2–3.5 mg cm− 2. The as-prepared materials have been subjected to various characterizations; the detailed characterization conditions and instrument details are given in the supporting information.

Results and discussion

Morphological and structural characterization

The surface morphology of PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 electrospun nanofiber membranes was examined using a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FE-SEM), as shown in Fig. 2. The PP electrospun fibers in Fig. 2 (a) exhibit a dense cross-stacked fiber architecture with continuous filaments. Similar to the morphology of the PP electrospun fiber, a more entangled network with flawless fiber formation is observed for PG1 and PG3 samples, as depicted in Fig. 2(b) and (c). However, when the graphene content increased to 5 wt.%, continuity of the fiber is disrupted, leading to particle coalescence, as evidenced in Fig. 2 (d). The average diameter of randomly selected fibers from multiple regions of the FE-SEM image was measured using Image J software, and the graphical representation is shown in Fig. 2 (e-h). The measurement indicates that the average fiber diameters are 374.60, 401.10, 417.90, and 393.40 nm for the samples PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5, respectively. It is observed that the average fiber diameter increases with increasing graphene concentration up to 3 wt% due to its conductivity, but at a higher graphene loading (5 wt.%) in the PAN solution, the conductivity effect becomes stronger, increasing electrostatic stretching and slightly reducing the fiber diameter compared to PG1 and PG3. The EDAX spectrum of PP in Fig. S1 (a) reveals prominent peaks attributable to carbon and nitrogen, which are the intrinsic constituents of the PAN polymer backbone. A minor signal for oxygen was also identified due to atmospheric absorption, and the absence of extraneous elements confirms the membrane’s chemical purity. Fig. S1 (b-d) depicts the EDAX spectra of PG1, PG3, and PG5, showing prominent peaks corresponding to carbon and nitrogen, similar to those of the PP membrane. The marked increase in the carbon signal may be due to the high carbon content in the graphene structure. Fig. S2 (a-d) shows elemental maping of the electrospun membrane, confirming the spatial distribution of carbon and nitrogen within the samples. The PP, PG1, and PG3 membranes have a uniform distribution of their respective elements, whereas the PG5 membrane appears to exhibit compositional zoning due to the high carbon content of few-layer graphene.

FE-SEM images (a–d) and average fiber diameter distribution (e–h) of PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 electrospun nanofibrous membranes.

The structural characteristics of the nanofibrous membrane were investigated using XRD analysis. Fig. 3 (a) displays the XRD spectrum of PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 composite membranes. From the figure, it is observed that the PP membrane exhibited a typical diffraction peak at 2θ = 16.9° as the characteristic peak, indicating a linear chain packed in a hexagonal manner, and the broad peak indicates the presence of organic molecules in the polymer matrix, pertinent to the (100) plane, indicating a semi-crystalline nature43. The broad diffraction peak is centered at approximately 2θ = 16.9°, predominantly indicating the amorphous nature, which affirms that the PP nanofibers are not fully crystalline. Upon the addition of graphene, significant changes in the XRD patterns are observed. The XRD spectra of PG1, PG3, and PG5 reflect an intense peak at 2θ = 26.5° corresponding to (002) plane44. The peak intensity increases with graphene concentration, indicating an ordered arrangement of graphitic atoms aligned within the associated lattice planes. Fig. S3 shows the XRD spectrum of few-layer graphene, depicting the characteristic graphitic peak at 2θ = 26.5°, which is consistent with the PAN- and graphene-added membranes.

a XRD and b FTIR spectra of the PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 non-woven nanofiber membranes.

Fig. 3 (b) indicates the FTIR spectra of the prepared PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 composite membranes. The FTIR spectrum of PP membrane exhibits characteristic peaks at ~ 3430 cm-1, indicating O-H stretching, ~ 2933 cm-1, attributed to asymmetric C-H2 stretching of the methylene group45 and ~ 1631 cm-1 represents the C = C stretching vibration46. At ~ 2249 cm-1, the peak is associated with the stretching vibration of the nitrile group , representing the characteristic functional group of PAN polymer. The peak at ~ 1660 cm-1 corresponds to the carbonyl stretching, confirming the presence of methacrylate (H2C=C(CH3) CO2R)47. The characteristic peaks obtained at~1453 cm-1 and ~ 1375 cm-1 indicate the bending vibrations of CH2 scissoring and CH3 groups48,49. Upon incorporation of graphene, PG1, PG3, and PG5 retain all major peaks, confirming the presence of PAN backbone. However, the noticeable reduction in intensity for O-H stretching (Fig. S4) suggests interfacial interactions between the functional groups of PAN polymer and -electron system of graphene, though - stacking. Thus, FTIR spectra corroborate the structural integrity of the PAN matrix and confirm the interaction of graphene within the PAN membranes.

Fig. S5 represents the Raman Spectra of the commercial few-layer graphene, depicting a D-band at 1344.88 cm-1, a G-band at 1566.60 cm-1, and a 2D-band at 2682.61 cm-1. Shang et al.50 reported that the bands represent different carbon networks. The G-band arises due to the first-order scattering of the E2g optical phonons at the Brillouin zone, corresponding to in-plane carbon atom stretching vibrations (i.e., Sp2 carbon atoms), and is prominent in every graphitic material. The D-band depicts the defects or the disordered structure, induced by the double-resonance processes, which reflects the characteristic of phonon dispersions, and the 2D band is the overtone representing the crystallinity or the stacking order. The peak at 2D band is broad and less symmetric, with an I2D/IG ratio of < 1, representing the few-layered structure50,51.

Membrane performance as a separator in battery application

Contact angle, electrolyte uptake, and porosity of the membrane

In general, the wettability of the membrane plays a very important role in the electrochemical properties, enabling electrolyte absorption and, hence, battery performance. The separator acts as an electrolyte reservoir, ensuring uniform ion flow, thereby increasing ionic conductivity and improving charge and discharge rates. Fig. 4 (a) shows the initial contact angle analysis of PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 membranes, measured after dropping 1 M ZnSO4 + 0.2 M MnSO4 electrolyte onto their surfaces. The initial contact angle values of PP, PG1, PG3 and PG5 membranes are measured to be 126.06°, 128.03°,129.41°, and 132.07°, and all the membrane exhibits hydrophobic nature due to the strong adsorption phenomenon, resulting in a high surface tension of the alkaline electrolyte on the surface of the polymer. Fig. S6 shows the contact angle over an hour; the electrolyte slowly spreads, and complete absorption is observed for all membranes, indicating the membranes hydrophilicity over time. This might be due to the presence of pores among the fibers observed across the random alignment of the fibrous morphology, as evident from the FE-SEM analysis (Fig. 2 (a-d)). Thus, the presence of pores can facilitate a continuous ionic conduction pathway between the electrodes, thereby enhancing the electrolyte’s ionic conductivity. This indicates that the PAN and graphene-incorporated PAN membranes can serve as promising separators for battery applications.

a Contact angle measurement and b Electrolyte uptake of the PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 nanofiber membranes with an electrolyte (1 M ZnSO4+0.2 M MnSO4).

Furthermore, the electrolyte uptake and porosity of the separator play a crucial role in determining battery performance, allowing more electrolyte to be absorbed by the membrane and creating pathways for ion penetration through the membranes porous surface. The maximum electrolyte uptake of the fabricated PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 membranes was measured by immersing the samples in a 1 M ZnSO4 solution with 0.2 M MnSO4 for 24 h. The values were measured to be 1118.99% ± 27.9, 1091% ± 23.9, 1007.06% ± 17.7, and 957.07% ± 27.9, respectively, and are presented in Fig. 4 (b), which shows no significant change in the electrolyte uptake among the membranes. The comparatively low electrolyte uptake value for the graphene-incorporated membranes compared to the PP membrane can be attributed to the presence of graphene. This might have reduced the pores present in the fiber and partially prevented electrolyte absorption, thereby retaining them within the membrane. Apart from the high electrolyte affinity between the membrane and the electrolyte, porosity also plays a vital role in battery performance by facilitating ionic transport between the electrodes. In addition, a preliminary test is conducted to determine porosity, and the detailed procedure is described in the supporting data. The values for porosity were measured based on the evaporation rate (Equation S2) of the tert-butanol electrolyte through the pores between the fibres, and are represented in Table S1. The measured membrane porosity percentage showed a decreasing trend for PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5, which may be attributed to pore blockage with increasing graphene concentration in the membrane. These results correlate well with the membrane’s electrolyte uptake (Fig. 4 (a)).

Mechanical and thermal stability of the membrane

A tensile test was performed to evaluate the mechanical properties of the electrospun membranes, as the strength of the separator is crucial for assessing battery safety performance and for withstanding physical pressure during assembly. Fig. 5 (a) shows the typical stress-strain curve of the PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 membranes. The tensile strength of the PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 membranes was 4.07, 4.18, 7.23, and 4.74 MPa, respectively, as depicted in Fig. 5 (b), which was obtained from the stress reached at the maximum point in the inelastic region. The noticeable increase in tensile strength up to the PG3 membrane is attributed to the strategic integration of graphene; this enhancement is facilitated by strong interfacial interactions and homogeneous dispersion of graphene within the PAN matrix at sufficient loading. Further loading of graphene leads to agglomeration, thereby preventing effective interfacial interactions between the particles and the polymer matrix. The irregular interface may disrupt polymer chain alignment, reducing the tensile strength of the PG5 membrane.

a Stress-strain curve, b Overall tensile strength, c TGA curves, and d Flame retardant performance tests of PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5 nanofiber membranes.

The thermal degradation behaviour of both PP and graphene-incorporated membranes was studied in air over the temperature range 0–700 °C. The TG curves corresponding to weight loss can be divided into three stages for all the samples. It can be clearly seen from Fig. 5 (c) that for the PP electrospun nanofiber, the first stage occurred up to 100 °C, characterized by considerable or minimal weight loss (i.e., moisture evaporation bound in the membrane). The second stage is the first decomposition stage, characterized by a sudden steep increase at ⁓318 °C, indicating a significant decomposition event with a maximum weight loss. The steepness at around 381.29 °C indicates the degradation of structural integrity. Above this temperature, the completion of the polymer backbone results in char residue52,53. A significant change in weight loss is observed for PG1, especially at approximately ⁓315 °C, but it is lower than that of PP; this may be due to the incorporation of graphene, which improves polymer bond strength. The measured weight loss was highest for PG3 membrane, indicating that the uniform distribution of graphene facilitates greater interaction with the atmosphere. The PG5 membrane has considerably lower weight loss than PG3, indicating that coalescence (Fig. 2 (d)) reduced the interaction of the graphene with atmospheric air, thereby further reducing the weight loss.

Flame retardancy, a critical safety parameter, was assessed via direct flame exposure tests. Flame was applied for 10 s, and a digital photograph was taken and is shown in Fig. 5 (d). Both PP and PG5 membranes combusted completely within few seconds of flame exposure, indicating poor fire resistance. However, the PG1 membrane exhibited superior flame-retardant properties, as evidenced by a longer ignition delay and a slower burn rate compared to other samples. This enhanced performance is consistent with the higher residue retention observed in TGA, underscoring the potential of graphene incorporation to improve thermal and fire resistance in electrospun membranes. The thermal shrinkage of the prepared membrane was assessed by placing the membrane in a hot-air oven at 120 °C for 6 h. Digital photographs of the membrane are captured before and after thermal treatment, as shown in Fig. S7. The PP membrane exhibited minimal shrinkage under these conditions, whereas the graphene-incorporated membranes showed no observable shrinkage. This indicates that the addition of graphene significantly enhances the dimensional stability of the membranes at elevated temperatures.

In essence, the PP membrane demonstrated superior porosity and electrolyte uptake; its thermal stability, mechanical strength, and flame retardancy were comparatively limited. The incorporation of graphene into the PAN matrix, even at low concentrations, significantly enhanced the membranes overall performance. Specifically, the graphene-incorporated PAN membranes exhibited improved mechanical properties, exceptional resistance to thermal shrinkage, and enhanced flame-retardant behaviour. These enhancements are attributed to the reinforcing effect of graphene, which not only enhances structural integrity but also reduces polymer degradation even under thermal conditions. Among the composites, the PG1 membrane emerged as the optimal formulation, striking a balance between structural stability and functional performance, making it suitable as a separator in battery applications.

Electrochemical performance of the separator

To assess the practical viability of the as-prepared separators, Zn//Zn symmetric cells were prepared using the developed membranes. The electrochemical performance of the symmetric cell is configured, and Fig. S8 (a) depicts the Nyquist plot of the Zn//X//Zn (X = PP, PG1, PG3, and PG5) cell configured using 1 M ZnSO4+0.2 M MnSO4 electrolyte. The Nyquist spectra for all the specimens exhibited a semicircular region corresponding to charge-transfer resistance (Rct) in the high-to-medium-frequency range, followed by a small Warburg diffusion at lower frequencies, which is associated with the diffusion of Zn²⁺ ions through the electrode–electrolyte interface. It is observed that the diameter of the semicircle is maximum for the PP membrane, followed by lower ion diffusion, whereas the diameter of the semicircle decreases for the PG1 membrane, indicating a very low charge transfer resistance, but with increasing concentration of graphene, demonstrating that the graphene incorporation effectively increases the interfacial resistance and restrict ion transport pathways between the graphene-incorporated PG separator and electrodes, Fig. S8 (b) represents the cyclic stability test of the zinc anode analysed using the Zn//X = PP, and PG1/Zn cells with PP and PG1 membranes at 5 mA cm-2 specific current density; the cell with a PP separator exhibits a lower voltage after the 30th cycle, indicating an irregular stability or the possibility of short circuit after few cycles. Conversely, the PG1 separator demonstrated very good cycling stability even after 30 cycles, without any short-circuit or abrupt cycling behaviour. Symmetric cell with PG1 membrane exhibits a voltage hysteresis of 166 mV, which is lower than that of the PP membranes (215 mV). This further confirms the ease of stripping and plating of Zn, which improves the uniform, homogeneous deposition of zinc on the anode, enhancing cyclic stability. These findings confirm that, compared to the PP separator, PG1 exhibits a longer cycle life.

Fig. 6 (a) displays the Nyquist plot of the Zn//X (PP, and PG1)//MnO2 measured in 1 M ZnSO₄ + 0.2 M MnSO₄ electrolyte using a Swagelok cell configuration. The diameter of the curve is smaller for the PG1 separator than for the PP, indicating that PG1 has lower Rct and is therefore more compatible and stable with the electrodes. Moreover, the Warburg diffusion is found to be maximum for PG1 compared to PP, which might be due to the interaction between the Zn-ion active material and the membrane, which minimizes charge accumulation and improves electrolyte wettability at the interface. The porosity and electrolyte uptake might also facilitate ion transport, indicating a secondary pathway for ions to pass efficiently through the membrane. The increase in the Warburg diffusion value of the PG1 membrane suggests that it can be used as a separator and that it is high enough for battery applications54. The curve was fitted (Fig. 6 (b)) using an equivalent circuit, R1 + [Q2/(R2 + W2)], depicted in Fig. S9, and its values are shown in Table S2. In the circuit, R1 represents the solution resistance across the electrode and separator, R2 represents the charge-transfer resistance, Q2 represents the non-ideal double-layer capacitance (CPE), and W2 represents the Warburg diffusion, respectively. From Table S2, the charge-transfer values for PP and PG1 are 196.1 and 162.4 ohm, respectively. Further, to understand the battery’s performance, it is necessary to determine the ionic conductivity of the material through which ions can be transported easily between the electrodes. The ionic conductivity depends on various parameters, including thickness, porosity, electrolyte uptake, and the material’s structural morphology. These factors contribute to the overall ionic conductivity of the separator. From Equation S3, the PP separator has an ionic conductivity of 1.96 × 10− 3 S/cm, and the PG1 membrane has an ionic conductivity of 5.74 × 10− 3 S/cm. This enhanced ionic conductivity of PG1 may be attributed to the contribution of graphene which introduces a 2D interconnected network within the polymer matrix. As a result, continuous channels for Zn²⁺ migration rather than relying solely on PAN polymer segments will be created, improving the ionic pathway without clogging ion transport55,56.

To evaluate the charging and discharging performance of the Zn//(X = PP, PG1)//MnO2 cells, the rate performance was measured at various currents ranging from 0.3 to 4 mA as shown in Fig. 6 (d). The results indicate that the discharge specific capacities of the PP separator are slightly lower than those of the PG1 separator. The discharge specific capacities of the PP separators were 251.08, 228.76, 199.71, 182.31, 159.12, 131.27, 107.75, 86.89, 67.43, 51.17, and 22.19 mAh g− 1 capacity at 0.3, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1, 1.4, 1.8, 2.2, 2.6, 3, and 4 mA. The PG1 separator achieved 287.39, 281.25, 253.77, 225.24, 205.94, 180.45, 162.62, 150.16, 142.79, 130.57, and 109.17 mAh g− 1, which is much higher than that of the cell with a PP separator under the same current density. The high capacity may be attributed to the stable reversibility of the zinc anode and enhanced ionic conduction between the separators, resulting from lower ionic resistance. But a decay in the discharge capacities of the PP membrane is observed, which is due to polarisation. However, the PG1 membrane exhibits stable, excellent rate performance due to the structural stability of the anode and cathode materials preserved by the separator. This separator retains more zinc ions and provides a much smoother ion-transport channel. Hence, PG1 separator has a faster ion migration rate, even at high current densities, and exhibits improved discharge specific capacities.

The cell with the PG1 separator exhibits a longer discharge time and better reversibility than the cell with the PP separator, confirming the greater storage capacity of the PG1 separator cell. The incorporation of graphene enhances the reversibility of the cells, thereby reducing internal side reactions, lowering polarization, and facilitating smooth operation and prolonged operation. The cycle performance of the battery was tested at 1 mA with the Zn//X = PP and PG1//MnO2 assembled full cells at 25 °C, as shown in Fig. 8 (a, b). The cell with the PG1 separator exhibits a good Coulombic efficiency during cycling and a comparatively greater discharge time, indicating better performance of the cell. The operating time of the PG1 separator exceeds 250 h, while the PP separator operates for only 160 h. The impedance of the cell after 100 cycles was recorded and is reported in Fig. S12, indicating that the PG1 separator exhibits a lower semicircle depicting a lower Rct than the PP separator after 100 cycles, validating a greater ion transfer. The full cells with a PP separator have an initial discharge capacity of 111.5 mAh g− 1, and a PG1 separator has a discharge capacity of 189.8 mAh g− 1 after 10 cycles. The subsequent capacity of full cells was noted and is shown in Fig. 8 (c, d). Still, the capacity was comparable up to 200 cycles and is reported in Fig. S13. After 200 cycles, the cell with the PP separator drops suddenly to minimal capacity, and specific, uncertain chemical reactions occur at the full cell (PP separator); the stability and performance of the cells drop. Comparatively, the cell with a PG1 membrane runs well after 200 cycles; additionally, the PG1 separator exhibits excellent electrochemical performance. Although the initial capacity was not very high, it began to follow an increasing trend after 300 cycles and can withstand upto 1000 cycles; this sequence is commonly observed in many metal oxide composites56–58. The noted unconventional behaviour is due to a mechanism that occurs during the charging and discharging process. Firstly, the material undergoes an activation phase, which is due to the intercalation and deintercalation of zinc ions, initiating structural deformation that unveils a larger number of reactive sites and defects, thereby revealing more active sites and increasing capacity in the cathode side, while in the anode side it is mainly due to the graphene nanofiber which enhances the regulated deposition of zinc on the surface i.e., PG1 acts as a multifunctional separator; the nitrile functional group in the polymer matrix improves the ionic conductivity exhibits a very high adsorption property allows only Zn2+ ions to pass through the membrane efficiently and the dispersed graphene with the hexagonal carbon bonding shows a better desolvation of [Zn (H2O)6]2+ by interfacial reactions, and reduce the water-related side reactions, creating an abundant number of sites for zinc deposition during charging and discharging and enhances the uniform deposition of zinc on the anode, which inhibits the dendrite growth. In addition, the post-analysis is reported in Fig. S14 (a-d). The photographic image confirms that the zinc foil of the cell with the PP separator has a rougher or coarser surface than the zinc foil with the PG1 separator, representing that the cell with the PG1 separator exhibits uniform deposition of the ions and the excellent diffusion of the Zn2+ ions. The FE-SEM image and the Ex-situ XRD pattern of the Zn//X = PP, and PG1//MnO2 of the zinc foil after the cyclic stability test further confirm the dendrite growth suppression. The FE-SEM image of the zinc foil in the Fig. S15 (a, b) has a flake-like formation, whereas the foil with PG1 separator has a smooth surface and the XRD analysis confirms the zinc foil with PP separator has an extra peak at 2 = 15°, 25°,and 32°, confirming the formation of ZnSO4.3Zn (OH)2.H2O (Fig. S15 (c)), resulting in the corrosion of the zinc foil and the uneven zinc deposition is observed during the charging and discharging. The foil with the PG1 membrane has no extra peak below 2 = 35°, indicating an efficient cycle life for very long cycles. Thus, these results confirm the successful replacement of a graphene scaffold-incorporated polyacrylonitrile electrospun nanofibrous membrane (PG1) as a separator in Zinc-ion batteries, improving their cycle life and electrochemical performance. Additionally, a comparison with reported PAN based separators shows that graphene based electrospun membrane delivers better performance (Table S3).

Conclusion

In summary, the graphene scaffolds reinforced polyacrylonitrile nanofiber membrane with varying graphene concentrations (1, 3, and 5 wt.%) as PG1, PG3, and PG5 were fabricated via a one-step electrospinning process. The 1 wt% graphene-added PAN membrane exhibited excellent thermal stability and electrochemical performance compared to the PP membrane. The symmetric cell plating and stripping studies confirmed that the cell with PG1 separator requires very low potential, resulting in easy Zinc plating and stripping on the anode during the charging and discharging process. The Zn//X//MnO2 (X = PP, PG1) cell configuration with PG1 membrane exhibits a high ionic conductivity of 5.74 × 10− 3 S cm− 1, a good retention capability and excellent cyclic stability running over 1000 cycles, effectively suppressing the dendrite growth by enabling homogeneous Zn deposition, but the PP separator runs over only 200 cycles efficiently, and the cell gets short-circuited. A higher specific capacity of 154 mAh g− 1 is maintained after 50 cycles, and the Zn//PP//MnO2 battery maintained a specific capacity of 100 mAh g− 1, verifying that the PG1 membrane was a better separator in AZIBs due to its physical, chemical, and electrochemical characteristics. Overall, studies have shown that the PG1 membrane as a separator significantly improves the cycling stability of the Zinc anode and is employed as a promising separator in AZIBs.
