Effects of multicomponent exercise on quality of life, coping with stress, and psychological well-being in aged women: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Sibel Çaynak, Nur Elçin Boyacıoğlu, Mine Oruç, Elif Yakışan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMulticomponent exercise programmes are increasingly used to address age-related physical and psychosocial challenges. However, evidence regarding their effects on quality of life, coping with stress, and psychological well-being in older women remains limited.AimThis study aimed to determine the effects of multicomponent exercise on quality of life, coping with stress, and well-being in elderly women.Materials and methodsThis study was conducted as a randomized controlled trial with pre-test and post-test measurements. A total of 40 elderly women were randomly assigned to the intervention and control groups using a simple randomization method. The intervention group performed multicomponent exercise twice a week for eight weeks. The control group did not perform any exercises. Data were collected using a Personal Information Form and the World Health Organization Quality of Life Short Form, Stress Coping Style Scale and Well-Being Scale. In the data analysis, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed, with baseline (pre-test) measurements included as covariates.ResultsA statistically significant difference was found between the experimental and control groups in post-test scores of the physical domain of the Quality of Life Scale (F = 4.72, p = 0.036). For the Stress Coping Styles Scale, statistically significant differences were observed between post-test scores of the helpless approach (F = 8.46, p = 0.006) and the submissive approach (F = 6.42, p = 0.016). No statistically significant difference was found between the post-test scores of the Well-Being Scale (F = 0.98, p = 0.328).ConclusionThe findings of this study indicate that the multicomponent exercise program did not result in a statistically significant change in well-being among older women. However, statistically significant differences were identified in the physical domain of quality of life and in coping styles, specifically the helpless and submissive approaches. These findings suggest that the program may contribute to certain aspects of quality of life and stress coping. Further studies with longer intervention periods and follow-up assessments are recommended. Exercise programs integrating physical and psychosocial components may be explored to better address the multidimensional needs of older adults.Trial registrationRegistry: ClinicalTrials.gov, Trial registration number: NCT06849427Initial data of registration: 01.14.2025 Last data of registration: 07.24.2025Keywords: Aged, Exercise therapy, Physical activity intervention, Randomized controlled trial, Stress management

Introduction

Current geriatric studies focus on active ageing and emphasise the importance of exercise for a healthy ageing process [1]. Inactivity accentuates the progressive loss of autonomy and quality of life of older people at an alarming rate [2]. Unlike general physical activity, multicomponent exercise consists of structured, planned, and supervised exercise sessions designed to improve specific physical and psychosocial outcomes. Although physical activity programs are supported in the elderly [3], recommendations vary according to the specific needs of individuals and the degree of aging. Exercise programs that increase strength, facilitate the performance of daily living activities, strengthen posture, and reduce muscle fiber weakening are recommended for physical and psychosocial development [4]. In a study comparing three different types of exercise in elderly women, it was found that aerobic activity or strength training provided great benefits [5].

Health problems in old age, loss of professional and social status, loss of function and increased dependence on others can all negatively impact quality of life and well-being [6, 7]. Multicomponent training increases expectations of living independently and improves the quality of life [8]. A study with individuals over 60 years of age showed that a multi-component exercise program is recommended to improve quality of life [9]. Sedentary older adults have more anxiety, depression, and stress than those who exercise [10]. Older individuals may use more proactive and predictive coping methods to avoid stressors [11]. Exercise has recently been highlighted as a method used to cope with excessively stressful life events [12, 13]. Exercise may also have benefits on psychological variables such as depressive symptoms, anxiety, perceived social support, and loneliness [14, 15].

Well-being is a subjective experience characterized by happiness or contentment. For older adults, well-being often stems from their success in life experiences [16]. Increasing well-being helps older adults maintain their health and encourages them to adopt a more positive outlook on life. The findings of studies investigating the effects of exercise on well-being in older adults vary significantly [17]. While some studies have found a positive association between exercise and well-being in older individuals [18, 19], others have found no association [20, 21].

Multicomponent exercise programmes have gained increasing attention in older populations due to their potential to address multiple age-related physical and psychological challenges simultaneously. While previous studies have demonstrated the benefits of exercise interventions on physical outcomes and general quality of life in older adults, evidence regarding the combined effects of multicomponent exercise on quality of life, coping with stress, and psychological well-being in older women remains limited. Therefore, investigating the effects of a multicomponent exercise programme on these psychosocial outcomes may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of its potential benefits for older women. This study aimed to determine the effects of multicomponent exercise on quality of life, coping with stress, and well-being in older women. The study aimed to establish whether multicomponent exercise has a positive impact on quality of life, well-being and coping strategies in older women, thereby contributing to existing knowledge on the health benefits of such exercise.

Method

Research design

This study was designed as a parallel-group randomized controlled trial with pre-test and post-test measurements. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for randomized trials [22]. The trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06849427). The study’s dependent variables were quality of life, stress coping and well-being, while the independent variable was a multicomponent exercise intervention.

The research hypotheses

H1: Multi-component exercise interventions can improve quality of life in older women.

H1: Multi-component exercise interventions can improve quality of life in older women.

H2: Multi-component exercise interventions positively impact stress management in older women.

H2: Multi-component exercise interventions positively impact stress management in older women.

H3: Multi-component exercise interventions improve well-being in older women.

H3: Multi-component exercise interventions improve well-being in older women.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the exercise intervention, blinding of participants and intervention providers was not feasible. However, outcome assessments and statistical analyses were performed by a researcher who was blinded to group allocation. Participants were not informed about the study hypotheses or expected direction of outcomes in order to minimize expectancy effects.

Location and date of research

The research was conducted at the Antalya Active Elderly Centre. Institutional permission was obtained for the study. Following approval from the Ethics Committee, the study commenced and the multi-component exercise programme was conducted between March and May 2025.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software based on the primary outcome variable, quality of life. A moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.50) was assumed, in line with previous exercise intervention studies in older adults reporting small-to-moderate changes in quality of life outcomes [23]. With a two-tailed significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, a minimum sample size of 20 participants per group was required. No additional sample size inflation was applied, as all participants completed the study and no missing outcome data were observed.

Participants and eligibility

Women aged 60 years and older who were registered at the Active Elderly Centre were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: being female, aged ≥ 60 years, not in the acute phase of a systemic disease, not participating in regular structured exercise, and being able to communicate verbally and complete self-report questionnaires independently.

Participants with diagnosed neurological or psychiatric disorders affecting cognitive functioning, or with medical conditions contraindicating physical exercise, were excluded. Cognitive status was assessed informally by healthcare staff at the centre based on functional communication ability and orientation.

Data collection tools

Personal information form

This 8-question form covers participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, the presence of chronic diseases and their exercise status.

World health organization quality of life scale short form

This is a shortened version of the 100-question World Health Organization Quality of Life Assessment (WHOQOL) designed to assess an individual’s perception of their quality of life. The scale includes closed-ended questions and consists of four subdomains: physical, social, environmental and psychological. Questions 3, 4, 10, 15, 16, 17 and 18 make up the physical domain; questions 5, 6, 7, 11, 19 and 26 make up the psychological domain; and questions 20, 21 and 22 make up the social domain. The environmental domain consists of questions 8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 23, 24 and 25. There is no perfect score on the scale, and an increase in score indicates an improvement in quality of life. A Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Eser and colleagues in 1999 [24]. During this study, a national question was added to the scale, bringing the total number of questions to 27.

The Styles of Coping Scale (SCTQ)

Developed by Folkman and Lazarus in 1988, it was adapted into Turkish by Şahin and Durak in 1995 [25]. Validity and reliability studies were subsequently conducted [26]. Subsequently, Şahin and Durak (1995) adapted the scale for use with university students. The scale comprises 30 items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all appropriate, 1 = not appropriate, 2 = appropriate, 3 = completely appropriate). Items 1 and 9 are the only ones that are reverse-scored. The two-dimensional scale consists of five subsections. The first dimension focuses on solving the encountered problem effectively and is called ‘problem-oriented/active’. This dimension consists of the subsections ‘seeking social support’, ‘optimistic approach’, and ‘self-confident approach’. The other dimension, emotional coping, is considered ineffective and is called ‘emotion-oriented/passive’. This dimension consists of the subsections “Helpless approach” and “Submissive approach”. Individuals who can cope with stress effectively use the active approach more often, while those who cannot cope use the passive approach more often. Higher scores indicate that an individual uses a particular style more frequently (Şahin & Durak, 1995). The self-confident approach is measured by items 8, 10, 14, 16, 20, 23 and 26; the helpless approach by items 3, 7, 11, 19, 22, 25, 27 and 28; the submissive approach by items 5, 13, 15, 17, 21 and 24; the optimistic approach by items 2, 4, 6 and 12; and the social support-seeking approach by items 1, 9, 29 and 30. A total score is not obtained from the scale. Instead, the total score for each subscale is calculated by dividing the sum of the scores for that subscale by the number of items used to calculate it. A higher score indicates that the individual uses that approach more frequently [25].

Well-Being scale

The Well-Being Scale was developed by Diener et al. (2009) [27] and adapted into Turkish by Fidan and Usta (2013) [28]. The scale has a 7-point Likert-type structure. The highest possible score is 56 and the lowest is 8; there are no reverse-scored items. High scores indicate a high level of well-being for the individual.

Procedure

The study was conducted at the XXX Active Elderly Centre. The purpose of the study was explained to both groups of individuals who agreed to participate and met the inclusion criteria, and randomisation was performed. Both groups completed a pretest, after which the intervention group undertook 60 min of multi-component exercise twice a week for eight weeks. Post-tests were collected from both groups after the study had been completed.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Randomisation involves randomly assigning participants to intervention and control groups. This means that each participant has an equal and known probability of being assigned to either group [29]. Participants were allocated to the intervention and control groups using a simple randomization method. A total of 40 eligible participants were numbered, and group assignment was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence. Allocation to the intervention or control group was based on the randomization sequence. To prevent selection bias and ensure allocation concealment, the randomization process was conducted by an independent researcher. Data collection process

Registration with ClinicalTrial was completed before the study began (NCT06849427). Data collection began after ethics committee approval and institutional permission were obtained. Informed consent forms were obtained from individuals who agreed to participate in the study, and they were informed about the research. The intervention group performed a multi-component exercise routine in an open area adjacent to the institution. The study involved 40 participants: 20 in the intervention group and 20 in the control group. After randomisation, both groups underwent pre-tests. The experimental group performed 60 min of multi-component exercise twice a week for eight weeks. Data were collected between March and May 2025. The forms and questionnaires were completed in written form by the participants. During data collection, for participants who requested assistance, the researcher read the items aloud, and the responses were marked on the forms according to the participants’ self-reports without any guidance or influence.

All outcome measures were collected at baseline and immediately after the 8-week intervention period. Assessments were conducted under similar conditions for both groups. Outcome assessments were conducted by a researcher who was blinded to group allocation. All assessments were performed at similar times of day for both groups to minimize potential diurnal variation. There were no missing outcome data, and all randomized participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis.

Intervention

The exercise sessions were led by a trainer who holds a degree and a master’s degree in Physical Education and Sports Sciences, as well as a coaching certificate and training in Feelings, Wellness and Yoga. Two researchers participated in the study and accompanied the sessions. The training area was checked by the researchers before each session to ensure a comfortable environment for the participants. The training was conducted with light music, and materials such as balance bars, exercise bands and ankle weights were used, depending on what was suitable for each individual. The multi-component exercise programme included flexibility training, strength training, balance training, and mobility training (centre of gravity control training, multisensory training, postural strategy training, gait pattern variation training, and dual-task exercises), as well as cardiorespiratory fitness training. Exercise intensity was prescribed at a low-to-moderate level appropriate for older adults, and all sessions followed a standardized structure. Progression was applied gradually based on participants’ tolerance and physical capacity. Attendance was systematically recorded to monitor adherence to the intervention protocol.

Data analysis

The data obtained in the study were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, version 25. Descriptive statistical methods (number, percentage, minimum, maximum, mean and standard deviation) were employed. The conformity of the data to a normal distribution was tested. Conformity to a normal distribution can be examined using a Q-Q plot. Additionally, a normal distribution of the data depends on the skewness and kurtosis values being between ± 3, and this was examined using the relevant values. An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted with pretest scores entered as a covariate. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta squared (η²). A significance level of 0.05 was used to determine whether the obtained values were significant.

Among the control group participants, 65% were married, 10% were divorced and 25% were widowed. Ninety-five per cent of participants were identified as having some form of social security. Furthermore, 30% reported that their income was less than their expenses, 45% that it was equal, and 20% that it was greater. 85% of participants lived in the city centre and 15% lived in the district. Participants were found to be homogeneously distributed across groups in terms of age, gender, marital status, health insurance status, income, residence, partners, smoking status, and alcohol consumption (p > 0.05). However, the distribution of chronic diseases was not homogeneous; in other words, the control group had a higher rate of chronic diseases than the experimental group.

Table 2 presents the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) for the Quality of Life Scale. When the pretest score was controlled as a covariate, a statistically significant difference was observed between the experimental and control groups in posttest scores of the physical domain (F = 4.72, p = 0.036). The mean score of the experimental group (66.79 ± 12.96) was higher than that of the control group (61.43 ± 13.74). The partial eta squared value for the group effect (η² = 0.113) indicated that 11.3% of the variance in physical domain scores was explained by the intervention. When the pretest score was controlled as a covariate, no statistically significant difference was found between the experimental and control groups in posttest scores of the psychological domain (F = 3.24, p = 0.080). Similarly, no statistically significant difference was observed between the experimental and control groups in posttest scores of the social relationships domain (F = 2.22, p = 0.144) when the pretest score was controlled as a covariate. Likewise, no statistically significant difference was found between the experimental and control groups in posttest scores of the environmental domain after controlling for the pretest score as a covariate (F = 0.55, p = 0.463).

Discussion

As the elderly population increases, the concept of healthy ageing has come to the fore, as has the quality of life achieved. The quality of life experienced by older individuals is a key indicator of healthy ageing. Physical activity and exercise are crucial for older adults to perform and maintain daily activities independently, thereby improving their quality of life [30, 31]. A systematic review examining the effects of physical exercise on quality of life in older adults reported varying degrees of positive effects [32]. Another study found that moderate to vigorous intensity aerobic physical activity was positively associated with quality of life in older adults with or without physical activity limitations [33]. In addition to its impact on general physical health, exercise also benefits mental health. It has been reported to alleviate pain and reduce psychological problems, leading to an improved quality of life [34]. A different study showed that there is an indirect relationship between sleep quality, mental health and quality of life [35].

The absence of a significant effect on overall quality of life suggests that a short-term multi-component exercise program alone may be insufficient to influence this multidimensional outcome in older adults. However, the observed improvement in the physical domain indicates that such interventions may preferentially affect physical aspects of quality of life before broader psychological, social, or environmental changes become evident. Physical functioning may be more responsive to short-term exercise interventions, whereas changes in psychological and social domains of quality of life may require longer intervention periods or additional psychosocial components. While the health benefits of an active lifestyle are well recognised, older individuals often avoid exercise, perceiving themselves as too old or unfit to participate in physical activity. Consequently, participation rates in exercise are low [36]. One of the inclusion criteria was that participants did not exercise regularly. The lack of prior exercise habits and the relatively short duration of the intervention (two months) may have limited its impact on quality-of-life outcomes.

As people age, they may face challenges such as increased loneliness, bereavement, frailty and leaving the workforce, which can exacerbate stress levels [37]. However, exercise offers a potential way of safely reducing stress [38]. A review of randomised controlled trials involving older individuals provides supporting evidence that exercise, particularly qigong, is associated with reductions in perceived stress. Combining aerobic and anaerobic exercise has also been found to effectively reduce stress [39]. A study with older people in a nursing home examined the effect of exercise on perceived stress and found a reduction in stress levels [40]. Furthermore, another study suggests that moderate exercise significantly improves emotional positivity in older adults and can effectively regulate stress hormone levels, improving cognitive appraisal of psychological stress [41].

The observed reduction in helpless and submissive coping strategies indicates that the multi-component exercise program may contribute to decreasing passive coping responses to stress in older adults. In contrast the lack of a significant change in self-confident and optimistic coping approaches suggests that the intervention may not have been sufficient to influence positive coping strategies in the short term. As the development of such positive coping strategies is closely related to personality traits, social environment and life experiences, longer-term interventions or exercise programmes incorporating psychosocial support may be necessary. A decrease in scores was observed in the social support-seeking approach subscale, but this change was not significant. This may be because individuals experience less need for social support during group exercise. Alternatively, it suggests that the limited perception of available social support resources or low expectations in this regard among older individuals may also have influenced the results.

A holistic state of physiological, psychological and social well-being in older age is considered a basic indicator of the healthy aging process [42]. One study found that physical exercise had a positive effect on subjective well-being in older individuals [43]. Exercise in older age is an effective way to increase energy levels, maintain functional independence, protect cardiovascular health, reduce disease and pain symptoms, as well as contribute to weight control. In addition, regular physical activity has positive effects on cognitive functions and is known to play a healing role in mental clarity, mood balance, and memory [44]. In a different study conducted on older individuals, Tai Chi exercise was found to have a positive effect on subjective well-being [45]. Moderate-intensity physical exercise performed by older individuals offers multifaceted health benefits. Such activities; It contributes to the improvement of balance, flexibility, coordination, muscle strength, cognitive functions, and general mental health [46]. Although subjective well-being scores showed a slight increase, the lack of statistical significance may reflect the limited duration of the intervention or the complexity of well-being as an outcome influenced by multiple psychosocial factors, which may require longer-term or more comprehensive interventions to achieve measurable change. Subjective well-being is a complex construct shaped by long-term psychological, social, and life-course factors, which may be less sensitive to change following short-term exercise interventions.

Conclusıon and recommendatıons

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that an 8-week multicomponent exercise programme was associated with a reduction in helpless and submissive coping strategies among older women, suggesting a beneficial effect on passive stress-coping responses. In addition, the intervention led to a significant improvement in the physical domain of quality of life, while no statistically significant effects were observed in the psychological, social, or environmental domains. These findings indicate that short-term multicomponent exercise interventions may preferentially influence physical functioning and certain stress-coping mechanisms before broader psychosocial outcomes become evident.

No statistically significant improvement was observed in overall quality of life or subjective well-being following the intervention. This may reflect the multidimensional and complex nature of these outcomes, which are shaped by long-term psychological, social, and contextual factors and may require longer intervention durations or the inclusion of additional psychosocial components to achieve measurable change.

Based on these results, multicomponent exercise programmes may be considered a complementary approach to support physical functioning and reduce passive stress-coping strategies in older adults. Future research should focus on longer-term interventions, larger and more diverse samples, and the integration of psychosocial and behavioural components to better address complex outcomes such as overall quality of life and subjective well-being.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, there was a baseline imbalance in the prevalence of chronic diseases between the experimental and control groups, with a significantly higher proportion of participants with chronic conditions in the control group. Although baseline scores were controlled for using ANCOVA, this difference may have influenced physical functioning and stress-coping outcomes and should be considered when interpreting the results. Because the study sample was limited to elderly women living in a specific geographic area who were willing to participate, the generalisability of the findings to the entire elderly population is limited. Furthermore, the study was conducted exclusively with women, preventing the assessment of gender-related differences. As the exercise programme was relatively short, it is not possible to draw conclusions about long-term effects. Self-report measures were used to assess variables such as quality of life, stress management and well-being, which may have influenced responses based on subjective perceptions and social desirability. Finally, participants’ regular exercise participation and physical or social activities outside the home were not fully controlled during the intervention period, which could have affected the results.
