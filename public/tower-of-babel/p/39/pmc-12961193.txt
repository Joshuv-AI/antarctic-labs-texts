Stable and functional regulatory T cell attenuates fibrotic remodeling in heart failure
Authors: Takura Taguchi, Takuji Kawamura, Norihisa Mikami, Kosuke Torigata, Akima Harada, Lisa Fujimura, Emiko Ito, Maki Takeda, Kenji Miki, Shimon Sakaguchi, Shigeru Miyagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHeart failure involves myocardial fibrosis and impaired cardiac function. The transverse aortic constriction (TAC) model in mice replicates pressure overload-induced fibrosis. Immune-mediated inflammation, particularly through T lymphocytes and macrophages, plays a pivotal role in the progression of fibrosis. In vitro–induced regulatory T cells (Tregs) are limited by Foxp3 instability. To address this, stable and functional in vitro induced Tregs (S/F-iTregs) have been developed under CD28-deprived conditions, exhibiting sustained Foxp3 expression and epigenetic stability. We aimed to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of intravenously administered S/F-iTregs in a murine TAC model of heart failure.MethodsS/F-iTregs were generated from CD4+ conventional T cells under CD28 costimulation-deficient conditions. BALB/c mice underwent TAC surgery and received S/F-iTregs intravenously. Cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography. Myocardial fibrosis was evaluated using histological staining. Bulk RNA sequencing was performed to assess transcriptomic changes in the myocardium.ResultsS/F-iTreg administration significantly improved left ventricular function and reduced myocardial fibrosis compared to those in controls. PKH26-labeled S/F-iTregs were detected within the cardiac tissue, confirming in vivo migration. RNA sequencing revealed downregulation of inflammation- and fibrosis-related pathways, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha/nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cell, platelet-derived growth factor, transforming growth factor-beta signaling, and modulation of macrophage-related transcriptional programs.ConclusionsSystemic administration of S/F-iTregs attenuates cardiac fibrosis and preserves cardiac function in pressure overload–induced heart failure. These findings support S/F-iTreg–based immunomodulation as a potential approach to limit adverse fibrotic cardiac remodeling.Keywords: Heart failure, Regulatory T cells, Fibrosis, Immunomodulation, Transverse aortic constriction

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a major global health burden, characterized by a gradual decline in cardiac function and high morbidity and mortality rates, which continue to increase annually [1,2]. A pivotal feature underlying HF progression is myocardial remodeling, particularly cardiac fibrosis, characterized by excessive deposition of collagen and other extracellular matrix proteins leading to ventricular stiffness and impaired cardiac contractility [3,4].

Growing evidence suggests that immune-mediated inflammation is a central driver of cardiac fibrosis in HF [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. In response to pressure overload, inflammatory cells, such as T lymphocytes and macrophages, infiltrate the myocardial tissue and release pro-inflammatory cytokines that induce fibroblast activation, cardiomyocyte injury, and extracellular matrix remodeling [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Mice lacking T and B lymphocytes (Rag2−/− mice) are resistant to transverse aortic constriction (TAC)-induced HF and adoptive transfer of CD4+ T cells reconstitutes cardiac dysfunction and fibrosis [8]. Recent evidence indicates that Mac-1 deficiency attenuates TAC-induced HF by suppressing pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and promoting reparative M2 polarization, thereby limiting pathological remodeling [9].

Regulatory T cells (Tregs), characterized by CD4, CD25, and Foxp3 expression, are crucial suppressors of immune activation and tissue inflammation [10,11]. Interleukin (IL)-2/anti-IL-2 complex-induced endogenous Treg expansion mitigates inflammation, fibrosis, and functional decline in TAC models [12]. However, the mechanisms underlying the effects of systemic administration of Treg in pressure-overload HF remain unclear. Current adoptive Treg therapies predominantly utilize natural Tregs (nTregs); however, their clinical efficacy remains limited due to challenges including low cell yield, difficulty in achieving robust expansion in vitro, and poor persistence following in vivo infusion. To address these limitations, the artificial in vitro-induced Tregs (iTregs) have been extensively investigated in both experimental and clinical contexts [13]. Despite this progress, iTregs have shown limited therapeutic benefit, largely owing to unstable Foxp3 expression and low suppressive capacity. Recently, efforts to improve iTreg stability and function have led to the development of stable and functional iTregs (S/F-iTregs), generated through approaches such as co-stimulation removal, CDK8/19 inhibition, and repeated stimulation [14,15]. Consequently, these S/F-iTregs demonstrate enhanced proliferation and survival compared with nTregs and represent a promising strategy for advancing the clinical application of Treg-based therapies [14,15].

We hypothesized that S/F-iTregs suppress fibrotic remodeling via immunomodulation. As the in vivo stability of S/F-iTregs has been previously reported [14,15], we aimed to evaluate the therapeutic impact of S/F-iTregs in a murine TAC model, specifically assessing their ability to mitigate myocardial inflammation, fibrosis, and cardiac dysfunction.

Methods

Animals and TAC surgery

Male BALB/c mice (8–10 weeks old) were purchased from CLEA Japan, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). TAC surgery was performed under general anesthesia via partial sternotomy. The TAC model was created by ligating the aortic arch between the brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries using a 27G needle. At 1 week post-surgery, mice with reduced cardiac function, as assessed by echocardiography, were included in the experimental group. All experiments were approved by the Ethics Review Committee for Animal Experimentation of Osaka University (approval no. 04-037-000). Animals were handled in compliance with the Principles of Laboratory Animal Care formulated by the National Society for Medical Research and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health.

Induction of S/F-iTreg and dendritic cell (DC)

The cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 60 μg/mL penicillin G, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol. Conventional T cells (Tconv) were isolated from the spleens and lymph nodes of BALB/c mice using CD4 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and Magnetic Separators for cell separation (Miltenyi Biotec). To generate S/F-iTregs, Tconv were stimulated with plate-bound anti-CD3e (clone 2C11, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) in the presence of recombinant human IL-2 (Imunace®35, Kyowa Pharmaceutical Industry Co., Osaka, Japan) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)1 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Importantly, to ensure CD28 costimulation-deficient conditions, no anti-CD28 antibody was added, following previously established protocols (Fig. 1a) [14,15]. After 3 days of initial stimulation, cells were washed and transferred to fresh IL-2–containing media for a 4-day resting culture. Subsequently, cells were re-stimulated for 2 additional days with plate-bound anti-CD3e, IL-2, and TGF-β1, supplemented with anti-FasL antibody (clone MFL3, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Bone marrow-derived DCs were obtained by culturing femoral bone marrow cells in a medium containing granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) (R&D Systems) [16].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Treg and Tconv were adjusted to a concentration of 5.0 × 105 cells/mL, and DC to 1.0 × 105 cells/mL. Next, 600 μL of Treg and Tconv and 300 μL of DC were seeded in a 24-well plate with 1 μg/mL anti-CD3e per well. The culture conditions were either Tconv or Treg, Tconv + DC, and Treg + DC. Culture supernatants were harvested after 36 h of incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cytokine concentrations (IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), TGF-β, and interferon-γ) in the culture supernatants were measured using ELISA. ELISA was performed using commercially available ELISA kits, such as the Quantikine ELISA kits (R&D Systems) and the Simple Plex Ella platform (ProteinSimple, San Jose, CA, USA; distributed by Filgen Inc., Nagoya, Japan), following the manufacturer's instructions.

In vitro suppression assay

Tconv was labeled with Carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Treg and Tconv were adjusted to a concentration of 5.0 × 105 cells/mL and DC to 1.0 × 105 cells/mL. The cells were seeded in flat-bottom 24-well plates, each containing 1200 μL with 1 μg/mL anti-CD3e for T cell activation. Then, 300 μL of CFSE-labeled Tconv and 300 μL of DCs were added to each well. Tregs or unlabeled Tconv were added to the wells at Tconv:Treg or Tconv:Tconv ratios of 1:0, 1:1, 2:1, 4:1, 8:1, and 16:1. The co-cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 72 h [17]. Following incubation, the cells were harvested, and CFSE fluorescence intensity was measured using a flow cytometer to assess the proliferation of CFSE-labeled Tconv cells.

Administration of Treg and Tconv

S/F-iTregs and Tconv were suspended in 0.2 mL of HBSS(−) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 2.0 × 105 cells each and administered intravenously via the internal jugular vein. Cell infusion was performed 1 week after TAC surgery. The mice were divided into three groups: Control, Tconv, or Treg (Fig. 2a). The control group received only 0.2 mL of cell-free HBSS(−) intravenously.

Cell tracking analysis of Treg after systemic administration

Tregs were labeled with the cell membrane-labeling dye PKH26 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The mice were sacrificed 2 days after systemic administration of Tregs, and the hearts, lungs, livers, and spleens were harvested. The presence and distribution of Treg were assessed with histological evaluation. Organ samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Nacalai Tesque), frozen in liquid nitrogen, and cryosectioned. Actin fibers were stained with Alexa Fluor 488 phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). The sections were imaged using a confocal microscope (Olympus, FV10i, Tokyo, Japan) to evaluate cell distribution.

Transthoracic echocardiography

Cardiac function was assessed preoperatively and weekly for 4 weeks using transthoracic echocardiography (Vivid-I, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The left ventricular diameter in diastole and the left ventricular diameter in systole were measured. The left ventricular ejection fraction was calculated using the Teichholz method.

Histopathological analysis

Heart sections were stained with picrosirius red to quantify fibrosis. The sections were imaged using a light microscope (KEYENCE, BZ810, Osaka, Japan) to assess the fibrotic area. The fibrosis rate in each section was quantified by dividing the total fibrotic area by the total ventricular area using MetaMorph software (version 7.10.3; Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Flow cytometric analysis

The cells were analyzed using a FACS Canto II system (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA). Data were processed using the FlowJo software (Tree Star, OR, USA). The cells were characterized using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting lineage-specific markers. The antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Western blotting

Left ventricular samples were collected at the designated time points, snap-frozen, and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. After homogenization, lysates were centrifuged to remove debris, and total protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. For immunoblotting, equal amounts of protein (50 μg per lane) were separated on 4–20% gradient SDS–PAGE gels (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Novex, 0.2 μm; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were blocked with Blocking One (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in Can Get Signal Solution 1 (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in Can Get Signal Solution 2 (TOYOBO) for 60 min at room temperature. Signals were developed using Amersham ECL Prime (Cytiva, Tokyo, Japan) and captured with a ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and normalized to GAPDH. Primary antibodies were anti-lysyl oxidase (LOX) (F2C8F, #39774; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) and anti-GAPDH (14C10, #2118S; Cell Signaling Technology). HRP-linked anti-rabbit IgG (NA9340V; Cytiva) was used as the secondary antibody.

Bulk RNA sequencing data analysis

RNA extraction, followed by library preparation and sequencing, was outsourced to the Bioinformatics Center, NGS core facility, the University of Osaka. Normalized read counts were generated following the standard procedures according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, total RNA was extracted using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), and RNA quality was assessed using a Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA sequencing libraries were constructed using TruSeq Stranded mRNA (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and subjected to a paired-end 101bp sequencing run on a NovaSeq X plus (Illumina). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 package in R with default parameters [18]. A fold-change and false discovery rate q-value cutoff (|fold-change| > 1.5, false discovery rate q-value <0.05) were applied to the results table, and DEGs were visualized as MA plots in R. The consistency of expression patterns among biological replicates was evaluated using a heatmap generated using the pheatmap package in R.

Functional analysis using the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis software

DEGs were imported into Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (QIAGEN). Core Analysis was performed to explore knowledge-based functional annotations. Results from the Canonical Pathway module were visualized as volcano plots to assess the directional enrichment of biological functions and gene ontologies following Treg treatment based on statistical significance (p-value <0.05) and activation z-score (|z| > 2.0).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was performed using the GSEA Java application in pre-ranked mode with default parameters [19]. The ranking list of genes was generated from the DESeq2 result table using the “stat” column for ranking. Gene set files (.gmx and.grp) were obtained from the molecular signature database of the Broad Institute. The enrichment analysis results were visualized as enrichment plots. Leading-edge genes—representative DEGs contributing to the enrichment signal—were visualized using the igraph package in R.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. A one-way analysis of variance with Tukey's multiple comparisons was used for group comparisons. Changes in left ventricular diameter in diastole, left ventricular diameter in systole, and ejection fraction following TAC were analyzed using a mixed-effects model for repeated measures, which included explanatory variables for time, group, and the interaction between group and time. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Generation of S/F-iTreg and DC

To generate induced S/F-iTreg, Tconv were isolated from the spleen and lymph nodes of BALB/c mice and cultured in the presence of CD3e, IL-2, and TGF-β. The starting Tconv population lacked Foxp3 and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) expressions, confirming the conventional phenotype before induction (Fig. S1a). After stimulation under CD28-signal-deprived conditions, flow cytometric analysis revealed high expression of CD4+Foxp3+ and CTLA-4+ populations, indicating successful generation of S/F-iTreg (Fig. 1b). DCs were generated from bone marrow cells cultured in GM-CSF. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that the differentiated cells expressed high levels of CD11c, validating the successful generation of DC with a typical immunophenotype (Fig. S1b).

In vitro suppressive function of S/F-iTreg

To assess cytokine secretion, the Treg- and Tconv-only groups and co-cultures with DC were established, and the supernatants were collected after 36 h. When cultured alone, Tregs and Tconv cells secreted low levels of cytokines. However, co-culture them with DCs substantially enhanced cytokine release, demonstrating the role of antigen presentation in T cell activation and regulation. ELISA revealed distinct cytokine profiles depending on the T cell subtype. Inflammatory cytokines, including IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α, and interferon-γ, were predominantly elevated in the Tconv + DC group, whereas the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β were significantly increased in the Treg + DC group (Fig. 1c).

CFSE-labeled Tconv cells were co-cultured with DCs and Tregs at varying ratios to evaluate their suppressive function. Flow cytometric analysis showed the progressive inhibition of CFSE-labeled Tconv proliferation as the Treg:Tconv ratio increased, confirming the dose-dependent suppressive capacity of Tregs (Fig. 1d). Control cultures containing only Tconv with increasing numbers of non-labeled Tconv cells were also established to exclude the possibility that the proliferation inhibition was due to increased cell density. The Tconv:Tconv control cultures showed no significant changes in CFSE dilution across different ratios (Fig. S1c).

S/F-iTreg administration suppresses cardiac dysfunction

The mice underwent TAC surgery and were divided into three groups: control, Tconv, and Tregs, to investigate the therapeutic effects of Tregs in vivo. At 1 week post-surgery, 2.0 × 105 Tregs or Tconv cells were administered via the jugular vein. Serial echocardiography was performed weekly to monitor cardiac function (Fig. 2a and b). At 4 weeks post-surgery, the ejection fraction was significantly higher in the Treg group than in the Tconv and control groups (Fig. 2c). The left ventricular diameter in diastole remained comparable across all three groups, with no significant differences. The left ventricular diameter in systole tended to be smaller in the Treg group than in the control and Tconv groups, although the differences were not significant.

S/F-iTreg administration reduces myocardial fibrosis

To evaluate fibrosis, cardiac tissues were stained with Picrosirius Red 4 weeks post-TAC surgery (Fig. 3a). Histological examination revealed pronounced interstitial fibrosis in the control and Tconv groups, whereas the Treg group showed visibly reduced collagen deposition. Quantitative image analysis revealed that the fibrotic area was significantly decreased in the Treg group compared to that in the control and Tconv groups (Fig. 3b).

To further support the anti-fibrotic effect of S/F-iTreg administration, early fibrosis-related signals were evaluated in left ventricular tissue at day 9 after TAC. Western blot analysis demonstrated that LOX, an extracellular matrix–modifying enzyme involved in collagen cross-linking, was reduced in the Treg group compared with the control and Tconv groups (Fig. 3c).

Migration into organs after systemic S/F-iTreg administration

Tregs were labeled with the fluorescent dye PKH26 and injected into TAC mice to confirm their arrival in cardiac tissue after systemic administration. At 2 days after injection, histological analysis revealed the presence of PKH26-labeled Tregs in the heart, lung, liver, and spleen (Fig. 3d). In the heart, Tregs were detected predominantly in regions exhibiting structural changes associated with pressure overload, particularly areas consistent with early interstitial fibrotic remodeling. Tregs were relatively more abundant in the lung than in the spleen and liver, and they were primarily located in the pulmonary vasculature.

S/F-iTreg administration modulates fibrotic and inflammatory pathways

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the cardioprotective effects of S/F-iTreg therapy, RNA sequencing was performed using left ventricular myocardium harvested at day 9 after TAC (Fig. 4a). The MA plot and heatmap showed 587 upregulated and 491 downregulated DEGs in the Treg group compared to the control group (Fig. 4b). The volcano plot revealed the enrichment of multiple canonical pathways consistent with known biological responses, with significant downregulation of several fibrosis- and inflammation-related signaling pathways in the Treg group (Fig. 4c). These included elastic fiber formation, assembly of collagen fibrils and other multimeric structures, signaling by platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), collagen degradation, actin cytoskeleton signaling, regulation of actin-based motility by Rho, and the programmed death-1 and programmed death-ligand 1 cancer immunotherapy pathways. GSEA showed that inflammation response, TNF-α/nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway, macrophage-related transcriptional programs (classical M1 vs. alternative M2), TGF-β signaling, and epithelial mesenchymal transition were downregulated in the Treg group compared to the control group (Fig. 5a), with visualization of individual leading-edge genes (Fig. 5b). In addition, regarding cardiac hypertrophy, GSEA of left ventricular transcriptomes at 2 weeks after TAC showed positive enrichment of the Gene Ontology term “negative regulation of muscle hypertrophy” in the Treg group, with representative leading-edge genes including Pparg, Foxp1, Rgs4, and Gsk3a exhibiting higher expression compared with the time-matched control (Fig. S2).

Discussion

Myocardial fibrosis, mediated by collagen production of fibroblasts, plays a central role in cardiac remodeling associated with HF. Recent evidence indicates that such fibrosis is triggered by mechanical stress and promoted by inflammatory cytokines produced by T cells and macrophages [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. The TAC model, a representative experimental model reproducing fibrosis due to pressure overload, involves immune responses closely related to disease progression [5]. In this study, we found that intravenous administration of S/F-iTregs, induced under CD28 signal-blocking conditions, significantly suppressed cardiac dysfunction and fibrosis progression in a TAC mouse model. Comprehensive analyses, including the in vitro evaluation of immunosuppressive function, in vivo assessment of cardiac function and histological improvement, and RNA sequencing-based molecular mechanism analysis, supported the therapeutic effects from multiple perspectives.

S/F-iTreg exhibited high Foxp3 and CTLA-4 expression, confirming its phenotype as Treg. In a co-culture with antigen-presenting cells, the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β increased, demonstrating a typical anti-inflammatory cytokine profile [10,11]. Furthermore, the suppression assay using CFSE demonstrated that the proliferation of Tconv was suppressed in a Treg dose-dependent manner, indicating that S/F-iTregs exhibited an immunoregulatory ability similar to that of conventional Tregs [17].

Intravenous administration of S/F-iTregs to mice 1 week after TAC surgery preserved cardiac function and inhibited myocardial fibrosis. This is consistent with a previous report showing anti-fibrotic effects achieved by increasing endogenous Tregs using IL-2/anti-IL-2 antibody complexes [12]. However, a significant difference in this study was the use of epigenetically stabilized Tregs [14,15]. In addition, a recent study supports the concept that Treg-based immunomodulation can attenuate cardiac fibrosis and improve cardiac function by regulating immune responses [20]. While direct comparisons are limited due to differences in experimental models and Treg approaches, both studies converge on the notion that controlling inflammation-associated pathways is a key mechanism linking Tregs to reduced fibrotic remodeling. A notable finding was the detection of PKH26-labeled S/F-iTregs in the myocardial tissue 2 days after administration. S/F-iTregs are mainly distributed in areas experiencing structural changes caused by pressure overload, particularly in regions showing initial signs of myocardial fibrosis. Tregs reportedly tend to migrate to sites of inflammation and tissue damage [21,22], which may have facilitated their accumulation in the local inflammatory environment following mechanical damage due to pressure overload.

In this study, RNA sequencing analysis revealed that myocardial tissue following S/F-iTreg administration exhibited transcriptomic changes consistent with modulation of inflammatory and fibrotic signaling pathways. Cardiac fibrosis in the TAC model was initially triggered by mechanical stress from pressure overload, causing mechanical injury of cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells. This damage induced the production of reactive oxygen species [23], promoting the secretion of inflammatory cytokines. These events have been reported to recruit predominantly M1 macrophages [[3], [4], [5], [6],24], activating NF-κB signaling and sustaining inflammation alongside effector T cells [4,[24], [25], [26]]. In this inflammatory environment, PDGF and TGF-β are reported to activate fibroblasts, inducing their differentiation into myofibroblasts [3,7,[27], [28], [29]]. Myofibroblasts, activated via the Rho/actin cytoskeletal pathway, enhance contractility and mobility, and thereby lead to excessive extracellular matrix production, particularly collagen [[30], [31], [32]]. Additionally, epithelial mesenchymal transition is induced, increasing the supply of new fibroblasts and further promoting fibrosis [29].

S/F-iTreg administration significantly attenuated this process and was associated with modulation of macrophage-related transcriptional programs (M1 vs. M2 gene set signatures), patterns reported in other cardiac disease models [33,34]. This was accompanied by attenuation of TNF-α/NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling and PDGF-related signaling signatures, pathways implicated in fibroblast proliferation and migration [7,25]. Bulk transcriptomic analyses also suggested attenuation of TGF-β signaling–related programs following S/F-iTreg administration. While TGF-β has immunosuppressive properties, its chronic and excessive activation is known to promote fibroblast activation and fibrosis progression [35]. In this study, robust immunosuppression by S/F-iTregs likely occurred through their secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, together with suppression of IL-2–dependent effector T-cell proliferation [10,33]. Such an immunological milieu may help limit sustained profibrotic signaling. These coordinated transcriptomic changes—including attenuation of PDGF- and TGF-β–related programs—may converge to reduce downstream fibrotic pathways. Consistently, signatures related to Rho/actin signaling and epithelial mesenchymal transition were also downregulated, compatible with reduced fibroblast activation [29]. In parallel, LOX protein levels were decreased in the S/F-iTreg group, providing supportive evidence for reduced extracellular matrix remodeling, given that LOX contributes to collagen cross-linking and matrix maturation during fibrotic remodeling [8]. Importantly, these transcriptomic changes were not reproduced by Tconv transfer. The Treg vs Tconv RNA-seq comparison revealed similar suppression of inflammation- and fibrosis-related gene programs, suggesting that the aforementioned molecular signatures reflect S/F-iTreg–associated immunomodulation rather than nonspecific effects of cell administration. Thus, these findings support the potential of S/F-iTregs as an immunomodulatory approach for HF-associated fibrosis. Beyond antifibrotic remodeling, the improved inflammatory milieu may also contribute to cardiomyocyte protection. Given that TNF-α/NF-κB–driven inflammation is implicated in cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, cell death, and contractile dysfunction, the observed suppression of these pathways could plausibly help preserve cardiomyocyte viability and systolic performance [25]. Consistent with this possibility, hypertrophy-suppressive transcriptional programs were enriched, although direct cardiomyocyte-level validation remains warranted.

While IL-2 and IL-2 antibody complexes have shown efficacy in endogenous Treg expansion–induced HF [12], our findings provide evidence that adoptive transfer of epigenetically stabilized S/F-iTregs is associated with therapeutic benefit in the TAC model. Unlike endogenous expansion strategies, cell-based administration allows for controlled dosing, standardized quality, and selective enrichment of stable Treg populations, making it more amenable to clinical translation.

A notable advantage of S/F-iTregs is their epigenetic stability, achieved through CD28 signal deprivation during induction. Typically, CD28 costimulation is essential for full activation and differentiation of conventional T cells. However, removing CD28 signaling during the induction of Tregs leads to DNA hypomethylation at the Foxp3 locus, stabilizing Foxp3 expression and preserving their suppressive function even in inflammatory environments encountered in vivo. This epigenetic stability may contribute to their sustained therapeutic efficacy under pathological conditions, such as those observed in pressure overload-induced HF [14,15]. Accordingly, a direct comparison with conventionally induced iTregs in the TAC model remains an important future direction to further clarify whether this epigenetic stability translates into superior persistence, functional durability, and therapeutic efficacy under pressure-overload conditions.

This study has some limitations. First, the observation period was limited to 4 weeks, which may not be sufficient to assess the long-term persistence of therapeutic effects. Although short-term myocardial accumulation of S/F-iTregs was observed, their long-term engraftment and sustained function remain to be elucidated. Second, the systemic administration of S/F-iTregs may affect host immune surveillance, including tumor immunity and infection defense, which warrants a comprehensive safety assessment. Third, although our findings support immune modulation, cellular-level evidence for immune cell changes in the myocardium was not directly established in this study. Future studies using optimized immunophenotyping and single-cell approaches will be required to determine how S/F-iTregs modulate cardiac immune responses in the TAC model at cellular resolution. Moreover, we did not directly compare S/F-iTregs to conventionally induced Tregs. While the in vivo stability of S/F-iTregs has been reported previously, their phenotypic stability and functional persistence under pathological conditions such as pressure overload–induced HF remain to be fully elucidated, warranting further investigation. Finally, as this study was conducted in a murine model, it remains unclear whether similar therapeutic effects and stability can be achieved in humans. Further investigations are needed to determine the optimal administration route, dosage, and safety for clinical translation.

In conclusion, our results showed that systemic administration of S/F-iTregs was associated with improved cardiac function and reduced myocardial fibrosis in a TAC model. The therapeutic effects are supported by complementary evidence across functional, histological, and transcriptomic domains, consistent with attenuation of inflammation- and fibrosis-related programs. Given their multifaceted effects and in vivo stability, S/F-iTregs may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for immune modulation and fibrosis control in HF.
