Cervical Spinal Cord Injury Due to Direct Needle Damage During Acupuncture
Authors: Myoung Soo Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Causes of cervical spinal cord injury (c-SCI) as a result of direct needle penetration in acupuncture include migration of broken needles or needles, and deep needle insertion. Two cases of c-SCI due to insertion of acupuncture needles were encountered and managed by author. Here, I report the clinical course of these patients. A 35-year-old woman presented at our hospital with paresthesia and weakness of her left upper extremity. Two hours prior to this visit, she received acupuncture treatment around the neck as treatment for neck pain. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a high intensity lesion in cervical spinal cord. She managed pain with medication and physical treatment. Two years later, she showed significant improvement in her left-hand weakness and persistent paresthesia. A 48-year-old woman visited for left arm pain and weakness. She underwent acupuncture treatment two hours before visiting our hospital. MRI demonstrated a mixed intensity lesion in cervical spinal cord. Two months later, she showed marked improvement in left arm weakness. However, she demonstrated no improvement of her painful paresthesia. Physicians should always consider c-SCI as differential diagnosis of upper extremity weakness and paresthesia, especially if the patient has a history of acupuncture around the neck or back.

INTRODUCTION

Acupuncture has been widely used for treating various pain syndromes. Acupuncture has demonstrated efficacy in reducing pain, as well as exhibiting a significant impact on the management of radicular pain, characterized by a low incidence of adverse effects. However, several injuries have been reported as complications of acupuncture, especially in the cervical region, epidural hematoma, subdural hematoma, spinal cord damage, and infection.124568910) Traumatic myelopathy caused by direct injury of an acupuncture needle is another risk of acupuncture.581011) Most cases of cervical spinal cord injury (c-SCI) due to acupuncture needles are developed by migration of embedded needles or accidentally broken needles.8) Miyamoto et al.8) reported that 16 of 25 patients with c-SCI or brain injury due to direct injury by acupuncture needles developed symptoms more than 30 days after the accident.

Causes of c-SCI in acupuncture include direct penetration into the spinal cord by migrating broken needles, migration of long-term or permanently embedded needles, and deep needle insertion.9) Kang et al.5) and Woo et al.11) reported two cases of iatrogenic c-SCI resulting from deep acupuncture needle insertion. In the two cases reported here, c-SCIs developed following direct cord injury following deep needle insertion during acupuncture treatment.

Two cases of c-SCI due to direct penetration into the cervical cord following deep insertion of an acupuncture needle were encountered and managed by the author. The c-SCIs in these two cases were diagnosed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) within three hours of acupuncture treatment. Here, I report the clinical course and treatment of these patients, especially focusing on neurological deficit.

CASE REPORT

Case 1

A 35-year-old woman presented to the outpatient clinic of our hospital with paresthesia and weakness of the left upper extremity. Two hours prior to this visit, she had received acupuncture treatment for neck pain around the neck at a local oriental clinic. According to her statement, during the procedure, she assumed a prone position with a pillow supporting the chest, although the depth of needle insertion was unclear. As the needle entered her neck, she experienced an abrupt and intense pain that persisted for several minutes. Subsequently, she reported worsening pain accompanied by tingling paresthesia and weakness in her left upper extremity. She reported no headache.

Her medical history indicated intermittent neck pain due to mild herniation of the cervical intervertebral nucleus. However, she demonstrated neither paresthesia nor weakness in four extremities before acupuncture. She underwent no cervical spine operation. Neurological examination revealed a weakness in grasping of her left hand (muscle strength grade [MSG] 4− according to modified Medical Research Council System [mMRCS]). She complained of persistent paresthesia with an intensity of 8 on the numeric rating scale (NRS) over the entire left arm. MRI two hours after acupuncture revealed a high intensity lesion in cervical 4–5 level spinal cord on a T2-weighted sagittal image. An axial T2-weighted image demonstrated high density in the cervical cord (FIGURE 1).

Cervical magnetic resonance image on two hours after acupuncture. (A) Sagittal T2-weighted image demonstrates high signal intensity in cervical cord of 4–5 level. (B) Axial T2-weighted image shows high signal intensity in cervical cord (arrows: signal change of spinal cord).

Conservative treatment was chosen. Dexamethasone (Yuhan dexamethasone disodium phosphate; Yuhan Corporation, Seoul, Korea), amitriptyline (Myungin Amitriptyline HCl Tablet; Myung In Pharm Co, Seoul, Korea), gabapentin (Gabapentin Dong-A Cap; Dong-A ST, Seoul, Korea), and paramacet (Paramacet ET tab; Dong-A ST) were prescribed. Several days later, she showed significant improvement in her left-hand weakness. She also showed improvement of painful paresthesia after medication. However, she experienced the same NRS scale painful paresthesia without medication. She then undertook pain medication and physical treatment at another hospital. On two years after acupuncture treatment, she demonstrated mild grasping weakness (MSG 4+ according to mMRCS) in her left hand but experienced little disruption to her daily life during usage of her left hand. However, she complained of persistent paresthesia with an intensity of 4 on the NRS over the left hand with medication and no improvement of paresthesia without medication.

Case 2

A 48-year-old woman visited the Emergency Department in our hospital complaining of left arm pain and weakness. She had suffered a traffic accident one month before and treated herself conservatively in an oriental hospital. She underwent acupuncture treatment two hours before visiting our Emergency Department. The needling treatment was performed as an outpatient procedure by an oriental medical doctor. Dry needling was performed on the patient’s neck. However, the depth of needle insertion was uncertain. As the needle entered her neck, she experienced an abrupt and intense pain that persisted for several minutes. Subsequently, she reported worsening pain accompanied by tingling paresthesia and weakness in her left upper extremity. She reported no headache.

On neurological examination, she showed alert mentality and normal orientation. However, she demonstrated intense paresthesia with an intensity of 8 on the NRS over the entire left arm and weakness of her left upper extremity (MSG 3 according to mMRCS). She demonstrated no gait disturbance. MRI three hours after acupuncture demonstrated a mixed intensity lesion in cervical 5–6 level spinal cord on T2-weighted sagittal image. Axial T2-weighted image demonstrated high signal intensity in the cervical cord (FIGURE 2).

Cervical magnetic resonance image on three hours after acupuncture. (A) Sagittal T2-weighted image demonstrates mixed signal intensity in cervical cord of 5–6 level. (B) Axial T2-weighted image shows high signal intensity in cervical cord (arrows: signal change of spinal cord).

Conservative treatment was performed. Dexamethasone (Yuhan dexamethasone disodium phosphate; Yuhan Corporation), amitriptyline (Myungin Amitriptyline HCl Tablet), gabapentin (Gabapentin Dong-A Cap; Dong-A ST), and paramacet (Paramacet ET tab; Dong-A ST) were prescribed. Two months later, she showed marked improvement in her left arm weakness (MSG 4+ according to mMRCS). However, she demonstrated no improvement of painful paresthesia of her left upper extremity without medication. With medication, she experienced improvement of painful paresthesia.

DISCUSSION

Mechanism of c-SCI by acupuncture needle penetration into the spinal cord during acupuncture

In four cases,511) including my two patients, a needle was inserted perpendicular to the skin from the neck, possibly targeting the EX-B2 acupoint.7) After penetration of the dura mater, the needle damaged the cervical spinal cord. Gi et al.3) divided spinal cord injuries due to acupuncture penetration of the spinal cord into two categories: either posterior puncture (longitudinal injuries) or lateral puncture (transverse injures). In the posterior puncture group, sensorimotor disturbances were present. However, in the lateral puncture group, sensory disturbance was the only neurological deficit.

All four cases, including my two patients, displayed cervical cord damage via posterior puncture (TABLE 1). All four patients demonstrated sensorimotor disturbances. In all cases, motor weakness of the upper extremity was much improved on short-term follow-up. Three patients reported persistent painful paresthesia. One patient complained only of weakness on the first visit.11) Nevertheless, the clinical course of each patient was primarily explained by damage to the sensory system of the spinal cord, e.g., posterior column. An aspect of the motor system in the cervical cord, such as the lateral corticospinal tract and rubrospinal tract, was partially damaged by the acupuncture needle because of its location in the spinal cord.

Miyamoto et al.8) reported 26 cases of injury by an embedded or broken acupuncture needle. In all 26 cases, symptoms developed at some time from the day of the needle insertion up to more than 20 years after the insertion of the needles. Embedded or broken needles do not usually cause immediate neurological deficits by themselves.8) However, needles easily migrate deeper into the neck with day-to-day neck movements and in a few days may develop progressive neurological deficits. In four cases,511) including my two cases, the patients experienced no progression of neurological deficits during follow up.

Treatment of c-SCI by acupuncture needle penetration into the spinal cord

Miyamoto et al.8) presented cases of cervical cord or medulla oblongata damage due to embedded acupuncture needles or broken acupuncture needles. These needles should be removed urgently if symptomatic. In these four cases,511) including my two cases, the acupuncture needles did not remain in the patient’s body. Furthermore, these four cases showed neither infection nor hematoma in the cervical canal or cervical cord.

Motor deficits after acupuncture needle penetration into the cervical cord developed because of damage to the lateral corticospinal tract. These motor deficits easily recovered because of the limited extent of damage to the lateral corticospinal tract in posterior puncture.

Paresthesia after acupuncture needle penetration into the cervical cord showed a different clinical course and needed further treatment.5) In the patient with painful paresthesia, this symptom developed following acupuncture needle penetration into the posterior column. This painful paresthesia improved after treatment with dexamethasone, gabapentin, and amitriptyline, but remained on follow up. Where there is a contraindication of medical treatment, selective nerve root block of the cervical root might improve the paresthesia.5)

CONCLUSION

Physicians should always consider c-SCI as a differential diagnosis of upper extremity weakness and paresthesia, especially if the patient has a history of acupuncture to the neck. Medical treatment or nerve root block demonstrates good results.
