Student Perceptions of Cognitive, Psychomotor and Affective Learning in the Undergraduate Laboratory
Authors: Alexandra Cleaver, Lauren Crean, Susan Howitt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBiochemistry and molecular biology is an experimental discipline and therefore training students in experimental techniques and data analysis is an essential component of undergraduate degrees. However, the amount of practical work may be limited by financial constraints and can vary considerably in the quality of the student experience. We were interested in how students perceived their overall practical experience, in contrast to evaluating a single experiment or project. We surveyed second‐year students on their confidence in practical skills and what they valued and found challenging in laboratory work, followed by a small number of interviews. We found that students generally have a high level of confidence in their abilities and recognize a wide range of learning outcomes, encompassing the cognitive, psychomotor, and affective domains of learning. What students identify as challenging is similarly diverse. Most did not believe that online experiences were a good substitute for in‐person laboratories. The implications of this research are that scaffolding of practical work should accommodate the diversity of student experience and that assessment should take better account of psychomotor and affective learning, rather than relying solely on demonstration of cognitive learning via a laboratory report.Keywords: domains of learning, laboratory work, practical work, student experience

Introduction

Practical work has always been seen as an essential component of a biology degree. Since the Vision and Change Report in 2011 [1], there has been a greater focus on defining and evaluating skills and theoretical knowledge that biology graduates should obtain during their degree. This has been adapted for specific subdisciplines, with workshops leading to concepts and skills necessary for biochemistry and molecular biology [2]. Subsequently, the American Society for Biochemistry and Molecular Biology identified requirements for accrediting biochemistry and molecular biology degrees [3, 4] that include 400 h of practical work [4]. However, the centrality of practical work has been questioned following the COVID pandemic, when many laboratory experiences were replaced with online activities. While students are now back on campus, the financial pressures experienced by many universities in the post‐COVID environment, as well as the greater flexibility of online activities, have resulted in reductions in laboratory work in some institutions. This is generating increasing concern from science academics who perceive the quality of the degree experience is suffering from these changes. Indeed, the Biosciences 2030 Report recently released by the Australian Academy of Science [5] recommends that we should:

Use knowledge and experience gained from the COVID‐19 pandemic to reposition practical and hands‐on learning experiences and student engagement as an essential cornerstone of modern bioscience higher education programs.

Practical work, defined here as laboratory experience plus associated experiences such as data analysis and presentation, can potentially meet a wide range of learning outcomes, although this depends on the learning activity design and the assessment. Academic staff often view laboratory classes as opportunities for students to learn about how science is done and to experience collaborative learning. However, assessment generally takes the form of a lab report meaning that students are assessed mainly on their understanding of the experimental design and results; the cognitive aspects of the work. Cognitive learning is only one of the three domains of learning—cognitive, psychomotor and affective [6]. While most are familiar with Bloom's taxonomy for the cognitive domain [6] the psychomotor and affective domains are often just as important for learning. Novak's theory of constructivism [7] suggests that meaningful learning should involve thinking, feeling and acting, implying that good learning activities integrate the three domains of learning. Greater consideration of the psychomotor and affective domains in parallel to cognitive learning could therefore improve learning experiences, especially in the laboratory where psychomotor and affective learning is implicit in many activities. Similar approaches have proved useful in studying student behaviors and perceptions in undergraduate chemistry laboratory work [8, 9].

Psychomotor learning encompasses hands‐on learning and ranges from reflex movements and physical abilities to the performance of skilled movements and complex protocols [10]. This would include the ability to follow laboratory procedures and manipulate equipment and samples. Psychomotor skills are critical to becoming an effective scientist but are only rarely explicitly recognized and assessed in laboratory programs, for example, through practical exams or competency assessments. Practice and repetition are essential to developing these skills, emphasizing the importance of learning in the laboratory.

Affective learning refers to a learner's emotional state and includes motivational beliefs, values, and responses, as well as interaction with others [11]. It has been recognized for some time that the feelings that arise during learning can powerfully influence the effectiveness of any learning experience [12, 13]. Despite this recognition, there has been little impact on mainstream science teaching. In biology, this has been explored from the perspectives of self‐efficacy, sense of belonging, and science identity [12, 14]. These studies concluded that more could be done to raise awareness and foster positive emotions as part of learning activity design. This is especially true in the current environment, where increasing numbers of students are experiencing stress and mental health issues [15, 16]. Laboratory experiences, where there is potential for interaction and inspiration in a less formal setting, are an ideal setting for affective responses to be considered.

We wanted to investigate how university courses could better prepare students for their chosen scientific careers, particularly in the area of practical laboratory research. Much research has focused on predominantly research‐based experiences [17, 18] or evaluation of specific (and usually well‐designed) laboratory experiments, as will be evident to readers of this journal. There is abundant evidence that well‐designed inquiry learning activities—i.e. those that have clear learning outcomes, are appropriately scaffolded for the cohort, and include opportunities for student input and independence—have significant benefits for students. Research that leads to more widespread implementation of these activities is therefore now of greater importance [19]. Turning this around, there is actually very little evidence for what students gain from their overall practical experience where they may experience practical work of varying quality in different subjects and with different instructors. In some environments, students may experience quite poorly designed practical work for the majority of their degree. Consequently, evaluations of single experiments in the literature may give a misleading view of the total student experience. Our study addresses this gap by surveying students about the full range of practical experience across their degree.

Aim: To understand how students perceive the totality of their practical experience across the degree and their confidence in achieving practical learning outcomes.

Methods

Participants in this study were enrolled at a research‐intensive university with high entry standards that offers a three‐year undergraduate degree. We sent the survey to all students enrolled in a second‐year molecular genetics course (n = 250), which places them mid‐way through their degree. This course is a gateway to further study in genetics, immunology, microbiology, biomedical science, and genomics and is taken by most students with an interest in molecular biology and biochemistry. Most participants were also enrolled in other biology and/or chemistry courses and would therefore have had considerable exposure to laboratory classes offered by at least two departments.

We conducted a voluntary online survey consisting of Likert scale and short answer questions. The survey was based on one used previously to assess student confidence in a more research‐based course [20]. We made some modifications to adapt it to our context and added some open‐ended questions (Appendix S1). The survey was sent to the entire class, and we received a total of 102 responses (40% of the class enrolment) with the majority providing a response to all questions.

Results from the Likert scale questions were graphed and the short answer responses analyzed thematically. After initially categorizing these inductively, we grouped categories based on our theoretical approach, the three domains of learning (cognitive, psychomotor and affective). This resulted in some re‐alignment and condensing of primary categories.

Following the survey, we conducted short one‐on‐one interviews with individual students to gain deeper insight into their responses. Interview questions were designed to allow students to elaborate on the survey findings (Appendix S2). Students were selected for the interview based on demographic and enrollment characteristics to ensure that we obtained the diversity represented within the class. Six interviews were conducted. Interviews were transcribed and then analyzed deductively using the three domains of learning to identify responses that illuminated aspects of practical learning identified in the survey.

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of our institution (Protocol 2024/0527).

Results

Students' Confidence in Their Practical Skills

Students were asked to rate their confidence in their ability to do a range of skills, covering both in‐lab activities and results presentation and analysis. The results in Figure 1 demonstrate that students had a high degree of confidence, with at least 75% of the students feeling moderately or very confident in most skills. Where they were least confident was in those skills requiring greatest independence, such as designing an experiment, critically analyzing literature, and developing a research question.

Students' confidence in practical skills. Students were asked to rate their confidence in each of the listed skills on a four point Likert scale, ranging; very confidence, moderately confident, somewhat confident, not very confident. There were 102 responses to this question (40% of the cohort).

Interviewees generally felt that the most important factor in developing confidence was the opportunity for repeated practice of skills, although some noted that watching videos of lab skills could also be useful as well. For example, these responses were in answer to a question about whether the student felt confident to perform techniques covered in the lab on their own.

P3: I feel maybe 75% confident… The amount of practice that you'd have to put in to get to the point where you felt like 100% comfortable… it's not realistic that's going to happen in a Uni course.

P1 [on using lab equipment]: You'll keep doing that over and over again so you'll slowly, build up on confidence there.

We also asked students about the value of online laboratories. Almost 80% of survey respondents indicated that they felt they would not be as well prepared for the future if they had experienced only online labs. The main reasons given related to the psychomotor and affective categories. Unsurprisingly, students felt that online experiences could not provide the necessary practice in technical skills but they also reported that the social interaction, engagement and peer learning they experienced in the lab would be either absent or less effective online.

P2: online‐based labs are very different to in‐person based labs because it's not actually in there practising the techniques and talking to people… I would definitely choose in person because that also means I can talk to other people and to find out where I am wrong, and so I can also practise for techniques.

P4: I think you're missing out on key things, like the social interactions, you're having in the lab and also, just the actual skills that you need to learn in a lab.

Students generally recognized that most cognitive goals could be met by online activities and some valued the flexibility of online simulations.

Perceived Valuable and Difficult Aspects of Practical Classes

In the survey, students were asked open‐ended questions about what aspects of practical work they felt were most valuable and what they found most difficult. Responses were analyzed thematically and the results were shown graphically in Figures 2 and 3, with exemplar quotes illustrating the categories in Table 1. Interestingly, the responses to both questions reflected a wide range of potential learning outcomes from practical work that covered cognitive, psychomotor, and affective learning. Many of the same qualities of practical work were cited as both difficult and valuable, although often by different students. Responses that covered more than one category were common. In the interviews, when students were asked explicitly about these three different aspects of practical work, most felt that all were important. Most valuable outcomes were dominated by understanding theory and linking this to applications and the opportunity to practice and develop hands‐on skills (Figure 2), i.e., cognitive and psychomotor skills. However, there were more categories of what students found difficult, and a more even spread, with no single difficulty accounting for more than 16% of responses (Figure 3). This suggests that learning in the practical laboratory is complex and that multiple learning outcomes are possible but may be recognized and achieved to different degrees by different students.

Survey responses in the cognitive category accounted for about 50% of the mentions in both value and difficulty. Cognitive benefits were split into three categories: understanding theory and application, results analysis and interpretation, and report writing. All were mentioned as valuable, but understanding theory and application was the dominant aspect; students clearly valued practical work to enhance and clarify their learning from lectures, as this interview quote shows:

P6 [value of lab reports]: If it was just a lab with no homework or no report, then I don't think it would have stuck in my head.

The same three cognitive outcomes were evident in the difficulty responses (Figure 3) but they were close to equal in frequency.

The survey results demonstrated that the valuable aspect of labs pertaining to psychomotor skills was mainly about the ability to visualize and practice techniques. Students also recognized that becoming skilled at experimental work resulted in better time management and greater autonomy (Figure 2), as illustrated by these interview comments.

P2: Just like the technical skills, you know, you spend a good chunk of the lab just making sure, like the correct pipetting technique, that was a learning curve for me.

P6 [on lab skills]: I think that sort of stuff is probably helpful because it just means that when it's time to do something more independently, you can have some autonomy there and you don't have to be really annoying to some head of a lab who you don't want them to think you're an idiot.

The same points were raised under difficulties but with a greater emphasis on time management and we included a new category on ability to follow a protocol (Figure 3 and Table 1). Comments in this category expressed some uncertainty about the correct interpretation of instructions in lab manuals, from very basic aspects such as identifying which tube to use to mastering more complex procedures. This was reinforced in the interviews, as these comments demonstrate:

P3: It's these little things that are like unwritten instructions, that you only learn through doing it.

P1: when I first was reading [the lab manual] I was like, it doesn't make any sense. But once we sort of start explaining it within, like a lab context like what we'd actually be doing. It's like, Oh, well that makes sense now, it's not that complicated actually.

Especially important was the opportunity to link cognitive and psychomotor skills, with many students finding that doing experiments helped them understand the theory. Doing experiments made ideas and techniques concrete for many students and this was often linked to real world applications. This was also evident in the interview responses:

P5: You can see in your degree, how the theory and practice would actually work as like a job in the future.

P2: It helps me actually see how the lecture material is used, instead of it being just theoretical. So it's like, yes people do use this. This is how you use it.

In the survey questions about what made practical work valuable, the affective category generally related to the interaction with peers and teaching staff that led to collaborative learning, which students found helpful and enjoyable (Figure 2). However, difficulties associated with affective responses covered a much greater range (Figure 3 and Table 1). While interaction with both peers and teaching staff was difficult for a couple of students, more found labs difficult because of their emotional reaction. This was mostly because labs were perceived as stressful or overwhelming, while a few students were bored by repetition or the need to wait for reactions. While comparing these two results might suggest that practical work is more likely to be a negative experience, this was not the case; a separate survey question asked students what they found most interesting or fun about practical work. Eighty‐one students responded to this question very positively, with the majority of responses confirming that both cognitive and psychomotor aspects of practical work contributed to the affective domain by inspiring enjoyment and interest (Figure 4). The major categories included hands‐on work and interpretation and application of theory, ie psychomotor and cognitive learning. Observing results and understanding their significance and working with peers also contributed to satisfaction with laboratory work. Enjoyment of practical work was evident in the interviews, and it was clear that this was often linked to learning.

P5: [when putting on a lab coat to enter the lab] you feel very sciency and you feel like you're actually doing a science degree rather than just sitting in a classroom and being told to remember stuff.

P6: I love practical work. I don't think I'd understand any of the theory if I didn't have it applied.

Overall, this suggests that the majority of students find practical work a positive experience and that the affective domain is an important contributor to the value of practical experiences. However, this should not obscure the fact that some students found the laboratory environment stressful.

Discussion

The students in our study recognized a wide range of benefits from practical work, encompassing cognitive, psychomotor, and affective learning. Importantly, practical work had a key role in linking the three domains of learning, which is necessary for meaningful learning [7]. Figure 5 demonstrates these connections, which were evident from both the survey responses and interviews. Cognitive and psychomotor learning were linked as many students reported that seeing experiments in the lab and gaining results made the theory more concrete for them, leading to greater understanding. Similarly, enjoyment of the lab environment and peer interaction contributed to both cognitive and psychomotor learning. Learning from chemistry practical work also involves the integration of the three domains of learning [8, 9]. We have shown that students view practical work as an essential curriculum component, consistent with recommendations from a range of professional bodies [1, 2, 4, 5].

Intersection of different modes of learning in practical experiences.

Students were confident in their ability to perform a range of practical‐related skills, both in the laboratory and in the analysis and communication of results (Figure 1). They also recognized the need to repeat and practice skills to develop confidence. Developing student confidence is important as strong self‐efficacy has been linked to improved academic performance (see [12] for a discussion of self‐efficacy in biology education). Despite their confidence, it was clear from the open‐ended survey questions that some aspects of practical work were quite challenging for some students. These responses may come from those students who were less confident, as a wide range of difficulties was identified and no single difficulty was common to more than 16% of students (Figure 3). Alternatively, students may be over‐confident in their abilities, as has been found for a recent study on employability skills where industry feedback showed that students had lower levels of attainment in some skills than suggested by their confidence levels [21].

Our study has several implications for practical teaching and assessment. Firstly, students recognized and valued a much wider range of learning gains than is usually assessed. Although cognitive skills, which are assessed by a lab report, were most likely to be found valuable by students, they also valued psychomotor and affective aspects of practical work (Figure 2). A misalignment between what academic staff value in undergraduate research experiences and what they actually assess has been identified previously [22]; academics valued a broad set of learning outcomes that included psychomotor, affective, and metacognitive learning in addition to the cognitive learning demonstrated by a lab report. It appears likely that there is a similar mismatch between valued learning outcomes and assessment in much undergraduate practical work.

Not all students recognized the full breadth of learning, suggesting that reviewing practical assessment with a greater focus on constructive alignment to make other learning outcomes explicit and assessed would be beneficial. It was also evident that practical experience highlighted tacit practices that were invisible to students but taken for granted by experienced scientists. This was expressed both negatively, as discomfort in understanding protocols as well as feelings of stress in the laboratory, and positively in that seeing techniques and equipment helped visualize and understand theory. Recognizing this could improve scaffolding of practical experiences and also opens the way for competency assessment in the laboratory, where students are given opportunities to practice skills until they achieve confidence and competency. Making psychomotor skills an explicit learning outcome that is assessed via a practical exam can improve students' awareness of learning and how they value it [23]. Similarly, a competency‐based laboratory course improved students' abilities to perform and retain practical skills [24]. These assessment options could be extended with interactive oral assessments in which students are asked about specific aspects of protocols and the underlying theory [25]. This could include aspects of self‐evaluation and reflection on learning to encourage students to recognize the role of affective learning. Practical exams and competency assessments could also contribute to a learning portfolio as another strategy to incorporate reflective learning. An additional advantage of these strategies is that they could mitigate some of the challenges posed by generative AI by substituting in‐person assessment for some written assignments.

Secondly, emotional responses to practical work are a key factor in promoting learning and self‐efficacy. While most students enjoyed being in the laboratory and working with their peers, some found this quite stressful. Understanding these responses is important as other work has found that affective responses may determine behavior in the laboratory [9], in particular, determining how actively students engage with learning opportunities. Instructors may need to scaffold metacognitive awareness [9] and evaluate self‐efficacy [12, 14] to assist students to make the most of practical learning. A further complexity is that different students respond to the same experience differently, as is evident from the wide variety of responses students gave in the open‐ended survey questions of our study. Thus, the design of practical work would ideally include strategies for students to address challenges across all three domains of learning.

Thirdly, our student sample had quite a negative view of online laboratory replacement exercises. These students experienced the COVID lockdowns during high school and likely had seen only emergency responses, rather than well‐designed online activities. Our students valued psychomotor learning highly, and this cannot be replicated online, particularly the mastering of tacit knowledge highlighted above. They were also concerned about the lack of peer interaction and the fact that physical laboratories are more engaging, in line with other work finding that affective gains were more limited online [8]. Our results reinforce the need for good design of online laboratories and also the value of explaining to students how these activities might meet different learning outcomes. For example, peer interaction can be fostered online, but students may need explicit direction to assist them in identifying the value of such activities.

Conclusions

Our study fills a gap by examining how students perceive their overall practical experience, in contrast to investigations of a single laboratory experiment or project that may be designed with specific learning outcomes in mind. As a group, the students in our study placed a high value on their practical experience and recognized that it contributes to cognitive, psychomotor, and affective learning. However, there was wide variation in what individual students found most valuable or challenging, emphasizing the complexity and diversity of learning in the laboratory. It must be noted that these students were enrolled at an elite, research‐intensive institution; however, they were a self‐selected sample. Time constraints limited the number of interviews we could do to six, but these were used mainly to support and elaborate on the survey findings. Although these limitations restrict the generalizability of our work, our findings are broadly in line with similar work on undergraduate chemistry laboratories [8, 9]. It would be useful to repeat the survey and interviews at other institutions with different characteristics to determine if different student groups found similar value in practical work. More data showing that students, like academic staff, value time in the laboratory may help support arguments to maintain these activities as a key component of the curriculum.
