USG-guided unilateral retrolaminar block decreases pain and enhances patient comfort during extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy: a prospective study
Authors: Serpil Bayindir, Kadir Yildirim, Sait Fatih Oner, Oguz Kagan Bulut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to evaluate the analgesic efficacy, opioid consumption, patient-operator satisfaction, and hemodynamic changes of ultrasound-guided retrolaminar block (RLB) in patients undergoing extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL). A total of 60 patients who underwent ESWL were divided into two groups: the RLB group (patients who received RLB 30 min before the procedure, n = 30) and the control group (patients who did not receive any block, n = 30). The primary outcome measure was the assessment of analgesic efficacy using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Secondary outcome measures were opioid consumption, hemodynamic variables (heart rate and mean arterial pressure), patient-operator satisfaction scores, stone-free rate, and discharge time. VAS scores were significantly lower in the RLB group (p < 0.001). Mean tramadol consumption in the control group was found to be 19.33 ± 16.64 mg, which was statistically significantly higher (p < 0.001). Patient and operator satisfaction was significantly higher in the RLB group (p < 0.001), hemodynamic stability was maintained throughout the procedure, and no block-related complications were observed. RLB provided effective analgesia and significantly reduced opioid requirements in ESWL patients. Balanced hemodynamic stability was achieved throughout the procedure, maximizing patient and operator satisfaction. Therefore, RLB is a fascial plane block that provides effective analgesia that can be applied for outpatient urological procedures such as ESWL.Keywords: Extracorporeal shockwave lithotripsy, ESWL pain, Ultrasound-guided block, Retrolaminar block, AnalgesiaSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Urology

Introduction

Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) is an outpatient procedure used in the treatment of ureteral and kidney stones smaller than 1 cm when appropriate patient selection is made. Many factors, including the nature, size, and location of the stone, can influence the stone-free rate. Its success rate generally ranges from 70 to 90%1. As extracorporeal shock waves pass through the tissues, they can cause tension in the renal capsule or muscle spasm in the ureter. Pain in the patient can lead to dislocation, and anxiety can lead to hyperventilation. Patient movement can disrupt the shock wave’s targeting and, most importantly, negatively impact the success of the procedure2. Sedoanalgesia is generally required for a successful procedure. Furthermore, prolonged procedure time and hemodynamic fluctuations can increase the risk of complications in high-risk patients3.

For outpatient procedures like ESWL, where the discharge time is short, it may be more prudent to choose opioid-free anesthesia methods that allow for rapid recovery. Opioid analgesia can cause side effects such as excessive sedation, hemodynamic instability, respiratory depression, nausea and vomiting, and prolonged recovery time4. Therefore, multimodal methods that allow the procedure to be performed under optimal conditions and produce conscious sedoanalgesia should be preferred5.

Ultrasound-guided retrolaminar block (RLB) is one of the fascial plane blocks that has been described as easy and relatively safe. It provides analgesia by blocking the dorsal branches of the spinal nerves between the vertebral lamina and the paraspinal muscles6. It creates a reservoir of local anesthetic between the laminar floor and the muscle tissue, with its distinctive ultrasonographic landmarks. It has become a preferred choice in urological surgeries because it provides postoperative analgesia, reduces intraoperative opioid requirements, and shortens the time to discharge with early mobilization7.

The primary objective of this study was to prospectively evaluate the analgesic efficacy of USG-guided RLB in ESWL patients. Secondarily, it was aimed to determine the patient comfort and operator satisfaction, stone-free rate, effects on hemodynamic parameters and complications of RLB.

Methodology

Study design and settings

This was a prospective, single-center observational study designed to evaluate the impact of RLB on patient comfort and analgesic effectiveness during ESWL procedures. The study was conducted at Elazığ Fethi Sekin City Hospital, a tertiary care center located in Elazığ, Türkiye, involving adult patients undergoing ESWL between 20 September 2025 and 10 November 2025. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (decision no: E-13281952-514.10-288174239; approval date: 04/09/2025) and the study was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07177625; first registration date: 26/08/2025), before the inclusion of the first participant. The written informed consent forms were signed before enrolling in the study. All study procedures were performed in full compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Protocol

All ESWL procedures were performed using a standard lithoscope (ELMED lithoscope, Ankara, Turkey, Model X123) in accordance with routine clinical practice protocols. Gradual voltage escalation protocols were applied at 12–24 kV. During the procedure, a maximum of 2500 shocks was allowed at low-frequency SW delivery (60–90 shocks/min) in accordance with the kidney injury protection protocol. Mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR), and oxygen saturation (SpO₂) were continuously recorded throughout the procedure. After the procedure, patients were monitored in the observation unit and discharged once they achieved a Post-Anesthetic Discharge Scoring System (PADSS) score of 9 or higher.

In this study, patients were divided into two groups based on the clinical practice:

Grup RLB: Approximately 20 min before ESWL, those who received RLB with 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine from the T9–T10 level to the side where the stone was located, under ultrasound guidance (1–15 Hz convex probe, 8 − 4, Hitachi Europe Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. 2).

Grup RLB: Approximately 20 min before ESWL, those who received RLB with 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine from the T9–T10 level to the side where the stone was located, under ultrasound guidance (1–15 Hz convex probe, 8 − 4, Hitachi Europe Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. 2).

Grup C: Those without any block applied.

Grup C: Those without any block applied.

Ultrasound-guided RLB application at the T9–T10 level to the side of the stone before ESWL.

According to our clinic’s standard protocol, rescue analgesia (tramadol 0.5 mg/kg IV) is administered to patients if their VAS score is > 5 during the procedure. Block application time was defined as the interval from completion of skin preparation to injection of the local anesthetic into the retrolaminar space. The total time, including block application and waiting for the onset of analgesic effect, corresponds to the interval between the onset of anesthesia and the start of the surgical procedure, as recorded on the anesthesia monitoring forms. Bupivacaine was used as the local anesthetic, and the expected onset of analgesic effect was considered to be approximately 15–20 min, in accordance with routine clinical practice.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case. All information that could reveal the patient’s identity has been removed to ensure confidentiality.

Blinding/masking

This study was a prospective, observational, two-arm study (RLB group and control group). The outcome assessor and data analyst were blinded to the study’s results. VAS scores, hemodynamic measurements (HR, MAP), and analgesic consumption were recorded using standardized forms at predefined time points by a clinically independent research assistant blinded to the groups. The assessor was blinded to group information in the electronic records. Data were coded as “Group A/Group B” for analysis; group identities were not disclosed until the completion of statistical analysis. Indications for rescue analgesia, drug type, and doses were predefined by protocol before the study and administered identically in both groups.

Sample size

Based on the results of previous clinical studies, it was hypothesized that RLB would provide adequate analgesic efficacy in ESWL compared to standard practice. It was calculated that a minimum of 25 participants per group was required to achieve a statistically significant 1-point difference in VAS values ​​between groups, with a standard deviation of 1.5, 80% power, and a two-sided significance level of 0.05. To compensate for missing data and protocol deviations, a total sample size of 60 patients (30 in each group) was included to ensure adequate statistical power.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 27.0 (SPSS Inc. Chicago, USA) package program was used for statistical analysis. Normality tests were performed for all measurement data, and the descriptive statistics of the study were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data when appropriate. The means of normally distributed parameters were compared using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and intergroup comparisons of variables with normal distribution were analyzed using the Independent Samples t-test. The Mann-Whitney U test was employed for intergroup comparisons of non-normally distributed data. Inter-group comparisons of qualitative variables were performed using the Pearson Chi-Square test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages). For all analyses, a p-value of < 0.05 was used as the significance threshold.

Discussion

ESWL remains one of the primary treatment options, depending on the stone’s characteristics and patient selection. The European Association of Urology (EAU) guideline emphasizes that pain control during ESWL is critical not only for patient comfort but also for the correct focus of the stone and treatment success8. During ESWL, a sharp, stabbing pain is felt at the point where the shock waves enter the skin. Insufficient analgesia can cause involuntary movements, leading to the shock waves reaching the wrong target. This can result in both tissue damage and reduced treatment effectiveness. Therefore, a successful ESWL procedure can only be achieved if the patient remains immobile and cooperates9. However, due to the multifactorial nature of pain, there is still no consensus on the optimal analgesia method. RLB, one of the newer ultrasound-guided thoracic truncal blocks, demonstrates its analgesic effect by diffusing into the paravertebral area and providing a wide dermatomal distribution. It has been suggested that RLB not only provides incisional somatic analgesia but also induces visceral analgesia and reduces the neuroinflammatory response10. Nobukuni et al. reported that ultrasound-guided RLB provides pain control similar to thoracic epidural analgesia (TEA) and has a low risk of complications11. Another study has shown that RLB reduces opioid use and pain scores compared to ESPB and is similar in terms of analgesic efficacy compared to PVB12. The fact that RLB reduced opioid requirements and significantly lowered VAS scores in our study (Fig. 5; p < 0.001) supports these results reported in the literature at the clinical level.

The non-opioid analgesic efficacy provided by RLB theoretically offers an approach that could contribute to reducing opioid-related side effects seen in classical sedoanalgesia applications. In this respect, it is consistent with ERAS (Enhanced Recovery After Surgery) protocols. Opioid-related adverse effects are known to be largely dose-dependent. Therefore, opioid-saving analgesia methods offer a theoretical advantage in terms of reducing the risk of opioid-related side effects13. In our study, no respiratory side effects, hypotension, or cardiac arrhythmias were observed in the anesthesia observation records of the RLB group. However, the fact that these side effects were not prospectively evaluated using a standardized scoring system is a limitation of the study. Hemodynamic parameters (HR and MAP) were determined to be stable throughout the procedure (Figs. 3 and 4) in RLB group. This demonstrates that RLB offers not only analgesic efficacy but also a safe profile in terms of hemodynamic stability. The literature demonstrates that USG-guided bilateral RLB reduces opioid consumption in postoperative pain management in children undergoing open heart surgery via median sternotomy, resulting in faster recovery and shorter intensive care stays14. For postoperative analgesia after breast surgery, ESPB and RLB are performed at similar puncture sites, have lower risks, and are equivalent in effectiveness to ESPB, with similar time to first postoperative rescue analgesia15,16. Zhu et al. compared ESPB, RLB, and PVB in single-incision video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery and concluded that RLB and ESPB provide good analgesia and are safe alternatives17. Murouchi and Yamakage found that RLB was a safer procedure with similar analgesic efficacy to ESPB in a randomized controlled trial18. Our results are also consistent with these findings.

RLB, compared to other thoracic blocks, may increase the effectiveness of the safe procedure by being easier to apply. In our study, no serious complications, hematomas, pneumothorax, or neurological side effects were observed during the application of RLB. These findings support the good safety profile of RLB. Comparative studies have reported that regional blocks result in lower VAS scores, less need for additional analgesics, and higher patient satisfaction19,20. Maldonado-Avila et al. It was concluded that the 12th subcostal nerve block analgesic regimen with 2% lidocaine was as effective alone or in combination with other analgesics and could therefore be used as the sole analgesic method during ESWL21. Another randomized controlled trial found that qauadratus lumborum block provided adequate analgesia for ESWL and significantly reduced additional opioid consumption compared to the control group22. Fluoroscopy is performed at short intervals during each shock wave application to determine whether the stone is located at the focus of the shock wave. Longer fluoroscopy times increase the radiation dose. Reducing fluoroscopy time (e.g., achieving good analgesia and maintaining a stable patient position) is an important quality and safety indicator. A prospective randomized controlled trial examining the effect of ESPB on pain management and stone-free status in patients undergoing ESWL found lower VAS scores and, most importantly, less fluoroscopy and radiation exposure in these patients23. Another study reported that the combination of unilateral USG-guided TAP block and diclofenac is a viable option for pain relief during ESWL procedures24. ESPB is an effective and safe opioid-free analgesic in pediatric ESWL patients25.

In addition to the analgesia provided by RLB, the stone-free rate was 63.3% in the RLB group and 43.3% in the control group. Although the difference between groups did not reach statistical significance, the RLB group had a higher stone-free rate. Stone-free rates after ESWL are affected by patient-related factors such as stone localization, BMI, skin-to-stone distance, stone size, stone composition and density (Hounsfield Unit), and the presence of a ureteral stent, as well as technical factors such as applied shock wave parameters and treatment protocol, independently of the level of analgesia26–28. No statistically significant difference was found between the groups in stone size, stone localization, or BMI, and the effect of these variables on the stone-free rate was similar in both groups; stone density was not evaluated. It is known that high stone density negatively affects the success of ESWL, and the lack of such data may have limited the ability to detect differences in stone-free rates. In our study, a protective ESWL protocol aimed at minimizing renal parenchymal damage was applied to all patients, and shock-wave counts and energy levels were individualized based on patient characteristics. This approach may have limited the improved analgesia provided by RLB, thereby providing an additional contribution to fragmentation efficiency.

Although the retrolaminar block is considered a safe and controlled technique, potential complications remain. Possible complications such as hematoma at the injection site, infection, unwanted epidural/paravertebral spread, hypotension, pneumothorax, and local anesthetic systemic toxicity have been reported, albeit rarely29. It has been stated that the risk of major complications is lower than with PVB and epidural blocks because it is applied to a superficial area, and this risk is further reduced with ultrasound guidance10. Complications associated with the ESWL procedure: Serious conditions such as bleeding (hematuria, perirenal hematoma), risk of infection and sepsis, ureteral obstruction by stone fragments (steinstrasse), ureteral perforation, tissue and organ damage (pancreatitis) may occur30. Unstable hemodynamic conditions have been identified as the predominant modifiable risk factor for renal hematoma development. Hematoma rates have been reported to increase from 0.66% to 3.8% due to unstable MAP during the procedure, particularly in hypertensive patients31. Therefore, maintaining a normotensive state is crucial for the development of complications. This can only be achieved by reducing patient anxiety, minimizing pain, and improving patient comfort. In our study, patient and operator satisfaction was found to be statistically significantly higher in the RLB group (Table 2; p < 0.001). No arrhythmia, respiratory depression, or respiratory complications were observed in any patient undergoing RLB. This confirms the high safety profile of RLB and its ability to prevent undesirable hemodynamic fluctuations during ESWL.

This study supports the use of RLB as an effective and safe analgesic method in ESWL procedures. Future studies will further evaluate the long-term outcomes and efficacy of RLB in different stone morphologies, strengthening its integration into clinical practice.

Limitations

The limitations of this study are its single-center, observational design, relatively small sample size, and only partial blinding. Multicenter, larger-sample, randomized controlled trials are needed to improve the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, further research is needed to more comprehensively evaluate the analgesic efficacy of ESWL in patients with standardized stone morphology. The duration of the block application and the time to onset of analgesic effect were retrospectively evaluated from anesthesia monitoring forms. The application of a protective ESWL protocol aimed at minimizing renal parenchymal damage in patients may have limited its additional contribution to RLB fragmentation efficiency.

In conclusion, this prospective observational study demonstrated that RLB is a safe, effective, and feasible method for analgesia in patients undergoing ESWL. RLB effectively reduced pain and opioid requirements, contributed to hemodynamic stability, and was associated with a higher stone-free rate, although this difference was not statistically significant. RLB can be considered a safe, practical, and effective analgesic alternative for clinical application in minimally invasive urological procedures.
