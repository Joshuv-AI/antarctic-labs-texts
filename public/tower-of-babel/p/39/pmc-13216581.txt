Spectral demixing enables reliable dual color pair correlation function analysis of viral and cellular proteins in live cells
Authors: Natalia Philipp, Melina Magalnik, Berta Pozzi, Anabella Srebrow, Laura C Estrada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPair correlation function (pCF) is a powerful approach that has gained increasing popularity in recent years for studying protein dynamics in living cells; however, its application remains limited by methodological constraints and the absence of standardized protocols. In this work, we optimize key aspects of pCF and dual color-pCF analysis, showing that reliable results can be achieved with shorter acquisition times, reducing motion artifacts. We demonstrate that spectral bleed-through as low as 1% produces spurious cross-correlations, underscoring the need for rigorous correction. To address this, we introduce a robust signal demixing strategy that removes false-positive correlations while preserving genuine molecular interactions. This advance expands the reliability of correlation analyses in complex live-cell environments. Applying this approach, we investigate the nucleo-cytoplasmic shuttling of the splicing factor RBM10 in living cells expressing dengue virus (DENV) NS5 polymerase under poly(I:C)-triggered innate immune response. Our findings reveal that RBM10 and NS5 not only interact but also co-shuttle between the nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments. Furthermore, RBM10 transport is differentially modulated by both NS5 and innate immune induction, reinforcing the role of DENV in altering host nuclear transport. Overall, this work establishes signal demixing as a key advance for live-cell correlation techniques and demonstrates its potential for uncovering complex host-virus interactions.Subject terms: Biological fluorescence, Biophysical methods

Introduction

Understanding protein behaviour in living cells requires imaging techniques with high spatial and temporal resolution. Advances in fluorescence microscopy, combined with the development of targeted fluorescent sensors, have enabled the live-cell imaging of specific molecular components under physiological conditions1. Among these techniques, Fluorescence Correlation Spectroscopy (FCS) has emerged as a powerful and minimally invasive approach for quantifying molecular diffusion, concentration, oligomeric state, and transport barriers based on the analysis of spontaneous fluorescence fluctuations. Compared to techniques such as Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP) or Total Internal Reflection Fluorescence (TIRF), FCS enables detailed kinetic studies using standard confocal microscopy setups with minimal photodamage.

In this work, we apply the pair correlation function (pCF), a variant of FCS that enables spatially and temporally resolved measurements of protein transport within cells2. By correlating the fluorescence signal between two spatially separated pixels over time, pCF allows not only the estimation of molecular diffusion times but also the detection of diffusion barriers and active transport processes. This method has been successfully used to study protein shuttling3,4 and diffusion around cellular structures5. However, despite the method’s sensitivity to experimental conditions and its susceptibility to detecting artifacts, the literature offering standardized protocols for data acquisition and analysis remains limited. While a detailed acquisition protocol for pCF was only recently published by Sánchez et al.6, our group has been working in parallel for several years to refine and apply this technique to a diversity of biological contexts4,7,8. In this work, we present our independently developed methodology for dual-color pCF experiments, with particular emphasis on minimizing spectral artifacts and promoting reproducibility.

One of the main challenges when extending pCF to the simultaneous measurement of multiple molecular species is spectral bleeding, where emission from one fluorophore contaminates the detection channel of another. This can result in artificial cross-correlation signals, which may be misinterpreted as molecular interactions. In previous works, hardware-based solutions such as pulsed interleaved excitation (PIE) and time-resolved detection have been implemented to reduce spectral overlap9,10. These approaches are often inaccessible to most laboratories due to their reliance on specialized equipment. Computational alternatives, such as linear demixing or matrix-based corrections, can mitigate spectral bleed-through; however, they may require multiple reference measurements and rely on additional assumptions. In this work, we introduce a demixing strategy that employs only a single fluorescent tag per protein and a single control experiment per fluorophore, whereas other approaches frequently require multiple tags. Despite its simplicity, the method presented in this work achieves a suppression of artificial crosstalk of approximately 86%.

While previous studies have implemented demixing techniques, either through hardware modifications or software corrections, these have been presented as optional enhancements aimed at improving sensitivity or resolution. In contrast, our findings demonstrate that demixing is not merely advantageous but indispensable for obtaining accurate and meaningful measurements under the studied conditions. By systematically quantifying the impact of demixing on the signal and demonstrating its necessity to prevent misinterpretation of the data, our work underscores the crucial role of this step in ensuring the validity of the results. This perspective not only builds upon earlier approaches but also provides a more rigorous framework for future studies employing similar methodologies.

We developed a simple, post-acquisition correction method to overcome spectral bleeding on dual-color pCF based on control measurements. The proposed strategy enables robust spectral demixing without the need for specialized hardware, thereby significantly improving the reliability of cross-correlation analysis in dual-color experiments. To facilitate the broader application of the methodology, we developed homemade routines in Python for pCF data analysis.

To rigorously validate our methodology under biologically meaningful conditions, we applied it to an already characterized experimental system involving the interaction between a cellular protein, RBM10, and a viral protein, NS5, in cultured human cell lines.

RBM10 is an RNA-binding protein known to regulate pre-mRNA splicing in mammalian cells11–13. It shows nucleoplasmic localization as well as enrichment in membrane-less nuclear compartments known as S1-1 nuclear bodies14. However, there are reports of cytoplasmic localization, primarily associated with human pathological conditions15–18.

NS5 is one of the seven non-structural proteins encoded by the genome of dengue virus (DENV, a member of the Flaviviridae family) and is the polymerase responsible for its replication19. Even though this process takes place mainly in the cytoplasm of infected cells, the steady-state localization of NS5 varies among serotypes. In particular, NS5 from DENV serotype 2 shows a strong nuclear accumulation20.

Recent studies have linked RBM10 to multiple human pathologies21, and one of our laboratories has reported a novel antiviral role for RBM10 during DENV infection17. The cited study showed that RBM10 induces the expression of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), enhancing the innate immune response and ultimately limiting DENV replication. Moreover, upon DENV infection, a slight relocalization of RBM10 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm was observed. Besides, it was shown that RBM10 interacts with NS5, and the mere expression of this viral protein promotes RBM10 proteasomal degradation. We hypothesize that this regulatory phenomenon could be part of the molecular mechanisms deployed by DENV to counteract cellular innate immunity22–25. Whether the altered RBM10 localization is also a consequence of the viral interference or is solely part of the cellular antiviral response remains unclear.

In the aforementioned study, the interaction between RBM10 and NS5 was only demonstrated by co-immunoprecipitation assays, lacking spatial and temporal resolution17. Here, we characterize the dynamics of RBM10-NS5 interaction and the nucleo-cytoplasmic shuttling of RBM10 under conditions that mimic viral infection, using our open-source tools, validating our demixing approach, while providing new insights into viral-host protein interplay.

Results

Defining practical limits in pCF: impact of acquisition length on data quality

The reliability of pCF analysis critically depends on acquiring data at sufficiently high temporal resolution. Pioneering studies2,5 have established that the line scan speed should exceed the diffusion timescale of the fluorescent species by at least an order of magnitude and last several times their characteristic time. Although conventional pCF experiments typically involve acquisition durations ranging from 60 to 200 seconds (corresponding to approximately 200,000 lines for typical scanning times), the minimum number of lines required to achieve accurate and reproducible measurements has not been established. This limitation is particularly critical in live-cell applications, where prolonged acquisition increases the risk of motion-induced artifacts that can compromise the interpretation of the results. To systematically evaluate the effect of acquisition length on pCF accuracy and precision, we conducted simulations of freely diffusing molecules (106 counts/s/molecule, cpsm) in a confined space with a diffusion coefficient D = 10 µm2/s. Raster scanning was simulated at a line time of 0.64 ms, and pCF curves were computed for subsets of the data ranging from 5000 (3.2 s) to 200,000 (128 s) lines.

For consistency with our live-cell analyses, all simulations were analyzed using a fixed correlation distance of 16 pixels (0.8 µm), a distance chosen to be sufficiently larger than the simulated point spread function (~ 0.3 μm) to minimize self-correlation effects2. Each simulated condition was performed in ten independent replicates to evaluate both the accuracy (defined as the deviation from the expected correlation time value) and precision (assessed as the variability among replicates) of the data. Given the simulated diffusion coefficient, the expected characteristic correlation time (τc) was approximately 10 ms. Therefore, all analyzed subsets of data satisfied the condition Δt ≫ τc, making them theoretically sufficiently long for capturing the underlying diffusion dynamics.

As shown in Fig. 1, the diffusion coefficient obtained from τc estimates remained consistently accurate across most acquisition durations. Notably, only the shortest analyses (5,000 lines) exhibited a substantial deviation from the expected τc value, accompanied by increased variability, indicating reduced measurement reliability under this condition. Precision, assessed as the standard deviation of τc across replicates, improved substantially with increasing analysis duration (Fig. 1c). Variability decreased beyond approximately 10,000 lines, reaching a plateau around 20,000 lines (~ 13 s). Further increases in acquisition time yielded no significant enhancement in precision.

These findings demonstrate that robust pCF analysis can be obtained from substantially shorter simulated recording windows than those typically employed, provided that the temporal analysis window significantly exceeds the characteristic diffusion timescale. This offers two key advantages: (i) reduced acquisition durations minimize the risk of motion-induced artifacts in live-cell imaging, and (ii) the resulting data can be temporally segmented, enabling selective analysis or averaging of motion-free intervals to enhance measurement reliability.

In conclusion, the results shown here establish a practical lower limit of approximately 20,000 lines for accurate and reproducible pCF measurements under standard experimental conditions. This refinement improves both data quality and experimental flexibility, particularly in the context of live-cell measurements.

To evaluate the impact of acquisition time length on live-cell pCF analysis, we repeated the previous systematic study using cells transiently overexpressing RBM10 fused to EGFP on its C-terminus (RBM10-EGFP) while varying the number of processed lines.

Lines of 128 pixels across the nuclear membrane of HEK 293 T cells were acquired. In Fig. 2, the first ~80 pixels of the linescan correspond to the nucleus and the remaining pixels to the cytoplasm of the cell. To assess the effect of the number of analyzed lines (or experiment duration), we compared pCF curves obtained from varying subsets of the original dataset, analyzing between 5000 and 100,000 lines (3 to 60 s acquisition time). The intensity kymogram (Fig. 2a) shows minimal displacement of the nuclear membrane, indicating that no cell movement occurred during the experiment. For RBM10-EGFP- overexpressing cells, we measured two peaks of correlation (Fig. 2b) associated with two different dynamical behaviours. Across all conditions, the extracted correlation times, τc, ranged from 25 to 100 ms, consistent with the expected diffusion behavior of nuclear proteins (apparent D ~ 1 to 20 μm2/s)26. It is worth noting that the results for 100,000 lines are suboptimal, displaying a high correlation plateau, rather than a well-defined peak. This is probably due to cell movement in the order of seconds, which is negligible for shorter analysis sections; however, in this case, it becomes relevant. As the number of analyzed lines decreased, variability among equivalent measurements increased; however, the obtained τc remained consistent across all the analyzed cells, supporting the robustness of the pCF analysis even under reduced acquisition durations. Based on these findings, we adopted a strategy of analyzing between 20,000 and 50,000 lines with the exact number optimized on a per-experiment basis. This range offers a balanced compromise between temporal resolution, measurement reliability, and resilience to motion-induced artifacts in live-cell imaging.

Evaluating the impact of spectral bleeding in pCF and dual-color pCF analyses

Dual-color experiments are designed to investigate the behavior of two proteins simultaneously and typically involve multicolor labeling with distinct excitation and emission detection channels for each fluorophore. Despite careful optimization, some degree of spectral overlap between channels is inevitable. Although often negligible in conventional fluorescence imaging, cross-channel bleeding can pose significant challenges for correlation-based techniques such as the pCF analysis applied to dual-color experiments, which rely on detecting subtle intensity fluctuations. Previous studies6 suggest that spectral bleeding levels of approximately 5% are acceptable and unlikely to substantially bias the results. However, this threshold appears to be more of a practical guideline than a rigorously established limit. To critically assess the extent and potential impact of signal bleed-through, we performed a systematic study designed to evaluate how even low levels of spectral bleeding may distort two-channel correlation analysis results. This approach enables an evaluation of whether the commonly cited 5% threshold is broadly valid across different experimental contexts or if a stricter control of spectral separation is necessary to ensure accurate and reliable data interpretation.

We simulated line-scan confocal experiments in which particles diffuse freely in a 128×128 pixel field (1 pixel = 50 nm), with a diffusion coefficient of D = 10 μm2/s and a concentration of 1 particle/μm2. The simulated scans consisted of 64-pixel long lines acquired at 2.5 μs/pixel, with a total of 200,000 lines per simulation. To isolate the effects of spectral bleeding, we evaluated three distinct scenarios.

In the first scenario, two independent populations of diffusing particles are emitting in different spectral regions (green dots in Fig. 3a are detected exclusively in the green channel, and red dots exclusively in the red channel). All particles shared the same diffusion coefficient, D, fluorescence brightness in their respective channel, and moved independently of one another, as no interactions were introduced between them. In the second scenario, we simulated heterodimers composed of one green and one red particle, with each particle contributing signal exclusively to its respective channel (Fig. 3b). This case represents an interaction in which correlated motion across channels is expected. The final scenario combines elements from the previous two. Although the red and green particles diffuse independently, without coordinated motion, spectral bleeding of 1% was introduced by allowing one of the populations to emit primarily in its assigned channel (106 cps), while also contributing a small (10⁴ cps), unintended signal to the other channel (olive particles in Fig. 3c). This condition emulates a more experimentally realistic scenario where some spectral bleeding could occur between detection channels. We then performed pCF analyses at 16 pixels (0.8 μm) across the scanned lines for both channels, as well as the cross correlation between them (Fig. 3d–f). In the case of non-interacting, spectrally distinct particles (Fig. 3d), the pCF kymogram for both channels was homogeneous and highly similar, reflecting isotropic diffusion with no structural or interaction constraints. The cross-channel pair correlation function (ccpCF) in this condition was, as expected, essentially flat, confirming the absence of cross-channel dynamics (Fig. 3g, yellow and purple).

Effect of spectral bleeding on cross-correlation analysis.

a Schematic of the line scan experiment with two distinct fluorophores (green and red dots) freely diffusing through the detection volume (overlapping PSFs are shown in green and red colors). b Illustration of green and red molecules mixed with heterodimers containing both fluorophores. c Scenario where one fluorophore (olive) emits primarily in the first channel but also bleeds 1% signal into the second channel, while the second fluorophore (red) emits exclusively in the second channel. Panels d, e, and f display the corresponding pCF(16) kymograms for both channels in each scenario. Correlation profiles for each channel, along with the cross-channel pair correlation function (ccpCF, gold and purple), are shown in (g), (h), and (i), revealing no cross-correlation in (g) and clear positive cross-correlations in (h) and (i) due to heterodimerization and spectral bleed-through, respectively.

In contrast, in the second scenario involving heterodimers, the pCF kymograms showed high similarity, as both particles diffused as a complex (Fig. 3e) and produced a clear, positive cross-correlation signal, consistent with joint correlated movement (Fig. 3h). This served as a benchmark for how true interaction should look in the correlation analysis.

Finally, in the third case, despite the absence of any interaction between the particles, the ccpCF analysis revealed a significant false-positive cross-correlation peak (Fig. 3i, yellow and purple curves). This result is purely due to the spectral bleeding introduced during the simulation. While the individual pCF kymograms remained consistent with particles’ free diffusion, the appearance of artificial cross-channel correlation underscores how even minimal bleed-through (1% of the total intensity) can lead to misinterpretations in ccpCF studies. Despite optimizing detector settings to maximize spectral separation, bleed-through below 1% cannot be guaranteed for most fluorophores.

Altogether, these results demonstrate that while pCF itself is robust against minor spectral overlap, ccpCF is highly sensitive to even low bleeding levels. Careful experimental design, including the implementation of a rigorous spectral separation and proper controls, is therefore essential when interpreting cross-correlation data as evidence for protein-protein interaction in live-cell imaging.

Spectral bleeding correction

In this work, we detail a method for demixing the information contained in two detection channels inspired by the work for Raster image cross-correlation in Schrimpf et al.27. This approach is readily scalable to multiple channels. To apply any correction for spectral bleeding, it is essential to perform control measurements that enable quantification of the contribution of each fluorophore to all detection channels. These control experiments, performed by imaging cells expressing a single fluorophore at a time, allow us to determine the extent of bleeding and the correction matrix that characterizes the relative signal contributions from each fluorophore to all detection channels. As an initial validation of this approach, we tested it on a system in which two species freely diffuse within a three-dimensional cubic volume (128x128x128 pixels). To simulate spectral bleeding, green particles were set to emit at 10⁶ cps in the green channel and 10⁵ cps in the red channel (10% bleeding). Conversely, red particles emitted 10⁶ cps in the red channel and 10⁴ cps in the green channel (1% bleeding). Raster scan acquisition was simulated along a 64-pixel line with a pixel dwell time of 2 μs/pixel, yielding 200,000 total lines (Fig. 4a, schematic). The simulated time series were then analyzed using the pair correlation function at a distance of 16 pixels, pCF(16), as before. Given that the simulated diffusion is isotropic, we averaged the correlation functions across all pixels to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. Additionally, we simulated control images to obtain the p matrix and explored the influence of different intensities of the fluorophores in each channel (see Supplementary Fig. 1).

The resulting cross-correlation pCF (ccpCF) computed in both directions (green to red and red to green) exhibits artificial positive correlation (Fig. 4b). These spurious cross-correlations are solely attributable to spectral bleeding as they are not reflective of any true interaction between particles. Meanwhile, the resulting pCF curves for each channel (green and red) yielded identical correlation times, as expected due to the identical D value of the two species (Fig. 4c).

We then repeated the analysis on the spectrally demixed signals (schematic Fig. 4d of how the molecules ideally should look). As shown in Fig. 4e, the cross correlation is gone while the pCF(16) curves for each channel retained their original correlation times (Fig. 4f), confirming that the demixing procedure preserved the intrinsic dynamics of each population. Importantly, the ccpCF curve was eliminated in both directions, whereas the correlation times for the pCF of each individual channel remained unchanged. These results demonstrate that the spectral demixing method successfully removes cross-channel bleeding without distorting the underlying dynamic information, confirming the validity and robustness of the proposed correction procedure for dual-color pCF analyses.

Experimental validation of bleeding correction for live-cell imaging: application to the study of the interaction and intracellular dynamics of the cellular protein RBM10 and dengue virus NS5 polymerase

We next validated the performance of our demixing approach in a biologically relevant system involving the host protein RBM10 and the viral polymerase NS5 in the context of dual-color live-cell imaging experiments by dual-color pCF. Given its subcellular distribution and its recently described involvement in the cellular antiviral response17, RBM10 provides an interesting model to assess the robustness of ccpCF measurements in a live-cell context.

DENV NS5 localization varies among serotypes, and intriguingly, DENV-2 NS5 displays a predominantly nuclear localization20, as does RBM10. The interaction between these two proteins was previously described by one of our laboratories based on biochemical data from co-immunoprecipitation assays17. Moreover, RBM10 had been detected among the interactors of NS5 from Zika virus, another member of the Flaviviridae family, through an unbiased proteomics approach28. Even though these techniques are informative, they lack spatio-temporal resolution. To further characterize this reported interaction between RBM10 and NS5, gaining novel insight into its dynamics and subcellular localization, we used correlation methods applied to living cells. To that end, we cotransfected a human cell line with expression vectors for two fusion proteins: RBM10 fused to EGFP on its C-terminus (RBM10-EGFP) and DENV-2-NS5 fused to mCherry on its N-terminus (mCherry-NS5). Having these proteins tagged with different fluorophores allows us to study how NS5 and RBM10 dynamics are intertwined. For all dual-color experiments, fluorescence signals were pre-processed with the spectral demixing procedure before pCF analysis. Two detection channels were employed, each assigned to a specific fluorophore as detailed in the Methods section. However, due to partial spectral overlap between fluorophores, cross-channel bleeding was observed (Fig. 5a). To quantify and correct the bleeding, control experiments were performed in which only one fluorescent protein was expressed at a time. Figure 5b, c presents the results of the spectral demixing procedure for two representative cells: one transfected exclusively with RBM10-EGFP and the other with mCherry-NS5 expression vectors. These single-fluorophore control experiments were used to derive the demixing matrix used to correct for spectral bleed-through in the following dual-color analyses. Figure 5b demonstrates that spectral bleed-through occurs in both detection channels, as the specific distribution patterns of each protein are discernible in all channels regardless of the fluorophore used. Figure 5c shows the application of the matrix to the corresponding images. Following this spectral demixing procedure, fluorescence signals in the unintended channels were effectively reduced to background levels, confirming the efficacy of the correction strategy.

Although the correction procedure led to a modest reduction in the signal intensity of the target channel, this effect is expected. Remarkably, the spatial intensity distribution within the images remained unchanged, preserving high contrast relative to the background and thereby maintaining data integrity. While balanced expression levels are generally preferable for optimal visualization, the demixing procedure effectively corrects the correlated EGFP leakage, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

As further validation of the effectiveness of our demixing strategy, we performed ccpCF analysis on cells cotransfected with RBM10-EGFP and mCherry. Since mCherry is an unrelated fluorescent protein commonly used as a tagging control, no cross-correlation between the two signals was expected. Figure 5d, e shows the results of the cross-correlation analysis (computed at 16 pixels) before and after applying the spectral demixing procedure.

In the original data (Fig. 5d), a positive cross-correlation peak is observed at an intermediate time between the characteristic correlation times obtained from the individual pCF curves of RBM10-EGFP (green) and mCherry (red). Given the lack of biological interaction between these proteins, this peak is attributable to spectral bleed-through of the RBM10-EGFP signal into the mCherry detection channel (gold curve) and mCherry bleed-through into the EGFP detection channel (purple curve). Upon applying the bleeding correction and recalculating the pCF and ccpCF for the same dataset, the previously observed positive cross-correlation was effectively eliminated, whereas the individual pCF peaks for each channel remained unchanged (Fig. 5e). The crosstalk subtraction step can increase the overall noise level, occasionally leading to negative correlation peaks. These negative fluctuations do not reflect any underlying physical or biological dynamics. This interpretation is supported by the fact that they do not persist when the correlation is computed in the reverse channel order (gold and purple curves in Fig. 5e), indicating that they arise from noise amplification rather than from genuine anti-correlated motion or a demixing-related artifact.

This result demonstrates that the introduced bleeding correction effectively eliminates spurious correlations while preserving the authentic dynamic information associated with each protein. Consequently, when such a correction is applied, observed dual-color cross-correlations can be reliably interpreted as evidence of true molecular interactions.

Having performed the appropriate spectral bleeding corrections, we investigated the interaction between RBM10 and the viral protein NS5 by performing pCF experiments in cotransfected cells. Line scans were acquired across the nuclear envelope, connecting the cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments (Fig. 6a). This scanning path ensures that approximately half of the pixels sample the nucleus and the rest the cytoplasm. By selecting appropriate pixel pairs on either side of the nuclear membrane, we could independently assess both nuclear import and export within the same dataset by correlating the pixels in one way or the opposite. We focused the analysis on pCF(16) (equivalent to 0.8 μm) as this distance is sufficiently larger than the PSF size and enables multiple pixel pairs to be correlated across compartments, yielding robust results upon averaging. In the majority of the cells analyzed, the pCF curves exhibited two distinct correlation peaks (Fig. 6b), which we interpret as indicative of two molecular populations with different dynamical behaviors. Similar dual-peak profiles have been previously reported for other proteins4. The first, faster peak, occurring on the millisecond timescale, likely corresponds to a freely diffusing population. The second, slower peak, appearing at a correlation time one order of magnitude longer, indicates the presence of a second population with substantially slower dynamics, presumably due to reduced mobility resulting from transient interactions with sub-cellular structures and/or macromolecular complexes. Based on the known RNA-binding ability of RBM10 and its role as a splicing regulator, it is possible to envision its association with chromatin within the cell nucleus, along the course of transcription and pre-mRNA processing events, as well as its presence in large ribonucleoprotein complexes accompanying RNA molecules along their nucleo-cytoplasmic journey and metabolism. Altogether, these possibilities support the hindered movement as the most likely interpretation for the slower population, turning it into a particular interest as it may reflect biologically relevant interactions and will therefore be the focus of our comparative analysis across experimental conditions.

To further investigate potential co-transport or complex formation, we computed the ccpCF analysis in both directions, correlating the green channel (RBM10-EGFP) with the red channel (mCherry-NS5), and vice versa. As shown in Fig. 6b, both ccpCF analyses yield a positive correlation, with peak times consistent with those observed in the individual single-color pCF curves for each channel. This pattern was consistently observed in RBM10-EGFP + mCherry-NS5 co-transfected cells (N = 26, from 3 independent experiment days (D)), with robust positive cross-correlation. In contrast, 86% of control cells co-transfected with RBM10-EGFP and mCherry (N = 31, D = 4), and 80% of cells co-transfected with GFP and mCherry-NS5 (N = 25, D = 3) showed no cross-correlation signal (Supplementary Fig. 5). Together, these results provide strong evidence that RBM10 and NS5 are co-transported across the nuclear membrane, either by direct interaction or by assembling into the same protein complex. Importantly, the absence of cross-correlation in control conditions confirms that the measured co-transport is specific to the RBM10-NS5 interaction and not an artifact of the fluorescent tags used.

The scale bar represents 5 μm. Out of the at least 200,000 acquired lines, 50,000 were analyzed by selecting the region with minimal motion. Panels d–f show an example for each condition of the pCF(16) performed for pixels correlating from cytoplasm to nucleus (full line) and from nucleus to cytoplasm (dashed line). The transport times for both entry (g) and exit (h) are shown for all conditions as well as for the control condition (cells cotransfected with RBM10-GFP and mCherry, N = 31 cells, D = 4). We have already demonstrated that RBM10-EGFP is associated with mCherry-NS5 throughout its nuclear cytoplasmic shuttling. This may impact the functional role of RBM10, in particular its involvement in host cell innate immune response. To further explore this hypothesis, we performed a complementary analysis by co-transfecting human cultured cells not only with RBM10-EGFP and mCherry-NS5 expression vectors but also with poly(I:C), a synthetic analogue of viral RNA. This compound activates the cellular sensors that trigger signalling cascades leading to the induction of a cellular antiviral state27. This viral-free approach recapitulates a cellular innate immune response without the complexity of the dynamic interplay between cellular and viral proteins and processes that take place during the course of an infection23,29, and it was previously used to study the involvement of RBM10 in innate immunity17. The combination of poly(I:C) with NS5 ectopic expression enables us to study the interference carried out by a single viral protein of interest, simplifying the experimental model. This approach has already been employed for studying DENV NS5-mediated alteration of host cell gene expression30.

Upon transfecting cultured cells with expression vectors for RBM10-EGFP, mCherry-NS5 together with poly(I:C), we measured the transport times across the nuclear membrane (Fig. 7b, e). The addition of poly(I:C) did not significantly alter the translocation times of RBM10-EGFP measured in the presence of mCherry-NS5 (Fig. 7g, h, Mann–Whitney test p value = 0.37 for entry and p value = 0.97 for exit). We also detected a positive cross-correlation (ccpCF) between RBM10-EGFP and mCherry-NS5, consistent with the formation of a molecular complex between the two proteins in this new cellular context (Supplementary Fig. 4).

We had previously observed that RBM10 levels modulate the induction of Interferon Stimulated Genes (ISGs) triggered by poly(I:C)17. With this in mind, to rule out any potential effect of poly(I:C) on RBM10-EGFP dynamics that might be masked by the presence of mCherry-NS5, we co-transfected cells with RBM10-EGFP and poly(I:C), using mCherry alone as a control instead of mCherry-NS5 (Fig. 7c, f). The impact of poly(I:C) was evaluated by pCF analysis. As previously done for the mCherry-NS5 co-transfection experiments, this analysis allowed us to extract nuclear import and export dynamics from a single dataset by simply reversing the order of the correlated pixel pairs (Fig. 7f). As observed in Fig. 7g, h, both RBM10-EGFP nuclear import and export are fastened upon poly(I:C) addition (Mann-Whitney test p-value = 1.2×10-5 for entry and p-value = 1.5×10-4 for exit). Cells overexpressing unfused EGFP under the same experimental conditions do not reproduce these results (see Supplementary Material Fig. 6), suggesting that the effect of poly(I:C) is specific to gated nuclear transport, and not to membrane diffusion.

It is worth noting that the evident effect of poly(I:C) on RBM10-EGFP shuttling time disappears when NS5 is present (no significant differences between this population and the cells without poly(I:C) treatment, as shown in Fig. 7g, h). This suggests that even though the sole presence of mCherry-NS5 does not impact RBM10-EGFP translocation time, this viral protein is, in fact, modulating it, and its effect overrides that of poly(I:C). Altogether, these observations suggest a differential modulation of RBM10-EGFP shuttling by mCherry-NS5 and poly(I:C), both on nuclear entry and exit.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated critical yet often overlooked parameters affecting pair correlation function (pCF) analysis. While previous studies typically report the acquisition of approximately 200,000 scanning lines, we demonstrate that reliable results can be achieved with significantly shorter acquisition times, with 50,000 lines representing an optimal compromise. This reduction is particularly advantageous for certain cell cultures, where longer acquisitions increase the likelihood of motion artifacts affecting the measurements.

Another neglected parameter is spectral bleeding during cross-pCF analysis. To our knowledge, the commonly accepted guideline of 5% bleed-through is not supported by experimental evidence. In both simulated and live-cell imaging experiments, we showed that even 1% spectral bleeding can introduce artificial correlations. To address this, we developed a straightforward signal demixing approach that effectively eliminates these false cross-correlation positive peaks while preserving true correlation.

We applied this method to study the dynamics of RBM10 nucleo-cytoplasmic shuttling in the context of the combined ectopic expression of a viral protein with the induction of the innate immune response triggered by the viral RNA analog poly(I:C). Mass spectrometry analysis previously provided evidence of the interaction between many splicing-related proteins and NS5 from DENV31 and ZIKV28, both members of the Flaviviridae family. Specifically, the interaction between RBM10 with ZIKV-NS528 and DENV-NS517 was detected by biochemical techniques in cell lysates. By cross-correlation analysis, we were able to determine that these proteins not only interact in living cells but also are transported together from and into the nucleus (Fig. 6). To the best of our knowledge, the use of cross-correlation to evaluate protein interactions in living cells has been barely exploited6 and, being an extremely powerful tool, warrants further research and development.

NS5 is the DENV polymerase responsible for the replication of the viral genome, a process which is known to happen in the cytoplasm. The nuclear localization of this protein in certain serotypes raises intriguing questions regarding possible serotype-specific nuclear functions of NS532. The techniques developed in the current study may contribute to further exploring this still enigmatic topic.

Hardly anything is known about RBM10 nucleo-cytoplasmic shuttling. However, there are available reports showing the cytoplasmic localization of RBM10 in the context of different human pathological conditions16–18 and our own bioinformatics prediction using nuclear export signal (NES) mapping software (https://ehubio.ehu.eus/wregex/) suggests the presence of NES within RBM10 protein sequence. As an alternative to the established heterokaryon assay33, the current study clearly demonstrates that RBM10 shuttles between the nucleus and the cytoplasm in living cells (Figs. 6, 7, Supplementary 3, and Supplementary 4).

As for RBM10-EGFP nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling times, they were not significantly altered upon mCherry-NS5 co-expression (Fig. 7a, d, g, and h, Mann-Whitney test p value = 0.9 for entry, p value = 0.76 for exit). We therefore propose that RBM10 and NS5 form a relatively small protein complex, based on the Stokes-Einstein law of diffusion34, since only a large protein complex would be expected to produce a pronounced shift in their diffusion times. On the other hand, we can hypothesize that the fastened transport of RBM10-EGFP (and not EGFP alone, Fig. 7c, f, g, h and Supplementary Fig. 4) caused by poly(I:C) transfection could be due to changes on RBM10-EGFP protein interaction network, post-translational modifications and/or other cellular events resulting from the induction of the innate immune response. Of note, the effect of mCherry-NS5 on RBM10-EGFP nuclear transport was only perceptible when combining both the ectopic expression of mCherry-NS5 with poly(I:C) (Fig. 7b, e, g, h). For both nuclear entry and exit, the combined effect was indistinguishable from mCherry-NS5 alone, overriding the poly(I:C) effect.

Despite our experimental evidence of NS5-mediated suppression of the poly(I:C) effect on RBM10 transport, the underlying mechanism remains unsolved and constitutes an intriguing open question. As mentioned above, RBM10 interactome could be altered upon poly(I:C)-triggered innate immune response either by changing protein post-translational modifications and/or the expression of particular RBM10 interactors. Under this scenario, it is possible to speculate that NS5 could be: sterically outcompeting RBM10 partners, preventing RBM10 post-translational modifications, triggering a different set of RBM10 post-translational modifications, and/or fostering new multimeric complexes, altogether overriding the poly(I:C) effect on RBM10 shuttling dynamics. It is worth noting that poly(I:C)- or NS5-triggered post-translational modifications may not be exclusive to RBM10 but also to other cellular proteins, ultimately impacting the cellular interaction network of RBM10. A deeper understanding of the involved molecular mechanisms, together with their cellular implications, warrants further investigation.

Host cell nuclear import and export machineries are known to be targeted by positive-strand RNA viruses, impacting nucleo-cytoplasmic trafficking of RNA molecules as well as of splicing factors35,36, consequently altering host cell gene expression. In particular, RBM10 increases its cytoplasmic localization upon DENV infection17. A similar localization change has been reported upon Sindbis virus infection37 for certain components of the U2 snRNP, a ribonucleoprotein complex of which RBM10 is an associated factor38.

Altogether, the results presented here contribute to the current knowledge of how viral infection impacts host cell nuclear transport through the development and application of state-of-the-art live-cell imaging techniques.

Methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

HEK 293 T cells (human embryonic kidney cell line, ATCC CRL-1573) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. HEK 293 T cell line was obtained from ATCC without authentication conducted in our laboratory and tested negative for mycoplasm contaminations. For imaging experiments, 25 mm glass coverslips (Marienfeld) were placed in 35 mm culture dishes and coated with 200 μl of 1 mg/ml poly(L-lysine) solution (MW 30,000–70,000; Sigma-Aldrich) for 20 min at room temperature. After coating, 4 × 10⁵ HEK 293 T cells were seeded onto each coverslip and transfected 24 h later.

DNA and poly(I:C) transfections

Plasmid DNA was transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s instructions. At 24 h after plasmid transfection, intracellular administration of polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid [poly(I:C) HMW; InvivoGen] was carried out by transfecting cells with poly(I:C) using Lipofectamine 2000 at a 5:1 poly(I:C)-to-lipid ratio, to a final concentration of poly(I:C) of 10 μg/ml. Cells were incubated for an additional 9 h before analysis.

RBM10-EGFP plasmid was kindly provided by Dr. Inoue’s laboratory (Osaka City University Graduate School of Medicine, Japan)39. The expression vector for mCherry-DENV2 NS5 was generated for this work. DENV2 NS5 coding sequence was amplified from Streptag-NS5 plasmid31 by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the following primers: FW-NS5-BsrGI ATTGTACAAGGGAACTGGCAACATAGG and REV-NS5-XhoI ATCTCGAGTTACCACAGAACTCCTGCTTCTT (Macrogen). The PCR product was digested with BsrGI and XhoI restriction enzymes and cloned into BsrGI/XhoI-digested pmCherry2-C1 plasmid (Addgene #54563).

Confocal Imaging

In vivo laser scanning confocal microscopy was performed using a Zeiss LSM 980 inverted microscope equipped with a ×40, 1.3 NA, oil-immersion objective (Zeiss, Germany) and a pinhole set to 1 Airy unit. Coverslips were mounted in Attofluor cell chambers (Invitrogen) and maintained in complete growth medium within a temperature-controlled incubation chamber at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for the duration of the experiments.

For imaging of cotransfected cells, fluorescence excitation was achieved using two laser lines, an Ar+ laser at 488 nm and a He-Ne laser at 543 nm. An MBS488/543 dichroic mirror was chosen to filter the laser’s line for detection. Laser power was optimized to achieve a photon count rate of approximately 1 × 106 cps, ensuring a high signal-to-noise ratio while minimizing photobleaching and phototoxicity. Emission was detected in two separate channels using band-pass filters set to 499–543 nm and 641–694 nm, respectively. Fluorescence was detected using GaAsP detectors (Hamamatsu) operated in photon-counting mode. In cells transfected with a single fluorophore, the second laser was turned off during imaging, except for control experiments performed to assess and correct for spectral bleed-through.

For line correlation analysis, data were acquired using the software line-scan mode with a line length of 128 pixels and a pixel size of 55 nm. A total of 200,000 consecutive lines were collected per measurement, with a line acquisition time of 0.61 ms.

All data were analyzed using a custom Python-based routine.

Statistics and reproducibility

In this study, we performed statistical tests to compare different conditions in both simulations and in cells. Given the inherent variability of biological processes, we selected a test that evaluates the entire distribution of results rather than relying on a single representative metric such as the mean. Specifically, we used the non-parametric Mann-Whitney test, which compares two independent samples that do not necessarily have the same size under the null hypothesis that both samples come from the same distribution. Each sample included data from 10 to 30 cells (defined as biologically independent samples or N), collected over different experimental days (D). The test was performed as a two-tailed analysis, and the null hypothesis (the two samples come from the same distribution) was rejected for p-values below 0.05.

Pair correlation function theory

The pair correlation function (pCF) is a fluorescence fluctuation-based technique developed to study protein dynamics in complex and heterogeneous environments such as living cells2. The method relies on computing the spatiotemporal correlation of fluorescence intensity fluctuations between two spatially separated pixels, G(τ,δr). When proteins can diffuse from one location to another, a peak in the correlation function appears at a time delay corresponding to the average time it takes for them to transit. The spatiotemporal correlation of fluorescence intensity, or pCF, is defined as

where F(r,t) is the fluorescence signal at position r and time t, δr is the spatial distance between the pixels, τ is the time lag, and angle brackets denote temporal averaging. When molecules diffuse freely between the two positions, the pCF exhibits a positive peak at a lag time determined by their diffusion coefficient. If no diffusion occurs between the points, the pCF remains flat and close to zero across all time lags. In cases where diffusion is hindered, for example, due to transient interactions or the presence of barriers, the correlation peak is delayed, reflecting the additional time required for proteins to traverse the space. This sensitivity to transport delay enables pCF to detect both impermeable and permeable barriers, as well as to distinguish between free and obstructed motion.

The method can be extended to dual-channel fluorescence imaging to investigate the coordinated dynamics of two distinct fluorescently labeled species. where F1 and F2 represent the fluorescence signals from channels 1 and 2, respectively. A positive ccpCF indicates coordinated motion between the two molecular species, suggesting an interaction or shared transport mechanism. If both proteins exhibit coordinated behavior (they form a complex), the cross-correlation function is expected to have similar correlation peaks observed at similar times, regardless of whether one channel is used as the reference one. Conversely, discrepancies in the position or shape of the correlation peaks may indicate an asymmetric influence of one molecule on the transport dynamics of the other, without the formation of a complex. This dual-color pCF analysis provides valuable insights into the spatial and temporal relationships between proteins within living cells, revealing dynamic interactions not detectable by static colocalization alone.

Correlation analysis

Accurate analysis of protein dynamics is fundamental for elucidating cellular processes and characterizing interactions between molecular species using cross-correlation techniques. A critical prerequisite for obtaining reliable cross-correlation measurements is the effective spectral separation of fluorescence signals from different detection channels, as spectral bleed-through can introduce artifacts that compromise data interpretation.

To address this challenge, we developed a streamlined workflow and software tool for processing and interpreting scanning line fluorescence correlation spectroscopy data. After completing the experiment, pCF curves are computed and analyzed using this custom Python-based software, which is available both as a graphical interface and as open-source code. The software is designed to be accessible for users familiar with the SimFCS software suite (LFD, UC Irvine), including features tailored to the needs of our research, with particular attention to avoiding bleeding artifacts in dual-color measurements. The software enables users to confidently extract mobility and interaction parameters, ensuring that signal mixing does not compromise data interpretation.

By improving the accuracy and reproducibility of cross-correlation analyses, it facilitates more reliable investigation of dynamic processes in living cells, where effective spectral demixing is essential for extracting biologically meaningful insights.
