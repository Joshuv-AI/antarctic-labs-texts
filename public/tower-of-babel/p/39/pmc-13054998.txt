From burden to advocacy

The perception of headache disorders as minor problems in neurology and global health has persisted for too long, obscuring their true impact.

The consistent estimates from GBD studies indicate that nearly four billion people worldwide live with a headache disorder, and migraine alone is one of the leading causes of disability, especially in young and middle-aged adults. The impact goes far beyond pain and affects education, work, families, and communities. Addressing headache disorders as a major brain health priority must become a global imperative, requiring improved access to diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. This persistent underestimation has held back progress in science, policy, and patient care [1, 2]. People living with headache disorders are often misunderstood by employers, educators, and even health professionals. This neglect comes at a cost, as untreated illness reduces well-being, participation, and productivity for affected individuals [3, 4]. It is precisely this battle for legitimacy, resources, and recognition that makes the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) not just relevant, but absolutely fundamental to any effective advocacy project for headache disorders [5].

At first glance, linking a headache to a set of global goals designed to guarantee well-being, end poverty, and consequently improve access to care, and ensure prosperity by 2030 might seem like a stretch [6, 7]. But this is the very power of the SDG framework. It provides a universal language and a powerful lens through which a personal health issue is revealed as a significant impediment to sustainable development. By anchoring advocacy in the SDGs, we can reframe headache disorders from a minor medical complaint into a major public health, economic, and social justice issue that demands action [8].

The Journal of Headache and Pain has always been at the forefront of inclusive advancing scientific knowledge and clinical practice in headache medicine. Our vision is to serve as the premier global platform for high-impact research that shapes policy and improves patient lives. With this dedicated Collection, we aim to extend our role beyond publishing cutting-edge science; we seek to actively shape the discourse, foster collaborations between clinicians, economists, and policymakers, and provide the evidence base needed to drive practical meaningful change.

The SDG advocacy framework

Here is why the SDGs are the essential bedrock for this advocacy.

The most direct link is, of course, with SDG 3, which aims to ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages. This goal explicitly includes Target 3.4: to reduce by one-third premature mortality from non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and promote mental health and well-being.

Advocates can powerfully argue that headache disorders are the most prevalent NCDs in the world. They are a leading cause of years lived with disability (YLDs), particularly for migraine in young and middle-aged adults. The well-being aspect of this target is critically compromised by the constant fear of the next attack, the social isolation derived from preventing participation in social situations which is strictly connected to headaches’ unpredictability, and the high comorbidity with anxiety and depression. Using SDG 3, advocates can demand that headache disorders be taken as seriously as other NCDs like diabetes or cardiovascular disease, requiring dedicated research funding, trained specialists, and inclusion in universal health coverage packages.

One of the most compelling arguments for policymakers is the economic one, perfectly framed by SDG 8. This goal promotes sustained, inclusive and sustainable economic growth, full and productive employment, and decent work for all.

Headache disorders are a stealthy drain on the global economy. The majority of sufferers are of working age. The cost is not just in absenteeism (days off work), but far more significantly in presenteeism—namely reduced productivity while working despite a headache. This directly undermines the productive employment that SDG 8 seeks. By framing the issue within this goal, advocacy shifts from a plea for sympathy to a data-driven argument for workplace accommodations, flexible policies, and better treatment. Investing in headache care is not a cost; it is an investment in a more productive and resilient workforce, directly contributing to economic growth.

The SDGs are built on the principle of leaving no one behind. SDG 1 addresses poverty as a barrier to basic headache care, given the scarcity of healthcare facilities and the high cost of medications in resource-limited settings. SGD 10 focuses explicitly on reducing inequality within and among countries. This is a powerful lens for headache advocacy, as the burden of these disorders is not distributed equally.

Gender Inequality: Migraine, for instance, disproportionately affects women by a ratio of roughly 3:1. This is not just a biological fact; it has profound social and economic consequences, contributing to the gender pay gap and limiting women’s career progression. Women in low-income rural settings may endure headaches for years without seeking care due to social norms, cost barriers, and fear of stigma. At the same time, men might likewise be less aware of headaches, particularly in high-income countries, and face fear of stigma which might prevent care-seeking as well. Perhaps the most practical reason for using the SDGs is SDG 17: Partnerships for the Goals. No single patient group, medical society, research institute, or drug company can solve the global burden of headache alone.

This cross-bencher collaboration, central to the SDG ethos, amplifies the message and creates a chorus of voices far more powerful than one alone [10–12].

Advocating for headache disorders without the SDGs is like trying to navigate a ship without a compass: you know the direction you want to go, but you lack the tools to chart a convincing course. SDGs provide the evidence-based, universally accepted framework that transforms a neglected neurological condition into a clear obstacle to global development.

By strategically aligning our message with SDG 3 on health, SDG 8 on economic productivity, SDG 1 on poverty and 10 on inequality, and others, we move the conversation from the margins to the mainstream. We stop asking for help for a headache and start demanding action on a critical barrier to a sustainable, equitable, and prosperous future for all. This is not just good advocacy; it is the only way to make the invisible burden of headache disorders finally, and undeniably, visible [13, 14].

The way forward

The first steps have already been completed through Collections launched in The Journal of Headache and Pain. These continental initiatives across Africa, China and India have begun to shape a new direction for headache advocacy and research. The first SDGs book dedicated to migraine and headache disorders has helped frame the problem within the broader global health agenda [8]. The Journal of Headache and Pain global webinar series on the SDGs has created a shared platform for discussion, learning, and collaboration [15]. Together, these initiatives begin to build the foundations of a coordinated global effort.

The opportunity ahead is even greater. More than five billion people live in the Global South. which carries a large share of the global burden of headache disorders. By aligning headache advocacy with the SDGs, stakeholders across these regions can accelerate progress in awareness, access to care, workforce development, and research. Partnerships between clinicians, patient organisations, policymakers, and the pharmaceutical industry can help expand translational and clinical research while improving access to effective treatments. In many LMICs, headache care remains concentrated in urban tertiary centers, leaving rural and peri-urban populations entirely without specialist access — a gap that digital health, telemedicine, and trained community workers could begin to bridge.

Importantly, the headache community does not need to start from scratch. Strong advocacy platforms already exist and can be mobilised at scale. The WHO has provided to all stakeholders around the world the goals and targets for innovation also in the headache field with its WHO Global Action Plan on epilepsy and other neurological disorders, approved in 2022 by the World health Assembly. Several international and national initiatives pave the way for increased brain health awareness and thus more attention for the most burdensome neurological and mental disorders. The EAN Brain Health Mission together with several Brain Health international initiatives of the WFN, WFNR, AAN, EAN, EHF, AFAN, ARCH, EBC as well as many national neurological societies, provide the right framework for advocating for headache disorders’ reduction worldwide.

Similarly, World Brain Day, led by the WFN each year on 22 July since 2014, has grown into a major global campaign for brain health. World Migraine Day on 18 June, championed since 2018 by the Migraine Special Interest Group of the WFNR together with the Migraine Foundation and many partners, has similarly created a powerful global voice for people living with migraine and other headache disorders. These platforms provide ready-made opportunities to connect clinicians, researchers, patient groups, policymakers, and the public across continents [14, 16].

Over the past decade, campaigns such as World Brain Day and World Migraine Day have reached more than a billion people worldwide through educational materials, media engagement, and community activities [17]. These initiatives have helped bring brain health into public conversation and have strengthened awareness that conditions such as migraine and other headache disorders are central to the global burden of neurological diseases. This momentum helped pave the way for the WHO to articulate a clear definition of brain health, i.e. “the state of brain functioning across cognitive, sensory, social-emotional, behavioral, and motor domains that allows a person to realise their full potential over the life course” [18]. This definition moves the conversation beyond individual diseases and places neurological conditions, including headache disorders, within a broader framework of well-being, function, and participation in society. In doing so, it reinforces why improving access to care, prevention, and advocacy for headache disorders is essential to advancing global brain health. What the scientific society now has to do is to translate such a wide definition into a set of clear and, valid and reproducible endpoints which will enable measuring brain health of individuals and groups [19].

By bringing these initiatives together under the framework of the SDGs the global headache community can move from fragmented efforts to coordinated action. This approach can strengthen awareness, improve access to care, stimulate research, and place migraine and headache disorders firmly within the broader movement for global brain health [20].

This is the practical vision for untying the Gordian knot of headache disorders: a coordinated, inclusive effort that brings together science, policy, advocacy, and community action. Pursued collaboratively across regions and disciplines, and supported by existing global platforms, this pathway can help ensure that headache disorders finally receive the attention they deserve within the wider brain health agenda. The Journal of Headache and Pain future role will be to champion a more inclusive, holistic and integrated approach to headache, ensuring it is recognized as a critical component of global sustainable development.

Only then can we truly say that the inclusion of headache within the global brain health movement has moved from aspiration to reality.
