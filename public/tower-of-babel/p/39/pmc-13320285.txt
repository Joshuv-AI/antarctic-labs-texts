Hardware in context

A Glovebox (also known as a Glove Box or Dry Box) is a sealed container used to manipulate substances in a controlled environment. Gloveboxes play a vital role in protecting products from human or environmental contamination, as well as protecting individuals and the environment from workplace hazards.

Designed to provide a controlled, enclosed work environment that is separated from workers by a barrier, they ensure the containment of sensitive and critical materials.

Gloveboxes are widely used across industries, including pharmaceutical and electronics manufacturing, food processing, nuclear applications, and more. For instance, in pharmaceutical companies, it is common to find 30–600 glove ports per site.

A Glovebox typically features a transparent front panel for visibility, two or more built-in gloves for manipulating objects inside without breaking the seal, and an airlock or antechamber for transferring items in and out without contaminating the internal atmosphere.

Often, a Glovebox embeds an atmosphere control system, such as an inert gas purge (e.g., nitrogen or argon) to prevent reactions with air or moisture, a vacuum system for removing air before introducing inert gas, and humidity and oxygen sensors to monitor internal conditions.

Due to the properties of the materials to be processed, Gloveboxes can be classified into three main categories based on the specific hazards present or the required level of cleanliness.

Restricted access barrier systems (RABS) and isolators (Fig. 1a)

They are designed as physical barrier methods for product protection and containment, used during manufacturing operations to separate operators from the process. They can be equipped with multiple ports for when inside access is required.Fig. 1Restricted Access Barrier System – RABS − or Isolator (a), Isolation Glovebox (b) and Glovebox (c).

Restricted Access Barrier System – RABS − or Isolator (a), Isolation Glovebox (b) and Glovebox (c).

The main differences between an isolator and a RABS are in the air handling, cleaning & bio-decontamination procedures, and the ISO cleanroom class in which they operate [1].

Isolation gloveboxes (Fig. 1b)

Designed to protect product (sterility) or personnel (chemical hazard), these gloveboxes have at least two ports and are for operators to do manual work inside the box, having features such as product transfer areas, controlled air filtration, positive or negative pressure airflow, and are often used for compounding chemo drugs or handling hazardous microbiological agents [2].

Glovebox (Fig. 1c)

Separates the operator from the object or item inside the glovebox. These units have only two ports and require operators to perform tasks manually inside the box. There are minimal sterility, particulate, airflow, and hazard control measures. Gloveboxes are likely to be found in non-critical areas, academic settings, laboratories, etc., with infrequent glove changes required [2].

Gloveboxes can be classified according to other criteria too, i.e., based on their application and atmosphere type.

As for the atmosphere type, four possibilities arise:

Inert Atmosphere Glovebox: Filled with inert gases like nitrogen or argon; used for handling air-sensitive materials.

Vacuum Glovebox: Maintains a vacuum to eliminate all gases; often used in physics or materials science.

Positive-Pressure Glovebox: Maintains internal pressure above atmospheric to prevent contamination (common in chemistry).

Negative Pressure Glovebox: Keeps internal pressure lower than outside to contain hazardous substances (used in biology or pharmaceuticals).

As for the application, it is possible to classify them into:

Research Glovebox: Used in laboratories for chemical synthesis, materials science, or electronics.

Pharmaceutical Glovebox: Designed for sterile compounding or handling potent drugs.

Nuclear Glovebox: Built with shielding for handling radioactive materials.

Industrial Glovebox: Used in manufacturing processes requiring controlled environments.

After this introduction, it is possible to establish the requirements of this project and compare them with respect to existing solutions, which are all proprietary equipment.

Exploring the solutions proposed by various glovebox manufacturers, such as Jacomex [3], Glovebox-Systems [4], MBraun [5], Inert [6], Cleantech [7], Labconco [8], and Vigor [9], raises several considerations. First of all, a glovebox is usually composed of a welded stainless-steel frame, with glass panels sealed by gaskets and bolts, resulting in a heavy structure. On the other hand, to achieve portability, some solutions rely on a mobile, disposable isolation glovebox made of flexible PVC in a pyramid shape [10]. In general, gloveboxes are built in standard sizes and are not compatible with other laboratory equipment. Only Jacomex [3] and MBraun [5] offer the capability to design and manufacture complete solutions, i.e., larger, more complex gloveboxes and custom enclosures that are usually integrated into industrial production lines. But these solutions are heavy and expensive; furthermore, they are monolithic, meaning that once the glovebox is built, it cannot be modified.

Moreover, not all manufacturers integrate robots into their applications, which is a primary requirement for our project.

From a scientific point of view, gloveboxes are a strategic research topic because they are an enabling technology for the chemical, biological, biotechnology, pharmaceutical, and nuclear industries. For this reason, in America, a specific association was founded in 1986: American Glovebox Society (AGS) [11]. The purpose of this association is to promote safety and quality of glovebox systems, promote communication, and disseminate knowledge in the field of glovebox technology through symposia, publications, and other media. Moreover, it recommends standards and guidelines for glovebox technology to existing national and international associations and promotes their adoption to reduce costs, both fabrication and operational.

In the literature, this research topic is addressed from different perspectives; for example, in [12], a telemanipulation system inside a glovebox is analyzed, and its task performance is assessed. The application is in the nuclear industry, where requirements are much stronger than ours. Another work in the nuclear industry is described in [13], where the robot is in a glove, and the end-effector is inside the glovebox. It presents the challenges of robotic and AI solutions for nuclear gloveboxes and introduces a step forward for bringing cutting-edge technology to gloveboxes. A similar work is presented in [14]. All these works addressed the application of existing robot and telemanipulator systems to specific glovebox designs to protect the operator from dangerous radiation, improve operational posture, and enhance dexterity.

Other scientific works on gloveboxes discuss very specific topics, as in [15], where a LIBS (Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy) instrument has been modified to be efficiently inserted into a glovebox. In [16], the issue of surface contamination in gloveboxes is discussed, and a novel glovebox design is presented.

However, for the objectives addressed in this paper, particularly regarding its modularity and flexibility, the most similar one is presented in [17]. It aimed to create an affordable, ready-to-use, and airtight workstation for research on nanomaterials applied to carbon composites. Airborne nanomaterials are a health risk if inhaled, so the design requires a hermetic seal to protect the user. Key design features included acrylic panels, a transfer chamber, glove mounts, and a main access door, which were all sealed to ensure a controlled environment. This solution has been optimized to be cheaper than comparable alternatives.

Unfortunately, none of the glovebox designs in the literature reviews can be classified as open hardware.

Only in [18] is the description of a portable, open-source Glovebox designed for scientific applications in the chemical and materials science fields. The proposed design is particularly notable for its cost-effectiveness. It demonstrates that, by using off-the-shelf components and additive manufacturing techniques, the cost of the validated devices is reduced by 95% compared to a proprietary system.

However, limiting the discussion to a proper laboratory setup, gloveboxes (and, in general, RABS) are usually monolithic structures designed for a specific purpose and not adaptable for interfacing with existing equipment. No glovebox has been designed to be modular or scalable for the lab environment. Moreover, gloveboxes intended for research laboratories are often made of steel and glass, and therefore very heavy.

Below is a summary of the Glovebox requirements.

Glovebox requirements

The setup to be isolated is composed of a FAMI [21] workbench on which a Universal Robot UR10e [22] is installed, see Fig. 2. For this application, a collaborative robot is preferred, because some jobs can be done in a shared workspace between the robot inside the glovebox and the human operator.

The workbench is equipped with grooved profiles and clamps to bracket the workpiece. The working plane is a rectangle of 2000 × 750 mm.

The Glovebox must fully enclose the workbench. For this particular application, the Glovebox must follow both the workbench design and the manipulator requirements. In this case, the enclosure length must be 2000 mm. Dimensioning the rest of the cabinet involves considering the robot's reachability in the working area. For the width, an offset of 350 mm has been considered on both the front and rear sides, so the total width is 750 + 350 + 350 = 1450 mm.

This sizing criterion ensures the robot has enough space to move within the Glovebox. For this reason, the Glovebox is larger than the workbench, and it is symmetrical with respect to the workbench midplane.

The Glovebox height is about 1400 ÷ 1500 mm, measured from the worktop.

A key feature is the possibility to assemble the Glovebox “on-site”, from aluminum alloy profiles, mounting them by special joints, without welding. This simplifies assembly operations and allows the design to be modified on demand.

The front and rear windows allow access to the robotic cell when it is in stand-by. These windows are equipped with two glove ports and butyl rubber gloves for operating inside the Glovebox.

The Glovebox is equipped with auxiliary systems, including suitable lighting and the ability to route power and data cables, as well as vacuum and pressure hoses, inside it.

Choosing the right aluminum profiles is fundamental. In principle, every brand of aluminum profiles for industrial automation could be used. However, upon deeper analysis, it is recommended to choose a brand with a catalogue specialized in components for building machine cabins. To this aim, the ITEM supplier [23] was chosen. ITEM has a specialized product line, the XMS/X series, which is dedicated to the realization of machine cabins. Moreover, the XMS/X series is fully compliant with the MB series, which is the ITEM series dedicated to industrial automation.

The XMS/X series features a clean design to prevent dust accumulation, especially at junctions; it offers a variety of joints and profiles for panel installation, with the option to interpose sealing gaskets. Fig. 5 illustrates the sections used to construct the Glovebox: all geometrical dimensions are multiples of a base module of 40 mm.Fig. 5Profiles section from ITEM X 8 series (a ÷ d) and automatic fastening (e, j).

Profiles section from ITEM X 8 series (a ÷ d) and automatic fastening (e, j).

In Fig. 5, the profile (a) – 120 × 80 – is used for the upper beams, the profile (b) – 80 × 80 – is used for the vertical uprights and the middle beams, and the profile (c) – 80 x 40 is used for the bottom beams and the dynamics elements. In this section, all the relevant dimensions are quoted. Finally, the profile (d) – 40 × 40 – realizes the horizontal and vertical separators. It is also used in the walls, the ceiling, and the worktop.

The connections are realized using the Automatic Fastening Set 8 (Fig. 5e–f); this choice enables joining two profiles without machining. Fig. 5g illustrates all the sizes offered by the Automatic Fastening Set. Automatic Fasteners should generally be used in pairs: to speed up the installation process, sets that comprise two automatic fasteners and one T-Slot Nut with two threads are also available for the sizes 40 and 80.

Fig. 5(h–j) resumes the installation procedure for the entire Glovebox frame.

At first, the Allen bolts are inserted into the grooves of the first profile, and the T-Slot Nut is inserted into the groove of the other profile, as shown in Fig. 5h.

Then (Fig. 5i), the external bodies of the Automatic Fasteners are screwed into the grooves of the first profile; they are self-tapping with a left-hand thread. Once the external bodies are screwed, the two profiles are joined by screwing Allen bolts into the T-Slot Nuts on the other profile (see Fig. 5j). This provides a reliable, easily removable, and invisible connection from the outside. Moreover, it is the only recommended method for connecting profiles leaving the possibility of installing a panel between them. For example, if you use brackets, you fill the angle with the bracket itself, excluding the possibility of installing panels on the structure.

When reading the Built Instruction section, please refer to the above notes whenever a profile connection is needed; no other explanation is necessary.

The design of the Glovebox includes, in the ceiling, the implementation of a Cartesian gantry in the form of two passive X-Y axes. This gantry, coupled with a spring compensator for the Z axis, allows for a partial “unloading” of the tool weight from the robot. In other words, the tool weight is shared between the robot and the gantry, enabling the use of a tool weighing more than the robot's payload.

The symmetry of the Glovebox is the basis of this project, and it is obtained as follows. The left wall (1) is identical to the right wall (2). The ceiling is divided into two identical halves to reduce the size of the polycarbonate panel. The front part of the worktop (4a) is identical to the rear part (4b) of the same element, and the front wall (5a) is identical to the rear wall (5b): both embed a window (6–7) of the same size.

The X-Y gantry (8) is an optional sub-assembly; the electrical ducts (9a – 9b) are symmetrical, and the Robot Controller (13) and the Electro-Spindle Cabined (14) depend on the specific application.

The Glovebox is equipped with a lighting system (10) and a suction system (12), as described below.

Fig. 7 illustrates the Vacuum System. The design of the Vacuum System is customized for this specific application. The same Glovebox can be equipped with a different Vacuum System if required.Fig. 7Diagram of the Glovebox Suction System.

Diagram of the Glovebox Suction System.

It is realized by connecting a special Vacuum Cleaner (certified to pick up hazardous dust in class H) to a general manifold. All the pipelines are made of PVC-U pipes. These pipes are joined by gluing; they are easy to assemble and cheaper than other solutions. Flexible hoses are used inside the Glovebox to connect the suction cup installed on the robot end-effector and a brush for cleaning the Glovebox. Particularly important is the routing of the hoses inside the Glovebox, which must be airtight. It is realized by N° 4 entry frames equipped with grommets. These entry frames are also used for electrical and data cables.

The vacuum can be addressed where necessary by turning on/off the shut-off valves installed near the entry frames, outside the Glovebox.

For this type of application, it is recommended to convey the exhaust air (cleaned by the three-stage filtration) outside the laboratory via a dedicated pipe.

Fig. 8 is the electrical diagram of the lighting system. Also, this system can be customized for specific applications. In this project, special lamps certified for installation in machine tools are used: LED lamps powered by low voltage (24 V) with a high degree of isolation. Two sizes are chosen: 6 W and 24 W. The idea is to use the 24 W lamps for top lighting and 6 W lamps for bottom lighting, but any other idea can be considered. A special power supply allows adjustment of light intensity via a potentiometer; furthermore, the circuit comprises two channels: one for the top lighting and the other for the bottom lighting.Fig. 8Diagram of the Glovebox Lighting System.

Diagram of the Glovebox Lighting System.

As open-source hardware, the Glovebox design can be easily modified through simple edits to the 3D models provided in the supplementary material of this paper (see dataset on Mendeley Data - http://doi.org/10.17632/mr7tvp4d5t.1).

Other changes are possible in its specifications: first, the external dimensions can be easily customized to accommodate a different experimental setup (0 in Table 1). External services (vacuum, ventilation, electrical supply) can be added or removed as needed. Finally, the number of glove ports can be adapted to specific needs.

In summary, this Modular Glovebox for Hazardous Material Machining provides the following advantages:•It can be used in a Laboratory to separate a “polluted zone” from a clean one. In this way, a hazardous task can be carried out in a Laboratory where there are sensitive instruments;•It can be easily adapted, in a design phase, to enclose an arbitrary setup, which in itself cannot be modified;•It can be assembled “on-site”, with simple tools. The Glovebox can be easily transported, before assembly, by a standard lift, also in a Laboratory located on high floors in a building;•Being Open-Source Hardware, the requirements in terms of internal pressure, ventilation, power supply for tools inside the Glovebox are fully customizable.

The “Mechanical structure” block introduces the components used to build the Glovebox frame. The whole frame is built using structural profiles for automation, supplied by ITEM [23]. It is divided into sub-assemblies; each sub-assembly is identified by the “Designator” in Fig. 6. A better description of these sub-assemblies can be found in the “Build Instructions” section.

The block “Clamping System” refers to the clamps used to fasten the workpiece to the worktop. Without it, the automatic machining of the workpiece wouldn't be possible. The clamping system is installed in the special grooves on the worktop; these grooves have a center distance of 40 mm, so this constraint must be satisfied. The clamps supplied by Speedy Block [25] are a good choice for this application.

The “Electrical Material” block includes everything you need for the electrical system, particularly for powering the lighting system. This material has been purchased from RS Components [26], but any electrical materials supplier can be used.

The Glovebox is equipped with a system comprising three Cartesian axes to support the robot's end effector. The first two axes (X-Y) are included in the Mechanical Structure; a tool balancer realizes the third axis (Z). This balancer and its accessories are listed in the “Robot Accessories for Lifting System” block. They are supplied by CDU [27], which is a dealer of industrial products.

Dust removal and vacuuming are ensured by a special vacuum cleaner supplied by NILFISK [28]. This vacuum cleaner and its accessories are certified for asbestos dust vacuuming (H-Class) and are included in the block “Hazardous Dust Vacuum Cleaner”.

Gloves are a fundamental element for a Glovebox; for this specific application, antistatic butyl rubber gloves have been preferred because they don't attract dust and are gas-tight. They are manufactured by Piercan [29], which is a world leader in Glovebox gloves; in Italy, they are supplied by ATAG [30]. As for the Glove Ports and the window panels, they are manufactured by ATAG [30] based on custom drawings.

An important issue to address is the airtight routing of hoses and cables inside the Glovebox. The “Cable Glands” block resolves this issue by providing a system based on entry frames and grommets. This very flexible system is supplied by ICOTEK [31], which is a leader in this field.

Finally, a glued PVC-U piping system has been chosen for building the vacuum piping. Among the available solutions, the IDOR solution [32] was adopted.

For a detailed list of the items included in each block, please refer to the Excel file BoM-Rev2.xlsx.

Build instructions

The starting point for the build instructions is Fig. 6. In this figure, the Glovebox is exploded into its main sub-assemblies. The numbers in the balloons identify the sub-assemblies throughout the procedure; they are referenced in round brackets. For example, (1) refers to the main sub-assembly in Fig. 6; (1.1) is the first element to be identified in the actual operation. If needed, a third level of indentation (1.1.1) identifies an item at the lowest level of the assembly hierarchy. It is also important to note that, in some cases, the assembly tasks involve more than one sub-assembly; for this reason, the build instructions refer to multiple sub-assemblies simultaneously.

The order in which the assembly tasks are listed is important. In fact, the assembly procedure has been tested during the real mounting of the Glovebox. Following this exact order ensures that each task does not conflict with the previous or the next one.

The following points illustrate the assembly procedure.

Apply flat gaskets to the workbench worktop

To ensure a good airtight seal to the whole Glovebox, some precautions must be taken. As a preliminary step, some gaskets must be applied: they ensure impermeability at the joints between the aluminum structure and the worktop. Fig. 9 represents the application of a flat rubber gasket to a side of the worktop. The gasket has a rectangular section measuring 9 × 4 mm and is self-adhesive; it must be applied along all sides of the worktop where it connects to the aluminum structure. For better isolation, the gasket is applied twice as illustrated: one strip up and the other down.Fig. 9Double air gasket applied to the worktop.

Double air gasket applied to the worktop.

After this operation the workbench is ready to be connected to the Glovebox.

Assembly of the left wall

Fig. 10 represents the mounting of the Left Wall (1). An exploded view is provided in Fig. 10a. The first assembly operation consists of screwing the base plates at the two extremities of each vertical upright 80x80 (1.1). Then, one of the two base plates is completed by installing the Knuckle Foot with the Rubber Insert. This operation must be repeated twice, for the two vertical uprights.Fig. 10Assembly the Left Wall: Exploded view (a), Final assembly (b) and detailed view of the connection to the worktop (c).

Assembly the Left Wall: Exploded view (a), Final assembly (b) and detailed view of the connection to the worktop (c).

The two uprights (1.1) are linked by the crossbar 120x80 (1.2), 80x80 (1.3), and 80x40 (1.4). All connections between the profiles used to build the Glovebox are made using Automatic Fastening Set 8 (see Fig. 5e–f in the “Hardware Description” section).

A vertical 40 × 40 crossbar (1.5) is introduced to split the window into two smaller ones. Two smaller windows can better support the stress due to atmospheric pressure during Glovebox operation.

At this point, the Left Wall can be connected to the Workbench using two bolted brackets (see Fig. 10c). It is important to install the Left Wall in the middle of the Workbench; otherwise, the WorkPlane (4a and 4b) doesn't match the Side Wall (1). As for the height, it can be regulated by the Knuckle Foot at the base of each upright (1.1).

The two Side Windows (1.7 and 1.8) must be assembled later. It is preferable to build the entire mechanical frame before installing the windows.

Finally, the 40 × 40 crossbar (1.6) reinforces the entire structure and will later be used to install the lighting system.

Assembly of the right wall

The Right Wall follows the same rules as the Left Wall. It is assembled by the same procedure. Please refer to the previous section for additional information.

At this point in the building procedure the two side walls have been connected to the workbench. Now they must be connected to each other to realize the front and rear walls.

Assembly of the front and rear crossbar to the worktop

The Front and Rear Crossbar (4a.2 and 4b.2 in Fig. 12) must be connected to the worktop by screws. They belong to the sub-assemblies (4a) and (4b) in Fig. 6.

With a section of 40 × 40 mm they fit exactly the thickness of the worktop. To do this operation, it is necessary to drill the bars. The connection is realized with N° 6 wood screws in stainless steel, with a 6 mm thread and a length of 70 mm. The screws are countersunk and recessed into the bars.

Ceiling

The ceiling is the first paneling to be installed on the Glovebox. The general idea is to close the internal space in a sequence that allows the following assembly phases to proceed without obstruction. For this reason, the paneling is not mounted in a single step. The ceiling (Fig. 15) is composed of two 6-mm-thick polycarbonate panels (3.5). They are joined by a central crossbar (3.3) with a section of 40 × 404mm.Fig. 15Ceiling: section view (a) and detail of panel clamping (b).

Ceiling: section view (a) and detail of panel clamping (b).

The method to clamp the polycarbonate panels on the frame is illustrated in the following rows and is also used for the front and rear wall (5a and 5b in Fig. 6) and for the opening windows (6 and 7 in Fig. 6). So this description must be considered for those elements too.

Once the frame is completed, the Aluminum Panel Clamping Strip 8 (3.4) is inserted into the frame grooves along the entire surface to be closed.

Now the polycarbonate panel (3.5) can be inserted and supported by it (3.4). All the elements involved in this process (3.4), (3.5), and (3.6) must be cut according to precise rules before being installed; to this aim, see the Item Catalogue [23]. The last operation inserts the Rubber Panel Clamping Strip 8 (3.6) into the frame groove to clamp the panels. This operation is performed along the entire perimeter of the surface to be closed. It is important to note that the Rubber Panel Clamping Strip 8 is produced in different versions according to the thickness of the panel to be clamped. In this case the size 6–8 mm must be adopted.

Glove Ports

At this point, the Glovebox is complete, with all panels and technical systems installed. The next step is installing the Glove Ports. Glove Ports serve as the interface between human operators and the experimental setup inside the Glovebox. For this reason, their design needs some consideration, and there are possible alternatives.

Their number greatly influences accessibility: the number employed is a trade-off between the cost and the comfort of the adopted solution. More Glove Ports offer better accessibility, but impact costs. Some solutions offer four or more Glove Ports, installed one near the other at a convenient distance, since the gloves are ambidextrous. In this way, each Glove Port can be used with either the right or left hand, allowing the operator to access a larger workspace within the experimental setup.

The second feature is the Glove Port diameter. The standard diameters are: 136 mm (5″), 156 mm (6″), 186 mm (7″), 220 mm (8″), 250 mm (10″) and 300 mm (12″). As previously mentioned, a larger diameter is more comfortable for the operator but also more expensive. As an alternative, oval Glove Ports can be considered for special applications, where a wider range of movement along the vertical direction is required.

Gloves are a fundamental item for a Glovebox. The Glovebox embeds ports with a 220 mm diameter, but the final choice is application-driven. The long gloves are the simpler, cheaper choice: they are installed on the glove ports with an additional O-Ring and don't require any further accessories. This alternative is good for a Glovebox with a low duty cycle, as in our case.

Wearing the gloves

Before each use, it is the user's responsibility to check the gloves' quality and integrity. The gloves should be discarded if they have tears, holes, or changes in surface appearance or color that could indicate alteration by chemical products.

The gloves must be worn with clean, dry hands and nails cut short. Another recommendation is to avoid wearing jewelry.

To prevent potential allergy risks and ensure hand hygiene, it is recommended to wear disposable pre-gloves and to cover the forearms (e.g., with long sleeves and cuffs).

Do not expose gloves to sharp or pointed objects, such as wire brushes, sandpaper, screwdrivers, or similar tools. If contaminated with chemicals, the gloves are for single use only.

Safety Issues

The European Machinery Directive 2006/42/CE is transposed into Italian law by the Consolidated Law on Safety at Work (Legislative Decree 81/2008). According to this regulation, a protective device cannot introduce additional risks for the workers. Specifically, Article 76 of Legislative Decree 81/08 establishes that protective devices must be adequate to the risks they are intended to prevent, without inherently posing a greater risk. Furthermore, it must be appropriate to the workplace conditions and the worker's ergonomic and health needs.

The Glovebox is a Collective Protective Equipment (CPE) and must follow these rules. In other countries, specific regulations must be applied if they are in place.

The only precautions to be universally adopted concern the presence of electrical apparatus near and inside the Glovebox; for this reason, the Glovebox frame must be grounded.

Conclusions and future work

In conclusion, the paper introduces a modular, scalable Glovebox that can be tailored to the user's needs. The proposed solution is designed to adapt to existing laboratory setups, enabling further isolation of the machining within a self-contained space and dramatically increasing operational safety.

Although the design should adapt to any context, for further demonstrating its versatility, a scarfing process adaptable to CFRP material [20] has been considered for testing, as it generates non-negligible hazardous and conductive dust.

Design highlights include:•It is made of aluminum alloy and polycarbonate instead of steel and glass like traditional Gloveboxes, increasing portability;•It can be assembled “on-site,” which means transportability in every kind of laboratory, also those with narrow entries or located on high floors in a building;•If the laboratory setup to be isolated changes, it can be easily reconfigured to satisfy the changed needs, since no welding is required;•It is more cost-effective with respect to a traditional Glovebox;•Because of its design, it can be interfaced with a robotized cell, enabling human-robot cooperation wherever required.

Regarding future work, when the system operates properly and as designed (gasket integrity is preserved, operations are conducted under negative pressure, etc.), it enables working on hazardous-material operations as well. However, additional testing with PM sensors would be invaluable to further assess this claim. The test could be organized as follows:1.One PM sensor is placed inside the Glovebox, and the other outside.
