Fetoscopic Vesicostomy for Lower Urinary Tract Obstruction: A Proposal for a New Surgical Technique
Authors: Marcio Lopes Miranda, Kleber Cursino de Andrade, Marcos Caetano, Patricia Traballi Carvalho Pegolo, Renato Ximenes, Rodrigo Ruano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Summary What's already known about this topic?◦Lower urinary tract obstruction (LUTO) is associated with elevated perinatal mortality and morbidity.◦Fetal interventions have been proposed for severe forms of LUTO, including vesicoamniotic shunt placement and fetal cystoscopy.◦Experimental studies suggest that fetal vesicostomy could be a valid therapeutic option especially for fetuses with LUTO associated with Prune Belly.◦Open fetal vesicostomy through maternal hysterotomy has been proposed, but the risk of maternal and obstetrical outcomes was not encouraging.What does this study add?◦We propose a new intervention consisting of fetal vesicostomy through a maternal laparotomy‐assisted fetoscopic procedure.◦Fetoscopic vesicostomy through maternal laparotomy is feasible and seems safe for fetuses with severe LUTO associated with Prune Belly.◦Fetoscopic vesicostomy may be a therapeutic alternative for fetuses with severe LUTO associated with the Prune Belly after unsuccessful vesicoamniotic shunt placements and fetal cystoscopy. Keywords: bladder outlet obstruction, fetal surgery, fetoscopy, hydronephrosis, lower urinary tract obstruction, prenatal diagnosis, prune belly, renal anomalies, ultrasound, vesicostomy

Introduction

Congenital lower urinary tract obstruction (LUTO) is a defect caused by posterior urethral valves as well as urethral atresia or stenosis. It affects 2.2 per 10,000 live births, with 90% of cases affecting males [1]. Early diagnosis (before 24 weeks) of megacystis is associated with severe oligohydramnios leading to pulmonary hypoplasia and a high mortality rate (> 90%) [2].

Prenatal interventions such as fetal vesicoamniotic shunt placement and fetal cystoscopy have been proposed for severe forms of LUTO [2, 3]. However, fetal vesicoamniotic shunts are associated with high displacement rates and frequent need for repeated procedures, which increase obstetrical risks [2, 3], and fetal cystoscopic ablation of posterior urethral valves increases the risk of retro‐urethral fistula [4].

Postnatally, vesicostomy is the most definitive way to decompress the urinary tract obstructions, decreasing the risks of renal damage [5]. Fetal bladder vesicostomy with marsupialization via maternal hysterotomy was performed many years ago with the goal toallow for both prenatal and postnatal decompressions of the bladder; however, the authors showed considerably obstetrical outcomes suggesting that possiblly less invasive procedure through fetoscope could reduce those risks [6].

We report a successful fetoscopic vesicostomy through uterine exposure (maternal laparotomy) for severe LUTO (urethral atresia) after multiple vesicoamniotic shunt dislodgements. To the best of our knowledge, no similar procedure in humans has been reported in the literature yet.

Case Report

A G1P0 pregnant woman was referred to our fetal center unit because of her male fetus was diagnosed with severe megacystis at 13 weeks' gestation. A follow‐up ultrasound at 18 weeks' gestation revealed an extremely enlarged bladder with bilateral hydronephrosis, normal kidney echogenicity and severe oligohydramnios. Ultrasound‐guided amniocentesis was performed (further revealing normal male fetal FISH and karyotype, chromosomal microarray was not performed) and fetal vesicocentesis for fetal urinary biochemistry (revealing favorable fetal urinary biochemistry). Forty‐eight hours after the vesicocentesis, there was adequate fetal bladder refilling. These findings were suggestive of stage II LUTO [3].

Following local maternal‐fetal ethical board approval and patient signing the informed consent, the mother underwent fetoscopic cystoscopy at 18 weeks' gestation, leading to the diagnosis of urethral atresia. At this time, a Harrison fetal bladder stent was placed during the procedure for vesicoamniotic shunt. The catheter worked effectively, but 1 week later it was dislodged, resulting in recurrent megacystis and severe oligohydramnios. Two more vesicoamniotic shunt placements were performed at 19 weeks and 20 weeks' gestation, but again the shunts dislodged a week later after the placements. Based on this situation, a fetoscopic‐assisted vesicostomy was considered and offered to the patient, because of the diagnosis of fetal urethral atresia by fetal cystoscopy and multiple consecutive dislodgements (failures) of the Harrison vesicoamniotic shunts, in addition to the possible diagnosis of a severe Prune Belly feature on ultrasonography. After detailed multidisciplinary prenatal counseling, despite the patient being counseled about possible associated abnormalities, the patient chose to proceed with this intervention and refused medical termination of the pregnancy. The hospital maternal‐fetal ethical boards approved the procedure. The patient signed the surgical informed consent.

Surgical Procedure Description

The procedure was performed in a tertiary hospital with expertise in fetal surgeries and a level III Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU). The laparotomy‐assisted fetoscopic approach and the maternal‐fetal anesthesia protocol followed the same protocol for the utero‐exposed fetoscopic myelomeningocele repair [7]. A Pfannenstiel laparotomy was performed, followed by uterine exposure, and an ultrasound mapping of both placental position and fetal bladder location in relation to the fetal umbilicus. Under ultrasound‐guidance, amnioinfusion was performed with a total of 500 mL of warm and sterile of Lactate Ringer's solution. Three 3‐mm trocars with the Seldinger technique were introduced, partial amniotic fluid was drained, and warm carbon dioxide (8 mmHg) was insufflated. The fetus was positioned with the back facing posteriorly and the lower limbs apart from the hypogastrium. The fetal skin incision was guided by the fetoscopc view using anatomical landmarks of the abdominal wall, umbilical cord insertion and genitalia. Umbilical cord insertion and male genitalia were identified, and a longitudinal incision was made in the fetal belly between these structures (Figure 1A). Due to the fragile abdominal wall (Prune Belly), two transuterine polypropylene sutures were placed to expose the bladder wall, which was then incised. After urine drainage, four absorbable sutures were placed at the cardinal points between the skin and the bladder, following the Bloksom's technique [8]. The carbon dioxide was evacuated, the cavity was filled with warmed Ringer's solution (no antibiotics were added to the amnioinfusion), and the membranes and the trocar sites were closed together. The uterus was relocated into the maternal abdominal cavity, and the fetal well‐being was confirmed by ultrasound. The patient received prophylactic intravenous antibiotics with Cefazolin 2 g in one dose during the procedure and prophylactic tocolysis with Magnesium sulfate 4 g loading dose followed by 2 g/h and nifedipine 10 mg Q8 starting during the procedure and maintained for 24 h. The pregnant woman was discharged on the second postoperative day without complications.

Follow‐Up

Subsequent ultrasound examinations demonstrated an empty bladder with normal amniotic fluid volume and preserved renal corticomedullar appearance. Premature rupture of membranes occurred at 28 weeks' gestation and the patient was admitted to the hospital. At 32 weeks's gestation, the patient progressed to uterine contractions, and a cesarean section was performed. A 1820 g boy was born with mild respiratory distress and needed intubation for a few days then CPAP. Neonatal evaluation revealed Prune Belly features with urethral atresia, imperforate anus and congenital rectovesical fistula, and an effective patent vesicostomy (Figure 1B). On day one of life, the neonate underwent a rectovesical fistula ligation by laparotomy, creation of a terminal colostomy in the left flan, and relocation of the vesicostomy to the right flank. He showed adequate urine output, age‐appropriate creatinine levels. At 2 weeks of life, the baby was extubated but presented with perforated necrotizing enterocolitis and subsequently underwent a partial colectomy. The patient was discharged home from the hospital on day 44 of life on room air, tolerating oral feeding, with colostomy, patent vesicostomy and oxygen supplementation. Currently, at 9 months of age, the infant exhibits good pulmonary function, normal creatinine levels, mild microalbuminuria, and renal scintigraphy indicating normal bilateral tubular function.

Discussion

The present case report suggests that fetoscopic‐guided fetal vesicostomy is feasible particularly indicated in situations of severe LUTO (stage II) associated with previous failures of vesicoamniotic shunts, prenatal diagnosis of fetal urethral atresia by fetal cystoscopic and severe Prune Belly features. This fetal vesicostomy can be performed using a technique similar to that used for fetoscopic repair of myelomeningocele.

In 2001, Holmes et al. [6] reported their experience with fetal vesicostomy through maternal laparotomy and hysterotomy in a small number of cases. However, the outcomes in the studied cohort were suboptimal, particularly due to severe prematurity with high perinatal mortality (43%). In 2004, Ponsky et al. [9] successfully performed transuterine fetal vesicostomy in a sheep model. The procedure involved the removal of blurred amniotic fluid followed by infusion of warmed glycine into the uterine cavity to enhance visualization. A tree trocar was placed and an incision was made in the bladder wall, which was then secured using a continuous suture. Some animals developed incisional hernias, while others exhibited vesicostomy stenosis. Despite these complications, the authors demonstrated the feasibility of the technique and expressed the intention to extend the procedure to primate models and, eventually, to clinical humans [9]. In 2007, Polzin et al. [10] reported a successful case of human fetal vesicostomy performed via maternal hysterotomy.

Regarding fetal vesicostomy by fetoscopy (not by uterine hysterotomy), when reviewing the literature, there have been no documented clinical reports of fetal vesicostomy performed through uterine exposure and fetoscopy [10]. Therefore, to the best of our knowledge, the present patient is the first report of a successful fetoscopic‐performed fetal vesicostomy for severe LUTO.

The rationale of performing this procedure using a fetoscopic approach was based on the large team's experience with both fetoscopic repair of fetal myelomeningocele and postnatal vesicostomy in neonates with LUTO. Although, we believe, maternal laparotomy and uterine exteriorization are still required, the access of the amniotic cavity using 3 mm trocars and membrane fixation reduce the risk of uterine wall bleeding, membrane separation, and uterine dehiscence/rupture, in addition to preserve the possibility of vaginal delivery in the present and future pregnancies [8]. Consequently, the creation of an effective fetal vesicostomy via minimally invasive uterine access represents an innovative, elegant and less invasive procedure when compared to the open techniques through maternal hysterotomy as previously reported [7].

In the present case, we believe that the fetoscopic vesicostomy can be considered in fetuses with severe LUTO particularly in those with Prune Belly features after multiple failures of fetal vesicoamniotic shunts and in cases of suspicion of urethral atresia. The possible advantage of fetoscopic vesicostomy is to achieve effective fetal bladder drainage while maintaining a low‐pressure urinary system, thereby promoting the preservation of residual nephrons. Furthermore, there is the potential that the fetal vesicostomy may remain effective in the neonatal period, eliminating the need for a second postnatal intervention, since postnatal vesicostomy is frequently performed in neonates with intravesical obstruction.

Conclusion

Our case report suggests that the fetoscopic vesicostomy procedure represents a promising therapeutic option for severe LUTO in selected cases with preserved renal function. Despite its technical complexity and risks of premature rupture of membranes, the procedure is feasible when performed by experienced fetal surgeons. Further clinical cohort studies are necessary to confirm our findings.
