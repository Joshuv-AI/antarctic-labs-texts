A Retrospective Cohort Propensity-Matched Analysis of Colorectal Cancer Risk in Isolated Small Intestinal Crohn’s Disease
Authors: Saqr Alsakarneh, Omar Al Ta’ani, Sandra Quezada, Jean-Pierre Raufman, Aasma Shaukat, Hassan Ghoz
Published: 2025

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is associated with an elevated risk of colorectal cancer (CRC), driven by chronic inflammation and dysplasia.1 Among patients with CD, this risk is well-established in those with colonic involvement, where chronic inflammation of the large intestine is a key driver of carcinogenesis.2 In contrast, while small intestine CD is a recognized risk factor for small bowel adenocarcinoma, its association with CRC remains less clear. Previous research focused largely on colonic disease, leaving the potential role of small intestine CD in colon neoplasia underexplored.3,4 To address this gap in knowledge, we investigated the risk of CRC in patients with CD confined to the small intestine.

We used the TriNetX platform to access deidentified electronic health records of 116 million patients from 68 healthcare organizations across the United States. Adults with IBD were identified using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 9th and 10th Revisions, Clinical Modification codes plus RxNorm codes for any IBD-related medication. We identified patients aged ≥18 years with small intestinal CD using ICD code K50.0, excluding those with colonic involvement (ICD code K50.1), and matched them 1:1 with individuals without IBD using propensity score matching. This resulted in two groups: patients with small intestinal CD and no prior CRC diagnosis, and a reference group of individuals without IBD or a history of CRC. For inclusion in the study, patients were required to have a minimum of 5 years of follow-up. The primary outcome was the risk of the first diagnosis of CRC within 15 years from the index event (CD diagnosis). Secondary outcomes included comparing CRC risk in subjects with small intestine CD to those with UC or Crohn’s colitis. We also stratified CRC risk based on the use of biologicals. With censoring applied, Kaplan-Meier analysis with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was used to compare time-to-event rates at daily time intervals.

We identified 29,615 subjects with CD limited to the small intestine (mean age, 42.4 ± 19.5 years; 52.9% female; Table A1). Patients with isolated small intestine CD were at increased risk of CRC compared to the non-IBD cohort (adjusted HR [aHR] = 2.99; 95% CI: 2.02–4.44). Compared to the general population, both males (aHR = 3.00; 95% CI: 1.67–5.37) and females (aHR = 1.73; 95% CI: 1.06–2.82) with CD limited to the small intestine were at increased risk of CRC (Table 1).

Compared to small intestine CD, patients with colonic CD (aHR: 1.42; 95%: 1.02–1.97) or UC (aHR = 1.3; 95% CI: 1.04–1.6) were at higher risk of CRC. Additionally, patients with small intestine CD were at increased risk of noncancerous colorectal polyps (aHR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.14–1.39). Consistent findings were observed in women, men, and different age groups.

Patients taking biologicals had similar risk compared to those who were biological-naïve (aHR = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.43–2.22). All-cause mortality was higher in subjects with both small intestine CD and CRC compared to those with CRC without IBD (aHR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.01–3.61; P = .042).

In this cohort study, CD restricted to the small bowel was associated with a higher risk of CRC compared to the general population; patients on biologic therapy had a similar elevated risk as biologic-naïve patients. However, patients with colonic CD or UC had a comparatively higher risk of CRC compared to patients with small bowel CD, consistent with the established role of chronic colonic inflammation in carcinogenesis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest real-world study from the United States to demonstrate this risk, challenging the notion that CRC risk is exclusively associated with colonic involvement with CD.

The association between small bowel CD and CRC risk may be explained by chronic inflammation and its downstream effects. Persistent inflammation in the small intestine can disrupt tissue homeostasis, promoting oxidative stress, DNA damage, and the activation of tumor-promoting pathways such as nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells and tumor necrosis factor-α signaling. Additionally, microbiome dysbiosis in small bowel CD may amplify carcinogenic processes through altered microbial diversity and the production of harmful metabolites, including secondary bile acids and hydrogen sulfide, which exacerbate inflammation and induce genetic instability.5,6 We acknowledge that increased endoscopic and other forms of surveillance or disease staging in patients with IBD whether involving the small or large intestine may also lead to earlier CRC detection, raising the possibility of lead time bias in evaluating cancer risk.7

In our study, biologic therapies did not mitigate the elevated risk of CRC in small bowel CD patients, as both biologic-treated and biologic-naïve patients exhibited comparable CRC risks. While some studies have suggested that tumor necrosis factor inhibitors may decrease the risk of CRC in IBD patients,8 others have found no significant difference in risk.9,10 Our findings align with the latter studies, showing that while biologic therapies are known to be effective in controlling inflammation, they may not fully address the pathophysiology of CRC development in this patient population. This is particularly relevant for small bowel CD, where colonic inflammation—traditionally regarded as the primary driver of CRC risk in IBD—is not the main contributing factor. Instead, our findings suggest that other mechanisms, such as systemic inflammation, genetic predisposition, microbiome alterations, and environmental exposures, may play a more prominent role in CRC development in this subgroup.5,6 Current guidelines recommend CRC surveillance for patients with colonic IBD but not for those with small bowel CD, yet our findings suggest this subgroup may also warrant consideration.4

Our study's strengths include its large, population-based cohort derived from a national database, providing robust real-world evidence. However, the observational and retrospective design introduces potential biases, including reporting and selection bias, and variability in follow-up. A key limitation was the inability to evaluate cancer subtypes or disease severity, which limits detailed insights into small bowel CD-related malignancies. In addition, disease location was classified using ICD-9/10 coding within the TriNetX platform, which may be subject to misclassification and could introduce bias into our estimates. Furthermore, surveillance bias is possible, as patients with IBD are more likely to undergo repeated colonoscopies and follow-up procedures compared to non-IBD controls. These limitations provide grist for future research.

Our findings highlight the elevated risk of CRC in small bowel CD, regardless of colonic involvement, emphasizing the need for CRC surveillance strategies in this subgroup. Further research is needed to explore the mechanisms driving this association and assess the impact of biologics and small molecules on CRC risk in small bowel CD.
