Sialendoscopy in the management of obstructive sialadenitis: clinical experience and treatment outcomes
Authors: Majid Althobaiti, Muhammad Usman Amin, Ahmed Elbana, Nader S Alharbi, Lama AhmedAlsamiri, Mubarak Aldossari, Abdulelah Magid Althbety
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND: Obstructive sialadenitis is a common salivary gland disorder. This retrospective study evaluated the outcomes of sialendoscopy in 90 patients treated between July 2018 and December 2023.OBJECTIVE: To evaluate the success rate, complications, and predictors of treatment outcomes of sialendoscopy in patients with obstructive sialadenitis.DESIGN: Retrospective cohort studySETTING: Security Forces Hospital, Saudi ArabiaPATIENTS AND METHODS: A retrospective cohort study was conducted using patient medical records. Patients with obstructive sialadenitis were included.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Success of sialendoscopy, defined as complete endoscopic stone retrieval with confirmed ductal patency on post-retrieval irrigation. Four procedures in which no sialolith was identified intraoperatively were excluded from the stone-retrieval success denominator (n=94 stone-confirmed procedures).SAMPLE SIZE: Ninety patientsRESULTS: Submandibular glands accounted for 74% (67/90) of cases. Success rates for complete stone retrieval were 79% (19/24 parotid) and 79% (55/70 submandibular). Key predictors of success included stone size, location, and number: stones >10 mm (OR=0.17, 95% CI: 0.04–0.67; P=.01), intraglandular location (OR=0.13, 95% CI: 0.03–0.54; P=.005), and multiple stones (OR=0.18, 95% CI: 0.05–0.60; P=.006) significantly reduced success. Complications occurred in 11.2% (11/98) of cases, primarily ductal stenosis and postoperative swelling, with submandibular glands contributing most complications.CONCLUSION: In this one of the larger reported single-centre Saudi series, sialendoscopy demonstrated high success for obstructive sialadenitis, with stone size >10 mm, intraglandular location, and multiplicity as independent failure predictors. These findings support tailored approaches in Middle Eastern populations.LIMITATIONS: Retrospective single-center design, lack of long-term follow-up data, and absence of patient-reported outcome measures.Keywords: Sialendoscopy, obstructive sialadenitis, salivary stones, surgical outcomes

INTRODUCTION

Obstructive sialadenitis is the leading cause of major salivary gland dysfunction.1 Marsupialization of Wharton's duct papilla was a classic for distal (papilla-near) submandibular stones. For proximal (hilar/parenchyma-near) stones in Wharton's or Stensen's ducts, options were limited to gland excision, risking morbidity.2,3 Without endoscopic visualization and confirmation of complete extirpation of the stone, marsupialization without endoscopic duct patency confirmation, increases the risk of possible subsequent iatrogenic stenosis.4 Similarly, blind duct dilation with risk of unidentified duct perforation or false passage increases morbidity. Over the past two decades, there has been a notable shift from invasive surgical approaches to more conservative and minimally invasive treatments, significantly reducing the number of patients undergoing traditional sialadenectomy.4

Sialendoscopy, introduced in the early 1990s, revolutionized minimally invasive management of obstructive sialadenitis, enabling endoscopic visualization of salivary gland ducts for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes.5 The use of miniaturized instruments, such as forceps, baskets, balloons, graspers, laser fibers, and microdrills; along with the ability to administer steroids and antibiotics, makes salivary gland endoscopy an effective interventional approach for managing various benign salivary gland disorders.6 The aim of sialendoscopy is to visualize the lumen of salivary gland ducts while interventional sialendoscopy is mainly aimed to treat sialolithiasis and dilating the stenosed ducts to prevent stasis of saliva which is a common cause of stone formation.7 The safety and efficacy of sialendoscopy in adults are well established, as is its role in pediatric cases.8 A meta-analysis study from Saudi Arabia (2014) was conducted to review the techniques available on management of parotid and submandibular types of sialoliths and determine the safety and efficacy of sialendoscopy with conservative removal of stones.9 Results of the analysis showed that success rates of sialendoscopy combined with minor surgical removal ranges from 69 to 100%. This study retrospectively reviews outcomes in 90 patients at a Saudi tertiary center, one of the larger single-centre series from our region, identifying stone-specific predictors and gland differences to guide patient selection in resource-limited settings. We present our experience regarding the management, outcomes and complications of obstructive sialadenitis at our center.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design

Between July 2018 and December 2023, a retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Otorhinolaryngology, Head and Neck Surgery at Security Forces Hospital, Saudi Arabia, focusing on patients who underwent both diagnostic and interventional Sialendoscopy for obstructive sialadenitis. The diagnosis of sialadenitis was determined based on the patients' clinical signs & symptoms and imaging findings. Ethical approval and waiver of informed consent for this retrospective review of medical records were granted by the Institutional Review Board of Security Forces Hospital (Approval no. SFH-2024/0511; dated 11 May 2024).

Eligibility criteria

This was a retrospective cohort study of patients who underwent sialendoscopy (diagnostic and/or interventional) for obstructive sialadenitis at the Department of Otolaryngology, Head and Neck Surgery, Security Forces Hospital, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, between July 2018 and December 2023. All patients were identified through a systematic review of the hospital's electronic medical records and surgical procedure logs. Eligible participants were those with a clinical diagnosis of obstructive sialadenitis that did not respond adequately to initial conservative medical management. Indications for interventional sialendoscopy included confirmed ductal obstruction where endoscopic intervention was deemed appropriate by the treating otolaryngologist. All eligible cases during the study period were included.

Surgical procedures

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia as the primary anesthetic. A standardized protocol was followed, with minor variations based on stone size, location, mobility, and intraoperative findings. The ductal orifice was identified and serially dilated using salivary duct probes of increasing diameter. The ductal system was then explored with a semirigid sialendoscope under continuous intermittent irrigation with isotonic saline solution to maintain visualization and distend the duct. Supplementary local anesthesia (1% lidocaine injection at the papilla) was used as an adjunct in select cases to reduce tissue response during orifice dilation when resistance was encountered or in cases requiring prolonged manipulation; this was not the primary anesthetic modality. Debris and mucus plugs were irrigated and removed. Sialoliths were retrieved endoscopically using a Dormia basket when permitted by stone size and mobility. For larger, impacted, or immobile stones, a combined approach was employed: the stone was first localized endoscopically (via direct visualization or transillumination), followed by a targeted intraoral mucosal incision over the stone site in the submandibular duct to facilitate removal. This combined approach was used more frequently in submandibular cases than parotid. Perioperative corticosteroid irrigation was performed in nearly all cases to reduce inflammation. Oral antibiotics (cefuroxime) were administered for 5 days postoperatively in all cases per protocol.

Surgical outcomes and complication evaluation

Outcomes were analyzed at the gland/procedure level (n=98), as eight patients underwent bilateral procedures; this approach is consistent with contemporary literature on chronic obstructive sialadenitis.10 We categorized the sialendoscopic findings into sialolithiasis, stenosis, and other causes. The sialolithiasis was described by the gland involved, stone location, size and number of stones. The primary outcome was technical success, defined as complete endoscopic stone retrieval with confirmed ductal patency on post-retrieval saline irrigation. Procedures in which no sialolith was identified intraoperatively were included in the overall cohort (as they were performed for suspected obstruction with therapeutic intent) but excluded from the stone-retrieval success analyses. Follow-up was defined as the interval from the index sialendoscopy to the last documented clinic visit. Patients were routinely seen at approximately 2 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively, with additional visits as clinically indicated. Technical success was determined at the index procedure based on intraoperative findings (complete endoscopic stone retrieval with confirmed ductal patency on post-retrieval saline irrigation); the 3-month visit (or earliest available visit thereafter) was used for follow-up assessment of symptom resolution, recurrence, reintervention, and any delayed complications. Follow-up duration varied owing to the retrospective design. Complications were classified into early (≤30 days) and late (>30 days) events. Early complications included: (a) postoperative swelling/pain, defined as self-limiting glandular swelling or pain managed conservatively without reintervention; and (b) infection/abscess, defined as purulent discharge, fever, or imaging-confirmed abscess requiring antibiotics or incision and drainage. Late complications included iatrogenic ductal stenosis, defined as new-onset recurrent obstructive symptoms (meal-related pain or swelling) developing >30 days postoperatively in a duct not previously noted to be stenotic, confirmed by at least one of the following: ultrasonography demonstrating ductal dilation proximal to a narrowing, non-contrast CT, or repeat sialendoscopy demonstrating luminal narrowing at a site of prior instrumentation. This was distinguished from pre-existing stenosis, which was documented as an intraoperative finding during the index procedure and recorded separately under sialendoscopic findings not counted as a complication. Other potential complications monitored included hematoma, lingual nerve injury, and obstructing sialocele.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical, and procedural information were retrieved from medical records. The collected data encompassed gender, age, comorbidities, smoking status, affected salivary glands, initial clinical presentation and symptoms, diagnostic investigations, surgical indication, type of anesthesia, operation time, type of procedure, endoscopic findings, materials used, complications, adjunctive treatments, follow-up duration, use of antibiotics, and corticosteroids, and treatment outcomes.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.1 (R Core Team). Associations between stone characteristics (size, location, number) and outcomes were assessed using Fisher's exact tests for small sample sizes or Chi-square tests. Logistic regression models were constructed to identify independent predictors of technical success among stone-confirmed cases (n=94); four diagnostic-only procedures with no stone identified were excluded from the regression analysis. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with P<.05 considered significant.

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics

A total of 90 patients underwent sialendoscopy between July 2018 and December 2023. The cohort comprised 57 (63.3%) males and 33 (36.7%) females, with a mean age of 43.2 years (range: 13–72). Submandibular glands were more frequently involved (67/90, 74%) than parotid glands (23/90, 26%). Chronic diseases were present in 26% of cases, including Hypertension, Diabetes Mellitus, Sjögren's Syndrome, Asthma, Coronary Artery Disease, Dyslipidemia, Obstructive Sleep Apnea, and Hypothyroidism. The most common presentation was pain and gland swelling. The demographic clinical data are shown in Table 1. The median follow-up duration was 9.4 months (IQR: 4.2–18.6; range: 1–52 months).

Surgical outcomes

A total of 98 salivary gland procedures were performed, including 26 parotid glands and 72 submandibular glands. Of these, four procedures (2 parotid, 2 submandibular) yielded no sialolith despite preoperative suspicion of obstruction, instead revealing Sjögren's syndrome (n=2) and ductal stenosis (n=2); these were included in the overall cohort but excluded from the stone-retrieval success denominator, leaving 94 stone-confirmed procedures (parotid n=24; submandibular n=70) for the primary outcome analysis. Stone characteristics, surgical approach, and outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The majority of stones in both glands measured 5–10 mm (parotid: 10/26, 38%; submandibular: 33/72, 46%), though larger stones (>10 mm) were more prevalent in the parotid gland (parotid: 7/26, 27% vs. submandibular: 15/72, 21%). Intraductal stone location dominated in both glands (parotid: 17/26, 65%; submandibular: 56/72, 78%), while single stones were most common (parotid: 21/24, 88%; submandibular: 55/72, 76%). Sialendoscopy alone was the primary approach for both glands (parotid: 16/26, 62%; submandibular: 42/72, 58%), with combined approaches used more frequently in submandibular cases (submandibular: 28/72, 39% vs. parotid: 8/26, 31%). Among stone-confirmed cases (excluding 4 diagnostic-only procedures), full stone retrieval was achieved in 79% (19/24) of parotid and 78.6% (55/70) of submandibular cases, with partial or failed retrieval rates being higher in submandibular glands (21% vs. 21% in parotid). Complications occurred in 11% (11/98) of gland procedures (Table 3). Early complications comprised postoperative swelling/pain in 4% of patients (n=4; parotid: 1, submandibular: 3), all self-limiting and managed conservatively, and one submandibular abscess case treated with antibiotics and incision and drainage. Late complications consisted of iatrogenic ductal stenosis in 6% of cases (n=6; parotid: 2, submandibular: 4), diagnosed by recurrent obstructive symptoms confirmed on repeat sialendoscopy; all six were managed with repeat sialendoscopy and balloon dilation, and none required sialadenectomy. No cases of hematoma, lingual nerve injury, or obstructing sialocele were observed. Most procedures (parotid: 23/26, 88%; submandibular: 64/72, 89%) had no complications. Submandibular glands accounted for 73% (8/11) of complications.

Predictors of surgical success

Stones >10 mm had significantly lower odds of success compared to those <5 mm (OR=0.17, 95% CI: 0.04–0.67; P=.01). Success rates were 88% for <5 mm, 82% for 5–10 mm, and 50% for >10 mm. Intraductal stones had higher success rates than intraglandular stones (OR=7.69, 95% CI: 1.85–33.3; P=.005). Success rates were 84% for intraductal vs. 33% for intraglandular stones. Multiple stones reduced success odds by 82% compared to single stones (OR=0.18, 95% CI: 0.05–0.60; P=.006). Success rates were 84% for single vs. 45% for multiple stones. Neither age (OR=0.99 per year, 95% CI: 0.95–1.03; P=.77) nor sex (OR=1.44, 95% CI: 0.35–6.67; P=.62) predicted surgical success.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study evaluating sialendoscopy for obstructive sialadenitis provides valuable insights into procedural outcomes, predictors of success, and complications. The findings align with broader literature while highlighting unique clinical nuances, particularly regarding stone characteristics and gland-specific challenges. While meta-analyses report 70-90% success, few studies address Middle East demographics or report on the burden and phenotyping of obstructive sialadenitis in this region. Our predictors align but quantify risks, supporting preoperative imaging/CT for certain patients. Notably, four procedures (4%) yielded no stone despite preoperative suspicion of obstruction, instead revealing Sjögren's syndrome or ductal stenosis. This underscores the diagnostic value of sialendoscopy even when therapeutic goals are not met, and highlights the importance of comprehensive preoperative imaging to reduce the rate of non-therapeutic explorations. Below, we contextualize these results, discuss their implications, and address limitations.

The overall technical success rate of 79% (74/94 stone-confirmed cases) falls within the reported range of 69–100% for combined sialendoscopy and minor surgical approaches, as noted in prior meta-analyses.8,9 Success rates were comparable between parotid (79%, 19/24) and submandibular glands (79%, 55/70), suggesting that with appropriate technique selection both glands can achieve similar outcomes despite their distinct anatomical and stone-related challenges. The tortuous course of Stensen's duct and the prevalence of intraglandular stones in the parotid, together with the larger sizes and higher multiplicity often encountered in submandibular cases, each present specific intraoperative considerations that were addressed by tailoring the surgical approach. These observations corroborate studies emphasizing that stone size, location, and multiplicity are critical determinants of success.11 The strong association between stone size and reduced success mirrors findings from other studies which noted that larger stones often require fragmentation or combined approaches.12,13 Similarly, intraductal stones had significantly higher retrieval rates, likely due to better endoscopic accessibility. Multiple stones posed a substantial challenge, consistent with reports that fragmented or clustered stones increase procedural complexity.14 Notably, neither age nor sex predicted outcomes, suggesting that sialendoscopy is equally viable across demographics when stone characteristics are controlled.

The low complication rate underscores sialendoscopy's safety.9 However, submandibular glands accounted for most of the complications, primarily ductal stenosis and postoperative swelling/pain. This may relate to anatomical factors: Wharton's duct is shorter but wider, potentially increasing susceptibility to iatrogenic trauma during instrumentation. Importantly, all six stenosis cases were late complications, consistent with the expected pathophysiology of post-instrumentation fibrosis and scar contracture. All were successfully managed with repeat sialendoscopy and balloon dilation, avoiding sialadenectomy and reinforcing the gland-preservation. Early complications were limited to self-resolving swelling/pain and one abscess, reflecting the overall safety of the procedure.15 Additionally, submandibular stones are often calcium-rich and impacted, necessitating more aggressive retrieval techniques.16 Parotid glands, while less frequently involved, exhibited comparable stenosis rates, possibly due to the duct's narrower lumen and longer course.15 The use of combined approaches, as also demonstrated for hilar lithiasis with favorable duct/parenchyma and quality-of-life outcomes,17 highlights the need for flexibility in surgical planning. For instance, intraoral incisions or balloon dilatation may be critical for larger or impacted stones, as seen in cases with partial retrieval. The study's routine use of corticosteroids and postoperative antibiotics may have contributed to the low infection rate observed in this cohort, although this association was not directly analyzed. Recent randomized evidence supports that routine use of antibiotics prophylaxis in sialendoscopy may not be necessary,18 but further prospective investigations are warranted.19

This study's retrospective design introduces inherent biases, including incomplete records and variable follow-up durations. The sample size (90 patients) limited subgroup analyses, particularly for rare complications or chronic diseases. Additionally, the median follow-up of 9.4 months limits assessment of long-term recurrence rates. Within this follow-up period, 12.5% (9/72) of submandibular cases required reintervention at a median of 6.8 months post-procedure, predominantly for residual or recurrent stones. Longer follow-up may reveal additional recurrences, particularly beyond the first year. Finally, the absence of a control group restricts direct comparison of morbidity and cost-effectiveness. Additionally, although outcomes were analyzed at the gland/procedure level—a strategy consistent with contemporary literature on chronic obstructive sialadenitis—a small number of patients contributed bilateral procedures, which may introduce some within-patient correlation that was not formally accounted for in the statistical model. Importantly, our study did not incorporate patient-reported outcome measures, which are increasingly recognized as essential endpoints in obstructive sialadenitis research. Validated instruments such as the Chronic Sialadenitis Symptom Questionnaire,20 the Multidisciplinary Salivary Gland Society consensus measures,21 the Chronic Salivary Obstruction Checklist,22 and the Salivary Patient Interview Tool23 should be incorporated in future prospective studies to capture patient-centred outcomes beyond technical success. Nevertheless, our study has several recommendations. First, high-resolution ultrasound or non-contrast CT should be standard to assess stone characteristics including size, location, and multiplicity. Preoperative imaging with ultrasound and/or non-contrast CT is recommended to characterize stone burden prior to sialendoscopy. This could guide the choice between pure endoscopic vs. combined approaches, optimizing first-attempt success. Second, patients with large, intraglandular, or multiple stones should be informed of lower success rates and potential need for staged procedures. Third, for challenging cases, early adoption of laser lithotripsy or microdrills; tools not routinely used here, could have improved outcomes.24

CONCLUSION

This study reinforces sialendoscopy as a safe, effective first-line intervention for obstructive sialadenitis, particularly for single, intraductal stones <10 mm in this cohort. However, stone characteristics; size, location, and multiplicity, remain pivotal to outcomes. While submandibular glands dominated caseloads, their anatomical and stone-related challenges necessitated tailored approaches. By integrating preoperative imaging, clinicians can further enhance success rates and minimize morbidity.
