Severe Maternal Morbidity During and After Pregnancy Among Veterans With Systemic Lupus Erythematosus or Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Deirdre A Quinn, Florentina E Sileanu, Gregory T Procario, Shannon Mitchell, Mehret Birru Talabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe severe maternal morbidity (SMM) among veterans with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or rheumatoid arthritis (RA) who use Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) health care.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of VA national administrative data to compare rates of SMM among veterans with and without SLE or RA. Our sample included veterans aged 18 to 45 years who had at least one pregnancy between October 2009 and September 2019 and at least one documented VA primary care visit within the year before pregnancy. International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision codes were used to identify SLE, RA, and medical and mental health comorbidities within two years before pregnancy. SMM was assessed using Centers for Disease Control and Prevention definitions and included events during and up to 42 days after pregnancy.ResultsAmong 29,713 veterans, 113 had SLE, and 92 had RA. Of these veterans, 36% with SLE and 30.4% with RA experienced a nonlive birth outcome, including stillbirth, ectopic pregnancy, and spontaneous abortion, compared with 25.2% of other veterans. Nearly 10% of veterans with SLE and 4.3% of veterans with RA experienced SMM, compared with 3.2% of other veterans.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first study of SMM among veterans with SLE and RA. Veterans with SLE appear to have an elevated risk of SMM, and veterans with SLE and RA appear to have high rates of pregnancy loss. Our findings highlight the potential utility of comprehensive maternity care models for these veterans with SLE and RA within the VA health care system.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are chronic systemic and autoimmune rheumatic diseases (SARDs) that disproportionately affect women and birthing people. Many people with SARDs experience healthy and safe pregnancies. 1 However, as compared with the general birthing population, SLE pregnancies are associated with higher rates of miscarriage, fetal growth restriction, thromboembolic disease, postpartum infection, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy than those of the general population, as well as a 5‐ to 20‐fold increased risk of maternal mortality. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Many patients with RA experience less synovitis and joint pain during pregnancy 6 ; however, RA pregnancies are associated with an increased risk of preeclampsia, acute renal injury, cardiomyopathy, and venous thromboembolism as compared with the general population. 7

Although pregnancy outcomes among people with SLE and RA have been described in the general US population, they have rarely been described among veterans in the US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) health care system. In general, veterans of childbearing age have a high prevalence of chronic medical conditions and social risks that elevate their risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as severe maternal morbidity (SMM). 8 , 9 In a multicenter pregnancy cohort of veterans, only 25 veterans self‐reported autoimmune diseases, including SLE and RA; 44% of these veterans reported that they had experienced pregnancy complications, and only 32% used VA medical subspecialty care during pregnancy—suggesting a high burden of adverse pregnancy outcomes and potential gaps in peripartum health care. 10 Little else is known about pregnancy outcomes among people with SARDs in the VA.

In addition to health‐related factors, pregnancy outcomes among veterans with SARDs may differ from nonveterans due to differences in clinical care delivery within the VA health care system. For example, as compared with non‐VA health care systems, the VA generally does not provide on‐site obstetric services, so pregnant veterans must use community‐based obstetric care resources. However, the VA does offer a unique model of integrated clinical‐care and pregnancy‐care benefits to all veteran enrollees, which begins before pregnancy and extends into the postpartum period. Veterans using VA maternity benefits may access community‐based pregnancy‐related and postpartum care while continuing to receive nonpregnancy‐related care, including subspecialty medical care, within the VA (eg, rheumatology). 11

In this brief communication, we use national VA administrative data to describe pregnancy outcomes among veterans with and without SLE or RA and who received VA maternity care benefits over a 10‐year timeframe. We focused our analysis on pregnancies among veterans with SLE and RA because these conditions are fairly prevalent among pregnancy‐capable people with SARDs, 12 and validated algorithms facilitate the identification of patients with SLE and RA from administrative data. In our discussion, we propose ways in which the existing VA model might feasibly integrate rheumatology clinical care into the current maternity care model for pregnant veterans with SARDs.

METHODS

Using VA national administrative claims, we identified a retrospective cohort of veterans aged 18 to 45 years with at least one recorded pregnancy between October 2009 and September 2019 and evidence of recent engagement with VA primary care (ie, at least one VA primary care visit within one year before the start of the pregnancy). Abstracted records included VA inpatient and outpatient billing data and non‐VA billing data for prenatal, labor and delivery, and early postpartum care services for all pregnancies for which any pregnancy‐related care either was provided in VA or was paid for by VA through VA‐contracted community providers. 13 , 14 Prepregnancy medical and mental health comorbidities were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) diagnosis codes extracted from the VA's Corporate Data Warehouse. This study was approved by the VA Pittsburgh Healthcare System Institutional Review Board (IRBNet 1628889) and granted a waiver of informed consent.

To identify people who had diagnoses of SLE or RA before pregnancy, we modified published algorithms to identify pregnancies with two or more relevant diagnosis codes at least 30 days apart in the two years before pregnancy. 15 , 16 Additional prepregnancy physical and mental health comorbidities of interest included antiphospholipid syndrome, end‐stage renal disease, chronic hypertension, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder; these comorbidities may be more likely to occur among people with SLE and RA and/or may independently affect pregnancy outcomes. 17

Codes for pregnancy outcomes included live birth, stillbirth, spontaneous abortion, or ectopic pregnancy. Because the VA did not pay for or provide abortion care during the study timeframe, we were unable to identify within the administrative data any pregnancies that ended in elective terminations. SMM was identified using ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes corresponding to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's (CDC) 21 indicators for SMM during delivery hospitalization (Supplemental Table 1). 18 We assessed for SMM codes during pregnancy and up to 42 days postpartum, reflecting published recommendations for the SMM definition to include the postpartum period, which is when most cases of maternal mortality occur. 19 In accordance with prior studies, we examined SMM both including and excluding codes for the indicator for blood transfusion; because the volume of transfusion is difficult to capture using ICD codes, including transfusion alone as an indicator may result in overestimation of SMM. 20 , 21

Veteran characteristics included race and ethnicity, age at pregnancy, and VA enrollment priority status (categorized in three groups: indication of a service‐connected disability [ie, an injury or illness deemed to have been incurred or aggravated during active military service, priority groups 1–4]; low income [priority group 5]; and no service‐connected disability or low income [priority groups 6–8]).

We used descriptive statistics to describe veteran‐level demographic, health, and pregnancy characteristics of the sample. Bivariate associations were assessed between veterans with (1) SLE versus no history of SLE or RA (comparison group) and (2) RA versus the comparison group, with respect to demographic, health, and pregnancy characteristics and outcomes. Because our SLE and RA groups were relatively small, we used Fisher's exact tests, which can assess differences between groups with small sample sizes. We considered a two‐tailed P value of less than 0.05 to be statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26. 22

RESULTS

Demographic and health characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1. Descriptive data were coarsened as necessary in accordance with cell size suppression policies for federal data. 23 Among 29,713 veterans with a recorded pregnancy during our study timeframe, 0.4% (n = 113) had a documented history of SLE, and 0.3% (n = 92) had a documented history of RA. Nearly half of veterans with SLE (50.9%) and one‐third of veterans with RA (31.4%) were Black; over 60% of veterans with SLE or RA were younger than age 34 years. Medical and psychiatric comorbidities were common among veterans with SLE and RA, including antiphospholipid syndrome (SLE >5%; RA >2%), end‐stage renal disease (SLE 17.7%; RA >5%), chronic hypertension (SLE 29.2%; RA 15.2%), pulmonary hypertension (SLE 34.5%; RA >5%), depression (SLE 45.1%; RA 51.1%), and posttraumatic stress disorder (SLE 26.5%; RA 32.6%). Fisher's exact tests indicated significant associations among a variety of demographic, health, and pregnancy characteristics and a diagnosis of SLE or RA.

DISCUSSION

In a national sample of veterans who experienced pregnancy, veterans with SLE and RA had multiple disease‐related characteristics and comorbidities with the potential to contribute to adverse pregnancy and perinatal outcomes. The incidence of nonlive birth outcomes among veterans with SLE and RA was 36% and 30.4%, respectively, as compared with 25.2% among veterans in the comparison group. The incidence of SMM was similar between veterans with RA and those with neither condition, but veterans with SLE had a significantly higher risk than those in the comparison group of experiencing any SMM event, excluding transfusion.

The incidence of SMM in SLE pregnancies in the current study is broadly consistent with the high rates of pregnancy complications reported in other SLE cohorts. 2 , 3 , 4 For example, one study using inpatient delivery data from the National Inpatient Sample reported a 4% incidence of nontransfusion SMM among pregnant women with SLE as compared with the 5.3% incidence of nontransfusion SMM observed in the current sample. 24 In our sample, veterans with SLE also appeared to have serious medical and psychiatric comorbidities, including renal disease, chronic hypertension, pulmonary hypertension, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder. In addition, half of the veterans with SLE were Black; in prior published work involving a national sample of pregnant veterans with and without medical comorbidities, we found that pregnancies among Black veterans had 69% greater likelihood of any adverse event than among White veterans. 21 Although the small sample size of patients with SLE precluded our ability to assess interactions between race and SLE as a driver of SMM in the current study, our findings underscore the need for research to evaluate the social and structural factors that may contribute to adverse pregnancy outcomes among veterans with SLE.

At present, the VA's current maternity care model integrates community‐based maternity care with VA‐based primary care because obstetric services are generally not available at VA facilities. VA maternity care coordinators (MCCs) establish and maintain communication with veterans’ VA Patient Aligned Care Teams and enable electronic alerts within a veteran's medical record to inform VA providers when a veteran is either pregnant or lactating. 25 During and after a veteran's pregnancy, MCCs facilitate veterans’ continuity of care by liaising with veterans and their VA and non‐VA clinicians, including but not limited to obstetrics, mental health, primary care, and social work. 25 MCCs also monitor the delivery of non‐VA maternity services, facilitate care coordination between VA and non‐VA providers, and track veterans’ health outcomes. Although veterans with SARDs can access routine rheumatology clinical care, rheumatology care and consultation has not specifically been integrated into the maternity care model for individuals with SARDs. Although our study was not designed to ascertain characteristics in health care use, a published survey of veterans with autoimmune diseases who had experienced pregnancy found that 40% did not seek VA health care during their pregnancy, which could have included rheumatology care; suboptimal autoimmune disease control and management could contribute to adverse pregnancy outcomes in this group. 10 Because our data suggest that the risk for adverse pregnancy outcomes is high among veterans with SLE and RA but the absolute number of these pregnancies is low, a centralized approach (ie, non–site specific) may be optimal for connecting MCCs with rheumatologists—preferably rheumatologists with pregnancy expertise. Recruitment or training of VA rheumatologists with this expertise or an interest in consulting on rheumatology‐obstetric cases could therefore be critical to supporting an integrated care model within the VA that enhances outcomes among pregnant veterans with SARDs.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use national VA administrative data to evaluate the pregnancy outcomes of veterans with RA and SLE. Strengths of our study include the use of multiyear national data and the inclusion of pregnancy and the postpartum period for a more expansive exploration of SMM. However, the low absolute number of pregnancies among veterans with SLE and RA limited our ability to make statistical comparisons among subgroups with and without SLE and RA. Our data are limited to veterans for whom the VA provided or paid for any pregnancy‐related care and may not be representative of all birthing veterans. Because the VA did not provide or pay for any abortion care during the study timeframe, we were unable to identify pregnancies that ended in elective abortions; thus, some pregnancies among veterans—including those that may have been terminated for health reasons—would not have been captured in our analysis. Our study evaluated pregnancy outcomes using the published CDC SMM indicators but did not include all adverse pregnancy‐related conditions that might affect maternal and fetal health; outcomes such as preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction, and neonatal hospitalization require future study in the VA population. Our dataset was also not designed to capture all VA primary care or subspecialty health care visits; future work will involve chart review of RA and SLE pregnancies and evaluation of clinical encounters before, during, and after pregnancy to clarify how VA health care engagement affects peripartum outcomes. Finally, the small sample size precluded our ability to assess how medical advancements in SLE and RA care may have influenced pregnancy outcomes over the 10‐year study timeframe.

In summary, some veterans with SLE and RA experience adverse pregnancy and perinatal outcomes, including SMM and fetal loss. The VA is the largest integrated health care system in the United States, provides care for a medically and socially complex patient population, and has developed an integrated obstetric care delivery model that addresses social and health issues. Additional work is needed to determine how the VA maternity care system can be adapted to support healthy pregnancies among veterans with SLE, RA, and potentially other SARDs.
