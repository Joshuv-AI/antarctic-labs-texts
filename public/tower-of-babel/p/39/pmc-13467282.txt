Long-Term Outcomes, Morbidity and Quality of Life in Node-Negative Early-Stage Vulvar Cancer: Sentinel Node Biopsy versus Inguinofemoral Lymphadenectomy
Authors: Sarah Tanskanen, Björn Lampe, Peter Mallmann, Fabinshy Thangarajah, Pierce Heiden, Dirk M. Forner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare recurrence, survival, morbidity, and quality of life in early-stage vulvar cancer patients with tumour-free lymph nodes undergoing sentinel lymph node biopsy (SNB) versus inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy (IFL).Study DesignA total of 74 vulvar cancer patients (SNB: n = 58; IFL: n = 16) with a tumour size of < 4 cm and tumour-free lymph nodes were included. Endpoints were overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and disease-free survival (DFS). DFS was further divided into local, inguinal, or distant recurrence. Additionally, morbidity and quality of life were analysed.ResultsNo significant differences in survival outcomes were observed after a median follow-up of 58.0 months for OS, 59.1 months for DSS, and 40.0 months for DFS. Inguinal recurrences occurred in two patients in the SNB group (3.77%) and in none of the patients in the IFL group (0%). However, DFS related to isolated inguinal recurrence did not differ significantly between the groups (96.2% vs. 100%, p = 0.374). Complications were less frequent following SNB, with a significant difference observed only for lymphocele formation (13.8% vs. 50%; p = 0.02). Quality of life scores did not differ significantly between the groups.ConclusionThis study confirms SNB as an oncologically safe procedure. Although inguinal recurrences were slightly more frequent after SNB, there was no significant difference in DSS, DFS, or OS. Lymphocele was significantly more common after IFL, without impacting self-reported quality of life.

Introduction

In Germany in 2023, vulvar cancer was the fourth most common malignancy of the female genital tract, with approximately 3160 new cases per year, an age-standardized annual incidence rate of 4.1 per 100000, and accounting for 1.3% of all newly diagnosed cancers 1 . Incidence peaks in the seventh decade of life 1 . The relative 5-year survival rate in stage I lies around 90% 1 2 .

Surgical staging reveals inguinal lymph node metastasis in approximately 26.3–31.6% of patients with early-stage vulvar cancer 3 4 5 . Imaging for lymph node screening is not reliable in detecting smaller lesions, and around 30% of patients are found to have lymph node metastasis in pathological analyses after surgical staging, even when lymph nodes were clinically determined to be tumour-free 6 7 .

If the tumour is unifocal, measures less than 4 cm in diameter, and the lymph nodes are clinically negative, two approaches to invasive lymph node assessment can be applied: the sentinel lymph node biopsy (SNB), where only the first lymph nodes in the drainage pathway of a tumour’s lymph flow are resected, or the total inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy, where all (a minimum of 9–10) inguinofemoral lymph nodes are removed 4 8 9 10 . In this cohort, SNB has replaced inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy, the previous standard procedure, over the past decades 11 12 .

The advantages of SNB over IFL are lower per- and postoperative morbidity 4 10 13 , shorter hospitalisation 4 , and fewer long-term complications, particularly lymphoedema 10 13 14 . At the same time, overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) after SNB do not seem to be compromised compared to IFL 15 16 .

The concept of SNB is based on the assumption that if the first inguinofemoral lymph nodes are tumour-free, a more distant spreading of tumour cells along the lymph drainage pathway is unlikely 6 17 . In 2012, Levenback et al. showed the diagnostic accuracy of SNB in detecting inguinal metastasis in vulvar cancer patients 3 .

Inguinal recurrence rates do not seem to differ between both procedures, with 0–5.3% 4 5 10 15 after SNB and 0–5% after IFL 10 18 in early-stage node-negative vulvar cancer.

However, the first SNB recommendations were mainly based on the prospective multicenter Groningen International Study on Sentinel Nodes in Vulvar Cancer I (GROINSS-V I), which did not find differences in survival and recurrence rates in early-stage clinically node-negative vulvar carcinoma patients undergoing SNB with tumour-free lymph nodes when compared to patients receiving complementary IFL after detection of metastasis in SNB 4 8 . Since the presence of inguinal lymph node metastases is associated with higher inguinal recurrence rates and compromised survival 5 6 , the comparability of the two groups in the GROINSS-V I analysis may have been affected by the fact that IFL patients had lymph node metastasis 4 8 .

In the late 2010s, a few international studies compared outcomes in SNB to IFL in early-stage node-negative vulvar carcinoma with the same tumour features required for SNB and did not find differences in inguinal recurrence rates or survival 5 10 12 .

None of the previous studies, however, have analysed OS, DSS, and disease-free survival (DFS) in relation to vulvar, inguinal, or distant recurrence, morbidity, and quality of life in node-negative vulvar cancer (tumour size < 4 cm, N0), comparing SNB with IFL, which was the aim of our study.

Materials and Methods

Study design

The study was a retrospective observational study with an inquiry into quality of life.

Patient population

We reviewed all patient files, including “vulvar carcinoma diagnosis” between July 2009 and April 2019 at University Hospital Cologne, Protestant Hospital Köln Kalk, and the Florence Nightingale Hospital in Düsseldorf.

Exclusion criteria were lymph node metastasis, multifocality, a tumour size above 4 cm in the histopathological analysis, chemotherapy and radiation. Finally, 74 patients with a tumour size < 4 cm, undergoing local tumour resection, and having either SNB or IFL with tumour-free lymph nodes in surgical staging (N0), were eligible for inclusion.

Data on complications, surgical procedures, morbidity, and readmissions were retrieved from the medical records. All patient data was pseudonymised.

Ethical considerations

The Cologne ethical committee approved the study on Jun 25, 2019, under file number 18–381.

Clinical endpoints

Endpoints were OS, DSS, and DFS regarding vulvar, inguinal, or distant recurrences. Survival time was calculated based on the differences in months between the initial operation and the last follow-up, death, or recurrence. The cut-off for follow-up was May 01, 2022.

Further endpoints were defined as morbidity, consisting of the composite “complications of any type” of local and inguinal complications (including wound breakdown, infection, hematoma, abscess, chronic vulvitis, chronic urethritis, stenosis, pain, and postoperative hemorrhage). Inguinal complications were divided into a composite of inguinal wound healing disorders (including wound breakdown, infection, and abscess), lymphocele, and lymphoedema.

For quality of life evaluation, a survey composed of the 12-Item Short Form Survey, a valid and reliable tool for assessing quality of life in cancer patients 19 , and additional questions was sent to all patients at the earliest six months after the initial surgery. With the help of an evaluation tool, a physical and a mental component summary was calculated.

The additional survey questions addressed impairments in daily life, analgesic use, paraesthesia, and whether patients would recommend the same operation to a friend.

Tumour and patient characteristics

As potential confounders, patient characteristics such as age and body mass index, as well as the tumour characteristics size, grading, infiltration depth, marginality, and localisation (central or lateral) as well as data on surgical procedure and laterality of the lymphadenectomy were collected from patient records at the respective hospital. Further, data on the surgical procedure and laterality of the lymphadenectomy were recorded. Central tumour localisation was defined as within one cm of the midline.

SLN detection was performed by lymphography using a radioactive tracer and/or blue dye, in accordance with the respective institutional protocol. Ultrastaging was performed routinely on SNB.

Statistical analysis

The statistical evaluation was carried out using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Descriptive statistics for patient and tumour characteristics are provided separately for each group (SNB and IFL patients) as well as for the total population. Quantitative data were described by median and range.

The Mann-Whitney U test was conducted to test for differences in the central tendency of the two groups.

Qualitative data was summarised by counts and percentages, and a two-sided Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was conducted to test for equal distributions in both cohorts.

Time-to-event analyses were supported by Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test. All statistical tests were conducted based on a two-sided significance level of 0.05.

Results

Descriptive analyses

Out of the 74 included patients, 58 underwent SNB and 16 inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy. In the SNB group, three patients were lost to follow-up and excluded from survival analyses. Two additional patients had follow-up data but were not included in the full survival analyses (one contributed to OS and DSS, and one to OS only). The final survival analysis included 53 SNB patients with follow-up (24 with quality-of-life data) and all 16 patients in the IFL group with follow-up (8 with quality-of-life data), for whom OS, DSS, and DFS were assessed. Group sizes were uneven due to the transition from IFL to SNB over the past few years, including the study period.

Regarding appropriate statistical testing of differences, all analysed and potentially confounding patient and tumour characteristics were evenly distributed between groups ( Table 1 ).

Patients who did not answer the survey or who were lost to follow-up did not differ in tumour characteristics, body mass index, or age compared to those with follow-up and those who answered the survey (Supplementary Material, Table S1 ).

Survival analyses

OS was 90.9% in SNB patients (50/55) and 93.8% in IFL patients (15/16) after a median follow-up time of 58.0 months (range: 8–144). The log-rank test for equality of the time-to-event distribution between both cohorts was not significant (p = 0.430) ( Fig. 1 ).

Kaplan-Meier curves of overall survival in SNB and IFL patients. Time to last follow-up or death in months. Censored at last follow-up.

DSS was 94.4% in the SNB group (50/54) and 100% in the IFL group (16/16) after a median follow-up time of 59.1 months (range: 8–116). The log-rank test for equality of the time-to-event distribution between the cohorts was not significant (p = 0.176).

Regarding DFS, the log-rank test for equality of the time-to-event distribution was not significant. DFS was 88.7% in SNB patients (47/53) and 93.8% in IFL patients (15/16) after a median follow-up time of 40.0 months (range: 8–115) (p = 0.417) ( Fig. 2 ).

Kaplan-Meier Curves of disease-free survival in SNB and IFL patients. Time to last follow-up or recurrence in months. Censored at last follow-up.

DFS with regard to isolated inguinal recurrences was 96.2% in the SNB group (51/53) and 100% in the IFL group (16/16). The time-to-event curves did not differ significantly between groups according to the log-rank test (p = 0.374). Both patients diagnosed with isolated inguinal recurrences had undergone partial vulvectomy and bilateral SNB, with a total of four and five lymph nodes removed, respectively ( Fig. 3 ).

Distribution of recurrence by site (%). Overall, recurrences with vulvar involvement occurred in 7.54% of SNB patients and 6.25% of IFL patients. In 1.89% of SNB patients and 6.25% of IFL patients, the recurrence was confined to the vulva. The incidence of isolated inguinal recurrences was 3.77% in the SNB group and 0% in the IFL group. Concurrent vulvar and inguinal recurrences as well as concurrent vulvar, inguinal, and distant recurrences were observed in 3.77% and 1.89% of SNB patients, respectively, whereas only isolated vulvar recurrences occurred among IFL patients.

DFS with regard to isolated vulvar recurrences was 98.1% in the SNB group (52/53) and 93.8% in the IFL group (15/16). No significant differences in the time-to-event curves between groups were observed (log-rank test, p = 0.471).

DFS with regard to concurrent vulvar and inguinal recurrences was 96.2% in the SNB group (51/53) and 100% in the IFL group (16/16). The time-to-event curves did not differ significantly between groups (log-rank test, p = 0.350).

DFS with regard to concurrent vulvar, inguinal, and distant recurrences was 98.1% in the SNB group (52/53) and 100% in the IFL group (16/16). No significant differences in the time-to-event curves were found between groups according to the log-rank test (p = 0.569).

No isolated distant recurrences were reported.

Morbidity analyses

Morbidity was analysed in all included patients, comprising 58 SNB patients and 16 IFL patients. Complications of any type were identified in 51.7% of SNB patients (30/58) and 75% of IFL patients (12/16). According to the chi-square test, the null hypothesis of equal proportions between the two groups could not be rejected (p = 0.134).

Due to the small sample sizes, Fisher’s exact test was applied for the following morbidity outcomes. Wound-healing disorders of the inguinal incision occurred in 13.8% of SNB patients (8/58) and 25% of IFL patients (4/16), without statistically significant differences (p = 0.277). Lymphocele occurred significantly less frequently in the SNB group than in the IFL group (13.8% (8/58) vs. 50% (8/16), p = 0.02).

The proportions of patients diagnosed with lymphoedema did not differ significantly between the groups, affecting 15.5% of SNB patients (9/58) and 25% of IFL patients (4/16) (p = 0.439).

Quality of life analyses

A total of 43.2% (32/74) of all patients completed the quality-of-life survey, including 75% (24/32) in the SNB group and 25% (8/32) in the IFL group. One SNB patient responded to the 12-item Short Form Survey but not to the additional questions. The median time from surgery to answering the survey was 57.5 months (range 6–109 months) in SNB and 75.0 months (range 58–115) in IFL patients, respectively. This difference was statistically significant (Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.033).

The sample median of the SF-12 score for physical health (PCS) was calculated to be 48.7 (range 18.8–57.4) in SNB and 51.6 (range 42.6–55.9) in IFL patients, and the median of the score for mental health status (MCS) was 55.2 (range 30–66.9) in SNB and 50.2 (range 41.9–61.3) in IFL patients. A Mann-Whitney U test was applied to determine group differences in PCS and MCS, respectively. No statistically significant differences in scores were observed in either analysis (PCS: p = 0.63; MCS: p = 0.84). Fisher’s exact tests were used to test for differences in proportions of events in the following features: Impairment of daily life was experienced by 20.8% of SNB patients (5/24) and 0% of IFL patients (0/8) (p = 0.296). Analgesics were used by 8.3% of SNB patients (2/24) and 0% of IFL patients (0/8) (p = 1.000). Paraesthesia was reported by 29.2% of SNB patients (7/24) and 25% of IFL patients (2/8) (p = 1.000). All 32 patients stated that they would recommend the same surgical method to a friend.

Discussion

In this retrospective observational study involving 74 patients with early-stage vulvar cancer, there were no significant differences in OS, DSS, or DFS concerning local, inguinal, and distant recurrences between patients who underwent SNB and those who received IFL. IFL patients developed lymphocele significantly more frequently. Quality of life evaluation was similar for both groups.

None of the three previous studies with similar inclusion criteria on the topic showed worse survival or higher risk for recurrence in SNB patients 5 10 12 . All of them were retrospective studies, with a median follow-up of 33, 44 and 60.4 months 5 10 12 .

OS in our study was better than in similar studies, in which rates ranged between 81.0–82.5% in SNB patients and 77.5–88.2% in IFL patients after 3 5 to 5 years 10 12 , but comparable to the German-wide 3–5-year survival in FIGO stage I 1 2 5 . Median age in our study was lower than in comparable collectives, which could explain higher OS rates 10 20 . Zhou et al. reported age as a factor adversely affecting even DSS 12 , which may partly explain why the DSS rates observed in our study were higher than the five-year DSS rates reported by Rodríguez-Trujillo et al. (83.3% for SNB and 92.2% for IFL) 10 .

Regarding DFS, incidences of isolated inguinal recurrences in our study were similar to those in previous studies 4 8 18 . Rodríguez-Trujillo et al. found isolated inguinal recurrences in 4.8% of SNB patients and 2.0% of IFL patients (p = 0.587) 10 .

In both our study and that of Rodríguez-Trujillo et al. (n = 42 [SNB] and n = 51 [IFL]) a slight but non-significant trend toward higher inguinal recurrence rates and worse DSS was observed in SNB patients 10 .

In this regard, we have to underline the small sample size, which characterises our analysis as one major limitation of our study. Thus, whether a more powerful analysis would have detected an actual and clinically significant difference in DSS and DFS rates remains unclear. This is especially concerning because both patients with inguinal recurrences in our study died from their disease, highlighting the high lethality associated with inguinal recurrences 8 21 . However, Zhou et al., who included in an American, register-based study 129 SNB and 1346 IFL patients, did not find differences in DSS 12 and Klapdor et al. reported similar inguinal recurrence rates of 3.0% vs 3.4% (p = 0.845), comparing 69 SNB to 487 IFL patients in a subgroup analysis of the AGO CaRE-1 study from 2016 after a median follow-up of 33 months 5 . Studies may, though, not be directly comparable, as some calculated inguinal recurrence rates by groins 4 18 , while others by patients 10 , and not all analysed recurrences by localisation 12 .

Klapdor et al., however, reported significantly larger median tumour sizes and infiltration depths in IFL patients 5 . Tumour diameter and infiltration depth have been related to a higher risk for inguinal metastasis and a worse survival 3 10 12 20 . Given similar recurrence rates despite bigger tumour sizes and deeper infiltration in inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy, potential risks with SNB may be masked since a cohort with smaller tumour sizes and lower infiltration depths may have an inherently lower risk for recurrences and better survival. Nonetheless, multivariate Cox regression analyses in Klapdor et al.’s study, adjusting for tumour diameter and infiltration depth, did not affect recurrence rates or survival 5 . Similarly, Rodríguez-Trujillo et al. reported comparable tumour sizes in both groups and did not find differences in survival 10 .

The slight tendency towards higher inguinal recurrence rates in SNB than in IFL may raise concerns about false negative results in SNB. However, using preoperative injection of a radioactive tracer for lymphoscintigraphy and blue dye around the vulvar tumour as well as pathological ultra-staging, detection, and accuracy of SNB is good with a false negative rate below 10% and sensitivity of 91.7–95% 3 22 . Differences in the detection mode of the sentinel lymph node could affect false negative rates and thus recurrence risks.

In the studies by Klapdor et al. and in our study, surgeons used either blue dye and/or scintigraphy 5 , whereas Rodríguez-Trujillo et al. combined both methods 10 .

Further, false negative rates in SNB may depend on a surgeon’s experience in detecting the sentinel lymph node 23 , which may be worse outside tumour centres due to the lower volume of SNB 8 24 . In this aspect, differences may exist between countries, as only some countries have fully established centralisation of vulvar carcinoma operations to tumour centres 24 .

In our study, SNB patients suffered significantly less frequently from lymphocele than IFL patients, which aligns with lower SNB complication rates in previous studies 4 10 13 .

Lymphoedema occurred in 15.5% of SNB patients in our cohort, exceeding rates reported in earlier studies, while the incidence in IFL patients (25%) was similar to that previously described 10 13 25 . For instance, lymphoedema rates reported by Rodríguez Trujillo et al. were 0% in SNB and 33.3% in IFL, respectively 10 , and in van der Zee et al.’s study 1.9% in SNB vs 25.5% in IFL (p < 0.0001) 4 .

Differences in the radicality of surgery or distribution of risk factors such as age, diabetes, or body mass index could be addressed as underlying causes for observed differences 25 . A Swedish register-based study on vulvar cancer patients (n = 182), however, could not associate age or body mass index to higher complication risks 13 .

Despite higher complication rates in the IFL group, neither our study nor the study by Oonk et al., including patients from the GROINSS-V I cohort, demonstrated significant differences in patient-reported quality of life between SNB and IFL patients 14 . This is a surprising result, considering the previously reported reduced quality of life in IFL patients 26 27 . However, in our study, only lymphocele occurred significantly more often in IFL patients – a short-term complication, which presumably was rehabilitated by the time the survey was answered.

Strengths include an extended follow-up and the inclusion of a wide variety of tumour characteristics, analysis of recurrence by localisation, and quality of life evaluation.

Limitations of this study arise from its retrospective and not randomised design, and potential confounding cannot be excluded. Due to the small sample size, especially in the IFL cohort, the applied statistical tests had low power to detect possible actual differences.

However, due to the rarity of the diagnosis, small sample sizes are common in studies on vulvar cancer. To obtain larger and numerically even groups, recruitment of patients undergoing IFL could have extended further back in time, as treatment patterns have shifted in recent years toward increased use of SNB and reduced use of IFL in early-stage vulvar carcinoma 11 12 . Accordingly, in our study, IFL procedures were performed between 2009–2016 and SNB procedures between 2010–2019, significantly affecting the median time to survey response. Extending recruitment further back in time, however, might have introduced additional confounding due to evolving medical standards, as well as greater differences in follow-up duration. Another limitation is the low response rate to the QoL survey. Furthermore, the number of removed lymph nodes was not systematically recorded and was incompletely documented in the medical records. Data on some outcome variables was missing, but the missing data was evenly distributed between both groups.

Conclusions

The SNB seems to have similar survival and recurrence rates but lower risks for lymphocele than IFL, which supports the implementation of SNB as a first-line procedure. Results should, though, be interpreted with caution, as significance may have been compromised by the small study population.

Lymphoedema rates were higher and local recurrences lower than in previous studies. More research is needed to understand underlying factors. Self-reported quality of life did not differ between groups.
