Strengthening anal cancer prevention in people with HIV without immediate HRA access-correspondence
Authors: Sandhya Nagarakanti, Christopher Mathews, Edward Cachay
Published: 2025

By the end of 2025, an estimated 10 930 people in the United States will be diagnosed with anal cancer, with approximately 2030 projected deaths[1]. Those with HIV (PWH) are at the highest risk, especially if they have had profound immunosuppression. A major randomized trial showed that early detection and treatment of precancerous lesions can lower the risk of progressing to anal cancer by 57%, highlighting the need for proactive screening and prevention[2]. This study was prepared in accordance with the TITAN 2025 reporting guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness in reporting[3].

Current screening modalities for high-risk populations

Professional guidelines from the International Anal Neoplasia Society (IANS), the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons, the European AIDS Clinical Society (EACS), and NIH/CDC/HIVMSA/IDSA jointly recommend routine screening for high-risk groups: PWH, especially HIV-positive men who have sex with men (MSM), transgender women, women with a history of lower genital tract neoplasia, organ transplant recipients, and those with anogenital warts. An age-risk stratified approach to screening is advised[4–7].

The three recommended modalities include: (1) Digital Anorectal Examination (DARE)[8], (2) Anal Pap smear, and (3) Rectal PCR testing for high-risk HPV testing (hr-HPV).

The goal is early detection of anal high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL), the immediate precursors to anal squamous cell carcinoma[9], so they can be treated before progressing to anal cancer[2]. Abnormal results on any test should prompt high-resolution anoscopy (HRA), the gold standard for diagnosing and managing HSIL.

IANS supports using DARE followed by anal cytology alone or with hr-HPV testing for those at risk. A positive result on either test calls for HRA referral. In places lacking HRA, using cytology and hr-HPV testing is discouraged due to the absence of follow-up and possible anxiety[4].

Challenges in access to HRA and limitations of DARE

HRA access varies widely in the United States, with few trained clinicians and most expertise concentrated in urban academic centers[10,11]. Provider expertise influences diagnostic accuracy of HRA; missed lesions can occur due to technical or interpretive limits[12]. Discrepancies such as anal cytologic HSIL with no corresponding lesion on HRA-guided biopsy, may not indicate an anal cytology false positive result, but rather reflect missed lesions due to sub-optimal technical or interpretive variability[13].

Where HRA is unavailable, DARE is often the default method, but it only detects palpable, established lesions and misses subclinical HSIL, limiting its role in prevention[14]. Evidence backing routine DARE is limited, and provider compliance is low. For example, when DARE was recommended for prostate cancer screening, just 17% of primary care providers complied[15]. Physical exam skills, crucial for DARE, have declined due to a shift toward less intrusive modalities, reducing confidence and leading to errors or patient refusal[16].

Rectal hr-HPV DNA PCR testing, though off-label for screening, is used in some centers, for its negative predictive value, helping triage patients when HRA is delayed. With wait times for HRA sometimes exceeding six months[15], some institutions use algorithms combining cytology and hr-HPV results to prioritize HRA referrals[16].

Anal cytology: challenges and opportunities

Anal cytology, using the Bethesda classification, helps identify HSIL and guide referral for HRA. Many focus on anal cytology diagnostic limitations such as inter-patient sample variability, inconsistent correlation with HRA biopsy, and high rates of unsatisfactory samples, often due to blind sampling, contamination, or low cellularity (<5000 mL). Nonetheless, cytology HSIL is the strongest longitudinal predictor for anal cancer progression[17]. It is a viable and cost-effective tool[18], particularly in resource-limited settings[19] and is useful for longitudinal monitoring with concurrent DARE, given the typically slow 5-year progression from cytologic HSIL to anal cancer[17,20].

Recent cohort studies support a nuanced approach to anal cancer screening among PWH, emphasizing the value of integrating clinical risk markers with cytology. A longitudinal 20-year follow-up U.S. study involving over 4100 PWH demonstrated that combining cytology results with nadir CD4 count can effectively stratify risk. Those with cytological anal HSIL and nadir CD4 below 200 cells/µL had a 9-fold increased hazard of anal cancer, with 5- and 10-year probabilities of 3.4% and 4.27%[21].

A multicenter study in Spain also found a strong inverse link between nadir CD4 and cancer risk, though it lacked cytology data[22]. The U.S. study identified a synergistic effect between cytology and immune status on risk.

These findings challenge expert consensus against cytology in settings lacking HRA. For select high-risk individuals, particularly older adults with nadir CD4 < 200, shared decision-making may be warranted. Offering cytology, even without HRA, can guide follow-up and intensified DARE or efforts to obtain HRA when HSIL is found.

Toward a risk-based, patient-centered screening framework

Until HRA capacity is expanded, anal cytology should remain a practical screening tool and a core component of provider training. Strengthening training in DARE proficiency is equally important as it remains essential in many settings.

We propose a Bayesian approach: update a patient’s risk profile based on factors such as nadir CD4, with new findings such as anal cytology. In the context of limited HRA availability, cytology can help prioritize referrals and guide the urgency of follow-up, particularly annual DARE, for those at highest-risk patients. For instance, individuals with HSIL on cytology and nadir CD4 below 200 cells/µL face the highest risk among PWH.

As anal cancer incidence rises, it is essential to expand screening access, train more HRA providers, and improve diagnostic physical exam skills. A flexible, risk-based framework that integrates immune status, cytology, and physical exam findings will better reflect clinical realities and support patient-centered care.
