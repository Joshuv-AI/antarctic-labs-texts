Atypical Predictive Processing Is Associated With Sensory Over‐Responsivity in Autism
Authors: Bar Yosef, Valerie Burgess, Megan Banchik, Joshua Ceballos, Mirella Dapretto, Shulamite Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAutism is characterized by sensory processing atypicalities including sensory over‐responsivity (SOR), a heightened negative response to sensory stimuli. Although the neural mechanisms underlying SOR are not fully understood, SOR has been associated with sensory‐limbic hyper‐reactivity during sensory stimulation. Altered predictive processing may play a key role in atypical sensory processing but few studies have directly probed the relationship between SOR and prediction, particularly using aversive stimuli that bother autistic individuals in everyday life. Here, we examined whether visual cues signaling the onset of aversive sensory stimuli would either (a) reduce neural hyper‐reactivity in autism by making stimuli less unexpected or (b) show limited effects in autism due to atypical predictive processing hindering the use of the cue to attenuate responses. Fifty‐five autistic (ASD; 30 female, mean age 21.01 ± 3.49) and 28 typically developing (TD; 16 female, mean age 19.76 ± 3.00) adolescents and young adults underwent functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) while they were exposed to mildly aversive auditory and tactile stimulation. Stimulus blocks were either presented with a visual cue representing the upcoming sensory stimulus (Predictable), or no cue (Unpredictable). Results showed sensory cortical hyper‐reactivity in ASD compared to TD in the Unpredictable condition, but responses to Predictable stimuli were overall similar between groups. In addition, expected stimuli were associated with increased frontal activity in autism, suggesting that visual cues facilitated regulation of sensory responses. Notably, within the autism group, SOR was associated with increased responses to expected stimuli in relevant sensory cortices, suggesting that reduced expectancy suppression may be specifically related to SOR within ASD rather than being a general autistic trait.Keywords: autism spectrum disorder, fMRI, neural activity, predictive processing, sensory over‐responsivity, sensory processing

Introduction

Sensory over‐responsivity (SOR) is a heightened negative response to sensory stimuli such as loud noises. SOR is present across neurodevelopmental conditions and is particularly prevalent in autism spectrum disorder (ASD), affecting more than half of youth with ASD (Ben‐Sasson et al. 2009). Despite the prevalence and disruption to daily life associated with SOR, its neural mechanisms are still not fully understood. Further, there are few interventions directly targeting the sensory symptoms of ASD (Yuan et al. 2022), and studies evaluating available treatments have had mixed results (Siemann et al. 2020). Advancing the understanding of the neural basis of SOR may lead to the development of targeted therapeutic approaches. A growing body of work suggests that predictive processing may play a role in sensory atypicalities in ASD (Lawson et al. 2014; Pellicano and Burr 2012). However, few studies have directly examined how predictive mechanisms relate to sensory sensitivity in ASD, especially using aversive stimuli that resemble real‐world sensory experiences. In this study, we examined the role of predictive processing in neural responses to mildly aversive sensory stimulation in autistic compared to typically developing (TD) individuals, and their relationship to SOR using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI).

Atypicalities in predictive processing have been proposed as a core mechanism underlying sensory symptoms in ASD (Angeletos Chrysaitis and Seriès 2023; Cannon et al. 2021; Gonzalez‐Gadea et al. 2015; Lawson et al. 2014; Palmer et al. 2017; Pellicano and Burr 2012; Van de Cruys et al. 2014). According to predictive coding theories, the brain continually compares incoming sensory input to prior expectations, generating reduced neural responses to expected versus unexpected stimuli—a phenomenon known as expectancy suppression (Koster‐Hale and Saxe 2013; Feuerriegel et al. 2021). Pellicano and Burr (2012) proposed that individuals with ASD may rely more heavily on bottom‐up sensory input and less on prior expectations when forming perceptual representations. This imbalance might impair the ability to anticipate when repeated incoming stimuli are likely to continue and could therefore contribute to a reduced ability to habituate to sensory stimuli.

These theoretical accounts align with prior work linking SOR to reduced habituation in brain regions including primary sensory cortices and the amygdala (Green et al. 2015). Habituation—a process thought to reflect the brain's prediction that a repeated stimulus will continue—may depend on intact predictive mechanisms. Supporting this, Millin et al. (2018) found that autistic adults showed reduced habituation only for stimuli at predictable intervals, suggesting that ASD habituation difficulties stem from impaired temporal predictions. At the same time, evidence for predictive differences in ASD is mixed. For example, fMRI studies in adults found reduced repetition suppression (RS)—a diminished neural response to repeated stimuli suggestive of prediction—for faces but not shapes in the ASD group (D’Mello et al. 2023; Ewbank et al. 2017). Autistic individuals have also been shown to demonstrate reduced RS for unexpected but not expected images (Utzerath et al. 2018). These studies suggest atypical reductions in neural responses to repeated or expected stimuli in ASD may be selective based on certain properties of the stimuli, particularly when predictions are shaped by stimulus salience or prior learning, likely reflecting disruptions in top‐down modulation rather than just bottom‐up adaptation. Top‐down modulation also appears to be important for SOR; attentional control can reduce the impact of sensory distractions on social functioning in ASD, and SOR is associated with altered attentional allocation (Green et al. 2018; Than et al. 2024). However, no research has examined how prior knowledge‐based predictions shape responses to aversive stimuli in the context of SOR—a key gap addressed by the current study.

Here, we used a predictive cueing paradigm in which participants were either presented with a visual image cue indicating the upcoming sensory stimulus (Predictable), or no cue (Unpredictable). Our sensory stimuli were modeled on stimuli that individuals with SOR report as bothersome in their daily lives, such as sirens and scratchy fabrics. Auditory and tactile modalities were specifically selected because they represent the two most frequently reported areas of SOR in ASD (Tomchek and Dunn 2007) and, when presented simultaneously, have been shown to most effectively recapitulate the neural signatures of SOR (Green et al. 2015). Tactile and auditory stimuli may cause the greatest challenges in real‐world environments because they represent stimuli that are difficult to avoid. Auditory stimuli, such as ambient noise, often require intrusive external aids (e.g., noise‐canceling headphones) that may interfere with social communication, while tactile stimuli are constant due to the necessity of wearing clothing and engaging in daily self‐care routines (MacLennan et al. 2022). According to predictive processing frameworks, predictable stimuli should evoke decreased neural responses as oncoming stimuli are conforming to the expected percept (Auksztulewicz and Friston 2016; Summerfield et al. 2008). Some accounts also suggest that more predictable environments can reduce SOR by minimizing unexpected sensory input and promoting a sense of control (Neil et al. 2016; Unwin et al. 2022). However, if individuals with SOR have deficits in predictive coding, they may struggle to extract or utilize environmental regularities, limiting the potential benefits of certain cues in facilitating accurate prediction (Lawson et al. 2017).

To directly test these hypotheses, we examined the role of prediction cues in the sensory processing of mildly aversive auditory and tactile stimuli in adolescents and young adults with ASD. Specifically, we compared brain responses to Predictable (cued) and Unpredictable (uncued) stimuli in individuals with ASD and in age‐ and sex‐matched TD controls. We considered two potentially competing hypotheses: (1) Predictable environments may reduce sensory responsiveness in ASD (Boulter et al. 2014; De Domenico et al. 2024; Wigham et al. 2015). Based on this, we would expect both groups to show reduced brain responses in the Predictable compared to the Unpredictable condition (i.e., expectancy suppression), but we would expect greater reductions in the ASD group if they over‐react more strongly in the Unpredictable condition and then show greater effects of predictability on dampening these SOR responses in the Predictable condition. (2) Alternatively, if predictive coding is affected in ASD, individuals may have difficulty using predictive cues to attenuate their responses, leading to greater reductions in the TD compared to the ASD group in the Predictable compared to the Unpredictable condition. In addition, we tested the relationship between expectancy suppression and SOR within the ASD group. We predicted that SOR would be associated with reduced expectancy suppression, suggesting a role for predictive processing mechanisms in sensory processing heterogeneity within autism.

Methods

Participants

Participants included adolescents and young adults with ASD (N = 65) or TD (N = 37), aged 13–27 years. Five ASD and two TD participants were excluded due to excessive motion, and two ASD were excluded due to data acquisition issues. Three ASD and seven TD participants were excluded because their siblings were in the sample. The final sample included 55 ASD (30 female) and 28 TD (16 female) participants aged 13.88–27.5 (mean = 20.59 years). See Table 1 for demographic information.

Behavioral Measures

We used self‐report on the Sensory Processing 3‐Dimensional (SP3‐D; Mulligan et al. 2019) Inventory to measure SOR. The SP3‐D asks responders to indicate stimuli they are over‐responsive to, under‐responsive to, or seek out. For the SOR items, participants are asked to rate on a Likert scale of 0–5 the extent to which each stimulus bothers them. In the current study, we obtained an SOR total score by summing the Likert‐scale ratings across all auditory, visual, and tactile SOR items, yielding a continuous measure of SOR. Directly after the sensory fMRI scan, participants also reported how much they were bothered by each stimulus and how much they wanted each stimulus to stop. The ratings ranged from 0 (not at all) to 10 (extremely), and the responses were averaged to obtain one sensory rating per participant. Sensory ratings were not obtained from one TD participant, and thus ratings were available for 55 ASD and 27 TD participants.

Sensory Paradigm

Participants were presented with concurrent mildly aversive tactile and auditory stimuli in the fMRI scanner. The multimodal sensory stimulation varied between four types of auditory stimuli (helicopter, firetruck, construction, or school bell sounds) and four types of mildly aversive tactile stimuli (a toothbrush or sponge rubbed on the left palm or forearm). The tactile and auditory stimuli used were adapted from prior studies, in which mildly aversive stimuli were selected based on pilot testing in neurotypical adults and were subsequently reported as mildly aversive by both neurotypical and ASD groups (Hernandez et al. 2020; Jung et al. 2021). We validated the mildly aversive nature of the stimuli in the current sample by obtaining sensory ratings (see Section 3.1). To modulate the expectancy of the sensory stimuli, we compared two trial types: Unpredictable trials, where stimulation occurred without prior visual cues (Figure 1A), and Predictable trials, which were preceded by an image indicating the stimuli to be applied (Figure 1B). The variability in the specific sensory stimuli presented was intended to increase the unpredictability of stimulation in the Unpredictable condition. Sensory stimuli were presented in a pseudorandom combination and order in each block to ensure a balanced distribution of stimuli across the two conditions. There were four blocks containing five trials each of the same condition (Predictable or Unpredictable) for a total of 20 trials. A block design was employed to induce an overall state of predictability or unpredictability in each block. The conditions alternated (i.e., Predictable followed by Unpredictable and so forth) and the order of conditions as well as the order of the specific stimulus pairings presented for Predictable versus Unpredictable were counterbalanced among participants. Rest periods in which participants fixated on a crosshair took place between each trial, and varied between 8 and 12 s to increase the unpredictability of the stimuli in the Unpredictable condition. In contrast, Predictable stimuli always followed directly after the 2‐s visual prediction cue.

MRI Acquisition and Analysis

A Siemens Prisma 3‐T MRI scanner was used to acquire MRI data. The following parameters were used to acquire each functional run using an EPI multiband acquisition: TR = 800 ms, TE = 37 ms, FOV = 208 mm, voxel size = 2 mm3, 72 slices, multiband acceleration factor = 8. Stimuli were presented using E‐prime 2 (Psychology Software Tools Inc., Sharpsburg, PA, USA). Auditory stimuli were presented with noise‐canceling, MR‐compatible headphones to minimize the noise from the scanner (OptoAcoustic), and visual stimuli were presented via a projection screen. Tactile stimuli consisted of a toothbrush and a sponge attached to wooden dowels, allowing the experimenter to apply the stimuli to the participant's palm or forearm without direct physical contact. Strokes were administered at a standardized rate of 1 stroke per second, guided by a countdown timer. To ensure consistency in pressure, the experimenter underwent extensive reliability training, with performance verified by a trained research assistant prior to each session. A T1‐weighted high‐resolution magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo scan (MPRAGE; TR = 2500 ms, TI = 1000 ms, TE = 1.81/3.6/5.39/7.18 ms, FOV = 256 mm, voxel size = 0.8 mm3, 208 slices) was acquired for use in registration.

Analyses were conducted using the FMRIB Software Library (FSL), version 6.0.7.4 (Smith et al. 2004). Preprocessing steps included skull stripping, motion correction to the mean image, spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel full width at half maximum of 5 mm, and high‐pass temporal filtering (cutoff = 90s). Functional data were linearly registered to a common stereotaxic space by aligning to the in‐plane T1 image (6 degrees of freedom) then to the MNI152 T1 2 mm template (12 degrees of freedom).

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using FSL's fMRI Expert Analysis Tool (FEAT). Fixed‐effects models were run for each subject individually with 12 motion parameters as covariates. Volumes that were identified as significant motion outliers using fsl_motion_outliers were covaried out through regression analysis. Then, a higher‐level mixed effects model was used to examine within‐ and between‐group differences. Mean absolute motion differed significantly between groups (p = 0.045), and although mean age did not differ significantly (p = 0.11), the age range was different between groups (ASD range = 13–27; TD range = 13–23), thus motion and age were included as covariates in all between‐group analyses (Table 1).

We first examined neural activation to the Predictable and Unpredictable stimulation by modeling activity in each condition with respect to fixation, as well as directly comparing the two conditions. We also examined neural activity during the prediction image cue, a visual image that preceded sensory stimulation in the Predictable condition.

Higher‐level group analyses were conducted using FSL's local analysis of mixed‐effects state (Smith et al. 2004). Results were thresholded at z > 2.3 and cluster corrected for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05). For between‐condition and between‐group contrasts, we applied a posthoc mask using regions that showed significant activation compared to rest in the within‐condition/within‐group contrasts at a liberal threshold (z > 1.7; p < 0.05) to facilitate interpretation of effects as driven by greater activation by the condition or group of interest rather than deactivation in the compared condition or group.

Next, we evaluated the correlation of SOR with neural activity in each condition and the between‐condition contrast. As in prior work (e.g., Than et al. 2024), we limited this analysis to the ASD group. This is because SOR was significantly higher in this group and had more clinical relevance given that SOR scores were significantly associated with subjective sensory ratings of the fMRI stimuli for the ASD but not TD group (see Section 3.1). We used bottom‐up regression analyses with demeaned SOR total score as the independent variable to determine how SOR correlated with activation across the whole brain. Analyses were thresholded at z > 2.3, p < 0.05.

Results

Behavioral Results

As expected, SOR was significantly higher in the ASD (M = 109.22, SD = 28.94) than the TD (M = 73.96, SD = 25.79) group (t(81) = 5.44, p < 0.001; see Table 1). Sensory ratings of the actual stimuli did not significantly differ between the ASD (M = 3.85, SD = 2.50) and TD (M = 3.36, SD = 1.95) groups (t(80) = 0.878, p = 0.383), indicating that both groups found the stimuli mildly aversive on average. Sensory ratings and SOR were significantly correlated in the ASD group (r = 0.49, p < 0.001), suggesting that within the ASD group, responses to the sensory stimuli used in the task were related to their real‐world sensory experiences. This was not the case for the TD group (r = 0.16, p = 0.42).

fMRI Results

We first examined neural responses to sensory stimulation in each condition (Unpredictable and Predictable) in the ASD and TD groups separately (Tables 2 and 3; Figure 2). Next, we contrasted brain activity in the two conditions in each group (Tables 4 and 5; Figure 3). Then, within the ASD group only, we examined the correlation between SOR and neural responses to sensory stimulation in each condition and the between‐condition contrast (Table 6; Figure 4). Finally, we examined brain activity in both groups during the prediction image cue (Table 7; Figure 5).

Neural Responses to Sensory Stimulation During the Unpredictable Condition

Comparing neural activity during sensory stimulation in the Unpredictable condition to fixation, both groups displayed activation in the cerebellum (primarily in left Lobules V–VII), bilateral primary auditory cortices and association regions, and bilateral sensorimotor cortices. The ASD group also activated the bilateral lateral occipital and dorsomedial prefrontal cortices. Between‐group comparisons found that the ASD group showed significantly greater activation compared to the TD group in bilateral visual cortices, left sensorimotor cortex, and bilateral sensory association regions including middle temporal gyrus, supplementary motor cortex, and superior parietal regions, as well as in the left superior frontal gyrus (Figure 2; Table 2). There were no regions where brain activity was greater in the TD group compared to the ASD group.

Neural Responses to Sensory Stimulation During the Predictable Condition

During sensory stimulation in the Predictable condition, both groups activated the bilateral cerebellum (Lobule VI), primary auditory cortices, and association regions, sensorimotor regions, thalamus, and basal ganglia as compared to fixation. The ASD group also showed activation in the bilateral frontal cortex, including the frontal pole and superior frontal gyrus, spanning both dorsolateral and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dlPFC/dmPFC). When directly comparing groups, the ASD group showed significantly greater activation than the TD group only in the left precentral gyrus (Figure 2; Table 3). Similarly to the Unpredictable condition, there were no regions where brain activity was greater in the TD group compared to the ASD group.

Comparisons of the Unpredictable and Predictable Conditions

In both groups, there was higher activity in the cerebellar lobule VI during the Unpredictable condition compared to the Predictable condition. In the ASD group, there was also higher activity in bilateral auditory cortices, right precentral gyrus, and right paracingulate gyrus (Figure 3; Table 4).

Areas with greater activation in the Predictable condition compared to the Unpredictable condition included multiple cerebellar regions, with a peak in crus II, bilateral caudate, left postcentral gyrus, and visual association cortex in both groups. In the ASD group, there was also significantly greater activation in bilateral sensorimotor cortex, visual cortex (temporal occipital fusiform cortex), frontal pole, and middle and superior frontal gyri (dlPFC/dmPFC; Figure 3; Table 5). However, there were no significant group‐by‐condition interactions (see the Supporting Information for a figure illustrating why no group‐by‐condition interaction was observed).

Associations of SOR With Neural Responses to Predictable and Unpredictable Sensory Stimulation in the ASD Group

SOR correlations with neural activity were examined within the ASD group. There was a positive association between SOR and activity in the right cerebellum (crus I) during the Unpredictable condition. There was a positive association between SOR and activity in the bilateral sensorimotor cortices and left prefrontal cortex during the Predictable condition. SOR was also correlated with higher activity in the right somatosensory cortex, auditory cortex, and central opercular cortex during the Predictable condition contrasted with the Unpredictable condition (Figure 4; Table 6). There were no regions where brain activation was negatively correlated with SOR.

Neural Responses to the Prediction Image Cue

Both groups exhibited activity in bilateral frontal and visual cortices, as well as the precentral gyri during the visual prediction image cue, which preceded sensory stimulation in the Predictable condition, compared to fixation. The ASD group showed additional within‐group activation in several regions, including the auditory cortices and subcortical regions such as the bilateral hippocampi and thalami. However, between‐group comparisons revealed that the TD group demonstrated significantly greater activation in higher‐order visual processing regions, specifically the bilateral lingual gyri and right lateral occipital cortex (Figure 5, Table 7).

Discussion

Sensory processing challenges such as SOR are prevalent in ASD, yet the mechanisms underlying SOR remain poorly understood. Recent theoretical accounts propose that differences in predictive processing may contribute to sensory symptoms in ASD, but few studies have examined how these mechanisms relate to SOR. In this study, we investigated the relationship between predictive and sensory processing by manipulating the expectancy of mildly aversive multisensory stimuli and measuring brain responses using fMRI in individuals with ASD and TD controls. We tested two competing hypotheses. First, if individuals with ASD have altered predictive processing, they might not benefit from prediction cues, leading us to expect a greater reduction of responses (i.e., expectancy suppression) in the TD compared to the ASD group for Predictable compared to Unpredictable stimuli. Alternatively, if the ASD group shows particular sensory over‐reactivity to Unpredictable stimuli, they might show greater benefits from prediction cues and thus show greater reduction of neural sensory responses for Predictable compared to Unpredictable stimuli. Overall, we found evidence that both mechanisms might be involved in atypical sensory responses in ASD. Notably, the ASD group as a whole showed a pattern more consistent with Predictable stimuli causing less hyper‐reactivity, with fewer diagnostic group differences in this condition. However, within the ASD group, SOR was positively associated with increased responses to Predictable compared to Unpredictable stimuli in the relevant sensory cortices, suggesting that individual differences in sensory processing in ASD may be linked to reduced expectancy suppression.

In general, across conditions, we found greater activation in the ASD group compared to the TD group and increased activation with increased SOR in the ASD group. Specifically, the ASD group showed sensory cortical hyper‐activation compared to the TD group in the Unpredictable condition, consistent with prior fMRI research on neural sensory hyper‐activity in autism, in which sensory stimuli are generally uncued (e.g., Green et al. 2013, 2015, 2019). This hyperactivity was particularly evident in left sensorimotor cortex, higher‐order auditory cortical regions, and bilateral visual cortices. This pattern of hyper‐activation in unrelated sensory regions (i.e., visual and ipsilateral sensorimotor cortex) is consistent with prior research (Green et al. 2019; Jao Keehn et al. 2017) and could reflect decreased inhibition resulting in a lack of sensory processing specificity. In contrast, there were minimal diagnostic group differences in the Predictable condition, where the ASD group showed a small region of greater activation in the left sensorimotor cortex. The relatively minimal group differences in the Predictable condition suggest that the visual cues might reduce some of the hyper‐reactivity seen in the ASD group in the Unpredictable condition. These results are consistent with a model in which unexpected sensory stimuli cause a more heightened SOR response in autism and suggest that interventions that make an environment more Predictable could reduce unpleasant sensory experiences in some individuals with ASD.

When directly contrasting brain activity between the Predictable and Unpredictable conditions, we observed that each condition engaged partially distinct neural networks. We found greater activation in stimuli‐relevant regions during Unpredictable trials, particularly, in the ASD group, where bilateral auditory cortices and right sensorimotor cortex showed greater activation compared to the Predictable condition. We also found greater activation in the bilateral cerebellar lobule VI in both groups, which is consistent with prior studies showing this subregion of the cerebellum is connected with sensorimotor cortex and encodes sensory prediction errors (Cakar et al. 2024; Schlerf et al. 2012; Stoodley and Schmahmann 2018). Its recruitment alongside sensorimotor cortex in our data suggests that cerebellar‐sensorimotor circuits are jointly engaged when processing Unpredictable sensory events and could reflect the cerebellum updating an internal model based on unexpected stimuli (Schlerf et al. 2012).

In contrast to our initial hypothesis that predictability would attenuate neural activity, several brain regions showed greater activation during Predictable trials, including visual association cortex, thalamus, basal ganglia, and crus II of the cerebellum. Broadly, this activity might reflect top‐down modulation involved in reconciling the initial visual image with the following auditory and tactile stimulation. Activity in visual association cortex could be related to maintaining the representation of the prediction image cue (Ramesh et al. 2018). Greater thalamic activity may reflect multisensory integration between the preceding visual prediction image cue and the oncoming auditory and tactile stimulation (Tyll et al. 2011). The basal ganglia may be engaged in sensory expectation or evaluation of the ongoing stimuli relative to what was expected (Colder 2015). We also saw activity in cerebellar crus II, known for its connectivity with prefrontal regions and role in cognitive functions like working memory, further supporting the idea that predictability engages higher‐order modeling and internal monitoring processes (Küper et al. 2016; Stoodley and Schmahmann 2018). Interestingly, both groups also showed increased activation in the left primary somatosensory cortex, ipsilateral to stimulation. Such ipsilateral activity is unexpected during unilateral arm/hand stimulation. Its significant engagement in the Predictable condition may reflect a mismatch response: because the predictive image cue was not side‐specific, participants may have anticipated stimulation on either arm, leading to a surprise signal when the right arm was not stimulated (van Ede et al. 2014). In the ASD group specifically, we observed additional regions of greater activation during Predictable trials in frontal cortex, including dorsolateral and dorsomedial PFC. These ASD‐specific patterns may reflect differences in how predictability is processed—potentially involving greater recruitment of regulatory networks or compensatory activity such as increased effort to reconcile the visual cues with sensory stimulation (Belmonte and Yurgelun‐Todd 2003). This is consistent with previous research showing that ASD youth who engage top‐down regulatory mechanisms show reduced neural hyper‐reactivity during sensory stimulation and lower levels of parent‐reported SOR (Green et al. 2015, 2019). Overall, these findings point toward a multilevel predictive processing framework where anticipation does not just reduce neural activity but also orients it toward confirmatory, evaluative, and regulative processing across sensory, subcortical, and cognitive brain regions (Bubic et al. 2010).

In addition to examining group‐level effects of predictability, we examined the relationship between individual differences in SOR and neural responses within the ASD group. We found that individuals with higher SOR exhibited greater activation in somatosensory and auditory cortices during Predictable compared to Unpredictable trials, suggesting reduced expectancy suppression. These findings directly support the hypothesis that SOR is associated with reduced attenuation of neural responses to expected stimuli. These individuals might struggle to generate or apply accurate sensory predictions, such as mapping a visual cue (e.g., a helicopter image) onto its associated sound (Van de Cruys et al. 2014). As a result, Predictable stimuli may elicit heightened neural responses.

Since predictive deficits may arise not only from disrupted sensory attenuation but also from difficulties in forming predictions (Press et al. 2020), we next examined neural responses to the prediction image cue. Both groups showed bilateral frontal and visual cortex activity. Interestingly, the TD group showed significantly greater activation in higher‐order visual processing regions. These differences may reflect group differences in attention or neural effort and may be related to reduced effectiveness of using the cues to accurately predict the oncoming sensory stimuli in the ASD group (Khachouf et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2005; Orekhova and Stroganova 2014; Zhao et al. 2022). For example, the TD group might be interpreting these prediction images more efficiently or be better able to map them to the actual stimuli.

In line with these findings, one limitation of this study is that we cannot differentiate between autistic individuals with SOR having overall reduced expectancy suppression versus having difficulty using these prediction cues which require mapping visual images onto tactile/auditory stimuli. Such representational cues might recruit mechanisms that differ from those underlying repetition‐based expectancy suppression, which has been the predominant paradigm for investigating sensory predictions (though see Todorovic et al. 2011). At the same time, this approach mirrors real‐world predictive demands, which often involve constructing mental models from cross‐modal cues and updating them when confronted with counterfactual input (Clark 2013). Future studies directly comparing cue‐based and repetition‐based paradigms will be critical for teasing apart distinct forms of prediction‐related attenuation (Cannon et al. 2021). Future studies should also examine neural responses to stimuli that vary in predictability more subtly, such as through learned associations (Utzerath et al. 2018), as these may lead to greater group‐by‐predicted condition interaction effects (Angeletos Chrysaitis and Seriès 2023).

Despite these limitations, a key strength of the study is its use of ecologically valid, multimodal stimuli that closely resemble real‐world sensory experiences. This design enhances the translational relevance of our findings, informs potential strategies for reducing SOR in everyday contexts, and begins to fill critical gaps in the literature. The lack of significant group differences in subjective sensory ratings suggests that the aversiveness of the stimuli was reported similarly across participants. These similar ratings serve as a methodological strength, suggesting that the observed neural variations are likely driven by the manipulation of expectancy rather than being confounded by baseline differences in the perceived intensity or unpleasantness of the stimuli. The potential contradiction between group differences in the neural data but similar average ratings of the stimuli could reflect differences in subjective rating strategy. For example, participants might rate stimuli relative to their own internal scale of low to high—if all stimuli are more aversive for autistic participants compared to neurotypical participants, a “5” on the autistic scale could reflect a much more aversive experience than the “5” on the neurotypical scale. This theory is consistent with the fact that the stimulus ratings in the autistic group were significantly correlated with their self‐reported SOR scores, which were much higher than those in the TD group. In other words, a “5” rating did indeed map onto much higher real‐world sensory reactivity for the ASD group.

Although theoretical accounts have proposed that predictive processing differences contribute to sensory symptoms in ASD (Pellicano and Burr 2012), few studies have directly examined this in relation to SOR. By using a cue‐based expectancy manipulation, our findings highlight the potential contribution of top‐down prediction mechanisms to sensory responses—complementing prior work focused on RS and habituation (Ewbank et al. 2017; Utzerath et al. 2018). Individual differences in traits such as SOR may help explain mixed findings in the literature examining predictive processing in ASD (Angeletos Chrysaitis and Seriès 2023; Cannon et al. 2021). Importantly, the association between SOR and reduced expectancy suppression in our study suggests that predictive processing differences may not be universal across individuals with ASD but instead more pronounced in those with elevated sensory reactivity. These results have implications for developing therapeutic strategies for addressing unpleasant sensory experiences in autistic individuals. Presenting individuals with a visual representation of upcoming sensory stimulation altered sensory processing in both TD and ASD groups, with anticipation serving not only to reduce sensory cortical activity but also to reorient processing toward top‐down reconciliation of cues and stimuli and, for autistic individuals, prefrontal cortical‐driven regulation. These findings indicate that predictive mechanisms in autism cannot be captured by a single model of deficit or intact processing, and that the efficacy of predictive cues as interventions may depend on individual SOR severity. One approach is to help individuals mentally prepare for upcoming sensory stimulation using visual schedules (Knight et al. 2015), though individuals with higher SOR may require prediction cues that map more closely onto the anticipated stimulus and extra practice connecting cues to expected events.
