Clostridium perfringens infection following open globe injury due to firecracker blast: A rare case report from Southern India
Authors: Sangitha Jayagandan, Mary Stephen, Sudarsan Bagavane, Rakhi Biswas, Amit Kumar Deb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeOpen globe injuries [OGIs] are ocular emergencies which can lead to visual impairment, especially when associated with infection.MethodsThis case report presents a rare case of Clostridium perfringens isolated from the eye following a firecracker injury in a 38-year-old man.ResultsThe patient underwent evisceration as the globe tissue was friable and could not be salvaged and promptly started on antibiotics. Anaerobic culture of a foreign body removed during surgery showed growth of C. perfringensConclusionC. perfringens, an anaerobic bacterium, is a rare but highly virulent ocular pathogen that can cause fulminant endophthalmitis, which may progress to panophthalmitis and require globe removal.Keywords: Open globe injury, Clostridium perfringens, Intraocular foreign body, Firecracker injury, Endophthalmitis, Evisceration, Anaerobic ocular infection

Introduction

In India, monocular blindness is primarily caused by ocular trauma, with reported incidence rates ranging from 4.5% to 7.5% [1]. The resulting work hours lost put both the patient and their family under significant financial strain. At an individual level, this adversely affects physical and mental health. Herein, we report a case of post-traumatic open globe injury that was managed surgically. Microbiological evaluation of the removed foreign body revealed growth of Clostridium perfringens.

Case description

A 38-year-old male, who was non-diabetic and non-hypertensive patient, was admitted to the Department of Ophthalmology at a tertiary care hospital in the southern part of India. He sustained an open globe injury to the right eye following an accidental firecracker explosion. The patient developed sudden onset, painful diminution of vision in the right eye. On ophthalmological evaluation, the left eye had a visual acuity of 6/6 [Snellen’s chart] with brisk pupillary response, while the right eye had no perception of light. Slit lamp examination revealed a disorganised globe with full-thickness vertically oriented limbus to limbus laceration with scleral tear and uveal tissue prolapse extending posteriorly, along with retained foreign body material (Fig. 1). The anterior segment details could not be appreciated. The patient was diagnosed with right eye globe injury - Type D, Zone I, II & II, Grade V [2].

Image showing an open globe injury of the right eye (Left side) and post-operative picture of the right eye following evisceration (Right side).

On computed tomography (CT) scan [1 mm cut], the contour of the right eye globe was lost, with the presence of an ill-defined hyperdense lesion suggestive of an intraocular foreign body (IOFB). There was no evidence of orbital wall fracture or traumatic discontinuity of the optic nerve.

The patient underwent surgery on the same day. Intraoperatively, it was noted that the laceration extended posteriorly from the limbus into the sclera both superiorly and inferiorly, with the posterior-most extent non-determinable. Multiple black particles, likely firecracker debris, were found within the globe, along with complete extrusion of the vitreous and uveal tissue through the wound. Attempts at wound closure were unsuccessful. The patient subsequently underwent evisceration, as the globe was not salvageable due to friable ocular tissue and the extent of injury. The intraocular contents were sent for microbiological evaluation. Following surgery, the patient received a five-day course of intravenous (IV) ciprofloxacin 400 mg twice daily along with analgesics and anti-edema measures. At follow-up, the patient’s right socket was healthy with no signs of infection at the follow-up (Fig. 1), and the left eye was normal. Rehabilitation was later planned with an orbital implant with a prosthetic shell.

The foreign body removed during surgery was received in the Department of Microbiology for aerobic, anaerobic, and fungal culture. The Gram-stained smear of the sample showed gram-positive bacilli without pus cells (Fig. 2). On aerobic culture of the sample, Bacillus cereus was isolated. Fungal culture and potassium hydroxide (KOH) wet mount showed no fungal elements. C. perfringens was isolated on anaerobic culture and was further identified in the laboratory with a positive Reverse CAMP test, opalescence on egg yolk agar and esculin hydrolysis on bile esculin agar (Fig. 2). The isolate was susceptible to both meropenem [10 µg] and metronidazole [5 µg] and resistant to clindamycin [2 µg]. Complete blood counts, renal function tests and electrolytes were within normal limits.

Discussion

Open globe injury [OGI] has enormous financial repercussions, along with a negative impact on the patient's physical and mental health. Occupational eye injuries continue to be the most frequent cause of ocular injuries in rural India, where they are most common in agriculture, carpentry, chiselling, and hammering sectors [1]. Another important cause of OGIs in both adults and children is firecracker-related injuries, especially during Deepavali, the festival of lights that is celebrated in India [1].

Clostridium species, although being uncommon ocular pathogens, are notorious for causing fulminant panophthalmitis, particularly in the presence of IOFB following soil-contaminated eye injuries. Rarely, they may also cause endogenous endophthalmitis secondary to gastrointestinal perforation [3]. Despite its rarity, C. perfringens eye infection has been associated with keratitis, acute abdominal pain, IV drug use, cataract surgery, penetrating keratoplasty, and keratitis [4]. A few rare cases of clostridial ocular infection have also been reported following intravitreal anti–vascular endothelial growth factor injections and in association with colorectal carcinoma [4], [5]. Dave et al., conducted a retrospective interventional case series of seven patients with culture-proven clostridial endophthalmitis from January 2005 to March 2018. They reported 75% cases included in the study were post-OGIs, 12.5% post-trabeculectomy, 12.5% post-intravitreal injection [6].

Gram-positive and gram-negative microorganisms both have the potential to cause post-traumatic endophthalmitis. Reports of fungal and polymicrobial infections have also been documented. Due to their aerobic nature, the common gram-positive endophthalmitis-causing microorganism, such as Staphylococcus epidermidis and Bacillus cereus, creates a relatively low-oxygen environment that is favourable for the growth of anaerobic organisms like C. perfringens [3], [7]. The other common species that cause endophthalmitis and panophthalmitis in humans is C. septicum [5], [6].

In our case, the patient had a globe injury following a firecracker burst and the foreign body removed during surgery on anaerobic culture grew C. perfringens and also on aerobic culture grew Bacillus cereus, a coinfection which may or may not be directly involved in the pathogenesis of eye infection of our patient.

C. perfringens is a ubiquitous gram-positive anaerobic bacterium most commonly found in soil, faeces, decaying vegetation and part of the normal gastrointestinal microbiota of humans and animals [8]. The organism typically gains access to the ocular region following a penetrating eye injury, often associated with a retained IOFB [5], [9]. Toxin production is a major virulence factor that contributes to the pathophysiology of C. perfringens infection [8]. Historically, toxins [beta, epsilon, iota, and alpha toxins] have been used to identify five toxinotypes [A–G]. Later, two other toxinotypes [F and G] were included, adding enterotoxin CPE and NetB toxin, respectively, to the scheme. In addition, C. perfringens produces numerous degradative enzymes, including hydrolytic enzymes, membrane-damaging enzymes, pore-forming toxins, and intracellular toxins such as proteases, collagenase, hyaluronidase, and endoglycosidases [10].

Potent exotoxins secreted by C. perfringens cause necrosis of ocular tissues, resulting in extensive ocular destruction. Once the infection is established, it is highly unlikely that antibiotics alone can halt this process, which often culminates in fulminant endophthalmitis and subsequently panophthalmitis [7]. Clinical features of C. perfringens endophthalmitis include rapid onset, severe pain, early rise in intraocular pressure, blood- or coffee coloured discharge along with gas bubbles in the anterior chamber, eyelid edema, and vision loss [4]. With rare exceptions, almost all reported cases demonstrate an aggressive clinical course and progress to evisceration or enucleation despite treatment, as observed in our case report [7].

C. perfringens is a gram-positive, rod-shaped anaerobic bacterium that typically shows a double zone of β hemolysis [Target hemolysis] on blood agar, which may be enhanced by exposure to cold. Unlike other Clostridium species, C. perfringens is reverse CAMP test positive. The organism produces opalescence around the streak line in egg yolk agar due to the lecithinase activity of the α toxin [Fig. 2] [11].

Accurate identification of the IOFB following penetrating open globe injury is essential for optimal management and surgical planning. However, detection can be challenging in certain cases, particularly with the increasing use of plastic materials in projectile objects, which may pose a growing clinical challenge [12]. In our case, an IOFB was noted on the CT scan. Multimodal imaging should be considered in patients with a high index of suspicion for an IOFB, even when it is not initially detected on CT [12].

Routine antibiotic therapy is often insufficient to prevent the fulminant clinical course of C. perfringens endophthalmitis. Systemic, subconjunctival, and intravitreal antibiotics have frequently failed to halt disease progression. In the majority of cases, infection control ultimately necessitates evisceration or enucleation [9]. A better visual outcome is more likely with early intervention. Conversely, delayed presentation or treatment may result in persistent inflammation, secondary infection, and irreversible structural damage to the eye. If endophthalmitis develops due to delayed management, the prognosis worsens significantly and surgical intervention becomes unavoidable [1].

Conclusion

Prevention is more crucial than treatment in cases of ocular injuries. It is important to note that open-globe injuries are ophthalmic emergencies that require prompt evaluation and management to prevent complications such as infection and loss of vision. Although rare, C. perfringens is an aggressive anaerobic ocular pathogen. Unless there is a high degree of clinical suspicion, it may remain undetected if anaerobic cultures are not specifically requested. This leads to a delay in timely intervention, which further complicates the patient’s clinical progression and treatment outcome. In this case, the patient underwent surgery on the same day to manage the injury. Antibiotics were administered to prevent infection, and the patient was closely monitored for signs of infection at follow-up visits. Evisceration was performed after attempts at wound closure proved unsuccessful. Rehabilitation measures, including placement of an orbital implant with a prosthetic shell, were planned to improve the patient’s quality of life.
