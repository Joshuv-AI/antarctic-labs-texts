Do opportunities exist to improve the use of the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist in cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery? Perspectives of patients and staff in a multimethod study in New Zealand
Authors: Tanisha Jowsey, Kim Ward, Arul Baradi, Derryn Gargiulo, Kaylene Henderson, Simon J Mitchell, Matthew Moore, Jithendra Somaratne, Alan Forbes Merry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (SSC) can improve patient outcomes through checks and enhancing the safety climate, provided the team engage. Patients are central to that team and may be awake during part or all of many procedures. Their greater inclusion in the SSC process could enhance its checking objectives by using patient knowledge of details relevant to proposed procedures, and its objective of improving culture by emphasising patient identity, seeing patients as individuals rather than items on a production line. We aimed to evaluate SSC use, including clinicians’ and patients’ perspectives, in cardiothoracic operating rooms (CTORs) and cardiac catheterisation laboratories (CCLs), and to identify opportunities to refine its use in these spaces.DesignWe undertook a multimethod study based on positivism and interpretivism, with evaluation of SSC administration and interviews. We evaluated SSC administration during 20 Sign Ins, 20 Time Outs and 20 Sign Outs in CTORs and 20 Time Outs in CCLs (11 coronary angiography and 9 electrophysiology cases) using the WHO Behaviourally Anchored Rating Scale (WHOBARS, 1–7, where higher scores indicate better practice). We additionally interviewed 10 clinicians and 17 patients about their experiences and perceptions of safety and SSC use. We undertook thematic analysis of interview data.SettingCTORs and CCLs in an Auckland public hospital.ParticipantsWe observed 171 clinicians during 20 cases. We interviewed 10 clinicians (Pakeha/New Zealand/European n=6; male n=5; doctor n=5). We interviewed 17 patients (Pakeha/New Zealand/European n=12; male n=10; age range 45–81 years).ResultsIn CTORs, the SSC was used in full: median (IQR) WHOBARS score was 5.9 (5.2, 6.5). In CCLs, its use differed between electrophysiology and angiography CCLs: median (IQR) WHOBARS score was 4.50 (3.2, 5.6). In both CTORs and CCLs, high levels of carefulness and respect between team members were observed. Clinicians described the SSC as valuable and identified context-related reasons for variations in its use. Patients wanted to feel cared for and respected (including culturally). The SSC contributed positively to this. Clinicians expressed reservations about increasing patients’ involvement with the SSC. However, patients’ comments supported their increased engagement in the SSC process (including establishing their preferred names).ConclusionsThe SSC is used at Auckland City Hospital with some variation between services and with high levels of carefulness and respect. The SSC increased patients’ sense of being cared for. With modification (eg, by verifying their preferred name during the Sign In phase), the SSC could enhance patients’ perception of being seen as individuals, their cultural safety and the safety climate in operating and procedure rooms, and hence potentially improve patient outcomes. Our findings reinforce the value of regular evaluation and context-relevant modification of SSC for its effective use.

Introduction

The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (SSC) is a tool to minimise errors and improve communication and teamwork.1 Its use can improve patient outcomes after surgical, cardiological and other interventional procedures through enhanced checking and improving the safety climate in operating and procedure rooms, but only with effective implementation and sustained engagement of the surgical teams.15 This requires regular training.6 Additionally, adaptations and modifications to fit local practice are encouraged in its Adaptation Guide.7 Thus, the use of the SSC should be periodically evaluated and modifications made as indicated.8

Auckland City Hospital (ACH, a large public hospital in Auckland, New Zealand) was one of eight sites in the original study of the SSC.1 Routine use of the SSC has extended to all operating rooms and some procedure rooms, including those for cardiology procedures. Several iterations of review and implementations of improvement in its use have been undertaken for most surgical services,915 but not for cardiothoracic surgery or cardiology. This reflects the fact that cardiothoracic surgery and cardiology are undertaken in dedicated (but separated) operating rooms on different floors from other surgical services at ACH. These operations and procedures are provided by a distinct, specialised team of clinicians, with mutual support and some overlap of members between these two services (notably of anaesthetists and anaesthetic technicians in instances, for example, whereby it becomes necessary to proceed from a cardiology procedure to surgery or if cardiology expertise is required to inform decisions during cardiac surgery). Furthermore, we have not previously investigated the perspectives of the clinical team members administering the SSC or the patients with whom it is used.

The SSC requires clinicians to confirm with awake patients their identity and the nature of the proposed operation. Beyond this, patient participation is limited even though patients are almost always awake during the Sign In checks. In procedures without general anaesthesia (including coronary angiography), patients are also awake during Time Out and Sign Out. Thus, there may be a missed opportunity to check key facts and points of personal importance with patients. Furthermore, as with any routine process, there is a pervasive risk of the use of the SSC becoming token, and thus ineffective. Interacting directly with patients could potentially address this risk by increasing clinicians’ sense of each patient’s identity and seeing them as a person rather than just another item on a production line. These interactions could also promote safer practice by prompting recall of particular details relevant to each individual patient and by increasing the team’s focus on patient-centred care. Such interaction, done well, could also be reassuring for patients. Conversely, some patients might find some SSC checks alarming (eg, if the need for blood or difficulties with the airway are anticipated). However, we could find no previous studies concerning patients’ experiences with the use of the SSC.

Therefore, we aimed to evaluate current use of the SSC in cardiac catheterisation laboratories (CCLs) and cardiothoracic operating rooms (CTORs) at ACH through observations, and by interviewing patients and clinical team members. Our objective was to identify context-specific opportunities (if any exist) to improve the use of the SSC at ACH, and to explore the potential for a more patient-centric SCC.

Methods

Study design

This was a multimethod exploratory study, underpinned by both positivism (quantitative data) and interpretivism (qualitative data). We took a pragmatic approach towards generating evidence of current SSC use and SSC-related experiences, and to provide practical recommendations for enhanced SSC use.

Between January 2022 and January 2023, we observed clinical teams using the SSC and then interviewed clinicians and patients who had been observed about their perceptions and experiences of the SSC’s use in CTORs and CCLs. We undertook sequential design, whereby WHO Behaviourally Anchored Rating Scale (WHOBARS) observational data were collected, followed by interviews with clinicians and patients.

Recruitment of clinicians and patients

Following the distribution of verbal and written information about the study, clinicians from the relevant procedural teams were invited to participate in the observations and interviews. Criteria for inclusion in the observations were registered health professionals who worked in CTORs and CCLs at ACH, undertaking elective (non-emergency) and acute procedures. We obtained written or verbal informed consent from 188 clinicians over the two services to be observed. Of these, 118 participants agreed to be contacted and invited to interview. At each observed procedure, patients were informed that an observer would be present and asked if they were willing to be contacted further about the study. From those who agreed, we purposively selected patients of different ages, genders and ethnicities, to reflect as far as possible the diversity within our patient population. TJ contacted patients 2 months after their procedure (to allow recovery and to minimise any potential anxiety they may have had about discussing their experiences) and invited them to participate in a one-off interview with her. KW contacted clinicians and interviewed those who consented to participate. TJ and KW are trained qualitative researchers and clinicians. Written consent was obtained from those who agreed.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design of research priorities, defining research questions or outcome measures, providing input into study design and conduct, dissemination of results or evaluation of studies.

Observational data collection and analysis

Congruent with sample size estimates in previous studies using WHOBARS,16 we observed 20 Sign Ins, 20 Time Outs and 20 Sign Outs in the CTORs. AT ACH, the four CCLs are divided (two each) between angiography and electrophysiology. In angiography, only one set of checks is undertaken at a point corresponding to Time Out, so we observed only (20) Time Outs for the cardiology service (although it became apparent after starting that electrophysiology also undertakes Sign In and Sign Out). We purposively selected elective and acute procedures to include a range of teams and avoid observing individual clinicians more than five times. KH, a clinician experienced in observing SSC use, observed procedures using WHOBARS, a validated behaviourally anchored rating scale for evaluating SSC use over five domains for each of the three checklist phases (ie, Sign In, Time Out or Sign Out).1618 The WHOBARS score for each observed phase was calculated as the mean of the scores for each of the five domains (see figure 1). Numerical ratings and free comments were collected on paper forms, entered into a REDCap (project-redcap.org) database and analysed using R (r-project.org) in RStudio (posit.co). Simple descriptive statistics and graphs were used to present the data. Field notes were summarised.

Interviews and analysis

We designed a semistructured interview guide for clinicians and patients. The questions for both guides were designed to elicit participants’ experiences of use of the SSC in Cardiology and Cardiothoracic Surgery rooms. Interview questions for clinician participants were designed to elicit experiences and perceptions of SSC use generally, rather than specifically exploring the recent WHOBARS observation/s. Interview questions for patient participants were designed to elicit experiences and perceptions of safety and care in their recent WHOBARS-observed procedure. We tested the question guides with representatives of our planned interview participants. The clinician interview guide elicited responses to our overarching questions and was deemed fit for purpose and was used thereafter unchanged. The patient interview guide was modified to explore patient cultural safety (changes are detailed in online supplemental appendix 2). No changes were made to the guide after the start of the study.

We undertook semistructured interviews over the telephone or in person (online supplemental appendix 1 and appendix 2). All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim by an independent transcribing company. KW and TJ checked transcriptions for accuracy, deidentified data and assigned pseudonyms. They then conducted an inductive thematic analysis16 17 following a six-phase approach: (1) familiarising the researcher with data; (2) generating initial codes; (3) searching for themes; (4) reviewing themes; (5) defining and naming themes and (6) producing the report. NVivo V.10 (software program: V.10. QSR International) was used for the clinicians’ interviews, but not for the patients. To ensure rigour during each phase, identified themes were discussed in concert with the research team and revised before producing the final report. All authors reached consensus regarding data excerpts for each theme throughout the write-up process.

Research team: bias, positionality and diversity

The authors include a range of clinical professions: cardiothoracic anaesthesia (AFM), anaesthesia (SJM), anaesthetic technician (KH), cardiology (AB, JS), nursing (KW) and pharmacy (DG). The authors identify with a broad range of ethnicities, including Māori (MM). We also have experience conducting research in the New Zealand context, in which responsiveness to Māori people and alignment with the Treaty of Waitangi are expected. While the whole team has research experience based on positivism, we also had team members trained in interpretivism (TJ and KW) and one Māori researcher (MM). Our diverse views informed our robust discussions about methods, analysis and results.

Results

We begin with a summary of the observer’s field notes to provide context for the formal results. We then report our results from the observations and then from the interviews of clinicians (n=10) and patients (n=17).

Summary of field notes from the observer

Cardiothoracic surgery: Wall-mounted SSC posters were on display in all five CTORs and were used to guide the SSC leader during the three phases. Surgeons and perfusionists, when present for the case, routinely participated in nurse-led Sign Out, sometimes returning to the OR for this. One nurse used a step-stool to ensure her visibility.

Cardiology

Wall-mounted SSC posters were used in the electrophysiology CCLs. The SCC was not on display in the angiography CCLs, where a modified Time Out was conducted by referring to patient admission information in a manner that varied in content depending on the administrator.

In all locations, a high level of respect between team members was noted.

Data from the observations

Our observations included a total of 171 clinicians, most of whom took part in more than one observed case (median (IQR)=2 (1, 3). Eight staff members participated in both cardiothoracic surgical and cardiology cases, and one hybrid case in a CTOR involved teams from both services. Clinicians included 17 senior surgeons, 14 trainee surgeons, 18 anaesthetic technicians, 23 senior anaesthetists, 13 trainee anaesthetists, 7 senior cardiologists, 3 trainee cardiologists, 52 nurses, 11 perfusionists, 10 physiologists, 2 radiographers and 1 radiologist. In the cardiology service, the 20 observed Time Outs included 11 in coronary angiography and 9 in electrophysiology CCLs.

Time Out was conducted in all observed cases. Sign In and Sign Out were also always conducted in CTORs and electrophysiology CCLs, but not in angiography CCLs. The median (IQR) WHOBARS score (out of 7) for the use of the SSC was 5.40 (4.00, 6.25) overall, 5.90 (5.20, 6.50) for cardiothoracic surgery, and 4.50 (3.15, 5.45) for cardiology (see figure 2). Three themes (labelled C1–3) of the interviewed clinicians’ experiences were identified and are described in turn and evidenced with participant excerpts. In addition, some possibilities for improvement of the SSC were identified.

Theme C1: participants viewed the SSC as valuable despite the differences in their locations of work and the clinical needs of the different patient groups they cared for

All participants described the SSC as important for assuring the quality of care and, therefore, ‘really valuable’ (3: junior nurse, cardiology). Participants identified the SSC as ‘straightforward’ and essential for maintaining patient safety by establishing they had ‘the right person’ and procedure (10: anaesthetist). Historically, its introduction was challenging due to ‘a disparaging attitude primarily from the surgeons’ (4: perfusionist). However, its utility improved after 2016, when a rotating leadership was introduced, with surgeons designated to lead the Time Out. Cardiology adopted the SSC later than the CTORs and experienced the same transition from its being viewed with cynicism to a ‘wider acceptance’ (7: cardiologist). The SSC is now viewed as ‘embedded’ and an integral part of OR work (6: anaesthetist). Participants noted the SSC as particularly important for sharing information, especially when information was missing for some individual team members. Clarifying the plan for surgery as part of the checklist process was considered valuable as it allowed for contingency planning. Participants also commented on the importance of open communication and teamwork facilitated by the checklist: “Sometimes the expectation about a surgery is different from what the surgeon or other people have. And I think the Time Out, especially if it’s well-done, makes everybody understand what’s happening. That means everybody” (5: anaesthetist).

Cardiothoracic surgery and cardiology patients were described as having different needs from patients undergoing general surgery, which led to differences in the appropriate content of the SSC. For example, cardiothoracic surgery participants reported thromboprophylaxis as unnecessary for their patients and preventing bleeding as more relevant (1: cardiothoracic surgeon). Participants noted that some items had been added to the SSC, and others had been altered to meet speciality-specific needs, facilitating better SSC use. In CCLs, time was identified as a constraint: “So in an angiogram, you’d probably summarise that 20-check SSC and say he’s diabetic, XYZ in your Time Out. But in other rooms, it’s more of a safety check before the procedure. If there were any issues, you’d highlight it, and then once you’re in the room, the doctor’s Time Out is really ‘this is who he is, this is the procedure we’re doing, this is why. Any considerations? Any concerns?’ That’s it” (8: senior nurse, cardiology). However, the notion of not using the SSC was unpopular with all participants.

Theme C2: engagement during the SSC is variable, causing tension for the team

Although overall commentary about the usefulness of the SSC was positive, engagement during its execution was described as variable, between clinical disciplines (eg, anaesthesia, surgery, perfusion, nursing) and specialty services. Sign In occurred at a busy time, particularly for anaesthetists, and tensions were described for those who tried to initiate the process. Participants also identified Sign In as a part of the process that “we often gloss over” and “where people haven’t hushed” as a point of lower engagement from the whole team (10: anaesthetist; 6: anaesthetist). One participant summarised the observations of others, saying “there are marked differences in the way individuals engage, so to the people that go right through very thoroughly, to the people that are more perfunctory” (4: perfusionist). Another put it more bluntly: “I don’t want to slag just the surgeons off because some of the anaesthetists are just as bad, so yeah. If we could just get them all to play on the same set of swings at the same time, it would be really good” (9: senior nurse, cardiothoracic surgery).

Participants acknowledged that the process was supposed to be shared equally and that everyone was invited to comment. However, participants also described times they felt other team members were ‘going through the motions’ or ‘going through the process’ (3: junior nurse, cardiology; 10: anaesthetist). The variable engagement and commitment to the process, particularly by medical staff, created tensions for some, who reported developing strategies to gain the attention of others in the room, such as calling ‘shoosh’ (6: anaesthetist). Although this worked for those with the confidence to speak up, for others, it was challenging, particularly for some nurses. Indeed, participants noted a power imbalance and described the difficulty nurses had in being heard. One junior nurse described attempts to gain attention, saying “it feels silly [when people aren’t paying attention], you feel a bit silly” (3: junior nurse, cardiology). Another nurse reported, “It’s definitely intimidating for a nurse to have those conversations with a consultant” (8: senior nurse, cardiology). One participant also observed: “I’m yet to hear them [nurses] say ‘we’ve got a particular concern’ or speak up at the Time Out. […] [the surgeons] do actually say ‘perfusion, are you okay?’ They seldom say it to the nurses” (4: perfusionist). An anaesthetist commented on the “slight imbalance of power” stating that in their experience, “if the anaesthetist really supported the process and enabled the nurses to lead it, it went really well” (6: anaesthetist).

Theme C3: clinicians view patients as already having some involvement in the SSC and that further involvement was not needed or would be difficult

Overall, participants described some limited patient involvement in the SSC process but they were not further included because it did not “have much of an impact on them” (8: senior nurse, cardiology). What was already done was considered “enough for the patients” as they were adequately included during the Sign In, while awake, simply by answering the questions put to them as part of administering the SSC (1: surgeon). Examples of when staff involved the patient included letting them know what was happening during the SSC: “I think that it provides reassurance that they have teams who are following procedures to check who they are to get things right. […] Simply by the staff explaining, ‘now we’re going to do this, this is what we do, this is why we do it’, you know. To other patients, it represents bureaucracy and the endless faffing before they finally get to go to sleep and have their operation” (6: anaesthetist).

Participants from the CCLs said that the patients rarely chose—or were invited—to speak out at any point and doubted that there was a need for this: “I perceive that patients who are awake enough appreciate hearing that there’s a checklist going on. Whether there’s any utility in actually actively getting them to participate in things, I don’t know how useful that would be” (7: cardiologist). Asking sedated patients to comment was viewed as potentially intimidating for them, anxiety-provoking or challenging. Although some participants viewed having family or other support people present as a good thing, the logistics and limited resources were perceived as a constraint to their inclusion, along with potential concerns from staff. “I would be very happy to have one or two members of the whānau or family or, with me, no problem. […] Actually, it’s not easy, I agree, we try to do that [in special circumstances]. Though, unfortunately, the environment is not that friendly on this subject” (5: anaesthetist). “My biggest concern with adopting that for everyone would be that we don’t have the staff to then walk that person back to the waiting room” (8: senior nurse, cardiology).

One participant described anxiety over perceived potential aspects of culturally related practices, such as offering a karakia (prayer) in the Māori language for a Māori patient, and resource or time constraints to do this. They supposed “we might draw some cynicism, … often medical staff come from a very scientific background and they may not necessarily engage that well with having to do that” (10: anaesthetist).

Possible improvements to the SSC

Participants described four possible improvements to the SSC: (1) by removing items that were not needed in their clinical context from the SSC, such as thromboprophylaxis in the context of cardiothoracic surgery; (2) by including an item for a contingency plan during Time Out, for the surgeon to outline plans for any anticipated patient-specific adverse events; (3) by using Sign Out as an opportunity to review events that had occurred during the surgery to identify areas of concern or for improvement (1: surgeon; 4: perfusionist); and 4) by taking steps to empower all members of the team to speak up. Generally, participants’ focus was on making it easier for nurses to contribute, for example, by inviting comments from them (and others) and by reframing statements into questions. “A directed, specific question is more likely to elicit a response than a vague, you know, undirected question. So just general encouragement for when people raise ideas, you know, like as opposed to shutting them down and saying thanks or, you know, giving an appropriate response is helpful” (10: anaesthetist). The importance of recognising potential differences in culture with respect to feeling able to speak up was also mentioned: “Some of the cultures are just not [likely to speak up] and they need to be encouraged”. Additionally, if Time Out was also heard by the patient (if awake), there would be potential for the patient to raise concerns if present (9: senior nurse, cardiothoracic surgery).

Five of these patients were undergoing cardiothoracic surgical procedures (all under general anaesthesia), and 12 were undergoing cardiology procedures (11 under conscious sedation and 1 under general anaesthesia). Theme P1: demonstrations of care matter

Participants reported that their primary goals were to survive their procedures with as few complications as possible. They also reported wanting or needing to feel cared for during their entire procedural experience. Patients who underwent a procedure under conscious sedation unanimously reported feeling relieved to have the procedure behind them and feeling cared for during their whole periprocedural and procedural experience. They said the procedural team was “busy and professional” (Vernon), deployed “an excellent communication process” (Sam), and were “positive” (Fadi). “The team that attended to me were just totally efficient and everything was sort of going like clockwork. Their manner to me was, couldn’t be better. They kept asking me do I understand, can I hear, and so I’m absolutely sure that I understood all their instructions. And their instructions I’d have to say were extended and very clear. … the importance is the empathy that comes with that communication and I thought the empathy level was just the best there could be. So, you know, they made me feel extremely comfortable. There was no anxiety at all because of, you know, the way that they were communicating. So there’s the communication process and then the individuals who were communicating and they were exemplary” (Sam). “I just felt really well looked after” (Judith).

Varnie is a Pacific Islander woman. She was the only participant who reported a negative experience. While the OR team demonstrated to her their professionalism in terms of procedures and routine, she felt uncared for by the perioperative and OR teams. Her memory of the perioperative procedures was of nurses and anaesthetists struggling to insert lines in a way that made her feel “like a lab rat”. Varnie said, “It was painful…And, they didn’t make me feel comfortable…I felt like I was one of the, you know, rats there for testing. I think it’s about being inclusive. Make sure I’m included in anything they do, that that will make me feel appreciative of what they are trying to do so that they can get to do the surgery for me” (Varnie).

Theme P2: effective communication as care and feeling safe

When asked about feeling safe, participants emphasised the importance of feeling included. For most participants, this meant clear communication, setting up their expectations and keeping them apprised of progress. “They included you in everything and they talked right through the procedure telling you what they were doing,” (Aroha, Māori woman). “So, it sort of got slightly uncomfortable at one point. Just, you know, I wasn’t sure what was, you know, how much longer it was going to go on. And, then they said, it’s finished, it’s done (laughter)” (Vernon).

Awake patients appreciated when staff let them know they were three-quarters of the way through or nearly finished the procedure. Others did not want to receive too many updates on what was happening; they wanted to listen to—but not necessarily participate in—staff conversations about activities beyond the procedure room. Participants described these distracting conversations as a common form of demonstrating care, the subtext being that the team was not worried and the procedure was progressing as expected. Participants found such conversations enjoyable and comforting. “I remember their conversations. […] they were talking about, you know, all their running and all their marathons and I was lying on the table going, oh dear lord, it just sounds like too much of hard work, […] I was just listening……to them, you know, talking about normality……while he’s digging in my, you know, digging around in my shoulder and in my arm and, you know, in my body. And wiring up my heart and I was thinking, God, this is just so fascinating that it’s just like, it’s like so normal for them, yeah, which I enjoyed. […] because they were so relaxed, I was relaxed” (Natasha, European New Zealander woman).

What was it that made them feel safe or included? Participants said: “They made it all beautiful and warm. They got blankets, they were chatty” (Natasha). “I thought it was all really fantastic. I was incredibly aware of what was going on, I’ve been told what was going on” (Vernon). Freddie suggested that having “somebody talking to me beside the table” may make her feel more included, though she added, “But sometimes as a patient, you just want some quiet time” (Freddie).

When explicitly asked about their experiences of the SSC in procedure rooms, participants unanimously reported feeling reassured by staff, including and questioning them. “It was very useful, yeah. It was really useful. […] they checked everything. They explained to me everything there in the theatre again, that you are having this procedure, checked my lines, all the medicine lines, everything. And the medicine lines they checked, they checked my name, everything again, they checked everything and before starting the procedure, yeah.” (Fadi). “It was just that they had great patient support, they asked you questions all the time as they went through” (Aroha). “Everyone was like, it became like, really, again? (Laughing) That was very thorough, you know, I found that everywhere I’ve gone is very thorough. And actually [I’m] grateful that they do that because, you know, you wouldn’t want a mistake. But everybody, like someone checked me, then another nurse when we got in there, she checked me. And then the doctor, he asked me, you know, date of birth, names, that kind of thing” (Natasha).

When asked ‘What would you change that would help you to feel more included?’ most participants reported they would change nothing. For the participants who were awake for their entire procedures, the central theme of their various experiences was that effective communication was a demonstration of both care and safety. These participants reported that the OR teams looking after them found the right balance in providing care through effective timely communication and just the right amount of communication with the patient.

Theme P3: asleep and unaware: as it should be

Four of the five participants undergoing cardiothoracic surgery (all under general anaesthesia) reported very few memories of going into the OR. They talked about the care they received perioperatively but had few memories of entering or being in the OR.

For example, when asked whether OR staff said anything that made him feel included or excluded, Michael responded: “I was out to it. I’ve got no memory of anybody speaking to me, doing anything to me, or anything at all. I just went to sleep and after the operation I woke up. […] Well, I don’t remember very much. They told me that I would go in and there would be a needle in the back of my hand and I would be out to it. […] at all stages I got asked my name and my date of birth. […] They explained to my wife that the operation would take six to seven hours. They did it ten minutes short of six hours and they put in a call to [my wife] straight away” (Michael).

Theme P4: My island name

A Cook Island Māori woman, Palila, who underwent general anaesthesia, had forgotten most of her experience going into and being in the operating room. However, what stood out in her recollection was that the perioperative teams called her by her island name (the name people call her when she is living in the Cook Islands) rather than her Catholic name. “I heard my island name being called by every nurse in that place. And to me, that was like my grandfather and my mother calling my name, and our island family. And it just felt like, it made me amazed that every single person called me my name just like my mum and my grandfather” (Palila). When asked what this meant, she replied, “I woke up and it just kind of put me back in to where I was supposed to be. I just felt like there was a purpose for everybody and they knew what they were there to do. […] it just felt like family being around me. … The hospital experience was out of this world” (Palila, Cook Island Māori woman).

For Palila, the correct pronunciation of her island name signalled to her a metaphysical, even spiritual, experience. That OR and perioperative staff used her island name, meant that Palila’s ancestors were there with her, clearly caring for her and ensuring her successful outcome. Palila interpreted the competence that OR and perioperative staff demonstrated by using her island name as having enormous cultural and spiritual importance.

Discussion

… the secret of the care of the patient is in caring for the patient.

(The Care of the Patient, Peabody 1927).19

The contributions of this study to the existing literature lie in its comprehensive, multimethod evaluation of the use of the SSC in a cardiology and a cardiothoracic service, with an emphasis on patients’ and clinicians’ experiences of its use. The insights from our findings have wide applicability and local relevance. We found some variation in approach and some ongoing room for improvement. In particular, there was a sense that the consistent engagement of all team members could be improved (see theme C.2). As indicated in our introduction, engagement is essential for achieving the SSC’s potential to improve patients’ outcomes.18 Encouragingly, we also found evidence of high levels of carefulness and respect between colleagues, which are elements likely to promote better patient outcomes.

This is the sixth of an ongoing series of initiatives91113 15 to re-evaluate and enhance the use of the SSC at ACH following its introduction in 2009,1 and our results reinforce the importance of regular review, local modification and training to increase clinicians’ engagement and ensure that the SSC is indeed being well used and is relevant to its context.20 The findings from our interviews provide powerful reminders of the importance of ensuring that patients are always treated as individuals and made to feel cared for, and new evidence that using the SSC can contribute to this. They also suggest that, despite some potential constraints, greater involvement of patients with the SSC process might have the potential to further improve the safety climate in operating and procedure rooms. Several other potential opportunities to improve the effectiveness of the SSC emerged from our data (see box 1). Some or all of these warrant further exploration and careful implementation in a continuation of the ongoing process of review and improvement at ACH,915 and perhaps elsewhere.

The use of the SSC in our CTORs is broadly in line with the original design of the SSC and that in other surgical operating rooms at ACH.91214 Thus, the SSC is wall-mounted, and the process is led by a clinician from a different discipline for each phase. The SSC is used as designed, in full, and high WHOBARS scores reflect good engagement by most team members (see figures1 2).21 In previous observations of other surgical services at ACH, Sign Out has been the least well conducted phase of the SSC. The high scores seen for Sign Out in this study may reflect ongoing initiatives to evaluate and improve the use of the SSC at ACH overall, which includes evidence of the value of Sign Out in reducing errors with surgical specimens.11

In our CCLs, most WHOBARS scores during Time Out were at least reasonable and, as with CTORs, observations and interviews indicated high levels of carefulness and respect between team members. However, there was a marked difference in the use of the SSC between electrophysiology CCLs, which was also broadly in line with the original design of the SSC, and angiography laboratories, which was not. This reflects important differences between angiography CCLs and most surgical operating rooms. Angiography CCLs are characterised by a high turnover of a large volume of short cases with a mix of elective, urgent and emergency procedures in each angiography CCL on any given day. Most cases are performed under conscious sedation and do not require anaesthetic support. The team in a room is typically set for the whole day and is drawn from a wider group of staff that is stable and small enough for everyone to know each other on a first-name basis. The procedures undertaken in the angiography CCL cover a broad spectrum. And importantly, a list of critical clinical information requires careful checking from each patient’s notes. An important difference between the angiography and the electrophysiology CCLs lies in the use of general anaesthesia, which is rare in the former and common in the latter. This means that the latter are more closely aligned with the CTORs. Furthermore, the influence of the anaesthetists (who all work primarily in the CTORs) on the process will probably have been greater.

Checklist use seems to be reasonably common in CCLs, at least in the UK, although the details vary.22 A recent consensus statement by the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) in the USA recommended the use of a ‘preprocedure checklist for cardiac catheterisation’ on each patient’s arrival in the ‘procedure room’, with a ‘time out’ procedure aligned with Joint Commission Standards; it indicates that individual institutions may add to the listed requirements. 23 In the UK, Cahill et al22 describe the development of two versions of a British Cardiovascular Society ‘cath lab checklist’. A ‘stand-alone version’ is said to follow the paradigm of the SSC, although there does not appear to be a separate Sign In. An ‘integrated version’ provides for relevant patient information to be checked before the patient gets to the CCL, with a view to streamlining the process in the CCL. 3 years later, Lindsay et al2 demonstrated reductions in radiation exposure and reported complications after developing and introducing a ‘safe procedure checklist’ to their CCLs. This checklist was based on the SSC and has the same three phases. The approach to checking in the ACH angiography CCLs appears to be closer to that of the SCAI and Cahill et al than that of Lindsay et al As indicated, discipline-specific modifications to the SSC are entirely appropriate and likely to increase compliance and engagement, and therefore, patient safety. However, a question arises over whether data supporting the use of the original SSC in surgical contexts can be extrapolated to substantially different checking procedures in other contexts. Collecting outcome data for each setting is not practicable, but periodic review of the use of these checklists (as we have done in this study) is. In our CCLs, our observations of care, engagement and respect, reinforced by the comments of the interviewed clinicians, suggest that the underpinning aims of the SSC are being achieved, and probably contributing to the safety climate and to patient safety more generally.

The introduction of a rotating leadership and the replacement of ticking a paper-based form with reading a wall-mounted SSC that is visible to all members of the team was associated with a substantial improvement in the use of the SSC at ACH,12 and the potential for the angiography CCLs to more closely align this aspect of their approach with that of the rest of the hospital should be given consideration. The amalgamation of Sign In with Time Out seems sensible in the context of angiography, but there is likely to be value in including a Sign Out.11

One strength of the WHOBARS is that it is agnostic of the particular checklist under evaluation. Instead, it focuses on evaluating five domains that experts involved in the design of the SSC agreed were critical to its success.18 Thus, we could evaluate the approach to checking in the angiography CCLs in the same way as the use of the SSC in the electrophysiology CCLs and the CTORs. It is slightly worrying that the lowest scoring domain during Sign In in the CTORS was ‘Communication: process completion’ which evaluates how key safety processes and procedures are verified as completed or addressed if not completed. Efforts to improve this domain of this phase may be warranted.

Our exploration of patients’ experience of the SSC appears to be novel. Not surprisingly, patients in our study wanted to be looked after well and to experience high-quality and safe care during their procedures. This aligns with the findings of Espin et al that most patients undergoing surgery in their study were supportive of full disclosure of errors.24 The idea that high-quality care should be patient-centred is widely accepted today, so it is gratifying that the majority of patients described their experiences in positive terms. It is interesting that they uniformly appreciated the care implied in the use of the SSC, and found the checks reassuring: patients differ, of course, but anecdotal concerns about the possibility of frightening patients through certain SSC checks may be overstated.

An important objective of the SSC is to enhance the safety culture in the operating or procedure room and promote an environment in which all members of the team feel safe to speak up if they notice something wrong. An aspect of this that has received little previous attention relates to cultural differences between individuals. Such differences are found in many countries, but the New Zealand healthcare system has specific obligations under The Treaty of Waitangi to provide culturally safe care.2527 Cultural safety is especially important for indigenous people who, globally, experience poorer health outcomes than non-indigenous people.28 Cultural safety is distinct from cultural competence27 29 30 and focuses on addressing unequal power within healthcare interactions,29 30 particularly (but not only) when clinicians and patients are from different cultural groups.

Cultural safety aligns with one objective of the SSC, that of reducing the impact of power gradients within operating room teams. Comments related to culture emerged from interviews with some clinicians and some patients. For example, nurse 9 said, “Some of the cultures are just not [likely to speak up] and they need to be encouraged”. Anaesthetist 10 expressed anxiety over some patients’ possible culturally relevant needs. The one Māori and one Pacific patient in this study voiced experiences that were distinctive from those of the other participants. Varnie (Samoan) could have been made to feel that she was an important person about whom the staff were concerned. Instead, she described feeling “like a lab rat”. The lessons from her interview have wide applicability to the many occasions in which patients are obliged to undergo painful, uncomfortable31 32 or frightening procedures and underline the importance of empathy and compassion. These go beyond the role of the SSC, but an increased emphasis in the SSC on personalising the patient could potentially reinforce this important aspect of patient care. For example, Aroha (Māori) expressed appreciation of the use of her island name and imbued this with cultural and spiritual significance. In fact, many people have a preference for how they want to be addressed. Adding an item to the SSC to identify and use each patient’s preferred name, pronounced correctly, could remind clinicians that every patient is an important individual, worthy of empathy and respect. This could, potentially, be followed by a question on what was important to the patient during the procedure, perhaps by asking whether there was anything the patient specifically wanted the team to know. Although preliminary in nature, these findings suggest that more research is needed into the question of cultural safety in the OR, and into the operating and procedure room experiences of patients, particularly those who identify with indigenous and other minority ethnicities. Our findings about using people’s preferred names and correct pronunciation as elements of cultural safety add empirical weight to existing literature concerning cultural safety in K-12 education and higher education contexts in the USA.33 34 One opportunity to raise this with patients is during and immediately following the Sign In phase of the SSC by asking the patient to confirm their name. The clinicians could include these follow-up questions: “am I pronouncing your name correctly?” and “what name would you like us to use during this procedure? For example, do you prefer [insert patient’s first name here] or [insert patient’s title and last name here]?

Unsurprisingly, conscious patients appreciated updates on progress from the OR staff. Surprisingly, social conversations among the clinicians were interpreted by patients as reassuring. These conversations appeared to convey a sense of calm and the idea that things were going well. Similar observations have been made by patients in an intensive care unit.35 Social conversations that take place in the presence of patients are informed by notions of professionalism. It is not always clear what is—and what is not—appropriate to discuss conversationally during a surgical procedure. That some patients may welcome the distraction and find it reassuring warrants attention. Future research could explore whether social conversations at particular times during procedures are more helpful than others.

The possibility that some patients may take a different view and see the conversations as evidence of failing to concentrate on them, for example, cannot be excluded by our study.

Strengths and limitations

We set out to interview a representative selection of the clinicians and patients whom we had observed during the SSC evaluation, but some chose not to participate, and this may have introduced selection bias in both groups and recall bias among patients. Among clinicians, selection bias may have been exacerbated by the impact of COVID on workload—it may well be that those clinicians who initially agreed to be interviewed but subsequently declined on these grounds were different in some important ways from those who were interviewed. However, we were able to include clinicians in the same roles as those who declined, which may have mitigated missed perspectives. Had we been able to interview random samples of these groups, at least some responses may have been different. We delayed contacting our patients for 2 months because we wanted to reduce potential stress associated with a request to be interviewed about their procedure, but it is possible that, by this time, participants had forgotten or misremembered details of their experience. Thus, their responses may have been different had they been interviewed earlier.

This was our first evaluation of the use of the SSC in a non-surgical service at ACH. We did not anticipate the difference between electrophysiology and angiography CCLs. In retrospect, we should have planned to evaluate all three phases of the SSC in the electrophysiology laboratories, and in future studies we would certainly do this.

As participants were recruited from a single quaternary care hospital in New Zealand, our findings may not be generalisable to other settings. Although we sought ethnic diversity among our participants, we were unable to achieve adequate representation of each of the diverse ethnic groups within New Zealand (see footnote, table 1). Strengths of this study include the novelty of exploring patients’ experiences of operating and procedure rooms, the use of a behaviourally anchored rating scale to evaluate the use of the SSC that was validated and is agnostic of the particular SSC being evaluated,14 18 the triangulation of data from three sources, and that the interviews were conducted and analysed by experienced, clinically knowledgeable qualitative researchers.

Conclusions

The SSC is used at ACH with high levels of carefulness and respect between team members, consistent with its original aims. There is some variation between services and some potential for further improvement, which reinforces the value of regular evaluation and context-relevant modification for maintaining and improving the effectiveness of its use. Use of the SSC increased our patients’ sense of being cared for. Our patients’ comments also suggest that, with modification (eg, by verifying their preferred name during the SSC Sign In phase), the SSC could enhance their perception of being seen as individuals, their cultural safety, and hence the overall safety climate in operating and procedure rooms. Our results also suggest that more research into cultural aspects of the safety climate in operating and procedural rooms is warranted.
