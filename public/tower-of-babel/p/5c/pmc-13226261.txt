A manganese-luteolin co-loaded antioxidant hydrogel for synergistic alleviation of oxidative stress and immunomodulation in intervertebral disc degeneration
Authors: Yaohong Wu, Qin Chen, Mingliang Zhong, Yunsheng Chen, Rongchun Chen, Jiaolan Wu, Guiyong Jiang, Hangzhan Ma, Xiaochuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a multifactorial degenerative disorder primarily driven by oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and progressive extracellular matrix (ECM) breakdown. Current therapeutic approaches largely aim to alleviate symptoms but remain ineffective in halting or reversing the underlying pathological processes. To address this challenge, we designed an injectable, pH-responsive manganese–luteolin hydrogel (Mn-LU@Gel) capable of concurrently mitigating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses within the intervertebral disc microenvironment. Synthesized via Schiff base chemistry, Mn-LU@Gel demonstrated robust antioxidant activity and controlled release behavior under acidic conditions. In vitro, the hydrogel effectively scavenged reactive oxygen species (ROS) in macrophages and nucleus pulposus cells, reprogramming macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory M2 state. This immunomodulatory shift resulted in the downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, along with enhanced expression of anabolic ECM markers and suppression of catabolic enzymes, as verified by Western blot and immunofluorescence assays. In vivo, administration of Mn-LU@Gel in a rat model of IVDD preserved disc height, hydration, and structural integrity, thereby maintaining ECM homeostasis. Collectively, these findings reveal Mn-LU@Gel as a multifunctional therapeutic platform that integrates redox regulation and immune modulation, offering a promising strategy for the biological restoration of intervertebral disc tissue.Keywords: Intervertebral disc degeneration, Manganese-luteolin complex, pH-responsive release, Oxidative stress, Macrophage polarization, Extracellular matrix homeostasis

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) represents a major musculoskeletal disorder and a fundamental pathological basis underlying multiple spinal diseases, profoundly impairing quality of life and imposing a heavy socioeconomic burden worldwide [[1], [2], [3]]. Despite the availability of a wide range of therapeutic approaches—from conservative physical rehabilitation to surgical interventions such as spinal fusion—current treatments are largely palliative, aiming to alleviate symptoms rather than addressing the biological deterioration of the intervertebral disc itself [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Consequently, there is a pressing and unmet clinical need for novel therapeutic strategies that can effectively intervene in the degenerative cascade at a biological level.

The etiopathogenesis of IVDD is multifactorial, characterized by a self-perpetuating vicious cycle of oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and progressive degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) [[8], [9], [10]]. Within the avascular and hypoxic microenvironment of the nucleus pulposus (NP), disc cells exhibit limited antioxidative capacity, rendering them highly susceptible to oxidative damage and metabolic stress [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Among these immune components, macrophages infiltrating the degenerative disc play a pivotal role in shaping the local microenvironment. ROS and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from stressed NP cells drive macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, which amplifies the production of cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, as well as matrix-degrading enzymes including MMPs and ADAMTS [[15], [16], [17]]. These mediators accelerate extracellular matrix (ECM) catabolism and further intensify oxidative and inflammatory stress within the disc [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Conversely, alternatively activated M2 macrophages contribute to tissue repair by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines and promoting ECM synthesis [22]. Therefore, the imbalance between M1 and M2 macrophage polarization, coupled with excessive ROS accumulation, establishes a deleterious oxidative-inflammatory-immune loop that drives progressive NP cell degeneration, ECM breakdown, and eventual disc failure.

Current biological strategies for IVDD, including the administration of single-agent antioxidants, anti-inflammatory drugs, and other molecular therapies, often yield suboptimal and transient outcomes because they target only limited downstream events within a highly interconnected degenerative network and generally lack targeted, sustained delivery to the disc [23,24].

In recent years, biomaterial-based strategies have gained increasing attention for their ability to simultaneously modulate inflammation and support ECM homeostasis. Notably, multifunctional hydrogel systems have been developed to integrate anti-inflammatory and matrix-regulating properties for disc regeneration [25]. In addition, hydrogel platforms capable of regulating macrophage polarization have demonstrated promising therapeutic potential in tissue repair [26]. More broadly, recent advances highlight the role of hydrogel-based systems in orchestrating immune modulation while providing structural support for ECM remodeling in regenerative medicine [27].

An ideal disease-modifying regimen would therefore need to intervene at multiple pathological nodes simultaneously while maintaining durable activity within the hostile disc microenvironment. In this context, increasing attention has been directed toward endogenous antioxidant enzymes, particularly superoxide dismutases (SODs), as promising candidates to mitigate oxidative stress and thereby delay IVDD progression [28]. However, native SOD proteins exhibit several intrinsic shortcomings, including poor stability, susceptibility to proteolytic degradation and inactivation, short biological half-life, and difficulties in achieving efficient penetration and retention within the dense extracellular matrix of the intervertebral disc, all of which severely limit their translational potential [29,30]. Manganese (Mn), serving as the catalytic center of mitochondrial manganese superoxide dismutase (Mn-SOD), can undergo reversible valence cycling to catalytically decompose superoxide and other reactive oxygen species, providing a robust mechanistic basis for the design of Mn-based biomimetic nanozymes with SOD-like activity and potentially broadened antioxidant functions [31]. Accordingly, Mn-based nanozyme therapy has emerged as an advanced and attractive strategy to remodel the oxidative microenvironment, suppress inflammation, and protect nucleus pulposus cells from degeneration in IVDD.

To translate this therapeutic concept into a viable clinical intervention, a sophisticated delivery platform is paramount [32]. An ideal carrier should not only enable minimally invasive implantation but also provide a protective, sustained-release reservoir within the harsh mechanical and biochemical environment of the degenerate disc [33]. Herein, we engineered an injectable hydrogel based on oxidized starch (DAS) and quaternized chitosan (QCS), which undergoes rapid gelation via a facile and biocompatible Schiff base reaction. This dynamic covalent network inherently confers excellent self-healing properties and injectability. More importantly, the DAS-QCS hydrogel (Gel) can serve as an ideal scaffold for co-loading therapeutic agents.

Building upon this concept, we developed a multifunctional injectable hydrogel (Mn-LU@Gel) designed to deliver the Mn-luteolin (Mn-LU) complex directly to the degenerative disc tissue. This hydrogel is composed of oxidized starch (DAS) and quaternized chitosan (QCS), which form Schiff base bonds and respond to the acidic and inflammatory microenvironment typical of IVDD. This pH-responsive system allows for the rapid and localized release of Mn-LU, where the manganese component serves as a bioactive center with both catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activities, providing effective antioxidant protection. Luteolin, a powerful natural anti-inflammatory flavonoid, further enhances the therapeutic potential by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Together, these components synergistically modulate the oxidative-inflammatory microenvironment at the site of degeneration, facilitating the polarization of M1 macrophages to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, which supports tissue repair and prevents further ECM degradation. This dual-action approach targets both oxidative stress and immune imbalance, offering a promising strategy for halting or even reversing the pathological progression of IVDD (Scheme 1).

Synthesis and characterization of the manganese-luteolin complex (Mn-LU)

The Mn-LU complex was synthesized via a coordination reaction. Briefly, LU (100 mg) was dissolved in 40 mL of anhydrous ethanol under stirring and mild heating (40 °C). An aqueous solution of manganese acetate tetrahydrate (Mn(CH3COO)2·4H2O) was added dropwise at a molar ratio of Mn2+ to LU of 1:2. The pH of the mixture was adjusted to approximately 8.0 using a 0.1 M NaOH solution to facilitate the deprotonation of LU's phenolic hydroxyl groups. The reaction proceeded under reflux at 70 °C for 4 h. The resulting solution was cooled, centrifuged, and the complex was precipitated by adding excess cold diethyl ether. The precipitate was collected, washed, and vacuum-dried to obtain a solid powder.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS, Escalab 250Xi, UK): The surface elemental composition and chemical states of the samples were analyzed by XPS using an Al Kα source. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet 6700, USA): FTIR spectra were recorded on a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer using the KBr pellet method. The scanning range was 4000-500 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1.

Zeta Potential Measurement (Zetasizer Nano ZS, UK): The zeta potential of the LU and Mn-LU (dispersed in deionized water) was measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument at 25 °C.

In vitro release

In vitro release profiles of LU and Mn2+ from the Mn-LU complex were assessed in PBS at pH 6.5 (simulating degenerative disc microenvironment) and pH 7.4 (physiological condition). Samples were incubated in release medium at 37 °C with shaking (100 rpm). At predetermined intervals, 1 mL of medium was collected and replaced with fresh PBS. LU concentration was determined by HPLC (Agilent HPLC 1260, China), while Mn2+ release was quantified by ICP-OES (ICAP RQ, Germany). Cumulative release percentage was calculated and release kinetics were plotted.

Preparation of hydrogel

Disperse a predetermined amount of synthesized Mn-LU composite powder uniformly in an oxidized starch aqueous solution (DAS, 10% w/v) under mild vortexing to form a homogeneous mixture. Subsequently, an equal volume of QCS solution (2% w/v) was added to the above DAS/Mn-LU mixture. Vortex the final mixture briefly for about 10-15 s, then let it stand at room temperature or 37 °C. Through the Schiff base reaction between the aldehyde group of DAS and the amino group of QCS, the covalently crosslinked DAS-QCS hydrogel (Gel) is formed within a 5 min, and the Mn-LU complex is encapsulated in Gel (Mn-LU@Gel). For subsequent biological evaluation, the final concentrations of Mn and LU in the hydrogel were set to 100 μM and 200 μM respectively.

Rheological testing

The rheological properties of the hydrogel were characterized using a rotational rheometer (MCR301, Austria). The hydrogel precursor solution was gelled in a custom mold (2 mm thick, 25 mm diameter). The sample was then placed on the rheometer stage and equilibrated at 37 °C. Following this, the upper plate was lowered until it made contact with the hydrogel surface. A frequency sweep test was conducted from 0.1 to 10 Hz at a fixed strain amplitude of 2.0% to determine the mechanical properties.

The injectability of the hydrogel: 1) Macroscopic assessment by extruding 3 mL of hydrogel from a syringe into PBS to observe shape retention; 2) Quantitative rheological measurement of viscosity at 37 °C using frequency sweep mode.

Self-healing capability was characterized via: 1) Oscillatory rheology with alternating strain amplitudes (1% and 600%) at 1 Hz to monitor G'/G″ recovery; 2) Visual fusion test where bisected hydrogel discs (one stained with Rhodamine B) were brought into contact at 37 °C to observe autonomous rejoining.

Mechanical performance test

The mechanical resilience and fatigue resistance of the cylindrical hydrogels (10 mm in diameter, 10 mm in height) were evaluated by cyclic compression tests using a universal mechanical testing system (CMT1502, China). The samples were subjected to consecutive compression cycles at a fixed strain of 70% of their original height and a constant crosshead speed of 10 mm/min. The stress-strain curves were recorded for each cycle.

Isolation of nucleus pulposus (NP) cells and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Nucleus pulposus (NP) cells were isolated from the coccygeal intervertebral discs of 6–8-week-old male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats. After euthanasia, tail discs (Co3–Co8) were collected under sterile conditions. The annulus fibrosus and surrounding tissues were removed, and the NP tissue was extracted. The NP tissue was minced and digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by digestion with 0.2% collagenase type II for 4–6 h. The digested tissue was filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and centrifuged. The collected cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Passage 3 cells were used for experiments.

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were obtained from the femurs and tibias of 6–8-week-old male SD rats. The rats were euthanized, and bone marrow cells were flushed out using sterile PBS. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL M-CSF. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. On day 3, half of the medium was replaced. By day 6–7, adherent macrophage-like cells were collected for use in experiments.

Biocompatibility evaluation

NP cells and macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. Cells were treated with Mn-LU@Gel extracts at various concentrations (0, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 mg/ml) for 48 h. Following incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well, and cells were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as a percentage of the control.

NP cells and macrophages were seeded in 24-well plates and treated with 20 mg/ml Mn-LU@Gel extracts for 48 h. After incubation, cells were stained with a Live/Dead Cell Viability Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were then observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX81).

Cellular internalization of Mn-LU@Gel

NP cells and macrophages were seeded onto confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 104 cells per dish and incubated overnight. The cells were then treated with Mn-LU@Gel extracts at 20 mg/ml for 8 h. To label Mn-LU@Gel for visualization, rhodamine B was incorporated into the hydrogel. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI, and the actin filaments were stained with phalloidin-Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The stained cells were mounted using anti-fade mounting medium and observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope. (LSM 800 With Airscan, Germany).

Intracellular ROS detection using DCFH-DA fluorescent probe

The intracellular ROS levels in macrophage cells under different treatments Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using DCFH-DA probe. Macrophages were treated for 24 h under four conditions: Control, H2O2 (200 μM), Gel extract + H2O2, and Mn-LU@Gel extract + H2O2. Cells were then incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium (37 °C, 20-30 min, dark). After PBS washing, ROS generation was visualized by fluorescence microscopy. For flow cytometry, trypsinized cells were stained with DCFH-DA (37 °C, 30 min, dark), washed with cold PBS, and analyzed immediately using Flowsight cytometer (Flowsight, USA).

Induction of M1 macrophage polarization by LPS

To evaluate immunomodulatory effects on macrophage polarization, cells were treated with LPS (100 ng/mL, 24 h) to induce M1 phenotype under four conditions: Control, LPS only, LPS + Gel extract, and LPS + Mn-LU@Gel extract. Protein expression of iNOS and CD206 was detected by immunofluorescence. After fixation, permeabilization and blocking, cells were incubated with primary antibodies (4 °C, overnight) and fluorescent secondary antibodies, followed by DAPI nuclear staining. Images were acquired using a laser confocal microscope (LSM 800 With Airscan, Germany).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The mRNA expression levels of iNOS, IL-1β, IL-4 and IL-10 were detected by qRT-PCR. Total RNA was extracted, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and then amplified using specific primers. The relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

Analysis of protein expression levels by Western blot

Protein expression of M1/M2 macrophage markers was analyzed by Western blot. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST (1 h, RT), then incubated with primary antibodies (iNOS, TNF-α, IL-4, CD206, β-actin) at 4 °C overnight. After TBST washing, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1 h, RT). Protein bands were visualized using ECL detection system.

Immunofluorescence staining for COL2/MMP13 and F-actin

NP cells on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (10 min), and blocked with 1% BSA (30 min). Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against COL2 or MMP13, followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1 h, dark). F-actin was stained with Alexa Fluor 555-phalloidin (30-40 min), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 min). After mounting, images were acquired using confocal laser scanning microscopy. Fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software, and cellular morphology was assessed via F-actin staining.

RNA extraction, sequencing, and analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. RNA quality was verified by measuring A260/A280 ratio (NanoDrop, USA) and RNA Integrity Number (Agilent Bioanalyzer,USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared from 1 μg total RNA through mRNA purification with oligo(dT) beads, fragmentation, double-stranded cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation and PCR amplification. Library quality was confirmed prior to 150 bp paired-end sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Specifically, genes with a P value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Animal model of intervertebral disc degeneration

8-week-old male SD rats were used to establish a tail puncture-induced intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) model. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of GUANGZHOU Seyotin BIO-TECH CO.Ltd (ethical approval number: SYT2024156) and conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines. Rats were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). The coccygeal intervertebral discs (Co6–Co8) were located using lateral fluoroscopy. A 21-gauge needle was inserted into the center of the disc at a depth of approximately 5 mm and rotated 360° for 30 s to induce disc degeneration [38,39]. The needle was then withdrawn carefully. After model induction, the rats were randomly divided into 4 groups: (1) Control (no puncture), (2) IVDD, (3) Gel treatment, and (4) Mn-LU@Gel treatment. For the treatment groups, 10 μL of Gel or Mn-LU@Gel was injected into the punctured disc space immediately after needle withdrawal.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All biological experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Statistical comparisons between multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For two-group comparisons, a Student's t-test was used. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results and discussion

Characterization of Mn-LU

The successful synthesis and fundamental properties of the Mn-LU complex nanoparticles were systematically characterized. SEM imaging (Fig. 1a) revealed that the synthesized Mn-LU complex formed spherical or near-spherical nanoparticles. EDS elemental mapping confirmed the homogeneous distribution of carbon (C), oxygen (O), and manganese (Mn) throughout the nanoparticles, providing direct evidence for the successful incorporation of manganese into the complex and its uniform composition.

Morphology, structural characterization, and release profile of Mn-LU. (a) SEM image and corresponding elemental mapping of Mn-LU. Scale bar = 200 nm. (b) Particle size distribution of Mn-LU. (c) Zeta potential of Mn-LU. (d) FTIR spectra of LU and Mn-LU. (e) XPS survey spectrum of Mn-LU. (f) High-resolution XPS spectrum of the Mn 2p region. (g) High-resolution XPS spectrum of the O 1s region. (h, i) In vitro release profiles of Mn-LU (n = 3).

DLS analysis was employed to determine the hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution of the nanoparticles in an aqueous solution. As shown in Fig. 1b, the Mn-LU nanoparticles exhibited a narrow, monomodal size distribution with a peak diameter centered at approximately 190 nm. The surface charge of the nanoparticles, a key factor influencing colloidal stability and cellular interactions, was assessed by zeta potential measurement. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, the zeta potential of the pure LU was slightly negative. Notably, the Mn-LU complex displayed a significantly higher negative zeta potential of approximately −12 mV. This increase in negative charge can be attributed to the coordination of Mn2+ ions with the deprotonated phenolic hydroxyl groups of LU, which alters the surface properties of the resulting complex. This moderate negative zeta potential value contributes to the stability of the nanoparticle suspension by generating sufficient electrostatic repulsion to prevent aggregation.

As shown in Fig. 1d, the appearance of a peak at 809 cm−1 in the FTIR spectrum of the Mn-LU complex, attributable to the ν(Mn-O) stretching vibration, which is absent in pure LU, indicates the formation of the metal complex. Furthermore, the characteristic C=O stretching vibration shifted from 1662 cm−1 in LU to 1620 cm−1 in the complex [41]. This shift can be explained by the coordination of the carbonyl oxygen atom to the metal ion, confirming that manganese (II) has coordinated with the 5-hydroxyl group (5-OH) on the A-ring and the 4-carbonyl group (4-C=O) on the C-ring of LU [42].

XPS analysis offered further direct evidence at the elemental level. The survey scan (Fig. 1e) clearly confirmed the presence of Mn (along with C and O) in the Mn-LU complex, which was absent in pure LU. The high-resolution Mn 2p spectrum (Fig. 1f) exhibited two peaks at binding energies of 641.5 eV and 653.2 eV, corresponding to Mn 2p3/2 and Mn 2p1/2, respectively [43]. The spin-orbit splitting of 11.7 eV is characteristic of Mn2+. Most importantly, the high-resolution O 1s spectrum (Fig. 1g) was deconvoluted into two component peaks. The peak at 531.5 eV is attributed to the C=O and C-O bonds from LU. The new peak identified at a lower binding energy of 530.78 eV is a hallmark of O atoms in the Mn-O coordination bond. The emergence of this specific peak provides definitive proof for the successful formation of the coordination complex.

To directly evaluate the intrinsic stability and pH-sensitive release behavior of the Mn-LU coordination complex itself, the cumulative release of free LU and Mn2+ ions from the complex was monitored in PBS at pH 7.4 and pH 6.5, without incorporation into the hydrogel.

As shown in Fig. 1h, the release of LU from the Mn-LU complex exhibited a remarkable pH dependence. At the pathological acidic pH of 6.5, the complex demonstrated rapid disassociation, releasing nearly 80% of the bound LU within 24 h. In contrast, at the physiological pH of 7.4, the release was significantly slower and sustained, reaching only about 35% in the same period. This behavior is a direct consequence of the coordination chemistry involved. Under acidic conditions, the increased concentration of H+ protons competitively binds to the electron-donating oxygen atoms (from the phenolic hydroxyl and carbonyl groups of LU), effectively disrupting the coordination bonds with Mn2+ and triggering the decomposition of the complex and the release of free LU.

Similarly, the release profile of Mn2+ ions (Fig. 1i) mirrored this trend, with a faster and more substantial release observed at pH 6.5 compared to pH 7.4. The release of Mn2+ was more rapid than that of LU, which may be attributed to the simpler process of ion dissociation compared to the release of the larger organic ligand.

Multifunctional antioxidant activities of the Mn-LU

The antioxidant capacity of the Mn-LU complex was rigorously evaluated through a series of assays to decipher its multifaceted mechanism of action against various ROS [44].

The Mn-LU complex exhibited dose-dependent peroxidase-like activity, catalyzing the decomposition of H2O2 (Fig. 2a and b). This was further corroborated by ESR spectroscopy using DMPO as a spin trap. The characteristic 1:2:2:1 quartet signal of the DMPO-·OH adduct generated by the Fenton reaction was significantly attenuated upon treatment with Mn-Lu (Fig. 2c). he ability to neutralize the ·O2− was assessed by a NBT reduction assay under light irradiation. The increase in absorbance due to formazan formation decreased with increasing concentrations of Mn-LU (Fig. 2d), indicating effective ·O2− scavenging. Characterization of hydrogel

The injectable hydrogel was fabricated through a simple and rapid Schiff base reaction between DAS and QCS. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, upon mixing the aqueous solutions of DAS and QCS, the aldehyde groups on DAS readily reacted with the amino groups on QCS, leading to the formation of a covalently cross-linked hydrogel network within minutes under physiological conditions. This facile gelling process is highly advantageous for minimally invasive applications. The internal microarchitecture of the freeze-dried hydrogels was examined by SEM. After loading with the Mn-LU (Fig. 3b), the Mn-LU@Gel maintained a well-defined porous structure. No significant aggregation of Mn-LU was observed on the pore walls, indicating the successful and homogeneous incorporation of the therapeutic complex within the hydrogel matrix without disrupting its intrinsic architecture. This uniform distribution is crucial for ensuring the consistent and sustained release of the active agents. The formation of the Gel via Schiff base reaction was first confirmed by FT-IR spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 3c, the spectrum of DAS showed a characteristic peak at 1730 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibration of aldehyde groups (C=O). The spectrum of QCS) exhibited a peak at 1650 cm−1, attributed to the amide I band. In the spectrum of the formed Gel, the aldehyde peak from DAS significantly diminished, while a new prominent peak emerged at 1633 cm−1. This new peak is assigned to the C=N stretching vibration of the Schiff base, providing direct evidence for the successful covalent cross-linking between DAS and QCS [48]. The mechanical strength and viscoelastic properties of the hydrogel, which are critical for its application as an injectable biomaterial in the disc, were characterized by rheological measurements. A strain sweep test (Fig. 3d) determined the linear viscoelastic region, where the storage modulus (G′) is independent of strain and dominates over the loss modulus (G″), indicating solid-like behavior. The flow point (where G' = G″) defines the critical strain beyond which the gel structure yields. The viscosity of the hydrogel as a function of shear rate is shown in Fig. 3e. A pronounced shear-thinning behavior was observed, where the viscosity decreased dramatically with increasing shear rate. This property is essential for injectability, as it allows the hydrogel to flow easily through a fine needle during injection and rapidly recover its viscosity (and thus structural integrity) upon cessation of shear stress in the disc space. Furthermore, the self-healing and recovery capability of the hydrogel were evaluated by subjecting it to alternating low (1%) and high (600%) shear strain (Fig. 3f). When the strain was switched from 1% to 600%, G′ immediately dropped below G″, indicating a transition from a gel state to a sol state. Remarkably, upon instantly returning the strain to 1%, G′ and G″ recovered their original values immediately and completely. This rapid and full recovery over multiple cycles demonstrates the excellent thixotropic and self-healing properties of the hydrogel, which is crucial for withstanding repetitive mechanical loading in the spine. Two cylindrical hydrogel discs were prepared: one was stained with red, and the other was stained with blue (Fig. 3g). Each disc was carefully cut into two halves with a scalpel. Subsequently, the two hydrogel pieces were brought into intimate contact at their cross-sections. The two pieces spontaneously adhered to each other upon contact without any external intervention. After standing at room temperature for a short period, the re-joined hydrogel could be lifted by holding one half, and the healed interface was strong enough to withstand manual handling without detachment. The ability of the Mn-LU@Gel to withstand repeated mechanical loading was evaluated through cyclic compression tests, simulating the physiological stresses experienced by intervertebral discs in daily activities. The hydrogel samples were subjected to continuous compression cycles at a fixed high strain of 70% of their original height (Fig. 3h). As shown in Fig. 3i, the stress-strain curves for the first four cycles are nearly superimposable. There is no significant decrease in the maximum stress at 70% strain from the 1st to the 4th cycle, and the hysteresis loops remain consistent. Furthermore, the unloading curve of each cycle returns almost to the origin, indicating a negligible residual strain and a highly elastic recovery. This remarkable performance demonstrates that the covalently cross-linked network of the Gel possesses exceptional mechanical resilience and fatigue resistance. The robust Schiff base bonds and the flexible polymer chains enable the hydrogel to efficiently dissipate energy and fully recover its original structure upon the release of load, even after multiple cycles of large deformation.

The in vitro release profiles of the therapeutic agents from the Mn-LU@Gel were critically evaluated under physiological (pH 7.4) and pathological acidic (pH 6.5) conditions to simulate the microenvironment of normal and degenerated intervertebral discs, respectively.

As shown in Fig. 3j, the release of LU from the Gel exhibited a pronounced pH-dependent behavior. At pH 7.4, the release was sustained and moderate. In stark contrast, at pH 6.5, both the rate and the extent of LU release were significantly accelerated, achieving a much higher cumulative release over the 24-h period. This intelligent release behavior is attributed to the synergistic effect of two pH-sensitive mechanisms: The acid-labile Schiff base bonds within the Gel network hydrolyze faster under acidic conditions, leading to a more rapid loosening and degradation of the Gel matrix, thereby facilitating drug diffusion [49]; The Mn-LU coordination complex itself is prone to dissociation in an acidic environment, as previously demonstrated, liberating free LU.

A similar, though not identical, trend was observed for the release of Mn2+ ions (Fig. 3k). The release of Mn2+ was also faster at pH 6.5 than at pH 7.4. However, Mn2+ generally exhibited a higher initial burst release and a faster overall release rate compared to LU at both pH values. This difference can be explained by the different states of the two agents within the system: Mn2+ ions are likely associated with the Gel matrix via weaker electrostatic interactions or residual coordination, allowing for easier elution, while the larger LU molecules are more reliant on the degradation of the Gel network for release.

The CCK-8 assay (Fig. S1) was conducted to evaluate the cytotoxicity of Mn-LU@Gel extracts at various concentrations (0–100 mg/ml) on NP cells and macrophages. The results showed that both cell types exhibited high viability at concentrations up to 20 mg/ml, with significant reductions in viability observed only at higher concentrations (50 and 100 mg/ml). Based on these findings, 20 mg/ml was selected as the optimal concentration for further experiments. Live/dead staining (Fig. S2) confirmed that at this concentration, both NP cells and macrophages showed predominantly live staining, with only a few dead cells, indicating minimal cytotoxicity.

To comprehensively assess the cellular internalization and in vivo retention of Mn-LU@Gel, confocal microscopy and IVIS fluorescence imaging were conducted. Confocal imaging (Fig. S3) confirmed effective internalization of Mn-LU by NP cells and macrophages in vitro, while IVIS imaging tracked the hydrogel's in vivo retention and degradation over time following injection. As shown in Fig. 3l, a strong fluorescence signal was observed at the injection site on Day 1, confirming successful delivery and initial retention of the hydrogel in the intervertebral disc region. Remarkably, although the fluorescence intensity gradually decreased over time due to material degradation and release of payloads, a detectable signal was still present on Day 21. The quantitative changes in ROI fluorescence signal are shown in Fig. S4 The IVIS fluorescence signal exhibited a rapid decrease during the initial phase, followed by a gradual and sustained decline over time.

Furthermore, existing studies indicate that Mn2+ released from the Mn-LU@Gel system is unlikely to accumulate to toxicologically relevant levels [[50], [51], [52]]. The pharmacokinetics and metabolic fate of luteolin have been extensively investigated in recent literature, and substantial evidence demonstrates that luteolin does not undergo long-term retention in vivo [53,54]. However, while these findings suggest that the present study holds potential value for translational applications, further studies are required to confirm these results in the context of long-term, repeated treatments and to better understand the long-term biodegradation and systemic distribution of Mn2+ and luteolin.

This long-lasting signal suggests that Mn-LU@Gel exhibits excellent in situ retention and slow degradation kinetics within the harsh, enclosed intervertebral disc microenvironment. The prolonged presence of the material is highly beneficial for therapeutic applications targeting disc degeneration, as it ensures a sustained release of active components over an extended period. Furthermore, such long-term retention minimizes the need for repeated administration and enhances therapeutic efficacy by maintaining consistent local concentrations.

The Mn-LU@Gel effectively attenuates oxidative stress and protects mitochondrial function in RAW264.7 macrophages

To evaluate the cytoprotective effect of the Mn-LU@Gel against oxidative damage, RAW264.7 macrophages were subjected to H2O2-induced oxidative stress. Intracellular ROS levels were first detected using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. As shown in Fig. 4a, a strong green fluorescence was observed in the H2O2-treated group, indicating a substantial increase in ROS generation. In contrast, treatment with the Mn-LU@Gel extract markedly reduced the fluorescence intensity, comparable to the baseline level seen in the control group. The blank Gel group showed a minor mitigating effect. Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4b) and flow cytometry (Fig. 4c and d) confirmed these observations, demonstrating that Mn-Lu@Gel significantly decreased the percentage of ROS-positive cells induced by H2O2.

Given the critical role of mitochondrial dysfunction in oxidative stress-induced cell damage, we further investigated the effect on mitochondrial health [55]. The mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was assessed using the JC-1 dye. In healthy cells, JC-1 forms aggregates emitting red fluorescence. Under H2O2-induced stress, the MMP collapsed, indicated by a shift from red to green fluorescence (Fig. 4e and f). Quantification of the aggregate/monomer ratio (Fig. 4g) and the percentage of JC-1 monomer-positive cells (Fig. 4h) revealed that H2O2 caused severe mitochondrial depolarization. Importantly, treatment with Mn-LU@Gel largely preserved the MMP, as evidenced by the restored red/green fluorescence ratio and reduced monomer-positive cells, significantly outperforming the Gel group.

The ultrastructural changes of mitochondria were directly visualized by TEM. Control cells displayed normal, elongated mitochondria with well-defined cristae (Fig. 4i). H2O2 treatment resulted in severe mitochondrial damage, including swelling, cristae fragmentation, and vacuolization. While the Gel group offered slight protection, the Mn-LU@Gel group effectively maintained mitochondrial integrity, with most organelles appearing normal.

The antioxidant effects observed in Mn-LU@Gel are primarily attributed to the Mn-based nanozyme activity. As a key component of mitochondrial superoxide dismutase (Mn-SOD), Mn catalytically decomposes reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly superoxide anions (O2•-) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), as previously reported by Zhao et al. [56].

These results collectively demonstrate that the Mn-LU@Gel potently scavenges excess ROS, thereby preserving MMP and ultrastructural integrity in RAW264.7 macrophages under oxidative assault.

Mn-LU@Gel modulates macrophage polarization by suppressing the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and promoting the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype

Given the critical role of chronic inflammation in IVDD progression, we investigated the immunomodulatory potential of the Mn-LU@Gel on macrophage polarization using RAW 264.7 cells [57]. Macrophages were stimulated with LPS to induce a classic pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and concurrently treated with hydrogel extracts (Fig. 5a).

Immunofluorescence staining and quantitative analysis of characteristic surface markers provided clear evidence of phenotypic regulation. As shown in Fig. 5b, LPS stimulation significantly increased the expression of iNOS. Treatment with the Mn-LU@Gel extract dramatically reduced iNOS expression. Conversely, the expression of the M2 marker CD206 (Fig. 5c) was markedly enhanced in the Mn-LU@Gel group compared to the LPS group, indicating a shift towards an anti-inflammatory/reparative phenotype. The blank Gel group showed minimal effects on both markers. Flow cytometry analysis further quantitatively confirmed these findings. As shown in Fig. S5, LPS stimulation markedly increased the proportion of CD86+ (M1) macrophages, while Mn-LU@Gel treatment significantly reduced CD86 expression and promoted the proportion of CD206+ (M2) macrophages.

This immunomodulatory effect was further quantified by measuring the fluorescence intensity of key markers. Consistent with the morphological observations, quantitative analysis confirmed that the Mn-Lu@Gel treatment significantly reduced the intensity of iNOS (Fig. 5d) and, conversely, enhanced the intensity of the CD206 (Fig. 5e) compared to the LPS-stimulated group. This immunomodulatory effect was further confirmed at the transcriptional level by qRT-PCR. LPS stimulation led to a sharp upregulation of M1-associated genes, including TNF-α, and IL-1β (Fig. 5f and g). The Mn-LU@Gel treatment significantly suppressed the expression of these pro-inflammatory genes. Meanwhile, the mRNA levels of IL-4 and IL-10, were significantly elevated in the Mn-Lu@Gel group (Fig. 5h and i).

Finally, Western blot analysis (Fig. 5j and k) validated these findings at the protein level. Consistent with the gene expression data, the protein levels of iNOS and TNF-α were highly induced by LPS, and this induction was potently reversed by Mn-Lu@Gel. Simultaneously, the expression of CD206 was upregulated. Exact P values were provided in Table S1.

Mn-LU@Gel 's anti-inflammatory effects are driven by luteolin's ability to modulate macrophage polarization. Luteolin specifically influences macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype towards a reparative M2 phenotype, thereby alleviating inflammation and supporting tissue repair [58].

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the Mn-LU@Gel effectively reprograms macrophage polarization by concurrently inhibiting the pro-inflammatory M1 pathway and promoting the anti-inflammatory M2 pathway, thereby creating a favorable immunomodulatory microenvironment conducive to disc repair.

Differential gene expression analysis revealed 181 genes upregulated and 265 genes downregulated upon Mn-LU@Gel treatment compared to the LPS group (Fig. 7b and c), indicating substantial transcriptional remodeling.

The results from GSEA further confirmed the anti-inflammatory effect of Mn-Lu@Gel. As shown in Fig. 6f, the "Toll-like receptor signaling pathway" upstream of NF-κB was markedly inhibited in the Mn-Lu@Gel group compared to the LPS group. Concurrently, significant negative enrichment was observed for the "IL-17 signaling pathway" (Fig. 6g) and the "NF-κB signaling pathway" (Fig. 6h), demonstrating effective blockade of these key pro-inflammatory pathways.

Western blot analysis validated the transcriptomic findings at the protein level. Mn-LU@Gel markedly reduced the expression of TLR4, phosphorylated P65 (p-P65), and IL-17A in LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 6i), indicating that Mn-LU@Gel inhibits the canonical NF-κB pathway and upstream TLR/IL-17R signaling.

The proposed mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 6j. Mn-LU@Gel suppresses the TLR4 and IL-17R signaling axes, inhibiting MyD88/TRAF6-mediated NF-κB activation, leading to decreased iNOS and TNF-α expression. Concurrently, it promotes polarization toward the M2 phenotype, characterized by increased CD206 and IL-4 expression. This immunomodulatory effect reshapes the inflammatory environment, contributing to downstream ECM restoration in NP cells.

To further connect the transcriptomic findings with IVDD pathology and ECM regulation, we focused on differentially expressed genes associated with macrophage-driven inflammatory and reparative responses (Fig. S6). Mn-LU@Gel treatment downregulated inflammatory and oxidative stress-related genes, including Tnf, Cybb, Cd14, Il17ra, Tlr13, Cxcl14, Cxcl16, Cx3cr1, and Fpr2, while upregulating M2/reparative macrophage-associated genes such as Arg1, Mrc1, Mgl2, Socs1, Il4i1, Cish, Cd36, and Cd74. These transcriptional changes suggest that Mn-LU@Gel attenuates the macrophage-mediated inflammatory microenvironment and promotes a reparative phenotype, which may indirectly reduce ECM catabolism in IVDD.

Mn-LU@Gel restores ECM homeostasis in NP cells by reprogramming macrophage inflammation and reducing oxidative stress

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is primarily characterized by the disruption of extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis [59]. To evaluate whether Mn-LU@Gel could restore ECM integrity under inflammatory conditions, a Transwell co-culture model was established using RAW264.7 macrophages and nucleus pulposus (NP) cells stimulated by LPS (Fig. 7a).

Western blot results (Fig. 7b and c) revealed that LPS markedly reduced the expression of key anabolic markers—Aggrecan, COL2, and SOX9—while upregulating matrix-degrading enzymes MMP1, MMP3, and MMP13. Treatment with Mn-LU@Gel effectively reversed these trends, indicating potent anabolic and anti-catabolic effects.

Immunofluorescence staining further supported these findings. As shown in Fig. 7d, control cells exhibited robust expression of COL2, which co-localized with a well-organized F-actin cytoskeleton. LPS stimulation severely diminished COL2 expression and disrupted cellular morphology. Treatment with the Mn-LU@Gel extract effectively preserved the expression and pericellular distribution of COL2 and maintained a healthier cellular architecture. Conversely, staining for MMP13, a primary enzyme responsible for COL2 degradation, revealed the anti-catabolic potency of the hydrogel (Fig. 7d). LPS stimulation strongly induced MMP13 expression. In contrast, cells in the Mn-LU@Gel group showed markedly suppressed MMP13 signal, demonstrating a potent inhibition of this critical catabolic pathway [60]. Quantitative fluorescence analysis (Fig. 7f and g) confirmed that Mn-LU@Gel significantly enhanced COL2 expression and reduced MMP13 levels compared to LPS.

The regulation of ECM equilibrium was further validated at the transcriptional level by qRT-PCR. LPS stimulation significantly downregulated the expression of key anabolic genes, including Aggrecan, COL2A1, and the master transcription factor Sox9 (Fig. 7h–j). The Mn-LU@Gel treatment markedly rescued the expression of these genes, indicating a promotion of matrix synthesis. Simultaneously, LPS-induced overexpression of catabolic genes, including MMP1, MMP3, and MMP13, was significantly attenuated by Mn-LU@Gel (Fig. 7k–m).

Mechanistically, Mn-LU@Gel restores ECM homeostasis in NP cells by regulating both immune and oxidative pathways (Fig. 7n). It inhibits LPS-induced activation of the TLR/IL-17R - NF-κB signaling in macrophages, promoting their shift from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This leads to decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines and increased anti-inflammatory mediators. Simultaneously, Mn-LU@Gel nanoparticles directly alleviated intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) in NP cells, mitigating oxidative stress. This combined anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effect resulted in a more favorable microenvironment that facilitated upregulation of matrix-synthetic genes and proteins, while suppressing catabolic markers. The coordinated reprogramming of immune responses and oxidative balance underlies Mn-LU@Gel's capacity to reestablish ECM equilibrium in the degenerative disc context.

Mn-LU@Gel ameliorates disc degeneration in a rat in vivo model

The therapeutic efficacy of the Mn-LU@Gel was ultimately evaluated in a rat tail puncture-induced model of IVDD (Fig. 8a). Representative X-ray images (Fig. 8b) and quantitative analysis of the DHI% (Fig. 8c) clearly showed a severe decrease in disc height in the IVDD model group at both 4 and 8 weeks post-operation compared to the Con group. While the Gel group offered a slight mitigation, the administration of the Mn-LU@Gel resulted in a significant preservation of disc height, with the DHI% approaching near-normal levels, particularly at the 8-week time point.

T2-weighted MRI, which reflects the water and proteoglycan content of the NP, provided further evidence (Fig. 8d and e). The IVDD group exhibited a progressive loss of MRI signal intensity and higher Pfirrmann degeneration grades. In contrast, the discs treated with Mn-LU@Gel maintained a significantly higher signal intensity and lower degeneration grades, indicating successful retention of hydration and matrix integrity.

Histological evaluation with H&E and S&O staining at 8 weeks offered microscopic insights (Fig. 8f). The IVDD group displayed characteristic degenerative features: a disorganized annulus fibrosus (AF), a collapsed and fibrotic NP with severe loss of cellularity, and markedly diminished proteoglycan content. The Mn-LU@Gel treatment group, however, demonstrated a remarkable restorative phenotype, characterized by a well-structured AF, a clear NP-AF border, and a preserved NP tissue rich in cells and proteoglycans. Semi-quantitative histological scoring of NP/AF morphology, cellularity and proteoglycan content confirmed that the Mn-Lu@Gel group had significantly lower total degeneration scores than the IVDD and Gel groups (Fig. 8g).

In vivo, the Mn-LU@Gel effectively halted the progression of IVDD and promoted structural restoration, as evidenced by the maintenance of disc height, hydration content, and healthy tissue architecture. These findings strongly support the therapeutic potential of this multifunctional hydrogel for treating disc degeneration.

Mn-LU@Gel maintains matrix homeostasis and suppresses inflammation in the in vivo IVDD milieu

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the observed structural preservation, we performed IHC analysis on disc tissues to assess key anabolic and catabolic markers, as well as inflammatory mediators. Aggrecan staining in 4 weeks and 8 weeks revealed substantial ECM loss in the IVDD group (Fig. 9a), consistent with disc degeneration. While Gel alone showed minimal improvement, Mn-LU@Gel markedly preserved aggrecan content, with staining intensity approaching that of healthy controls at both 4 and 8 weeks. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 9c) confirmed a significant increase in aggrecan-positive area in the Mn-LU@Gel group compared with IVDD and Gel groups.

Conversely, the catabolic enzyme MMP-13 was highly expressed in degenerated discs, particularly within NP and inner annulus regions (Fig. 9b). Treatment with Mn-LU@Gel substantially reduced MMP-13 expression, indicating effective suppression of ECM degradation. This was further validated by quantification (Fig. 9c), which showed that MMP-13–positive area was significantly lower in the Mn-LU@Gel group at both time points.

To further assess the inflammatory status of disc tissues, we evaluated iNOS expression as an indicator of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage and cellular oxidative stress. Robust iNOS staining was observed in the IVDD group, persisting at both 4 and 8 weeks (Fig. 9d). Gel alone produced limited improvement, whereas Mn-LU@Gel markedly reduced iNOS expression, consistent with an anti-inflammatory microenvironment and reduced oxidative burden.

To further evaluate the in vivo biosafety of Mn-LU@Gel, serum biochemical parameters, routine blood indices, and histological analysis of major organs were systematically assessed. As shown in Fig. 9e, no significant differences were observed in liver and kidney function-related biochemical markers among all groups, indicating that Mn-LU@Gel did not induce apparent systemic toxicity. Similarly, routine blood analysis revealed no abnormal changes in hematological parameters, suggesting that the treatment had no adverse effects on blood physiology. Histological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys (Fig. 9f), showed normal tissue architecture without noticeable inflammation, necrosis, or structural damage across all groups.

These in vivo results demonstrate that Mn-LU@Gel effectively restores ECM integrity by promoting matrix preservation (increased aggrecan) and suppressing matrix degradation (reduced MMP-13). The notable reduction in iNOS expression further supports that Mn-LU@Gel alleviates local inflammatory stress, which aligns with its mechanistic role of inhibiting TLR/IL-17R–NF-κB activation and reducing ROS, ultimately contributing to disc tissue repair.

Conclusion

We have developed an intelligent, injectable Mn-LU@Gel that co-delivers manganese and LU for synergistic disc repair. This system demonstrates potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects by scavenging ROS, modulating macrophage polarization, and inhibiting the NF-κB pathway. Consequently, it rebalances ECM metabolism, enhancing anabolism while suppressing catabolism. Crucially, in vivo results confirm that Mn-LU@Gel effectively mitigates disc structural degeneration, positioning it as a highly promising therapeutic strategy for combating IVDD.
