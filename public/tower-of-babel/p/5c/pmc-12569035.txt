Joint analysis of sQTL and Hi-C reveals spatial proximity between sQTLs and target genes in cancer tissues
Authors: Batuhan Eralp, Emre Sefer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gene expression and regulation with or without alternative splicing are crucial for tissues and cells to function correctly. They have been studied from three almost independent perspectives at the genome level: 1- Recognition of splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTLs), 2- Expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) recognition, and 3- Recognition of longer-range physical chromatin interactions between genome segments which model 3D dynamics of cells and tissues. Even though the associations between eQTLs and longer-range chromatin interactions have been previously studied, a similar relationship between sQTLs and chromatin interactions has yet to be analyzed. In this case, examining whether sQTLs control the alternative splicing of their target genes’ mRNA via physically interacting genome segments is crucial. We have jointly analyzed high-throughput chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) and sQTL datasets over eight human cancer tissues. We have discovered a positive association between the number of genes having sQTLs and chromatin interaction frequency. Such a positive association still exists when we also control for eQTLs. Additionally, sQTLs and their target genes generally exist inside identical topologically associating domains (TADs). These findings are observed over the whole set of analyzed cancer types and functional subsets of the sQTL dataset, such as survival-related sQTLs. Furthermore, tissue-specific sQTLs are statistically enriched in tissue-specific frequently interacting regions (FIREs) in 6 out of 8 human cancer tissues (Chronic Myeloid Leukemia, Colon Adenocarcinoma, Acute Myeloid Leukemia, Lung Adenocarcinoma, Prostate Cancer, Sarcoma). Our sQTL and Hi-C datasets have shown the existence of closer spatial distance between sQTLs and their target genes with possible alternative splicing across several different cancer types in humans. Such a closer spatial distance also exists, independent of whether we integrate eQTLs into the analysis. We found that sQTLs regulate alternative splicing through chromatin interactions. Source code of the analysis in this research is available on https://github.com/seferlab/sqtlhic .

Introduction

Gene expression, gene regulation, and alternative splicing (AS) of mRNA for protein synthesis are crucial for adequately functioning tissues and cells. Gene regulation as well as gene expression differ between multiple tissues within a given species, which are mainly accountable for morphological and cellular differences. In this case, alternative splicing is responsible for gene expression differences and, consequently, for many cellular differences. Alternative Splicing is the alternative mRNA splicing throughout gene expression by which each gene can encode different proteins1. Alternative splicing, as a molecular process, results in several different transcript isoforms from a unique gene, which, as a result, increases the diversity of the proteome and the diversity of transcripts in terms of structure. The transcript frequency over the human transcriptome greatly surpasses the frequency of genes that encode proteins2. On average, every gene encodes for 7 transcript variants3. In humans, alternative splicing occurs in greater than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$90\%$$\end{document} of the overall mRNAs transcribed from genes in a species-, condition-, or cell type-specific fashion. Such splicing significantly expands the possible protein count over the available set of genes throughout the genome4.

As the accumulating evidence shows, alternative splicing events can be modulated by sequence variants throughout the genome. In this case, splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTLs), which are genetic variants affecting the possible alternative splicing events, are crucial towards complete comprehension of gene expression and regulation and, as a result, have an impact on response to drugs and susceptibility to diseases5. SQTLs basically encompass QTLs for isoform ratios6, and sQTLs can be caused by single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), which change alternative splicing patterns by modifying the splicing factor’s binding affinity to pre-mRNA, which is specific to the splicing factor sequence. Previous studies have found that no less than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%$$\end{document} of disease-originating SNPs impact splicing7. Depending on where sQTLs are located within the gene, they may have a different impact on the splicing machinery. In terms of lessening the abundance of functional mRNAs, sQTLs can cause alternative splicing events that lead to exon skipping, intron retention, or the use of alternative splice sites8,9. This can generate non-functional or less efficient mRNA transcripts, resulting in decreased functional mRNA abundance10. In terms of increasing the abundance of functional mRNAs, Certain sQTLs may promote alternative splicing forms that are more stable or efficient, leading to an increase in the production of functional mRNAs. For example, promoting the inclusion of critical exons can enhance protein function11. In another scenario, some sQTLs may induce splicing variations without affecting the function or stability of the resultant mRNAs, keeping the abundance of functional mRNAs unchanged12. sQTLs are crucial to understanding genetic variants complete impact on disease progression. For instance, alternative splicing is frequently modified across cancer tissues to generate functional and non-functional abnormal proteins, affecting cancer development. We often observe abnormal splicing motifs in cancer initiation, progression, and prognosis, and these aberrant motifs have a significant impact on metastasis, dedifferentiation, and carcinogenesis13. In particular, the gene that encodes spleen tyrosine kinase protein has a different alternatively spliced transcript isoform in breast cancer tissues, which is not expressed in normal cells14. In this case, a detailed comprehension of Alternative Splicing misregulation will be essential in developing specific gene targets to intervene in cancer progression pharmacologically.

The recent development of high-throughput sequencing techniques enables us to analyze gene expression, gene regulation, and alternative splicing at the genome level across various standpoints. Some examples of these high-throughput techniques are SNP chips, which are DNA microarrays testing genetic variation around more than 100,000 specific locations simultaneously throughout the genome, DNA sequencing, and RNA sequencing techniques1,15–18. Capturing expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) has been investigated by many studies19,20, where eQTLs represent variants in the genome that are related to gene expression levels among individuals according to robust statistics. Similarly, as defined previously, splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTLs) are genome loci that control pre-mRNA alternative splicing that might be identified over RNA-seq datasets1,15,21,22. Several computational approaches have been introduced to extract sQTLs from RNA-seq data23–25. As a result, the inference of sQTLs, particularly over cancer cells, may be a crucial step in comprehending the impact of genetic variants on cancer progression and tumorigenesis.

The significance of the identified genomic variants across gene regulation and functioning motivates many of the sQTL studies. Cis-regulatory modules (CRMs) are non-coding DNA regions regulating the transcription of the next-door genes. By modifying cis-regulatory modules (CRMs) across the genome, these sQTLs modulate their target genes’ expression. There are several examples of cis-regulatory elements, some of which are mediators, insulators, promoters, and enhancers26,27. Meanwhile, the studies on three-dimensional spatial chromatin shape have shown the significance of interactions between different chromatin segments for the regulation of genes28,29. For instance, cis-regulatory elements could regulate the expression of genes that are further away from the regulatory element via making up longer-range interactions between corresponding chromatin segments30,31. Widely adopted high-throughput chromatin conformation capture techniques such as High-throughput Chromosome Conformation Capture (Hi-C) provide interactions between chromatin segments at a genome-wide scale32–35. Such a Hi-C interaction dataset is generally composed of interaction frequencies between different chromatin segments, and it is, in general, represented as a contact matrix where the genome is partitioned into equal-sized bins. Each matrix entry shows chromatin interaction frequency (CIF), which is the read pair count aligned to a given bin pair.

Further analysis of Hi-C experiments has revealed A and B compartments, which correspond to the partition of the spatial genome into open and closed chromatin, respectively33. Among these compartments, the A compartment is related to easily accessible, transcriptionally active euchromatin. Similarly, the B compartment is related to condensed, transcriptionally inactive heterochromatin. A compartments are characterized by elevated GC content, are enriched with histone modifications associated with active transcription, often form self-interacting chromatin domains, and tend to coincide with early replication origins. In contrast, B compartments typically have lower GC content, are marked by histone modifications linked to gene repression, and are generally found near the nuclear periphery32,36. The resulting analysis of the Hi-C interaction matrix at a higher resolution has discovered topologically associating domains (TADs), which are frequently interacting, sequentially closely located interaction matrix areas37–41. TADs are pervasive genome organization units that are stable attributes of Hi-C matrices. TADs correspond greatly with cellular differentiation and long-range transcriptional control42,43.

sQTL and Hi-C studies are complementary methods focusing on non-identical gene expression and regulation characteristics. sQTL studies require a linked single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), which is the variation at one position in a DNA sequence among individuals. As a result, sQTL results are statistically defined among individuals. On the other hand, chromatin interactions are physical interactions for a given sample, and polymorphism does not need to exist in the dataset. Integrating the outcomes of sQTL and Hi-C methods will be helpful in adequately understanding the connection between them. In this case, one open question will be how important the chromatin interactions become while sQTLs regulate target genes. To the best of our knowledge, this question still needs to be answered, but similar questions on the relationship between chromatin interactions and eQTLs instead of sQTLs have been answered. For example, Duggal et al44. found that eQTLs are statistically close to their target genes in 3D over human IMR90 fibroblasts and ESCs Hi-C datasets. Such statistical closeness is more critical for eQTLs detected overlapping with CRMs and eQTLs detected inside the same topologically associating domain (TAD). According to their study, genomic segments with eQTLs generally have a greater chromatin interaction frequency (CIF). Similar to the findings by Duggal et al., another research19found that CRM-enriched eQTLs are spatially close to target gene promoters over Hi-C datasets. Hi-C datasets in both research are solely for cell lines, whereas eQTL conclusions were derived over both cell line and tissue datasets. Lastly45, has analyzed the proximity between Hi-C and eQTL datasets, and their results have shown the spatial closeness between eQTLs and their corresponding genes among multiple human primary tissues as well as enrichment of the two within the same TAD and compartment domains. The remaining studies accepted the association between eQTL and Hi-C data results a priori. For instance, some of these studies focus on detecting regulatory SNPs46, while others focus on predicting enhancer-promoter interactions47,48.

Even though interactions between chromatin segments are considered one of the fundamental processes defining eQTLs and sQTLs, no direct proof of such a relationship for sQTLs exists for tissues. sQTLs are tissue-specific24,49. Furthermore50, has extracted frequently interacting special regions called Frequently Interacting Regions (FIREs) that are also hotspots of local interactions between chromatin segments over the Hi-C dataset. The existence and distribution of those FIREs are strongly tissue-specific, and they are defined as bins that often interact with nearby segments that are less than 200 kb apart. In this case, the degree of overlap between tissue-specific sQTLs and FIREs has yet to be discovered.

sQTL and Hi-C datasets have recently and systematically become available for several human cell lines and primary tissues, especially cancer tissues. For instance49, has developed a CancerSplicingQTL database, which includes sQTL datasets for 33 cancer types. Various experiments have also generated Hi-C interaction datasets for many cancer tissues32,51–56. Similarly57, has systematically mapped eQTLs at a genome-wide scale across 33 cancer tissues. Among these cancer resources, eight cancer tissues overlap, as seen in Table 6, which facilitates a robust assessment of the connection between sQTLs and chromatin interactions across several cancer tissues while also controlling for eQTLs. Table 6 in the Results section summarizes these datasets.

In this paper, we have jointly analyzed the relationship between sQTLs and Hi-C datasets across eight cancer tissues while considering eQTLs. We have identified a positive association between the frequency of sGenes and CIF, where sGene is a gene whose expression is significantly related to an sQTL. Such positive association still exists when we also control for eGenes, which represent genes whose expression is significantly associated with an eQTL instead of an sQTL. Additionally, sQTLs colocalize with their target genes more frequently inside the same TAD than a randomly created control dataset. This colocalization result is also valid when we consider subsets of sQTLs, such as GWAS sQTLs49, which overlap with identified GWAS (Genome-wide association study) regions associated with linkage disequilibrium regions and survival sQTLs49, which impact patient survival time for a given cancer. All our results are compatible across all the evaluated cancer types and tissues. Due to the tissue specificity of both FIREs and sQTLs50, we have analyzed the degree of association between tissue-specific FIREs and tissue-specific sQTLs and identified a positive relationship between them across most of the cancer tissues.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze the relation between sQTLs and chromatin interactions across a number of human cancer types and to analyze the association between tissue-specific FIREs and tissue-specific sQTLs. Our results are important to enhance our comprehension of the roles of sQTLs and chromatin interactions on gene expression and regulation through alternative splicing. Results

Hypothesis 1: the number of chromosome-wide chromatin interactions is significantly positively related with the number of sGenes

As a hypothesis, we can expect a pair of loci located as an sQTL-gene association to interact more frequently, granted that 3D chromatin organization impacts the sQTLs regulation of their target genes. We test this hypothesis by fitting negative binomial and linear regressions and evaluating the association between CIF and the number of sGenes associations between two loci over an interaction dataset with 10 kb resolution by using these regression models. As discussed in the Methods section, we have ignored the interactions within the same bins and only used sQTL-gene pairs that are located in different bins. Once we have controlled for the genomic distance between any loci pair, the relationship between a number of sGenes and CIF becomes significantly positive (p-value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<0.001$$\end{document}) overall human cancer tissues as seen in Fig. 1a. For instance, in LAML cancer tissue, according to negative binomial regression, the relationship between the number of sGenes and CIF is 0.21 (p-value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<0.001$$\end{document}). According to this effect, CIF would have been \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.23 = e^{0.21}$$\end{document} times greater for each additional scene in a single pair of bins. This effect coefficient changes across different cancer tissues, taking values between 0.05 and 0.21. Across these experiments, effect sizes are similar to each other for relatively more similar tissues such as CML (Chronic Myeloid Leukemia) and LAML (Acute Myeloid Leukemia). Furthermore, in the same regression models, the coefficient between CIF and genomic distance is significantly negative across all cancer tissues. Such a negative coefficient is expected since interaction frequency between two loci in general decreases as the distance between these loci increases33. They have also discovered that a pair of genome regions with different compartment types has a lower interaction frequency than regions inside the same compartment types. According to this result, gene density might play a critical role in chromatin interactions. To control for the essential role of gene density in our analysis, as seen in Fig. 3a, we have integrated the absolute difference in tested gene counts among the pair of bins as an extra attribute into the regression framework. We have discovered that the relationship between the difference in gene density between pairs of bins and CIF is significantly negative in all cancer tissues. Moreover, we have conducted a stratification analysis by stratifying the data based on the gene density difference. Stratification divides the population into subsets (called strata), within each of which an independent sample is selected. In this analysis, the gene count difference ranges between 0 and 8, and sample sizes are relatively small for the strata with differences greater than 5. The results were similar to those in Figs 1a-3a for many of the analyzed strata. In a few strata, the estimated coefficients for sGene count were negative but without statistical significance.

The degree of association between tissue-specific chromatin interactions and tissue-specific sQTLs is useful for identifying genes regulated by sQTLs via chromatin interactions in the analyzed cancer tissue. For instance, we analyzed 3961 tissue-specific FIREs and 11005 tissue-specific sQTLs in lung cancer tissue. Considering both factors, we identified 534 lung adenocarcinoma-specific sQTLs within these lung adenocarcinoma-specific FIREs. Among these sQTLs, we found statistically significant associations with six genes: XPA (DNA Damage Recognition And Repair Factor), EGFR (Epidermal growth factor receptor), KRAS (Kirsten rat sarcoma virus), DDR2 (Discoidin domain-containing receptor 2), ALK (Anaplastic lymphoma kinase), and ADGRB2 (Adhesion G protein-coupled receptor B2). EGFR is a transmembrane protein and an epidermal growth factor receptor that regulates cellular pathways involved in cellular proliferation. It is expressed in more than 50% of lung adenocarcinomas and is a critical focus in therapeutic strategies for these tumors60,61. KRAS proteins transmit growth or differentiation signals from the cell exterior to the nucleus, and mutations leading to increased KRAS expression are well-studied events in lung adenocarcinoma and other epithelial cancers62. DDR2 mutations are also implicated in adenocarcinoma development and are targeted in lung cancer therapy63. ALK plays a role in gut development during embryonic development, but is also involved in lung cancer64–66. ADGRB2 exhibits tissue-specific expression patterns during development as a signaling receptor37, and its expression levels increase during lung development67. The transcription start site of ADGRB2 is located at position 32,192,718 on chromosome 1, approximately 47 kb away from the tissue-specific FIRE located between positions 32,240,000 and 32,320,000 on chromosome 1.

Hypothesis 3: TADs and compartment domains are enriched in terms of GWAS and cancer survival sQTL-gene associations

Similar to the analysis in the previous section, we investigated whether topologically associating domains (TADs) are enriched for GWAS and cancer survival sQTL-gene associations49. GWAS sQTLs overlap with genomic regions identified by GWAS as being associated with linkage disequilibrium regions, providing insights into the functional interpretation of genetic variants. Survival sQTLs, on the other hand, are associated with patient survival time in cancer, making them crucial for prioritizing sQTLs that might influence cancer prognosis. According to Table 4, both GWAS sQTLs and cancer survival sQTLs show statistically significant enrichment for sQTL-gene pairs located within the same TAD compared to simulated datasets across nearly all cancer types, as determined by Fisher’s exact test. This indicates that the physical proximity within TADs may play a role in the regulatory mechanisms mediated by these sQTLs. Furthermore, when logistic regression is applied while considering genomic distances as an independent variable, the odds ratio for an SNP-gene pair being located within the same TAD remains significantly greater in real datasets compared to simulated datasets across all cancer tissues, confirming the robustness of these associations.

The degree of association between tissue-specific chromatin interactions and tissue-specific sQTLs is crucial for identifying genes regulated by sQTLs via chromatin interactions in the analyzed cancer tissue. We found both survival-sQTLs and GWAS-sQTLs to be significantly associated with four genes specific to lung adenocarcinoma development: EGFR, KRAS, DDR2, and ALK. Additionally, survival-sQTLs are associated with APC, CASP8, and TP53 genes. APC and CASP8 are implicated in the metastasis of lung cancer68. APC, a tumor suppressor gene involved in cell adhesion, exhibits mutations in lung adenocarcinoma69. CASP8 polymorphisms influence prognosis in advanced lung cancer even post-therapy70. Hypothesis 4: Cancer tissue-specific sQTLs are enriched in cancer tissue-specific FIREs

sQTLs exhibit significant tissue-specificity49. Building upon this finding, we investigated whether tissue-specific sQTLs are enriched within tissue-specific FIREs. Across the 8 cancer tissues analyzed, we identified a total of 443,567 tissue-specific sQTLs. Calculated odds ratios indicate that tissue-specific sQTLs are enriched in 7 out of 8 tissues, with values exceeding 1, suggesting an enrichment within tissue-specific FIREs. Specifically, 6 of these tissues (CML, COAD, LAML, LUAD, PRAD, SARC) exhibit statistically significant odds ratios greater than one after Bonferroni correction, as detailed in Table 5; GBM nearly meets this criterion. This statistically significant enrichment suggests that tissue-specific sQTLs may exert their regulatory effects through tissue-specific chromatin interactions, which is particularly evident in at least seven cancer tissues. However, we observed a negative association in Multiple myeloma (MM) cancer tissue. Potential reasons for this negative association include 1) Variations in the cancer samples used to construct the interaction and eQTL datasets, possibly collected from different individuals or different locations within the lung cancer tissue; 2) Complex associations between chromatin organization and sQTLs specific to lung cancer tissue compared to other tissues; 3) Heterogeneity in the samples, reflecting diverse cell type compositions. Further experimental datasets are necessary to assess the impact of these factors comprehensively. Discussion

Gene regulation is understood to be affected by sQTLs and the spatial organization of chromatin. In this research, we have focused on identifying the association between spatial chromatin interactions and sQTL-gene associations across 8 human cancer tissues in a systematic way. We have identified CIF to have a positive association with eGenes count. Also, TADs are enriched for sQTL-gene associations. We observed these results in all 8 assessed human cancer tissues. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to come up with well-grounded statistical support for these results across multiple cancer tissues. Furthermore, the added perspective of TADs is crucial for our understanding of alternative splicing. Establishing sQTLs and their target genes tend to reside within the same TAD is a significant aspect of our research; though expected, this correlation provides concrete statistical evidence that sQTLs regulate their target genes through chromatin interactions. The correlation between regulation manner and residence within a TAD is suggested by45, and our results are statistically solidified with a similar approach.

Tissue-specific sQTLs are statistically significantly enriched in tissue-specific FIREs in 6 out of 8 human cancer tissues. According to this result, tissue-specific sQTLs could operate via tissue-specific chromatin interactions in these six tissues. However, the association between tissue-specific sQTLs and chromatin interactions is significantly negative in lung adenocarcinoma tissue. Several factors can explain these results. The more complex functioning of lung cancer tissue may be the principal underlying mechanism. Another factor could be heterogeneous tissue cell types or sample sources. Noise may have introduced possible heterogeneity into our analysis, and we might have obtained more robust results if we had analyzed more homogeneous datasets.

One of our main observations is the correlation between TADs and sQTL locations. This may support a current hypothesis: Disruption of the established TAD structure near an sQTL could lead to significant differences in the alternative splicing pattern of its associated gene. For instance, if an sQTL associated with a splicing gene is located inside a TAD and is also linked to an enhancer (such as one from the collection of inside-TAD sQTLs associated with functional regulatory elements examined earlier), this hypothesis could be experimentally tested by physically separating the relevant parts of the sQTL so that the splicing gene and mutation are not in close proximity in 3D space. The experimental method described in71 allows for testing such hypotheses by manipulating and merging TADs.

The association between chromatin interaction frequencies and sQTLs (splicing Quantitative Trait Loci) can indicate complex, sometimes unexpected, regulatory effects, where sQTLs influence gene expression through chromatin conformation and interactions. In some cases, an association of greater chromatin interaction frequencies with sQTLs might provide evidence in favour of surprising unilateral effects caused by such sQTLs, which have been observed regardless of functional consequences for these genomic entities. This surprising unilateral specificity has been discussed in72, where they discuss long-range chromatin interactions and their potential link to gene regulation through promoters and enhancers, relevant to sQTLs’ regulatory mechanisms. Another study by73 provides insight into how chromatin accessibility, influenced by genetic variants like sQTLs, can be linked to altered chromatin interactions and gene expression patterns.

We have conducted our analyses using Hi-C datasets with a 10 kb resolution, significantly higher than that used in similar joint eQTL and Hi-C analyses45. The strength of sQTL analysis is mainly influenced by sample size. Due to the sample size in our datasets, we may have overlooked some SNP-gene splicing associations in this study. Additionally, the analysis of enrichment in topologically associating domains did not consider linkage disequilibrium (LD) among sQTLs. If there are any effects of LD, they are likely to have balanced out between simulated and real datasets with a high probability.

Our study may have several areas for improvement. For instance, our approach could be further refined to control for additional confounding factors. Additionally, our datasets may have inherent limitations. For example, eQTL and sQTL studies are valuable for understanding the genetic regulation of gene expression. However, like any scientific research, eQTL and sQTL studies have their weaknesses and limitations. Common weaknesses associated with these studies include population bias, sample size, and issues of causality74. Similarly, Hi-C datasets are collected from an ensemble of cell populations, where individual cell data may vary slightly. Moreover, although we independently matched Hi-C datasets with the remaining sQTL and eQTL datasets for each cancer tissue, the combined Hi-C, sQTL, and eQTL datasets were obtained from different sources, potentially introducing heterogeneity that may not have been eliminated in our analysis.

Conclusions

As a summary, we have discovered that sQTLs are spatially located near their target genes, potentially influencing alternative splicing across multiple human cancer tissues. This proximity exists regardless of whether we integrate eQTLs into the analysis. Our findings indicate that sQTLs regulate alternative splicing through chromatin interactions, which are robust across different Hi-C binning resolutions. These results contribute to understanding how sQTLs and chromatin interactions complement each other in regulating gene expression through alternative splicing mechanisms. In the future, our methods could be applied to other genomic datasets involving pairwise interactions, such as promoter-enhancer pairs. Furthermore, our approach can be adapted to control for additional confounding factors effectively. As more high-resolution interaction datasets specific to tissues become available, our proposed approach could provide deeper insights into how chromatin structure influences association pairs across various cell types.

We utilized sQTL, eQTL, and Hi-C datasets across eight human cancer tissues: Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML), Colon Adenocarcinoma (COAD), Glioblastoma (GBM), Acute Myeloid Leukemia (LAML), Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD), Multiple Myeloma (MM), Prostate Cancer (PRAD), and Sarcoma (SARC)32,49,51–57,75. Further details about these datasets can be found in Tables 6, 7. In our study, we analyzed only autosomal chromosomes with hg19 as the reference genome. Additionally, Fig. 10 shows the log-transformed Hi-C interaction matrix at 10 Kb resolution for chromosome 20 in GBM. At a high level, our analysis can be summarized as in Fig. 11. The number of raw intra-chromosomal unique read pairs across eight human cancer tissues exceeds 3 billion, with over 800 million pairs having a distance greater than 15 kb between their endpoints. We analyzed Hi-C datasets with a binning resolution of 10 kb. Enrichment study of sQTL-gene relationships in topologically associating and compartment domains

To assess whether splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTLs) are spatially closer to certain genes (such as EGFR or DDR2) than expected by chance, we performed a permutation-based significance test using Hi-C chromatin interaction data. For each target gene, we first computed the observed mean 3D chromatin distance between the gene’s genomic bin and all specific sQTL-containing bins. This observed mean distance served as the test statistic and reflects the average physical proximity between the sQTLs and the gene in the three-dimensional chromatin structure. To evaluate whether this observed proximity was statistically significant, we generated a null distribution by randomly reassigning the genomic locations of the specific sQTLs 1000 times while preserving their count. For each permutation, we recalculated the mean Hi-C distance to the gene of interest. An empirical p-value was then derived by computing the proportion of permutations in which the randomized mean distance was less than or equal to the observed distance.

We also assessed whether TADs are enriched for sQTL-gene associations across all human cancer tissues. The enrichment analysis proceeded as follows: For each tested SNP-gene pair, we generated a matched pseudo-pair as a control. This pseudo-pair was created by flipping the SNP’s direction while keeping the gene’s transcription start site (TSS) position unchanged, ensuring the SNP remained at the same distance but on the opposite side of the TSS. For example, if the actual SNP were located 90 kb upstream of the TSS, its flipped position in the control dataset would be 90 kb downstream of the TSS. This procedure maintained the distributions of SNP-TSS distances and gene locations identical between the pseudo pairs and actual SNP-gene pairs. If the flipped location extended beyond the chromosome boundary, both the exact pair and its matched pseudo-pair were excluded from further analysis.

We analyzed SNP-gene pairs using two attributes: (1) whether the transcription start site (TSS) of a gene and SNP belong to the same TAD, and (2) whether the SNP-gene pair is an actual pair or a pseudo pair. This resulted in a 2x2 matrix where we observed if the probability of SNP-gene pairs switching from being within the same TAD to different TADs is significantly greater. To test this hypothesis, we applied McNemar’s test. Additionally, we evaluated the relationship between these two attributes using Fisher’s exact test.

Additionally, we incorporated distance into the analysis using logistic regression, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y \approx X + D$$\end{document}. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D$$\end{document} represents the distance between the gene’s transcription start site (TSS) and SNP, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X$$\end{document} indicates whether the SNP-gene pair is actual, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y$$\end{document} indicates whether the pair is located within the same TAD. Furthermore, we stratified the dataset by genomic distance, ranging from 10 Kb to 250 Kb, and conducted stratified analyses to examine the relationship between these variables.

We applied a similar procedure to compartment domains while analyzing survival-sQTLs and GWAS-sQTLs.

Analysis of cancer tissue-specific FIREs and cancer tissue-specific sQTLs

Lastly, we analyzed whether the Hi-C dataset is tissue-specific. We characterized tissue-specific FIREs as those detected exclusively in specific cancer tissues but not in others. Subsequently, we assessed whether tissue-specific FIREs are enriched near genes and examined their association with tissue-specific sQTLs defined similarly by the CancerSplicingQTL database.

We analyzed whether tissue-specific sQTLs are enriched in tissue-specific FIREs for each cancer tissue. In this enrichment analysis, we partitioned all sQTLs based on whether they overlap with tissue-specific FIREs and whether the sQTL itself is tissue-specific. We calculated the odds ratio, where the numerator is the proportion of tissue-specific sQTLs mapped to tissue-specific FIREs, and the denominator is the proportion of tissue-specific sQTLs mapped to remaining FIREs within the same tissue.
