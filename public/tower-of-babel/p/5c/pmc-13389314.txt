Clinical Pharmacology of Heart Failure in Frailty
Authors: Mai H Duong, Martha Gulati, Parag Goyal, Shaun G Goodman, Julee McDonagh, Sarah Hilmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Frailty is an important consideration in the pharmacological management of heart failure (HF). A complex bidirectional relationship exists between HF and frailty, particularly for individuals with advanced stages of HF and severe frailty. Physiological changes, due to biological ageing and frailty in the presence of multiple chronic conditions and polypharmacy can limit optimisation of pharmacotherapy due to the increased risk of severe adverse events, leading to poorer outcomes in individuals with HF. While our understanding of the impact of frailty on HF aetiology and treatments is still emerging, HF may contribute to or worsen symptoms of frailty. This narrative review describes the interactions between clinical pharmacology and frailty and how to consider these in HF management. We examine the impact of physiological changes in HF and frailty on pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics effectiveness and safety of HF pharmacotherapy in men and women. There are significant opportunities to integrate frailty assessment in emerging research and models of care for HF.

There is a complex bidirectional relationship between frailty and heart failure (HF). Frailty, a multi-system syndrome that reduces an individual’s physiological reserve, can increase the risk of adverse outcomes in patients with HF.1 The use of objective frailty scores, such as the Rockwood frailty index (FI) and Fried’s phenotype, can screen for frailty status.2,3 Frailty risk depends on cumulative deficits in physical, psychological, social, cognitive, nutritional and pharmacological characteristics.4,5 People with HF and frailty may experience diminished capacity to maintain homeostasis while optimising HF pharmacotherapy, putting them at risk of treatment failure, severe adverse drug events (ADE) and poorer outcomes. Physiological, social and cognitive risks associated with frailty may contribute to variable tolerability and non-adherence to HF pharmacotherapy leading to poorer outcomes. Similarly, HF may contribute to or accelerate the progression of frailty symptoms.

Ageing is complex and involves consideration of chronological age (fixed number of years lived) and biological age (modifiable physiological changes in cells and tissue). With ageing, multi-system domain function, related to frailty and diseases including cardiovascular disease (CVD), can reflect a person’s age more completely than the number of years lived, which is reflected in the emerging evidence examining the impact of frailty in HF.6 Multi-organ physiological dysfunction during the ageing process puts older people at high risk of CVD and multimorbidity. The presence of comorbidities often requires HF patients to receive concurrent treatment for CVD and other conditions such as cancer, diabetes, obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, anaemia and chronic kidney disease.7

Management of multimorbidity and CVD with pharmacological treatments increases polypharmacy (defined as the use of ≥5 regular medications) and hyperpolypharmacy (≥10 regular medications).8 Polypharmacy increases the risk of drug–drug interactions, drug-disease interactions and ADEs. Consequently, adherence to complex medication regimens becomes increasingly challenging for older individuals with cognitive and/or functional impairment and limited access to healthcare services. Where patients with comprehensively assessed frailty cannot tolerate optimal HF pharmacotherapy, a personalised approach to management of HF is required, with consideration of the risks of harm related to frailty balanced with the potential benefits of HF pharmacotherapy in the individual. Optimisation of pharmacotherapy is challenging in HF patients with frailty, particularly in people aged >75 years who have changing goals of care.9,10 This narrative review describes how physiological changes in people with HF and frailty may affect the clinical pharmacology of HF pharmacotherapy, specifically pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics, sex-specific differences, adverse events and withdrawal effects of HF pharmacotherapy.

Methods

The authors determined the relevant topics to form the framework of the review based on our research and clinical expertise. Specific literature searches were performed in Medline and Embase to inform each section, including clinical trials, systematic and narrative reviews, original research articles, guidelines, consensus statements and opinion pieces.

Epidemiology

HF is increasingly prevalent as the population ages, affecting more than 64 million people worldwide (8.5 per 1,000 inhabitants).11,12 The prevalence of HF in developed countries is estimated at 1–2% of the adult population and 10% in those ≥75 years, with an age and sex-adjusted incidence of 30,000 (2.1 per 1,000 patient years) new cases of HF diagnosed each year in individuals aged ≥65 years.13 This places a heavy burden on the healthcare system and resources, despite optimisation of new and emerging treatments which have the potential to improve patient survival and outcomes.14 Frailty is prevalent in 4% of people >50 years, but rapidly increases to 17% of people aged ≥65 years.15

Frailty is even more prevalent in HF, estimated to affect 39% (95% CI [0.33–0.45]) of people with HF.16 This increases to 3 of every 4 individuals ≥75 years living with HF.3,17 Frailty risk in people with HF is associated with prolonged and more frequent hospital stays, higher mortality and healthcare costs.18 In chronic HF, frailty is associated with a 1.5-fold increased risk of death and hospitalisation.19 Increased frailty risk predicts the risk of hospitalisation over time in a dose-dependent way.20,21 Older people with HF and frailty are at higher risk of suboptimal treatment of HF and adverse outcomes. The severity of frailty is an independent predictor of the underuse of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for HF, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), β-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) and sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2Is).22 Social isolation and loneliness, which are common in frailty, are associated with hospital readmission and mortality.23

Sex differences have been reported in HF, where for women, compared with men, there is worse quality of life, increased survival, higher prevalence of HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and the presence of more chronic conditions, which contributes to sex differences in frailty.24,25 A meta-analysis of people with HF demonstrated that women have a 25% higher risk of frailty than men.26 Frailty is common in all types of HF, including HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and HFpEF. Among women who live in the community aged ≥70 years with HF, those who were frail were found to have a higher risk of falls or fracture-related hospitalisation and all-cause mortality than those who were not frail.27

Frailty Assessment

There is no gold standard frailty score for HF. More than 60 tools have been developed to assess frailty, most fitting into one of two major conceptual frameworks: the physical frailty phenotype and the deficit accumulation model (Frailty Index, FI).28 These tools include categorical measures such as phenotype scores, the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) and the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS); or continuous measures using the FI.4,29–33 Because HF and frailty share overlapping symptoms, such as decreased exercise tolerance and exhaustion, finding a frailty assessment tool that is not heavily weighted with physical symptoms is important to improve validity in people with HF. Also, including a measure of weight loss as part of a frailty tool for HF may be inappropriate due to the frequent fluctuations in weight with fluid status. Frailty tools that include composite measures of cognition and mood may offer additional prognostic benefits in HF patients.34 To our knowledge, there are no preferred approaches to evaluating frailty in people with HF, including by sex.

Objective frailty assessment tools have been applied in different clinical settings, with various advantages and disadvantages. Routine objective frailty assessment at all stages of HF may support early detection of frailty to employ prevention and interventions to improve overall prognosis, outcomes, functional status and avoid disability. Subjective assessments of frailty often incorrectly identify and frequently underestimate frailty in HF patients, which may overlap with symptoms of worsening HF. A study of patients with HF referred for advanced therapies found that frailty was significantly underestimated by clinicians, with frailty identified in approximately 2–18% of patients by cardiologists on subjective assessments compared with 63% of patients with the objective modified frailty phenotype.35

Physiological Changes in Frailty and Heart Failure

Over a lifetime, cumulative molecular and cellular damage can lead to a loss of physiological reserves in multiple organ systems, which increases the risk of disease, mortality and disability. These physiological changes may be amplified in frailty.36–38 Pathophysiological changes in frailty and HF can have an impact on the pharmacokinetics of cardiovascular (CV) and non-CV medications (Table 1).

Understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying frailty continues to evolve. Emerging evidence suggests that frailty may represent a clinical state resembling cellular ‘accelerated ageing’, characterised by chronic inflammation, cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction and deregulated nutrient sensing, and the resulting impairment of multiple physiological systems.39–41 This makes it difficult to differentiate the effects of frailty on pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics from the effects of ageing and disease. Increased oxidative stress and inflammation overlap in the ageing process and CVD, such as HF.42 Overlapping physiological and pathophysiological changes can occur simultaneously and involve multiple organs including the brain, heart, skeletal muscle and circulatory system (Figure 1). In HF, physiological changes may occur as a result of systemic lowered arterial perfusion, venous congestion and neurohormonal activation, which can negatively affect key organ functions beyond the heart, particularly the kidney, liver and gastrointestinal tract (Table 1). Dysregulation of cytokines, involving interleukin-1β and tumour necrosis factor α (TNF-α), trigger muscle wasting and increase oxidative stress, leading to sarcopenia (loss of muscle mass and strength) in frailty and HF and is also linked to cognitive decline.43

The impact of frailty on sex-specific differences in clinical outcomes in HF is unclear. Differences may be driven by worse symptoms of sleep impairment and pain intensity among women, compared to greater body composition characteristics, such as lower appendicular muscle mass, higher body fat percentage and lower haemoglobin, among men.44 Women are more likely to have HFpEF with increased left ventricular (LV) mass and higher N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels in HF compared with men.24

Absorption and Distribution

Biological ageing may lead to a reduced first-pass effect that increases the oral bioavailability and plasma concentration of some CV medications, such as metoprolol, nitrates and dabigatran.45 Frailty may delay the absorption and increase the bioavailability of some orally administered CV medications, due to impaired gastric motility and emptying, or reduced first-pass metabolism.36,38,45 Compared to robust older adults, those with frailty have reduced diversity of the microbiome and less abundant short-chain fatty acid-producing micro-organisms.46 These changes may be pro-inflammatory and may affect passive and active drug absorption and drug metabolism by the gut. Older people with frailty may also have lower muscle mass (sarcopenia) and higher adiposity, both of which are more common in women, affecting volume of distribution. Increased chronic inflammation, which is associated with reduced plasma albumin and increased α1-acid glycoprotein may also alter distribution of highly protein-bound drugs in the body.36,47

Metabolism and Elimination

Cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes are significantly altered during HF, influencing disease progression and drug metabolism. In HF, CYP activity may be affected by chronic hypoxia. Specific cardiac CYP isoforms (e.g. 1B1, 2J2, 4A) are typically upregulated during cardiac remodelling, while hepatic CYP activity may change, impacting drug efficacy.48 The inhibition of enzymes can reduce biotransformation that may increase drug plasma concentrations and ADRs, resulting in reduced maintenance dose requirements. For prodrugs, such as ACEI and clopidogrel, reduced biotransformation to active metabolites may reduce effectiveness.45 Inducing drug metabolism, which can occur with drug interactions, can decrease the plasma concentration of drugs, requiring an increased maintenance dose and vice versa. Phase I metabolism is reduced in healthy ageing and both Phase I and II metabolism may be impaired in frailty.38 Hepatic drug clearance can be reduced by decreased hepatic blood flow caused by frailty, HF or medications and hypoalbuminaemia that reduces plasma protein binding, resulting in higher plasma drug levels.49 Serum concentrations of liver enzymes, especially alanine transaminase (ALT), are generally lower in frail older people.50 This is important to consider when monitoring liver function, particularly when prescribing highly metabolised drugs or drugs with hepatotoxicity.

Reduced renal clearance also increases the risk of increased serum drug concentrations resulting in ADRs. In HF, renal perfusion is reduced and acute or chronic renal impairment is common.51 Glomerular filtration rate is reduced in old age, and further reduced in frailty, with implications for renal drug clearance.52 Creatinine-based estimates of glomerular filtration rate may overestimate renal function in the presence of sarcopenia. The alternative serum cystatin C-based estimates of renal function are not as dependent on muscle mass but may be influenced by inflammation.53 Lower doses for women compared with men may also be needed owing to physiological sex differences in drug metabolism and elimination.54

Pharmacodynamics

The literature describing pharmacodynamic changes in frailty and HF is scarce.9 Pharmacodynamic responses resulting in therapeutic and adverse effects of HF pharmacotherapy, such as efficacy/Emax and potency/EC50, are not well characterised in people with frailty. The therapeutic index of HF medications is poorly understood in people with frailty and HF, who may be more vulnerable to adverse effects, potentially leading to lower tolerated doses and worse adherence.55,56 Different doses for women compared with men may also be needed owing to physiological sex differences in drug effects.24

Drug Interactions

Drug interactions with HF medications may also exacerbate frailty risks. For example, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) and loop diuretics can increase the risk of lithium toxicity and exacerbate delirium.45,57 Risk of falls due to interactions with other drugs may be considered in patients with frailty on digoxin, loop diuretics and calcium channel blockers experiencing digoxin toxicity, urinary incontinence or hypotension. Supplementary Table 1 provides common examples of potential drug-drug and drug-herbal product interactions with HF medications that may potentially exacerbate frailty. Some CV medications, such as sotalol, flecainide and dronedarone, and non-CV medications, such as doxazosin, thiazolidinediones, saxagliptin, alogliptin and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, can cause or worsen HF symptoms.58,59 Non-dihydropyridine and short-acting calcium channel blockers, such as diltiazem, verapamil and nifedipine, can exacerbate HF.

Pharmacotherapy

Prescribing

Pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments are recommended for the management of various HF phenotypes. GDMTs, also known as the four pillars of HF pharmacotherapy, are recommended by international cardiac societies as the gold-standard first-line pharmacological treatments for the management of chronic and acute HF.60 These include renin angiotensin system inhibitors (RASI), β-blockers, MRA and SGLT2Is.

Efficacy

New and emerging pharmacotherapies have provided additional benefits to managing HF. SGLT2Is demonstrate CV mortality and HF hospitalisation benefits. In the EMPEROR-Preserved trial, HFpEF patients with moderate FI score (0.30–0.39) demonstrated a 23% reduction in hospitalisation and mortality with empagliflozin compared with the placebo groups.61 The benefit for HF hospitalisations was similar for patients taking SGLT2Is with ejection fraction >40%.62 Angiotensin receptor antagonist/blocker (ARB)-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI) are preferred to ACEIs for the benefits of improved outcomes including better quality of life (QoL).63,64 Further research is needed to understand the effect of glucagon-like-peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators and cardiac myosin activators for acute decompensation in the presence of frailty.

International guidelines support the optimisation of GDMT in HFrEF with a high certainty of evidence and benefits for mortality and hospitalisation.58,60 In HFrEF and HFpEF, clinical trials also support the use of therapies to improve HF symptoms and reduce hospitalisations when prescribed at target doses.65 There are limited clinical trials involving frailty assessment in interventions to treat acute and severe onset of HF symptoms requiring hospitalisation. There are no high-quality trials evaluating frailty for ACEIs, ARBs and β-blockers. A post hoc analysis of frailty-specific treatment effect on composite CV death and HF hospitalisation in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) (Figure 2, Supplementary Table 4) mostly identified studies that reported outcomes in patients with HFpEF. Use of SGLT2Is in frailty demonstrated improved benefits in primary outcomes for treatment of HFpEF and HFrEF.61,62,66

In the FINEARTS-HF trial, the non-steroidal MRA finerenone reduced the risk of the primary composite outcome of CV death and total HF events and improved health-related QoL in patients with HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) or HFpEF.67 In a pre-specified secondary analysis according to frailty status of the 6,001 patients randomised in FINEARTS-HF trial, FI was calculable in 5,952 patients (mean age 72 years; 46% female).68 Compared with patients with no frailty, those with more frailty and most frailty had an almost two- and four-fold higher risk of the primary outcome, respectively. However, the beneficial effects of finerenone on the primary outcome (CV death and total worsening HF events) and QoL did not vary significantly by frailty class. Further, the effects of finerenone compared with placebo on experiencing hypotension, elevated creatinine level, hyperkalaemia or hypokalaemia did not differ by frailty class. A recent meta-analysis demonstrates GLP-1 receptor agonists reduced the composite of CV death or worsening HF event compared to placebo (HR 0.68, 95% CI [0.51–0.89], I2=47%, p=0006).69 While there are no frailty data for RCTs of GLP-1 receptor agonists, patients with frailty may be at increased risk of sarcopenia, hypoglycaemia, dehydration and falls.

The evidence shows a lower proportion of women are recruited to clinical trials compared to real-world populations. The prevalence of HFpEF is higher in women, but women in these RCTs made up <50% of study populations, ranging from 18–60% across frailty groups (Supplementary Table 4). Women have a higher risk of frailty, but there is no significant sex difference in composite outcomes identified between frailty groups in these trials. The DELIVER and EMPEROR-preserved trials reported reduced risk of composite outcomes in frail HFpEF patients on SGLT2Is, with the proportion of women being 44% and 45%, respectively.66,67 The proportion of women was 23% in the DAPA-HF trial, which reported reduced risk of composite outcomes in HFrEF patients across all frailty groups.68 The EMPEROR-preserved trial reported more women participants were severely frail.67

The PARAGON-HF trial reported reduced risk of composite outcomes from sacubitril/valsartan compared to valsartan in women (adjusted rate ratio 0.73; 95% CI [0.60–0.90]) but no difference in men (adjusted rate ratio 1.02, 95% CI [0.83–1.24], p=0.022).70 A secondary analysis of the PARAGON-HF trial by frailty status showed higher prevalence of frailty in men with HFpEF, but the proportion of women recruited in the trial was less than observed in real-world populations.71 Although no RCT of digoxin with secondary frailty analysis was identified, lower digoxin concentrations may be considered in women due to higher risk of death in women compared to men (HR 1.23, 95% CI [1.02–1.47]).72 As our understanding of HF aetiology and treatments evolves, it is expected that more data will better inform treatment selection for people with frailty with all HF phenotypes.

Safety

Adverse Drug Events

The use of HF pharmacotherapy requires continuous vigilance to adequately control HF symptoms while monitoring tolerance to adverse effects, often exacerbated in frailty by interactions with comorbidities and polypharmacy. While there is clear benefit to treating HF with pharmacotherapy, most HF patients experience at least one ADE.73 In frailty, ADEs become increasingly complex to manage as there is reduced capacity to recover from stressors (including effects of drug use and drug withdrawal), while simultaneously managing multiple comorbidities, polypharmacy, cognitive impairment and changing goals of care.

Diuretics, β-blockers and RASIs are among the most common therapeutic classes involved in ADEs.74 A systematic review by Butzner et al. reported that bradycardia, dizziness, hypotension, hyperkalaemia, cough and renal impairment were the most common ADEs across GDMTs in patients with HFrEF.75 In patients with frailty, these symptoms may also exacerbate other geriatric syndromes like hyponatraemia causing confusion, hypotension leading to falls, and urinary frequency resulting in incontinence. However, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated sarcopenia in HF is not associated with loop diuretics (OR 1.09, 95% CI [0.73–1.63]; I2=47%), ACEI/ARB (OR 0.78; 95% CI [0.54–1.13]; I2=45%), or β-blockers (OR 0.95; 95% CI [0.57–1.57]; I2=61%) use.76 Table 2 summarises common characteristics of ADEs, adverse drug withdrawal events (ADWEs) and frailty considerations (particularly multi-domain geriatric syndromes) of HF pharmacotherapy. There is very limited data informing the safety of GDMT in older people with frailty. A systematic review reported only three observational studies which evaluated frailty in people aged >65 years and mostly reported on mortality, hospitalisation and worsening HF as ADEs.9

Most ADEs occur within the first weeks of initiating pharmacotherapy. Other precipitants include a change in dose, change in hepatic or renal function and a new interacting drug or disease. Strategies to improve tolerance and/or mitigate the impact of ADEs can guide clinicians to up-titrate to target doses and inform patients about how to manage adverse effects, including when to seek advice from a healthcare professional. In a cross-sectional study of primary care and outpatient clinics, 91% of patients with HF perceived ADEs as a chronic problem.73 Almost half of these patients perceived the ADEs as serious, and 40% reported that the ADE(s) had major consequences for their daily activities and QoL.73 Consequently, a cohort study of previously hospitalised HF patients with a mean age of 70 years reported that medication changes were approximately twofold more likely if patients were perceived to have experienced an ADE.77 Physiological and pharmacological changes can account for some differences in patient experiences. A recent systematic review reported that approximately one-quarter of older hospitalised patients experienced an ADR during admission, of which 90% were predictable and preventable.78 Among the predictors of ADRs during hospitalisation were polypharmacy and disease-related risks, such as HF.78

Deprescribing

Non-adherence is a strong predictor of hospitalisation and mortality and can lead to treatment failure and risk of adverse outcomes. Approximately 50% of older people are non-adherent to >1 medication and, overall, adherent to 70–80% of all medications.79 Up to 44% of HF patients are non-adherent with GDMT.80 Non-adherence is associated with 31% increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.31; 95% CI [1.28–1.33]) and 11% increased risk of emergency visits (subdistribution HR 1.11; 95% CI [1.10–1.13]). Lower target doses, fewer GDMT and non-adherence are associated with advancing age.81 A nationwide Danish study showed higher discontinuation rates of ACEI/ARB (HR 1.60; 95% CI [1.51–1.69]) and β-blockers (HR 1.33; 95% CI [1.25–1.41]) in patients aged >80 years compared to those <80 years.81 A retrospective study observed physical frailty in patients without GDMT (54%) compared to patients on GDMT (38.5%, p=0.014).82

One approach to reducing polypharmacy is deprescribing, which is the planned and supervised process of dose reduction or stopping of medication that might be causing harm or no longer be of benefit.83 While deprescribing can reduce ADEs, there is conflicting evidence for deprescribing HF pharmacotherapy in the presence of frailty, warranting an individualised approach to managing the underuse of GDMT.10,84 In some situations, reducing the complexity of a medication regimen and drug interactions can support the maintenance of independence and wellbeing. This process includes down-titration and the discontinuation of CV medication and non-CV medications that increase adverse geriatric syndromes, such as falls, delirium, incontinence and frailty.85

Adverse Drug Withdrawal Events

ADWEs are symptoms or signs related to the discontinuation of a drug that can be characterised as physiological withdrawal reactions or the return of the underlying disease.86–88 Although physiological ADWEs are more likely to occur immediately (in the space of hours or days), the return of the underlying disease and worsening HF symptoms can occur up to 4 months after discontinuation.87 Physiological withdrawal reactions to β-blockers, α-blockers, nitrates and digoxin have been reported, but have not been determined for other HF pharmacotherapies, and the effects are uncertain in older people with frailty (Table 2).86,89

Conversely, ACEIs/ARBs are among the CV agents/classes without physiological withdrawal reactions in frail older people, according to the STOPP-Frail criteria.86 Trials in younger non-frail cohorts, such as TRED-HF, report that concurrent withdrawal of multiple GDMTs with HFrEF or HF with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF) over 6 months leads to worsening left ventricular function and natriuretic peptide levels, the recurrence of HF symptoms and worsening QoL.90 A systematic review of adults aged >18 years with HFrEF reported that deprescribing increased the risk of worsening HF, hospitalisation and mortality.87 Although some evidence of physiological withdrawal reactions has been established, Table 2 shows that there is scarce evidence from clinical trial data to determine whether deprescribing HF pharmacotherapy in older patients with frailty would result in physiological withdrawal symptoms and the worsening of HF symptoms.

No deprescribing clinical trials in HF and frailty were identified.10 A previous systematic review of deprescribing HF medications in older people did not identify evidence of RCTs with frailty subanalyses. This gap highlights the need to include frailty evaluation in future clinical trials. In a meta-analysis of people aged >50 years with chronic stable HF, discontinuation of digoxin was associated with an increased risk of death, hospitalisation and worsening HF symptoms.10 Observational studies suggested ADWEs were associated with withdrawal of ACEI/ARB (orthopnoea, dyspnoea), β-blocker (arrythmias, acute coronary artery syndrome, syncope), MRA (CV events), SGLT2Is (a need for an increased diuretic dose) and loop diuretics (worsening HF, other CV events and oedema).85 Supported physical exercise may also be required during prescribing and deprescribing of SGLT2Is to address potential weight loss and sarcopenia.91

Discussion

Practical Tips for Tailoring Heart Failure Prescribing in Frailty

Limited evidence is available to address prescribing in the setting of overlapping physiological changes and symptoms in people with HF and frailty. The six steps in the World Health Organization’s Guide to Good Prescribing can be practically applied to integrate frailty assessment and interventions into routine care to tailor HF pharmacotherapy for people with frailty.92

Step 1: Define the Patient’s Problem

Objective, accurate assessment of both frailty and HF can guide treatment. Frailty and progression of frailty are not routinely measured in people with HF. There is a potential benefit to including objective frailty assessment in routine care to detect short-term risk of functional deterioration and engage in multidomain frailty interventions that may lead to improved global outcomes. Objective frailty measurements, such as the CFS, could potentially be integrated into the care of all HF patients. Categorisation of the severity of HF symptoms can potentially consider physical frailty risks (Supplementary Table 3).58,60 Classification of HF phenotype, severity and evaluation of precipitating or causative factors (including disease progression, comorbidities, adverse events, drug interactions and/or palliative care) may also contribute to pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatment decisions. Use of quantitative measures, such as jugular venous pressure and frailty assessment, in HF patients experiencing ADEs may help distinguish between non-specific adverse symptoms due to worsening HF, frailty or effects of medications.

Step 2: Specify the Therapeutic Objective

Assess the intended pharmacodynamic effects of the drug when frailty is present. When targeting a symptom or sign as a therapeutic objective, the overlap in how HF and frailty present in patients can be challenging to differentiate and treat. In robust individuals with single-organ failure states, dysfunction in one system generally manifests as classic disease symptoms, such as reduced exercise tolerance, where individuals have enough functional reserve to maintain homeostasis. People with frailty have multiorgan dysfunction, which complicates the presentation and management of HF.93 Further, frailty can be predominantly caused by HF or can be multifactorial with HF as one of the many contributing factors. In frailty, HF may present with less typical symptoms – loss of appetite, decline in cognitive function, confusion, delirium, depression, dizziness or falls – and less specific signs, such as weight loss in advanced HF and cachexia (Supplementary Table 2). This can be accompanied by a decline in global function and eventually lead to disability.60 In addition, compared to men, older women with HF tend to present with more advanced symptoms both in severity and acuity, such as orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea and cardiogenic shock.24

Step 3: Verify Whether the Treatment is Suitable for the Patient

Evidence is limited to determine the likely effectiveness and safety of treatments in people with frailty. Post-hoc trial data summarised in Figure 2 suggest improved mortality and hospitalisation with SGLT2Is in people with frailty risk with HFrEF or HFpEF, as well as ARNI in HFpEF, if tolerated in those with severe frailty. However, limited reporting of adverse events according to frailty requires treatment to be individualised according to patient response and guided by clinical assessment. Treatment of HF in people with frailty must consider comorbidities, comedications, overall prognosis and the person’s goals and priorities. When considering comedications, it is important to consider other medications that could contribute to adverse effects, such as postural hypotension, delirium, falls and incontinence.

Step 4: Start the Treatment

Clinicians must consider the physiological, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic response unique to frailty when optimising pharmacotherapy, particularly in people with HF aged ≥75 years. Consider these factors when selecting dose, formulation and titration frequency of medications in people with HF and frailty. Frailty may also influence the model of acute care used to provide treatment. Acute HF warrants urgent treatment and immediate hospitalisation for patients presenting to hospital with acute decompensated HF, including acute pulmonary oedema, right ventricular failure or cardiogenic shock.60 For people with severe frailty or those living in rural communities, hospital-at-home models may also be involved in managing HF and frailty.94

Multidomain frailty interventions, such as exercise, nutrition, comprehensive medication, management of adverse events, psychological support and social engagement, are among some of the interventions that can reduce frailty to support improved outcomes.95 Interventions may potentially lead to complete, prolonged or incomplete recovery from insult, but for some individuals it may be irreversible.96 Cardiac transplant, along with cardiac rehabilitation programmes, may reverse signs of frailty due to improved cardiac function and increased physical functional status along the frailty spectrum and, in some cases, a return to robust status and improved QoL.97,98

Step 5: Provide Information, Instructions and Warnings

Information that people with frailty and HF may need about their medications may not be readily available in standard resources, due to a lack of high-quality data. Clinical trials commonly report mortality and hospitalisation endpoints. Increasingly, HF treatments focus on improving QoL and, in some cases, may also improve frailty risk and outcomes since patients also want to maintain their QoL and activities of daily living to live independently.58,60 However, data on the effects of HF pharmacotherapy on these endpoints in people with frailty are limited. Adverse drug events, such as falls, confusion and incontinence, are rarely measured rigorously in clinical trials, so are not reported on standard consumer medicines information. Emerging resources for management of polypharmacy may be relevant, such as patient leaflets to guide what questions to ask about medications or adherence aids.99

The ability for patients and carers to receive and retain information is variable due to cognitive impairment, available carer support, ability to determine if concerns or symptoms are due to the effects of the drug or disease and follow-up with the appropriate healthcare provider. Multidisciplinary management of these conditions can cause tension in cases where treatment recommendations are conflicting across specialties, which further burdens patients and carers to navigate the health system.

Step 6: Monitor (Stop) the Treatment

Multidisciplinary team (MDT) care is the gold standard for the management of HF and frailty, yet the translation of evidence into clinical practice remains suboptimal and the availability of this model of care differs significantly between different health services.95 Patients and their carers must coordinate between a wide range of healthcare professionals, which may include GPs, specialists, nurses and allied health professionals in both acute and primary care settings as part of MDT care. Monitoring frailty outcomes, such as falls, social isolation and delirium, may be included in care plans in addition to normal CV monitoring. Passive and active monitoring by patients and multiple clinicians requires coordination and access to follow-up and laboratory investigations. Frequent monitoring is necessary to determine whether the treatment is effective, whether frailty or HF treatment was helpful in improving or worsening symptoms, reassessing if a continuation of the treatment is required, considering dose adjustments or the need to deprescribe temporarily or permanently.100

Advanced HF is characterised by patients experiencing more severe and persistent symptoms despite optimised therapy, often leading to recurrent hospitalisation. Traditional evidence-based treatments may no longer be effective, for example, the individual may become resistant to loop diuretics. Early referral to advanced care services is recommended for patients who progress from stable chronic HF to advanced HF to optimise patient outcomes. Advanced HF interventions include IV diuretics or inotropes, left ventricular assist devices, mechanical circulatory devices, heart transplantation and palliative care planning.58,60 Advanced care planning is directed by patient goals of care to manage QoL, HF symptoms and comorbidities through shared decision-making. This may include end-of-life care, guided by frailty assessments that may consider the decisions to withdraw non-essential interventions and pharmacotherapy to reduce medication burden and potential adverse effects.

Frailty Treatment Pathways for People with HF

This review highlights the significant opportunities to objectively evaluate frailty in future research. Clinical and pharmacoeconomic evaluations are needed to determine how integration of frailty assessment and interventions into routine care for people with stable chronic HF can support initiation, dose titration and discontinuation of pharmacotherapy. Figure 3 summarises how multidomain frailty interventions may potentially be integrated in HF treatment pathways by overlaying the proposed frailty considerations with treatment algorithms from the European Society of Cardiology guidelines.60 Frailty interventions include frailty assessment, comprehensive medication reviews, management of adverse events, exercise, nutrition, diet, psychological support and supportive social engagement.39 There is a complex two-way relationship between medication use and healthy ageing and frailty.101 This approach merits validation and further investigation to understand whether these proposed strategies are feasible and effective.102,103 Future research could explore strategies to optimise HF pharmacotherapy and manage adverse events when frailty is present.

Evidence Gaps and Future Directions

Denkinger et al. and the International Union of Basic and Clinical Pharmacology have described the gaps in the evidence and recommendations for future opportunities to include functional measures and objective measures of frailty in the development and evaluation of medicines.100,104 People with frailty are not typically recruited or appropriately identified in pivotal RCTs. When measured, the distribution of frailty in RCTs is likely to be lower than that of the real-world population (Supplementary Table 4). Inclusion of more frail and functionally impaired older adults in clinical trials is recommended to ensure that this patient enrolment is representative of target treatment populations. Expanding inclusion and exclusion criteria will enable recruitment of people with multimorbidity and polypharmacy, while ensuring outcomes and endpoints are functionally relevant to frail older people, for example, physical activity, gait speed, cognition, falls, hospital admission and living independently.

Use of validated individual goal attainment scales as secondary outcomes to measure what matters most to a frail older adult may be considered.105 Sub-study approaches could also include studying multimorbidity-related potential interactions among commonly prescribed medications, specialised comprehensive geriatric and pharmacology assessment and comparative safety efficacy in adequately powered subgroups stratified by frailty. Sufficient duration of clinical trials would allow long-term safety and effectiveness assessment, to better inform optimal treatment length or beneficial treatment time.

It is recommended that product information summaries for medication and patient leaflets include clear information on frailty-specific or age-specific safety concerns. Frailty-related safety concerns are difficult to measure because of their atypical presentation and are inconsistently recorded by investigators in clinical trials and healthcare providers in post-marketing settings. Stronger certainty of evidence, such as deprescribing trials involving frail older people, is needed to evaluate the relative risk of worsening HF and frailty-related outcomes to support the full spectrum of HF pharmacotherapy optimisation.

Conclusion

The limited knowledge of the clinical pharmacology of HF treatments in people living with frailty demonstrates the need for representative enrolment of this population in clinical trials. Recommendations to encourage community engagement and partnerships with communities of diverse populations align with guidance from trials.106 This also presents an opportunity to advocate for inclusion of older people, those with frailty and women, in addition to reporting geriatric outcomes in future clinical trials of HF pharmacotherapy. Consideration of novel methodologies may also be warranted, such as emulation trials of real-world patients, propensity-matched cohorts, predictive artificial intelligence-simulated models and natural language processing machine learning.107 Such data are needed to inform personalised medicine to optimise important outcomes for people with frailty and HF.
