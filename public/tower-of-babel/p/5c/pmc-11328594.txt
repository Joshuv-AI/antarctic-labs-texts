Catastrophic selection: the other side of the coin
Authors: José de la Fuente
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecently, a machine learning molecular de-extinction paleoproteomic approach was used to recover inactivated antimicrobial peptides to overcome the challenges posed by antibiotic-resistant pathogens. The authors showed the possibility of identifying lost molecules with antibacterial capacity, but the other side of the coin associated with catastrophic selection should be considered for the development of new pharmaceuticals.

Hominids evolved with multiple events of catastrophic selection, defined by Harlan Lewis as ‘whereby an entire population is suddenly eliminated by an environmental extreme except for one or more exceptionally adapted individuals, providing the conditions necessary for the establishment of a population characterized by deviant genomes’ [1]. Catastrophic selection is mediated by the inactivation of certain genes/proteins with the trade-off of conferring to the ancestors of modern humans an improved protection against diseases caused by viruses, bacteria, arthropod infestations, and vector-borne diseases [2–6]. Additionally, transposable sequences of viral origin known as endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) are dormant in the modern human genome and when activated may regulate transcription and contribute to innate immunity [7] but also to cellular senescence and tissue aging [8].

In a recent pioneering study, the group of Cesar de la Fuente-Nunez at the University of Pennsylvania reported a machine learning molecular de-extinction approach to recover inactivated antimicrobial peptides with the potential to face challenges posed by antibiotic-resistant pathogens [9,10]. The authors were inspired by the Jurassic Park film and showed the possibility of identifying lost molecules with antibacterial capacity through in silico paleoproteome mining [9].

Although these results are supported by in vitro and in vivo animal model studies [9], the other side of the coin or the opposite aspect of a matter needs to be considered referring to the possible negative effects that may be related to lost molecules with antibacterial capacity. For example, inactivation approximately 20–30 million years ago of the capacity to synthesize carbohydrate epitope alpha-gal (galactose-α-1,3-galactose) through inactivation of the α-1,3-galactosyltransferase enzyme was driven by anti-alpha-gal IgM/IgG antibody-mediated resistance to infection by pathogens with this post-translational modification. However, this molecular inactivation resulted in the possibility of developing IgE-mediated allergic reactions to products derived from mammalian meat containing this modification and known as the Alpha Gal Syndrome (AGS), associated with exposure to tick bites producing this biomolecule [2,3,11]. Another hypothesis of catastrophic selection is driven by the loss of body hair that may be associated with improved protection against arthropod infestations and transmitted pathogens but with trade-off of reducing thermoregulatory adaptation and antioxidant capacity among other [2,6,12]. Other proteins inactivated by catastrophic selection are sialic acid (Neu5Gc) and sialic acid-recognized signalling receptors (singlecs), which are associated with improved protection against pathogen infection but increasing risks for neurological diseases [6,13,14]. In this context, the hypothesis of fossilized diseases referring to ancient diseases that may played a role during hominids evolution can be considered to identify potential targets for catastrophic selection [15].

Based on this hypothesis, what is the other side of the coin referring to potential negative effects of the discovered inactivated antimicrobial peptides? We may consider the possibility that the use of this biomolecules to enhance protective response to antibiotic-resistant pathogen infection may result in negative effect in some individuals. For example, regarding the AGS, treatment with α-1,3-galactosyltransferase enzyme will activate the production of alpha-gal, which may increase the risks of allergic reactions in some individuals exposed to tick bites by mechanisms still under characterization [16].

To address this question, it may be necessary to evaluate whether some of these biomolecules are associated with catastrophic selection events with possible negative trade-off effects in some individuals (Figure 1). As shown by Maasch et al. [9], research may apply comparative genomics, paleoproteomics, paleogenomics and machine learning to undertake comparative analysis of modern and archaic human proteome and genome to identify inactivated proteins that may be related to evolutionary adaptations to pathogen infection. Paleoproteomics applies to the study of ancient proteins with the intersection of molecular biology, paleontology, archaeology, paleoecology, and history [17]. Additionally, a systems biology approach for the analysis of omics datasets (transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, interactomics, regulomics) may be used to characterize molecular pathways and biomolecules associated with the identified inactivated proteins and possible implications in catastrophic selection. Methodologies using machine learning and explainable artificial intelligence can be implemented for potential management of infectious diseases based on catastrophic evolution events [18,19]. For example, molecular de-extinction aimed at resurrecting molecules was recently applied using deep learning of available proteomes from extinct organisms for the identification of antibiotic peptides [20]. Selected biomolecules may then be then tested in vitro for the assessment of cellular toxicity [9] and in vivo using humanized mice or other animal models as shown for the AGS [16,21].
