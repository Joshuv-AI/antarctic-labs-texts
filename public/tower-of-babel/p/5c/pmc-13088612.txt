A temperate phage encoding a catalytically active endolysin: characterization of phage P7869 and its Lys7869 against Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Authors: Jiao Feng, Xuexue Wang, Liangyu Wang, Yuming Zhang, Xiaogang Cui, Yan Meng, Xiaoxia Zhou, Changxin Wu, Chenrui Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPseudomonas aeruginosa poses significant therapeutic challenges due to its acquired and innate resistance mechanisms. Bacteriophages and their purified lysis proteins have emerged as promising alternatives for combating P. aeruginosa infections.MethodsPhage P7869 was isolated from hospital sewage and characterized. Whole-genome sequencing was conducted to elucidate the genomic features of P7869. The recombinant endolysin Lys7869 was expressed and purified using a prokaryotic system, and its stability, minimum inhibitory concentration and antimicrobial spectrum were assessed.ResultsP7869 exhibited a narrow host range and high stability. Genomic analysis confirmed its temperate lifestyle, yet its recombinant endolysin, Lys7869, demonstrated therapeutic potential. Lys7869 demonstrated high stability across a broad range (pH 4–7, 4–50 °C), making it suitable for diverse formulation and storage conditions. It exhibited potent activity with a minimum inhibitory concentration of 500 ng/mL. Lys7869 displayed a broader antimicrobial spectrum against chloroform-permeabilized Gram-negative pathogens, suggesting its potential as a versatile agent to combat various multidrug-resistant strains.ConclusionsThis study identified a novel temperate phage, P7869, and highlights its endolysin Lys7869 as a highly promising antibacterial agent. These findings lay a critical foundation for protein engineering efforts to enhance its outer-membrane penetration and create next-generation enzybiotics against multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04933-9.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen that is widely distributed in the environment [1]. It can easily infect immunocompromised individuals and cause a variety of infections, including pulmonary infections [2], urinary tract infections [3], osteoarticular infections [4], bloodstream infections [5] and among others. The bacterium is equipped with an outer membrane characterized by low permeability and an array of efflux pumps, rendering it intrinsically resistant to many antimicrobial agents. Moreover, P. aeruginosa is capable of acquiring resistance to a broad spectrum of antimicrobial agents through genomic mutations and horizontal acquisition of resistance determinants [6]. The high level of intrinsic and acquired drug resistance, coupled with its persistent colonization on humid surfaces, make P. aeruginosa a challenging pathogen to treat and a model pathogen for the study of bacterial pathogenicity and drug resistance [1, 7]. P. aeruginosa is considered as an important cause of both community-acquired and hospital-acquired infections [8, 9]. There is an urgent need for novel antibiotic replacement therapies.

Bacteriophages, or phages, are viruses that specially target bacteria, and are ubiquitously present in various environments [10]. Based on their life cycles, phages can be classified into two types: lytic phages and temperate phages. Lytic phages rapidly propagate by lysing their host cells [11]. Their clinical potential has been demonstrated against multidrug-resistant (MDR) P. aeruginosa [12, 13]. For example, the combination of phage AB-Pa01 with antibiotic regimens has been shown to effectively eliminate persistent lung infections [14]. Additionally, the dsRNA phage phiYY has demonstrated efficacy in reducing pulmonary colonization in critically ill patients [15]. In contrast, temperate phages, which can integrate into the host genome or exist as plasmids, dynamically respond to environmental stimuli through lysogenic-lytic switching [16, 17]. Their potential to mediate horizontal gene transfer via specialized transduction has raised concerns about therapeutic use, as this mechanism may enhance bacterial virulence or promote antibiotic resistance [18].

Endolysins, peptidoglycan hydrolases produced during the phage lytic cycle, offer advantages over conventional antibiotics, including high specificity, rapid bacteriolysis, and low resistance risk [19–22]. Endolysins with a Gram-positive background generally have a modular structure consisting of an N-terminal catalytic domain and a C-terminal cell wall-binding domain [23–25]. However, most lysins from phages that target Gram-negative bacteria have a globular structure with a single catalytic domain [26]. Studies demonstrate that lysins from temperate phages or prophages exhibit antibacterial activity in both in vitro and in vivo models, such as ORF28 from the Enterococcus faecalis phage φEf11 [27], LysSAP27 from Staphylococcus aureus phage vB_SauS-SAP27 [28], Ply57 from Bacillus cereus phage Izhevsk [29], and PlyPa03 and PlyPa91 from the P. aeruginosa prophages [30]. Phages and endolysins are considered promising alternatives for treating MDR bacteria. While highly effective against Gram-positive pathogens, their efficacy against Gram-negative bacteria like P. aeruginosa is fundamentally limited by the protective outer membrane, which physically blocks access to the underlying peptidoglycan layer [31]. This barrier restricts the activity of native endolysins.

In this study, we isolated a temperate phage P7869 with a narrow host range specific to P. aeruginosa. The biological and genomic characteristics of the phage were systematically investigated. Additionally, the endolysin of phage P7869, named Lys7869, was expressed and thoroughly studied. Its biological characteristics and bactericidal activity were further characterized in detail.

Materials and methods

Phage isolation and morphological characterization

The clinical isolate P. aeruginosa 7869, a carbapenem-resistant strain, was used as the host for phages isolation. The untreated sewage sample, collected in Shanxi Bethune Hospital (Taiyuan, China), was enriched with P. aeruginosa 7869. Phage isolation and purification was performed as previously described [32, 33]. Briefly, sewage sample was centrifuged, then the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane (Millipore, PES membrane). P. aeruginosa 7869 was cultured in Brain Heart Infusion Broth (BHI) at 37℃ to log-phase. The filter lysate co-cultured with P. aeruginosa 7869 at 37 °C 220 rpm for 5 h. The bacterial cells were removed by centrifugation, then the supernatant was filtered by a 0.22 μm filter membrane to collect the phages. The filtrate was ten-fold serial diluted by SM buffer (100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO₄, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, supplemented with 0.01% gelatin). 100 µL filter lysate under each dilution gradient was mixed with 300 µL log-phase P. aeruginosa 7869 and was incubated at room temperature. After 5 min, the mixture was added to 5 mL top agar (BHI with 0.75% agar), poured onto the bottom agar layer (1.5% agar). Phage plaques were formed after 12–16 h incubation at 37 °C. Phage purification was carried out by picking single-plaque with the double-layer agar plate method and was repeated three more times. The purified phage preparation was stored at 4 °C for further studies.

For TEM, 10 µL of purified phage (108 PFU/mL) was placed on carbon-coated copper grid and then negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid negative stain solution (G1870, Solarbio, China) for 10 min. The phage morphology was observed by transmission electron microscopy (JEM-2100, JEOL, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV.

Optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) determination

The MOI was determined as previously described [32]. Phage P7869 was mixed with P. aeruginosa 7869 (108 CFU/mL) at different MOI ratios (100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001) and incubated at 37 °C with shaking for 5 h. The mixture was centrifuged to remove bacterial cells, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Phage titers in the filtrate were quantified via the double-layer agar plate method. The MOI yielding the highest phage titer was designated as optimal. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Host range analysis

The host range of the phage was determined through a spot test using 42 different bacterial strains (22 strains of P. aeruginosa and 20 strains of other bacteria, Table 1). All 22 P. aeruginosa strains tested, including the host strain 7869, were clinical isolates collected from the same hospital. For the assay, an undiluted phage P7869 lysate with a titer of 1.47 × 10¹⁰ PFU/mL was used. A 10 µL aliquot of this lysate was spotted onto the double-layer agar plates. Plaque formation was assessed after incubation. A bacterial strain was considered susceptible if clear plaques were observed and recorded as ‘+’; the absence of plaques was marked as ‘-’.

Phage thermal and pH stability tests

To investigate the thermal stability, phage P7869 was incubated in BHI broth at different temperatures (40 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C, 70 °C, and 80 °C) for 1 h. To analyze the pH stability of the phage, the phage was incubated in BHI broth adjusted to different pH levels (1–13) using HCl or NaOH for 1 h. The titer of the phage after incubation was determined by the double-layer agar plate method.

Phage genomic DNA extraction, sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Phage genomic DNA was extracted using TIANamp Virus DNA/RNA Kit (DP315, Tiangen, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted DNA was resuspended in deionized water and stored at -20 °C for further sequencing analysis. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeqTM DNA Sample Prep Kit (20015965, Illumina, USA), and sequencing was performed on an Illumina Novaseq platform. Raw sequencing data were processed using Trimmomatic (v0.32) to remove adapters and low-quality reads. The cleaned reads were assembled into a complete genomic sequence using SPAdes v3.13.0, and the Pilon software was employed to polish the assembly, yielding the final phage genome sequence. Restriction enzyme digestion SacI at 37 °C for 5 h was performed to validate the sequencing results, and the digested products were analyzed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Bioinformatics analysis was performed as follows. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted independently using both the Rapid Annotation using Subsystem Technology 2.0 [34] and PHASTER [35], with the final gene set determined by integrating results from both. Functional annotation of predicted protein sequences was performed by conducting BLASTP searches against the UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot (accessed on 4 March 2025) [36] and NCBI RefSeq (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/, accessed on 4 March 2025) databases. A hit was considered significant and used for functional assignment only if it simultaneously satisfied all three of the following thresholds: an E-value of < 1e-5, amino acid sequence identity of > 50%, and query coverage of > 60%. Potential tRNA-encoding genes were identified using tRNA scan-SE [37]. Potential virulence factors were screened against the VFDB (https://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/main.htm), and antibiotic resistance genes were analyzed using the CARD (https://omictools.com/card-tool). Gene organization maps were constructed using Inkscape v1.1.

The physicochemical properties of the proteins were analyzed using ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The secondary structure and disulfide bonds were predicted with the Scratch Protein Predictor (http://scratch.proteomics.ics.uci.edu/index.html). Transmembrane domains were identified using TMHMM 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/), and signal peptide sequences were detected with SignalP 4.1 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-4.1/). Conserved domain analysis was performed using the NCBI Conserved Domain Search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi). The three-dimensional structure of the protein was predicted using AlphaFold3, with structural visualizations generated in PyMOL v2.5.0.

Phylogenetic analysis

The amino acid sequences of the terminase large subunit (TerL) of indicative phages, selected by BLASTp with high similarity, were aligned using MUSCLE 3.8.31 [38]. Unrooted Maximum Likelihood trees were generated from the aligned TerL sequences using MEGA7 [39], and evolutionary distances were estimated using the Maximum Likelihood method with a boot-strap iteration of 1000.

Preliminary identification of the phage receptor type

The receptor properties of P7869 were measured as described previously [40]. P. aeruginosa 7869 was treated with proteinase K (0.2 mg/mL; P9460, Solarbio, China) at 37 °C for 3 h to destroy surface proteins. Another set of P. aeruginosa 7869 was treated with sodium acetate (50 mM; pH 5.2) or sodium acetate containing 100 mM IO4− for 2 h, with precautions taken to shield from light. To exclude the possible effect of other factors, a control without proteinase K or IO4− addition was included. Crucially, after all treatments, the bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and washed twice with fresh BHI broth to completely remove the enzymes or chemical reagents. The washed cell pellets were then resuspended in BHI for subsequent assays.

To measure phage adsorption rate, treated bacterial cells were mixed with phage P7869 at a MOI of approximately 0.01 and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min to pellet the bacteria along with any adsorbed phage particles. The titer of free, unadsorbed phage remaining in the supernatant was immediately determined using the double-layer agar plate method.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) adsorption inhibition assay

This experiment introduced modifications based on the previous experimental protocol [41]. LPS of P. aeruginosa 7869 was extracted using LPS Extraction Kit (EX1740, Solarbio, China). P. aeruginosa 7869 was cultured in BHI at 37℃ to approximately OD600=1, then the bacterial culture was centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 3 min at room temperature to harvest log-phase P. aeruginosa 7869 cells. The pellet was resuspended thoroughly in 500 µL of Reagent A, then centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 3 min to discard supernatant, and this wash cycle was repeated twice. Following the final wash, the pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of Extract B. The suspension was ultrasonicated, and then the lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was carefully transferred to a new tube. 500 µL of Extract C was added to the supernatant, following a 20 s vigorous vortex to achieve complete mixing. The mixture was incubated in a water bath maintained at 66–68 °C for 30 min, gently mixing by pipetting at 10-minute intervals. Subsequently, the sample undisturbed was incubated at 4 °C overnight, and then was centrifuged at 4 °C and 3,000 × g for 20 min. The uppermost supernatant was carefully obtained as the purified LPS sample. P. aeruginosa 7869 was cultured to the logarithmic phase, mixed with an equal volume of the purified phage at a pre-optimized MOI. And then, 50 µL of LPS was added to this mixture. After 10 min of incubation at 37℃, the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C. The supernatant was diluted, and its titer was determined by the double-layer agar plate method.

Cloning, expression, and purification of Lys7869

Lys7869 endolysin was amplified from the P7869 genome by PCR (for all primers used, see Supplementary Table S1) and cloned into the expression vector pET32a(+), which was obtained from our laboratory stock. The Rosetta (DE3) (CB108-02, Tiangen, China) strain was transformed with the pET32a(+)-Lys7869 plasmid. Expression of the recombinant endolysin was induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) when the OD at 600 nm reached 0.6, followed by incubation at 25℃ for 12 h. Bacterial cells were suspended with lysis buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0; 500 mM NaCl; 25 mM imidazole) and disrupted by sonication on ice using the ultrasonic processor (Q500, Qsonica, USA) at 35% amplitude with a 5 s on / 3 s off pulse cycle for 10 min. N-terminal His-tagged endolysin was purified with Ni-NTA acid agarose resin 6FF (P2010, Solarbio, China) with an imidazole gradient according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The N-terminal tags (TrxA-6His) were removed using enterokinase through incubation at 4 °C for 4 h. The digestion mixture was loaded onto the nickel column to separate the His-tagged proteins from Lys7869. The His-tagged proteins bounded to the nickel resin, while Lys7869 was collected in the flow-through fraction. Subsequently, the expressed protein Lys7869 was subjected to buffer-exchange into 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) to remove residual imidazole and concentrated using a 3 kDa ultrafiltration concentrator, followed by analysis via SDS-PAGE. Western blotting was performed with 1:5,000-diluted anti-His tag antibodies (ABT2050, Abbkine, China) and 1:2,000-diluted horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (IgG-HRP antibody) (AS003, ABclonal, China). Protein concentration was determined by BCA protein assay kit (PC0020, Solarbio, China). The purified protein was stored at -80 °C in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) after flash freezing of liquid nitrogen.

Bacterial outer membrane treatment

Because the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria prevents endolysins from reaching the peptidoglycan layer, we pre-treated cells to increase outer membrane permeability [42]. P. aeruginosa 7869 and other Gram-negative strains were grown to mid-exponential phase, incubated with 5% (v/v) chloroform (CHCl₃) for 10 min under continuous gentle shaking (following an initial brief vortex for mixing). Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 5 min. The pellets were washed three times with 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) to remove residual CHCl₃ prior to downstream assays.

Determination of the minimum effective concentration of Lys7869

Following outer-membrane permeabilization as described above, P. aeruginosa 7869 cells were harvested and resuspended in 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) to approximately OD₆₀₀=1. Serial dilutions of purified Lys7869 were added to aliquots of this suspension, and OD₆₀₀ was recorded every 5 min for 30 min at 37 °C. Two parallel control assays were performed: (i) a buffer control, where cells were incubated with buffer instead of endolysin; and (ii) an intrinsic activity control, where the lytic activity of Lys7869 was tested under identical conditions but using bacterial cells that were not pre-treated with chloroform, to assess its activity against an intact outer membrane. The MEC was defined as the lowest concentration yielding ≥ 50% reduction in OD₆₀₀ relative to the buffer-only control at 30 min.

Meanwhile, a colony-forming unit (CFU) reduction assay was conducted. Permeabilized cells (approximately OD₆₀₀=1) were incubated with different concentrations of Lys7869 for 30 min at 37 °C. For negative control, bacterial suspension was treated with 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer instead of Lys7869. The viable cell count was determined after the incubation overnight at 37 °C.

Thermal stability, antimicrobial spectrum and optimal reaction pH of Lys7869

Following outer-membrane permeabilization, bacterial pellets was resuspended with 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) to approximately OD600=1 for thermal stability and antimicrobial spectrum assays. Lys7869 was treated at various temperatures (4℃, 25℃, 30℃, 37℃, 40℃, 50℃, 60℃, 70℃, 80℃) for 1 h. Then the cell suspensions were treated with endolysin at a final concentration of 500 ng/mL for 30 min and the OD600 was measured.

The bacterial strains used for antimicrobial spectrum determination were same as those described in Table 1. The cell pellet was resuspended in 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0). The cell suspensions were treated with endolysin at a final concentration of 500 ng/mL for 30 min and then the OD600 was measured.

Results

Phage isolation and morphology

Phage P7869 was isolated from hospital sewage in Taiyuan, China, using P. aeruginosa 7869 as the host strain. It was subsequently purified by picking the single-plaque using the double-layer agar method. The purified phage formed clear plaques with a central white lysogenic colony on the lawns of P. aeruginosa 7869 (Fig. 1A) [43]. TEM revealed that P7869 had a long, non-contractile tail approximately 130 nm in length and an isometric capsid about 65 nm in diameter (based on measurements of ten individual phage particles), with a relatively significant baseplate structure at the tail end (Fig. 1B and C). These morphological features are suggestive of a siphovirus morphology. This preliminary assignment was further confirmed by genomic analysis, supporting the classification of P7869 within the class Caudoviricetes.

Host range of phage P7869

A total of 22 strains of P. aeruginosa and 20 strains from other bacterial species were used to determine the host range of phage P7869. The results showed that P7869 did not infect any of the 20 non-Pseudomonas strains tested (Table 1), and it was capable of lysing only two out of the 22 tested P. aeruginosa strains.

Physiological characterization of phage P7869

The optimal MOI for phage P7869 was determined to be 0.01, at which the phage titer reached its peak at (1.5 ± 0.7) ×1010 PFU/mL (Fig. 2A). This MOI was subsequently utilized in all further experiments. Regarding thermal stability (Fig. 2B), phage P7869 showed no substantial loss in titer after 1 h of incubation at 40, 50, and 60 °C compared to the initial stock (1.5 × 10¹⁰ PFU/mL). However, a significant decline in phage titer was noted when the temperature was elevated to 70 °C and above. Notably, no phage activity was detectable at 80 °C. In the pH stability assessment (Fig. 2C), phage P7869 demonstrated stability over a broad pH range, spanning from 4 to 12. The phage titer was highest after incubation at pH 7, suggesting optimal activity under neutral conditions. In contrast, no phage activity was detected at the extremes of pH 1, pH 2, and pH 13, highlighting the phage’s sensitivity to highly acidic and basic environments.

Phage receptor of P7869 on the bacterial cell surface

The phage receptor on the bacterial surface is typically outer membrane proteins (OMPs) or LPS; for P. aeruginosa phages, pili can also serve as receptors [44, 45]. To obtain preliminary insight into the nature of the P7869 receptor, we treated P. aeruginosa 7869 with Proteinase K (to disrupt OMPs) or periodate (to oxidize LPS) [46]. Proteinase K treatment did not affect phage adsorption efficiency, while periodate treatment reduced it (Fig. 3A). Additionally, we extracted LPS from P. aeruginosa 7869 and used it to compete with live bacteria for phage adsorption. LPS significantly reduced the binding of phage P7869 to P. aeruginosa 7869 (Fig. 3B). These results suggest that the adsorption receptor in P. aeruginosa 7869 for phage P7869 is LPS.

Genomic and phylogenetic analysis of phage P7869

The genome of phage P7869 was a 52,373-bp linear double-stranded DNA molecule with a GC content of 58.26% (Fig. 4). Endonuclease SacI, which has two recognition sites within the genome, was predicted to digest the phage P7869 genome into three fragments: 10,107 bp, 7,468 bp and 34,798 bp (Fig. S1A). The experimental digestion results were consistent with expectations (Fig. S1B), confirming that the P7869 genome is indeed a linear double-stranded DNA molecule.

BLASTn analysis showed that the genome of P7869 limited overall homology with sequences currently available in databases. The highest similarity was observed with the genome of P. aeruginosa NCGM 1984 (GenBank accession number: AP014646), showing 66% query coverage and 94.23% nucleotide identity. Analysis using PHASTER further indicated that these homologous regions correspond to prophage sequences.

The phage P7869 genome contained a total of 94 ORFs and three tRNAs (Table S2). These ORFs could be divided into six modules based on the predicted function, including replication and regulation-related module, morphogenesis module, DNA packing proteins, lysis module, lysogeny-related protein, and hypothetical proteins (Fig. 4). No genes encoding virulence or antibiotic resistance were identified in the genome. However, three lysogeny-related ORFs were detected: integrase (ORF31), phage repressor protein CI (ORF69), and prophage anti-repressor CRO protein (ORF70). The phage repressor protein CI (ORF69) binds to phage DNA, physically inhibiting the binding of any transcription machinery and may be responsible for the lysogeny of P7869 [47]. The prophage anti-repressor CRO protein (ORF70) is hypothesized to counteract the primary repressor through direct binding [48].

To investigate the origin and evolution of phage P7869, phylogenetic analysis was performed. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the amino acid sequences of terminase large subunits by the Maximum Likelihood method, using the program MEGA 7.0 [39]. Phage P7869 has been classified under genus Detrevirus (Fig. 5).

Expression and purification of Lys7869

The ORF28 in the phage P7869 sequence was identified as encoding Lys7869, an endolysin. This 435 bp sequence encodes a 144 amino acid protein. NCBI Conserved Domains identified a lysozyme conserved domain within the protein sequence (Fig. S2A). Physicochemical analysis revealed a theoretical isoelectric point of 9.52, an instability index of 18.59 (indicating high stability), an aliphatic index of 93.47, and a grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) of -0.269 (Table S3). No disulfide bonds, transmembrane domains, or signal peptides were predicted in the protein sequence (Fig. S2B, S2C, and S2D). Structure prediction and analysis of Lys7869 by AlphaFold indicated that Lys7869 exhibits a structure akin to lysozyme (Fig. S2E). Furthermore, comparative sequence analysis against previously characterized P. aeruginosa endolysins revealed that Lys7869 shares high amino acid identity (95%) with the prophage-derived lysin PlyPa03 (WP_070344501.1), but shows lower identity (≤ 40%) with the majority of others [30].

Then, the Lys7869 gene was cloned into the pET32a(+) prokaryotic expression vector and expressed in E. coli with an N-terminal His-tag. Partial protein solubility was achieved after IPTG induction (Fig. 6A and B). Enterokinase digestion removed the melt-aiding tag (TrxA) carried by pET32a(+) (Fig. 6C). Finally, Lys7869 was purified to a concentration of 287 µg/mL for subsequent enzyme activity assays.

Enzyme activity, stability and antimicrobial spectrum of Lys7869

The minimum effective concentration of Lys7869 against chloroform-permeabilized P. aeruginosa 7869 cells was determined to be 500 ng/mL, based on the criterion of ≥ 50% OD600 reduction. Lysin concentrations at or above the minimum effective concentration caused a clear, dose-dependent decrease in turbidity and caused a reduction of approximately 2 log10 in the bacterial count (CFU/mL) after a 30-min incubation (Fig. 7A and B). Critically, Lys7869 displayed no detectable lytic activity against intact cells lacking chloroform pre-treatment. Even at a concentration of 100 µg/mL, no reduction in OD₆₀₀ was observed over the 30-minute assay period, confirming that outer membrane permeabilization is essential for its activity. In terms of stability, Lys7869 exhibited remarkable thermal stability, maintaining over 90% of its activity within the temperature range of 4 °C to 50 °C (Fig. 7C). Even after exposure to 80 °C for one hour, Lys7869 retained more than 20% of its activity. Furthermore, the enzyme demonstrated optimal activity at pH values ranging from 3 to 7 (Fig. 7D).

When evaluating the host range using the same bacterial strains tested for phage P7869, Lys7869 displayed a broader spectrum of lytic activity. Lys7869 was effective against a wider range of Gram-negative bacteria, though it did not lyse all tested Gram-positive strains (Fig. 7E). These findings indicate that Lys7869 possesses a wider antimicrobial spectrum.

Discussion

As a leading cause of nosocomial infections, P. aeruginosa continues to pose a therapeutic challenge because of the high rate of morbidity and mortality associated with it and the possibility of development of drug resistance during treatment [49]. Phages, as viruses that specifically lyse bacteria, provide a new protocol for the treatment of MDR P. aeruginosa infections [50]. In this study, we isolated a new temperate phage P7869 specific to P. aeruginosa from hospital untreated sewage. The observed cloudy centers within the clear plaque morphology, indicative of lysogeny, are consistent with the genomic identification of lysogenic modules, including integrase, CI, and CRO [43]. Although temperate phages like P7869 are generally excluded from direct therapeutic use due to the risk of horizontal gene transfer, they remain valuable as sources of endolysins and as templates for engineering obligately lytic derivatives. This approach has been successfully employed in mycobacterial phage therapy [51], and for P7869, it could involve using CRISPR/Cas to delete its lysogeny module.

P7869 exhibited an extremely narrow host range, infecting only 2 of 22 tested P. aeruginosa isolates (9%). This rate is markedly lower than that reported for other Pseudomonas phages. For example, phage fMGyn-Pae01 infected 43% of randomly selected strains, and phage PsIn showed lysis in 33% of strains [52, 53]. This high specificity is correlated with its LPS-dependent adsorption mechanism, demonstrated through acetate IO4− susceptibility and LPS adsorption inhibition assay. In many Gram-negative bacteria, alterations in O-antigens or O-polysaccharides on outer membrane LPS molecules can pose a resistance risk to phage adsorption [54]. Notably, P7869’s stability across a broad pH range (4–12) and at elevated temperatures (40–60 °C) is notably broader than the stability profiles reported for many other P. aeruginosa phages [53], enhancing its potential utility in settings where cold chain storage is a challenge.

Genomic analysis confirmed the presence of genes encoding proteins essential for host lysis, including a lysin (ORF28, named Lys7869) and a holin (ORF89). Lys7869 is a glycosyl hydrolase 24 family protein with lysozyme activity. It degrades bacterial cell walls by hydrolyzing peptidoglycan 1,4-beta linkages between N-acetyl-D-glucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid. Structural predictions indicate that Lys7869 has a globular structure with a single enzymatic activity domain.

Using a prokaryotic expression system, we successfully expressed and purified the recombinant Lys7869 protein. Lys7869 is the first endolysin from a free, naturally occurring temperate Pseudomonas phage to be functionally characterized. It provides a new sequence scaffold that is different from all previously reported P. aeruginosa endolysins, which derive only from lytic phages or chromosomally integrated prophages [42, 55].

As a Gram-negative bacterium, P. aeruginosa possesses an outer membrane that limits the access of endolysins to the underlying peptidoglycan layer. This inherent barrier represents a general challenge for the direct use of native endolysins against Gram-negative pathogens. The activity of purified Lys7869, for instance, is dependent on the addition of outer membrane permeabilizers such as chloroform, underscoring this limitation [56, 57]. To overcome this constraint, various engineering strategies have been developed. A prominent approach involves creating “Artilysins” by fusing endolysins with outer membrane-destabilizing peptides [20]. For example, fusing the antimicrobial peptide cecropin A to the N-terminus of endolysin ST01 enabled it to disrupt the outer membrane and enhanced its bactericidal activity against Gram-negative bacteria [58].

The functional difference between Lys7869 and PlyPa03 underscores both the necessity for engineering and the unique research value of Lys7869. Despite sharing 95% amino acid identity with the prophage-derived lysin PlyPa03, a critical functional divergence exists: PlyPa03 is active against intact cells, whereas Lys7869 requires outer membrane permeabilization [30]. This contrast demonstrates that minor sequence variations can fundamentally alter the interaction with the outer membrane, making Lys7869 a unique variant and a valuable candidate for structure-function studies.

Notably, Lys7869 demonstrated broad activity against Gram-negative strains and exhibited stability at physiological temperatures (4–50 °C) and moderate acidity (pH 4–8). The observed activity of Lys7869 against multiple Gram-negative species following outer membrane permeabilization highlights its potent, conserved catalytic function.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations remain. First, P7869 is a temperate phage, which requires genetic modification to eliminate lysogeny risks before it can be considered for clinical use. Second, Lys7869’s activity in vitro depends on artificial disruption of the outer membrane, a condition that is difficult to reproduce in vivo. Future studies should focus on using genetic engineering to address these limitations and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of P7869-derived variants and Lys7869-based fusion proteins.

Conclusions

In this study, we isolated and characterized phage P7869, a narrow-host-range temperate phage specific to P. aeruginosa. Notably, we identified and functionally characterized its encoded endolysin, Lys7869, which represents the first endolysin derived from a free, temperate Pseudomonas phage. Lys7869 exhibits potent lytic activity against permeabilized cells and maintains high stability under physiological conditions. However, its strict dependence on outer membrane disruption confirms the major barrier to its therapeutic application in its native form. Future work should focus on engineering Lys7869 to overcome the outer membrane barrier and develop effective antimicrobial agents against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa.
