Future of CT body composition research: Methodological discrepancies and advances
Authors: Cecily A. Byrne, Sandra L. Gomez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBody composition research utilizing computed tomography (CT) has increased over the past several decades as researchers use clinically acquired CT for opportunistic screening or the identification of phenotypes such as sarcopenia, myosteatosis, and sarcopenic obesity associated with various clinical outcomes. While there continues to be exciting work being done in CT body composition research, including the use of artificial intelligence (AI) to streamline the assessment of body composition, fundamental methodological discrepancies continue to hinder clinical integration. In this narrative review of studies published between 2022 and 2025, we describe newer approaches to normalize skeletal muscle measurements for differences in body size. We provide an overview of the heterogeneity in deriving currently published CT reference criteria for body composition quantity and quality in a limited subset of studies from published literature. We also summarize currently published thresholds from a limited subset of studies, including those most commonly cited, applied to define low muscle quantity or poor muscle composition and compare and contrast these values, as well as their derivation. Finally, we highlight emerging areas utilizing AI technologies and the integration of CT body composition assessment into clinical practice, and on the flip side, the lack of guidance on how this information will be implemented to achieve personalized nutrition care and improve clinical outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Computed Tomography (CT), along with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are considered the reference standard for non‐invasive body composition assessment at the tissue‐organ level, as it not only provides information on muscle quantity and composition, but it also distinguishes the types of adipose tissues found in the body. 1 Adipose tissues include subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) found beneath the skin, visceral adipose tissue (VAT) found within the abdominal cavity surrounding the organs, and intermuscular adipose tissue (IMAT) found within skeletal muscle and between muscle groups. 2 , 3

Using CT to assess body composition

Clinically acquired CT images obtained for various diagnostic purposes are used opportunistically by researchers to assess body composition. CT‐assessed body composition analysis most frequently occurs at the lumbar 3 (L3) vertebral landmark via an abdominal CT scan as the assessment of skeletal muscle (SM) and adipose tissues in this region are most highly correlated with whole‐body volumes. 4 , 5 Single slice, axial or transverse images depicting a cross‐sectional image at L3, are extracted using DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) compatible software from CT diagnostic protocols performed as clinically indicated. Body composition software such as SliceOmatic (Version 5.0, TomoVision) is then used to segment body tissues and organs in various colors using standard Hounsfield Units (HU) of −29 to +150 HU for SM, −150 to −50 for VAT, and −190 to −30 for SAT and IMAT. 5 The values for SM, SAT, VAT, and IMAT cross‐sectional areas (CSA) are calculated by the software program. Traditionally, SM CSA (cm2) is normalized by height (m2) to yield skeletal muscle index (SMI), which allows for the comparison of SM across different heights. 6

Segmentation of body tissues and organs is completed manually by trained researchers using tools within body composition software (e.g., region growing mode in SliceOmatic). Technological advancements have moved the field from semi‐automatic segmentation with manual correction to now fully automated segmentation via deep learning neural networks. Trained researchers complete segmentation of SM and adipose tissue either manually or correct segmentation after a semi‐automatic or fully automatic analysis by this specialized software. These procedures are labor and time intensive with manual correction of SM and adipose tissue taking ~15–20 min to complete after semi‐automatic segmentation. Most recently, technological advances in artificial intelligence (AI) allow for the fully automated analysis of body composition from CT. This includes automated vertebral landmarking and segmentation of tissue and organs across a larger scanning area, allowing for the analysis of within person differences in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue across vertebral areas, 7 all while saving labor and fiscal resources. It must be noted, however, that manual segmentation of body tissues and organs remains the reference standard against which deep learning neural networks are trained and validated, and there are still challenges to fully automated methods (e.g., poor quality images, significant artifacts, unique anatomies) that require human review and correction.

Potential limitations of CT‐assessed body composition

The advantages of CT for direct and accurate measurements of body composition are numerous, and new advances and innovations in this field continue to facilitate the use of this technology for this purpose. However, the CT procedure has a few significant limitations beyond cost that should be noted. Two of its major limitations are significant radiation exposure and the lack of portability. 8 Thus, the use of CT for body composition assessment has been restricted mainly to hospital and clinical populations where the CT scan is performed routinely for diagnosis and treatment planning. Another important limitation of CT‐assessed body composition research is selection bias. This bias could be attributed to reasons such as non‐random selection of patients (i.e., retrospective cohort studies, samples of convenience, patients with CT scan in electronic medical records), exclusions due to poor quality CT images (i.e., artifacts, anasarca, movement), exclusions due to unevaluable CT images (i.e., patients with higher adiposity often exceed the field‐of‐view of the CT scanner), disparities in access to imaging technologies, and/or disparities in treatment planning, particularly for oncology populations.

APPROACH FOR THE NARRATIVE REVIEW

Previous work informing the scope of the narrative review

Various comprehensive reviews have been published on CT body composition assessment 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 and provided a summarization of body composition tissues with short and long‐term clinical outcomes in cancer 11 , 13 and other clinical diseases, such as critical illness, metabolic disease, COVID, and cardiovascular disease. 9 , 10 Potential body composition reference values or thresholds have been published in both healthy and clinical populations; 11 , 12 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 however, these data are often not representative of worldwide human populations. This evidence is also limited to investigations in cancer, 11 , 14 or include alternative vertebral landmarks to L3, 14 or assess muscles other than total abdominal musculature, 12 , 17 and all but one review 16 included studies that were published before December 2020. While Weston et al. 18 is an original research study and not a systematic review, they provide a comparison of their reference values for subcutaneous adipose tissue index (SATI), visceral adipose tissue index (VATI), SMI, skeletal muscle radiodensity (SMD), bone area index, and bone density against recently published values by Magudia et al., 19 Navin et al., 20 and Pickhardt et al. 21 Ju et al. is a systematic review and meta‐analysis of all studies published through January 1, 2025 for SMI and SMD reference values and T‐scores or thresholds for SMI and SMD in healthy adults 18–45 years of age. 16

Aims of the narrative review

Despite decades of progress, CT‐derived body‐composition research remains fragmented by methodological discrepancies and unanswered questions that require consensus. Key issues include which variables best represent whole‐body muscularity (and how to normalize them for stature or body size), how to select population‐appropriate reference values, and how to derive consistent CT‐based thresholds for low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition. Establishing standardized, population‐representative CT reference data and uniform thresholds is essential to enable valid cross‐study comparisons and accurate identification of clinically relevant phenotypes (e.g., sarcopenia, myosteatosis, sarcopenic obesity). Sarcopenia is defined by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 as low muscle strength and low muscle quantity or quality, 22 whereas myosteatosis is a measure of muscle composition or fat infiltration into the muscle as assessed by CT. 23 , 24 Sarcopenic obesity is defined as the coexistence of obesity or excess adiposity and sarcopenia or low muscle mass or function. 25

In this narrative review, we aimed to: (1) describe newer approaches to normalize skeletal muscle measurements for differences in body sizes, (2) provide an overview of the heterogeneity in deriving currently published CT reference criteria for body composition quantity and quality in a limited subset of studies from published literature, (3) summarize currently published thresholds from a limited subset of studies, including those most commonly cited, applied to define low muscle quantity or poor muscle composition and compare and contrast these values, as well as their derivation, and (4) highlight emerging areas utilizing AI technologies and the integration of CT body composition assessment into clinical practice.

Literature search strategy

Because previous publications have included studies on CT body composition quantity and poor muscle composition (“muscle quality”) before December 2021, we conducted a literature search in PubMed for research studies published between January 2022 and December 2025 to be inclusive of all body composition metrics based on four concepts: body composition, CT, L3, and reference values/thresholds. The articles were filtered to include human subjects only, adults aged 19 years and older, published in the English language, and duplicates were removed. 106 article abstracts were screened in Rayyan, with articles excluded based on the wrong outcome (e.g., association between body composition and postoperative complications, long‐term survival, long‐term mortality, or agreement between assessment methods) or population (e.g., pediatric subjects) studied (n = 80), not utilizing an abdominal CT or axial image at L3 (n = 5), and not measuring all muscles from the abdominal CT (n = 8), resulting in 13 full articles reviewed. Of these 13 articles, three were further excluded due to assessing the association of body composition with various clinical disease states and characteristics (e.g., cardiac dysrhythmia, decubitus ulcers, sleep disorders) and determining sarcopenia and myosteatosis prevalence and prognostic implications based on various published thresholds. Eight of the articles specifically informed the summarization of reference values for body composition (Table 2) and thresholds for low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition (Table 3). The references in the 13 articles were further searched for applicable literature, which resulted in the identification of four articles to inform the summarization of reference values and thresholds (Tables 2 and 3, respectively). The search strategy can be found in the supplementary materials. In addition, a secondary search was conducted to scan the literature for articles pertaining to body composition analysis via AI and clinical integration or clinical implications. The articles were filtered for those published between January 2022 and December 2025. While this strategy was not within the scope of this narrative review, it provided a glimpse into emerging research in AI assessment of body composition.

CURRENT ISSUES IN CT BODY COMPOSITION ASSESSMENT

Challenging the status quo: Moving beyond skeletal muscle index

Skeletal muscle mass is dependent on body size. 1 , 3 Similar to the calculation for body mass index (BMI), where it was found that weight scaled to height squared, 26 , 27 SMI was created as a measure to compare skeletal muscle mass across individuals with different heights as skeletal muscle mass scaled to height with a power of ~2. 28 Recently, research has shown a negative correlation between SMI and height and a positive correlation between SMI and BMI; therefore, the use of SMI may bias the results to identify sarcopenia in taller individuals and those with low BMI. 29 Additionally, failing to adjust skeletal muscle mass area by BMI may inadvertently fail to identify individuals with obesity and with low muscle mass (sarcopenic obesity). Recent studies have challenged the use of SMI as conventionally calculated. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 The optimal body size adjustment should be independent of height and BMI in a healthy, young adult population and would distinguish between muscularity at any height and BMI; 33 however, consensus does not currently exist amongst body composition experts on the most appropriate adjustment for body size.

In a cohort of colorectal cancer patients, body composition measurements at L3, particularly SM CSA, scaled to height at a power different than 2 using allometric analysis with different powers for males and females, indicating that calculating SMI as conventionally done may lead to misclassification of sarcopenia and biased results in prognostication. 32 In a large cohort of individuals who received an abdominal CT, divided into two groups based on the presence of major medical conditions, SM CSA scaled to height at a power of 1.02 in men and 1.08 in women 40 years of age and younger and 1.07 in men and 1.10 in women older than 40 years of age. Using SM CSA scaled to a power of 1 versus 2 in the group with major medical conditions, the concordance index was higher in both males and females in predicting all‐cause mortality. 30 In light of this evidence, the authors called for a possible revision to the conventional calculation of SMI. 30 In a smaller cohort from a single institution including individuals with abdominal CT, SM CSA scaled to a power of 0.98 in males and 1.54 in females. The use of skeletal muscle height (SMH), calculated as SM CSA (cm2) divided by height (m), may be a more accurate measurement to assess loss of muscle using a single CSA at L3 via CT. 31 SM CSA is positively correlated with height, however, the correlation subsided once SM CSA was divided by height (SMH), except in males over the age of 40 years. 31 The authors proposed the use of SMH over SMI to reduce confusion in the literature on the indices used to assess the loss of muscle. 31 Finally, in a large cohort of healthy kidney donors, relative muscle index (RMI) has been proposed as an unbiased method to scale muscle CSA across different heights and BMI. 33 Using an equation that includes SM CSA, height, BMI and sex, RMI is calculated with values <0 indicating below average SM and values >0 indicating above average SM. RMI −2.0 standard deviations indicate low muscle quantity in adults <40 years. RMI needs to be further tested in diverse populations, as well as tested against clinical outcomes. 33 Derstine et al. recommends “SMA/height be used as the consensus height‐adjusted index” and SMI be discontinued. 33 However, consensus does not currently exist amongst body composition experts on the optimal body size adjustment for calculating SM CSA across different body sizes. Overview of currently published reference values and applicability to diverse populations

The lack of reference data for CT‐assessed body composition in regionally or nationally representative populations continues to challenge research progress and limit applicability to clinical settings. Additionally, the reference values published using clinical populations may be biased with limited external validity as they are based on populations that obtained CT for assessment of a medical condition. 18 , 34 Recent progress has been made in publishing reference values for adult body composition quantity and quality (see Table 2 for a summarization of currently published values) across different populations; however, the vast majority of the work has been completed in White populations, which is not representative of national or international populations. Additionally, these reference values have not been validated or accepted through a consensus process by an international collaboration of body composition experts. Validated reference values across diverse populations are still needed to establish CT‐based thresholds for low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition.

Table 2 provides an overview of currently published reference values for assessing body composition quantity and quality in adult populations using abdominal CT at L3, from a subset of the literature published between 2022 and 2025. The reference data published stems from individuals followed prospectively as part of the Framingham Heart Study (FHS), to kidney donors from the U.S., Israel, and the Netherlands, to 18–86 year olds from India undergoing CT for incidental findings on health check‐ups or acute abdomen, to a Korean population undergoing routine health screenings, to a cohort from the Rochester Epidemiology Project sampled to be representative of the upper Midwest population aged 20–89 years. The average ages of individuals assessed ranged from 30.2 ± 5.77 years in kidney donors 33 to 55.6 ± 11.8 years 37 , 40 in adults followed as part of the FHS with a BMI ranging from 22.8 ± 3.4 kg/m2, 41 in females to 31.2 ± 5.0 kg/m2 in males. 37 These studies were conducted in predominantly White populations, though three studies were specific to a Korean 41 and an Indian population, 35 , 39 and younger kidney donors (<40 years) had a higher proportion of Black representation (14.4% in females, 12.5% in males) compared with those >40 years (8.0% in females, 5.4% in males). 33

The body composition quantity assessments include skeletal muscle CSA, SMI, SMH, SAT CSA and index (SATI), VAT CSA and index (VATI), and vertebral bone area index. The body composition quality metrics include skeletal muscle radiation attenuation (SMRA) or SMD, skeletal muscle gauge (SMG), IMAT, subcutaneous adipose tissue radiation attenuation (SATRA), subcutaneous adipose tissue gauge (SATG), visceral adipose tissue radiation attenuation (VATRA), visceral adipose tissue gauge (VATG), and vertebral bone density. SMD (also referred to as SMRA) is a CT‐derived measure of skeletal muscle X‐ray attenuation, expressed in Hounsfield Units (HU), with lower values indicating greater adipose tissue infiltration and poorer muscle composition. 42 , 43 SMG is an integrated measure that combines both SMI and SMD (SMI X SMD) 33 , 44 and appears to be a better predictor of clinical outcomes in cancer than either quantity or composition measure alone. 44 , 45

Comparison of recently published reference values for body composition quantity

When comparing the body composition quantity metrics, the SM CSA is similar for Tonnesen et al. 37 and Derstine et al., 33 using the mid‐vertebral reference values, in females <45 and <40 years (120.9 ± 18.4 and 121.77 ± 18.34 cm2) and in males <45 and <40 years, respectively (194.3 ± 26.0 and 189.34 ± 26.92 cm2). The higher male SM CSA in the Tonnesen et al. results may be due to a higher mean BMI (31.2 ± 5.0 vs. 27.69 ± 4.69) compared with the male population in Derstine et al. as BMI is positively correlated with muscle CSA. 29 The median muscle CSA from the Weston et al. 18 population is comparable in males (188.9 cm2) but higher in females (131.7 cm2), which may be due to the larger age range from 20 to 89 years and larger representation from 20 to 44 year olds (35.7% and 35.6% of females and males, respectively).

The SM CSAs are much lower in the Korean population (females: 103.2 ± 14.9 cm2 and males: 162.3 ± 21.8 cm2) and Indian population (females: 106.3 ± 12.0 cm2 and males: 147.7 ± 11.76 cm2), possibly due to the lower BMIs (female: 22.8 ± 3.4 and male: 24.3 ± 3.3 kg/m2) in the Korean population 41 and due to there being no BMI available for the Indian population. 35 There are no CSA values available for comparison in Druckmann et al. 38

Similarly, the SMI for the younger age categories is comparable for the Tonnesen et al., Derstine et al., and Weston et al. populations in females and males, but the values are lower in the Druckmann et al. (41.2 ± 7.3 cm2/m2 and 51.4 ± 8.3 cm2/m2 in females and males <45 years), Shin et al. (39.4 ± 5.5 cm2/m2 and 53.0 ± 7.9 cm2/m2 in females and males), and Choudhary et al. (43.93 ± 6.05 cm2/m2 and 51.0 ± 5.70 cm2/m2 in females and males) populations and lowest for the Sidhu et al. population (for females 21–40 years: 37.30 cm2/m2; for males 21–40 years: 38.20 cm2/m2 for males). 35 , 38 , 39 , 41 These differences may be due to a larger average BMI in the Tonnesen et al. population and the higher average age in the Shin et al. population (~50 years), as SM CSA is negatively correlated with age over 40 years. 29

When comparing reference values at L3, measurements from an inferior aspect slice are significantly different compared with a mid‐vertebral location; therefore, appropriate reference values should be used for the appropriate slice location. 33 In two of the nine studies included in this review, the slice location was not disclosed, which may further contribute to heterogeneity and difficulty in comparing reference values. Overall, demographics of the population, including age, BMI, and race/ethnicity, as well as CT image specifications, including the slice location, contribute to differences in the currently published reference values across the studies included in Table 2.

Marquardt et al. 40 and Weston et al. 18 provide reference data for SAT CSA and index (cm2/m2). Interestingly, the SATI appears to increase with age in females (83.0 ± 50.1 at <45 years to 104.1 ± 47.8 cm2/m2 ≥75 years), whereas it appears to be more consistent in older males (69.6 ± 35.2 at <45 years to 77.3 ± 29.6 at 65–74 years). 40 This may be due to females accumulating more SAT following menopause, particularly in the abdominal area. 46 In Weston et al., 18 the 50th percentile for SATI ranges from 84 cm2/m2 in females 85–89 years to 116.4 cm2/m2 in females 40–44 years old. In males, the values are much lower compared with females and range from 41.8 cm2/m2 in 20‐ to 24‐year‐olds to 72 cm2/m2 in 45‐ to 49‐year‐olds. SATI appears to maximize in mid‐life with the highest values accumulating between 40 and 49 years in females and males. The differences in SATI reference values in males between the Marquardt et al. and Weston et al. populations may be due to the differences in age between the populations, as well as the reporting of mean versus median values. While the mean age is ~55 years in both populations, the age span is quite different with individuals 38–81 years in the Marquardt et al. population and 20–89 years in the Weston et al. population, where 20‐ to 44‐year‐olds make up 35.6% of the sample.

Additionally, VATI increases in both females (28.9 ± 25.5 to 59.5 ± 30.6 cm2/m2) and males (65.4 ± 30.8 to 99.1 ± 39.5 cm2/m2) as they age, though values are higher in males throughout the lifespan (see Table 2). 40 In the Weston et al. population, the VATI reference values range from 12.5 cm2/m2 in 20–24 year old females to 52.3 cm2/m2 in 70–74 year old females. 18 In males, the VATI values range from 16.4 cm2/m2 in 20–24 year olds to 87.8 cm2/m2 70–74 year olds. 18 Finally, Baggerman et al. provide reference data for VATI in females and males by BMI group (Table 2), which are more in line with the values from Weston et al. In females, the VATI ranges from 16.2 ± 12.0 cm2/m2 (BMI < 24.9) to 51.6 ± 23.1 cm2/m2 (BMI > 30), whereas in males, the VATI ranges from 30.8 ± 17.2 cm2/m2 (BMI < 24.9) to 72.2 ± 22.4 cm2/m2 (BMI > 30). 36 These values demonstrate differences by sex and BMI, and the lower VATI values from the Weston et al. population may be due to a higher representation of younger individuals as VAT increases with age. 47

Although reference values for body composition quantity continue to be published across different populations, clinicians should recognize that these reference values have not been validated in diverse populations or accepted through a consensus process by an international collaboration of body composition experts. This is a critical gap in body composition research and should be resolved prior to clinical implementation.

Comparison of recently published reference values for body composition quality

Tonnesen et al. and Derstine et al. provide reference data for SMD and SMG, while Weston et al. provides reference data on SMD. In all three populations, the SMD and SMG decrease with age, consistent with other studies that found worsening muscle composition with aging. 18 , 23 , 48 The SMD values are similar when comparing the Derstine et al. (43.95 ± 5.79 HU females <40 years; 48.09 ± 5.62 HU males <40 years) and Tonnesen et al. reference values (42.6 ± 5.5 for females <45 years; 45.2 ± 5.6 for males <45 years), but the SMD values from the Weston et al. population are lower in females and males (50th percentile values: 30.4–39.6 HU in 20–44 year old females; 36.6–46.3 HU in 20–44 year old males). It must be noted that it is unknown whether intravenous contrast was used during the procedure for the CT scans evaluated in the Weston et al. study. Recent reviews found that intravenous contrast significantly increased the muscle radiodensity (HU), and therefore, the use of contrast may impact reference values for SMD. 16 , 49 With the contrast status unknown, it is difficult to determine how the Weston et al. values compare to the Tonnesen et al. and Derstine et al. reference values. The Druckmann et al. and Choudhary et al. values cannot be compared as the radiodensity was only calculated for the psoas muscle and not all the abdominal muscles. 35 , 38 The reference values for SMG are not comparable for the Tonnesen et al. and Derstine et al. populations, as Derstine et al. computes the values based on height and not height squared. Despite this methodological difference, the SMG values decrease with age in both populations.

Marquardt et al. is the only study that published reference values for SATRA, SATG, VATRA, and VATG. The SATRA values are comparable between females and males (ranging from −103.9 ± 6.7 to −100.2 ± 6.8 to HU in females; −101.4 ± 5.6 to −98.2 ± 5.9 to in males), but the values for SATG are more negative in males (−6695.4 ± 968.6 to −5638.3 ± 809.6 HU*cm2/m2) compared with females (−4917.9 ± 840.9 to −4421.8 ± 774.6 HU*cm2/m2), indicating either more SAT volume or less SAT radiodensity or a combination of both. VATRA values are more negative in males (−93.9 ± 7.8 to −92.4 ± 9.0 HU) compared with females (−90.5 ± 7.6 to −80.6 ± 10.6 HU), indicating less radiodensity or more VAT accumulation, with VATG values similarly more negative in males (−6163.4 ± 1109.0 to −5341.9 ± 910.7 HU) compared with females (−4036.7 ± 965.1 to −3943.6 ± 959.0 HU). The reason for these differences in SAT and VAT radiodensity are currently unknown, but one reason may be due to sexual dimorphism or differences in how males and females accumulate adipose tissue throughout the body. 50

Comparison of recently published thresholds to define low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition

Heterogeneity in the use of thresholds and inaccurate use of terminology for describing body components leads to difficulty comparing results across studies. 3 In seminal research on body composition, some studies use thresholds based on optimal stratification or the risk for death in patients with respiratory or gastrointestinal cancer (<38.5 cm2/m2 in females and <52.4 cm2/m2 in males; 6 <43 cm2/m2 if BMI <25 kg/m2 and <53 cm2/m2 if BMI ≥25 kg/m2 for males; <41 cm2/m2 for females), 51 or the thresholds used to define low musculature in the cancer cachexia consensus definition (<55 cm2/m2 in males; <39 cm2/m2 in females), 52 or thresholds based on two standard deviations below the healthy reference values for SMI at L3 (≤45.4 cm2/m2 for males; ≤34.4 cm2/m2 for females). 53 In recent reviews, the thresholds from Prado et al. 6 and Martin et al. 51 were the most common values used with 17 others used to define sarcopenia, 11 and 32 different thresholds used to define myosteatosis in 73 studies. 15 Consensus from body composition experts does not currently exist on the most appropriate thresholds to use to define low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition. Additionally, the use of thresholds may not adequately capture individual risk for low muscle quantity or poor muscle composition or equate to clinical outcomes, particularly for those individuals whose measurements are close to the threshold. 53

The sex‐specific reference mean SMI and the thresholds for sarcopenia identification at vertebral levels ranging from the 10th thoracic (T10) to the 5th lumbar (L5) are based on groups that may not be reflective of diverse racial and ethnic populations. 53 Different thresholds for low muscle quantity have been published in healthy populations and in individuals with cancer, but these studies have predominantly assessed White populations. Evidence indicates that body composition differs by race/ethnicity; 19 , 54 however, as of yet, reference data do not exist for SMI or SMD in various racial/ethnic groups. These data would allow for the subsequent development of thresholds for low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition in both aging and in chronic disease. Derstine et al. argues that race/ethnic specific thresholds are not needed but rather the values need to be adjusted for age and BMI as skeletal muscle indices are highly correlated with these demographic and anthropometric characteristics. 29 However, consensus does not currently exist amongst body composition experts on the most appropriate thresholds to use to define low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition.

The prevalence of sarcopenia and its prognostic implications in various cancer populations varies greatly based on the evaluation criteria used to define sarcopenia, as well as the vertebral landmark used. 11 , 41 , 55 , 56 , 57 This highlights the need for standardization of criteria and the inclusion of diverse non‐clinical populations in development of the criteria. Table 3 summarizes the recent thresholds that have been published over the past 3 years (n = 7 studies) to define low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition in adults using CT‐derived values at L3. Also included are thresholds as published by Prado et al., 6 Fearon et al., 52 Martin et al., 51 and Derstine et al., 53 as these values have been used historically to define low muscle quantity in cancer and in other populations. As can be seen in Table 3, the values vary greatly depending on the population studied, and these thresholds should not be interpreted as consensus‐based since they are drawn from a limited subset of studies from existing literature.

Of the seven studies published over the past 3 years, six of the studies were conducted in healthy populations and one in a population with gastrointestinal cancers. The SMI thresholds for defining low muscle quantity are similar for Kong et al. 58 (female: 28.6 cm2/m2; male: 37.9 cm2/m2) and Shin et al. 41 (female: 28.4 cm2/m2; male: 37.2 cm2/m2 for Class 2 sarcopenia), which included populations of Asian descent (i.e., Chinese and Korean) with similar BMI. Sidhu et al. had a similar BMI to Kong and Shin et al. (22.9 ± 3.7 kg/m2) but their published thresholds based on the 5th percentile are the lowest values at 22.2 and 22.3 cm2/m2 in females and males, respectively. 39 The thresholds published in the other Indian population (31.83 cm2/m2 in females and 39.59 cm2/m2 in males) are slightly higher than the Asian populations but information on BMI or body size are not reported for comparison purposes. 35 Westenberg et al. 59 and Derstine et al. 33 both studied potential kidney donors <40 years old; however, the Derstine SMI thresholds for defining low muscle quantity are lower for females (31.76 cm2/m2) and higher for males (42.11 cm2/m2) when compared with the Westenberg values (37.5 cm2/m2 for females 20–29 years; 35.5 cm2/m2 for females 30–39 years; 38.8 cm2/m2 for males 20–29 years; 39.2 cm2/m2 for males 30–39 years). It must be noted that Derstine et al. recommends the discontinuation of SMI as it is inversely correlated with height, favoring diagnosis of sarcopenia in taller individuals; 33 the values provided here are for comparison purposes only. Aleixo et al. proposed specific thresholds for the Black population, studying individuals with gastrointestinal cancers. 60 The threshold for Black females (35 cm2/m2) is similar to the Westenberg et al. value, however, the threshold for Black males is higher and comparable to the value published by Shin et al. It should be noted that these Black‐specific values are similar to the values published by Derstine et al. 53 in a healthy kidney donor population, which are higher than the most recent SMI thresholds published by Derstine et al. 33 Overall, the different criteria used to define low muscle quantity (e.g., mean −2 standard deviations vs. 5th percentile for the population distribution vs. optimal stratification) as well as differences in the populations themselves, contributed to heterogeneity in the thresholds for low muscle quantity.

Regarding muscle composition, the SMD thresholds for low muscle radiodensity range from 32.38 to 40 HU for females and 36.85 to 45 HU in males for adults <40 years of age for publications in the last 3 years. These values are in line with previously published values of <33 HU for females or males with BMI ≥25 kg/m2 or <41 HU for females or males with BMI <25 kg/m2, 51 and 34.3 HU for females and 38.5 HU for males, 53 but there is a lot of variability amongst the values themselves. The thresholds for the Choudhary et al. study 35 (45.01 HU in females and 47.98 HU in males) are higher but are not comparable as they were calculated using psoas muscle only. In females, the highest radiodensity threshold stems from Black women with gastrointestinal cancers, 60 whereas in males, the highest value was in 20–29 year old healthy kidney donors. 59 The SMD thresholds by Westenberg et al. must be interpreted with caution, as the CT scans evaluated were enhanced with intravenous contrast, which may significantly increase muscle radiodensity per recent reviews. 16 , 49 In gastrointestinal cancers, mean SMD was higher in Blacks compared with Whites, but SMI was similar, indicating that muscle composition may differ by race/ethnicity in the context of cancer. 60

Sources of variation in CT scan acquisition

The CT image specifications for analysis may also be contributing to differences in threshold values for defining low muscle quantity or poor muscle composition. Most studies (6 of 11 in Table 3) disclose the timing of the CT assessed, however, there is variability in when the CT images are being conducted (e.g., ±30 days since initial/BMI assessment to 30 days after the initiation of treatment). Most of the studies published over the past 3 years are conducted in healthy populations (excluding cancer diagnosis), but cancer treatment may impact muscle metabolism and lead to sarcopenia, 61 , 62 affecting any thresholds determined after treatment initiation. Additionally, the axial slice location at L3 (e.g., mid versus inferior aspect) is not disclosed in most studies in Table 3, however, Derstine et al. demonstrated significantly different values based on the slice location. 33 The use of L3 at the inferior aspect slice versus mid‐vertebral level resulted in significantly different mean CSA across all vertebral bodies from T10‐L5, except in men at T10 with differences in CSA ranging from −7.2% to 7.5%. 33 While prior research has demonstrated differing SM CSA across vertebral bodies, 53 current research demonstrates within differences at L3, depending on the slice location. 33 Therefore, it is critical that studies disclose the slice location to ensure similar values are being compared. Finally, 8 of the 11 studies in Table 3 disclose the use of contrast in the CT procedure. Recent reviews found that contrast significantly increased the muscle radiodensity (HU), and therefore, the use of contrast may impact thresholds used to define myosteatosis and its classification. 16 , 49 However, a statistical difference may not always translate to clinical relevance. For example, in men, the mean difference was 5.4 HU higher in contrast versus non‐contrast images, and in women the mean difference was 8.3 HU. 16 How this magnitude of difference translates to clinical relevancy remains to be determined and needs to be further explored. For muscle area, contrast resulted in a 0%–2.58% change in muscle area from baseline to one or more contrast phases and likely represents clinical irrelevance. 49

Although various thresholds for low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition continue to be published across different healthy and clinical populations, clinicians should recognize that these thresholds have not been validated in diverse populations. Additionally, body composition experts do not agree on the most appropriate method to use to define these thresholds. This is a critical gap in body composition research and should be resolved prior to clinical implementation.

FUTURE OF BODY COMPOSITION RESEARCH

AI and enhanced body composition data

The advent of AI has allowed for the automated 3D analysis of CT using multi‐slice methods to estimate the volumes for SM, SAT, VAT, and IMAT, as well as organs such as the liver, spleen, and pancreas allowing for “extended body composition analysis.” 63 This includes automated vertebral landmarking and segmentation of tissue and organs across a larger scanning area, allowing for the analysis of within person differences in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue across vertebral areas, 7 especially when L3 via an abdominal CT may not be available. 14 While skeletal muscle and adipose tissue single‐slice measurements at L3 are highly correlated with total body volumes, 4 , 5 there may be variability at other vertebral landmarks in their correlation with total body volumes, as well as variation in tissue distribution across the body. 63 3D volumetric analysis provides a vast amount of information from an individual CT that was not previously available via single slice 2D analysis, but how to analyze this multi‐slice information efficiently and use these data to improve an individual's prognosis and create individualized treatment interventions is a challenge. 63 In addition, issues of legal liability and data privacy are real concerns that must be addressed before the clinical implementation of automated 3D body composition analysis occurs. 10

Few studies have validated the use of multi‐slice methods against the use of a single CSA for body composition analysis, as well as its relation to clinical outcomes. Anyene et al. validated the use of multi‐slice methods from thoracic 12 (T12) to lumbar 5 (L5) via Data Analysis Facilitation Suite (DAFS), a fully automated program for rapid segmentation of organs and body composition measurements previously validated at L3, 64 against the use of a single CSA in patients with colorectal cancer. 7 This study found that single slice areas at L3 and multi‐slice abdominal volumes at T12‐L5 were highly correlated for SM, SAT, VAT, and IMAT (r = 0.89–0.98, p < 0.001) with narrow limits of agreement on Bland Altman plots. In addition, the association with all‐cause mortality was similar for single versus multi‐slice measurements (e.g., per standard deviation for SM: HR 0.82 (0.72–0.92) for single CSA versus HR 0.85 (0.75–0.96) for multi‐slice volumes; per standard deviation for IMAT: HR 1.16 (1.06, 1.27) for single CSA versus HR 1.13 (1.03, 1.25) for multi‐slice volumes). 7 Similarly, Brown et al. found high correlations for single (L3) versus multi‐slice measurements (T12‐L4) for SM, SAT, VAT, and IMAT (r = 0.88–0.98) in patients with esophageal cancer with narrow limits of agreement for SM, SAT, and VAT and slightly wider limits of agreement for IMAT and SMD on Bland Altman plots. 65 The association with overall survival was similarly improved for SM, SAT, and VAT but less consistent for IMAT when comparing single versus multi‐slice body composition. 65 The authors note that, in obesity, single slice measurements may not accurately reflect the quantity and distribution of adipose tissue and, therefore, the concordance between single and multi‐slice body composition is lower. 65 Despite this, various seminal research studies have demonstrated that single‐slice area versus multi‐slice volume measurements are comparable when evaluating body composition cross‐sectionally, 7 , 65 but multi‐slice volume measurements may allow for longitudinal assessments to determine body composition changes within an individual in response to interventions or disease progression. 7

AI and clinical compatibility

The advent of AI has produced large amounts of data in body composition research, including the analysis of larger scanning areas, tissues, and organs, but the challenge on how to integrate these data into clinical practice to benefit individual health and improve clinical outcomes still exists. Thus, future studies need to clarify the benefits of integrating body composition assessment into clinical practice, and demonstrate how to use this information to develop personalized nutrition and exercise interventions. 66 For example, clinically integrated CT‐based body composition analysis would instantly, and potentially simultaneously, identify adverse body composition phenotypes including sarcopenia, sarcopenic obesity, and myosteatosis at any body weight. With this information at a cancer diagnosis, a multi‐modal approach can be developed to treat sarcopenia and prevent further loss of muscle through personalized nutrition interventions to improve calorie and protein intake and optimize chemotherapy dosing to minimize treatment toxicities. 66

The Opportunistic Screening Consortium in Abdominal Radiology (OSCAR) is a multi‐site trial led by investigators to develop an automated, vendor‐neutral body composition solution for clinical translation. 67 OSCAR has created an automated body composition analysis pipeline that integrates all stages of the process from data normalization, to anatomical landmarking and segmentation of tissues and organs, to biomarker extraction including segmentation of images, all done in an efficient amount of time. 68 Because the pipeline is compatible with various scanner types and imaging protocols, it has the potential for widespread clinical adaptability. 68 Future work includes validating the pipeline in various diverse clinical populations, as well as integration of the pipeline into electronic medical records. The authors also note that a key limitation of the pipeline is the requirement for GPU hardware, which may lead to significant implementation costs and limit accessibility in under resourced healthcare environments. 68 While body composition analysis via AI and clinical integration is not within the scope of this review, it is the new frontier for body composition research.

GAPS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

In this narrative review, we describe emerging research that challenges the traditional adjustment of skeletal muscle mass across different body sizes, summarize recently published reference values for body composition quantity and quality across diverse, healthy populations, highlight the use of inconsistent criteria for measuring body composition, and compare the different thresholds to define low muscle quantity or poor muscle composition. At present, body composition experts do not agree on how CT‐derived (or other) body composition data should be interpreted, and there is no consensus on how to define low muscle quantity or poor muscle composition. In addition, heterogeneity in vertebral landmarks and muscles assessed, CT specifications, and timing of the CT scans makes it difficult to compare body composition results across studies, creating further barriers to the development of validated, consensus‐based reference values for body composition and thresholds for low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition.

Body composition assessment is in a new era of discovery, development, and innovation. CONCLUSION

In conclusion, CT body composition assessment has revolutionized clinical medicine and other related fields as it slowly unravels the tremendous impact body composition phenotypes, particularly sarcopenia, sarcopenic obesity, and myosteatosis, have on clinical outcomes. Despite these advancements, there are still methodological discrepancies that hinder the field. Recent research over the past 3 years advocates for the use of a BMI and height‐independent metric to assess skeletal muscle quantity to ensure the unbiased identification of sarcopenia, but additional studies in larger, more diverse populations are needed. Additionally, consensus amongst body composition experts is needed to discontinue the use of SMI for skeletal muscle quantification, otherwise, we will continue to publish data and results that are not comparable across studies. This review confirms the need for validated, consensus‐based reference values for non‐clinical, diverse populations for body composition quantity and quality, along with the use of consistent thresholds to define low muscle quantity and poor muscle composition. We summarize and compare the values recently published and discuss the limitations of existing thresholds for these phenotypes in both non‐clinical and clinical populations and provide a list of research areas that need further investigation and exploration. While there is exciting work being done in body composition research using AI, we need to determine how to integrate body composition assessment into clinical practice so it is readily available for the practitioner and can assist with clinical decision making for personalized nutrition care and improved clinical outcomes.
