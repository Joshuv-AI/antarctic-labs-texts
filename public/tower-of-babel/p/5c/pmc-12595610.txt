Longitudinal echocardiographic monitoring and cardiovascular outcomes in adult survivors of childhood cancer
Authors: Eli Grunblatt, Ian K Everitt, Karen Kinahan, Aarati Didwania, Jane N Winter, Zhiying Meng, Abigail S Baldridge, Nausheen Akhter, Vera H Rigolin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdult survivors of childhood cancers are thought to be at high risk for late-onset cardiovascular complications of prior anti-cancer treatments. While cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) has previously been observed in this population, data are limited regarding the utility of longitudinal echocardiographic monitoring.Methods124 adult survivors of childhood cancer who were previously treated with anthracyclines and/or radiation therapy as children were included in this longitudinal cohort study. Participants were enrolled in Northwestern Medicine’s survivorship clinic: Survivors Taking Action and Responsibility (STAR) Program and followed for up to10 years between 2009 and 2022. Serial echocardiography was performed at baseline then at 1, 3, 5, and 7–10 years post-enrollment. Clinical data was collected by chart review. Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and CTRCD were adjudicated according to ACC/AHA MACE criteria and the ESC Cardio-Oncology guidelines respectively.ResultsOver 10 years of follow-up in the STAR Program, 17.7% (22/124) of participants developed MACE, with the highest incidence observed in patients treated with radiation alone. 10.5% (13/124) of patients developed CTRCD by left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) criteria while 36.3% (45/124) developed CTRCD by global longitudinal strain (GLS) criteria. New incidence of MACE and CTRCD occurred as late as 7–10 years post-enrollment. Participants who developed CTRCD showed subsequent improvement in both LVEF and GLS by the end of 10 years of follow-up and treatment in the STAR Program.ConclusionsSurvivors of childhood cancer are at high risk for both MACE and CTRCD even many years after initial diagnosis. Patients who received treatment for pediatric cancer, then subsequently enrolled in the STAR Program as adults and received appropriate cardiovascular monitoring and treatment showed improvement in both LVEF and GLS by the end of follow-up, underscoring the importance of a dedicated survivorship clinic for adult survivors of pediatric cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40959-025-00399-2.Keywords: Cancer survivorship, Pediatric cancer, Echocardiography, Cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction, Global longitudinal strain, Adult survivors of childhood cancer

Background

The majority of children and adolescents who are diagnosed with a malignancy become long-term cancer survivors, with some studies estimating that there are currently 480,000 cancer survivors in the United States who were diagnosed with cancer while under the age of 20 [1–3]. These patients are at increased life-long risk for adverse health conditions as sequelae of the anticancer therapies used to treat their primary malignancies [4–7]. Cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) is a common clinical outcome resulting from multiple anticancer treatments, including cardiotoxic chemotherapeutic agents, chest-directed external radiotherapy (XRT), and hormonal agents [8]. Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including coronary artery disease (CAD) and heart failure (HF) as defined by the ACC/AHA guidelines, have been most extensively documented with XRT and anthracycline chemotherapy, and are well-recognized complications related to these treatments [8–11]. The cumulative incidence of HF by 30 years from diagnosis is 12% for those exposed to both chest XRT and anthracycline therapy, and treatment-related cardiac death is the most common non-cancer cause of mortality in this population [12, 13]. Adult survivors of childhood cancer may be at particular risk with some studies showing significantly increased 10-year mortality following cardiovascular events for these patients [7, 14]. Studies have also shown that adult survivors of childhood cancer may experience accelerated biological aging, putting them at greater risk of cardiovascular complications compared to age matched controls [15]. As such, early detection and management of CTRCD is integral in the care of this high-risk population. The current standard of care emphasizes periodic surveillance using echocardiography to evaluate left ventricular function through measurement of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and global longitudinal strain (GLS) [8, 16, 17]. Reduction in GLS has been shown to independently predict cardiac mortality and MACE in both non-cancer and cancer populations, such that a relative decline in GLS by > 15% from baseline has been incorporated in recent European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines defining CTRCD, even in the absence of abnormal LVEF. Previously the main criteria for CTRCD was defined as a decrease in LVEF from baseline to an LVEF of ≤ 49% [8, 18–22]. As survivors of childhood cancers have increased risk of both CTRCD and MACE, dedicated cancer survivorship clinics provide an important platform for patients to undergo longitudinal surveillance for adverse cardiovascular sequelae of anticancer treatment in a setting designed to help manage these effects. In this observational cohort study, we describe an adult cohort of childhood cancer survivors followed in a single center multidisciplinary cancer survivorship program for a 10-year period after undergoing cancer directed treatment with anthracycline chemotherapy and/or XRT. We evaluated the trends in longitudinal echocardiographic parameters and cardiovascular outcomes in this patient population over a long-term follow-up.

Methods

Study participants and clinical data

Patients aged 18 years and older who were previously treated for childhood cancer and who follow in the Survivors Taking Action and Responsibility (STAR) Program at Northwestern Medicine were eligible for inclusion in the study. The STAR Program is a comprehensive, multidisciplinary clinic consisting of Internal Medicine providers and subspecialists including cardiologists who provide lifetime longitudinal follow-up care for adult survivors of childhood cancers that were diagnosed and treated before age 21. Analysis was limited to participants who received anthracycline chemotherapy and/or chest XRT during their treatment, and who underwent a baseline medical assessment including echocardiography between 2009 and 2019. Patients with moderate or severe valvular disease at baseline were excluded from analysis. Participants were followed for up to 10 years, or through December 2022, whichever occurred first. Clinical data including demographics, malignancy type, treatment history, cardiovascular co-morbidities, and medication history were collected via chart review. For patients in this cohort, the initiation of cardiovascular medications, including beta blockers, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), angiotensin receptor blocker/neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), and statins was directed by health care providers affiliated with the STAR Program based on established clinical practice guidelines when available or based on clinical judgement in instances where practice guidelines were not available. The use of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors was not assessed in this study as this class of medications was not yet recommended for HF patients at the time of the study period. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Northwestern University.

Echocardiography analysis

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using commercially available equipment (Philips iE33 or Epiq (Phillips Healthcare, Andover, Massachusetts), GE Vivid 90, or Vivid 95 (GE Vingmed Ultrasound, Horten, Norway). Comprehensive transthoracic echocardiograms with Doppler evaluation were performed using standard protocols according to American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines [23, 24]. Baseline echocardiograms were performed on enrollment in the STAR Program, as well as at 1 year, 3 years, 5 years, and 7–10 years post-enrollment. LVEF was quantified using the modified Simpson’s biplane method. GLS was calculated by tracing the endocardial border in the three standard apical views, using a commercially available software in the Philips and GE echo machines or offline using a vendor-independent analysis program (TomTec Imaging Systems, Munich, Germany). CTRCD was defined as either a reduction in LVEF from baseline to ≤ 49% (LVEF criteria) or a reduction in GLS by > 15% from baseline (GLS criteria) per ESC guidelines [8].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics and echocardiographic measures over time. Variables were presented as mean and standard deviations, median and interquartile ranges (if non-normally distributed), or frequency and percent. Baseline characteristics were compared between treatment groups using one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis Test, Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Mixed effects logistic mixed regression models were performed to evaluate the change of medication utilization over time. Analyses were performed using SAS (Base 9.4, Cary, NC, USA) and R (version 3.5.2, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), with two-sided p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Incidence of MACE and CTRCD were evaluated by calculating the total number of affected patients in each group (absolute terms) as well as a percentage of the entire group (relative terms).

Results

Characteristics of study population

A total of 124 STAR Program participants were enrolled in this study including 45 patients with history of lymphoma, 41 patients with history of leukemia, and 38 patients with prior history of other malignancies including rhabdomyosarcoma, neuroblastoma, and osteosarcoma. The median (IQR) follow up time after enrollment in the STAR program was 8 [5–10] years. Baseline characteristics of the study cohort are presented in Table 1. 31.5% of patients (39/124) had received treatment with anthracyclines, 17.7% of patients (22/124) had received treatment with XRT, and 50.8% of patients (63/124) had received treatment with anthracyclines + XRT. The mean (SD) age of the cohort at time of original diagnosis was 11.3 (5.5) years. The median (IQR) time from completion of initial pediatric cancer treatment to enrollment in the STAR Program was 17.6 (12.4–22.9) years. Patients in this cohort were 52.4% (65/124) female and 17.7% (22/124) non-White. At time of enrollment, 13.0% of patients (16/123) were receiving treatment with guideline directed medical therapy (GDMT) including beta blocker, MRA, ACEi, ARB, and/or ARNI therapies and 8.6% of patients (10/123) were receiving treatment with statins (Table 2). Incidence of MACE among STAR program participants

Over the course of 10 years of follow-up, we observed 22 occurrences of MACE (Supplemental Table 1, Fig. 1A). The most common MACE observed were acute heart failure admission, non-fatal stroke, and coronary revascularization among others (Supplemental Table 1). Incidence of MACE at 1 year of follow-up was 0%, 17.4%, and 6.3% respectively for patients treated with anthracycline, XRT, or anthracycline + XRT (Fig. 1A). The median (IQR) time from completion of pediatric cancer treatment to development of MACE (as observed while being followed in the STAR program) was 30 (24–33) years (Supplemental Table 2). Patients who received XRT alone had the highest cumulative incidence of MACE by the end of the study period, with 36.4% of patients developing MACE by 7–10 years of follow-up in the STAR program (Fig. 1A).

Incidence and trajectory of CTRCD in STAR program participants

Echocardiographic measurements of STAR Program patients at baseline and throughout the course of 7–10 years of follow-up are shown in Supplemental Table 3. At baseline, there were no statistically significant differences in LVEF or GLS between patients who received anthracyclines alone, XRT alone, or anthracyclines + XRT. Over the course of follow-up, 10.5% (13/124) of patients developed CTRCD by LVEF criteria while 36.3% (45/124) of patients developed CTRCD by GLS criteria per ESC guidelines (Fig. 2A, B). The majority of observed CTRCD events occurred during the first year of follow-up in the STAR program. The incidence of CTRCD by LVEF criteria at one year was 7.7%, 4.5%, and 6.3% for the anthracycline, XRT, and anthracycline + XRT cohorts respectively. The incidence of CTRCD by GLS criteria at one year was 10.3%, 27.3%, and 17.5% for the anthracycline, XRT, and anthracycline + XRT cohorts respectively (Fig. 2A, B). CTRCD events were observed as late as 7–10 years following enrollment in the STAR program. The median (IQR) time from completion of pediatric cancer treatment to development of CTRCD was 20 [16–30] years (Supplemental Table 4). Patients who were treated with both anthracyclines + XRT had the highest cumulative incidence of CTRCD by LVEF criteria at the end of the study period (Fig. 2A). Patients who were treated with XRT alone had the highest cumulative incidence of CTRCD by GLS criteria at the end of the study period (Fig. 2B). Over the course of follow up, patients who developed CTRCD by LVEF criteria and received dedicated cardiovascular management in the STAR program on average saw improvement in LVEF by 3 years post-diagnosis (Fig. 3A, B). By the conclusion of 7–10 years of treatment and follow-up, the majority of patients in all groups had an increase in LVEF from the time of CTRCD diagnosis (Fig. 3A, B). Similarly, patients who developed CTRCD by GLS criteria on average had subsequent improvement in GLS by the end of 7–10 years of follow up (Fig. 3C, D).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first longitudinal evaluation with up to 10 years of follow up of CTRCD using the 2022 ESC definitions in adult survivors of childhood cancers. Prior guidelines classifying cancer therapy related cardiotoxicities mainly emphasized decreased LVEF whereas the more recent ESC guidelines incorporate additional parameters including GLS which allows for more comprehensive capture of early cardiac dysfunction [8]. In this longitudinal cohort study, we followed 124 adult survivors of childhood cancer in the dedicated STAR Program over 10 years of follow up. We observed that MACE occurred at a significant rate in this population, with the highest cumulative incidence of MACE observed in patients who were treated with XRT alone with coronary revascularization being the most common adverse event. These findings are not entirely unexpected, given the well-studied cardiovascular adverse sequelae of anthracyclines and XRT [25–27]. It is somewhat surprising that the XRT alone population had a higher cumulative incidence of MACE compared to the anthracycline + XRT population, though this may be a product of our relatively small sample size, particularly for the XRT alone cohort. Notably, patients who received XRT alone were significantly older than patients in the other groups and had a significantly longer time lag between initial pediatric cancer treatment and enrollment in the STAR program. As such, it is possible that the higher incidence of MACE in the XRT populations may have been due to their larger window to develop adverse effects post-treatment. It is also possible that the dose dependent effects of XRT were more impactful than the dose dependent effects of anthracyclines, though this was not specifically addressed in this study. Furthermore, in this study, the majority of patients had hematologic malignancies, while 30% had solid tumors which may have affected the overall incidence of MACE in the different treatment groups. Further, larger scale studies are needed to better assess longitudinal development of MACE in these populations. It is notable that our study cohort included particularly young patients (mean age 20–40) who developed MACE at a far younger age than would be expected in the general population, indicating that these events are likely downstream sequelae of the effects that cancer directed therapies have on the body rather than age-related cardiovascular disease [28, 29]. Interestingly, we found that in some cases, de novo incidents of MACE occurred even as far out as 10 years post-enrollment. This finding reinforces the argument that patients in cancer survivorship clinics may benefit from extended surveillance for cardiovascular adverse events.

Importantly our study includes comprehensive echocardiographic evaluation over a longitudinal follow-up period for over a decade. We found that CTRCD (both by LVEF and by GLS criteria) occurred at a significant rate across all treatment groups, with the majority of events occurring within the first year of follow-up (Fig. 2A, B). Previous studies have observed abnormal GLS in pediatric cancer survivors treated with anthracyclines and XRT; however, this has not been evaluated specifically in the context of newer definitions of CTRCD [30]. We demonstrated that patients who develop CTRCD and are managed by a dedicated, multidisciplinary survivorship program which included practitioners prescribing cardiovascular medications in response to observed cardiotoxicities showed improvement in both LVEF and GLS over the course of follow-up from the time of CTRCD development. Importantly, the percentage of STAR Program patients on cardiovascular medical therapy increased in parallel with CTRCD detection over the follow-up period (Table 2), which likely contributed to the observed improvements in LV function. It should be noted that the decision to initiate heart failure therapy in STAR program patients was left to the individual judgement of practitioners and was largely done on the basis of decreased LVEF. Patients who exhibited decreased GLS alone did not receive specific heart failure therapies as this was not the standard of care during the study period. Of note, GLS changes were persistent longer than LVEF changes, which aligns with the higher sensitivity of GLS to detect LV dysfunction vs. LVEF alone and may further support a role for GLS as a marker of subclinical dysfunction.

Notably, this study is limited by lack of a control arm and relatively small sample size, and we are therefore unable to draw statistically significant conclusions from these findings regarding therapy initiation. However, these observations are nonetheless an encouraging trend for patients followed in the STAR Program and lend support to the notion that pediatric cancer survivors may substantially benefit from longitudinal follow-up in a survivorship clinic to facilitate early detection of CTRCD and initiation of appropriate medical therapy.

Conclusions

In this longitudinal cohort study of adult survivors of childhood cancer followed in a dedicated survivorship clinic including comprehensive serial echocardiography, both MACE and CTRCD occurred at a significant rate, including as far out as 10 years post-enrollment. These findings highlight the importance of extended surveillance for cardiovascular events, including with echocardiography, in this patient population. Notably, patients who developed CTRCD and were managed in the STAR Program (which included practitioners who prescribed cardiovascular medications) showed improvement in both LVEF and GLS by the end of the follow-up period. These findings suggest benefit from participation in a comprehensive, multidisciplinary and dedicated management program and highlight the important role that a longitudinal survivorship clinic can play in the care of adult survivors of childhood cancer.
