Anticancer proteasome inhibitors are detrimental to the growth of Toxoplasma gondii in vitro
Authors: Meir Zhang, Pascale S Guiton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveToxoplasma gondii is a globally widespread intracellular protozoan that infects an estimated 25% of the world’s human population. There is currently no cure for chronic toxoplasmosis, and treatments for acute infection often cause harmful side effects. Here, we used well-established growth assays to evaluate the anti-toxoplasmic effects of three FDA-approved anticancer proteasome inhibitors, bortezomib, carfilzomib, and ixazomib, on Toxoplasma growth in vitro.ResultsWe found that treatment of Toxoplasma with bortezomib, carfilzomib, and ixazomib inhibits parasite growth with IC50 values of 204.9 nM, 116.5 nM, and 7.05 µM, respectively. Additionally, bortezomib and ixazomib impair parasite replication 24 h post-infection and cause asynchronous cell division and the formation of aberrant parasites. Our study provides evidence in favor of further investigation of these anticancer drugs for in vivo efficacy and their repurposing for the treatment of toxoplasmosis.Keywords: Toxoplasma gondii, Anticancer drugs, Proteasome inhibitors

Introduction

Toxoplasma gondii is an obligate intracellular protozoan of the phylum Apicomplexa responsible for toxoplasmosis in humans and other warm-blooded animals. Toxoplasmosis is a globally prevalent foodborne parasitic disease with an estimated 25% of the human population harboring chronic infection [1]. It is transmitted through the ingestion of contaminated food or water as well as by consuming raw or undercooked meat from chronically infected animals. While infection in immunocompetent individuals is usually asymptomatic, Toxoplasma can cause severe, and sometimes fatal, disease in immunocompromised hosts and in neonates with congenital infection [2–4]. No cure exists for chronic toxoplasmosis, as parasites within tissue cysts are impervious to the few recommended treatment drugs such as pyrimethamine and sulfadiazine [5]. Notably, these drugs are linked to adverse events and reactions when used in the treatment of acute toxoplasmosis [6, 7]. Thus, there is a dire need for new therapies with minimal to no toxicity in the fight against this disease.

The proteasome is a large, multi-subunit protease complex responsible for the turnover of short-lived, unwanted, misfolded, or damaged proteins [8]. It plays a critical role in maintaining cellular protein homeostasis and is essential for regulating many cellular processes, including the cell cycle. The proteasome is a highly attractive therapeutic target against parasitic protozoans [9]. For instance, proteasome inhibitors show strong efficacy across multiple life-cycle stages of kinetoplastids [10] and throughout all growth stages of Plasmodium falciparum [9]. They also act synergistically with artemisinin-based antimalarials [11–14]. Proteasome inhibitors have also been found to reduce the growth and replication of Toxoplasma. Specifically, pretreatment of extracellular Toxoplasma with proteasome inhibitors, such as lactacystin, gliotoxin, and the FDA-approved anticancer drug bortezomib (Velcade® [15]), impairs Toxoplasma growth and replication in vitro [16–19]. These findings suggest that the proteasome is a potential drug target in the treatment of toxoplasmosis. Similar to bortezomib, carfilzomib (Kyprolis®) [20]), and ixazomib (Ninlaro® [21]) are the two other proteasome inhibitors approved by the FDA for the treatment of relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma in adults. Carfilzomib has been shown to affect the asexual blood stage of P. falciparum [22] and synergize with artemisinin activity [11]. As for ixazomib, it reduces the growth of Babesia species [23]. It is also effective against Tritrichomonas foetus trophozoites in vitro and in a murine model of trichomonad infection [24, 25].

Here, we used well-established growth assays to evaluate the anti-toxoplasmic effects of these anticancer drugs. We found that the inhibitory doses required for 50% growth inhibition (IC50) for bortezomib, carfilzomib, and ixazomib were 204.9 nM, 116.5 nM, and 7.05 µM, respectively. Unlike with carfilzomib, the IC50 of bortezomib and ixazomib were higher than their cytotoxicity doses (CC50) against human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs), which were 1.52 µM and 28.2 µM, respectively. We also found that parasite replication is hindered with asynchronous cell divisions and vacuoles containing aberrant parasites. Together, our findings underscore the importance of further study of these drugs and proteasome inhibitors for the treatment of toxoplasmosis as well as research aimed at a better understanding of the Toxoplasma proteasome.

Materials and methods

Parasite and host cells

Human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs) and RH 2F, a Type I strain expressing the Escherichia coli LacZ gene from the GRA1 promoter [26], were kind gifts from John Boothroyd at Stanford University and David Sibley at Washington University in St. Louis, respectively. Parasites were maintained in HFFs in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 0.25µg/ml fungizone, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (cDMEM) at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Compounds

Bortezomib, carfilzomib, and ixazomib were purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) at 10 mM in DMSO.

Parasite growth inhibition assay

Confluent HFFs in 96-well plate format were infected with 103 freshly released parasites in the presence of drugs at various concentrations following a 1:3 serial dilution from 30 μm (30 μm, 10 μm, 3.33 μm, 1.11 μm, 0.37 μm, 0.123 μm, 0.0411, 0.0137 μm, 0.00456 μm) with the final well containing only 0.6% DMSO (vehicle control). The plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 72 h. Parasite viability was assessed by measuring beta-galactosidase activity, as previously described [27]. All assays were performed in triplicate, and each biological experiment was repeated three times.

Cytotoxicity assay against HFFs

In parallel to the parasite growth assay, confluent HFFs in 96-well plates were treated with varying drug concentrations in a 1:3 serial dilution from 30 µM for bortezomib and carfilzomib and in a 1:2 serial dilution from 50 µM for ixazomib and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 72 h. The medium was then removed, and the monolayers were washed once with 200 µL of cDMEM. Each well was then filled with 100 µL of resazurin solution (Biotium, Fremont, CA) diluted 1:10 in cDMEM, and plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cell viability was assessed colorimetrically by subtracting the background absorbance at 600 nm from the resorufin absorbance at 570 nm.

Replication assay

Confluent HFFs on glass coverslips were infected with 105 freshly released parasites in cDMEM for 24 h with drugs or DMSO at the specified concentrations. The parasites were stained with mouse α-SAG1 (DG52) antibody obtained from the Boothroyd lab. The number of parasites per vacuole was determined by immunofluorescence microscopy, as previously described [28].

Immunostaining fluorescence assay

Infected monolayers were washed with PBS and fixed with 100% ice cold methanol for 3 min at room temperature (RT). They were incubated for 1 h in 3.5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA; Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) in PBS at RT. Primary antibodies (DG52) were diluted 1:2000 in 3.5% BSA/PBS then added to the monolayers and incubated for 1 h at RT or overnight at 4 °C. Unbound antibodies were removed with three 5-minute washes in PBS. The cells were then stained with secondary antibodies in 3.5% BSA/PBS (Invitrogen) at 1:5000 for 45 min at RT. After washing as described above, the coverslips were mounted with VECTASHIELD Mounting Medium containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA). Immunofluorescence images were obtained with a 100X oil immersion objective and parasites per vacuole were enumerated with a 60X oil immersion objective of an All-in-One Fluorescence Microscope BZ-X710 (Keyence Corporation of America, Itasca, IL). Images were processed using ImageJ version 1.53.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.1. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference between groups. IC50 and CC50 values were determined by plotting normalized, log-transformed data (x-axis) and fitting a sigmoidal dose-response curve with a variable slope using nonlinear regression.

Results and discussion

To determine the effects of bortezomib, carfilzomib, and ixazomib on the host cells, we treated confluent HFFs with varying concentrations of drugs and measured cell viability 72 h post-treatment using the resazurin assay. We determined the cytotoxicity dose (CC50) against HFFs to be 1.5 µΜ for bortezomib, 179.7 nM for carfilzomib, and 28.2 µΜ for ixazomib (Fig. 1A). In parallel, we infected HFFs with Toxoplasma tachyzoites and determined the IC50 of these drugs at 72 h by measuring the activity of beta-galactosidase. The dose response curves show that the IC50 for bortezomib is 204.9 nM, as previously reported [29], carfilzomib 116.5 nM, and ixazomib 7.05 µM. (Fig. 1B). The difference in bortezomib’s CC50 from previous studies may be due to the type of cells used and/or confluency levels of the monolayers. Together, our data suggest that bortezomib and ixazomib have a higher therapeutic potential against Toxoplasma than carfilzomib.

To evaluate the impact of bortezomib and ixazomib on parasite replication, we treated infected monolayers with 0.1 µM bortezomib and 2 µM ixazomib for 24 h. Using immunofluorescence, we observed vacuoles containing aberrant, misshapen parasites and more asynchronous division with odd numbers of parasites (3, 5, and 6) within vacuoles in the presence of the drugs (Fig. 2A and B). There was a significant difference in the distribution of parasites within vacuoles in the presence of drugs relative to vehicle control (Fig. 2B, p < 0.0001 by Chi-squared test). Specifically, while the vehicle control had approximately 66% of vacuoles with 8 or more parasites, bortezomib-treated samples had none, and ixazomib-treated samples had about 1%. Treatment with bortezomib resulted in more vacuoles with 1 or 2 parasites (93%) and ixazomib with 2 or 4 parasites (81%). Together, these data suggest that bortezomib and ixazomib inhibit Toxoplasma growth by impeding parasite replication, even at concentrations lower than their IC50. Our findings align with earlier studies that show that pretreatment of free tachyzoites with proteasome inhibitors (10 µM lactacystin or 5 µM gliotoxin) did not affect parasite entry or parasitophorous vacuole formation but significantly impaired parasite growth, daughter cell budding, and DNA replication [16, 17].

Proteasomes are present in all eukaryotes, archaea, and some bacteria. The eukaryotic proteasome is composed of a 20S catalytic core particle and one or two 19S regulatory particles, which together form the 26–30S proteasomes [30, 31]. The 20S core particle is a barrel-shaped complex made up of two heptameric β rings sandwiched between two heptameric α rings (α7β7β7α7). The β1, β2, and β5 subunits provide the proteolytic activity of the proteasome, specifically caspase-, trypsin-, and chymotrypsin-like activity, respectively [32]. Bortezomib and ixazomib are reversible drugs that target the chymotrypsin-like activity of the β5 subunit [33, 34].

Toxoplasma encodes orthologs for all 7 α and 7 β subunits as well as most components of the 19S proteasome, PA28, and PA200 [35]. A genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen revealed that genes encoding these putative proteasome subunits are essential for parasite fitness in vitro [36]. Paugam A. et al. conducted biochemical assays using parasite lysates and showed that the Toxoplasma proteasome exhibits only chymotrypsin-like activity, with kinetics comparable to those observed in other eukaryotes. However, the overall protease activity of the Toxoplasma proteasome more closely resembles that of prokaryotic proteasomes than that of mammals [37]. Furthermore, immunoblot analysis of Toxoplasma lysates using polyclonal antibodies raised against the rat proteasome revealed cross-reactivity, whereas antibodies against the human proteasome did not. These findings suggest potential structural differences between the host and parasite proteasomes. Unlike mammalian proteasomes that localize to the cytoplasm and nucleus, Toxoplasma proteasomes are only cytoplasmic [37]. These distinctions can be exploited to develop new drugs that selectively target Toxoplasma proteasomes over the host cell proteasomes. Such detailed comparative structural and enzymatic activity studies of mammalian proteasomes and those of parasites such as Trypanosoma and Plasmodium species have enabled the development of parasite-specific proteasome inhibitors against these pathogens [12, 38–42].

In conclusion, our study provides further evidence that the proteasome is a viable target for chemotherapy against Toxoplasma. While our in vitro data suggest that bortezomib and ixazomib are promising drugs, further studies in animal models of toxoplasmosis are needed. It will also be valuable to assess whether these drugs exhibit synergistic effects when combined with current treatments such as pyrimethamine and sulfadiazine.

Limitations

In this study, we did not evaluate the efficacy of these drugs in in vivo models of Toxoplasma infections. We also did not confirm whether carfilzomib, bortezomib, and ixazomib target Toxoplasma proteasomes.
