Prevalence, Clinical Associations, and Outcomes of Cardiorenal Anemia Syndrome Among Patients With Heart Failure Attending a Tertiary Hospital in Dodoma, Tanzania: A Prospective Observational Cohort Study
Authors: Gidion Edwin, Baraka Alphonce, Alfred Meremo, John Meda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCardiorenal anemia syndrome (CRAS) is a common complication in heart failure (HF), often leading to unfavorable outcomes. Despite clinical significance, the published data are scarce. This study assessed prevalence, clinical associations and outcomes of CRAS among patients with HF attending the Benjamin Mkapa Hospital in Dodoma.MethodsA prospective observational cohort study was carried out at cardiology clinic between August 2023 and April 2024. Adult patients aged ≥ 18 years confirmed with chronic heart failure were recruited by convenience (non‐probability) sampling. Age, sex, cigarettes smoking, dyslipidemia, obesity, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification, hypertension, diabetes mellitus and iron deficiency were included as predictor variables. The primary outcome was CRAS and termed as exposed. All‐cause mortality, HF rehospitalisation, chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression and progressive anemia were secondary outcomes. Data were summarized by descriptive and inferential methods. Logistic regression was used to establish the clinical associations whereas, Cox proportional hazards regression was used to establish association of CRAS and secondary outcomes. A two‐tailed p‐value ≤ 0.05 noted as statistical significance.ResultsThis study involved 268 patients with a median age of 56.5 (IQR 18–92) years, 57.5% (154/268) were females. Prevalence of CRAS was 41.8% (112/268). Obesity (AOR 1.77; 95% CI, 1.02–3.06; p = 0.042), iron deficiency (AOR 2.36; 95% CI, 1.36–4.13; p = 0.002), NYHA category (class III/IV) (AOR 4.11; 95% CI, 2.35–7.16; p = 0.001) and diabetes mellitus (AOR 1.59; 95% CI, 1.03–2.70; p = 0.047) were clinically associated with unfavorable outcomes of CRAS. All‐cause mortality was high in CRAS (HR 1.96; 95% CI, 1.30–3.01; p = 0.001). Furthermore, CKD progression and progressive anemia were comparably similar to both cohorts (56.3% vs. 48.1%, p = 0.116) and (53.6% vs. 47.4%, p = 0.193), respectively.ConclusionThe study showed that CRAS is highly prevalent and is associated with increased risk for all‐cause mortality. Obesity, iron deficiency, advanced NYHA functional stages and diabetes mellitus warrant special attention. This study was a single‐centered therefore, we propose a large multicentre study for generalizability.Keywords: cardiorenal anemia syndrome, clinical associations, dodoma, heart failure, prospective observational cohort study

Introduction

The coexistence of heart failure, CKD with an estimated glomerular filtration rate of ≤ 60 mil/min/1.73 m2 and anemia with a hemoglobin level of ≤ 12 g/dl for females or ≤ 13 g/dl for males is collectively termed cardiorenal anemia syndrome [1, 2].

CRS is particularly significant in patients with advanced heart failure due to left ventricular and renal dysfunctions [3, 4, 5, 6]. However, the coexistence of CRS and anemia has been less investigated despite its clinical significance [7, 8]. Additionally, this existing triad is challenging in terms of management, with increased costs and acute care utilization, resulting in unfavorable outcomes [9, 10].

Globally, the prevalence of CRAS varies widely in previous studies, ranging from 4.0% to 63% due to disparities in the clinical profiles of study patients, geographical settings and epidemiological trends of cardiovascular diseases [3, 11, 12, 13]. However, these existing studies were retrospective and longitudinal studies, and some of the associated methodological procedures are not fully understood [1, 14, 15].

In Africa, there are limited published data regarding CRAS with little attention among patients with HF, despite the significant regional trends of the HF population with worse outcomes [16, 17, 18]. In Tanzania, CRAS was reported in 44.4% of the HFrEF cohort, and most patients had unfavorable outcomes; 53.5% and 73.5% had HF rehospitalization and mortality, respectively [9]. Furthermore, the limited data in our local settings hinder comprehensive strategies to reduce the burden of the problem, especially in the era of noncommunicable diseases exemplified by heart failure.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted in the Department of Cardiology at Benjamin Mkapa Hospital in Dodoma, Tanzania. The detailed methods for this study have been published and readily available [2]. This study followed STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational studies [19].

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

In this study, a total of 268 participants after comprehensive screening process to met inclusion criteria were recruited for a period of 3 months from August 2023 to November 2023 (recruitment); thereafter, were followed for 6 months (follow‐up period) up to April, 2024 [20, 21]. We included eligible outpatients with chronic HF and then, categorization of HF phenotypes was performed by using a two‐dimensional transthoracic echocardiogram with either an echocardiographic report within 6 months or if so, performed during index recruitment. In addition, all patients had established diagnosis of CKD by the CKD‐EPI formula while considering the African–American correction factor. All patients with known haemoglobinopathies, CKD on dialysis or on iron supplementation or previous history of blood transfusion for the past 6 months, solid/hematological malignancies, pregnant women, obvious gastrointestinal bleeding, and post pacemakers or implantable cardiac devices were excluded.

Variables and Data/Source Measurement

Patient Evaluation

Baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, anthropometric data and medical history were collected using a well‐structured questionnaire. Additionally, laboratory investigations and two‐dimensional transthoracic echocardiograms were also performed. Subjective functional status assessment was determined by using the New York Heart Association functional criteria [9, 22, 23]. At baseline, sociodemographic characteristics, medical history and clinical associations data were recorded. Patients with CRAS were termed “exposed and labeled as CRAS + ,” while those without CRAS were termed “nonexposed and labeled as CRAS‐.” Each patient was followed up on a monthly basis for 6 months by using phone calls or during routine clinic visits, whichever necessary until occurrence of secondary outcomes which were all‐cause mortality and HF rehospitalization. Furthermore, CKD progression and progressive severe anemia were determined only, at the end of the study. A patient was considered lost to follow‐up if they could not be reached after three attempts on three different days during the follow‐up week or if they missed their routine clinic appointments as scheduled.

Measurements of Clinical Variables

Waist–hip ratio (WHR): was calculated from waist circumference and hip circumference. A cut‐off value of ≥ 0.9 for male or ≥ 0.8 for female indicates obesity. Compared with body mass index (BMI), the sensitivity and specificity of the waist–hip ratio are ≥ 85% in the prediction of obesity [24, 25].

Blood pressure (BP) was measured via the conventional method via a calibrated automated BP machine in accordance with the 2018 AHA/ACC hypertension guidelines for standard measurement of BP. Hypertension was defined as BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg [9, 21, 26].

Laboratory and Imaging Investigations

The comprehensive elaboration on the sample collection, processing and analysis was detailed from a published study protocol [27].

Blood sample collection: Laboratory standard operating procedures accredited and supervised by Benjamin Mkapa Hospital were adhered.

Lipid profile: Dyslipidemia was defined as total serum cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL, LDL‐cholesterol ≥ 130 mg/dL, serum triglyceride ≥ 150 mg/dL, or HDL‐cholesterol < 40 mg/dL for women and < 50 mg/dL for men [11, 21, 28].

Blood glucose measurements: Diabetes mellitus was defined as glycated hemoglobin (HBA1C) ≥ 6.5%, or fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7 mmol/l [29, 30].

Iron studies: Iron deficiency was defined as a serum ferritin concentration of < 100 µg/l or a serum ferritin concentration ranging from 100 to 300 µg/l and transferrin saturation (TSAT) of < 20% [23, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35].

Red blood cell indices: anemia was defined as a serum hemoglobin level of ≤ 12 g/dl for women or ≤ 13 g/dl for men [9, 21, 23].

Blood creatinine: Chronic kidney disease was defined basing on estimation of the glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Glomerular filtration rate was calculated by using the CKD‐EPI formula by adjusting the African–American correction factor, CKD was defined as eGFR < 60 mil/min/1.73 m2 [30, 36, 37].

Two‐Dimensional Transthoracic Echocardiography (2D‐TTE)

A two dimensional Transthoracic echocardiography (model Vivid TM T9 manufactured by GE Healthcare, USA 2018) was used to determine an ejection fraction, systolic and diastolic dysfunctions in order to categorize participants into two HF phenotypes, HFrEF (EF < 50%) or HFpEF (EF ≥ 50%).

Definition and Measurement of Outcomes

Primary outcome: CRAS, patients with CRAS were labeled as CRAS+ and termed as “exposed” whereas, those without CRAS were labeled as CRAS‐ and termed as “nonexposed”.

Secondary outcomes: The secondary outcomes were as follows:

1. All‐cause mortality: any death that occurred during follow‐up irrespective of primary cause [1, 9, 38]

All‐cause mortality: any death that occurred during follow‐up irrespective of primary cause [1, 9, 38]

2. Heart failure rehospitalization was defined as hospital admission more than once after recruitment, of which the cause was primarily heart failure [9, 13, 39].

3. Chronic kidney disease progression was defined as increase in serum creatinine level by ≥ 25% (≥ 0.3 mg/dl) or lowering of eGFR ≥ 4 mil/min/1.73 m2 per 6 months [40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45].

4. Progressive anemia synonymous to worsening anemia; was defined as decrease in hemoglobin level by ≥ 1 g/dl from baseline, or a need of blood transfusion, erythropoietin use or both [35, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50].

Sample Size and Sampling Procedure

Sample size estimation was done using Woodward's formula adopted for proportionate cohort studies [20]. A total of 268 patients were recruited to meet the study objectives. Convenience sampling method (nonprobability) was employed to recruit 268 adult patients who met the inclusion criteria. Nevertheless, we included 268 out of 300 planned participants following comprehensive screening which was done during recruitment (at baseline) to met the inclusion criteria. However, the small sample size might affect the validity and generalizabity of the study findings.

Data Analysis

Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) Windows version 26 program (IBM SPSS, Chicago IL) was used for data management and analysis. Descriptive analyses were performed, for continuous variables were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) while categorical variables, were reported as numbers and proportions. Prevalence of CRAS as a measure of disease frequency was reported by using numbers and proportions. Sociodemographic variables, anthropometrics and clinical associations of CRAS at baseline were analyzed and compared by using Pearson Chi squared (χ2) test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables [1, 9, 51]. Clinical associations, including age category, sex category, cigarette smoking, alcohol intake, hypertension, obesity, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, NYHA category, HF phenotypes and iron deficiency, were determined by binary logistic regression and were reported using Odds ratio (OR) and 95% Confidence interval (CI). Thereafter, all the variables that were fitted with a p‐value of < 0.2, were then subjected to multivariate logistic regression by using enter method through backward LR stepwise method. Log‐likelihood tests were performed during logistic regression which were included Cox & Snell's variances (R2); Hosmer and Lemeshow tests. Cox proportional hazard regression analysis using the Hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CI was performed to establish an association between CRAS and the incidence of all‐cause mortality and HF rehospitalization. Then, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis curves were used to present the data. Additionally, progressive anemia and CKD progression, were summarized by descriptive method using numbers and proportions at the end of study. A two‐tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [9, 12, 31].

Clinical Associations of Cardiorenal Anemia Syndrome

The model was found to fit the data adequately (Hosmer and Lemeshow's X2 = 7.302, p = 0.504). Seven predictors were included in the model, using the Enter method through backward stepwise. Four of these successfully clinically associated with CRAS. Assumptions for linearity and multi‐collinearity were satisfied with a 2‐sided p‐value of less than 0.05 as a significance level. Discussion

This prospective observational cohort study evaluated the prevalence, clinical associations, and outcomes of cardiorenal anemia syndrome among patients with heart failure phenotypes attending cardiology clinic at Benjamin Mkapa Hospital in Dodoma, Tanzania. We observed that the prevalence of cardiorenal anemia syndrome across heart failure phenotypes was 41.8%; and 51.4% of among patients with HFrEF.

A previous study in Tanzania by Pallangyo et al. [9] that included young, inpatients with HFrEF reported a 44.4% prevalence, congruent to our study. This could be due to similarities in the clinical profiles and demographics of the participants. Furthermore, other previous studies that assessed CRAS among hospitalized patients with HFrEF reported a prevalence ranging between 4% and 63% [3, 13, 53]. However, these studies included frail patients with multiple comorbidities and were retrospectively enrolled [51, 54, 55].

A previous study from Japan revealed that CRAS was present in 53.7% of cases, which was higher than that reported in our index study. The reasons were due to variations in the clinical profiles and sociodemographics of the involved patients including advanced age of > 65 years, and frailty [53]. A previous study conducted in the Netherlands, France and Spain reported a CRAS incidence of 4.0%, which was very small compared with our cohort, this was due to the exclusion of iron deficiency in the definition of CRAS incidence [56]. Additionally, this was an observational study with high risk of confounding bias. Nevertheless, our cohort had small sample size as compared to the previous studies with close related findings by Pallangyo et al; this hinders the generalizability of the study findings, however, it's valid since both settings were termed as referring and consultant hospitals attending patients with similar clinical profile.

We found that diabetes mellitus was significantly associated with CRAS, almost doubling the likelihood of occurrence. Similar findings were reported in previous studies from Tanzania, the Middle East and Japan [9, 13, 57]. The hyperglycaemic state, chronic inflammation, insulin resistance and toxic effects of diabetes mellitus on heart and kidney functions (ischemic heart disease and diabetic nephrosclerosis) accelerate cardiac and renal dysfunction [13, 58, 59].

Furthermore, iron deficiency emerged as an important clinical associate, doubling the likelihood of CRAS in our cohort. Similarly, studies previously conducted in Italy, Spain and Japan demonstrated that iron deficiency increased the likelihood of anemia, renal dysfunction and heart failure, after which CRAS ensued [46, 51, 52]. This finding highlights the role of iron in metabolic and physiological processes such as the production of myoglobin, oxidative and respiratory chain protein enzymes, and the strength of cardiac myocytes, which are crucial for cardiac and renal functions [10, 31, 60]. Therefore, iron deficiency with or without anemia could influence the dysfunction of oxidative and respiratory proteins, hence increasing the likelihood of CRAS [7, 56, 61].

We observed that obesity is a significant clinical associate that doubled the risk of CRAS occurrence. Our cohort showcased the similar findings to other previous studies conducted in Italy and middle East [1, 13]. However, the included patients from existing studies were patents with HFpEF, older more than 65 years, male predominance and most of them had diabetes and hypertension which are associated with obesity. Our findings were reflecting with what is known from regional trends of cardiovascular diseases in Sub‐Saharan African countries like Tanzania [9, 18, 30].

We observed that advanced NYHA category (class III/IV) clinically associated with the likelihood of CRAS. Our study showcased 4 times increased likelihood among patients who were in class III and IV to develop CRAS than those in class I and II. These findings were consistent to a previous study conducted in Spain and Italy. However, the majority were elder patients so the likelihood of frailty was high and sometimes misclassified as in advanced NYHA category [1, 12]. In Tanzania, a previous study reported consistent finding to ours [9]. However, the existing study recruited the hospitalized patients with HFrEF phenotype, which could influence the deterioration of HF symptoms and then, categorization to advanced NHYA classes [9].

The present study also revealed an increased risk for all‐cause mortality among patients with CRAS. Our cohort revealed almost doubling the risk for all‐cause mortality which were similar to other previous studies [1, 55]. Studies from Italy, Spain, the USA and the Middle East revealed a higher risk for mortality among patients with CRAS as compared to those without CRAS [1, 9, 13, 15, 51]. However, existing studies included relatively older hospitalized patients with HFrEF phenotype who were more likely to be frail and at risk of hospital‐acquired infections, hypotension and electrolyte derangements [1, 13]. A previous study in Tanzania demonstrated that all‐cause mortality was significantly high among patients with CRAS compared to those without CRAS. However, this study included the admitted patients with HFrEF, of which the risks of infections and worsening HF syndromes were likely [9].

Despite the significance on all‐cause mortality but the risks for HF rehospitalization were similarly in either CRAS groups; but, is statistically significant in crude comparison, which represents less than a 10% increase in risk and is therefore a relatively small effect size. These findings could be due to similar clinical profile of the study participants and censoring of the patients from the study. A previous study conducted in European countries reported findings consistent with ours, despite its retrospective design. In that particular study, censoring of the patients from the study, strengths of health care and insurance systems and recruitment of elderly patients could be influential factor for all‐cause mortality [56]. Nevertheless, in our study, the possible primary mechanisms which explain the higher mortality among patients with CRAS were cardiovascular versus non‐cardiovascular, renal related and infections, which could be described by worsening cardiac function, CKD progression, anemia‐related complications, or a combination of these mechanisms.

Our findings revealed that 50% of patients with CRAS+ experienced CKD progression and that 10% needed dialysis. Additionally, we found that 50% patients displayed progressive severe anemia among patients with CRAS + . Moreover, 10% needed both blood transfusions and erythropoietin injections. The index study was consistent with a previous study conducted in Tanzania, which showcased about 10.7% had severe anemia and worsening renal functions [9]. However, the existing study included the admitted patients which were more likely to develop worsening renal functions and sometimes a need of blood transfusion whereas, our study included only outpatients with chronic HF across phenotypes.

Limitations of the Study

Our study has several limitations. First, the single‐centered study with a moderate sample size may hinder the validity and generalizability of our findings despite being conducted in a tertiary hospital receiving patients from almost all over the country. Second, assessing the incidence of CKD progression and progressive anemia on a monthly basis is difficult, and even the statistical method used was descriptive in nature. Third, the use of convenience (non‐probability) sampling limits generalizability of study findings; since, it relies on the most accessible participants rather than a randomized selection. Therefore, the chance of selection bias is likely, ultimately lowering the study's external validity. Forth, the use of serum creatinine to estimate CKD could bias and underestimate the population of interest, especially those with diabetes mellitus. Therefore, the use of both the urine protein/creatinine ratio and the serum creatinine level could be preferable for increasing diagnostic accuracy. Furthermore, large, international and multicenter incidence studies of CRAS with long‐term follow‐up period should be conducted in the future.

Conclusions

The study showed that CRAS is highly prevalent and is associated with increased risk for all‐cause mortality. Obesity, iron deficiency, advanced NYHA functional stages and diabetes mellitus warrant special attention. This study was a single‐centered therefore, we propose a large multicentre study for generalizability.
