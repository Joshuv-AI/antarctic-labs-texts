Chiropractic students’ perceived preparedness in diagnosing and managing headache disorders at a selected university
Authors: Tamia Abrahams, Ashura Abdul-Rasheed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore and describe master’s chiropractic students’ self-perceived preparedness in diagnosing and managing headache disorders.MethodsA qualitative, exploratory, descriptive design was used. Thirteen master’s chiropractic students participated in semistructured interviews guided by an interview protocol. Interviews were thematically analyzed using Tesch’s 8-step approach.ResultsParticipants reported and expressed limited preparedness and low initial confidence in diagnosing and managing headache disorders. Educational and environmental challenges were identified as key roles in this result whilst clinical exposure positively influenced students’ perceptions of their preparedness.ConclusionThe exploration of students’ self-perceived preparedness in diagnosing and managing headache disorders identified multiple contributing factors. Educational and environmental challenges were associated with feelings of limited preparedness. However, increased clinical exposure enhanced both confidence and perceived competence.

INTRODUCTION

A headache is defined as “pain found in the head above the orbito-meatal line and or nuchal ridge” and have further been defined as a “clinical syndrome” that both widely affects male and females alike.1 Headaches are associated with impaired quality of life, substantial lost productivity and high economic costs in every country surveyed.2 Headaches can be treated both pharmacologically and nonpharmacologically depending on their classification of being either primary or secondary in nature.3 Chiropractic care plays a significant role in the treatment and management of headaches, with 1 in 5 new patients in clinical practice presenting with headache as their chief complaint.4-6 The general chiropractic approach to the treatment of headaches is holistic. This includes identifying triggers and utilizing active and passive treatment and management interventions tailored to the patient.7,8

The term “self-perceived preparedness” refers to how one views themselves in terms of confidence and competency with regard to certain skills.9,10 Self-perceived preparedness is essential as it relates to one’s confidence and the ability to accurately diagnose and manage conditions.9,11 Chiropractic Masters students engage in treatment and management during their clinical practicum with headache patients frequently seeking care.12 Hence, students must be adequately prepared to diagnose and manage headache disorders, making self-perceived preparedness essential for effective clinical practice. Similarly, effective, safe, and appropriate treatment depends on students’ self-perceived preparedness,11,12 underscoring the importance of exploring this topic.

In the South African chiropractic context, literature regarding students’ confidence, awareness, and self-assessment of skills is limited. Assessing students perceived preparedness in headache diagnosis, treatment, and management is therefore important, as it provides insight into how they may practice in the future. Globally, previous research has shown that healthcare graduates often perceive themselves as “unprepared” in certain clinical competencies, highlighting a gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application.11,12 Similarly, studies on the transition to clinical practice have shown that students often lack confidence in their ability to run a chiropractic practice and also perceive themselves as unprepared in certain clinical competencies.13,14 Exploring the qualitative experiences of the master’s students can identify gaps in the curriculum and clinical training, enabling targeted improvements that better prepare students for independent practice. Understanding students’ self-perceived preparedness also supports professional development by highlighting areas for growth and building confidence in clinical decision-making. Insights gained can enhance patient care by ensuring that students deliver safe, effective, and evidence-based treatment. Additionally, such exploration informs faculty and institutional strategies, bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and real-world clinical application, and provides a foundation for future research on barriers and enablers of clinical competence.

METHODS

Design

This study employed a qualitative, exploratory, descriptive design to explore and describe master’s chiropractic students’ self-perceived preparedness in diagnosing and managing headache disorders. The design was appropriate, as semistructured interviews provided in-depth insight into students’ personal perceptions and experiences, enabling the researcher to interpret concepts and identify meaningful patterns in the data.

Setting

Interviews were conducted face-to-face at the University of Technology Chiropractic Day Clinic in a private, prebooked room to ensure confidentiality. A semistructured interview guide was used, and sessions were audio-recorded using an access-controlled audio device. Only the participant and researcher were present during the interviews, and the researcher and study supervisor had exclusive access to the recordings to maintain confidentiality.

Sample

The sample consisted of 13 students enrolled in the master’s Chiropractic program. These students are postgraduate students who have completed the 4-year bachelor’s degree and are now undertaking the master’s component, which includes both clinical practicum in the student training clinics and a research project. All participants were in the clinical practicum phase of their studies, providing patient care under the supervision of registered clinicians to meet the requirements for their chiropractic qualification. Purposeful sampling was utilized in the study.

Sample Recruitment was done via the use of recruitment posters that were posted on closed social media platforms specific to potential participants. Only potential participants that met the inclusion criteria were eligible to participate. Participants that contacted the researcher and met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate in a semistructured interview.

Necessary gatekeeper permissions were attained, and ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Research and Ethics Council at the University of Technology (ethical number 111/23). Participation required informed consent; participants read a letter of information and signed a consent form prior to the interview. Participants were informed that interviews would be audio-recorded, that data would remain confidential, and that they could withdraw at any time. Personal identifiers were omitted, and pseudonyms were assigned to maintain confidentiality.

Data Collection

The interview guide was developed by the researcher with guidance from the supervisor, based on the study’s theoretical framework. This study is underpinned by 2 established theoretical frameworks: Bem’s Self-Perception Theory (1972)15 and the Theory of Cognitive Dissonance.16 These frameworks are widely applied in qualitative research and offer insight into how individuals interpret their behaviors, beliefs, and experiences. Together, they explain how learned behaviors, cognitions, and internal evaluations shape confidence and self-perceived preparedness.17 In this study, the frameworks are used to interpret chiropractic students perceived preparedness in diagnosing and managing headache disorders, particularly how students evaluate their readiness based on clinical experiences and reconcile discrepancies between expected competence and actual confidence.

A pilot study was conducted to ensure clarity, validity, and reliability; no changes were required following the pilot. Data collection included audio-recorded semistructured interviews with 5 key questions with relevant probes. These were conducted until data saturation was achieved. Interviews lasted an average of 24 minutes. Saturation was reached after 12 participants, with an additional interview conducted to confirm, resulting in a total sample of 13.

Data Analysis

Tesch’s 8-step approach to qualitative analysis was employed to identify themes and subthemes.18 The researcher transcribed, reviewed, and coded the interviews, using field notes to verify the data. Repeated readings helped fully understand the information, and similar topics were grouped together. From these groups, key themes were identified to capture the main patterns in the data. An experienced qualitative researcher (the study supervisor) reviewed the transcripts and data, confirming the themes identified by the researcher. Merging themes were verified collaboratively using participants’ verbatim statements, and relevant literature was consulted to validate the findings and support conclusions.

Reflexivity and Researcher Positioning Statement

The researcher conducted this study while enrolled as a Chiropractic master’s student within the same academic and clinical environment as the participants. This insider position provided valuable contextual understanding of the curriculum, clinical expectations, and institutional culture. However, it also carried the potential for bias, including assumptions about shared experiences and interpretations shaped by personal training. To minimize these influences, the researcher employed reflexive journaling, maintained awareness of power dynamics during data collection, and adhered to systematic analytic procedures. Regular consultation with a supervisory team further supported analytical rigor and helped ensure that findings reflected participants’ perspectives rather than the researcher’s own experiences.

Trustworthiness and Research Rigor

Research rigor for qualitative research refers to the trustworthiness of one’s research. Sometimes referred to as scientific merit, it aims to explain the validity and accuracy of qualitative research.19 The criteria utilized to establish trustworthiness consist of 4 principles. These 4 principles are credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability.

Credibility refers to the confidence in the truth of the particular study.20 To solidify credibility, the normative methods of qualitative research need to be utilized. These aspects include prolonged engagement with the data, repeated review of the data and reflective journaling. Credibility was maintained and supported by conducting interviews which lasted approximately 24 minutes on average, from which rich narrative data was generated and recurrent themes abstracted.

Dependability refers to the reliability of the research and the data’s stability over a passage of time as well as the consistency of the data.19,20 The same questions need to be asked to all participants and the data collected and transcribed needs to be looked over to ensure this has occurred. Dependability was maintained as the same set of questions was asked to each study participant and was transcribed accordingly.

Confirmability speaks to the consistency in the answers and outcomes. This consistency has to be found by 2 or more independent people.19,20 This principle validates the information each participant has provided and that they are real, true and not made up by the researcher. This principle ensures that the research is steered away from bias and motivations of the researcher’s perspective.19,20 Confirmability was elicited by asking the research supervisor to scrutinize the data through independent checking.

Transferability speaks to the data that was captured and its ability to be transferred on to other settings or groups. This was maintained by providing a detailed description of the research context, settings, and processes utilized in the study.

Theme 1: Level of Preparedness

The overarching theme of Level of Preparedness, and subthemes encompassing participants’ skills, confidence, and ability to translate theoretical knowledge into practice, emerged strongly from the interviews. Most participants reported feeling underprepared to diagnose and manage headache disorders, particularly in identifying underlying pathology and applying appropriate clinical tests. Two participants noted, “I don’t feel confident enough. I don’t feel like I’m prepared in dealing with these conditions with patients” (P3, P5). Diagnostic uncertainty was further reflected in participants’ reliance on limited assessment strategies, with some prioritizing case history while expressing uncertainty about other specific clinical tests. As stated by participant 10, “Probably the case history is most important when it comes to headache” (P10), while participant 3 acknowledged, “So I’m not really sharp in that area… doing your Brudzinski’s and meningitis examinations helps to distinguish between serious pathology and headache, as well as considering myofascial factors” (P3). Application of knowledge also emerged with a marginal agreement that applying theoretical and practical skills were important. This was reflected on by participant 1 who stated, “With headaches, it would be more theory for me in terms of practical like trying to discern whether it’s a headache or maybe like a trigger point referral from like a trigger point or something like that—so you have to check.” (P1).

Theme 2: Educational and Environmental Challenges

Participants described multiple educational and environmental factors that affected their preparedness. The extensive workload within course modules often made it difficult to fully grasp headache-specific content, particularly when taught alongside broader diagnostic material. Participants reported that the volume and structure of the content limited their ability to engage meaningfully with the topic. As 1 participant noted, “The amount of content was a lot. We weren’t necessarily able to grasp what we were being taught because it was so much over a short period of time, combined with diagnostics” (P4). Another highlighted that headaches were not afforded sufficient focus, stating, “It wasn’t its own module… it’s such a big topic that it requires its own attention” (P5). It also emerged that limited headache-specific practical training within the educational program left participants feeling uncertain when managing headache patients in the clinical setting. This lack of hands-on instruction contributed to feelings of under preparedness in clinically diagnosing and managing headache disorders. Participants described difficulty recalling practical guidance specific to headache assessment, as illustrated by 1 participant who stated, “I don’t even remember being taught much with regards to practical associated with headaches… for actual headaches, We were taught a lot of tests for neck pain, but for actual headaches, I can’t identify anything specifically that they taught us that would say, Oh, this should explain this headache, or…” (P7). Another participant similarly reflected, “I feel like we haven’t been taught much practical-based skills… how would you handle a patient if anything had to happen pertaining to a very bad headache” (P5).

Limited avenues of support from faculty and clinicians emerged as a key subtheme. Participants described a reliance on self-directed learning, which they perceived as insufficient for developing confidence in managing headache disorders. This lack of structured guidance was experienced as discouraging and contributed to ongoing feelings of unpreparedness. One participant explained, “There’s a lot of self-study… most of it was basically given to us and told to go self-study; it wasn’t taught to us in class” (P11). In addition, participants reported inconsistencies in clinical support, noting that the level of assistance received from clinicians varied, as reflected by 1 participant who stated, “Although some of the clinicians are very helpful, I do find on certain days some can be more helpful than others” (P6).

COVID-19 and the shift to online learning further compounded these challenges by limiting engagement and opportunities for clarification. Participants described difficulty concentrating, reduced interaction, and decreased confidence in their understanding of headache-related content. One participant explained, “Learning online during COVID was a challenge that a lot of us experienced not to have the face-to-face option. Having to attend lectures online and not being able to in person. I don’t know, I definitely learn better in in person environments and feel more comfortable to ask questions or just have a better understanding in in person. So that was a challenge” (P2), while another similarly reflected, “We started learning headaches during COVID time… it was very difficult trying to grasp so much information without face-to-face interaction” (P9). Collectively, these educational and environmental challenges intersected to negatively influence students’ confidence, engagement, and perceived readiness to diagnose and manage headache disorders.

Theme 3: Clinical Exposure

Clinical exposure, gained through the participants’ active involvement in the clinical practicum under the supervision of registered clinicians, played a pivotal role in shaping their’ feelings of preparedness. It was described as a positive and transformative experience that enhanced self-perceived readiness to diagnose and manage headache disorders. Participants reported that seeing real patients improved confidence, diagnostic ability, and management skills, although gaps in knowledge were sometimes revealed. One participant stated, “So, it’s improved in the sense that I can pick up those red flags a lot easier and also asking the right questions related to those headaches” (P10). Another reflected, “I think a big one for me—has my confidence improved, so I’m not doubting myself the whole time with it, as well as I’m able to pick out what’s important now when I never used to be able to” (P13). Exposure to real patients improved case history-taking, recognition of red flags, and the development of treatment and management plans, while also reducing self-doubt. However, some participants noted that clinical exposure simultaneously revealed gaps in their knowledge, which at times reinforced feelings of incompetence. This dual effect was illustrated by 1 participant who stated, “Positive because I’ve gained a little bit more experience, but negative because I need more exposure and knowledge… I see there’s a big gap” (P12).

The results indicate that master’s chiropractic students’ self-perceived preparedness to diagnose and manage headache disorders was influenced by their clinical skills, confidence, and ability to apply theoretical knowledge in practice. Educational and environmental challenges, including workload demands and limited support, hindered readiness, whereas clinical practicum exposure enhanced confidence, clinical skills, and management ability, despite revealing persisting knowledge gaps.

DISCUSSION

Level of Preparedness

Level of preparedness has been widely explored in teaching and learning literature both locally and internationally.9,10,12,21 The findings of this theme in this study aligns with existing findings and is therefore not novel but adds value by contextualizing it within chiropractic education in South Africa. In this study, preparedness encompassed students’ clinical skills, confidence, and knowledge base, particularly in relation to diagnosis and management of headache disorders. Participants emphasized the importance of being competent in clinical tasks such as taking a thorough case history, echoing prior findings by Moore et al,12 who found that confidence in diagnosis was closely tied to the ability to collect comprehensive case information and establish differential diagnoses. However, students also reported suboptimal levels of confidence, a finding supported in nursing literature by Ramahlo,21 who noted that midwifery students, despite strong academic competence, often lacked clinical assurance. Similarly, the chiropractic students in this study appeared academically prepared but expressed uncertainty in applying their knowledge to practice, particularly in headache management.

A further dimension of preparedness was the relationship between theoretical knowledge and practical application. While many participants valued theoretical learning and acknowledged its role in guiding diagnosis and management, fewer regarded practical skills as equally important. This imbalance was evident in that strong academic competence did not always translate into clinical proficiency or confidence. Comparable findings have been reported elsewhere, with Hynes et al11 showing that academically competent externs still struggled in practice, and Pulkkinen and de la Ossa13 highlighting the persistent gap between education and workplace readiness. Together, these findings suggest that while students recognize the importance of both theoretical and clinical skills, challenges remain in bridging the transition from classroom learning to clinical confidence and proficiency. These findings are important because they reveal a gap between students’ theoretical knowledge and their confidence in applying it clinically, particularly in diagnosing and managing headache disorders. Such gaps can directly affect clinical decision-making and patient care, as self-efficacy is closely linked to performance. Highlighting this issue in the South African chiropractic context underscores the need for curricula that provide stronger clinical exposure and experiential learning opportunities. Ultimately, addressing these gaps can enhance graduate preparedness and improve patient outcomes.

Educational and Environmental Challenges

Education and environmental challenges emerged as significant influences on students’ preparedness. Within the educational domain, participants highlighted challenges such as heavy workload, limited educational support, and inadequate practical training. The most prominent concern was the extensive workload, which was perceived as hindering optimal learning. Specifically, the module in which headache topics were taught. These were described as rushed, leaving material insufficiently covered. These findings mirror existing literature on medical students’ workload burdens,22,23 while offering a chiropractic-specific perspective on the need for increased educational support. Limited support from lecturers, clinicians, and facilitators was another recurring theme. Self-directed learning tasks shaped participants’ academic experiences, but insufficient feedback and unconstructive responses from instructors were perceived as discouraging. This aligns with findings in midwifery students, who noted that inadequate clinical support negatively influenced learning.24 In contrast, a different study reported that access to supportive and competent faculty enhanced ophthalmology students’ confidence, highlighting that adequate support can improve perceived preparedness.25 Participants also pointed to limited practical training for headache disorder management. While theory provided a solid foundation, the lack of integrated practical components left students feeling underprepared for clinical application.

The absence of module-specific practical training therefore directly undermined students’ confidence in managing headache disorders. From an environmental perspective, the COVID-19 pandemic was identified as a major obstacle to preparedness. Participants reported difficulties engaging with course material, reduced lecturer interaction, and diminished clinical exposure. These challenges mirror broader trends in health professions education, where it has been observed that online and blended learning created isolation, hindered comprehension of preclinical content, and reduced students’ sense of preparedness in both dental and medical programs.26,27 Similarly, research with chiropractic students highlighted that the clinical environment is not only the setting where training occurs, but also a space of meaningful learning; this underscores the dynamic and evolving nature of educational environments.28 When curricula are restructured or evaluated, educational managers should therefore consider these factors as central to shaping effective learning conditions.28 The findings of this study align with emerging evidence. Importantly, they highlight systemic issues including workload, limited faculty support, insufficient practical integration, and the pandemic’s disruptions that directly affect students’ ability to feel clinically prepared. Although these challenges are widely reported across health professions,9,12,21,25,26 this study provides a chiropractic-specific perspective, emphasizing that curricular design, lecturer engagement, and structured clinical exposure are critical for bridging the gap between theory and practice. Addressing these factors can enhance the student learning experience, improve confidence, and ultimately ensure safer, more effective patient care.

Clinical Exposure

Clinical exposure emerged as a central factor contributing to students’ sense of preparedness, consistently rated as a positive influence on self-perceived readiness. It was shown to enhance students’ self-perceived preparedness and confidence, enabling them to translate theoretical knowledge into practical skills. Participants reported improvements in core competencies such as taking case histories, identifying red flags, and formulating treatment and management plans, reflecting the essential role of clinical experience in building proficiency. Literature has revealed that time spent in clinical settings enables the development and refinement of competencies and skills, which in turn fosters greater confidence.11,12,21,29 Exposure to real patient scenarios also facilitated the integration of theory with practice, promoted reflective thinking and problem-solving, and supported professional socialization within the healthcare team. Clinical skills are emphasized as essential for effective patient care.30 Further, it has been observed that additional clinical time improved proficiency in developing treatment and management plans and strengthened students’ preparedness.12 Collectively, these findings reinforce that clinical exposure plays a vital role in bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical competence, thereby enhancing students’ overall sense of preparedness and highlighting the critical role of clinical exposure in health professions education.

Future Studies

Future research should focus on evaluating headache-specific educational interventions within chiropractic programs, particularly those incorporating structured practical training and clinical assessment. Longitudinal studies tracking students from preclinical education through clinical practice may provide insight into how preparedness develops over time. Multi-institutional studies would further enhance the transferability of findings and allow for comparison across curricula. Additionally, exploring the perspectives of educators and clinical supervisors may help identify gaps between educational intent and clinical expectations. Finally, research examining the effectiveness of blended and face-to-face learning approaches in the post-COVID educational context may inform future curriculum development.

Strengths

A key strength of this study is the inclusion of chiropractic students’ perspectives as primary stakeholders in their education, offering valuable insight into self-perceived preparedness for diagnosing and managing headache disorders. The qualitative design enabled an in-depth exploration of participants lived experiences, generating rich and nuanced data that may not be captured through quantitative approaches. Conducted within a South African context, the study contributes to the limited literature on chiropractic education in this setting and provides an opportunity for benchmarking against international training programs. Furthermore, the identification of perceived gaps in teaching, learning, and practical training offers meaningful insight to inform curriculum development. Understanding these gaps is critical in supporting the preparation of competent graduates capable of delivering safe and effective patient care.

Limitations

The participants provided insightful and relevant information, contributing to the literature on chiropractic education within an African context. The sample size was limited and drawn from a single institution, which may not represent the experiences of the broader chiropractic student population. As with all qualitative research, subjectivity is inherent in both participants’ perspectives and the researcher’s interpretation, and responses may vary depending on participants’ understanding of questions and their honesty. Recall bias, social desirability, and the timing of data collection particularly during COVID-19 restrictions may have further influenced participants’ reflections. Additionally, volunteer bias and limited diversity within the sample may have affected the range of experiences captured. Finally, the limited existing literature within the chiropractic education context constrains comparisons and the transferability of findings to other populations or settings.

CONCLUSION

This study explored chiropractic master’s students’ self-perceived preparedness in diagnosing and managing headache disorders. Participants reported feelings of underpreparedness and limited confidence, largely due to educational and environmental challenges, including workload, insufficient practical training, and the impact of COVID-19. Clinical exposure, however, significantly enhanced their confidence, skills, and ability to apply theoretical knowledge in practice. The findings suggest gaps in the current curriculum and highlight the need for increased faculty support, earlier clinical integration, and improved practical training. The findings further point to several actionable improvements. These include introducing earlier and more frequent clinical exposure, integrating headache-specific practical components into diagnostic training, and strengthening faculty feedback mechanisms. Implementing these strategies may enhance student preparedness, support professional competence, and ultimately improve patient care.
