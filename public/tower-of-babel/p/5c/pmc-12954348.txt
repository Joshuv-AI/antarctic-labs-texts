Real‐World Analysis of Outcomes of Venetoclax+Azacitidine Versus 7+3 Induction in Acute Myeloid Leukemia
Authors: Nehemias Guevara Rodriguez, Noemy Coreas Mercado, Syeda Ashna Fatima Kamal, Sandeep Beesabathina, Dawson Foster, Nabeel Rajeh, Ranju Kunwor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVenetoclax plus azacitidine (V+A) is standard for older, intensive‐ineligible patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Its expanding use in younger, curative‐eligible adults lacks comparative evidence against conventional 7+3 induction, raising uncertainty about potential survival compromise.MethodsWe performed an age‐stratified, retrospective comparative effectiveness study using de‐identified TriNetX electronic health records (2018–2025). Adults receiving first‐line V+A or 7+3 were propensity‐matched 1:1 within prespecified age groups (18–59; ≥60) by demographics, comorbidities, socioeconomic factors, and performance status. The primary endpoint was 1‐year all‐cause mortality; secondary endpoints included complete remission and ICU admission.ResultsAfter matching, 214 younger and 1724 older adults per arm were analyzed. Among younger adults, V+A was associated with significantly higher 1‐year mortality versus 7+3 (20.6% vs. 8.9%; HR 2.55), with a number needed to harm of nine. Remission rates favored intensive induction (53.3% vs. 44.9%), while ICU use was similar. In older adults, mortality differences were smaller (23.1% vs. 20.5%; HR 1.34; NNH 38), though remission again favored 7+3.ConclusionV+A appears markedly inferior to 7+3 as first‐line therapy in younger, curative‐eligible AML, producing a nearly fourfold greater harm signal than in older adults. These findings support restricting V+A to intensive‐ineligible patients pending randomized confirmation.Trial RegistrationThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) remains one of the few adult malignancies in which a cure is possible, particularly in younger, fit patients, where cytarabine–anthracycline induction has been the evidence‐based standard for almost five decades. This paradigm is supported by modern dose‐intensification trials from CALGB/ECOG demonstrating that higher anthracycline dose within “7+3” achieves deeper remissions and superior survival in fit adults [1, 2], and reinforced in younger AML through UK NCRI AML17, where FLAG‐Ida failed to displace 7+3 as the curative control induction [3]. Authoritative reviews continue to anchor AML care on the principle that depth of remission (minimal residual disease, MRD) is the biological substrate from which durable survival and transplant readiness emerge [4, 5, 6].

In contrast, venetoclax plus azacitidine (V+A) was designed for a different denominator. VIALE‐A demonstrated clinically meaningful remissions in older, intensive‐ineligible patients [7], supported by companion data using venetoclax with low‐dose cytarabine [8]. However, multiple translational studies consistently demonstrate that venetoclax‐based responses are molecularly shallower, less frequently MRD‐negative, and enriched for residual leukemic burden compared with intensive chemotherapy [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14], features biologically misaligned with curative‐intent practice in younger adults.

Real‐world practice has increasingly extended V+A into younger AML despite an absence of randomized evidence supporting non‐inferiority versus curative induction in this population. This is not trivial: racial‐survival gaps remain clinically relevant in AML [15, 16, 17], and de‐intensification could theoretically amplify inequities if substituted without evidence‐based justification. Furthermore, the modern curative paradigm in younger/intermediate‐risk AML depends on achieving deep cytoreduction and MRD clearance to enable allogeneic transplantation in CR1 [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. European standards emphasize de‐escalation only when robust non‐inferiority has been demonstrated in randomized trials [23].

Whether the real‐world extension of V+A into younger, transplant‐eligible adults leads to inferior survival remains an urgent evidence gap. We therefore conducted an age‐stratified, real‐world comparative study of V+A versus 7+3 in newly diagnosed AML to quantify the treatment‐linked effect on survival in biologically distinct age cohorts.

Methods

We performed a retrospective comparative effectiveness study using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, a federated electronic health record platform containing de‐identified patient‐level data from >69 healthcare organizations across the United States. We identified adults (≥18 years) with AML who received first‐line induction therapy between January 1, 2018, and June 30, 2025. Two exposure groups were defined: V+A and intensive induction chemotherapy (7+3) (Table 1). Patients were stratified into two pre‐specified age cohorts: 18–59 years and ≥60 years (Figure 1). The primary endpoint was 1‐year all‐cause mortality; secondary endpoints included complete remission and 1‐year overall survival (OS). Covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, HIV status, comorbidities (chronic kidney disease, heart failure, liver disease, diabetes), socioeconomic hazard codes (Z55–Z65), and ECOG performance status. To minimize confounding, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was applied separately within each age stratum using a greedy nearest‐neighbor algorithm with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals were generated from Cox proportional hazards models, and OS was estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method. Number needed to harm (NNH) was derived from absolute risk differences. All analyses were conducted within the TriNetX analytics environment. Institutional review board approval was not required because all data are fully de‐identified within the TriNetX platform.

Results

Patient Cohorts

Between January 1, 2018 and June 30, 2025, we identified two age‐stratified AML cohorts initiating first‐line induction therapy with either V+A or 7+3 (Tables 2 and 3). After PSM, 214 younger adults (18–59 years) per arm and 1724 older adults (≥60 years) per arm remained available for comparative analyses (Tables 4 and 5). Matching achieved excellent balance across demographics, comorbidities, socioeconomic hazard codes, and ECOG performance status, confirming comparability of the exposure groups (Figure 1).

Younger Adults (18–59 Years)

Among younger adults (Table 6), V+A was associated with significantly higher 1‐year all‐cause mortality relative to intensive induction (20.6% vs. 8.9%). This translated into more than a 2.5‐fold increased hazard of death (HR 2.55, 95% CI 1.49–4.37) and corresponds to a NNH of only nine patients, meaning treating nine younger adults with V+A instead of 7+3 would result in one additional death at 1 year. Complete remission probability also favored intensive therapy (53.3% vs. 44.9%; HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.52–0.90). ICU admission rates were similar (13.1% per arm), indicating that the harm signal with V+A in younger adults is not explained by overt toxicity events but rather by inferior disease control.

Older Adults (≥60 Years)

In older adults (Table 7), the mortality difference between treatments was less (23.1% vs. 20.5%), with an HR of 1.34 (95% CI 1.16–1.54) and NNH of 38. Although remission rates again favored intensive induction (47.2% vs. 42.1%; HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.79–0.97), the magnitude of harm was more modest compared to younger adults. ICU admission rates did not differ (11.1% vs. 12.1%). These patterns demonstrate that 7+3 remains more effective than V+A in both age strata; the clinical penalty for substituting V+A is nearly fourfold greater in younger patients than in older patients.

Discussion

AML is one of the few adult cancers in which a cure remains realistic for a substantial proportion of younger, fit patients. That reality rests on a biologic chain withstood five decades of scrutiny: intensity → depth of remission → curative potential. Modern dose‐intensity trials from CALGB/ECOG showed that higher anthracycline dose within 7+3 yields deeper remissions and superior survival in fit adults, establishing that cytarabine–anthracycline induction is the correct curative backbone in younger AML [1, 2]. The genomics era did not overturn this architecture: in the extensive UK NCRI AML17 randomized study, FLAG‐Ida did not displace 7+3 as the control induction in younger adults, reaffirming the primacy of cytarabine–anthracycline as the benchmark for cure‐capable therapy [3]. Authoritative reviews have continued to frame AML management around this principle of remission depth as the substrate for durable survival and transplant readiness [4, 5, 6].

V+A was developed for a different denominator. The pivotal VIALE‐A trial enrolled older, intensive‐ineligible patients (median age 76 years) and demonstrated clinically meaningful disease control without the morbidity of intensive chemotherapy [7]; companion prospective work with venetoclax–low‐dose cytarabine extended the signal in frail populations [8]. However, translational and correlative studies consistently show that venetoclax‐based remissions are less often MRD‐negative than those achieved after intensive chemotherapy, and persistent measurable residual disease (MRD) is tightly linked to inferior survival in AML [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. In other words, venetoclax responses are frequently shallower at a molecular level—highly valuable for palliation in the elderly, but biologically misaligned with curative intent in the young.

Our real‐world, age‐stratified analysis quantifies the clinical consequence of that depth mismatch at scale. In younger adults (18–59 years), first‐line V+A was associated with >2.5‐fold higher 1‐year mortality (HR 2.55, 95% CI 1.49–4.37), with one additional death for every nine patients treated (NNH = 9). In older adults, the absolute penalty was less severe (NNH = 38), supporting a tolerability‐driven value proposition rather than curative ambition. These findings demonstrate that the oncologic cost of de‐intensification is magnified precisely where curative potential is highest (Tables 6 and 7).

This data shows the observed harm is not a demographic artifact. After propensity matching, race and ethnicity distributions were comparable between arms in both age strata, and the mortality penalty with V+A in the young persisted across White, Black, Asian, and Hispanic groups (Tables 4 and 5). This indicates a treatment‐linked effect rather than inequitable allocation or composition bias, and is in line with broader epidemiology showing that racial survival gaps in AML are multifactorial and not resolved by de‐intensified regimens [15, 16, 17].

Equally important, the conventional rationale for substituting V+A in younger adults' toxicity avoidance is not supported here. ICU admission, a pragmatic surrogate of acute toxicity and clinical deterioration, was identical in the younger cohort (13.1% both arms) and nearly indistinguishable in older adults, arguing that the excess deaths with V+A in younger patients reflect oncologic harm (insufficient remission quality), not safety trade‐offs (Tables 6 and 7). Weakening induction did not buy absolute safety.

The biological rationale is straightforward. Deep cytoreduction at diagnosis is the only proven path to MRD‐negative remission with a high likelihood of durable cure; in high‐risk or intermediate‐risk younger adults, it also prepares patients for allogeneic transplantation in first complete remission (CR1), the definitive consolidative strategy in modern AML [18, 19, 20, 21]. Genomic classification further clarifies why “one‐size‐fits‐all” de‐intensification fails: molecular subsets carry distinct relapse liabilities, and transplant‐readiness hinges on clearing measurable disease to a depth that low‐intensity venetoclax‐containing regimens do not consistently achieve [9, 10, 11, 14, 22].

The evidentiary lineage, therefore, matters for both practice and policy. The sequence from modern dose‐intensity trials [1, 2], contemporary UK AML17 [3] MRD‐anchored translational studies [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14] and transplant outcome literature [18, 19, 20, 21] draws a continuous thread: younger, fit AML requires intensive induction to achieve the biologic state from which long‐term survival emerges. By contrast, the venetoclax program was never designed to challenge 7+3 in this population [7, 8]. Extending V+A into curative‐eligible younger adults is thus an evidence‐free extrapolation attractive for convenience, but misaligned with AML biology and the heterogeneous AML population. While Decitabine+Venetoclax was non‐inferior in one study, the trial design was not optimal [24]. A flawed non‐inferior design may make us accept an inferior treatment. There is no doubt that a better‐designed randomized trial is needed to change the practice, and a trial is ongoing looking into this specific question of event‐free survival and OS benefit of V+A versus 7+3 [25]. Future “triplet” strategies venetoclax layered with FLT3 or IDH inhibitors, or integrated as a molecular intensifier alongside cytarabine–anthracycline, may eventually deliver MRD depths compatible with cure, but they require age‐appropriate, prospectively powered trials before practice can change [26, 27, 28]. Until then, disciplined restraint is warranted: in younger, physiologically fit adults, 7+3 remains the standard induction backbone. In contrast, V+A should stay in the population it was designed for, older or frail patients, where its balance of efficacy and tolerability is evidence‐consistent and ethically sound.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it leverages one of the largest federated real‐world datasets in AML available in the United States, capturing routine clinical practice across diverse health systems. Second, the analysis used age‐stratified PSM to isolate treatment effects within clinically meaningful biological strata. This allowed us to demonstrate that the harm signal associated with V+A in younger adults is not a product of compositional bias but a treatment‐linked effect. Third, the outcome measures were brutal clinical endpoints: death, remission, and ICU use, minimizing ambiguity in effect interpretation. Together, these features provide a robust real‐world counterweight to the unchecked extrapolation of V+A into younger AML populations.

However, limitations must be acknowledged. As with all real‐world EHR‐based analyses, residual confounding may persist despite matching, and molecular data (including cytogenetics and mutation status) were not uniformly available. They therefore could not be integrated into effect‐modification modeling. We also recognize that V+A dosing schedules and supportive care practices may vary across institutions, and we could not adjudicate post‐remission interventions, including transplant allocation, factors that may influence long‐term survival. Our data is not stratified by ELN risk group. We also acknowledge that AML patients are heterogeneous. Outcome depends on clinical presentation, immediate complications related to the disease itself, in addition to the co‐morbidities of the patient. These are difficult to match even in a randomized trial setting, let alone in retrospective data analysis. Finally, the absence of MRD data is a constraint inherent to real‐world datasets. Yet this limitation underscores, rather than weakens, the central interpretation: even without molecular granularity, the magnitude and consistency of the age‐stratified mortality penalty is sufficiently large that it cannot be plausibly explained by unmeasured confounding alone.

Conclusion

In this real‐world, age‐stratified comparative analysis, V+A was inferior to intensive induction in younger adults with AML, delivering a mortality penalty of a magnitude that is incompatible with curative‐intent care. These findings are not explained by racial composition, socioeconomic hazard, or toxicity mitigation; instead, they reflect insufficient depth of remission in a population where depth is biologically non‐optional. Historically, both classical induction evidence and modern UK NCRI AML17 data have consistently reaffirmed that 7+3 remains the correct curative backbone in physiologically fit adults. V+A should therefore remain within its evidence‐based domain, older and/or frail patients, until prospective randomized trials in younger adults demonstrate non‐inferiority on survival, MRD clearance, and post‐induction transplant readiness. The burden of proof must lie with those proposing de‐escalation, not those defending the cure. AML is one of the few adult cancers in which a cure remains attainable, and we should not trade that advantage away for a convenience that the data do not justify.
