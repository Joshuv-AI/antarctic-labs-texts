Tumor index predicts recurrences of patients with pathological stage Ⅰ gastric cancer after radical surgical resection
Authors: Yijia Lin, Huaxian Chen, Chengzhi Wei, Dandong Luo, Jianping Guo, Xiansheng Hu, Yinghan Chen, Junsheng Peng, Yongming Chen, Lei Lian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the favorable prognosis of patients with pathological stage I gastric cancer (GC), recurrence may occur in a subset of individuals, and the underlying risk factors are currently being investigated. Tumor index (TI) plays a valuable role in predicting the prognosis of GC. The study aimed to determine the prognostic effect of TI on stage Ⅰ GC.MethodsPatients with stage Ⅰ GC who underwent radical resection were enrolled from the two medical centers from 2010 to 2020. TI was calculated by the pathological T stage multiplied by the maximum tumor diameter. The primary outcome was disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS). Cox regression analyses were utilized to evaluate the correlation between TI and prognosis.ResultsThe final analysis included a total of 684 patients from two medical centers. Higher TI exhibited a significant correlation with several clinicopathologic features, including N0 stage, TNM stage ⅠB, and positive PNI (all P < 0.05). Patients with high TI showed inferior DFS and OS compared to those with a low TI (P < 0.05). Multivariate analysis revealed that TI was an independent prognostic factor for DFS (hazard ratio (HR) = 2.44, P = 0.010) and OS (HR = 2.39, P = 0.014) in patients with stage Ⅰ GC who underwent radical resection.ConclusionTI was an independent risk factor in recurrence and overall survival for stage Ⅰ GC patients who received curative gastrectomy. Incorporation of TI into clinical decision-making regarding adjuvant therapy and intensity of follow-up may be warranted.Keywords: Stage ⅰ gastric cancer, Tumor index, Prognosis, Recurrence

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most prevalent malignancy globally, and its mortality rate is the third-highest among all malignant tumors worldwide [1]. In the 8th TNM edition, Stage I gastric cancer is defined as tumor confined to the gastric wall (T1-T2), no distant metastasis (M0), and regional lymph node involvement limited to ≤ 2 nodes (N0-N1), subdivided into IA (T1N0) and IB (T1N1 or T2N0) [2]. A study conducted in China reveals that only 16.1% of gastric cancer cases are diagnosed at stage I [3].

Curative resection is the standard treatment for the majority of stage Ⅰ GC and adjuvant chemotherapy is not routinely recommended. Studies had demonstrated that the 5-year survival rate of patients with pathological stage Ⅰ GC ranged from 83.6% − 91.5% [4]. However, local or distant recurrence may still develop in some patients who received radical resection alone, suggesting that micro-metastases might have already existed when surgery is performed. A subgroup of patients with a higher risk of recurrence may require adjuvant chemotherapy. Therefore, the demand for further stratification of patients with stage Ⅰ gastric cancer is on the rise.

The maximum tumor diameter can be readily and objectively measured, allowing for accurate and standardized assessments in clinical practice. Tumor diameter serves as an independent prognostic factor in several tumor types due to its ability to indicate the degree of tumor infiltration in the horizontal direction [5–8]. In previous studies on GC, the authors reported that tumor diameter may be an independent prognostic factor in GC, although the association may be inconsistent [9–11]. Therefore, some researchers have proposed that comprehensive consideration of tumor infiltration and tumor diameter may be more accurate in predicts of prognosis [12]. The tumor index (TI) is calculated as the sum of the tumor penetration depth (T category) and maximum tumor diameter (in mm). For example, a 10 mm T1 tumor would have a TI of 10 (T1 = 1 × 10 mm diameter). TI has been reported in the literature to serve as an effective prognostic stratifier for patients diagnosed with stage pT3 and pStage II gastric cancer [12]. However, the ability of TI to predict future relapse in stage Ⅰ GC is inconclusive. The objective of the current study is to investigate the potential of TI as a prognostic indicator for disease recurrence after curative resection in patients with stage Ⅰ GC.

Methods

Study population

Patients undergoing radical surgery for stage Ⅰ GC were identified from the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (n = 468) and Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (n = 329) from January 2010 to September 2020 respectively. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, informed consent was waived. The Ethics Committee of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University granted approval for this study (No. 2024ZSLYEC-103).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) patients with pathologically confirmed primary stage Ⅰ gastric adenocarcinoma; (2) patients undergoing curative surgical resection with lymph node dissection. Patients were excluded if they presented with any of the following criteria: (1) incomplete clinical data and follow-up data; (2) a history of malignant tumor other than GC; (3) carcinoma at the gastric remnant stump; (4) acceptance of neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Data collection

The clinicopathological data, including age, sex, body weight index, preoperative CEA and CA199, tumor diameter, and pathological characteristics, were gathered from the cancer database of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University and Sun Yat-sen University cancer centre. Follow-up data were collected from the follow-up office of the hospital.

In accordance with prior research, preoperative serum biochemical indices, including carcinogenic antigen (CEA) (≤ 5 ng/mL vs. >5 ng/mL), and cancer antigen (CA)19 − 9 (≤ 37 kU/L vs. >37 kU/L) levels were classified in this study [13]. The longest tumor diameter was pathologically measured using resected specimens that were embedded in paraffin and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. TI was calculated using a previously reported formula: TI = pT category × tumor diameter (mm) [12].

The pathological characteristics included tumor diameter, histological differentiation, T stage, lymph node metastasis, TNM stage classification, vascular invasion, and perineural invasion (PNI). Histological differentiations were classified as well differentiated (papillary adenocarcinoma, well-differentiated adenocarcinoma, and moderate-differentiated adenocarcinoma) and poorly undifferentiated (poor-differentiated adenocarcinoma, undifferentiated adenocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, and signet ring cell carcinoma). TNM stage classification was determined based on the guidelines outlined in the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM staging system (8th edition) [2]. Regular follow-ups were conducted for the patients. The primary oncologic outcomes were disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) rates. DFS is defined as the time from the date of curative surgical resection of GC to the date of a patient’s recurrence or death due to any cause. OS is defined as the time from the date of curative surgical resection of GC to the date of a patient’s death due to any cause.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (http://www.r-project.org, version 4.12). The optimal cut-off of TI was ascertained by the survcutpoint function of the survminer R package. The cohort was divided into high-TI group and low-TI group, according to the cut-off of TI score. Continuous variables were tested for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Continuous variables with normal distribution are reported as mean ± standard deviation, while continuous variables with nonnormal distribution are presented as median (interquartile range). Continuous variables were analyzed using either the Mann-Whitney U test or independent samples t-test, while categorical variables were analyzed using either the Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Survival rates were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, while the log-rank test was employed to compare survival differences among various subgroups. The median follow-up duration was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Prognostic factors for overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were evaluated through both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. In the multivariate analysis, only the variables with P values < 0.05 from the univariate analysis were included. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General features of patients with stage Ⅰ GC

In the present study, a total of 684 patients were included from two medical centres in the final analysis. We found good consistency of the baseline data from two medical centres (Table 1). There were 437 males and 247 females aged from 22 to 88 years (median, 59 years, IQR: 49–66 years). There were 608 patients (88.89%) with BMI greater than or equal to 18.5 kg/m2 and 76 (11.11%) with BMI less than 18.5 kg/m2. The median BMI were 22.3 kg/m2. In this study, 550 had T1 stage (80.41%), 134 had T2 stage (19.59%), and 86 had N1 stage (12.57%). The median tumor diameter was 20.0 mm (IQR: 15.0–30.0 mm). The mean TI was 30.2, and the median value was 25.0 (IQR, 15.0–30.0). The median follow-up duration was 55.3 (95%CI: 48.9–63.2) months. The 1-, 3-, and 5-year DFS rates in the cohort were 97.5%, 95.7%, and 93.3%, respectively. The 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates in the cohort were 98.0%, 96.8%, and 94.7%, respectively.

Survival analysis according to the TI

The prognostic impact of TI was evaluated through Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. The patients with high TI had a significantly worse prognosis than those with low TI in terms of both DFS and OS (P < 0.001 and P = 0.003, respectively, log-rank test; Fig. 2).

Based on survival curve analysis, a significant association was observed between high tumor index and poor prognosis in terms of both disease-free survival and overall survival. (P < 0.001 and P = 0.003, respectively, log-rank test)

Clinicopathological factors associated with prognosis

In order to evaluate the predictive potential of TI for prognosis, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed. According to the univariate analysis, age, sex, PNI, and TI were associated with recurrence in patients with stage Ⅰ GC (Table 3). In the multivariate analysis of variables from the univariate analysis (P < 0.05), elderly patients (HR = 2.39, 95% CI: 1.16–4.89; P = 0.018), male patients (HR = 2.68, 95% CI: 1.10–6.53; P = 0.031), positive PNI (HR = 4.22, 95% CI: 1.88–9.50; P < 0.001), and high TI (HR = 2.44, 95% CI: 1.24–4.81; P = 0.010) were identified as independent prognostic risk factors for DFS.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the risk factors affecting the prognosis of stage I gastric cancer patients who underwent radical surgical resection, an idea derived from a previously conference paper [14]. Recurrence and overall survival are the primary prognostic indicators. The result showed that the patients with high TI had a higher independent risk of recurrence. In patients with stage Ⅰ GC, who should be evaluated for the risk of recurrence, it is important to take into account not only the TNM stage and pathological features but also the diameter of the tumor. TI, which consists of the tumor infiltration and tumor diameter, can be a useful predictive indicator.

The diameter of tumor can be used as a predictor of tumor stage for several types of tumors including gastrointestinal stromal tumors, liver cancer, and breast cancer [15–17]. It represents the tumor infiltration in the horizontal direction. Also, several studies have reported the diameter of GC was an independent prognostic factor in GC [18–20]. Gao et al. [19] classified the patients with stage III/IV GC using the cut-off of tumor diameter (5 cm and 7 cm). In addition, Zhang et al. [20] divided the stage III patients with GC into two groups by the tumor diameter (4 cm). There is no unified standard for the cut-off value of tumor diameter for GC.

For the TNM staging system, T staging is performed based on the depth of invasion regarding GC. If considering both tumor diameter and the depth of tumor invasion, it could evaluate the level of tumor infiltration more accurately and have better performance in predicting patients’ prognosis. TI consists of tumor diameter and the depth of tumor invasion [12]. Previous study suggested that TI could significantly predict OS and peritoneal metastasis in patients with GC [12]. Currently, there has been no relevant literature regarding whether TI could predict stage Ⅰ GC recurrence either. In the study, we confirmed the predictive value of TI, which was an independent prognostic factor for tumor recurrence. However, regarding which of the three factors—tumor diameter, T stage, or TI—can better predict recurrence, we believe that more in-depth data analysis and future specialized research are needed to answer this question.

As is well known, stage Ⅰ GC has a good clinical outcome. In terms of stage Ⅰ GC, current guidelines recommend that surgical resection is the major treatment strategy [21]. However, the role of adjuvant chemotherapy after resection is not clear. Considering some patients still suffer from metastasis and recurrence, it is necessary to distinguish the high-risk feature of recurrences to assist the decision of adjuvant therapy. The literature revealed that the recurrence correlated with poor differentiation, perineural invasion, and vascular invasion in stage Ⅰ GC [22, 23]. However, it is still a difficult task to identify the patients with high-risk recurrence and guide adjuvant treatment.

TI is a facile and effective indicator. In this study, univariate analysis showed that T stage was not an independent factor affecting the recurrence. But combined with tumor diameter, the new indicator showed the promising prognostic values. Other researchers also used a more complex method to think about T stage and tumor diameter. Tang et al. [24] study used tumor volume (combined with T stage and tumor diameter) as an indicator and found that tumor volume have displayed a predictive role toward the prognosis of GC. However, considering the simplicity of clinical use, we chose TI as an indicator which was more convenient and obtained a meaningful result. Because postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy has been proven to effectively improve the prognosis of patients with stage Ⅱ-Ⅲ GC, high-risk patients identified by TI may also need to receive postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy to reduce the risk of recurrence. In addition, these high-risk patients should be followed up more carefully and closely after surgical resection.

There are some potential limitations of this work. First of all, this is two-centres retrospective study with a relatively small sample size and may have a selection bias. Second, these data span from 2010 to 2020, and the method of surgery may have changed. In addition, the measurement of tumor diameter is controversial. The size of tumor before operation may be different from that of pathological tumor after operation. The current findings focused on the size of postoperative pathological tumors. Therefore, the significance of preoperative tumor size estimated by endoscopy or other methods also needs further study. It should be noted that this study primarily focuses on stage I GC, and our findings should be cautiously extrapolated to advanced GC.

Conclusion

The findings of our study indicate that TI is an independent prognostic marker in patients with stage I gastric cancer who underwent curative surgery. TI can play a crucial role in identifying high-risk cases of recurrence and help surgeons make informed clinical decisions.
