An epigenetic clock for chronological age estimation in East Asian populations
Authors: Kuan Chen Lu, Po-Hsiu Kuo, Amrita Chattopadhyay, Tzu-Pin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPopulation aging is increasing the burden of age-related disease, highlighting the need for accurate molecular tools to estimate aging. DNA methylation, a heritable epigenetic mark linked to development and age-related disease, can capture biological aging and predict chronological age; however, most existing epigenetic clocks were developed primarily in non-East Asian populations and may show reduced calibration across ancestries. We analyzed methylation profiles from East Asian cohorts from Taiwan, Japan, and China to develop an East Asian-specific epigenetic clock, termed the EAS Clock. After quality control and probe filtering, a parsimonious model based on 38 CpG sites was constructed using stepwise multivariate regression with forward selection and Bayesian Information Criterion. The EAS Clock showed significant correlations between predicted and chronological age in both training and independent testing datasets, with residuals centered near zero. Functional enrichment analyses indicated that genes associated with the selected CpGs were involved in neurobiological, musculoskeletal, immune-inflammatory, and lipid-metabolic pathways. These findings support the EAS Clock as a compact methylation-based estimator of chronological age tailored to East Asian populations.

Introduction

There has been a rapid growth of the older population over the past decade, with people over the age of 60 years projected to constitute greater than 22% of the global population by 2050 [1]. This trend raises concerns, as increased longevity is not matched by reduced chronic disease burden [2], placing significant burden on healthcare, families, and society. As a result, aging research has attracted growing attention in recent years. One goal has been to establish methods that can enable accurate measurement of biological aging, risk prediction and identification, and exploration of effective interventions. As opposed to chronological age, which is the total number of years a person has lived, biological age represents the true health and functional status of the body, and is influenced by factors such as lifestyle, genetics, and epigenetics. Biological age is increasingly recognized as being more accurate than chronological age in determining chronic health outcomes.

Biological aging is often accelerated compared to chronological aging, and accurate measurement of biological age early on can be used to identify a population at high risk for adverse health outcomes and who may be a target for clinical interventions. On the other hand, chronological age estimation also has applications in forensic science for identifying individuals with unknown or disputed ages, and in biomedical research to understand molecular aging processes and predict health outcomes.

DNA methylation is a heritable epigenetic alteration that is known to be linked with developmental processes in several eukaryotes [3]. It is a process by which a methyl group is added to cytosines in the DNA molecule, resulting in the formation of 5-methylcytosine, which leads to modification of DNA activity without any sequence alteration [4]. The landscape of DNA methylation is dynamic and is subjected to physiological and disease-associated changes [5]. While extensive methylation changes characterize early development, alterations in adult somatic tissues may indicate age-related degenerative processes [6]. Aging is characterized by a gradual decline of physiological, functional, and biological efficiency, the biological component of which has been explained through a variety of markers, including genomic damage such as chromosomal instability and telomere shortening, mitochondrial damage leading to reduced energy production, stem cell depletion, accumulation of damaged proteins, or modifications of the epigenome [7]. Many of the age-related diseases such as cancer [8], neurodegenerative diseases [9], atherosclerosis [10], and inflammation [11] are a result of the deregulation of pathways and modification in transcriptomes caused by alterations in DNA methylation. The nine “hallmarks of aging” as enumerated by Lopez-Otín et al. have also been suggested to be responsible [12]. Previous studies have shown partial overlap between age-related methylation and histone modification regions, with about one-third of methylation sites affected by aging [13–15]. The above findings indicate that methylation changes could be potential representatives of the natural aging process and age-related phenotypes, and thereby could be used as a predictor of chronological age.

Recent evidence has further demonstrated that the age-associated methylation patterns vary across populations, likely reflecting genetic background, diet, and environmental exposures. For example, epigenetic clocks developed in European-ancestry cohorts, such as the Horvath and Hannum clocks, tend to show reduced accuracy or systematic bias when applied to non-European populations [16]. These findings highlight the need to develop ancestry-tailored models that account for population-specific methylation dynamics. Understanding such differences is critical not only for improving prediction accuracy but also for revealing how environmental and lifestyle exposures interact with the epigenome in distinct genetic contexts.

Methylation variation across populations also contributes to observed health disparities [17]. Moreover, studying methylation profiles of subjects with identical ethnicity but living in different geographical locations would provide knowledge on conserved methylation patterns within populations. Investigation and evaluation of the epigenome is necessary to understand the ethnicity-specific effects of DNA methylation on aging. Epigenetic clocks have recently emerged as a promising tool for predicting both biological and chronological ages [18]. There exist several studies that have utilized differentially methylated regions as markers of age; however, they have mostly been directed toward Western populations. In this study, we explored the relationship between aging and changes in DNA methylation specifically among East Asian (EAS) cohorts from Taiwan, Japan, and Chinese mainland. We aimed to establish an EAS-specific epigenetic clock that captures ancestry-related methylation signatures, reflecting both genetic background and environmental influences unique to this population. A model was proposed for predicting chronological age among EAS. An accurate and population-optimized epigenetic clock can not only measure true chronological age, whether accelerated or not, but also estimate lifespan potential, supporting precision interventions to slow aging and promote healthier longevity.

Materials and methods

Datasets

This study utilized methylation data from individuals of EAS ancestry. A total of 3637 subjects were included, out of which 2090 were of Taiwanese origin obtained from the Taiwan Biobank (TWB), and the remaining 1546 subjects of Chinese, Japanese, or Korean origin were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (see Supplementary Table S1 for dataset details). Epigenetic data were profiled using DNA from whole blood samples utilizing the Illumina HumanMethylation450 BeadChip array, Infinium MethylationEPIC array, or Illumina HumanMethylation27 BeadChip array. The 27K array data were used only for preliminary dataset inspection and initial quality review, and were excluded from downstream merging and model development. This was because restricting all cohorts to 27K-compatible CpGs would have markedly reduced the candidate feature space relative to the higher-coverage 450K and EPIC platforms. Therefore, downstream model construction was based only on probes shared between the 450K and EPIC platforms. The TWB dataset initially consisted of 865 917 CpG sites across all samples. Datasets from both TWB and GEO were integrated by retaining 398 296 CpG sites that were common to the Illumina 450K and EPIC arrays. Samples from the 27K platform were excluded from this merging step. Raw signal intensity data were processed using standard pipelines described in the published studies associated with each dataset. The GEO accessions used in this study were: GSE83845, GSE104812, GSE106648, GSE107226, GSE107737, GSE117087, GSE117931, GSE118468, GSE118469, GSE139404, GSE178235, GSE42372, GSE52068, GSE71841, GSE74693, GSE77283, GSE87016, GSE92767, GSE42861, GSE141682, and GSE159898 (see Supplementary Table S1 for full metadata and references).

Beta values (ratios of methylated to unmethylated probes for a given CpG site) were used for this analysis instead of M values (standardized beta values—logit transformed) due to the challenges associated with it, such as the potential for infinite or undefined results. Beta values range from 0 to 1 and thus offer a more stable and interpretable methylation level for each CpG site. The individuals in the merged dataset were randomly allocated into two subsets: 2546 in the training set, which was used to develop the model; and 1091 in the testing set, which was used for evaluating the model’s performance, ensuring a comprehensive and representative sample for our epigenetic analysis. A total of 398 296 CpG targets that were common to all the datasets were included for analysis.

Quality control

A series of quality-control steps was performed. CpG sites with detection P-values ≥.01 and missing rates ≥5% were excluded. The ≥5% missingness threshold was selected as a pragmatic, literature-supported quality-control criterion commonly used in methylation-array preprocessing to remove loci with nontrivial rates of unreliable measurements while preserving overall probe coverage [19, 20]. Normalization using Illumina GenomeStudio V2011.1 was conducted before merging the TWB with the GEO datasets to harmonize methylation values across datasets. Coefficient of variation (CV) analysis was conducted on all CpG targets to identify a low variability internal reference for centering. The target with the lowest CV was designated as the reference and used as the baseline to normalize each CpG target by subtracting the reference value from each target. The use of low variability methylation markers as candidate internal reference controls has been described previously using CV and resampling-based criteria [21]. This step was intended to mitigate global scale differences across merged datasets and should not be interpreted as a complete genome-wide batch-correction procedure. After normalization, a simple linear regression was performed with age as the dependent variable and each CpG site as an independent variable. CpG sites that did not meet a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold were excluded from further analysis. Outlier CpG sites were identified by ranking adjusted coefficient of determination (Adj R²) values from linear regression; sites with greater than the third quartile (Q3) plus 0.15 times the interquartile range (IQR) (Adj R² > Q3 + 0.15 × IQR) were excluded from further analysis.

Construction of an epigenetic clock for East Asian populations: EAS Clock

The CpG targets that passed quality control and statistical filtering were used to construct the chronological age prediction model. A stepwise multivariate linear regression model with forward selection and Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC) was implemented to conduct variable selection for incorporating CpG targets as predictors into the final prediction model. The final model (EAS Clock) was selected as the one with the lowest BIC, ensuring optimal predictor selection while minimizing overfitting. This approach was chosen to favor a parsimonious predictor set by including CpGs only when they provided sufficient incremental improvement in model fit, consistent with our aim of developing a compact and potentially deployable epigenetic clock.

To assess robustness of CpG selection under high-dimensional and collinear settings, we conducted a bootstrap stability analysis using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression (LASSO) [22] with 500 resamples on the candidate CpG set, and summarized selection frequencies for CpGs included in the final model (Supplementary Figs S1 and S2; Supplementary Table S4).

Training and testing

Subjects from the merged dataset were randomly allocated into two subsets with an approximate training to testing ratio of 2:1. The training set was used to estimate regression coefficients, and the derived prediction equation to evaluate the EAS Clock’s performance, using the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) between predicted and actual chronological age, as well as the mean absolute error (MAE) and coefficient of determination (R²) to evaluate both accuracy and explained variance. A further subgroup analysis for different age groups (0–20 years, >20–40 years, >40–60 years, >60–80 years, and >80–100 years) was conducted toward understanding the EAS Clock’s efficacy for specific age intervals. Finally, a comparison analysis was conducted with existing epigenetic clocks from EstimAge (https://estimage.iac.rm.cnr.it/tutorial). EstimAge allows identification of specific CpGs in the genome that are globally correlated to chronological age in any tissue or cell type through their methylation state [23].

Association of environmental and lifestyle variables

We evaluated associations of environmental and lifestyle variables through Epigenetic Age Acceleration (EAA) analyses where exposure-related relevance was evaluated by analyzing TWB participants with both methylation data and questionnaire responses (complete case n = 1911). We examined questionnaire-derived environmental and lifestyle factors, including secondhand smoke exposure, current alcohol consumption, current betel nut chewing, and regular exercise habit (all coded yes/no). EAA was defined as the difference between methylation-derived age from the EAS Clock and chronological age (EAA estimated—chronological), with positive values reflecting acceleration. We compared EAA across exposure categories using two-sided t-tests (α = 0.05) and then fitted multivariable linear regression models with EAA as the outcome and the four exposures entered simultaneously. In a prespecified sensitivity analysis, models were additionally adjusted for sex (and array/batch where available). Model fit was summarized by R² and F statistics, and effects are reported as regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals and P values. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.2).

Functional enrichment analysis and complementary gene-set analysis

To investigate the biological functions associated with age-informative methylation markers, we conducted functional enrichment analysis at two complementary levels.

Ingenuity pathway analysis

Genes annotated to the CpG sites included in our final epigenetic clock model were analyzed using the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA, QIAGEN Inc.) [24]. We used IPA’s Core Analysis with default parameters and the Ingenuity Knowledge Base (Genes Only) as the reference background; enrichment was tested by Fisher’s exact test (two-sided), and terms with P values <.01 were considered significant.

Over-representation analysis

To complement IPA and to account for potential probe number bias, we performed a probe-bias-aware over-representation analysis (ORA) using the `gometh()` function from the R package `missMethyl`. CpG sites that were incorporated into the final clock were used as the “selected” set, and all CpGs common to the Illumina 450K and EPIC arrays (∼398 000 sites) served as the background. Enrichment was performed for both Gene Ontology (GO, Biological Process) and KEGG pathway collections with prior-probability correction enabled, and terms with P < 0.05 were regarded as significant.

Gene-set enrichment analysis

To provide a genome-wide perspective on age-associated methylation, we conducted a gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the `methylRRA()` function from the `methylGSA` package (method = “GSEA”). Each CpG site was individually tested for its association with chronological age using linear regression, and the resulting genome-wide test statistics were used as input for enrichment analysis on GO and KEGG collections. Pathways with P < 0.05 were reported as significant.

Together, these complementary approaches (IPA, ORA, and GSEA) enabled both model-specific and genome-wide functional interpretations of methylation changes linked to aging.

Results

Sample characteristics

Quality-control measures were implemented on an initial 2090 subjects from TWB and 1546 samples from other East Asian countries, providing a total of 398 296 CpG sites across 3637 individuals for analysis. A total of 2546 samples were allocated as the training dataset and 1091 as the testing dataset. The age ranged between 6.40 and 84.00 years, with a mean of 49.98 years (SD = 12.93) for the training samples, while the testing set comprised subjects of age 1.34–85.65 years, with a mean of 47.92 years (SD = 14.77) (Fig. 1). Regression analysis for model development

The distribution of the CV calculated across all 398 296 CpG sites is demonstrated in Fig. 2A, ranging from 0.96 to 242.90, with a mean (SD) of 51.72 (37.49). The CpG site cg10192265, located at chromosome 20 and at base pair 30 220 446 (GRCh38.p13), corresponding to the SNP rs1979233980, had the lowest CV and was selected as an internal reference for centering to harmonize methylation values across merged datasets prior to downstream analysis. The beta value distribution for cg10192265, as shown in Fig. 2B, demonstrates a range of 0.9095 to 0.9941 and a mean (SD) of 0.9638 (0.009).

Distribution of (A) coefficient of variation among all samples and (B) beta values of methylation for the reference target. The coefficient of variation across 398 296 CpG sites was used to select the reference target (cg10192265).

Each CpG site was individually tested using a simple linear regression with chronological age as the dependent variable, and 134 635 CpG sites showed significant associations (Bonferroni-adjusted P < 1.25 × 10⁻⁷). Among these, only 2087 sites were retained, after eliminating outliers, based on the Adj R2 values, to be used as potential predictors of chronological age. These candidate sites were then entered into a stepwise multivariate regression with forward selection, where the BIC guided the inclusion of 38 CpG sites in the final model (EAS Clock). Details of the selected CpG sites and their estimated regression coefficients (based on the training dataset) are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Bootstrap stability analysis showed moderate to high selection stability among the 38 CpGs in the final model, with a median selection frequency of 0.703 and an interquartile range from 0.533 to 0.916. Twenty of the 38 CpGs were included in a set of 72 CpGs with selection frequency at least 0.7 (Supplementary Figs S1 and S2; Supplementary Table S4). To evaluate whether a reduced (20 CpGs) or expanded (72 CpGs) marker panel would provide a better balance between model fit and parsimony, we fitted three nested linear models on the same training dataset. Both AIC and BIC supported the 38-CpG model as the optimal trade-off compared with the 20-CpG and 72-CpG models (Supplementary Table S5). Nested F-tests further showed that expanding from 20 to 38 CpGs significantly improved model fit, whereas adding additional CpGs beyond 38 did not yield further improvement, supporting the 38-CpG model as a statistically and practically balanced choice.

Performance of the model (EAS Clock) with the training and testing datasets

In addition to internal validation using the training data, the performance of the fitted regression model (EAS Clock) was also evaluated on an independent testing dataset. Pearson correlation analysis between predicted and actual chronological age showed significant positive correlations of r = 0.71 (P < .0001) in the training set and r = 0.68 (P < .0001) in the testing set (Table 2 and Fig. 3). Furthermore, the distribution of residuals (i.e. estimated age minus chronological age) showed that both the mean and median values were approximately zero in both the training and testing sets (Fig. 4), consistent with no systematic bias. In addition, the mean absolute error (MAE) was 7.48 years for the training set and 6.22 years for the testing set. These results indicate that our epigenetic clock, EAS Clock, achieved strong predictive performance, with precision and accuracy comparable to or exceeding other models reported in population-level epigenetic aging studies.

Environmental and lifestyle correlates of epigenetic age acceleration

We next examined whether common lifestyle exposures were associated with EAA. In univariate comparisons (Table 3), exposure to secondhand smoke and current alcohol consumption were associated with higher EAA, whereas having a regular exercise habit was associated with lower EAA. In multivariable linear model (Table 4) including secondhand smoke exposure, alcohol consumption, betel nut chewing, and regular exercise simultaneously, secondhand smoke exposure was associated with +2.04 years higher EAA (P = .0026) and current alcohol consumption with +3.63 years higher EAA (P = .0030). In contrast, having a regular exercise habit was associated with −6.51 years lower EAA (P < 2 × 10−6). Betel nut chewing showed a trend toward higher EAA (+2.87 years, P = .097). The model explained ~12% of the variance in EAA (R2 = 0.124; F-test P < 2.2 × 10−16).

Comparison with EstimAge

Six samples with the largest prediction errors using our proposed model were fed into the EstimAge platform, which provides epigenetic age estimates based on CpG sites strongly correlated with chronological age across various tissues and cell types. A comparison of the predicted age by our method (EAS Clock) and other existing models, including Epigenetic Pacemaker (EPM), Hannum 13, Horvath 13 and 18, PhenoAge, Zhang Enpred, and Zhang Blupred was evaluated using the actual chronological age as the reference. Table 5 lists the differences between the predicted age and the actual age for each of the methods, with red and blue indicating over- and underestimation, respectively. While deviations were observed in our model, similar or greater discrepancies were also present in the other methods, supporting the challenge of age prediction in these outlier cases. The EPM method was found to perform the best, likely due to its use of 20 031 CpG sites. In contrast, our model relies on only 38 CpG sites, offering a far more interpretable and efficient alternative while maintaining competitive performance.

Functional enrichment analysis

To explore the biological functions associated with the 38 CpG sites in our EAS Clock, we submitted their annotated genes to IPA’s Core Analysis and examined the top 25 enriched Disease & Function terms (Fisher’s exact test, P < .01; Supplementary Table S3). The most significant annotations fell into several coherent categories. Neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental processes were heavily represented, with terms such as “Grade 3–4 glioma cancer” (P = 4.22 × 10⁻⁶), “Grade 4 astrocytoma” (P = 6.83 × 10⁻⁶), “brain astrocytoma” (P = 7.00 × 10⁻⁶), and “dementia” (P = 1.10 × 10⁻⁵). Musculoskeletal decline emerged via enrichments like “curvature of spine” (P = 2.80 × 10⁻⁵) and “osteoporosis” (P = 4.00 × 10⁻⁵). Immune and inflammatory signatures appeared in terms such as “macrophage activation” (P = 6.20 × 10⁻⁵) and “cytokine signaling” (P = 8.10 × 10⁻⁵). Finally, cancer-related pathways beyond the central nervous system, e.g. “skin cancer” (P = 2.10 × 10⁻⁵) and “polycystic ovary disease” (P = 5.50 × 10⁻⁵), were also enriched. Across these categories, several genes recurred, most notably ELOVL2, DNMT3A, and CHRNA9, each implicated in multiple enriched annotations. Together, these results indicated that the CpG markers driving our EAS clock are functionally embedded in key biological pathways underlying systemic aging.

Complementary gene set analyses

Probe-bias-aware (ORA; Table 6) of the 38 EAS-Clock CpGs using missMethyl highlighted lipid-metabolic pathways in KEGG, including fatty acid elongation (P = .041) and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (P = .045). GO terms were enriched for immune and lipid-signaling functions, e.g. activating MHC class Ib receptor activity (P = 3.3 × 10−4) and N-acetylphosphatidylethanolamine-hydrolyzing phospholipase activity (P = .0012), as well as cell-cycle phase regulation (P ≈ .002). Complementary genome-wide gene-set (Table 7) enrichment analysis (GSEA; methylGSA) further identified KEGG pathways in neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, lysosome, and purine/pyrimidine metabolism (all P < 1 × 10⁻⁹), and GO categories involving transmembrane transporters, ion-channel complexes, and the intermediate-filament cytoskeleton (all P < 1 × 10⁻⁹). Together with IPA, these orthogonal approaches converged on neurobiological signaling, cytoskeletal/musculoskeletal structure, immune-inflammatory processes, and lipid metabolism—axes tightly linked to age-related pathophysiology.

Discussion

Given the well-established role of age-associated epigenetic modifications, particularly the global decline of DNA methylation with age, methylation is widely accepted as a reliable proxy for estimating chronological age [25]. In this study, we specifically focused on EAS ancestry and constructed an epigenetic clock, EAS Clock, based on methylation profiles, demonstrating accurate and reliable prediction of chronological age.

Residuals, defined as actual age minus predicted age, showed an increase in variance at the extremes of the age spectrum, particularly among older adults. This pattern indicated potential overfitting or that age-related methylation dynamics possibly reached a plateau or exhibited nonlinear trajectories in older individuals, thereby limiting model resolution at advanced ages. These observations are consistent with well-documented age-related noise accumulation and epigenetic drift, rather than indicating a novel biological mechanism. Accordingly, we caution against over-interpreting age estimates in advanced age groups, where increased biological heterogeneity may reduce model precision.

To further elucidate the biological relevance of age-associated methylation changes, we performed functional enrichment analysis using IPA. Results revealed that CpG-associated genes in our model were enriched for aging-related functions, including neurodegeneration (e.g. dementia), musculoskeletal decline (e.g. curvature of the spine), and immune/inflammatory pathways. Although cancer-related terms were also enriched, these likely reflect biological overlap with aging rather than model bias. These enrichments are intended to provide functional context for the clock rather than to imply that the model directly measures any single causal mechanism of aging. For clarity, we prioritized age- and development-related functions in our interpretation. The complete list of top 25 annotations is available in Supplementary Table S3. Notably, several genes recurred across multiple enriched terms, which may reflect their roles in aging-related biological pathways. Among recurrent genes, ELOVL2 is a well-established age-associated locus [26], DNMT3A is relevant to methylation maintenance and hematopoietic aging, and CHRNA9 has been linked to neurobiological and auditory processes [27] [28]. These findings support the biological relevance of the CpGs included in the EAS Clock and suggest that they are functionally embedded in pathways associated with systemic aging and tissue degeneration. Their recurrence in age-related annotations reinforces the biological interpretability and relevance of our model’s features. The nonlinear relationship between methylation and age, especially in early life and advanced age, as shown in our subgroup analysis (Fig. 5), may stem from rapid methylation changes during early development, leading to greater interindividual variability, and a biological plateau in older age, reducing sensitivity to age differences. These dynamics likely contribute to reduced model correlations at age extremes and underscore a key challenge for epigenetic clocks. To substantiate these biological associations, we complemented IPA with probe-bias-aware ORA and genome-wide GSEA. These orthogonal approaches consistently highlighted lipid metabolism, immune signaling, cytoskeletal regulation, and neuroactive ligand-receptor pathways that are core processes in aging biology, thus reinforcing the functional validity of the EAS Clock’s CpGs (Tables 6 and 7).

The correlation between predicted age and chronological age, for Horvath’s clock, was 0.96 with a median absolute difference of 3.6 years [29, 30]. Another is Hannum’s single-tissue DNA-methylation age predictor, developed by training an elastic-net model on 71 age-related CpGs from 482 Caucasian and 174 Hispanic adults [31]. In Hannum’s epigenetic clock, the correlation between predicted and chronological age was 0.96, with a median absolute difference of 3.9 years; however, this clock has some bias in estimation when applied to non-blood tissues [18, 32, 33]. Other established epigenetic clocks include the epigenetic pacemaker (EPM) [34], PhenoAge [35], and BluPred, Enpred [36], and GrimAge [37]. We compared our proposed epigenetic clock with the aforementioned models, and found that it produced the smallest residuals tightly clustered around zero. This suggested limited systematic bias under our evaluation setting in age estimation, particularly for EAS individuals. Using the TWB subset with questionnaire data, we also showed that secondhand smoke and alcohol consumption were associated with higher epigenetic age acceleration, whereas regular exercise was associated with lower acceleration (Tables 3 and 4), underscoring sensitivity to modifiable environmental influences.

In this study, we compared our EAS Clock with several widely used epigenetic clocks that estimate chronological age, including GrimAge, Horvath’s 2013 and 2018 clocks, Hannum’s clock, PhenoAge, EPM, and Zhang’s Enpred and Blupred clocks. Among these, GrimAge was successfully applied to our data where applicable, which exhibited residual distributions highly similar to those of PhenoAge (Fig. 6), reflecting potential convergence in the biological aging dimensions they capture. Another popular model, DunedinPACE [38], which estimates the pace of aging rather than accumulated age, was not included in this study for comparison, due to its conceptual difference and data requirements compared with those of the other epigenetic clocks, including EAS Clock. While it offers valuable insights into the rate of biological aging, it is not directly comparable to our model’s aim of estimating chronological age. Future studies may incorporate DunedinPACE to explore longitudinal aging trajectories in EAS populations.

To gain a better understanding of the connection between epigenetic age and chronological age, it is essential to conduct longitudinal cohort studies, which follow individuals over extended timeframes, providing valuable insights into the phenotypic changes that occur as they age and the factors that cause diseases [39]. A meta-analysis by Marioni et al. revealed that epigenetic aging progresses slightly more slowly than chronological aging over the life course, especially in older adults [40]. Other studies have also reported a nonlinear, logarithmic pattern of epigenetic aging during adolescence ([30], [41]). Longitudinal cohort studies can potentially identify deviations between epigenetic and chronological age, enabling early-life interventions [18]. However, the influence of genetics and signaling pathways on DNA methylation across different stages of aging remains unclear [42–44, 45].

A key limitation of current epigenetic clocks lies in their variability across CpG targets, Illumina array platforms, tissue sources, and population ancestry. Such inconsistencies contribute to considerable heterogeneity across studies and constrain the generalizability and applicability of epigenetic clocks. In our analysis, the internal reference centering step was intended to harmonize methylation values across merged datasets, but it is not intended to serve as a complete genome-wide batch-correction procedure, and residual dataset-level effects may remain. In addition, cg10192265 overlaps rs1979233980, and genetic variation could potentially influence methylation measurement at this probe. Utilizing tissue-specific CpGs to construct clocks can potentially lead to more accurate and robust predictions. However, adjusting for cell type heterogeneity remains essential when estimating age from DNA obtained from mixed or diverse cell populations [36]. Future studies will be conducted by leveraging multiple accessible DNA sources to produce more accurate and comprehensive age estimates [39]. Moreover, additional biological markers will be integrated alongside DNA methylation to enhance the current model’s (EAS Clock) ability to estimate biological age. Although the TWB dataset contains limited clinical and lifestyle variables, our analytic strategy required merging it with GEO datasets, which do not provide such annotations. As a result, we were unable to explore the associations between epigenetic age acceleration and clinical outcomes or environmental exposures. This highlights the need for well-annotated clinical cohorts to assess clinical utility. Collectively, these results suggest that the EAS Clock not only accurately estimates chronological age but also captures biologically and environmentally driven aging trajectories relevant to chronic disease and health among EAS populations.

Conclusion

This study presents a robust and accurate epigenetic clock, EAS Clock, tailored to EAS populations for estimating chronological age. While it performs well in most scenarios, its use may be limited when biological age diverges significantly from chronological age. Nonetheless, such discrepancies may themselves offer insights into age acceleration or deceleration. In the current era of increasing life expectancy, early and accurate prediction of epigenetic age could facilitate timely antiaging interventions and targeted treatments for age-related diseases. Such efforts may ultimately ease the aging process for individuals and reduce the burden on healthcare systems.
