Frailty assessment and effect of physical activity interventions in older adults: a scoping review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression
Authors: Evolène Fayolle, Edouard Ollier, David Hupin, Amandine Baudot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPopulation ageing is accelerating worldwide, leading to a rising prevalence of frailty, a potentially reversible condition. Physical activity (PA) is a promising strategy to mitigate frailty, but heterogeneity in its definition and assessment has led to wide variation in measurement tools and intervention approaches. This study aimed to map frailty assessment methods and PA interventions in frail and pre-frail older adults, and to evaluate their effectiveness using meta-analysis and meta-regression.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in April 2025 using PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library, including studies published up to 2024. Eligible studies were intervention trials involving adults aged ≥ 60 years, reported a PA program and a validated frailty assessment. Two reviewers independently screened and selected studies. Meta-analysis and meta-regression were performed on randomized controlled trials reporting Fried frailty scores. Mean differences were pooled using a random-effects model, with additional analyses to explore the influence of intervention characteristics.ResultsA total of 163 studies were included. Frailty was assessed using 47 different tools, most frequently the Fried phenotype. Interventions were predominantly multicomponent, combining resistance, aerobic, balance, and flexibility exercises, typically delivered 2–3 times per week in supervised settings. Eighteen studies were included in the meta-analysis. Overall, PA interventions significantly reduced Fried frailty scores (MD = -0.71; 95% CI [-0.92, -0.49]; p < 0.0001). Baseline frailty level (p = 0.0125), session frequency (p = 0.01), and intervention context (p = 0.0008) significantly influenced outcomes.ConclusionPhysical activity interventions are associated with reductions in frailty when assessed using the Fried phenotype. However, substantial heterogeneity in frailty assessment and intervention reporting limits comparability and clinical translation. Standardization of frailty measures and improved reporting of intervention characteristics are needed to optimize and implement effective PA strategies in older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07969-8.Keywords: Frailty, Frailty assessment, Older adults, Physical activity, Intervention

Background

Population ageing is accelerating worldwide, mainly driven by increasing life expectancy [1]. By 2050, the number of adults aged 60 years and older is expected to double, rising from 1 billion in 2020 to 2.1 billion [2]. This demographic shift is accompanied by a growing prevalence of frailty, a multidimensional syndrome for which no universal definition exists [1]. According to Cesari [3] and the WHO [1] frailty can be described as a « progressive age-related decline in physiological systems and chronic conditions, leading to reduced functional reserve».

Ageing is associated with a gradual decline in both physical and mental capacities [2], including motor function. This deterioration is reflected in reduced muscle mass and strength [4], decreased bone mass and density [5], and impaired cardiorespiratory fitness [6]. Together, these changes contribute to slower gait speed, a well-recognized marker of frailty [1], and increasing vulnerability to adverse outcomes. Frailty also encompasses cognitive, psychological, and social dimensions, reflecting the complex interaction between biological ageing and environmental and behavioral factors. Chronic diseases and multimorbidity further exacerbate this trajectory [1].

Frailty not only reduces quality of life in older adults but also increases the risk of hospitalization, dependency, and mortality, thereby threatening the achievement of healthy ageing, defined by the WHO as «the process of developing and maintaining the functional capacity that enables well-being in older age» [1]. Its prevalence remains high among adults over 50, ranging from 12 to 24% for frailty and 46–49% for pre-frailty, depending on the definition used [7].

Importantly, frailty can be attenuated or even reversed [8], making it a key target for interventions promoting healthy ageing. Regular physical activity (PA) improves physical capacities, including cardiorespiratory fitness, physical function [9] and muscle strength [10]. However, older adults remain the least likely to meet WHO recommendations of at least 150 min of moderate-intensity activity per week, combined with strength, balance, and multicomponent training [11, 12].

A key challenge in addressing frailty through PA lies in persistent gaps in knowledge. Frailty assessment remains highly heterogeneous due to the lack of a unified definition [1], making it essential to identify which tools are used in PA studies. In parallel, PA interventions vary widely in frequency, intensity, duration, and type, and the parameters most effective in reducing frailty remain unclear.

To address these gaps, we conducted a scoping review to map the current evidence, describe frailty assessments methods used in PA intervention, and characterize intervention components [13]. Building on this, we performed a meta-analysis and meta-regression to evaluate whether these interventions improve frailty, using the most commonly applied measure, and to identify key training parameters associated with effectiveness.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in April 2025 in three databases including articles published up to 2024 : (1) PubMed, using the keywords and Boolean operators («Exercise»[MeSH Terms] OR «Exercise Therapy»[MeSH Terms]) AND «Frail Elderly»[MeSH Terms]; (2) Embase, using ‘frail elderly’/exp AND ‘exercise’/exp;(3) Cochrane Library, using («Exercise» OR «Physical Activity») AND («frail» OR «frailty» AND «older»).

Study selection

A priori predefined methodological procedures, including study selection, data extraction, and analytical strategies, were systematically applied throughout the study.

Titles and abstracts retrieved from the search were independently screened by two reviewers (EF and AB). Disagreements were resolved by discussion. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were then reviewed to confirm eligibility (EF and AB). Screening was conducted manually in Rayyan, without the use of automation tools. Study selection followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews [15]. The selection process is illustrated in a flow diagram (see Results) in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR checklist [14] (Supplementary Data 1).

Data extraction

Data from included articles were entered into a structured table. Extracted variables included: article title, first author, study design, publication year, intervention year (if reported), frailty assessment used, frailty level, sample size, mean age, sex, presence of a specific pathology, intervention characteristics (PA type, duration, frequency, context, intensity, when available for meta-analysis), and any additional interventions (nutritional, psychological, motivational, etc.). Only information reported in the published articles was extracted.

Data analysis

For the scoping review, results were organized into three sections. Each section, describing the characteristics of: [1] the included studies [2], the frailty assessments used and [3] the PA interventions.

Meta-analysis and meta-regression methods

After mapping the characteristics of PA interventions and frailty assessment methods, we proceeded to quantitatively synthesize the effects of interventions across studies. The decision to conduct a meta-analysis was based on the identification of the most commonly used frailty assessment tool across studies. This approach aimed to ensure sufficient comparability of outcomes and to reduce methodological heterogeneity, while acknowledging that it captures only one dimension of frailty.

Data extraction

We extracted Fried frailty scores measured before and immediately after the intervention, together with their standard deviations.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R software using the metafor package. Effect sizes were calculated as the mean difference between pre- and post-intervention scores. When standard deviations (SDs) of the change scores were available, the standard error (SE) of the change was calculated using the formula: SEchange= SDchange / √n, with n representing the group size. A random-effects meta-analysis was first conducted to summarize the general impact of intervention on frailty. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q and the I² index and was graphically represented using prediction interval (95% level). Results are presented as a forest plot with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Significance was set at p < 0.05. The existence of potential publication bias was explored using a funnel plot.

In order to explain the potential heterogeneity found in the study results, the influence of several moderator was evaluated. Univariate meta-regressions were conducted to examine the effect of continuous moderators, including mean session duration and baseline frailty score. Stratified meta-analyses were performed for categorical moderators, including frequency, intensity, type of PA, nutritional supplementation, nutritional counselling, and intervention context. Weekly frequency was grouped into three categories: 1–2 sessions per week as «low», 3–4 as «moderate», and ≥ 5 as «high»; when studies reported variable frequencies, the mean frequency was calculated and assigned to the corresponding category. Intervention intensity was classified as low, moderate, or high when reported categorically. The intervention context was grouped into three categories: centre, home, and centre and home.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (EF and AB). Disagreements were resolved by discussion. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool, with the standard version applied to individual RCTs and the specific version applied to cluster RCTs [16].

Results

Selection of sources of evidence

A total of 163 articles were included in this scoping review (see Supplementary Data 2 for details), of which 18 were retained for the meta-analysis. The study selection process is summarized in a flow diagram (Fig. 1) in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR checklist [14], Supplementary Data 1.

PRISMA diagram of study selection

Description of articles included in the scoping review

The articles included in this study were published between 2003 and 2024. The peak years of publication were 2021 and 2022, accounting for 14.7% and 14.1% of the articles, respectively. Most of the studies (n = 95) were randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Other designs included pilot studies (n = 25), quasi-experimental or pre-post studies (n = 16), secondary analyses of RCTs (n = 13), feasibility studies (n = 6), cohort/longitudinal studies (n = 5), one cross-over study, one delayed-design study, and one pragmatic study compared with a previous RCT included in our present scoping review.

The included studies involved a total of 23,981 older adults, with a mean age of 76 ± 5 years (range 64.8–91.9). Among the 152 articles reporting sex distribution, 65.8% of participants were women.

Most interventions targeted both men and women (n = 149), while 13 studies focused exclusively on women and only one trial specifically on men.

Of the included trials, 124 did not target a specific disease. Others focused on cognitive impairment or dementia (n = 10), obesity (n = 9), cancer (n = 7), cardiovascular disease (n = 5), diabetes (n = 2), stroke (n = 2), osteoarthritis (n = 1), urinary incontinence (n = 1), acute heart failure with diabetes (n = 1), and kidney disease (n = 1).

Description of frailty assessment

Frailty was assessed using 47 different tools. The Fried phenotype [17] was the most commonly used (61.3%), followed by the FRAIL scale (8%), the Physical Performance Test (PPT) (7.4%), the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) (6.1%), the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) (5.5%) and the Frailty Index (FI) (3.7%) (see Supplementary Data 3 for details). Most of these instruments primarily assess physical frailty rather than adopting a multidimensional approach to frailty assessment. In six studies (50%) PPT was combined with VO₂ peak and difficulties in performing two instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) or one basic activity of daily living (ADL). In addition, 29 studies (17.8%) combined several assessments to measure frailty.

Description of PA interventions

The following sections summarize the most frequently reported characteristics of the interventions.

Intensity

Exercise intensity was generally not specified in the included studies.

Frequency

Intervention frequency ranged from once weekly (6.1%) to daily sessions (4.3%). Most trials offered two (28.8%) or three sessions per week (40.5%). Some included progressive or variable frequencies (9.2%).

Time (duration)

Session duration ranged from 7 to 120 min. Most studies delivered 60-minute sessions (39.9%) or 45–60 min (20.5%), while 34 (20.9%) proposed sessions shorter than 45 min.

Type

Many studies (66.9%) implemented multicomponent PA programs, whereas others (28.8%) focused on a single type of activity. Most interventions included resistance (85.3%), aerobic (64.4%) balance/proprioception/neuromotor (56.4%) and flexibility/stretching exercises (49.1%). Some (30.7%) also proposed functional/mobility training, dual-task or coordination exercises, Tai Chi, Kinect exergames, or dance.

Other characteristics

Most interventions were centre-or nursing-home-based (68.1%), while others took place at home (15.3%), combined centre and home settings (14.7%), or compared centre versus home interventions (1.8%).

Intervention length ranged from a few days to several years, but most lasted 3–6 months (65.6%), with peaks at 3 months (41.1%) and 6 months (17.8%). Fewer studies lasted under 3 months (22.7%) or extended beyond 6 months (11.7%).

Some programs combined PA with other interventions (49.7%): 50 focused on diet, 20 on motivation, 9 on cognitive training, 8 on education, 6 on psychological interventions, and 16 on other approaches.

Meta-analysis and meta-regression

In the scoping review conducted upstream, 61% of the included studies used the Fried frailty phenotype. This strong predominance of the Fried frailty phenotype across the included studies provided the methodological justification for moving beyond a scoping synthesis towards a quantitative meta-analysis restricted to this outcome, ensuring sufficient representativeness and comparability for statistical pooling. In contrast, the other frailty assessment tools were each used in less than 8% of studies, making meaningful comparisons across instruments difficult.

Eighteen studies (n = 18) were included in the meta-analysis, representing 25 intervention groups (IG, 1,252 participants) and 14 control groups (CG, 1,201 participants), totaling 2,453 participants. This allowed for 25 comparisons (k = 25) of Fried frailty scores. The results of the risk of bias analysis are presented in the supplementary Data 4.

Across the included studies, the overall mean age was 75.8 years. Exercise frequency ranged from 1 to 7 sessions per week, with a mean frequency of approximately 3 sessions per week. Session duration varied between 10 and 90 min, with most interventions lasting between 45 and 60 min. These characteristics are broadly representative of the intervention designs identified in the scoping review.

Overall, the interventions significantly reduced Fried scores (MD = -0.71; 95% CI: [-0.92, -0.49]; p < 0.0001) with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 91.2%). Cochran’s Q was also significant (Q = 274.08, df = 24, p < 0.0001), confirming heterogeneity. Meta-analysis results are presented in Fig. 2. No apparent publication bias was detected on the funnel plot (in Supplementary Data 5).

Stratified meta-analysis

No significant effects were observed for additional interventions, nutritional support, type of PA or intensity. Variable weekly exercise frequency and context of intervention were significantly associated with the observed effect (Figures in Supplementary Data 6).

A significant difference was observed in the change in mean frailty score depending on the weekly session frequency (p = 0.01). A low weekly frequency was associated with a reduction of -0.41 [-0.55; -0.28], moderate frequency with − 0.95 [-1.3; -0.61] and high frequency with − 0.84 [-1.55; -0.12].

The intervention context also showed a significant difference in the change in mean frailty score (p = 0.0008). Interventions conducted in a center were associated with a reduction in frailty of -0.86 [-1.17; -0.56], those combining center and home − 0.64 [-1.04; -0.24] and home-based interventions − 0.27 [-0.40; -0.14].

Discussion

The studies included in this review used a wide range of frailty assessments, underscoring its complexity of the construct and the lack of a standardized definition [3]. Nevertheless, frailty in PA interventions was most commonly assessed using Fried’s criteria, indicating that frailty was predominantly conceptualized through a physical condition rather than through a multidimensional perspective encompassing psychological, social, and functional domains. This predominance likely reflects the fact that, within PA interventions, frailty is primarily approached from a physical perspective. However, because our review specifically focused on frailty outcomes in the context of PA interventions, this may also have introduced a selection bias toward studies using physically oriented frailty measures. Based on Fried’s crietria, our meta-analysis showed that exercise interventions significantly reduced frailty (-0.71 points, p < 0.0001).

These reductions in physical frailty scores may reflect improvements in underlying domains, particularly physical function. Previous meta-analytic evidence suggests that PA is associated with enhanced physical performance (e.g., muscle strength, balance, and mobility) [18], as well as improved psychological well-being [19]. Given the established association between frailty and clinically relevant outcomes such as falls [20], hospitalization [21], and reduced quality of life [22], these improvements in frailty scores may therefore have potential clinical significance.

Our stratified meta-analysis and meta-regression further indicate that reductions in frailty depend on intervention context, weekly frequency and baseline frailty level.

Diversity of assessments

This scoping review identified a substantial lack of consensus in frailty assessment, with 47 different tools across included studies. Similar variability has been reported in previous studies with between 7 and 67 instruments used to define frailty or pre-frailty [23, 24]. Such heterogeneity complicates comparisons across study, limits generalizability, and may influence observed intervention effects. Differences in assessment tools can affect the identification of frailty, the severity detected, and responsiveness to change following interventions. Although Fried’s phenotype is widely used, questions remain regarding its comparability with other instruments, particularly as it mainly conceptualizes frailty as a physical syndrome rather than a multidimensional condition. Similarly, the other most frequently used frailty assessments in this review, including the CFS, PPT, and SPPB, also primarily evaluate physical performance and/or functional status, whereas multidimensional approaches such as the FI were rarely used (3.7% of studies). Estimates of frailty prevalence varied substantially according to the assessment instrument used, with physical frailty measures generally yielding lower prevalence estimates than multidimensional approaches such as the FI. Considerable heterogeneity was also observed within physical frailty instruments themselves, with prevalence estimates ranging from 13% frail and 47% pre-frail using the Fried phenotype, compared with 17% and 15% using the CFS, and 8% and 36% using the FRAIL scale, respectively [7]. Accordingly, improvements observed using one frailty instrument cannot necessarily be assumed to reflect equivalent changes when frailty is conceptualized using alternative models. Overall, these findings underscore the need for further methodological work aimed at harmonizing frailty measurement and better integrating multidimensional approaches.

Frailty level

Our meta-regression showed that higher baseline frailty was associated with greater reductions following intervention, suggesting that PA interventions may be particularly beneficial for more frail individuals.

PA intervention programs

Among the 163 included studies, most (n = 115) implemented multicomponent interventions combining several types of PA. Exercise intensity was often not reported, limiting our ability to assess training load and explore dose–response relationships. The most common modalities were resistance (n = 139), aerobic (n = 100), and balance training (n = 90), consistent with international recommendations promoting multicomponent exercise to maintain strength, gait and balance [25]. Resistance training plays a key role in counteracting sarcopenia and improving muscle strength, mass, and function, while also contributing to endurance. Aerobic exercise improves walking speed and cardiorespiratory capacity, both strong predictors of frailty and survival [26–28]. Balance training reduces fall risk, a key component of frailty, and combined resistance and. balance programs appear particularly effective [29]. These physiological and functional adaptations directly map onto key domains of the Fried frailty phenotype, particularly weakness, slowness, and low PA. Overall, multicomponent interventions have been shown to improve physical function, strength, walking ability, and stability [30], which may partly explain the observed reductions in frailty scores. However, our meta-regression did not identify a clear association between specific PA modalities and frailty reduction, highlighting the need for further research.

Some studies (n = 81) incorporated additional components such as nutritional interventions. Previous meta-analyses suggest that combining exercise with nutritional strategies may enhance frailty reduction compared with standard care [31]. However, in our meta-regression no significant additional effect was observed when nutrition was combined with PA, although this finding is based on a limited number of intervention groups (k = 6) and should be interpreted with caution.

Most interventions prescribed two to three sessions per week, a frequency that is both feasible and commonly recommended for older adults. Our meta-analysis showed that all frequencies were associated with reductions in frailty, with the greatest effects observed at moderate frequency. These findings suggest that even relatively low doses of PA may be beneficial and can be adapted to real-world constraints.

Session duration was most often between 45 and 60 min, but no clear relationship was observed between session length and frailty reduction. Further high-quality studies are needed to determine optimal session duration.

In many studies, exercise intensity was poorly reported, limiting not only the assessment of overall exercise dose but also the understanding of dose–response relationships and underlying mechanisms. Subgroup analyses based on reported exercise intensity (low, moderate, or high) did not demonstrate significant differences in frailty outcomes; however, these analyses were constrained by the absence of intensity data in several studies. As a result, the robustness of dose–response inferences remain limited. This lack of detail hinders the identification of optimal training stimuli and reduces the reproducibility and clinical applicability of interventions. The use of standardized reporting frameworks such as the TIDieR [32] checklist could help improve the completeness and consistency of intervention descriptions, and more robust cross-study comparisons in future research. Improved and standardized reporting of intensity is therefore essential to guide evidence-based exercise prescription. In contrast, frequency and session duration were more consistently reported and allowed partial characterization of exercise dose. Taken together, frequency and session duration may provide a partial proxy of overall training load, suggesting considerable variability in total exercise exposure across studies. Although moderate session frequency appeared to be associated with greater reductions in frailty, the current evidence remains insufficient to identify the optimal combination of exercise type, intensity, duration, progression, and supervision, owing to the heterogeneity and incomplete reporting of intervention characteristics.

Finally, our meta-analysis showed that center-based interventions were more effective, likely reflecting the benefits of supervision, reassurance, and improved adherence, a key determinant of intervention success [33]. However, most interventions were conducted in controlled settings, which may enhance adherence, safety, and engagement, potentially leading to an overestimation of effects. This makes it difficult to disentangle the specific impact of the intervention from contextual factors such as supervision and participant characteristics. Adherence and engagement were not systematically extracted in the present analysis, limiting our ability to examine their role in explaining observed effects. It also remains unclear whether similar benefits can be achieved in community or real-world settings, and whether observed effects are attributable to the exercise itself, the delivery context, participant-related factors such as motivation, or a combination of these factors. Moreover, highly frail, multimorbid, or socially isolated older adults remain underrepresented in many intervention studies, which may further limit the generalizability of findings. For more vulnerable populations, structured and supervised environments may be necessary to ensure both safety and effectiveness. Future studies should directly compare delivery settings, report adherence, and evaluate the feasibility and sustainability of interventions to better assess real-world applicability.

Disease-specific populations

Few studies focused on older adults with specific conditions such as diabetes or cancer, despite their high risk of frailty. This is consistent with previous literature highlighting the lack of disease-specific research in this field [34]. In clinical practice, frailty rarely occurs in isolation and is often accompanied by multimorbidity, including exhaustion, muscle loss, reduced cardiorespiratory capacity, physical inactivity [35], and undernutrition [36], often related to the disease itself or its treatment. Notably, many studies excluded these patients. This creates uncertainty regarding how well the findings translate to the populations most commonly encountered in clinical practice : although frailty rarely exists in isolation, much of the available evidence is based on relatively ‘healthy’ and selected populations. As a result, the external validity and clinical applicability of these findings are limited. Future studies should therefore focus on disease-specific and more clinically representative populations to better inform the adaptation and implementation of PA interventions in these higher-risk groups.

Strengths and limitations

A limitation of this study is the absence of a pre-registered protocol prior to data extraction. Although this is consistent with methodological guidance for scoping reviews [14], prospective registration, particularly for the meta-analysis component, would have strengthened transparency and reproducibility. Nevertheless, the scoping review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression were designed as complementary components of a unified analytical framework, moving from exploration of heterogeneity to quantitative synthesis and investigation of effect modifiers.

Another important limitation relates to the heterogeneity of frailty assessment tools. Different instruments capture distinct dimensions of frailty, ranging from a physical phenotype (e.g., Fried) to multidimensional constructs including cognitive, psychological, and social domains (e.g., FI). However, in our sample, multidimensional measures were rarely used (3.7%), reflecting a predominance of physically oriented assessments in PA intervention studies. By restricting the meta-analysis to the Fried phenotype, we improved comparability but narrowed the conceptual scope. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting changes in physical frailty rather than frailty as a whole and thus cannot determine whether PA is equally effective across cognitive, psychological, or social dimensions of frailty. Consequently, future research should not only include meta-analyses using alternative and multidimensional frailty measures but also routinely assess multidimensional frailty in PA intervention studies to better understand how different domains respond to these interventions and to capture their full potential impact.

Residual heterogeneity may also reflect unmeasured reported factors, including adherence and intervention fidelity across studies. The inconsistent reporting of these variables limited their robust inclusion in meta-regression analyses and may partly explain the residual variability observed across studies.

For the meta-analysis, the relatively small number of included trials may limit generalizability.

Nonetheless, the robustness of our findings is supported by the exclusive inclusion of randomized controlled trials, the use of rigorous statistical methods (including heterogeneity and moderator analyses), and systematic risk-of-bias assessment. The combination of scoping review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression provides a comprehensive and structured evaluation of the effects of PA interventions on frailty.

Conclusion

The Fried phenotype remains the most widely used frailty assessment tool in PA interventions. PA interventions are associated with reductions in frailty as measured by this tool, with baseline frailty, intervention context, and session frequency influencing outcomes.
