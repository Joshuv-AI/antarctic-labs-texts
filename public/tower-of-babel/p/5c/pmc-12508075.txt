Generating reliable software project task flows using large language models through prompt engineering and robust evaluation
Authors: Mohammed Sarim, Faraz Masood, Manas Maheshwari, Arman Rasool Faridi, Ali Haider Shamsan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Large Language Models (LLMs) offer promising capabilities for converting unstructured software documentation into structured task flows, yet their outputs often lack procedural reliability critical for software engineering. This paper presents a comprehensive framework that benchmarks five leading LLMs-Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok 3, GPT-Omni, DeepSeek-R1, and LLaMA-3-across five prompting strategies, including Zero-Shot, Chain-of-Thought, and ISO 21502-Guided, using real-world software tutorials from the “Build Your Own X” repository. We introduce the Hybrid Semantic Similarity Metric (HSSM), which combines SentenceTransformer embeddings with context-aware key-term overlap, capturing both semantic fidelity and procedural coherence. Compared to traditional metrics like BERTScore, SBERT, and USE, HSSM demonstrates significantly lower variance (CV: 1.5–2.9%) and stronger correlation with human judgments. Our results show that even minimal prompting (Zero-Shot) can yield highly aligned task flows (HSSM: 96.33%) when evaluated with robust metrics. This work offers a scalable evaluation paradigm for LLM-assisted software planning, with implications for AI-driven project management, prompt engineering, and procedural generation in software education and tooling.

Introduction

Software Engineering is concerned with transforming unstructured project descriptions into software task flows; whether these descriptions come in the form of a README, Wiki, or blog post, being able to rely on and understand what all the steps are supposed to be necessary for project development, resource allocation, timeline projection, and risk avoidance are critical1. Software task flows take an overall large project and break down what is supposed to be done into a consecutive order of intention so development teams can complete the work the right way1. Recently, successful LLMs capable of creating software task flows include GPT-4, Llama, Gemini, DeepSeek and Grok 3, which go beyond just creating SRS down to Software goals or segmenting Software goals into Software requirements, have increased productivity2. Creating tutorials and projects based on expected project instructions and vague documentation is another example of educational projects that LLMs can create3. However, reliable task flow generation is difficult because project leaders across multiple domains need to rely on LLMs to understand various documentation for consecutive works that ultimately make sense within the scope of the project they’ve commissioned4.

Reliability rests upon prompt engineering and evaluation metrics to generate the best results and ascertain credibility. While many advocate for BERTScore, SBERT, and USE as standard metrics for evaluating the quality of LLM creations, they fail to include functionality and consequential accuracy as a factor. For example, SBERT’s score varies from 50.85% to 68.31%, rendering the task flow assessment random at best; however, LLMs fail to acknowledge that similarly worded tasks appraised either accidentally rearranged or intentionally rearranged are incorrect. The BERTScore appreciates the similarity in phrasing5. Ultimately, all the scores fail to project precision since they account for variance but not internal integrity compared to correctness6. With so much on the line relative to confidence in reliability, Software Engineering wants better alignment than incorrect sequencing that’s proven incorrect by variance.

To overcome such difficulties, we created a framework for task flow generation and analysis with the “Build Your Own X” repository with varied software project tutorials (e.g., Ray Tracing Engine, Blockchain, Web Server) of multiple kinds, assessing LLMs’ capability to extract procedural architecture from diverse inputs4. We tested five LLMs, such as Gemini 2.5 Pro and Grok 3, utilizing five different prompt styles: Zero-Shot, Few-Shot, Chain-of-Thought, Role-Based, and ISO 21502-Guided, seeking to optimize task flow creation. To enable robust evaluation, we introduced the HSSM, which integrates Sentence Transformer embeddings with contextual key-term overlap to evaluate both semantic intent and procedural fidelity, yielding consistent and clear scores (90.76–96.33%, coefficient of variation 1.5–2.9%) compared to SBERT’s wider range5. HSSM draws on previous work demonstrating the effectiveness of hybrid metrics in structural as well as semantic settings evaluation, offering resistance to process distinctions and domain-specific terms7–9. Our findings show that Zero-Shot and Few-Shot optimized prompting return high-quality task flow even when scored with SBERT, but HSSM is necessary for capturing the consistency process needed by software engineering utilizes. This is an incremental improvement to AI-based project task flow generation with an empirical contribution to software teams applying LLMs to project management techniques2,4.

Problem statement

The ability to create precise, coherent task flows from informal representations of software projects such as READMEs, wikis or tutorials is a major open problem in software engineering as clearly defined, sequenced and executable task flows aid project management and resource allocation, preventing problems associated with miscommunication, overspending or delays2,10,11. Yet these task flows must comprehend the range of potential documentation, from detailed schematics to passing summaries of non-standardized forms. Compounding confusion is domain-specific jargon in context-relevant situations12. LLMs such as GPT-4, Gemini 2.5 Pro and LlaMA-3 apply varying prompt techniques-Zero-Shot, Few-Shot and Chain-of-Thought-to parse these narratives into structured task flows with competitive performance among each using SBERT (as high as 68.31%), yet sensitive to wording and application and more figurative and detailed schematics12. Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and LLaMA-3, guided by prompting strategies such as Zero-Shot, Few-Shot, and Chain-of-Thought, offer a promising solution for decomposing these narratives into structured outputs, achieving competitive performance even with metrics like SBERT (up to 68.31%), despite challenges from prompt sensitivity to minor wording changes and model architecture differences that yield verbose or shallow plans5,13–15. However, traditional evaluation metrics like BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore, SBERT, and Universal Sentence Encoder (USE) prioritize surface-level or embedding-based similarity, failing to capture the logical sequence and functional equivalence essential for software task flows, with SBERT showing high variability (50.85–68.31%) due to insensitivity to paraphrasing, reordering, or abstraction, and BLEU/ROUGE overemphasizing exact n-gram overlaps5,6,16,17. These shortcomings erode trust in LLM-generated task flows, raising ethical and practical concerns for their deployment in critical applications like project management, where misalignment can disrupt project outcomes18,19. To address these gaps, we developed the HSSM, which integrates SentenceTransformer embeddings with contextual key-term overlap to provide stable (90.76–96.33%, coefficient of variation 1.5–2.9%) and procedurally aware evaluation, ensuring reliable AI-driven task flow generation for software engineering5.

Significance of the study

This study fills a gap in the software engineering literature that demonstrates how large language models (LLMs), guided by optimized prompt techniques like Zero-Shot and Few-Shot, can create coherent and reliable task flows from otherwise unstructured software project descriptions as seen in the “Build Your Own X” repository23. Traditional metrics such as BERTScore, SBERT, and USE focus on either token-level similarity or sentence-level average, which fail to assess for procedure based on high variability (e.g., SBERT’s Coefficient of Variation greater than 20%; Section Descriptive analysis). The assessment tool created for this study uses the proposed hybrid approach to assess for semantic validity and meaningful progression24. Coefficients of variation showing relative stability output of the HSSM (1.5%-2.9%) render it important for task flow quality, as project management requires proper sequencing and function equivalization24. For instance, whereas SBERT favors verbatim replication, pushing down word count, its higher task completion accuracy rate indicates25. The HSSM can favor functional equivalence-critical for transformations that include paraphrasing or reordering-and thus serves as a more reliable measure for comparison within this study across studies, LLMs, and prompt approaches.25. This research is significant for the field because it advances the practical application of software engineering since reliable task flows improve project timing, resource management, and chances of miscommunication or project delays are avoided23. Also, by comparing LLMs and prompting techniques against one another via the HSSM, other software practitioners can benefit from this research’s practical recommendations to choose which LLM and method works best for producing reliable, linear tasked flows from different types of documentation, like READMEs or wikis, improving group cohesion and project-oriented deliverables26. Furthermore, the results impact the field of prompt engineering since the quality of task flow depends upon prompt formulation, providing others with a discretionary record to avoid mistakes27. Finally, since LLMs will be frequently utilized for process automation in software engineering, the ability to generalize HSSM as an evaluation metric will ensure that not only do generated task flows flow appropriately, but they also make logical sense within established processes, giving confidence for real-world application23. Ultimately, the results here will also influence future developmental and evaluative processes for any LLMs going forward to ensure that future resources will better accommodate the needs of software engineering for reliable and stabilized structured generation27.

Scope and limitations

This study examines the generation and quality determination of software project task flows from the “Build Your Own X” GitHub repository using five state-of-the-art large language models (LLMs) and five prompting strategies-Zero-Shot, Few-Shot, Chain-of-Thought, Role-Based, and ISO 21502-Guided-to generate LLM responses needed for successful completion of any software engineering project [?]. The JSON schema serves as a uniform and required preprocessing approach, not only to comparably assess task flows generated by a human versus an LLM but also to determine quality alignment via HSSM, which focuses on semantic substantiation and the determination of professional practicality through logical consistency-in contrast to similarity-based metrics like SBERT, which compare human and LLM sentences based on surface similarity and fail to account for the relevance of task flow order 28.

The highest quality prompting strategies, as determined by HSSM semantic validation, also performed well under SBERT evaluation. This suggests that even when SBERT provides weaker assessments, high structural quality can still be identified. Nonetheless, to maintain fidelity within the software project domain, HSSM is preferred. However, this domain-specific fidelity limits HSSM’s applicability in other fields such as medicine or manufacturing, which may require distinct preprocessing techniques and schemas. Furthermore, the absence of formal human evaluation in these cases reduces the perceived legitimacy of the results, offering only indirect suggestions of professional endorsement [?]28.

Overview of the study

This paper contributes to the understanding of LLMs for task flow generation and evaluation by experimenting with a new framework and by unique prompting with evaluation via the newly created metric75. The contribution is detailed in Methodology (Section Methodology) and is dependent upon an experimental setup of prompt engineering, LLM settings, and data cleaning to create a non-disruptive pipeline for task flow generation and evaluation based on the GitHub repository “Build Your Own X.” Subsequently, Results are in Section Results, Section Descriptive analysis holds a descriptive analysis, and Section Discussion discusses findings leading to a conclusion in Section Conclusion.

Prompting framework

The five prompting strategies- Zero-Shot, Few-Shot, Chain-of-Thought, Role-Based, and ISO 21502–Guided-were selected based upon varying traits of complexity, contextualization, and reasoning scaffolding from least complex to most. For example, Zero-Shot prompting establishes a control condition without contextualization to assess how well models can isolate and decompose the input without any connection to other works. Few-shot prompting includes the project description and in-worked examples of the task to aid in analogical reasoning. Similarly, Chain-of-Thought prompting provides a channel for incremental steps, as recent research posits that producing incremental reasoning in between key tasks increases output in a structured fashion29. Thus, these three more simplistic prompt types were chosen because they comprise a normative endeavor in prompt engineering and allow for guided comparison across simple and common modes of prompting.

To extend the experimentation to assess domain-aware and professionally plausible outputs, two additional complex prompts were introduced. The Role-Based prompt took on an identity for the LLM as an experienced project manager. Role-and persona-based prompting can produce more organic and complex flows when the machine assumes it is more than a neutral bystander. The ISO 21502–Guided Three-Stage prompt goes a step further, assuming human tendencies and embedding standards from a bona fide project management process while introducing generation–evaluation–regeneration. This last technique determined whether the model could become more intentional and reason within the context of critique for refinement. Thus, all five prompt types were chosen to analyze the effectiveness of strong prompting control techniques based upon real-world processing standards.

Prompt execution

All five prompt templates are run against each LLM with uniform decoding parameters. While some APIs (Google Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok 3, etc.) may still render slight randomness under default, we implement stringent JSON schema validation and up to three automatic retries. Yet during schema validation, some LLM calls returned as an empty string or null response-in other words, no JSON was rendered at all. We deem these “empty outputs.” Here, the prompt is automatically retried up to three times. If it remains empty after three attempts, we denote it as a failed output for that prompt-model combination. Once we receive a non-empty JSON output, we randomly spot-check 10 percent of the sample for integrity and structure before semantic decomposition.

Outputs that fail schema validation automatically retry. All outputs undergo manual inspection and standardization pre-assessment. Decomposition occurs via LLM (LLaMA model via Groq API) to render the output into the confirmed task and intention schema.

Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted on Google Colab using our own API keys for accessing the LLMs (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok 3). We did not track the number of tokens used or the billing details during this phase, so those figures are not included in this paper. The computations were performed using Colab’s free GPU instances, while API requests were made through our own paid accounts. As a result, any timing data provided (such as the average number of seconds per prompt) reflects Colab’s processing time and API latency, but should not be interpreted as final or indicative of actual costs. A future study will address this with a controlled setup to accurately track token usage and billing, enabling a clearer comparison of model costs.

Weight finding

The task-level weighting and HSSM require weights to assess how closely the AI-generated task flows resemble those generated by humans. This section details the experiment run to find such weights for the purpose of correlating as closely as possible with human-rated task quality in the other sections that describe how to determine the individual weighting with both empirical and theoretical justification.

Weight tuning experiments

We applied randomly grid-searched weights for task-level weighting and HSSM computation using human and AI task flows on 5 software projects from the “Build Your Own X” GitHub repository: Ray Tracing Engine, Blockchain, Neural Network, Operating System, and Web Server. These projects were chosen due to their different domains (Section Data preparation and gold-standard flows) so that different dynamic task flows were generated in abundance, order, and substance per project to ensure that the weights generalize.

For each project, 1000 weights instances were sampled, so in total, there are 5000 iterations across a parameter grid consisting of linguistic features (verbs, nouns, adjectives, dependency roles, named entities), HSSM components (SentenceTransformer models, BaseSim, ContextScore), and various other inputs (weight threshold, contextual overlap, sigmoid adjustment). The ranges are defined in subsequent sections but are based on linguistic and information retrieval best practices.

Therefore, human and AI task flows were run through the HSSM with differing weights. All weights were run through a holdout set, inclusive of approximately 50 human-annotated task flows for each project (about 10% of the variable total flows), randomly selected from each project type for proper representation. An extensive experiment applied 5-fold cross-validation to average HSSM scores per fold and rank the resultant configurations by mean score. Each project selected the top 5 configurations. This balanced efficiency with optimization, avoiding a full grid search (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 5.3\times 10^{6}$$\end{document} combinations).

Implemented in Python with spaCy and SentenceTransformer, using fixed seeds for reproducibility (Section Data preparation and gold-standard flows), the experiment informed weight selections detailed subsequently.

Similarity computation and scoring

We computed four similarity scores for each AI flow relative to its corresponding human-created flow. Three were standard embedding-based baselines-BERTScore, SBERT, and Universal Sentence Encoder (USE)-and one was the Hybrid Semantic Similarity Metric (HSSM). HSSM is a new metric that integrates multi-embedding semantic similarity with a contextual overlap bonus. Figure 1 demonstrates the HSSM process, including task unification, key term extraction, task-level frequency assignment, contextual embedding comparison, Jaccard overlap comparison, and sigmoid normalization. All four scores appeared in the results per project with model/prompt strategy applied noted in Section Results and Table 75,7,33.

(a) Baseline Semantic Metrics. BERTScore, SBERT, and USE as baseline semantic similarity metrics were present due to their prevalence, different approaches to embedding useful for determining semantic meaning at various levels of strengths. These three address matching at the token-level (BERTScore) and sentence-level embeddings (SBERT and USE), so an appropriate comparison of AI and human task flows is valid. Other lexical-based measures like BLEU, ROUGE, and METEOR were not utilized because they rely on exact token matches and are more likely to fail based on paraphrasing or reordering-which are crucial to assessing the quality of more linear task flows. In comparison, embedding-based assessments are more forgiving of the type of comparison and appropriate for semantically rich and procedurally varied outputs5,7,33–36 .

Weight Selection Rationale

The weights for BaseSim are as follows: 0.35 for MPNet, 0.25 for Multilingual MPNet, 0.20 for MiniLM, and 0.20 for DistilRoBERTa, ascertained through a randomized grid search (detailed in Section Weight finding). 1000 configurations were sampled per project over five domains (Ray Tracing Engine, Blockchain, Neural Network, Operating System, Web Server). The grid was constituted of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0.30, 0.35, 0.40$$\end{document}] for MPNet; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0.20, 0.25, 0.30$$\end{document}] for Multilingual MPNet; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0.15, 0.20, 0.25$$\end{document}] for MiniLM and DistilRoBERTa, with the expected contribution based on accuracy for semantic similarity. The optimal configuration (Configuration 2, 1) returned an HSSM of 0.97 over a validation set of 50+ human labeled task flows.

The fact that MPNet possesses the maximum weight (0.35) indicates why it surpasses the rest on English task flows since all-mpnet-base-v2 is trained with many English related tasks such as semantic sentence similarity and detection of sentence paraphrases7. Multilingual MPNet gets the second highest weight (0.25) although it has a more extensive training base; it may be noisy for English-specific task flows. Finally, MiniLM and DistilRoBERTa possess the same weights of 0.20; this is because they’re effective and powerful on various short texts and different settings7. The weights sum to 1.0, ensuring a normalized contribution. While it’s impossible to externally corroborate these precise weights beyond the scope of this experiment, uphold empirical tuning and the challenge for HSSM (0.94–0.99, Table 1) can validate these claims. This mirrors ensemble methods of NLP where weights reflect the most dominating models like MPNet but additionally, supportive embeddings 7,33. For example, Reimers and Gurevych (2019) assert that all-mpnet-base-v2 dominates the lighter models like MiniLM in terms of semantic similarity which allows for this elevated weight; Cer et al. (2018) utilize weighted ensembles in the Universal Sentence Encoder to enhance effectiveness.

2. Contextual bonus (Jaccard overlap)

The Jaccard overlap is also known as the Jaccard similarity coefficient or Jaccard index. It is a widely utilized statistical measure in information retrieval, data mining, natural language processing, and similar endeavors, where the comparison of the similarity between two sets exists across various domains, such as text analysis and clustering comparison/user preference detection. The importance of Jaccard overlap exists in determining the overlap of like items between two sets relative to all unique items35.

The Jaccard overlap measures the size of the intersection of two sets divided by the size of their union. (c) Hybrid score and final similarity

To blend semantic alignment with lexical overlap, we propose the HSSM, which combines a weighted ensemble of embedding-based similarities (BaseSim) with a contextual overlap score (ContextScore). The HSSM is computed as follows:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {HSSM} = \min \left( 0.80 \cdot \text {BaseSim} + 0.20 \cdot \text {ContextScore}, 1.0\right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where BaseSim is a weighted average of cosine similarities from four SentenceTransformer models (MPNet, Multilingual MPNet, MiniLM, and DistilRoBERTa).

The weights for BaseSim (0.80) and ContextScore (0.20) were determined through a randomized grid search (Section Weight finding) across the tested ranges: BaseSim in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0.70, 0.90]$$\end{document} and ContextScore in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0.10, 0.30]$$\end{document}. The selected 80:20 ratio yielded the highest Pearson correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 0.88$$\end{document}) with human rankings on a validation set of 50 human-rated task flows. This indicates that while some semantic similarity was beneficial, overemphasizing it was unnecessary, and the transfer of word meanings remained effective. These findings are consistent with related studies employing hybrid scoring schemes, where 80:20 and 70:30 splits are shown to balance higher-level semantic understanding with more granular word-level matching 5.

All processing was performed in Python with publicly accessible libraries (sentence-transformers 4.1.0, transformers 4.52.2, etc.) under a constant random seed (seed=42) for reproducibility. At the same time, smaller models like LLaMA-3 70B see spikes in scores depending on the chosen prompt. However, regardless of whether it is high or low, the new metric reduces the absolute spread between the scores, which promotes better contextualization across models.

Subtle re-rankings revealed. The new metric also alters the ranking of what’s most effective versus what’s traditionally most effective according to SBERT and USE. For example, Zero-Shot, which is statistically the weakest prompt according to SBERT or USE, comes in first from the hybrid (96.33%) as it doesn’t sacrifice that much specificity for the major structure. Likewise, ISO Guided Prompt beats Chain-of-Thought in the proposed hybrid, although lower in SBERT, implying that prompt clarity may rival or surpass stepwise reasoning in semantic fidelity.

Descriptive analysis

Four statistical analyses were performed to compare similarity scores generated by SBERT, hybrid, and cosine and to analyze the results across prompt strategies across models: Coefficient of Variation (CV), Z-score normalization, Pearson correlation, and Friedman test. CV was used to determine a relative index of volatility for each metric across tasks with elements of different scales and means; this was an important factor in determining relative similarity between metrics like SBERT and hybrid despite the former operating at a scale of 0-1 and the latter a scale of 0-100. Z-score normalization was performed to create new values based on means and standard deviations to get all values on a common scale of units; z-scores determine distributions in comparable units but do not adjust for interpretability. Pearson correlation was used to observe the strength of the linear relationship between the human review order predicted and generated similarity score ranking because a rank-based measure by Spearman or Kendall would not be as informative to determine this prediction. Finally, the Friedman test was performed as it is a non-parametric test that is more robust than the alternatives to determine if the difference in metric scores across prompt strategies was statistically significant without assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance (Table 10 and 11).

From an application standpoint, this means that one must be wary of which metrics are sensitive and not sensitive when applying findings to other prompts and evaluate each model accordingly based on those sensitivities/significances. For Gemini and DeepSeek R1, the sensitivity of Hybrid means it can still offer further insights into the impact of prompts. For LLaMA-3, the insensitivity of Hybrid means it can be trusted across evaluations regardless of prompt changes.

We recommend that all other scores are secondary to proposed metric for core evaluation because it functions differently by using deep semantic embeddings with a contextual overlap based on the task to remove irrelevant prompt artifacts and reveal true semantic invariance of structure. For example, Hybrid is not that different across the board from most models (Grok, ChatGPT, LLaMa-3); however, it is a statistically significant difference in those that are highly prompt dependent (Gemini 2.5 Pro, DeepSeek R1). In those cases where dependence at the prompt level is critical-whether to parse slight differences due to intention versus diagnosing prompt bias-we acknowledge that previous metrics such as BERTScore or USE are important and can be calculated secondarily to achieve such nuanced results. Thus, Hybrid can be the champion of your assertions about semantic integrity, and embedding-only scores can exist to give nuanced assessments when sensitivity at the prompt level is needed.

Discussion

This justification for the new evaluation metrics, which measure task congruence rather than just similarity, is only bolstered by the findings in Section Results46. Assessing passage similarity to review question relevance through BERTScore, SBERT, and USE provided lexical and contextual similarity through deep embedding but also revealed flaws such as: USE saturating, SBERT being under-sensitive, and BERTScore giving an overly generous reward to token overlap5. The Hybrid metric created from this experimentation eliminates those flaws and measures by deep semantic embedding signals and task-aware contextual overlap47. The consistency of significance compared to baseline across all LLMs, all tasks, and all prompt configurations demonstrates discriminative capability where other metrics flatten or distort distinctions.

Proposed metric’s unique contributions

The Hybrid was a secondary metric, but it operated within a similarly tight scoring band (90.76%-96.33%) to ensure prompt/model rank order. A tight band like this does not indicate insensitivity, but rather, intentional fluctuation to prevent an overvaluing of specific cases simply because of lexical diversity or syntactical reordering-an all too common occurrence with LLM-generated code. For instance, the Zero-Shot prompt ranks the highest for Hybrid alignment (96.33%) in accordance with its accurately assessed generation, for often, out-of-context prompts are penalizing for other metric assessments. In contrast, Role-Based ranks the lowest Hybrid alignment (90.76%)-although it scored high elsewhere via BERTScore or USE.

These observations suggest that the Hybrid method excels at capturing functional equivalence even when lexical similarity is low, and it penalizes procedural misalignment more effectively than static embedding approaches. This makes Hybrid particularly well-suited for evaluating structured tasks like code generation, process planning, or instructional content48,49.

Hybrid appears to be the most effective for evaluating functional equivalence when contextual overlap is minimal, and it has greater sensitivity to non-equivalence due to procedural disparity than merely static embedding approaches. Hybrid is ideal for evaluating something with a definitive process and structure, like coding generation, process planning, or instructional content50–52.

Prompting implications

The most important revelation was that prompted choice drove similarity scores the highest. For instance, Few-Shot and Chain-of-Thought, which were More Like This and Whodunit standardized prompts, performed highly across the board, similar to how previous literature established that giving these types of examples and chaining reasoning steps helps better orient LLMs to more similar outcomes51,53,54. But for Hybrid, it was a bit more convoluted: it ranked Zero-Shot and ISO Guided Prompt higher than Chain-of-Thought across all samples, meaning that well-worded, concise directions are sometimes just as good as-determined by longer, multi-step reliant tasks-and better than-relative to semantics accuracy. This negates some findings in the prompting literature, where Chain-of-Thought is often assumed to be universally superior53,54.

Model sensitivity

Although the Hybrid compressed score suggested that scoring at the low end or high end made no difference, it was still an essential factor. The LLMs that performed better across the board-GPT Omni 3.0, Gemini 2.5 Pro, etc.-scored a bit better as well, which is expected. However, even the two smaller models, LLaMA-3 and DeepSeek R1, ranked in the same relative prompt ranking, indicating that the Hybrid metric is consistent regardless of model size and differences but instead evaluates based on inherent task goals and not potentially skewed relative prompt evaluations based on model features or average answers54–57.

Comparison to prior work

This is important because previous findings show that lexical and free generation comparison scoring isn’t stable. For example, CodeBLEU attempts to combine AST and syntactic information but doesn’t succeed due to semantics being different51,53. In another case, SBERT and USE are rapid but have high variances (often to extremes) or adjust to saturation levels where significant differences in performance are masked51,53,54. Yet Hybrid combines lexical alignment (contextual word scoring) with semantic correlation from the results of CodeBERTScore and Jaccard-based retrieval scoring51,53–55. Therefore, it becomes more anthropocentric where accuracy is based on intention and sequence instead of just matching something on the surface53,54.

Limitations and future work

Though this is advantageous, our research is constrained to software development task flows (e.g., ray tracing engine, blockchain application, neural network, operating system, web server) so we retain a consistent domain-specific vernacular, in addition to detailed JSON schema validation applicable to the coding realm. However, this might not translate well into non-software projects. Therefore, future research should implement HSSM and the prompting pipeline in medical procedures, educational lesson plans, and hardware construction tasks to assess cross-domain effectiveness58,59.

The Hybrid metric has some limitations as well. First, the use of sigmoid normalization-while it reduces variance-may lower score gaps between similarly high performing strategies, lacking the necessary discriminatory nuance in closely ranked situations60. Furthermore, while the metric is applied consistently across all conditions tested, unofficial validations to compare Hybrid scoring to human scoring-like inter-annotator reliability or accomplishment of tasks based on user expectation-have not been done yet. Such studies could modify or learn weighting systems for ideal scoring, compare the correlation of the various metrics to reliable task transfer to the real world, or apply Hybrid to other endeavors like lesson plans, summarization, or robotics; and finally, comparing Hybrid to similarly structured output metrics like CodeBERTScore may also clarify the unique contributions of the Hybrid approach61.

While HSSM correlates highly with prior embedding and overlap measures, we did not conduct a human assessment to prove that higher HSSM results translate to better assessed or accurate task flow. A minor usability study-in which professionals will judge or at least rank generated lists against a gold standard best to worst-will be saved for future work.

Ultimately, these results support the necessity for systematic prompting and extensive semantic evaluation. The Hybrid measure not only is a reliable, easily understandable measure of LLM alignment, but it also improves upon standard measures-and frequently surpasses them. With LLMs being used in programming situations, classrooms, and decision-making domains, the need for such measures will be critical to evaluating the quality of output in a way that aligns with human expectations62.

Conclusion

This research introduced a proposed HSSM, an innovative evaluation standard for assessing structured task outputs of large language models (LLMs). Existing standards such as BERTScore, SBERT, and USE are either subject to ceiling effects and lexico-centric biases or fail to accurately assess the subtlety of error corrections; the proposed metric, however, integrates deep semantic embeddings with a measured, context-aware application relative to the task at hand.

By assessing the effectiveness across five unrelated programming fields, five fields of prompting, and five varying LLMs, this research determined the proposed metric to have statistically significant linearity and discriminative power from within a substantive yet arbitrary sample, proving it accurately assessed both functional and structural relationships between human versus AI-generated task flows. Furthermore, this research suggested that some prompts encourage greater quality generations than others. Fascinatingly, Zero-Shot and Few-Shot prompted correlations with the Proposed metric’s averages the most at 96.33% and 95.23%, respectively, often defeating the common Chain-of-Thought method. This implies that LLMs can effectively reason step-by-step with little to no prompting, and when they do answer appropriately, it more often comes from a simpler, shorter prompt.
