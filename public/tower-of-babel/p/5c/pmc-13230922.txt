Radiomics-based feature exploration and auxiliary diagnostic model construction for gaming disorder
Authors: Yifan Li, Shuhong Lin, Ying Tang, Qiuping Huang, Zhenjiang Liao, Xinxin Chen, Chenhan Wang, Jingyue Hao, Xuhao Wang, Ru Yang, Hongxian Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Subjective biases in current clinical assessments create an urgent need for objective biomarkers in Gaming Disorder (GD). Radiomics could help in diagnosis based on whole brain analysis and multidimensional indicators. This study aimed to find classification features of GD based on radiomics and to develop an auxiliary diagnostic model for GD. A total of 141 individuals with GD and 73 healthy controls underwent a clinical assessment and resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging scans. Radiomics was used for feature extraction and selection, support vector machine was employed to construct a classification model, and permutation test was applied to verify model performance. The model incorporated 67 brain functional features (accuracy: 86%, sensitivity: 93%, specificity: 75%, AUC: 0.92), primarily concentrated in the Default Mode Network, Executive Control Network, and Salience Network. Among these, right precuneus-left anterior cingulate cortex connectivity not only contributed most to the model’s classification but also was significantly correlated with GD symptom severity (total score, withdrawal symptoms, tolerance, persistent engagement despite harm, and escape from problems/negative emotions). Radiomics provides a promising framework to identify GD features, unravel neural mechanisms, and assist objective diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47980-y.

Introduction

Gaming disorder (GD) is an uncontrolled gaming behavior that primarily affects adolescents, severely impairing their social functioning. GD directly contributes to vision impairment, obesity and biorhythmic disorders1,2, and has a significant negative impact on academic and work performance3,4. Currently, GD is common worldwide, with an overall prevalence of approximately 3.3% and a prevalence of up to 12% in East Asia5,6. Under the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, the time adolescents spent on electronic games increased significantly during the pandemic period. A subset of them continued to engage in high-intensity gaming behavior even after the pandemic, which in turn led to a rise in the risk of GD and scores on its core addiction symptom (e.g., withdrawal symptoms, tolerance symptoms scores)7,8. However, due to a lack of awareness of the disease or a sense of stigma, people with GD tend to conceal or deny their behaviors9,10, and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fifth Edition (DSM-5) also states that people with GD “may lie to family members or doctors about their amount of gaming"11. Many studies have now diagnosed GD using self-report scales, which may lead to GD being missed or overshadowed by mood disorders12. Therefore, choosing the neurobiological indicators is valuable in the diagnosis of GD.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI), as a tool that objectively reflects brain functional status, has been widely used in GD research13–17. Existing studies have reported that individuals with GD exhibit increased activity in the ventral striatum and decreased activity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex18, as well as abnormalities in functional connectivity (FC) between brain regions. Additionally, some studies have proposed that subtype classification of GD can be performed based on FC patterns19. For example, Wang et al. categorized GD patients into two subgroups (craving-related and mixed psychological) based on FC19. From the network perspective, the Default Mode Network (DMN), Executive Control Network (ECN), Salience Network (SN), and limbic system, among others, have all been reported to exhibit functional abnormalities in GD20–22. However, there is heterogeneity among these rs-fMRI findings: different studies often report distinct brain regions with abnormal activity, and even contradictory results have been documented for the same brain region. For example, some studies have found increased activity in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) in GD patients23,24, whereas others have reported decreased activity in this region25,26; with respect to the functional connectivity of the DMN, some studies have demonstrated reduced connectivity27,28, while others have observed enhanced connectivity29. This heterogeneity may be related to the subjective selection of regions of interest (ROIs) or the adoption of different analysis strategies. Whole-brain-based multi-dimensional indicator analysis can reduce selection bias on a data-driven basis, but extensive computations and the integration of multiple types of indicators increase the difficulty of research.

Radiomics, by converting imaging data into high-dimensional quantifiable features and integrating machine learning for screening and modeling, can efficiently process whole-brain and multimodal data, reduce subjective bias, and explore potential biomarkers30. This technique has been increasingly applied in the field of mental disorders, including schizophrenia and major depressive disorder, facilitating the identification of imaging biomarkers that assist in early diagnosis and mechanism exploration31–34. In the specific context of GD, preliminary efforts have also been made to leverage radiomics. For example, Han et al. constructed a GD classification model with accuracy of 73%, specificity of 68% and sensitivity of 77% by using T1-weighted image and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data with a random forest algorithm35; Wang et al. also developed a GD radiomic model based on subcortical T1-weighted MRI features, achieving an AUC of 0.895 in the test cohort36. These results demonstrate the promising application of radiomics in the diagnosis of GD. However, most studies have demonstrated significant brain dysfunction in GD patients, and classification models constructed from structural MRI data alone are difficult to fully reflect their brain dysfunction. Notably, compared with structural MRI, which only captures morphological characteristics, fMRI has unique advantages in detecting dynamic functional activities of the brain. This is critical for revealing the core pathophysiological changes of GD, a disorder dominated by functional abnormalities. Using fMRI features can better capture these functional abnormalities, thereby enabling the development of more accurate auxiliary diagnostic models. Moreover, unlike traditional rs-fMRI analyses that mostly focus on single-dimensional indicators (e.g., amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations), the application of radiomics in multi-dimensional functional feature mining enables a more comprehensive characterization of brain functional alterations in GD. At present, there is a lack of studies that deeply mine the fMRI data of GD and uses multi-dimensional indicators to construct a diagnostic model for GD.

Given the significant abnormalities in brain function observed in individuals with GD and the advantages of radiomics in processing whole-brain and multi-dimensional data, this study aims to utilize radiomics to comprehensively analyze rs-fMRI data from individuals with GD and normal subjects to obtain classification features and construct classification models. We hypothesize whether radiomics can yield brain imaging features conducive to identifying GD, and based on these features, construct an auxiliary diagnostic model for GD. This, in turn, will provide support for uncovering the neural mechanisms of GD and offering objective diagnostic evidence.

Methods

Participants

This study recruited two groups of adolescents with GD and healthy controls from the psychiatric outpatient clinic of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University and the public, respectively, from January 2024 to June 2025. Two professionally trained psychiatrists diagnosed GD after evaluating game use information and impaired social functioning described by the subjects themselves and their families according to the International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD-11). Meanwhile, psychiatric disorders such as mood disorders, schizophrenia, and substance use disorders were excluded via standardized assessment with the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI), according to ICD-11. Healthy controls were neighborhood residents recruited by the study advertisement. The specific inclusion and exclusion criteria were as follows:

Inclusion criteria for the GD group: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria of ICD-11 GD; (2) age 14–25 years, > 6 years of education, able to understand the study and complete it; and (3) right-handedness.

Inclusion criteria for the healthy control group: (1) did not meet the diagnostic criteria for ICD-11 GD; (2) age, gender, dominant hand, and years of education matched those of the gaming disorder group, and (3) were able to understand the study and complete it.

Common exclusion criteria for both groups: (1) suffering from other mental disorders; (2) having used psychotropic drugs or psychoactive substances within 6 months; (3) suffering from organic brain disorders or somatic disorders affecting hemodynamics; and (4) history of cranial and cerebral trauma, history of febrile coma, and contraindications to magnetic resonance scanning.

Initially, this study recruited 147 patients with GD and 79 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. After MRI data preprocessing, 3 GD patients and 6 healthy controls were excluded due to excessive head motion, and another 3 GD patients were excluded due to obvious artifacts in the frontal lobe. Finally, 141 GD patients and 73 healthy controls were included for statistical analysis.

Each subject read the contents of the study project carefully and signed the informed consent form. For subjects under the age of 18, both the subject and their legal guardian provided their signatures on the form. The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (Ethical batch number: No. 2020004). All procedures in this study were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the relevant national and institutional guidelines and regulations.

Data acquisition

A self-administered questionnaire was used to collect socio-demographic information (age, sex, years of education) and gaming usage information (gaming expenditure[yuan/month], gaming time[hours/d]) from the participants in this study. Through interviews, participants’ gaming motivations were categorized into four types: seeking pleasure, escaping problems, relieving boredom, and maintaining relationships. The severity of GD was assessed using the DSM-5 structured interview.

Imaging data were acquired using a 3.0T MRI scanner (uMR 790, United Imaging Healthcare, China) with 32 coil channels. Specific acquisition parameters were as follows: (1) Rs-fMRI: Synchronized multi-planar echo sequence was employed with TR = 700 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 52°, field of view (FOV) = 210 × 210 mm, matrix = 84 × 84, Voxel size = 2.50 × 2.50 × 2.50 mm3; (2)T1-weighted images: TR/TE = 7.90/3.10 ms, flip angle = 10°, FOV = 230 × 256 mm, matrix = 460 × 512, voxel size = 1.00 × 1.00 × 1.00 mm3. The total scan duration of resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) was 7 min and 14 s, and the scan duration of T1-weighted structural MRI was 4 min. A total of 600 time points were acquired during the rs-fMRI scanning. During the scan, participants were in a supine position, with their eyes closed and kept awake; they wore earplugs to reduce noise interference, and head cushions were used to fix their heads to minimize motion artifacts.

Rs-fMRI data preprocessing

The rs-fMRI data were preprocessed using the GRETNA toolkit (v2.0)37: (1) Discarding the unstable data of the first 10 time points; (2) Conducting temporal correction and head motion correction (excluding participants with displacement > 2 mm or rotation > 2°, calculating the mean frame displacement (mean FD) for the remaining subjects); (3) Normalizing the T1-weighted images and rs-fMRI data to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space; (4) Using a 0.01–0.10 Hz band-pass filter to remove high-frequency physiological noise and low-frequency drift; (5) Regressing out covariates such as white matter signal and cerebrospinal fluid signals. Notably, global signal regression was not performed in this study. This decision was made to maximize the retention of spontaneous neural activity fluctuations associated with the core pathophysiological characteristics of GD, relying instead on the aforementioned combination of strict head motion control and covariate regression to ensure data reliability; (6) Eliminating linear drift.

ALFF values were calculated for each voxel in the whole brain based on unfiltered data; after segmentation and averaging using the aforementioned template, ALFF values for 142 ROIs were obtained.FC: Reflects the correlation of neuronal activity between different brain regions42. After 142 ROIs were identified via segmentation using the aforementioned template, Pearson correlation analysis was performed on their time series, resulting in 10,011 FC values (142 × 141/2).

Radiomics feature extraction and screening

The original feature space constructed above includes 142 ReHo, 142 ALFF, and 10,011 FC features (total initial dimensionality: 10,295). Each feature in this high-dimensional space corresponds to one dimension, which not only leads to enormous computational load in subsequent modeling but also obscures key classification features and increases the risk of overfitting. Therefore, feature screening was performed to address these issues and identify key discriminative features, using Python’s scipy (https://scipy.org/) and scikit-learn packages (https://scikit-learn.org/) in three sequential stages:Independent samples t-test (Filter method): Select features with statistically significant inter-group differences (P < 0.01), removing noise features without discriminative ability.Correlation-based redundancy elimination (Filter method): Calculate the Pearson correlation coefficients between all pairs of features. If the correlation coefficient of any two features is greater than 0.7, separately compute their respective mean correlation coefficients with all other features, and finally remove the feature with the higher mean correlation coefficient.Lasso regression (Embedded method): Perform further screening with group labels as the dependent variable. First, generate a candidate set of alpha values consisting of 50 logarithmically spaced points ranging from 10− 4 to 102 to determine the optimal regularization parameter (alpha) for the model. Then, conduct 5-fold cross-validation to select the optimal alpha that minimizes the mean squared error (MSE) of the regression model. Construct the final Lasso model with this optimal alpha and retain features with non-zero regression coefficients.

Model construction

A Support Vector Machine (SVM) was used as the classifier, and an optimal classification model was constructed through 100 independent repeated experiments. To avoid sampling bias from a single random split and to comprehensively evaluate the model’s robustness under different data distributions, a sequentially varying random seed strategy (random_state = i, where i ranged from 0 to 99) was applied during these 100 experiments, ensuring both robustness and reproducibility. The specific workflow is as follows: In each experiment, the screened labeled classification features were split into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 70%:30% based on the specific random seed; on the training set, grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation was adopted to optimize hyperparameters (the training set was divided into 5 mutually exclusive subsets—each time 4 subsets served as the training set and 1 subset as the validation set, and the optimal hyperparameters were selected after 5 cycles). For the grid search, the preliminary exploration was performed with C ∈ [10− 5,10] (100 uniform candidates, np.linspace) and γ∈[10− 5,10] (100 uniform candidates, np.linspace) with both radial basis function (RBF) and linear kernels included; subsequently, the RBF kernel was fixed for refined optimization, where C at was set to [0.5, 1.0] (30 logarithmic candidates, np.logspace, actual range: 3.16–10) and γ to [− 3.0,− 2.2] (30 logarithmic candidates, np.logspace, actual range: 0.001–0.006). The SVM model was fitted on the complete training set based on the optimal hyperparameters, and the test set was used to evaluate the model’s generalization ability. After 100 repeated experiments, the model with the highest test set accuracy was selected as the optimal model, and the mean and standard deviation of the model performance metrics (sensitivity, specificity, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]) across all experiments were calculated. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was used to analyze feature importance: A Kernel Explainer was constructed using the training set data, and the SHAP value of each feature in the test set was calculated to quantify the contribution of different features to the model’s classification results.

Statistical analysis

Post-hoc power analysis was performed using GPower 3.1 software to verify the statistical rationality of the sample size. SPSS 27.0 (https://www.ibm.com/cn-zh/spss) was used to analyze inter-group differences in clinical data (including sociodemographic information, gaming-related information, DSM-5 assessment results, etc.): The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to determine the normality of numerical data; independent samples t-tests were used for data conforming to a normal distribution, and chi-square tests were used for categorical data. The scipy and scikit-learn packages in Python 3.10 were used for correlation analysis and permutation tests: (1) For the feature with the highest SHAP value, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted with gaming motivations and scores of each DSM-5 item, while Pearson correlation analysis was performed with gaming expenditure, gaming time, and total DSM-5 scores; (2) Permutation test: Sample category labels were randomly shuffled, and the model was retrained. This process was repeated 10,000 times to construct the accuracy distribution of random models. The proportion of cases where the accuracy of the original model was higher than that of random models was calculated to determine the P-value. All statistical tests in this study were two-tailed, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic information

A total of 214 subjects (including 141 GD and 73 healthy controls) were included in the current analyses. Post-hoc power analysis showed that for the intergroup comparison of core radiomic features between GD and HC groups, with a two-tailed α of 0.05, a moderate effect size (d = 0.5) and the actual sample size (141 GD patients, 73 HCs), the achieved statistical power was 0.932 (> 0.8). This indicated that the current sample size was sufficient to detect the true intergroup differences in radiomic features, meeting the statistical requirements for the construction of the classification model and ensuring the reliability of the model’s statistical foundation. Results of inter-group baseline data comparison showed that there were no statistically significant differences between the GD group and the healthy control group in terms of gender (χ2 = 0.01, P = 0.95) and age (t = 1.67, P = 0.10). Statistically significant differences were observed in years of education (t = 4.22, P < 0.001), gaming time (t = − 5.52, P < 0.001), gaming expenditure (t = − 2.08, P = 0.04), and certain gaming motivations (seeking pleasure: χ2 = 23.00, P < 0.001; escaping problems: χ2 = 20.53, P < 0.001; relieving boredom: χ2 = 21.04, P < 0.001). Specifically, the GD group had fewer years of education, longer gaming time, higher gaming expenditure, stronger motivations for seeking pleasure and escaping problems, and weaker motivations for relieving boredom. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in the “maintaining relationships” motivation (χ2 = 1.31, P = 0.25). Detailed data are shown in Table 1.

Classification features

Through a three-stage radiomics features screening, the initial 10,295 imaging features were screened, ultimately yielding 67 classification features, including 66 FC features (98.5%) and 1 ALFF feature (1.5%). These features are highly concentrated in typical brain functional networks, including the DMN, ECN, Ventral Attention Network, and SN. Performance of the classification model

After optimizing hyperparameters using 5-fold cross-validation (embedded in grid search, with average validation accuracy as the evaluation index for parameter selection), the optimal parameters of the SVM classification model were as follows: the RBF was used as the kernel, with a penalty coefficient C = 8.53 and a kernel parameter γ = 2.43 × 10− 3.

The classification performance metrics of this model on the test set were as follows: accuracy of 0.86 ± 0.04 (range 0.78–0.95), sensitivity of 0.93 ± 0.04 (range 0.81–1.00), specificity of 0.75 ± 0.08 (range 0.53–0.91), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.92 ± 0.03 (range 0.86–0.99). In this study, the feature with the highest SHAP value was identified as the FC between the right precuneus and the left ACC. Detailed data are shown in Fig. 3. Correlation analysis was conducted between this FC value and gaming motivations, gaming expenditure, gaming time, individual DSM-5 items, and total DSM-5 score. The results showed that this FC value was positively correlated with DSM-5 Item 2 (withdrawal symptoms) (r = 0.20, P = 0.004), DSM-5 Item 3 (tolerance symptoms) (r = 0.14, P = 0.04), DSM-5 Item 6 (persistent engagement despite knowledge of harm) (r = 0.16, P = 0.02), and DSM-5 Item 8 (use to escape problems or relieve negative emotions) (r = 0.21, P = 0.002). It was also positively correlated with the total DSM-5 diagnostic score (r = 0.18, P = 0.01).

Discussion

This study employed an rs-fMRI radiomics approach to develop a machine learning model that can effectively distinguish between adolescents with GD and healthy controls. Based on the 67 screened brain functional features (primarily FC), the model exhibited strong classification performance on the test set (accuracy of 86%, AUC of 0.92) and passed the permutation test (P < 0.001), confirming that the classification results were not obtained randomly. SHAP interpretation showed that the FC between the right precuneus and left ACC made the greatest contribution to classification. More importantly, the strength of this connectivity was significantly positively correlated with the severity of GD symptoms as assessed by the DSM-5. This suggests that this FC not only has diagnostic discriminative potential but may also reflect the clinical severity of this disorder.

In this study, a radiomics-based auxiliary diagnostic model for GD was constructed using rs-fMRI features, achieving an accuracy of 86% (AUC = 0.92) in GD identification. Previous studies have made advances in developing discriminative models between GD and healthy people by integrating structural or single-dimensional fMRI features, but their performance is limited. Structural features-based models (e.g., T1-weighted imaging gray matter and diffusion tensor imaging white matter features) only reached 73% accuracy35, and subcortical structural feature-based models required additional clinical variables (e.g., gender, internet addiction scores) to achieve satisfactory efficacy (C-index = 0.987)36. Single-dimensional fMRI feature-based models (e.g., FC or ReHo alone) also showed limited performance (78.8% and 82.7% accuracy, respectively)44,45, due to the subjective selection of single indicators that fail to comprehensively reflect brain activity and network connectivity abnormalities in GD, compromising model classification performance and generalization ability. In contrast, this study directly utilized multi-dimensional rs-fMRI features and adopted radiomics methods to systematically and unbiasedly screen the most discriminative quantitative features from massive imaging features. In addition, permutation test this research used is well-suited for the heterogeneous rs-fMRI functional connectivity data and limited adolescent sample size, and when combined with 5-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter optimization, 10,000 permutations robustly verify that the model’s performance derives from genuine feature-label correlations rather than random chance. The finally constructed model exhibits superior stability and generalization ability, offering a more reliable technical support for the auxiliary diagnosis of GD.

Further network attribution analysis revealed that the above-mentioned 67 features do not exist in isolation, but are highly concentrated in typical brain functional networks, including the DMN, ECN, SN. These features also involve core nodes of the SN, such as the ACC and insula43,46. These findings point to functional impairments within the classic “triple-network model” in addiction neuroscience21,47. Specifically, the core function of the DMN is to support self-referential processing, internal thinking, and memory retrieval48,49. In this study, DMN-related features (e.g., abnormal connectivity between the precuneus and ventromedial prefrontal cortex) were highly consistent with the clinical manifestations of GD: excessive immersion in gaming-related internal activities (e.g., repeatedly reliving gaming experiences)50. This suggests that DMN dysfunction may be the neural basis for patients’ difficulty in escaping “gaming-related mental representations”. In contrast, the ECN is primarily responsible for cognitive control, impulse inhibition, and goal-directed decision-making51. The abnormal connectivity of nodes such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex in this study reveals that GD patients have reduced ability to regulate their gaming behaviors, thus failing to effectively inhibit the urge to engage in gaming excessively52,53. The core nodes of the SN—the ACC and insula—are also key features identified in this screening. The main role of this network is to identify and prioritize the processing of internal and external “salient information"54. However, abnormal SN-related FC in GD may lead to excessive attentional bias and emotional responses to gaming cues, while reducing sensitivity to non-gaming information in real life55, ultimately giving gaming-related behaviors much higher priority than other social functions. In summary, the classification features identified in this study collectively depict a picture of extensive dysfunction in brain functional networks among GD patients. In particular, the balance among the DMN, ECN, and SN is disrupted, which supports the hypothesis of abnormal network function in GD.

In the model constructed in this study, the FC between the precuneus and the ACC played a crucial role in the model’s classification performance, indicating that precuneus-ACC FC is a key factor in distinguishing between GD patients and healthy controls. The precuneus is a core component of the DMN and is closely associated with cognitive functions such as self-awareness, episodic memory retrieval, and mental imagery48,56. In the context of GD, precuneus activity may be closely linked to individuals’ intense gaming cravings, repeated mental rehearsal of gaming scenarios, and adoption of the “player-avatar” as part of their identity57–60. Separately, the ACC—acting as the intersection of the ECN and the SN—plays a core role in conflict monitoring, emotional regulation, and impulse control61,62. Impairments in ACC function are a repeatedly reported phenomenon in many addictive behaviors, and are particularly closely associated with difficulties in inhibiting addictive impulses63. Therefore, abnormalities in the precuneus-ACC FC may reveal the core contradiction of GD patients at the neural level: “an intense gaming desire and intrinsic motivation originating from the precuneus” vs. “a gradual impairment of cognitive control and emotional regulation mediated by the ACC”. This FC dysfunction further embodies the deep-seated internal conflict in patients: the imbalance and conflict between the urge to “play” and the rational control to “stop"64,65. Previous studies have also reported abnormalities in precuneus-ACC FC in patients with GD, and this alteration has been found to be more pronounced in GD patients with comorbid depression66. In addition, studies on other substance addictions have also demonstrated the presence of abnormal precuneus-ACC FC in heroin, areca nut, and nicotine addiction67–69. Among these, one study further indicated that this FC abnormality is associated with nicotine dependence and craving levels69. This suggests that the precuneus-anterior cingulate cortex circuit may possess addiction-related commonality, thereby providing important clues for exploring the universality of addiction mechanisms and identifying cross-addiction intervention targets.

This study found that precuneus-ACC FC was positively correlated with the total DSM-5 score and scores of specific items (e.g., withdrawal, tolerance, loss of control, and escape from negative emotions through gaming). This result indicates this metric may not only help distinguish between pathological and non-pathological gaming behaviors but also show potential in reflecting disease severity. First, the association between precuneus-ACC circuit connectivity and “withdrawal” or “escape from negative emotions” confirms the key role of this neural circuit in emotional regulation disorders66,70. Studies have shown that functional deficits of the ACC in emotional monitoring and regulation may lead individuals to escape unmanageable negative emotions via gaming71,72. Meanwhile, its positive correlation with “tolerance” and “loss of control” further highlights the connectivity’s critical role in abnormal reward mechanisms and executive function deficits. A core feature of GD is abnormal sensitivity of the reward system, along with impaired impulse control and self-regulation73,74. This finding aligns with neurobiological models of addiction, particularly supporting the “reward-driven and control-deficit” dual-pathway mechanism64. Thus, precuneus-ACC FC may not only serve as a biomarker for distinguishing affected from unaffected individuals but also help uncover functional deficits in addictive behaviors, thereby enhancing understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying GD. Based on these findings, future studies could explore whether this connectivity can act as a biomarker for different mental disorders, offering a new perspective for multidimensional symptom assessment of GD.

While this study yielded valuable findings, it has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes inference of a causal relationship between abnormal brain function and GD; future longitudinal studies could track high-risk populations to clarify whether these brain function features are risk factors for GD or consequences of the disorder. Second, the sample was sourced from a single center, and the proposed auxiliary diagnostic model was evaluated solely using internal cross-validation. The lack of an independent external validation cohort may limit the generalizability and robustness of the model when applied to data acquired from different scanners or diverse populations. Validating the model across multiple centers remains a critical next step. Third, the study sample has an unbalanced gender ratio (males account for approximately 93%), which may limit the model’s generalization performance in female populations; future studies should expand the female sample size and construct a gender-balanced sample cohort to improve the model’s universality. Fourth, although common psychiatric comorbidities were excluded, this may also mean the sample does not fully represent the complex comorbid profiles frequently seen in clinical practice. Finally, this study focused primarily on resting-state brain function; future research could integrate task-state fMRI (e.g., gaming cue exposure tasks) or multimodal imaging data (e.g., DTI) to more comprehensively unravel the neural mechanisms of GD. Addressing these limitations will be a key direction for future research, facilitating the development of more reliable and clinically useful neurobiological biomarkers for GD.

Conclusion

In this study, the classification model constructed using rs-fMRI radiomics and machine learning methods can effectively distinguish adolescent GD patients from healthy controls (accuracy: 86%, AUC: 0.92, p < 0.001). The FC between the precuneus and ACC plays a dominant role in the model and is positively correlated with the severity of GD symptoms, suggesting its potential value in diagnostic discrimination and assessment of disease severity. The results at the network level support the key role of functional imbalance among the DMN, ECN, and SN in the pathogenesis of GD. This study innovatively adopted whole-brain multi-dimensional rs-fMRI features combined with radiomics methods to screen the optimal discriminative features, and the constructed pure imaging diagnostic model has both high accuracy and stability, potentially assist in GD identification. Moreover, it further suggests that precuneus-ACC FC might serve as a promising candidate biomarker, providing a potential neural indicator for simultaneous GD diagnosis and disease severity assessment. Although further validation is required through longitudinal, multi-center, and multi-modal studies, this research lays a preliminary foundation for the development of objective biomarkers based on brain functional imaging and for an in-depth understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying GD.
