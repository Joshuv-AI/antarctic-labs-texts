Ultrasound-guided marker implantation as a method to assist in resection of schwannomas: illustrative case
Authors: Xujie Wang, Dan Zhao, Xiaofeng Wu, Jinjin Liu, Yang Gu, Ying Huang, Lizhu Hou, Fenglin Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDA cervical schwannoma originates from neural crest cells and presents as a slow-growing encapsulated mass in the lateral neck.OBSERVATIONSA 30-year-old man presented with arm numbness after thyroid cancer surgery 10 years earlier. Ultrasound revealed a solid mass in the left interscalene space. Prior to surgical intervention, ultrasound-guided marker implantation was performed to assist the surgical team in rapidly detecting the tumor. The final surgical pathology report indicated a schwannoma.LESSONSThis case demonstrates a new method for assisting the surgical team in detecting the tumor rapidly, reducing operative time and minimizing complications. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25455

Schwannoma is a benign tumor that arises from a Schwann cell. It presents as an asymptomatic, painless tumor in the neck.1 While MRI demonstrates the highest sensitivity for schwannoma detection among imaging modalities, it lacks definitive diagnostic criteria and frequently fails to identify the originating nerve.2 Preoperative imaging and fine-needle aspiration pose significant challenges in establishing diagnosis.3 The definitive diagnosis of schwannoma relies on surgical pathology. Conventional surgery provides a limited visual field, making it challenging to rapidly locate small-sized and deep-seated schwannomas. This report describes the use of ultrasound-guided marker implantation to assist surgeons in the precise localization and resection of peripheral nerve tumors. The method is easy to perform, radiation free, inexpensive, and accurate in localization. Also, markers do not damage peripheral nerves.

We report the case of a 30-year-old man who presented to the hospital with arm numbness and suspected metastasis. He had undergone a thyroidectomy 10 years ago. Currently, there was no dizziness, headache, nausea, or vomiting. Ultrasonography revealed a hypoechoic lesion measuring approximately 18 mm × 11 mm in the left interscalene space with well-defined margins and a visible “rat-tail sign,” contiguous with the nerve peripherally (Fig. 1). MRI revealed an abnormal signal in the region of the left brachial plexus lower trunk, measuring approximately 12 mm in length (Fig. 2). The mass demonstrated marked heterogeneous enhancement. These findings were most consistent with a schwannoma. FIG. 1.Preoperative ultrasonograms showing a heterogeneous hypoechoic nodule in the left interscalene space (arrow). Because of the small size and deep location of the tumor, ultrasound-guided marker implantation was used for rapid and precise intraoperative localization. Preoperative contrast-enhanced ultrasound was performed to evaluate the vascularity of the tumor, with findings consistent with those of the earlier contrast-enhanced MRI study. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from the patient.

After ruling out contraindications and reviewing the imaging findings, it was decided that preoperative localization and excision would be preformed. First, the stylet was removed from the intravenous cannula and its soft catheter was used as the marker. The catheter was cut to approximately 8 mm in length. We used a 24-gauge intravenous cannula with an outer diameter of 2.3 mm. Then we threaded a nonabsorbable suture through the soft catheter, which had been incised by 2 mm at both ends and secured. Next, we removed the stylet from the 18-gauge biopsy needle (with an outer diameter of 1.2 mm and an inner diameter of 0.9 mm) and placed the soft catheter of the intravenous cannula inside it from the needle tip. We then reinserted the stylet and advanced it to the tip of the biopsy needle (Fig. 3). FIG. 3.A: Intravenous cannula. Following local anesthesia, protective hydrodissection with normal saline was first established by injecting it into the interscalene space to create a zone during insertion of the marker (Fig. 4). The biopsy needle then penetrated the hydrodissection zone, entering without penetrating the nerve and muscle. The marker was then placed on the surface of the tumor (Fig. 5). Excision was performed under general anesthesia via a transcervical approach along the suture. Once the soft tissue between the clavicular head of the sternocleidomastoid muscle and the interscalene space had been separated, the well-encapsulated mass could be seen alongside the lower trunk of the brachial plexus. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring detected preserved electromyography signals, suggesting the presence of functional neural elements within the tumor capsule. Consequently, intracapsular dissection was performed, with complete preservation of both the neural sheath and native nerve fibers. The operative time was 110 minutes. Discussion

Observations

Approximately 25%–45% of schwannomas occur in the head and neck of patients,4,5 predominantly involving the brachial plexus, cervical plexus, vagus nerve, and cervical sympathetic nerve.6,7 Only about 5% arise from the brachial plexus.8 Patients exhibit no typical clinical symptoms associated with the mass. As the tumor grows, it may cause compression-related symptoms in the surrounding tissues, such as dysphagia, Horner’s syndrome, numbness, and paresthesia. The risk of postoperative nerve injury increases proportionally with tumor size. Early surgical intervention is strongly recommended to prevent potential neurological or vascular complications. Although both MRI and CT studies could help to confirm the diagnosis, MRI provides a better view of the relationship between the schwannoma and its nerve of origin.9–11 Ultrasound is useful not only for preoperative diagnosis, as was done in our case, but also for minimizing unnecessary tissue dissection while ensuring complete resection of the target lesion. Preoperative ultrasound-guided marker implantation assists surgeons in planning the incision. Intraoperatively, the tumor can be found via the interscalene space through the suture, which is especially for small and deep-seated cervical masses. When creating the location marker, the suture was positioned at 2 mm from each end of the soft catheter rather than at its extremities. This prevents the suture from being cut by the needle tip when the soft catheter is loaded into the needle sheath. The externally retained suture allows for rapid localization during the surgery. During preoperative localization, normal saline was used for dissection. The biopsy needle was punctured through the interscalene space to avoid direct advancement through the scalene muscles and minimize trauma to the muscles during both preoperative and operative stages. The marker was then placed on the surface of the mass rather than its center to minimize potential damage to the nerve.12 Meanwhile, given the hypervascular nature of neurogenic tumors, the localization marker was placed on the tumor surface to minimize hemorrhagic interference with the surgical field and compression of the nerve.13 Inserting the marker could also reduce the operatiove time. For traditional brachial plexus schwannoma surgery, a supraclavicular transverse incision is commonly preferred due to the deep anatomical location. Alternatively, an L-shaped incision along the clavicle and lateral border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle may be used. The transcervical approach demonstrates superior outcomes in terms of nerve preservation and reduced vascular compromise, as well as lower overall complication rates.1 For schwannomas located in the carotid triangle, the surgical approach typically involves an incision along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. The preferred method of removing a schwannoma is intracapsular enucleation. Complications are usually transient. According to the study by Valentino et al., intracapsular enucleation preserved the function of the nerve fiber by more than 30% when compared with complete tumor resection.11,14 This case illustrates that, for small, deep-seated schwannomas, preoperative ultrasound-guided marker implantation could be considered. During the preparation, we used an intravenous cannula, nonabsorbable suture, and biopsy needle, which could reduce the financial burden on patients. Other techniques such as methylene blue and radiocolloids are often used to evaluate sentinel lymph nodes in malignant tumor, but they are rarely used to trace schwannoma. Although metallic clips could be used to locate the tumor before the surgery, our method is less expensive and easy to obtain and does not involve radiation. It is worth noting that the marker has an external suture that extends outside the body. This could help the surgeon decide on the best surgical approach. In summary, this is a simple, easy-to-follow, and previously unreported method.

Lessons

This case illustrates that, for small and deep-seated schwannomas, preoperative ultrasound-guided implantation of the marker can be considered. The approach could reduce operative time through rapid tumor localization and minimize iatrogenic injury to surrounding tissues during exploration. We advise patients that early surgical treatment is critical. Preoperative localization combined with appropriate surgical approaches significantly reduces neural injury and decreases complication rates. Our study was limited by the small sample size, and we will conduct a larger study in the future to further demonstrate the advantages of this method.
