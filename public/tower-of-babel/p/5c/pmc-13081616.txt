A new nomogram for predicting the risk of ischemic early neurological deterioration after intravenous thrombolysis in acute ischemic stroke: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Chunhao Mei, Lingzhi Chen, Qin Shi, Xiaoning Guo, Yang Yang, Qingguang Wang, Bojun Han, Tao Fan, Jiajia Chen, Tingting Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to develop and validate a novel nomogram for predicting ischemic early neurological deterioration (END) following intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study that consecutively enrolled patients with AIS who received IVT at two hospitals from January 2020 to June 2025. We employed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression and multivariable logistic regression analyses to identify independent risk factors for ischemic END and to construct the nomogram. The performance of the nomogram was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsAmong the 880 patients included in the study, 18.6% experienced END. We identified six independent risk factors: age, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, systolic blood pressure (SBP), Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR). The nomogram showed a strong ability to discriminate effectively, with AUCs of 0.745 (95% CI, 0.684–0.805) in the training set, 0.722 (95% CI, 0.615–0.829) in the internal validation set, and 0.773 (95% CI, 0.708–0.838) in the external validation set. It also exhibited favorable calibration. DCA confirmed the clinical utility of the model across a wide range of threshold probabilities, from 0.1 to 0.9, in the external validation set.ConclusionThe novel nomogram that incorporates age, NIHSS score, initial SBP, TOAST classification, NLR, and SHR shows promise in predicting the risk of ischemic END after intravenous thrombolysis in stroke patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04802-y.

Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is characterized by its high incidence and significant disability burden [1]. Early Neurological Deterioration (END), a frequent complication following AIS, with an estimated incidence of about 14.1%, markedly adversely affects patient prognosis [2]. Recent advances in reperfusion therapies, particularly intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) and endovascular thrombectomy (EVT), have created new challenges in managing END.

IVT is the most effective guideline-recommended first-line reperfusion therapy [3]. The most severe complication, hemorrhagic transformation (HT), is a well-established cause of END that occurs after IVT [4]. Although several predictive models for END following IVT have been reported [5–7], their accuracy may be affected by the inclusion of HT. A previous study [8] confirmed that the risk factors for hemorrhagic END and ischemic END are distinct. Therefore, there is a clinical need to develop a risk prediction model specifically for ischemic END after IVT.

This study aimed to examine the risk factors associated with ischemic END following IVT. Additionally, it sought to develop a predictive model and evaluate its clinical utility. The ultimate goal is to provide clinicians with a tool for rapid and efficient risk assessment of ischemic END, thereby aiding in clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study population

This multicenter, retrospective study included patients with acute ischemic stroke who received intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) and were admitted to two hospitals: the Jiangyin Clinical College of Xuzhou Medical University (Hospital A) and the Jiangyin Hospital Affiliated to Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Hospital B) between January 2020 and June 2025. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: [1] age greater than 18 years; [2] a diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke [3]; and [3] receipt of IVT. The exclusion criteria included: [1] bridging endovascular therapy (EVT) following IVT; [2] the presence of hemorrhagic transformation, confirmed by head computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) within 72 h of hospitalization; and [3] a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score greater than 26 [9]. This study received approval from the institutional ethics committee and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical baseline data

Demographic variables, including age and sex, along with vascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation (AF), current smoking, and current alcohol consumption, were documented in the medical records. initial systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were recorded at the time of emergency presentation. Onset-to-treatment time (OTT) and door-to-treatment time (DNT) were also noted. Atrial fibrillation was identified through either a previous diagnosis or electrocardiographic evidence obtained during hospitalization. Diabetes mellitus was defined as either a prior diagnosis or an HbA1c level of 6.5% or higher. The admission NIHSS score was recorded upon emergency presentation, followed by reassessments every 4 h for the first 24 h after IVT, and every 8 h from 24 to 72 h afterward. All NIHSS scores were evaluated by certified and trained neurologists or research nurses.

Laboratory results

Fasting venous blood samples were collected at 06:00 the following morning and analyzed for lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides (TG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and HbA1c. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated as neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) was calculated as FBG (mg/dl) / [1.59 × HbA1c (%) – 2.59]. The triglyceride-glucose (TYG) index was computed as ln [TG (mg/dl) × FBG (mg/dl)] / 2.

Imaging data

A follow-up head CT was performed within 24 h after IVT or after an END event to exclude HT. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) was performed within 72 h of admission to identify the infarction site. Symptomatic atherosclerotic stenosis (SAS) was defined as 50–99% stenosis or occlusion of the responsible artery [10], as assessed by combined head and neck Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) upon admission. One radiologist initially evaluated all imaging and then reviewed it by a second, more experienced radiologist; any disagreements were resolved by a senior radiologist at the center. Based on the results of clinical and imaging evaluations, stroke subtypes were classified according to the Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification [11].

Clinical treatment

Treatment modalities were recorded as follows: [1] Antiplatelet therapy: Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) or single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) [2]. Receipt of statin therapy.

Outcome measure

Ischemic END was defined as an increase in NIHSS total score by ≥ 2 points or an increase in the motor score by ≥ 1 point within 72 h after IVT, with HT excluded by CT/MRI [2].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using one-way ANOVA or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test.

Variables with > 5% missing data were excluded from analysis; variables with ≤ 5% missingness were imputed using random forest imputation. The dataset was divided into a training set and an internal validation set (7:3 ratio) from patients at Hospital A, while patients from Hospital B formed the external validation set. For the training set, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression technique was employed for dimensionality reduction and initial predictor selection. Following this, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted on the variables chosen by the LASSO regression to develop a predictive model for END and to create a nomogram. This data-driven approach to feature selection, grounded in large-scale cohort studies, enhances the robustness and reproducibility of the identified risk factors, aligning with current methodological standards in complex disease research [12–15].

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the discriminative power of the nomogram. A calibration plot was generated to assess the agreement between observed and predicted probabilities. A decision curve analysis (DCA) was also performed to determine the net benefit threshold of prediction. Results with a p-value of < 0.05 were considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.2.2).

Results

Participants characteristics

During the study period, 1,231 patients with AIS underwent IVT, among whom 45 received bridging EVT (3.65%), and ultimately 880 cases were enrolled in the study (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of patients from different hospitals are summarized in Table 1. Compared to patients from Hospital A, those from Hospital B were more likely to experience ischemic END (p = 0.031), less frequently diagnosed with diabetes (p = 0.006), and had lower rates of smoking (p = 0.023). Furthermore, patients in Hospital B had significantly higher median levels of LDL (p < 0.001), NLR (p = 0.024), and SHR (p < 0.001). In contrast, they had lower initial SBP (p < 0.001) and DBP (p < 0.001). The substantial differences in various parameters among hospitals highlight the need for independent external validation.

Nomogram construction and validation

Based on these predictor variables, a nomogram was constructed as shown in Fig. 3. Figure 4A displays the AUCs of age, NIHSS score, initial SBP, TOAST classification, NLR, and SHR, which were 0.571, 0.641, 0.637, 0.581, 0.585, and 0.636, respectively. Figure 4B displays the performance evaluation of the nomogram using the AUC-ROC method for both the training and validation sets. In the training set, the AUC reached 0.745 (95% confidence interval: 0.684–0.805). In the internal validation set, the AUC was 0.722 (95% confidence interval: 0.615–0.829). In the external validation set, the AUC was 0.773 (95% confidence interval: 0.708–0.838). The Nomogram’s AUC was greater than that of individual variables, demonstrating that it is the most reliable predictor for patients’ prognosis with severe heat stroke and can be an effective predictive tool.

Discussion

This study focuses on ischemic END due to significant differences between ischemic and hemorrhagic END in terms of mechanisms, timing, and clinical decision points. Mechanisms: Ischemic END is primarily driven by thrombus progression, hemodynamic failure, collateral insufficiency, and inflammatory injury; hemorrhagic END results from blood‑brain barrier disruption and reperfusion hemorrhage. Timing: Hemorrhagic END often peaks within the first 12–24 h after IVT, while ischemic END can occur throughout the 72 h window. Clinical decision points: Ischemic END may be managed with intensification of antiplatelet therapy, statins, or blood pressure optimization; hemorrhagic END requires immediate reversal of antiplatelet and strict blood pressure control.

We utilized LASSO and multivariable logistic regression to identify independent risk factors for ischemic END. The factors we found include age, NIHSS score, initial SBP, TOAST classification, NLR, and SHR. Based on these factors, we developed a nomogram that demonstrated strong performance when validated in an independent external cohort. This supports its potential clinical utility in predicting patient outcomes and guiding management strategies.

Previous studies [16–19] have identified a variety of independent risk factors for END in patients with AIS. These factors encompass demographic characteristics (such as age), vascular risk factors (including smoking and diabetes), baseline clinical measures (like the NIHSS score, SBP, and stroke subtype), laboratory parameters (such as liver function, blood glucose levels, lipid profiles, and inflammatory markers), and neuroimaging markers (including white matter hyperintensities). These investigations have contributed to the development of various risk prediction models. Additionally, for patients undergoing IVT, factors such as prolonged DNT, elevated homocysteine levels, NLR, and the presence of large artery occlusion have been proposed as additional predictors [5, 6]. It is also important to note that the risk factors for ischemic and hemorrhagic END following IVT have been shown to differ significantly [20].

Admission NIHSS score and initial SBP

This study found that both the admission NIHSS score and initial SBP are independent risk factors for ischemic END. This finding aligns with the majority of previous research [5, 7, 20]. A higher initial NIHSS score is a well-established risk factor for END, regardless of whether patients receive reperfusion therapy [16, 21]. This trend holds across various definitions of END, including different thresholds for increases in the NIHSS score (such as 2 or 4 points) [2, 7] and assessment timeframes (ranging from 24 h to 7 days) [2, 22]. Elevated baseline BP exceeding these guidelines’ thresholds has been linked to END and poor long-term outcomes [23, 24]. Early evidence from the SITS-ISTR study [25] demonstrated a positive correlation between SBP elevation and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) risk, with significantly higher sICH rates observed in patients with SBP > 170 mmHg compared to those with SBP 140–150 mmHg. Even after excluding cases related to HT, this study observed a significant association between ischemic END and initial SBP. This relationship may be mediated by impairment of the blood-brain barrier caused by oxidative stress, along with an increased expression of aquaporin-4 (AQP4) [26]. This result confirms and strengthens the established role of initial SBP as a definitive risk factor.

TOAST

The results of this study indicate that among the TOAST subtypes, LAA carries the highest risk of END. This finding aligns with previous research [21]. The same association is observed when predicting long-term outcomes in younger stroke patients [27]. Ischemic stroke caused by LAA can occur through various mechanisms, such as in situ thrombotic occlusion, artery-to-artery embolism, and local branch occlusion [28]. The elevated risk of END in cases of LAA may be due to factors like reduced cerebral blood flow, poor collateral circulation, thrombus progression, and vasogenic edema [29]. These distinct pathophysiological mechanisms contribute to a higher incidence of END. Furthermore, hemorrhagic transformation resulting from reperfusion injury is commonly observed in patients with LAA [30], which may further explain why this subtype is consistently linked to an increased risk of END.

SHR

Admission blood glucose levels [18], HbA1c levels [31], and diabetic status [17] have been identified as risk factors for END in AIS. This study utilized the SHR due to its effectiveness in assessing acute glucose fluctuations under stressful conditions, such as AIS [32]. The SHR has also been successfully applied in research related to all-cause END after IVT [33] and post-stroke pneumonia [34], among other conditions. Our findings further confirm that the SHR is an independent risk factor for END after IVT in the overall study population, aligning with the results reported by Wang L. et al. [33]. The underlying mechanisms remain unclear but may involve the following: (a) direct mechanisms: chronic hyperglycemia induces adaptive antioxidant defenses in tissues and cells [35], such as downregulating aldose reductase expression to mitigate oxidative stress [36]; (b) indirect or mediating mechanisms: while hyperglycemia can induce oxidative stress via reactive oxygen species generation [37], reducing nitric oxide bioavailability and increasing SBP [38]—thereby indirectly elevating END risk—patients with long-term dysglycemia may develop arterial remodeling and increased wall stress [39], blunting the effect of peripheral SBP elevation on microcirculatory pressure, as evidenced in cardiovascular studies [40]. However, the predictive value of SHR may vary between diabetic and non-diabetic patients [34]. Therefore, future research should assess the predictive performance of this model separately in diabetic and non-diabetic populations.

NLR

This study confirms that the NLR is an independent risk factor, aligning with previous research findings [41–43]. It indicates that the inflammatory state represented by NLR is a major contributor to the risk of END. Neutrophils enhance oxidative stress through the release of free radicals [44] and increase levels of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), which can disrupt the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and elevate the risk of cerebral edema [44]. Additionally, specific lymphocyte subsets are involved in important cerebroprotective immunomodulatory processes during ischemic injury, which help reduce infarct volume and improve neurological outcomes [45, 46]. As a composite measure that combines neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, the NLR offers a more comprehensive reflection of cellular immune activity. It enables the stratification of patients based on their overall inflammatory profile and shows improved utility in predicting END after thrombolysis in patients with AIS.

Age

This study identified a correlation between younger age and an increased risk of END after IVT. This “age paradox” stands in contrast to the results of several previous studies [47], although the majority of existing research has not demonstrated a significant association between age and END [5, 6]. A meta-analysis [48] that evaluated risk factors for END after IVT found that while pooled results from 16 studies indicated advanced age was significantly associated with END, seven of the individual studies included suggested a protective effect of younger age (relative risk < 1). These findings highlight substantial variation in the age–END relationship across real-world studies. The discrepancies may be attributed to several factors: [1] The inflammatory response to acute ischemia may be more vigorous in younger patients. A more robust immune system could lead to an exaggerated release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), exacerbating blood-brain barrier disruption. This heightened inflammatory state, as potentially reflected by the NLR, might contribute to ischemic END [2]. Collateral status and hemodynamic reserve may differ by age. While older patients often have more co-morbidities, they may also have developed more robust chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, leading to better collateral circulation. Younger patients, with a sudden large-artery occlusion, might lack this pre-conditioning, making them more susceptible to hemodynamic failure and infarct progression [3]. Post-IVT HT: The proportion of older patients receiving IVT has been increasing annually, but this demographic also faces a higher risk of sICH following thrombolysis. Since previous studies that investigate overall END often do not differentiate based on specific causes (e.g., hemorrhage versus ischemic progression), this confounding effect may obscure the true relationship between age and END.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, including a relatively large sample size (n = 880) that encompasses demographic, clinical, and serological parameters—potentially offering greater predictive value than models based on a single data modality—and its development and validation within a multicenter framework, this study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design inherently carries the risk of selection bias and unmeasured confounding, even though efforts were made to include a comprehensive set of variables. Second, the study population was exclusively recruited from two hospitals in China, which may limit the generalizability of our nomogram to other ethnic groups and healthcare settings. Third, while we strictly defined ischemic END by excluding HT confirmed through follow-up imaging, the inherent limitations of clinical symptom assessments and the predefined reassessment intervals for the NIHSS score may have led to under-detection of fluctuating or transient neurological changes. Finally, certain potential confounding factors, such as detailed information on specific antihypertensive or hypoglycemic medications used during the acute phase, were not considered in the analysis and could have influenced the outcomes. Consistent with prior studies, more comprehensive data should be collected to further enhance the nomogram’s transportability [49].

Conclusion

The novel nomogram, which integrates age, NIHSS score, initial SBP, TOAST classification, NLR, and SHR, demonstrates significant potential for predicting the risk of ischemic END following intravenous thrombolysis in stroke patients. As a readily interpretable tool, it provides individualized risk quantification, aiding clinicians in stratifying patients and guiding post-thrombolysis management.
