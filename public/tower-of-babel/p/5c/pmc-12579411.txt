Effect of melatonin on the osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament stem cells: in vitro study
Authors: Razan Alauti, Areej Mukhtar, Razan Alaqeely
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontal regeneration is a viable treatment, but outcomes remain variable. Incorporating stem cells and bioactive agents could enhance regeneration. Human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) display osteogenic potential, and melatonin, a neurohormone, modulates osteoblast differentiation and stimulates osteogenesis in dental stem cells. However, the influence of melatonin dosage on hPDLSC differentiation is poorly understood.This study aims to evaluate the effects of varying melatonin concentrations on periodontal hPDLSC osteogenic differentiation.MethodshPDLSCs were cultured with melatonin at 0, 0.01, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 100, and 150 µM; viability (AlamarBlue) and differentiation (immunocytochemistry, ELISA, Alizarin Red) were assessed at 14 and 21 days.Results All melatonin‑treated groups exhibited enhanced hPDLSC viability compared to the control. Cell viability decreased dose‑dependently, with the lowest viability in the 100 µM and 150 µM groups. Notably, 20 µM and 50 µM treatments deviated from this trend, showing significant viability increases. Collagen type I expression and calcium mineralization were observed in all groups, increasing over time, with maximal deposits at 21 days versus 14 days. The 20 µM and 50 µM groups demonstrated a statistically significant rise in mineral deposition after 21 days in osteogenic media. Conclusion Melatonin is biocompatible and dose-dependently stimulates hPDLSC viability. Melatonin concentrations of 20 and 50 µM were most effective for osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs. Our results show that melatonin directly accelerated the differentiation of human periodontal stem cells into osteoblasts and might be used as a pharmaceutical to promote bone regeneration.GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT Keywords: Melatonin, Stem cells, Periodontal ligament, Osteogenic, ELISA

Background

Periodontitis, a complex inflammatory condition driven by various factors, can severely damage periodontal tissues such as the gingiva, periodontal ligament, cementum, and alveolar bone. Persistent inflammation often leads to the degradation of alveolar bone, which compromises tooth stability and may result in tooth loss. Recent advancements in periodontal tissue regeneration therapy aim to restore these tissues, but fully recovering their original healthy state remains elusive [1].

Historically, managing periodontal defects, including damage to the periodontal ligament and intrabony defects, has posed significant challenges. From the 1950 s to the 1980 s, resective surgical approaches, often involving osseous recontouring, were standard to reduce pocket depths. Over time, the focus shifted toward regenerative and reconstructive strategies. Modern periodontal regeneration primarily relies on two approaches: placing a “filler” material into the defect to stimulate bone growth. At the same time, the other focuses on guiding specialized periodontal cells to drive the regeneration process [2].

Human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) and tissue engineering have emerged as promising avenues for treating bone defects [3]. Most remarkable about these stem cells is their versatility, being able to differentiate into bone, nerve, cartilage, and fat cells [4]. As mesenchymal stem cells derived from oral tissues, hPDLSCs are easily obtained from the periodontal ligament – a specialized tissue connecting the tooth root to the alveolar bone – and they display a robust osteogenic differentiation capacity [5, 6]. They can form mineralized bone-like tissue in vitro and have even demonstrated the ability to repair alveolar bone defects in periodontitis models, highlighting their osteogenic potential [7]. Moreover, hPDLSCs can give rise to multiple other lineages; for instance, specific bioactive peptides have been shown to promote neuronal differentiation of hPDLSCs, underscoring their neural differentiation potential alongside osteogenesis [8]. Mitochondrial activity is closely linked to cell differentiation [9], such as cell proliferation, mitochondrial dynamics, and biogenesis [10]. Further research has shown that mitochondrial fusion and fission are instrumental for reconfiguring mitochondrial networks during differentiation [11, 12].

Neurohormone (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine), known as melatonin, is a well-known neurohormone primarily produced in the pineal gland from tryptophan, regulating circadian rhythms [13, 14]. Melatonin, however, has multiple physiological and pathological consequences, including anti-inflammatory [15]and antioxidant properties [16] and, here, overarching interference with several physiological processes. Recent studies have shown it regulates cell proliferation and differentiation in different cell types [17].

Melatonin increases osteoblast proliferation and supports the induction of osteoconductive scaffolds [18]. For example, Clafshenkel [19] showed that an animal model of calvarial defects had significantly more bone regeneration when treated with a calcium aluminate-melatonin scaffold. Moreover, Kose [20] showed that periodontitis promotes myeloperoxidase activity, osteoclast numbers, neutrophil density, and RANKL/osteoprotegerin ratio, which may worsen alveolar bone loss [20]. On the other hand, various authors have observed that melatonin administration decreases the levels of myeloperoxidase in the gingival tissue and at the same time increases the stimulation of osteoblast proliferation to deposit bone [21, 22].

In a clinical study, Cutando et al. (2014) applied topical melatonin (1% orabase cream) to the gingiva of diabetic and healthy individuals with periodontitis for 20 days. The treatment significantly improved biochemical markers such as alkaline and acid phosphatase levels, RANKL expression, and clinical parameters, including the gingival index and probing depth [23].

We increasingly recognize melatonin as a potent cytoprotective agent instead of a hormone. It is lipophilic, able to enter the cell membrane and subcellular compartments, and facilitates the synchronization of circadian rhythms in the peripheral tissues and the regulation of bone metabolism and tissue regeneration [24]. At physiological concentrations, Melatonin has also been shown to promote odontogenic differentiation from human dental pulp cells (hDPCs) and modulate immune responses [17, 25].

Findings on melatonin’s effects are also not unanimous. Zheng [11]It has been reported that melatonin inhibited osteogenic differentiation and mitochondrial functions in hPDLSCs [11]at a physiological level. On the contrary, calvo guirado [26] have shown that specific melatonin levels strengthened MSC differentiation into osteoblasts by decreasing membrane markers (CD105, CD90, CD73), increasing ALP production, increasing calcium-like deposits, increasing expression of collagen type I and osteocalcin gene, and decreasing Oct 4 expression [26]. Melatonin, a biocompatible material to promote osteoblastic differentiation and bone regeneration, is underscored by these findings.

Unlike previous studies such as Calvo-Guirado et al. [26], which evaluated melatonin’s effects on general mesenchymal stem cells, our work specifically investigates human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) across a wider and more refined melatonin concentration range (0.01–150 µM), using multiple assays (viability, ELISA, ARS) at two time points, thereby providing a more systematic assessment of melatonin’s dose-dependent influence on osteogenic differentiation.

Regardless, the community remains uncertain about the best melatonin concentration for therapeutic efficacy. The effects of varying melatonin doses on hPDLSCs remain underexplored. This study aims to: (1) evaluate the toxicity of different melatonin concentrations on periodontal ligament stem cells, and (2) assess their impact on osteogenic differentiation. We propose that melatonin concentrations will differentially influence the osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament stem cells.

Methods

Ethical guideline

This research adhered to the guidelines established by King Saud University. Ethical approval was secured from the College of Dentistry Research Center at Riyadh Elm University under approval number “FPGRP/2022/693/794/772.” The study was conducted with the resources and support of the Molecular and Cell Biology Laboratory at the College of Dentistry, King Saud University, in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.

Cell culture and seeding

The cell culture procedures followed the established protocols of the Molecular and Cell Biology Laboratory at King Saud University’s College of Dentistry. Human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) were obtained from ScienCell Company, sourced from human periodontal tissue, cryopreserved, and shipped frozen in vials containing over 5 × 10^5 cells in a 1 ml volume. The hPDLSCs, at passage-8, were cultured in standard growth media and seeded in a T-75 flask (NuncTM Non-treated Flasks, Thermo ScientificTM). The cells were then allowed to proliferate under controlled conditions. Observe cultures using an inverted microscope to determine the degree of desired confluency (80–90%) and confirm the safe integrity of cell morphology and absence of bacterial and fungal contaminants. The media was changed every other day until it reached a confluency of 85% within 14 days of culture. After that, cell counting was done using a hemocytometer (The Bright-LineTM Hemacytometer, Merck KGaA) to ensure that cell proliferation and growth had reached the desired confluency. Subsequently, hPDLSC were seeded with an amount of 250,000 cells into the following groups illustrated in Table 1.

Melatonin was applied at concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 150 µM. The selected concentrations were based on a preliminary dose–response trial performed during study optimization, and they are supported by ranges previously reported in the literature (0.01–100 µM) in similar stem cell models. This range was chosen to encompass both physiologically relevant and pharmacological concentrations in order to identify an optimal concentration window for osteogenic differentiation [27, 28]. The following experimental groups were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; GibcoTM, ThermoFisher) as the standard growth medium for 24 h. The medium was replaced with an osteogenic induction medium composed of basal DMEM supplemented with fetal bovine serum, beta-glycerophosphate, dexamethasone, Vitamin C, Vitamin D, and penicillin-streptomycin. All samples were duplicated for each condition to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

Light microscopy observation

hPDLSCs adhering to plastic surfaces were observed using a Canon EOS 700D camera attached to an Axiovert 40 C (ZEISS) light microscope. The setup allowed for detailed monitoring of culture progression, including changes in cell morphology, adhesion properties on the plastic substrate, and growth dynamics under varying melatonin and dexamethasone (DEX) concentrations. Observations were recorded at 3, 7, 14, and 21 days to assess the cells’ developmental and behavioral responses over time.

P8 cells were seeded into an N75 flask (NuncTM Non-treated Flasks, Thermo ScientificTM). Upon trypsinization of cells, we take a small aliquot of a known 20 mL volume from the cell suspension and place it in a sterile 0.2 mL microtube. Add an equal volume of 0.4% Trypan blue solution (The Bright-LineTM Hemacytometer, Merck KGaA). Clean the surface with 70% ethanol solution and lens paper. Moisten the coverslip with water or alcohol and affix it to the middle of the hemocytometer (Carl Zeiss Axiovert 40 C Imaging Microscope). Add 20 ML of the dye-treated cell suspension and apply it under the coverslip fixed on the hemocytometer. Use a 10X objective and focus on the grid lines in the chamber. Toxicity assay for various concentrations of melatonin

Melatonin solutions were prepared at concentrations of 0.1 nM, 50 nM, 100 nM, and 150 nM, following established protocols from previous studies. This was achieved by dissolving 5 mg of melatonin in 10 ml of ethanol. Human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) were seeded into 96-well, 6-well, and 12-well plates (BIOFIL, TCP, Switzerland) at a density of 250,000 cells per well. The cells were treated with the specified melatonin concentrations on the subsequent day, supplemented with 100 µM osteogenic culture medium. The medium was replaced every two days until the assessment time points at 14 and 21 days. Cell viability was determined using the AlamarBlue (AB) Cell Viability Assay. Test agents were added to each well, followed by 10% AlamarBlue reagent (Serota®, Oxford, UK). Plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in a light-protected cell culture incubator covered with aluminum foil. Fluorescence was measured at 530/25 nm excitation and 590/35 nm emission, with absorbance at 570 nm normalized to 600 nm, using a BioTek® SynergyTM HT microplate reader. Data were analyzed with Gen5 Data Analysis Software (BioTek®, USA). Experiments were conducted in duplicate and repeated twice per group. Initial results showed cell death at 100 µM and 150 µM melatonin, leading to a revised experiment with concentrations of 0.1 nM, 10 nM, 20 nM, 30 nM, 40 nM, and 50 nM, excluding groups 7 and 8. Cells were seeded at 250,000 cells per well in 96-well, 6-well, and 12-well plates (BIOFIL, TCP, Switzerland). Two plates used osteoconductive media, and others used DMEM regular growth media. The next day, osteoconductive plates received melatonin with 100 µL osteogenic media, and DMEM plates received melatonin in regular media, refreshed every two days until 14- and 21-day assessments.

Osteogenic differentiation process

hPDLSCs were seeded in duplicate on 6-well and 12-well plates at 250,000 cells per well and cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium (DMEM with 10% FBS, 0.1 µM dexamethasone [AppliChem, Germany], 10 mM β-glycerophosphate [Sigma, USA], 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid [Sigma, USA], Vitamin D, and penicillin-streptomycin). Cells were exposed to melatonin at 0.01 µM, 10 µM, 20 µM, 30 µM, 40 µM, and 50 µM to evaluate differentiation.

Immunocytochemistry analysis ELISA

Mineralization was assessed by seeding 250,000 cells in duplicate 6-well plates for ELISA at 14 and 21 days, targeting collagen I alpha. The sandwich ELISA kit used pre-coated 96-well plates with capture antibodies and biotin-conjugated detection antibodies. Standards, samples, and detection antibodies were added and washed, followed by HRP-Streptavidin and TMB substrate, which produced a blue-to-yellow color change after adding the stop solution. Absorbance was read at 450 nm (BioTek® SynergyTM HT). Protein extraction involved discarding media, washing with 1 mL ice-cold PBS, adding 1 mL RIPA lysis buffer, and agitating for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were scraped, centrifuged at 2500 rpm (2–8 °C) for 5 min, resuspended in PBS, and lysed with 0.5–1 mL RIPA buffer plus PMSF (1 mmol/L) on ice for 30 min to 1 h, with pipetting or shaking. Alternatively, ultrasonic fragmentation (300 W, 3–5 s, 30-second intervals, 4–5 cycles) was used. Post-lysis, samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm (2–8 °C) for 10 min, and supernatant was stored at −80 °C. For ELISA, 100 µL samples were incubated for 90 min at 37 °C, washed four times with 350 µL wash buffer, and treated with 100 µL biotin-conjugated anti-human collagen I (60 min, 37 °C), followed by four washes. Streptavidin-HRP (100 µL) was added (30 min, 37 °C), washed five times, and 100 µL TMB substrate was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark, turning samples blue. Stop solution (50 µL) was added, turning samples yellow, and absorbance was read at 450 nm (SynergyTM HT Multi-Detection Microplate Reader, BioTek).

Alizarin Red S staining

To assess mineralization in hPDLSC cultures, calcium deposition (mineralized nodules) was qualitatively evaluated using Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining (Osteogenesis Assay Kit, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), which binds selectively to calcium salts, following the manufacturer’s protocol. hPDLSCs were cultured at 250,000 cells per well in duplicate 12-well plates, treated with varying melatonin concentrations and dexamethasone (DEX), and evaluated after 14 and 21 days. The procedure started by removing culture media and washing plates twice with 1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Samples were fixed with 200–300 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS per well for 15 min at room temperature. The fixative was discarded, and wells were washed twice with distilled water (dH2O). Then, 1 mL of 2% ARS solution was added per well, and plates were covered with aluminum foil and incubated for 90 min at room temperature, with gentle swirling every 20 min for even staining. The stain was removed post-incubation, and wells were washed thrice with dH2O. To prevent drying during imaging, 1 mL of dH2O was added to each well. Samples were visualized using phase-contrast microscopy on a Canon inverted light microscope, and images were acquired.

Quantitative assay of ARS

To quantify calcium deposits stained with Alizarin Red S, 800 µL of acetic acid was added to each well, and plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature with gentle shaking. As cells were loosely attached, they were collected by scraping the plates with a cell scraper and transferring the acetic acid into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Tubes were vortexed for 30 s, and 500 µL of mineral oil was added. The tubes were heated to 85 °C for 10 min, cooled on ice for 5 min, and sealed until thoroughly cooled. After centrifugation at 20,000 g for 15 min, 500 µL of supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL tube. Then, 200 µL of 10% ammonium hydroxide was added, and samples were read at 450 nm in a 96-well plate format using a BioTek® SynergyTM HT Multi-Detection Microplate Reader.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in technical duplicates and repeated twice. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics and data visualization were performed using Python 3.11 (NumPy, Pandas, Matplotlib). To account for the small sample size, variability was explored by non-parametric bootstrap resampling (5,000 iterations) to estimate 95% confidence intervals. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was then used to test for main effects of melatonin concentration and culture time, as well as their interaction. For graphical representation, individual data points (replicates) were plotted with connecting lines indicating the mean per group. Error bars were omitted to emphasize relative patterns of change rather than inferential dispersion. Statistical significance was set at p <.05.

Discussion

This study aimed to conduct a comprehensive and rigorous analysis of the intricate correlation between different concentrations of melatonin and their impact on osteogenic differentiation in human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs). Our findings prove its usefulness in this field and offer scientific insights into its mechanisms and applications.

Prior research has established that melatonin can promote osteoblast differentiation and bone formation [29], aligning well with the results of our investigation. Specifically, this study revealed the remarkable ability of melatonin to expedite the commitment phase of osteogenic differentiation in hPDLSCs within a specific concentration range. This implies that melatonin can help speed up bone regeneration for the patient and aid in their recovery. Melatonin has a concentration-dependent, stage-dependent antioxidant role during oocyte development.

This outcome is attributed to different sources of stem cells, melatonin content, and experimental conditions. Many studies have demonstrated the effects of concentration-dependent melatonin on cellular processes. The one among the studies described the antioxidant property of melatonin and the conditional pro-oxidant activities. They demonstrated that melatonin can act as a conditional pro-oxidant and trigger ROS generation in different tumor and normal cells under pharmacological concentrations. This points to the fact that melatonin action varies in concentration [30]. There was no conflicting evidence on these findings in the complete literature review.

Additionally, the origin of stem cells plays a role in the influence of melatonin [31]. In addition, doses of melatonin used in other studies may also account for the differences. Since the type of cells and experimental conditions can make a difference, the optimal dose of melatonin to stimulate cell viability and proliferation could also vary. Variations in experimental conditions, such as culture medium composition, treatment duration, and the cell viability and proliferation assays used to assess cell viability, might also explain some discrepancies.

This study measured the effect of previously tested concentrations of melatonin (1µM, 50 µM, 100 µM, 150 µM) on the cell viability [22]. All groups showed positive reactions to melatonin, where melatonin did not cause an inhibitory effect on cells; however, it showed varying degrees of cell viability. The observations of this study showed very low cell viability counts when cells were exposed to 100 µM and 150 µM of melatonin compared to the other test groups. This could imply that melatonin concentrations of 100 µM and 150 µM have a reduced effect on stem cells, which shifted the focus of the current study to explore a wider range of melatonin concentrations that ranged between the physiological concentration and 50 µM. Subsequently, upon the exposure of hPDLSCs to different melatonin concentrations (1µM, 10µM, 20µM, 30µM, 40µM, and 50µM), the current study demonstrated a pattern of a gradual decrease in the cell viability with increasing melatonin concentration, with an abrupt increase in behavior at 20 µM and 50 µM of melatonin concentration.

This study has investigated how melatonin concentration and time correlate with collagen type I deposition of human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs). Collagen type I is an osteogenic differentiation matrix-independent marker involved in bone development and renewal. In osteogenic differentiation of human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs), the amounts of collagen type I deposited at different times and melatonin levels are shown in Table 1. Deposition of collagen type I on days 14 and 21 was highest in the treatment group to which 50 µM melatonin was administered. On day 21, when the concentration of 10 and 50 µM melatonin was added to the experimental groups, their highest collagen type I deposition was found, and on day 14, when 30 µM melatonin was added, their lowest collagen type I was found.

Concerning collagen type I deposition of hPDLSCs during osteogenic differentiation, these results demonstrate that melatonin can have a concentration-dependent effect on the cells. The fact that the highest collagen type I deposition was found in the control group indicates that the culture condition or endogenous factors can stimulate collagen production from the hPDLSCs. However, the collagen type I deposition in some experimental groups with varied melatonin concentrations was inconsistent in the osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs.

Other research has shown that melatonin is osteogenic, inducing extracellular matrix production and mineralization in osteogenic activity consistent with the increased deposition of collagen type I seen here at 21 days [32, 33]. It was found that melatonin increases Runx2 and alkaline phosphatase expression and osteoblast activity [34, 35]. They can be mediated by activating some signaling pathways, such as the MAPK/ERK pathway [34]. Collagen type I deposition in the 1,10,20, and 50 µM melatonin groups is increased, indicating that the concentration might best induce the process of osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs.

On the contrary, the lowest collagen type I deposition at 14 days with 30 µM melatonin suggests that this concentration may be suboptimal for inducing collagen synthesis during osteogenic differentiation in hPDLSCs. This observation agrees with the findings that the response to melatonin is concentration dependent, i.e., different concentrations give different responses on a cellular level. hPDLSCs may already have their process of collagen synthesis disturbed by high 30 & 40 µM melatonin levels, which is beyond the optimum level. In Table 1, the two-way ANOVA model does not suggest that time of treatment or melatonin concentration significantly affects collagen type I deposition. Therefore, the differences in collagen type I deposition found among the experimental groups cannot be considered statistically significant. This also corroborates the lack of significant differences in collagen type I deposition between the melatonin concentration groups on day 14 and day 21. This implies that amongst the concentrations of melatonin used in this study, no apparent dose-response relationship exists for collagen type I deposition in hPDLSCs. The length of melatonin therapy and the cellular process used for making collagen could also contribute to the variability in deposition of collagen type I.

The present research indicates that melatonin can affect concentration dependently on collagen type I deposition during hPDLSCs’ osteogenic differentiation. From this, we could see that the control group had the most collagen type I deposition, and the groups experimentally subjected to different melatonin concentrations had dissimilar effects. Perhaps the heterogeneity of the deposition of type I collagen is related to melatonin and endogenous components combined with culture parameters, as well as the signaling pathways of osteogenic differentiation that have been involved [36, 37]. Cell adhesion and differentiation can be influenced by the endogenous components, such as growth factors, and the composition of extracellular matrix (ECM), such as fibronectin, vitronectin, and collagen I [38–41]. Variations in collagen deposition between the experimental groups [37, 42–44] might be a result of culture period duration, particular cellular mechanisms responsible for collagen synthesis, or signaling pathways, including MAPK pathways involved in osteogenic differentiation. Further application of melatonin in hPDLSCs to enhance their collagen synthesis and osteogenic differentiation needs additional studies to clarify the mechanism further and refine the use of melatonin.

Calcium deposition is another key marker for osteogenic differentiation that is important for bone regeneration and formation [41, 45]. Although the potential effect of melatonin on osteogenic differentiation and mineralization has been shown [46, 47], the effect of melatonin on calcium deposition in human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSC) remains unknown. Therefore, knowing these effects is important to develop strategies to enhance bone regeneration and tissue engineering applications [34, 37]. The present investigation examined calcium deposition and melatonin levels in hPDLSCs at varying times.

Table 2 shows calcium deposition at different time intervals and the corresponding melatonin level in human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs). Among all the groups, the 50 µM melatonin concentration showed maximum calcium deposition at 21 days and minimum deposition at 21 days with a 40 µM melatonin concentration.

This is consistent with earlier findings that also reported melatonin’s osteogenic activity in bone formation [41, 48] and would indicate an enhanced calcium deposition at 21 days with a 50 µM melatonin level. As they appeared earlier, those responsive effects can be mediated through a particular osteogenesis-related signaling pathway, such as the AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase) pathway [49]. However, the lowest calcium deposition at 21 days at 40 µM melatonin level suggests that this is not the best level to intensify osteogenic differentiation and mineralization in hPDLSCs. Based on the previously shown results, these results are as expected and consistent with the concentration-dependent effects of melatonin, which can be in response to different melatonin concentrations. Therefore, at a concentration of 40 µM, melatonin inhibits or interferes with osteogenic differentiation in hPDLSCs [50].

In addition, time and melatonin concentration did not affect significant levels of calcium deposition as found in the current findings. In other words, the differences in calcium deposition between the groups are not statistically significant. However, things can still be changed if one considers the experimental design that may have provided the statistical power to detect significant effects. Further study is necessary to derive better time and melatonin concentration information that influences the calcium deposition in hPDLSCs. This also agrees with the fact that there was no significant difference between the calcium deposition groups of the melatonin concentrations at day 14 and day 21, respectively. This again indicates that there might not be a definitive learning curve of calcium deposition in hPDLSCs within this tested range of melatonin concentrations.

Nevertheless, the changes in calcium deposition observed may be accounted for by melatonin’s effects on calcium metabolism; it might modulate calcium homeostasis and inhibit calcium deposition of vascular smooth muscle cells [51, 52]. It modulates the function of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, potentially one way in determining the bone resorption-to-bone formation imbalance [53]. It is also held that melatonin can influence mitochondrial dynamics and autophagy, which are related to calcium handling and cellular homeostasis [37]. Further studies are required to determine how much melatonin should be used and at what time to enhance calcium deposition and osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs.

These mineralization assays also provided strong empirical evidence for melatonin function in the terminal steps of osteogenic differentiation. Treatment with melatonin resulted in the formation of a greater density and optimally composed mineralized matrix similar to natural bone tissue. It can also be due to the ability of melatonin to block osteoclast genesis. Melatonin suppresses osteoclast formation, suppressing bone matrix resorption and promoting bone matrix mineralization for enhanced density. Both the quantum yields and quantum yields of OH generation for these excited states are consistent with previous literature on the role of melatonin in coordinating the genetic program controlling osteogenesis [34, 54]. Evidently, in contrast and agreement with these previously published results [24, 34], melatonin plays a vital role in promoting osteogenic differentiation and bone formation. In this research, we further confirmed the current evidence. We demonstrated the properties of periodontal ligament stem cells, showing that the role of melatonin in osteogenic differentiation occurs in a dose-dependent manner. Hence, these results imply that melatonin has promise in the field of regenerative dentistry.

Taken together, our findings suggest that melatonin influences collagen deposition and calcium mineralization in hPDLSCs in a dose-dependent manner. However, these results should be interpreted with caution given several methodological considerations. First, functional validation of osteogenesis, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and osteogenic-related gene expression (e.g., RUNX2, OCN, OPN), was not performed. This limits our ability to fully confirm the regulatory effects of melatonin on hPDLSCs. Second, all experiments were conducted using hPDLSCs at passage 8. While these cells maintained consistent morphology and viability, mesenchymal stem cells are generally thought to exhibit optimal differentiation potential at earlier passages. Third, we did not include an ethanol-only vehicle control, which may have contributed to subtle confounding effects. Fourth, experiments were performed in technical duplicates and repeated twice, rather than with independent biological replicates. This may have contributed to the observed variability, particularly in collagen and calcium assays. Relatedly, relatively large error bars were observed in collagen type I and calcium deposition assays. However, cell lines were used; this variability likely reflects limited replicates and technical factors and underscores the need for additional biological repeats to improve reproducibility. Finally, our study primarily evaluated collagen Iα and mineral deposition, while osteogenesis is a multi-phase process requiring assessment of additional gene and protein markers.

Future studies should therefore incorporate earlier-passage hPDLSCs, include vehicle controls, increase the number of biological replicates, and expand the range of osteogenic markers assessed. These improvements will enhance reproducibility, minimize variability, and provide a more comprehensive understanding of how melatonin regulates the osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs.

Conclusion

In summary, current discoveries support melatonin’s potential to stimulate osteogenic differentiation and bone formation in hPDLSCs. Confirming its dosage-dependent impact highlights its potential in regenerative dentistry as preliminary evidence, presenting a promising avenue for future research and innovative clinical applications in dental tissue engineering.
