Genome-wide rare copy number variations potentially associated with drug resistance in epilepsy
Authors: Aphrodite Chakraborty, Krishan Kumar, Shashank Tripathi, Nitin Yadav, P. Sarat Chandra, Jyotirmoy Banerjee, Aparna Dixit, Manjari Tripathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epilepsy is a common neurological disorder with a strong genetic component. Copy number variations (CNVs) are structural genomic alterations that contribute to normal genomic variation as well as disease phenotypes. Persons with epilepsy reportedly carry a greater CNV burden compared to healthy individuals. These variations often affect crucial epileptogenic genes and pathways and may contribute to drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) which affects one-third of epilepsy patients. However, data on CNVs in DRE is scarce. We used whole genome sequencing technique (10X) to obtain the genomic data of 109 subjects diagnosed with drug-refractory epilepsy (DRE; n = 64) or drug-sensitive epilepsy (DSE; n = 45) and identified five rare exonic autosomal CNVs potentially associated with DRE. Bioinformatics-based analyses indicate that these CNVs may affect several genes, including a number of genes previously reported in epilepsy, general drug response genes, and genes with significant neurobiological roles. Biostatistical analyses show that some CNVs are associated with a lower age of onset of seizures or higher seizure frequency at statistically significant levels. Taken together, the results indicate that rare CNVs may contribute to DRE and highlight genomic variants that are suitable candidates for further evaluation as pharmacogenomic biomarkers and potential future therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42657-y.

Introduction

Globally, around 50 million people are affected by epilepsy, making it one of the most common neurological disorders in the world1. Epilepsy is characterized by an enduring predisposition to generate seizures, often comorbid with neurological, cognitive, and psychiatric conditions2. About one-third of the patients do not respond to treatment with anti-seizure drugs (ASDs), which is the mainstay of epilepsy treatment3. Patients with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), defined by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) as “failure of adequate trials of two tolerated and appropriately chosen AED schedules (whether as monotherapies or in combination) to achieve seizure freedom”4, have a significantly higher risk of premature death, injuries, psychosocial dysfunction, and reduced quality of life in addition to the severe neurobiological, cognitive, psychological, and social consequences associated with epilepsy. Despite the ever-increasing knowledge of molecular mechanisms driving epileptogenesis, leading to the advent of newer and more efficient ASDs, the global burden of DRE remains unchanged5, and the development of more effective therapies is an unmet clinical need.

Previous studies indicate that genetics play an important role in epilepsy and are a strong determinant of drug resistance. Pivotal single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) affecting single genes have been implicated as crucial effectors of drug resistance, suggesting that genetic variants strongly contribute to DRE6. Besides SNPs, copy number variations (CNVs) represent a prominent class of genetic variation wherein large (50 bp to several Mbp) deletions or duplications of DNA often affect several genes simultaneously, as well as influence the activity of other genes. CNVs are increasingly being recognized as an important source of normal and pathogenic genetic variations. Compared to SNPs (~ 1%), CNVs affect a much larger part of the genome (~ 10%)7, and have been found to play a significant role in a wide array of human diseases including nearly all types of cancers, and neurological diseases such as Parkinson’s disease, schizophrenia, mental retardation, and Alzheimer’s disease. Although the pathophysiological investigation of CNVs is a nascent domain, the latest studies have evidenced the promising potential of copy number signatures in disease prognostication and addressing specific clinical and biological questions.

Interestingly, even though CNVs have been recently reported as a risk factor for certain epilepsies8,9, their role in epileptogenesis, and their contribution towards pharmacoresistance in epilepsy, is not entirely clear. In particular, CNVs as risk factors for drug resistance in epilepsy have not yet been identified.

In this study, we analyzed the whole genome sequences of DRE and drug-sensitive epilepsy (DSE) patients generated using the 10X whole genome sequencing (WGS) technique to identify rare autosomal exonic CNVs that may be potentially associated with drug resistance. Additional bioinformatics and statistical analyses were applied to determine the functional and clinical relevance of these variants. The study workflow is depicted in Fig. 1.

Materials and methods

Study participant details

This study was conducted after obtaining the approval of the Institute Ethics Committee, All India Institute of Medical Sciences (IEC-13/02.02.2018, RP-44/2018, OP-38/08.01.2021). Patients for this study were recruited from a tertiary patient care center in North India (AIIMS New Delhi) and underwent detailed clinical analysis. Participants in this study were predominantly based in North India and ethnically matched to a reasonable extent. Study participants with a clinical diagnosis of drug-resistant epilepsy based on the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) definition4, which requires the failure of adequate trials of two appropriately chosen and tolerated ASDs, whether used alone or in combination, to achieve sustained seizure freedom, were classified as DRE. Participants who remained seizure-free for at least two years on an appropriate pharmacotherapeutic regimen were classified as DSE.

Among the DRE group, participants were clinically diagnosed with mesial temporal sclerosis (MTS) or focal cortical dysplasia (FCD), both associated with focal seizures. The DSE group consisted of persons with epilepsy (PWE) who presented either with generalized or focal (with or without secondary generalization) seizures with various etiologies such as calcific lesions, focal gliosis, post-encephalitic sequelae in temporal lobes, and normal imaging. Brief details of patients are provided in (Table 1) in the main text and detailed information is provided in Table S1 (Supplemental_file).

DNA library preparation

Genomic DNA from peripheral blood collected in EDTA tubes was extracted using QIAsymphony Blood DNA mini kit (Cat. No: 931255). Whole-genome libraries were prepared using NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit (Cat. No: E7370L). Shearing of DNA was conducted to generate fragments of 350 bp, followed by end repair, adenylation of 3′ ends, and adapter ligation. At each step the products were purified using AMPure beads (Beckman Coulter, Cat. No: A63882). Adapter sequences were then added onto the ends of DNA fragments to generate paired-end libraries. The resulting adaptor-ligated libraries were purified and added index tags by amplification, followed by purification. Libraries were assessed to check the quality and quantity by using Agilent 2200 Tape station.

10X whole genome sequencing

Prepared libraries were quantified using Qubit High Sensitivity reagent. The obtained libraries were diluted to a final concentration of 2 nm in 10 µl and were subjected to Cluster amplification. Once the cluster generation was completed, the flow cells were loaded onto the sequencer. Sequencing was performed in Hi Seq X10 to generate 2 × 150 bp sequence reads at 10X sequencing depth (~ 30Gb). A minimum of 75% of the sequenced bases was of Q30 value. Sequenced data was processed to generate FASTQ files. CNV calling was performed using a customized pipeline of CANVAS [v.1.25] and the output was obtained as a VCF file that included unfiltered and unannotated CNVs in each individual.

Copy number variation analysis

A number of filters were applied to reduce potential noise, technical artifacts, interpretation challenges, and to minimize redundancy in the data. CNVs with ≥ 80% overlap with each other were merged, generating discrete CNVs with breakpoints encompassing the maximum region. Annotation of these CNVs was conducted via an in-house script for AnnotSV10. CNVs smaller than 50kbp were filtered out. Discrete CNVs having any overlap with the following ENCODE blacklist regions11—known repeat sequences, and telomeric or centromeric regions—which are prone to generating signal artifacts were excluded, since even a small overlap can produce false positives. ENCODE ‘high signal’ annotations, originally developed for ChIP-seq peak-calling, were retained because high read depths in WGS reflect real structural variants rather than mapping artifacts. Due to heterogeneities associated with the interpretation of non-coding regions of the genome, regions containing no exons were filtered out. Further, CNVs in the X and Y chromosomes were filtered out. Next, because CNVs are frequently present in the normal human genome, ‘AF max’ values were used to delineate the variants into common and rare variants with a threshold of ≤ 0.01; if the variant was present in less than 1% of the population, it is classified as a ‘rare’ variant whereas those present in > 1% were classified as ‘common’ polymorphisms. These results were cross-checked using (a) the DGV database by inspecting previously reported known copy number polymorphisms of the same type (deletion or duplication) overlapping ≥ 80% of the region of our target CNVs and calculating their reported population frequency, (b) comparison against population-specific WGS data generated with the same variant processing pipeline from 132 healthy Indian subjects12, and (c) a manual search of previous literature listed in the database to confirm the “rare” variants as an additional measure. Only rare variants were retained for further analyses.

These rare autosomal exonic CNVs were pooled in a group-specific manner, bifurcated into the DRE and DSE groups. To identify the filtered variants significantly enriched in the DRE or DSE group, the Fisher Exact Test13 was applied with Benjamini–Hochberg (B–H) correction14 to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR) for multiple testing at a threshold of 0.05.

These group-enriched CNVs were classified into five categories: benign, possibly-pathogenic, likely-pathogenic, pathogenic, and variant of uncertain significance. The classification was based on a workflow adapted to CNVs in epilepsy15 based on the current understanding of such classification which includes the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines16 and, importantly, additional literature.

The list of RefSeq genes affected by these CNVs was manually extracted from the DGV17, and cross-verified with the list generated by AnnotSV. Characterization of genes in these group-enriched CNVs was carried out by (a) a literature search, and, (b) comparison against gene sets obtained from the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis Database18 and Gene Cards Database19 using the search terms “Epilepsy” and “Seizure” to identify known epilepsy-associated genes, and a set of terms including “ADME” (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion) processes, “pharmacokinetics”, etc., to identify CNV-affected genes involved in general drug responses. The functional and biological relevance of all CNV-affected genes were mined from the Gene Cards Database (https://www.genecards.org/) which provides comprehensive, information on all annotated and predicted human genes from ~ 150 web sources, including genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, genetic, clinical, and functional information. Accordingly, genes were classified as “epilepsy genes” if there were previous reports in epilepsy, “neurological genes” based on their reported or potential neurobiological roles, and “others”. The relevance of “epilepsy genes” was further confirmed by extensive literature review and comparison with “epilepsy gene” datasets from Epilepsy Genes (http://61.152.91.49/EpilepsyGene)20, EpiGad (https://www.epigad.org/)20, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis Database (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) databases, and literature search including perusal of published reviews22 and original articles. Detailed classification and characteristics of the significant CNVs are provided in Table S5 (Supplemental_table).

Gene Ontology (GO) annotations over-represented in the genes affected by group-enriched CNVs were searched using the PANTHER (Protein ANalysis THrough Evolutionary Relationships) classification system (http://www.pantherdb.org/) which was designed to classify proteins (and their genes) to facilitate high-throughput analysis23. The GO biological process complete annotation dataset, which includes both manually curated and electronic annotations, was used to conduct the Statistical Overrepresentation Test. The input was a list of genes affected by a particular type (Deletion/Duplication in the DRE/DSE group). PANTHER provides reference proteome datasets as the default reference list. Following the input, the statistical test is applied to determine whether there is a statistical over- or under-representation of genes/proteins in the test list relative to the reference list. The two statistical methods used in this test—Fisher’s Exact test and binomial test—are both standard statistical methods commonly used for the analysis of such data. For our dataset, we used the Fisher’s Exact Test with the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple testing, the default algorithm for the overrepresentation test. The results were visualized using custom R scripts generated by REVIGO (Reduce+Visualize Gene Ontology; http://revigo.irb.hr/) for building bubble plots of each list. REVIGO is a Web server that summarizes long, unintelligible lists of GO terms by finding a representative subset of the terms using a simple clustering algorithm that relies on semantic similarity measures. Furthermore, REVIGO visualizes this non-redundant GO term set in multiple ways to assist in interpretation: multidimensional scaling and graph-based visualizations accurately render the subdivisions and the semantic relationships in the data, while treemaps and tag clouds are also offered as alternative views24.

Statistical analysis

Fisher Exact Test with B-H FDR correction13,14 was used to identify group-enriched rare exonic autosomal protein-coding CNVs. It is a test of statistical significance which is useful for categorical data that result from classifying data in two different ways. For this test, the CNVs were classified as categorical variables (present/absent) in each patient to obtain adjusted p values (p-adj), and results were considered statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for each group-enriched CNV to quantify the association between CNV presence and DRE status, with statistical significance at p < 0.05 (FDR-adjusted).

The association between clinicopathological variables (age at onset, seizure frequency, seizure duration) and CNV metrics (discrete raw CNV count, filtered CNV count, and presence/absence of each group-enriched CNV) was assessed using appropriate statistical methods selected according to variable type. For these correlation analyses, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the B–H FDR procedure, and adjusted p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Considering the sample size (n > 50), the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test25 was applied to test the normality of continuous data sets. Point biserial tests were applied for continuous and dichotomous categorical variables26, Student t test (parametric) or Mann–Whitney U test (non-parametric) tests were used for group comparisons25,27, Pearson or Spearman correlation was applied for continuous–continuous pairs; to compare two categorical variables with binary outcomes, the Yule coefficient of association was applied28. Before using the contingency coefficient, the Chi-square test of association was applied, and Contingency Coefficients were estimated only if statistically significant results were obtained from the Chi-square test of association. Age, gender, and sequencing batch were included as confounding variables in the statistical models based on evidence from previous studies demonstrating their impact on genetic analyses29,30, and separate sets of correlation analyses were performed to rule out any possible association between the clinicopathological variables of interest and the confounding variables. The full list of tests and variable pairs is provided in Supplementary Table S9, S10.

Since the relationship between rare genomic variants and complex disorders is rarely simplistic and unilateral31,32, we used the multivariate linear regression model25 to measure the degree of the linear relation between the various multidimensional independent and dependent variables. Regression coefficients and standard errors reported were obtained from multivariate linear models fitted on the original (untransformed) outcome values with HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. Results with a value of p ≤ 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant. The advantage of using the multivariate linear regression model lies in its ability to allow the testing of the relationship between multiple variables from all possible angles by estimating the β-coefficient, with their corresponding standard error and p-values, between a pair of independent and dependent variables while adjusting all other variables in the study25. Results

The number and type of CNVs are comparable between the DRE and DSE groups

Genomic DNA from 109 PWE (Table 1), diagnosed with drug-resistant epilepsy (n = 64) or drug-sensitive epilepsy (n = 45), was used to generate whole-genome sequence reads (paired-end, 2 × 150 bp sequence reads) on the Illumina HiSeq X10 platform.

In concordance with previous reports, patients in the DRE group had significantly higher seizure frequency than those in the DSE group33. The DRE group comprises of focal epilepsy cases (100%), all with pathologically confirmed MTS or FCD. The DSE group comprises of a mixture of focal (29%) and generalized (71%) epilepsy cases. The concise clinical data and the brief summary sequencing QC metrics of subjects enrolled in the study is presented in Supplementary Table S1 and S2 (Supplemental_file) respectively.

CNVs with ≥ 80% overlap with each other were merged into a single discrete CNV with breakpoints encompassing the minimum start point and the maximum endpoint. It is a common practice to merge overlapping CNVs into ‘discrete CNVs’34 to ensure the accurate representation of structural variations affecting the same genomic region. This approach simplifies the dataset and aligns with standards in CNV analysis. The average number of discrete CNVs per subject was comparable (p = 0.87) in both groups (Fig. 2A) with 648.7 ± 53.32 CNVs per subject in the DRE group and 653.5 ± 41.59 CNVs per subject in the DSE group. In each group, the number of deletions was slightly higher than the number of duplications, although the difference was statistically non-significant (Fig. 2C). Results are presented in Table 2.

To reduce the risk of including false positives and to focus on high-confidence, clinically impactful variants, a number of filters were applied. Previous studies have highlighted the necessity of analyzing CNVs < 50Kbp and > 50Kbp separately35, and a small in-house study (unpublished data) demonstrated a high percentage of false positives and low true positives for variants < 50Kbp called using CANVAS. Furthermore, CNVs > 50kbp are more likely to have pathogenic potential since they are more likely to disrupt multiple genes. Therefore, only CNVs > 50kbp were included in this study. Secondly, although CNVs in the sex chromosomes, such as those in the X chromosome, have been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders15, the X and Y chromosomes are prone to false positives, leading to potential inflation of the reported frequencies of diagnostically relevant CNVs. Similarly, CNVs in specific ENCODE blacklist regions—(i) segmental duplications, (ii) centromeric and satellite repeats, and (iii) low-complexity repeats (but not ‘high signal’ regions)—are prone to a high degree of artefactual reads. Furthermore, although recent studies have indicated a highly significant role of non-coding genomic variants in epilepsy36, the interpretation and implications of these non-coding variants remain highly heterogeneous. Lastly, common variants tend to have milder effects, as they have been subjected to natural selection in populations, and detecting meaningful associations between common variants and a phenotype typically requires a large sample size due to their smaller individual effect sizes. In contrast, rare variants are more likely to disrupt critical genes or pathways, leading to significant biological consequences. Therefore, we investigated only rare variants that were not reported in more than 1% of the normal population in DGV. Since DGV lacks population-matched data from the Indian population, the rare CNVs were further compared against WGS data from 132 healthy Indian subjects12 to confirm their frequency in the Indian population and only variants with concordant findings were retained. Overall, small CNVs (< 50Kbp), CNVs in ENCODE blacklist regions, common variants, and those in the X and Y chromosomes were filtered out, and we retained 148 rare exonic autosomal “filtered CNVS” in 109 samples for further analysis.

The average number of filtered CNVs per subject was comparable (p = 0.31) between DRE and DSE patients with an average of 27.56 ± 5.16 CNVs in the DRE group and 25.64 ± 3.92 CNVs in the DSE group (Fig. 2B). Similar to the unfiltered CNVs, the number of filtered deletions was slightly higher than duplications at statistically non-significant levels (Fig. 2C).

Several rare exonic autosomal CNVs are more common in DRE patients

Although several CNVs in known epilepsy “hotspots” were identified among the rare exonic autosomal CNVs in our dataset (data not shown), since CNVs present in comparable proportions in both groups were less likely to contribute specifically to pharmacoresistance, despite their potential epileptogenic roles, we focused only on CNVs that were significantly enriched either in the DRE or DSE group. 5/148 rare exonic autosomal CNVs were over-represented in the DRE group as opposed to 2/148 in the DSE group (at p-adj < 0.05). Four of the DRE- enriched CNVs (7q35, 9q11-13, 9q21.11, 15q13.1) showed 2.7–3.5-fold increased odds of drug resistance (all FDR p < 0.05). The 16p12.2-p11.2 locus detected exclusively in DRE (7/64 vs. 0/45) yielded an infinite odds ratio. These findings are tabulated in Table 3 along with previous reports of these variants in various diseases. Notably, most of these variants had breakpoints adjacent to CNVs reported in epilepsy and other neuropsychiatric disorders37–49. Detailed findings are presented in Supplementary Table S5 and S7 (Supplemental_file).

To better understand the potential pathogenic relevance of these variants, the CNVs were classified into five categories (Table 3): benign, possibly-pathogenic, likely-pathogenic, pathogenic, and variant of uncertain significance using the classification criteria implemented by Coppola et al.15. Known epilepsy genes are affected by CNVs in DRE

Overall, the 7 rare exonic autosomal CNVs enriched in either group encompass 111 protein-coding genes. Of these, the DRE-enriched CNVs contain 95 genes (93 duplications, 2 deletions) whereas the DSE-enriched CNVs contained 16 genes (duplications only). To better understand their potential functional relevance, genes previously reported in epilepsy were classified as “epilepsy genes” by comparing them against gene sets obtained from the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis Database (GSEA)18, Gene Cards Database19, and confirmed using Epilepsy Genes Database20, EpiGAD21, supported by literature search including perusal of published reviews22 and original articles. 21/93 genes affected by duplications in the DRE group were “epilepsy genes” (Table 4). The remaining genes were classified either as “neurological genes” (24/95) with a functional role in neurobiological processes but not previously reported in epilepsy, or “others”(50/95). Notably, only the DRE-enriched duplications spanned “epilepsy genes” and “neurological genes” (Table S6; Supplemental_File). DRE-enriched deletions and DSE-enriched CNVs contained “other” genes of unknown significance. Interestingly, when queried for general drug response, 5 genes (3 “epilepsy genes” and 2 “others”) were found to be affected by the DRE-enriched duplications. The distribution of overlapping genes are depicted in Fig. S3 (Supplemental_file) and details of known physiological roles and disease associations of genes affected by DRE- or DSE-enriched CNVs are listed in Supplementary Table S7 (Supplemental_file).

Genes affected by CNVs in DRE show enrichment for sulfation and olfaction-related pathways

To better understand the potential biological implications of the genes within the enriched rare exonic CNVs, the genes affected were subjected to gene ontology (GO) analyses using PANTHER23. Duplications enriched in the DRE group showed statistically significant over-representation of terms related to sulfation and olfaction processes, whereas no meaningful enrichment was observed for genes affected by deletions in DRE or duplications in DSE. Details are represented as bubble plots in Supplementary Fig. S4 (Supplemental_file).

The presence of specific CNVs in DRE is associated with a younger age of onset and/or increased seizure frequency

To test for association with clinical parameters, correlation analyses were performed using appropriate tests depending on the outcome of the different variables. Furthermore, the relationship between the significantly enriched CNVs and clinical parameters (age of onset, seizure frequency, and seizure duration) was tested using multivariate linear regression with three dependent variables with CNVs as a dichotomous independent variable, and age, gender, and sequencing batch as covariates. The large 9q11-13 deletion enriched in the DRE was negatively associated with age of onset, and positively associated with seizure frequency, whereas the large 16p12.2-p11.2 duplication was negatively associated with age of onset. These associations remained after adjusting for heterogeneity arising from age, gender, and sequencing batch. Discussion

Patients with DRE carry the greatest burden of epilepsy treatment50, and the management of DRE remains a serious challenge for clinicians. Over the years, several hypotheses have been proposed to explain drug resistance including disease-related mechanisms (intrinsic severity hypothesis, neural network hypothesis), drug-related mechanisms (pharmacokinetic hypothesis, target hypothesis, and transporter hypothesis), and genetic mechanisms6. Extensive research thus far has generated inadequate evidence regarding the pathophysiology of DRE, further indicating the involvement of variable and multifactorial underlying factors. Genetic alterations have been established as major contributors in both focal and generalized epilepsies51, with clinical studies suggesting that approximately 70–80% of epilepsy cases have one or more underlying genetic components5,52. Numerous genetic variations associated with epilepsy including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)53, indels, and copy number variations (CNVs)5,54 have been catalogued using existing molecular techniques and technological advancements such assequence-based approaches includingmassive parallel sequencing, whole-genome sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, and targeted gene panels. Other factors such as altered gene expression55–57 and epigenetic modifications like DNA methylation, histone acetylation, etc. have been also linked to epilepsy58–61. In this study, we have used Whole Genome Sequencing at 10X depth to examine genome-wide CNVs in 109 epilepsy patients, classified as drug-resistant or drug-sensitive based on their response to ASDs, to identify copy number variations (CNVs) linked to DRE. The findings are limited to rare autosomal exonic CNVs > 50 kb since our methodology excluded X/Y chromosomal variants, CNVs < 50 kb, and non-coding regions due to technical and interpretation limitations detailed in the Methods section.

Previous studies have reported an increased burden of CNVs in epilepsy patients compared to controls across all investigated types of epilepsies9. Recurrent and non-recurrent copy number changes have been reported in various types of epilepsies such as genetic generalized epilepsy (GGE)62, focal epilepsy63, and epileptic encephalopathies64. Moreover, rare CNVs have been strongly implicated in epilepsy and other neuropsychiatric disorders7,65,66. In our study, 3250 unique CNVs were detected of which 148 were rare exonic autosomal variants, many of which had slightly overlapping or adjacent breakpoints to previously reported hotspot CNVs in epilepsy and other neurological disorders.

To identify CNVs relevant to pharmacoresistance, we conducted group-wise analyses of variants between the DRE and DSE groups and observed comparable proportions of average number of discrete raw and filtered CNVs per individual, as well as the proportion of deletions vs. duplications (both discrete and filtered). However, specific rare exonic autosomal variants, with breakpoints adjacent to, but not overlapping, CNVs reported previously in various neuropsychiatric diseases including epilepsy were over-represented in a group-specific manner.

Due to the inter-cohort heterogeneity, it may be argued that one or more of these variants are possibly associated with the type of seizure or underlying pathology/etiology rather than pharmacoresistance. However, statistical testing showed that the DRE-enriched variants (and other CNV metrics) are not significantly correlated to focal or generalized seizure onset type or brain pathology based subgroups in the DRE cohort (Table S3 and S4; Supplemental_file). To avoid misclassification bias, pathology‑based subgroup analyses were restricted to the DRE cohort, where post‑hoc histopathology was available, because the underlying pathology or etiology in the DSE cohort can only be inferred from routine imaging and clinical classification, which are heterogeneous across epilepsy syndromes.

Additional observations that indicate that these group-enriched variants may be potentially associated with DRE include the enrichment of ‘pathogenic’ and ‘likely pathogenic’ variants specifically in the DRE group but not the DSE group. Coppola et al., (2019), based on ACMG criteria and relevant literature, define pathogenic CNVs as those meeting any of the following: (i) size ≥ 3 Mb, (ii) ≥ 80% overlap with known epilepsy-associated CNVs, or (iii) size 1–3 Mb with de novo origin and gene(s) with known matched gain-or-loss-of-function mechanism in epilepsy. The 9q11-13 deletion (15.97 Mb) satisfies criterion (i) and was classified as pathogenic based on its size alone. Although none of the genes affected by this deletion has been linked to epilepsy previously, large deletions of this magnitude are classified as pathogenic as they are likely to disrupt multiple genes by exerting long-range regulatory effects on distant genes. Similarly, the DRE-enriched 16p12.2-p11.2 duplication is classified as pathogenic based on its size. Additionally, it also encompasses multiple known epilepsy-associated genes including CD19, PLK1, and PRKCB. The 15q13.1 duplication (547 Kb) is classified as likely pathogenic because the duplication affects HERC2 (which is absent in population-matched controls) that has been previously reported as loss-of-function in patients with refractory seizures67. Notably, HERC2 is predicted to be a triplosensitive gene (pTriplo = 0.76), indicating high risk for gain-of-function pathogenicity. However, since these classifications are based solely on bioinformatic predictions, further orthogonal validation and replication in independent cohorts are required.

Quantitative association analyses using odds ratios further supported these observations. DRE-enriched CNVs 7q35, 9q11-13, 9q21.11, and 15q13.1 showed significantly increased odds of drug resistance compared to DSE, indicating moderate effect sizes for pharmacoresistance risk. The DRE-enriched 16p12.2-p11.2 CNV yielded a mathematically infinite odds ratio. However, this likely reflects complete separation in group membership (absence in DSE) rather than definitive biological exclusivity. In contrast, no DSE-enriched CNVs showed significant associations with DSE status, suggesting that the identified DRE-enriched variants may exert stronger phenotypic effect.

Additionally, the presence of “epilepsy genes” only in DRE-enriched variants (Table 4) as well as the over-representation of genes encoding proteins with crucial roles in key epileptogenic processes such as neurite growth, signaling pathways, apoptosis, membrane excitability, microtubule stability, AMPA receptor transport, etc. (Supplementary Table S7 (Supplemental_file)) in the DRE group further indicates the potential involvement of these CNVs in pharmacoresistance. Gene ontology analyses also indicate that biologically significant pathways potentially associated with pharmacoresistance in epilepsy may be affected by the genes in DRE-enriched CNVs. For example, the DRE-enriched CNVs are statistically over-represented for the sulfation and olfaction pathways.

Olfactory impairment is a common clinical motif across neurodevelopmental disorders, suggesting that olfactory circuits are particularly vulnerable to disease processes and may provide insight into underlying disease mechanisms. The duplicated genes OR2A1, OR2A2, OR2A5, OR2A7, OR2A12, OR2A14, OR2A25, OR2F1, OR2F2, and OR6B1 constitute the olfactory receptor (OR) gene superfamily, representing a category of G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) involved in odor reception68. Although CNVs are known to be highly prevalent among the human OR genes69, individual duplicated OR genes observed in our DRE cohort are rare in population datasets like gnomAD, wherein the corresponding copy‑gain variants typically occur at allele frequencies between approximately 0.0008% and 0.0174%, and are absent or markedly under‑represented in 132 population‑matched healthy controls, suggesting genuine disease‑associated enrichment rather than simple background polymorphism. The association of olfactory pathways has been established in epilepsy previously70, with indications that smell dysfunction is a key element of temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE). Previous studies report olfactory modulation-associated alterations in excitatory and inhibitory activity in TLE vs. idiopathic generalized epilepsy (IGE), implying that such inhibitory responses may open up new non-pharmacological treatment strategies71. Overall, these findings suggest that OR gene duplications are preferentially enriched in DRE, but whether this enrichment reflects a direct contribution to pharmacoresistance or represents a genomic signature of disease-associated structural variation requires targeted functional investigation.

Sulfonation, on the other hand, is associated with detoxification of xenobiotics and small endogenous substrates by aiding their excretion via the kidneys or bile, and may modulate drug response in epilepsy. Since sulfonation modulates numerous target molecules in the brain, including neurotransmitters (e.g., serotonin, noradrenaline, and dopamine), and influences neurosteroid actions on GABAA, N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) glutaminergic and ơ-opioid receptors, neurological dysfunction is one of the most predominant phenotypes linked to disturbances of sulfate biology72. Sulfate maintenance genes have been linked with neurological phenotypes including seizures, impaired neurodevelopment, and behavioral abnormalities73. However, most human sulfate biology genes have not yet been considered in clinical genetics. The DRE-enriched duplications identified in our study affect the sulfotransferase genes (SULT1A1, the paralogous genes SULT1A3/4) and heparan sulfate proteoglycan (HSPG) genes (paralogous genes HS3ST2/4). These sulfotransferase genes are highly expressed in the cerebrum. SULT1A1 sulfonates phenolic compounds contributing to the detoxification and clearance of drugs and toxins in the brain74, whereas SULT1A3 catalyzes the sulfate conjugation of neurotransmitters such as dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin75. The HSPG family of cell-surface proteins is emerging as key regulators of connectivity, influencing axon guidance, synapse development, and synapse function76. These duplications could alter gene dosage77 and modulate dopamine metabolism whose anti-seizure activity is well-established)78 if accompanied by increased protein levels. Based on currently available evidence, the relationship between CNV and gene expression remains indirect, highlighting the need for direct targeted functional evidence linking sulfotransferase copy number gains to altered ASD metabolism in human patients.

The pharmacokinetics and the transport hypotheses implicate the inadequate concentration of drugs in the epileptic foci due to the overexpression of drug efflux vectors in DRE79, and changes in the properties of drug targets leading to a decrease in drug sensitivity are postulated by the target hypothesis of DRE6,79. The genetic variants hypothesis of DRE further implicates genetic changes associated with drug metabolism as a crucial aspect of drug resistance in epilepsy79. Interestingly, five genes including the CNV-affected sulfotransferase genes and two previously reported “epilepsy genes” CD19 and PLK1 overlap the list of genes reported in general drug response in human diseases. Whereas the role of CD19 (CD19 Molecule) in drug metabolism remains unclear, overexpression or dysregulation of PLK1 (Polo-like kinase 1) has been previously reported in association with resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapies80 by influencing cellular repair and survival mechanisms. Further research is needed to explore its potential role in DRE.

Statistical analyses revealed a significant association between the copy number gain of 16p12.2-11.2 and loss of 9q11-13 with higher seizure frequency. The presence of the large 9q11-13 deletion was also correlated to a lower age of onset of seizures. There was no statistically significant correlation between the DSE-enriched CNVs with any of the clinicopathological parameters. To evaluate whether these associations persisted after accounting for potential confounders and to assess the independence of CNV effects on multiple clinical outcomes simultaneously, we applied multivariate linear regression with age, gender, and sequencing batch as covariates. The 9q11-13 deletion was found to be weakly associated with lower age of onset and higher seizure frequency, whereas the 16p12.2-11.2 was moderately associated with a higher seizure frequency. Since a younger age of onset of seizures and high seizure frequency are among the risk factors most commonly linked to DRE 81, the findings suggest that these variants may be explored in further studies with larger cohorts as potential markers of DRE risk.

Taken together, our results indicate a potential role of rare exonic autosomal CNVs in epilepsy neurobiology contributing to DRE. However, the possibility that some variants may partly reflect underlying epilepsy substrates rather than pharmacoresistance alone cannot be entirely excluded, and will require further investigation in larger, phenotypically well-defined, and matched cohorts. Moreover, in-depth gene function analysis is essential to validate these observations and understand their functional and clinical relevance. The small sample size also limited the scope of the study to MTS or FCD pathologies. Despite being the two most common pathologies associated with focal DRE, there are several other etiologies associated with drug resistance. Studies on larger cohorts of DRE patients with more heterogeneous pathologies are required to draw more definitive conclusions concerning the association of CNVs with DRE. Due to limitations discussed previously, we were unable to evaluate the small CNVs, common variants, CNVs in sex chromosomes, and non-protein-coding CNVs, thereby excluding potential pathogenic variants in these classes. Additionally, even among the rare, autosomal, exonic CNVs analyzed, potentially pathogenic CNVs may have been missed because of an incomplete list of genes associated with epilepsy or other neurological disorders since new disease-associated genes continue to be reported. Therefore, it is important to note that the rare CNVs we identified may not be the sole variants contributing to DRE and there may be other variants contributing to the patient’s phenotype either independently or collectively. Finally, given that the CNVs in this study were ascertained using a single WGS-based pipeline without additional orthogonal assays, the findings must be regarded as a precursor for further exploratory studies to robustly establish their functional and translational value.

Despite these limitations, to the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to provide evidence that rare CNVs are potentially associated with DRE susceptibility—a finding that may inform future investigations into the genetic basis of pharmacoresistance. Robust variant calling strategies (size filtering, artifact-region exclusion, exonic focus) strengthen the dataset quality and the association of CNVs with established epilepsy pathways and putative pharmacoresistance mechanisms suggests biological relevance warranting further investigation. Prospective validation in larger, well-matched cohorts, functional studies, and investigation of additional structural variant classes is required to assess clinical utility and identify the full spectrum of copy number variants underlying DRE.
