Drug‐Induced Acrocyanosis: Highlighting Venlafaxine as a Potential Culprit
Authors: Sujita Manandhar, Arnija Rana, Rabin Baniya, Rojee Shrestha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcrocyanosis is a peripheral vascular disorder characterized by bluish discoloration of the distal extremities. While drug‐induced acrocyanosis has been associated with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), tricyclic antidepressants, and other medications, venlafaxine has not previously been reported as a causative agent. We present a case of a 40‐year‐old female who developed acute acrocyanosis following the initiation of venlafaxine for insomnia. Symptoms appeared within 24 h of intake and progressively involved the hands and feet. Laboratory investigations were largely unremarkable, and other potential causes, including Raynaud's phenomenon and vasculitis, were excluded. Discontinuation of venlafaxine led to complete resolution of symptoms within a week, confirming a strong temporal association. This case underscores the need for clinical awareness of venlafaxine‐induced acrocyanosis as a rare but possible adverse effect, emphasizing timely recognition and management to prevent complications.Keywords: acrocyanosis, adverse drug reaction, Raynaud's phenomenon, serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, vasculitis, venlafaxine

Introduction

Acrocyanosis is a functional peripheral vascular disorder characterized by bluish discoloration of distal extremities commonly affecting the hands, feet, and face [1]. Among the secondary causes of acrocyanosis, drug‐induced cases are often linked to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), tricyclic antidepressants, interferon alfa 2a, and ergotamine [2]. However, we noticed a case of acrocyanosis induced by venlafaxine in a 40‐year‐old female, which resolved upon discontinuation of medication. Therefore, such cases should not be overlooked but reported instead, for awareness and clinical vigilance.

Case History

A 40‐year‐old female was referred from psychiatry to the dermatology outpatient department for her acute onset of bluish discoloration of the palmar aspect of bilateral hands and the plantar aspect of bilateral feet for 2 days. She gives no history of any changes in finger color, numbness, or tingling in response to cold water or stress in the past. Additionally, there is no history of painful redness or burning sensation in her hands or feet when they are warmed. The patient has been taking mirtazapine, alprazolam, and zolpidem for depression for 5 months, thyroxine for hypothyroidism and rosuvastatin for hyperlipidemia for 3 months. She followed up in psychiatry OPD 2 days back with complaints of persistent insomnia for 2 months for which venlafaxine medicine was added. After 24 h of intake of two capsules of 37.5 mg of venlafaxine she noticed bluish discoloration of the palmar aspect of the right hand which was followed by the left hand (Figure 1a) which then progressed to the plantar aspect of bilateral feet over the period of 2 days.

(a) Acrocyanosis after use of venlafaxine (b) after a week of discontinuation of venlafaxine.

Case Examination

On physical examination, the patient was afebrile. Symmetric cyanosis extending from the bilateral wrist joint to the distal phalanges of the bilateral hand and the plantar aspect of the bilateral feet was present. Her hands and feet were noticeably cold and moist, and they blanched on pressure. Her pulse was 85 beats/min, blood pressure was 130/80 mmHg, SpO2 was 97% at room air and all peripheral pulses were palpable.

Investigations and Treatment

The patient's laboratory evaluation revealed hypertriglyceridemia, elevated VLDL, and low HDL levels. All other parameters, including liver function tests, renal function tests, urine routine examination, C‐reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were within normal limits. Antinuclear antibody (ANA) profile and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing were negative (Table 1). Capillaroscopy demonstrated normal findings. Cryoglobulin testing was not performed, as it was not available at our center. Based on the clinical presentation, a diagnosis of venlafaxine‐induced acrocyanosis was suspected. Venlafaxine was promptly discontinued, while the patient's other medications were continued. Following drug withdrawal, her symptoms gradually improved and resolved within 1 week (Figure 1b). At follow‐up visits after 1 month and 6 months, she demonstrated complete and sustained resolution of symptoms.

Discussion

The term acrocyanosis, first introduced by Crocq in 1986, originates from the Greek words akron (extremities) and kyanos (blue discoloration) [2]. It results from spasms or constriction of small arterioles in the hands, feet, nose, and ears. Acrocyanosis can be idiopathic or secondary to systemic, connective tissue, hematological, neurovascular, metabolic disorders, malignancies, infections, toxins, or medications [1].

Drug‐induced cases have been linked to SSRIs, tricyclic antidepressants, norepinephrine, clonidine, interferon, and amphotericin B. Venlafaxine, a serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI), has received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for the treatment of major depressive disorder, panic disorder, and social anxiety disorder [3]. Drug‐induced acrocyanosis is a rare but recognized phenomenon associated with various pharmacological agents. Each drug has unique pathophysiological mechanisms resulting in acral vascular compromise. Beta‐blockers, such as propranolol, are among the more commonly implicated drugs, exerting their effect through β2‐adrenergic receptor blockade, leading to peripheral vasoconstriction [4]. Similarly, ergot alkaloids like ergotamine and bromocriptine are potent vasoconstrictors acting via both α‐adrenergic and serotonergic pathways, often resulting in persistent cyanosis that may progress to ischemia if the drug is not withdrawn promptly [5, 6].

Likewise, chemotherapeutic agents, such as bleomycin and vincristine, have been found to cause asymmetrical acrocyanosis by direct endothelial injury and disruption of vascular tone regulation leading to vasospastic or ischemic signs [7, 8].

Antidepressants, particularly selective serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) like venlafaxine, have been infrequently reported to cause acrocyanosis. The proposed mechanism involves increased synaptic concentrations of serotonin and norepinephrine, both of which possess vasoconstrictive properties, leading to decreased peripheral perfusion and the characteristic bluish discoloration in acral areas [9, 10].

Although Raynaud's phenomenon has been more commonly associated with SSRIs and SNRIs, isolated acrocyanosis without digital ischemia remains exceedingly rare. Interferons, used in autoimmune and neoplastic conditions, may also precipitate vasospastic episodes, possibly through immune‐mediated vascular injury or endothelial dysfunction, and have been reported to mimic Raynaud's or vasculitis [11, 12].

In our case Raynaud's phenomenon and erythromelalgia were kept as differential diagnoses. The patient had no associated findings suggesting vasculitis, erythromelalgia or Raynaud's phenomenon but a strong association was present between the onset of acrocyanosis and the use of venlafaxine. TCA‐induced acrocyanosis was ruled out, as mirtazapine had been started 5 months earlier and was maintained despite symptom resolution. Venlafaxine‐induced acrocyanosis was confirmed based on its strong temporal association and symptom resolution following its discontinuation, despite the continuation of other medications.

In contrast to other drug‐induced acrocyanosis, venlafaxine‐induced acrocyanosis remains poorly documented, with no reports in the literature. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of venlafaxine‐induced isolated acrocyanosis. Recognition of this rare adverse effect is critical, as timely drug withdrawal typically results in full clinical resolution.

The limitations of this case report include the absence of a rechallenge with venlafaxine due to ethical considerations, which reduces the strength of causality confirmation. In addition, cryoglobulin testing could not be performed because of limited availability, leaving some secondary causes unexcluded. These factors should be taken into account when interpreting the findings, and further reports or studies are needed to establish a stronger association between venlafaxine and acrocyanosis.

Conclusion

This case highlights the significance of identifying acrocyanosis as a potential adverse effect associated with venlafaxine use. Although acrocyanosis is uncommon, its occurrence should prompt clinicians to investigate both primary and secondary causes, particularly drug‐related origins. Timely recognition and discontinuation of the causative agent are critical, as is the prompt management of any resulting complications. We emphasize the importance of physicians being vigilant and informed about this possible side effect of venlafaxine.
