How to Plan a Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in a Failed Aortic Homograft
Authors: Joshua G Crane, Michele Gallo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: aorta, aortic valve, complication, valve replacement

An aortic homograft is a cryopreserved, cadaveric aortic valve root. Over time, an aortic homograft degenerates secondary to cellular loss from procurement ischemia, lack of fixative pretreatment, cryopreservation thawing process, and host-dependent immune response.1,2 Patients with homograft degeneration and calcification experience valvular dysfunction—mainly regurgitation. In 50% of patients with aortic homograft degeneration, reoperation is required within 10 years after the index operation.3

Surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) or aortic root replacement in patients with a failing aortic homograft is a technically complicated procedure with significant risk for operative morbidity and mortality.4 The challenging nature of a reoperation on a patient with a prior aortic homograft is due to the repeat sternal entry, dense adhesions, and profound calcification of the conduit with limited aortic cross-clamping targets.4 In patients with aortic homograft valvular disease, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has been used as an alternative to cardiac surgery. Seedek et al4 revealed that patients who underwent TAVR for failing aortic homograft had similar mortality outcomes compared with patients who underwent SAVR. However, the patients who received TAVR had no requirement for reoperation, whereas 14% of the patients who received SAVR required a reoperation.4 Valve-in-valve (ViV) TAVR in a failing aortic homograft offers patients a viable option for replacement of an aortic valve, especially patients with prohibitive risk for open operations, but does not offer the benefit of concomitant operations that SAVR does.5

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Reisinger et al6 present a case of a patient with aortic homograft replacement 25 years prior. The patient presented with aortic regurgitation complicated by left ventricular dilation, pulmonary edema, and heart failure from homograft failure. The patient was deemed a prohibitive risk for surgery and underwent implantation of a 29-mm Evolut FX+ (Medtronic) TAVR system. The following day, the patient experienced severe aortic regurgitation secondary to valve migration. The patient underwent successful repeat TAVR with a 26-mm SAPIEN S3 Ultra RESILIA (Edwards Lifesciences) system. The patient was discharged, and there were no further issues during follow-up.

The authors present an interesting case of ViV TAVR in an aortic homograft followed by a TAVR-in-TAVR. ViV TAVR deployments in stentless bioprosthetic valves, such as a homograft, are known to be complicated by device malposition or migration because of the lack of a frame for anchoring and lack of radiopaque markers and because severe aortic regurgitation can lead to device instability.7 The sizing of the TAVR can be challenging due to the conical frustum shape of a common failing aortic root homograft, defined by the smaller aortic annulus flaring out to a larger left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT).4 This peculiar anatomy is related to the surgical implantation technique. During surgical implantation, the homograft is prepared and trimmed, leaving an approximately 3- to 5-mm cuff of muscular/fibrous LVOT below the annulus. The sutures are then placed through the muscular/fibrous LVOT of the homograft and hence to the crown-shaped anatomical aortic annulus of the recipient.

Reisinger et al6 used the geometric aortic annulus to determine their valve size. Additional data and further studies will be crucial to determine whether sizing the TAVR for a failed homograft should be based on the geometric area of the aortic annulus or the LVOT.4,5

TAVR oversizing historically risks annular rupture, but the heavily scarred and calcified neoannulus of the homograft may provide a rigid structure that prevents rupture, accommodating an oversized valve.4 Other studies have identified that in patients with a failing homograft, a ventricular fixation of 40% is ideal to avoid valve migration and paravalvular leaks.8 A novel technique of flaring the outflow tract was described in severe aortic regurgitation, and further studies will assess its feasibility in cases of homograft failure.9

Furthermore, there is growing evidence that a TAVR-in-TAVR technique could be necessary to treat a failing homograft at the index operation. In fact, several cases of TAVR-in-TAVR in failing aortic homograft have been reported.10,11 TAVR-in-TAVR has shown feasibility with acceptable success rates, low mortality rates, and acceptable mean gradient outcomes.12

We commend the authors on successful treatment of aortic homograft failure using a TAVR as well as a successful TAVR-in-TAVR. Identifying a conical frustum shape in a failed homograft may help to plan and optimize the operative strategy. This case reflects the forward motion of structural heart technology and minimally invasive treatment of high-risk cardiac disease.
