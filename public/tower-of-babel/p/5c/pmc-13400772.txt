Covalent Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitor HZ-A-018 in patients with relapsed or refractory B cell malignancies: A multicenter phase I study
Authors: Guolan Wu, JuYing Wei, Haiyan Yang, Lianlian Fan, Songxia Yu, Jingjing Zhang, Meihua Lin, Huili Zhou, Nana Xu, Miao Hu, Xinglu Zhou, Jie Jin, Wenbin Qian, Jianzhong Shentu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHZ-A-018 represents a highly selective, covalent, novel inhibitor targeting Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK). This multicenter, open-label phase I study enrolled 32 patients with relapsed/refractory B cell malignancies (including diffuse large B cell lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma, Waldenström macroglobulinemia, follicular lymphoma, mantle cell lymphoma, and marginal zone lymphoma) in dose-escalation and -expansion phases. Thirty-one patients received treatment: 15 in the dose-escalation phase (75–550 mg once daily) and 16 in the expansion phase (300 mg once daily). No dose-limiting toxicities or maximum tolerated dose were identified. The most common treatment-emergent adverse events included cytopenias, rash, and hypertension. Among 29 efficacy-evaluable patients, with a median follow-up of 4.9 months, the overall response rate was 44.8%. Pharmacokinetics revealed dose-proportional exposure, and median BTK occupancy in peripheral blood mononuclear cells surpassed 95% across all dose levels at steady state. HZ-A-018 demonstrated acceptable safety and antitumor activity, supporting further clinical development.

Introduction

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) stands out as the most frequent diagnosis globally among hematologic malignancies, where B cells give rise to a substantial share of instances (85%–90%).1,2 In 2020, China reported the highest number of new NHL cases globally, with 92,834 cases, representing 17.1% of the global total, as well as 54,351 deaths. The United States followed with 13.5% of the global new cases.1,3 As the global population continues to age, NHL remains a significant public health concern.

Signals via the B cell receptor (BCR) are transmitted with essential contribution from Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK), which modulates B cell proliferation and activation. As a result, BTK is recognized as a vital target for treatment in B cell NHL (B-NHL).4,5 Ibrutinib received FDA approval in 2013, representing the inaugural BTK inhibitor. It binds irreversibly to BTK’s ATP site, suppressing BCR signaling and tumor cell survival.6 The introduction of BTK inhibitors has substantially transformed therapeutic approaches for various B-NHL subtypes, such as Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM), chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL), and mantle cell lymphoma (MCL).7,8,9,10 Although ibrutinib treatment results in favorable clinical responses, off-target inhibitory activities frequently lead to adverse events (AEs), causing many individuals to cease therapy prematurely. These AEs include bleeding, diarrhea, atrial fibrillation/flutter, and hypertension.10,11 These adverse effects mainly result from off-target inhibition of key kinases, including interleukin-2-inducible T-cell kinase (ITK), B-lymphocyte kinase, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), and tyrosine kinase expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (TEC).12

Regulatory approval for the use of zanubrutinib and acalabrutinib in treating specific lymphoma subtypes has been granted in both the United States and China. These second-generation covalent BTK inhibitors show substantial promise in overcoming the limitations associated with ibrutinib.13 Clinical trials indicate that these selective BTK inhibitors are associated with a reduced incidence of off-target toxicities, particularly atrial fibrillation and bleeding, while maintaining comparable efficacy in treating B cell malignancies.14,15,16

In 2017, as the first-generation BTK inhibitor ibrutinib was being introduced into clinical use in China and newer generation compounds such as zanubrutinib and acalabrutinib were undergoing global clinical trials, HealZen Therapeutics Co., Ltd., initiated its research and development efforts for HZ-A-018, a novel covalent BTK inhibitor. HZ-A-018 incorporates structural optimizations that provide several key advantages. It demonstrates high selectivity, with an IC50 of 4.0 ± 0.7 nM for BTK kinase inhibition, while exhibiting minimal activity against off-target kinases such as ITK, HCK, SRC, and CSK.17 The compound possesses optimized pharmacokinetic properties, characterized by enhanced bioavailability and an extended half-life, which improve its systemic and tissue exposure, enabling less frequent dosing. Preclinical investigations have evidenced the promising efficacy of HZ-A-018, showing significant inhibition of proliferation in diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and MCL cell lines under in vitro conditions, as well as notable suppression of tumor progression in xenograft models of these malignancies, with performance exceeding that of ibrutinib.

HZ-A-018 is a covalent BTK inhibitor that binds irreversibly to cysteine 481 (Cys481), sharing this mechanism with approved covalent BTK inhibitors. Acquired resistance mediated by mutations at this covalent binding site has driven the development of non-covalent BTK inhibitors and emerging BTK degraders.18,19,20,21 However, long-term safety data for non-covalent inhibitors remain limited, and neither these agents nor BTK degraders have been clinically evaluated in central nervous system (CNS) lymphoma. HZ-A-018 demonstrates favorable CNS penetration characteristics, achieving cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) concentrations of 5.94–17.5 ng/mL with a 50% objective response rate (ORR) in an ongoing phase I/II study of R/R CNS lymphoma.22,23 These findings position HZ-A-018 as a distinct therapeutic option that combines the reliability of covalent BTK inhibition with optimized CNS exposure.

This first-in-human phase I trial evaluated HZ-A-018 in a Chinese cohort with R/R B cell malignancies. The study primarily aimed to establish the safety profile, dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), and the maximum tolerated dose (MTD). Secondary endpoints included assessments of the agent’s preliminary antitumor activity, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics.

Results

Patient disposition and baseline characteristics

As of the data cutoff (August 30, 2022), a total of 32 participants had been recruited into the study. Among these, 31 subjects were administered at least a single dose of HZ-A-018 (Figure 1). Within the dose-escalation segment (part I, n = 16), one individual discontinued prior to treatment initiation. The remaining 15 patients received HZ-A-018 once daily across various dosage cohorts: 75 mg (n = 3), 150 mg (n = 3), 300 mg (n = 3), 400 mg (n = 3), and 550 mg (n = 3). During the dose expansion phase, patients received HZ-A-018 at 300 mg once daily (part II, n = 16). Figure 2 presents the subject distribution flowchart. Safety and tolerability

The safety analysis set comprised 31 individuals who received HZ-A-018, with 15 subjects eligible for DLT assessment. Across dosing groups ranging from 75 to 550 mg once daily (QD), no incidents of DLT were documented, and the MTD remained undetermined. Every patient (100%) reported at least one treatment-emergent AE (TEAE), and treatment-related AEs (TRAEs) occurred in 30 patients, accounting for 96.8% of the cohort. Tables 2 and 3 summarize the most frequent TEAEs and TRAEs. Commonly observed TEAEs consisted of leukopenia (41.94%), thrombocytopenia (32.30%), neutropenia (25.80%), anemia (25.80%), rash (19.35%), and hypertension (19.35%). Eleven patients (35.48%) experienced 14 grade ≥3 TRAEs. The most frequently reported grade ≥3 TRAEs were infection (4 subjects, 12.90%; 2 lung infections, 1 urinary tract infection, and 1 herpes zoster) and thrombocytopenia (3 subjects, 9.68%). AEs of special interest (AESIs) at grade 3 or above included infections (12.90%), thrombocytopenia (12.90%), and hypertension (6.45%) (Table 2). Serious AEs (SAEs) occurred in 9 patients (29.03%), accounting for 11 events. These events comprised pneumonia (n = 2), pleural effusion (n = 2), hepatic dysfunction (n = 1), acute bronchitis (n = 1), herpes zoster (n = 1), choledocholithiasis with biliary infection (n = 1), and worsening hypertension (n = 1). One patient died due to severe pneumonia, which was considered possibly related to the study treatment. Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic

Following oral administration across all tested doses (75–550 mg), HZ-A-018 demonstrated rapid absorption and elimination. The time to Cmax was observed at about 1.0 h post-dosing on cycle 1 day 1 and around 2.0 h on cycle 1 day 9 (Figure 3; Table 4). The mean terminal half-life(t1/2) after multiple doses administration was 1.34–5.07 h. Steady state was achieved by day 7, with trough concentrations of HZ-A-018 remaining low (<1 ng/mL) and stable across all subsequent time points (days 8, 9, 14, 21, and 28). After repeated once-daily dosing, the mean accumulation ratio revealed a 1.02- to 1.44-fold rise in AUCτ exposure by day 9 compared with day 1, suggesting minimal accumulation of the drug following multiple administrations. Cmax,ss and AUCτ increased with the dose, with slopes of 0.9693 (90% confidence interval [CI]: 0.4095–1.5291) for Cmax,ss and 1.1662 (90% CI: 0.7374–1.5949) for AUCτ (Figures 4A and 4B). The CLSS/F was similar in all cohorts (Figure 4C). There was no evidence of variation in HZ-A-018 Cmax,ss or AUCτ by sex in 300 mg QD cohort (13 males and 5 females). Circulating tumor DNA mutation profiles of patients

Among the 29 patients with valid targeted NGS data, each patient exhibited at least one circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) mutation. Discussion

In this phase I investigation of HZ-A-018, no DLTs were observed across once-daily doses ranging from 75 to 550 mg. Additionally, the 300 mg administered once daily demonstrated a manageable safety profile. Earlier trials involving ibrutinib frequently reported grade 1 and 2 AEs; gastrointestinal events were observed in as many as 50% of subjects, diarrhea representing the most frequently reported event.24 HZ-A-018 was associated with a lower incidence and severity of such events. Grade 1–2 diarrhea occurred in 2 patients, abdominal pain in 2 patients, and constipation and nausea in one patient each. One case of grade 3 diarrhea was reported but was assessed as unlikely related to the study drug. Cardiovascular AEs, another key safety concern with BTK inhibitors, were infrequent and mild. Hypertension events of any grade occurred in 6 patients (19.4%), all considered unrelated to study treatment; this incidence is comparable with the 5%–19% range reported with approved BTK inhibitors. No grade 3 or higher atrial fibrillation events were observed. This finding contrasts with the 2.5%–8% incidence of such high-grade events documented for approved agents. Hematologic toxicities predominantly included leukopenia and thrombocytopenia. A transient decrease in neutrophil count was observed in seven cases, which was grade 1–2 in six individuals and grade 3 in one patient (3.22%). Grade ≥ 3 thrombocytopenia occurred in 9.68% (3/31). No grade ≥ 3 anemia or febrile neutropenia was observed. By comparison, the incidence of grade ≥ 3 anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia among patients receiving ibrutinib monotherapy is reported to be 5%, 10%–17%, and 5%, respectively.25

Grade ≥ 3 infections included lung infections, acute bronchitis, urinary tract infections, and herpes zoster. The infection rate with HZ-A-018 was comparable with that with ibrutinib (16.1% vs. 17.9%). HBV reactivation was prospectively monitored as a specific safety concern given the known risk with BTK inhibitors. HBV reactivation with ibrutinib has been reported at 9.5%.26,27 A rigorous monitoring protocol was implemented with baseline HBV DNA assessment for all patients (exclusion if > 1000 IU/mL) and monthly liver function monitoring for the first 10 cycles. Patients with prior or current hepatitis B infection underwent HBV DNA monitoring at baseline and every 2 cycles for the first 10 cycles, then every 3 months, with prophylactic antiviral therapy administered per institutional guidelines. A 74-year-old patient experienced HBV reactivation after three cycles of HZ-A-018.28 Before enrollment, HBsAg was negative, HBsAb was positive (33.69 mIU/mL), and serum HBV DNA was undetectable. These findings underscore the importance of close monitoring for HBV reactivation during BTK inhibitor therapy, even when HBsAg is negative.

Anti-tumor efficacy of HZ-A-018 was evidenced in patients with relapsed or refractory B cell malignancies. Given that enrolled subjects were heavily pretreated, complete responses were achieved by six participants (2 in FL, 4 in MCL). However, the phase I design, small sample size, and limited follow-up preclude definitive conclusions, and further study is needed to confirm activity in specific B cell lymphoma subtypes. MCL is an aggressive lymphoma, and current regimens elicit limited ORRs and CRRs. Although ibrutinib was withdrawn by the FDA for the MCL indication, next-generation BTK inhibitors remain important options for treating MCL patients. In patients with R/R MCL, zanubrutinib achieved a high response rate (84% [78% CR]) and prolonged PFS (median, 33.0 months).29 Correspondingly, HZ-A-018 also showed promising efficacy in this population, exhibiting an ORR of 75% and a CRR of 50%.

The pharmacokinetics of HZ-A-018 (75–550 mg) were evaluated following both single and multiple dosing. Based on Cmax and AUC0–24h, the mean accumulation ratio approximated unity, suggesting minimal drug accumulation upon repeated administration, which aligns with its terminal elimination half-life. Supporting its promising oral bioavailability observed in preclinical models, the 300 mg once-daily regimen produced substantial systemic drug exposure. This exposure level is similar to that achieved by zanubrutinib (160 mg twice daily) and ibrutinib (560 mg once daily).6,12,30 Additionally, the half-life of HZ-A-018 is comparable with that of zanubrutinib but exceeds that of acalabrutinib (approximately 4 h versus 1 h).31 The extended half-life facilitates prolonged target coverage, enabling full BTK inhibition in PBMCs, and could potentially enhance the depth and durability of clinical responses.

Currently, the evidence base for BTK inhibitors in CNS lymphoma remains preliminary, with clinical experience largely derived from phase I/II trials and retrospective cohorts characterized by limited sample sizes and absence of comparator arms. HZ-A-018 demonstrates favorable blood-brain barrier penetration,32 achieving CSF concentrations of 5.94–17.5 ng/mL across the 300–600 mg dose range,22 compared with 1.553–3.992 ng/mL for ibrutinib33 and ∼2.94 ng/mL for zanubrutinib.34 Across this drug class, preliminary efficacy has been observed in CNS lymphoma (pooled ORR approximately 73%; 60% with monotherapy vs. 79% with combination regimens), though the durability of response remains modest.35 With a favorable safety-tolerability profile enabling dose escalation to 600 mg QD, HZ-A-018 achieved a 100% ORR (50% CRR; n = 6) as monotherapy in R/R CNSL.22 As with other agents in this therapeutic class, these findings require validation in larger, prospective cohorts, further evaluation is ongoing.

This first-in-human evaluation of HZ-A-018 showed acceptable safety and tolerability, achieved BTK saturation, and completely inhibited BTK activity. These findings support further investigation of HZ-A-018 for R/R B cell malignancies. Sustained receptor occupancy and prolonged pharmacodynamic effects indicate the feasibility of a once-daily regimen. Collectively, the data suggest that this generation of highly selective BTK inhibitors, including HZ-A-018, is safe and effective for B cell malignancies and warrants evaluation in phase II and III trials.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample and the absence of subtype-specific enrollment in the dose-expansion cohort limited confirmation of preliminary efficacy. Future expansion studies in these specific populations are warranted to further characterize the dose-efficacy relationship and confirm therapeutic potential. Pharmacokinetic sampling was also sparse (n = 3 per dose level, except for the 300 mg cohort), reducing the robustness of the pharmacokinetic analyses and indicating the need for validation in a larger cohort. Second, the median follow-up of 4.9 months precluded a reliable assessment of long-term outcomes; at data cutoff, 24% of responders remained progression-free. Longer observation is required to evaluate cumulative toxicity and compare the long-term safety profile with other BTK inhibitors. Lastly, biomarker samples were not collected from patients experiencing disease progression following HZ-A-018 treatment. Consequently, the study did not conduct systematic genetic profiling of resistance-related mutations, such as BTK C481S and PLCG2 mutations, in these progressing lesions. Future studies should prioritize comprehensive genetic analysis of these mutations in progressing lesions to better understand and address treatment resistance.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patients

Patients aged 18 to 75 years were eligible to participate in this study. Eligibility for this study required a diagnosis of mature B-cell lymphoma confirmed either cytologically or histologically according to the WHO 2016 classification criteria. Enrolled cases included CLL/SLL, MCL, WM, DLBCL, FL, and MZL. Eligibility required that participants had received at least one prior line of treatment and presented with disease necessitating further therapy. Additionally, adequate hematologic and organ function, as determined by the investigator, was mandatory. Individuals were excluded from participation if they had a history of central nervous system involvement, had undergone prior treatment with any BTK inhibitor, or were taking concomitant strong inhibitors or inducers of cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A). Complete lists of both inclusion and exclusion criteria are available in supplemental information.

Study design and interventions

Multicenter, open-label, phase I trial tested HZ-A-018 monotherapy in Chinese patients with R/R B-cell malignancies. The trial design comprised two sequential components: escalation of dosage followed by expansion at selected dose levels. Study design

In part I, three patients (or six patients if one of the first three patients experienced a DLT) were randomly assigned to one of the treatment arms. If two or more patients experienced a DLT, dose escalation was stopped, and three additional patients were enrolled at the previous dose level. In part II, 16 patients were enrolled in the arm to assess the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics. DLT, dose-limiting toxicity; QD, once daily; RDFE, recommended dose for expansion.

The initial dose of 75 mg per day in Part 1 was determined based on a comprehensive evaluation. This evaluation took into account the NOAEL, the MRSD, the drug formulation specifications of HZ-A-018, as well as the initial doses used in phase I studies of ibrutinib (2.5 mg/kg per day) and acalabrutinib (100 mg per day). In preclinical repeated-dose toxicity studies in beagle dogs, the NOAEL was established at 25 mg/kg. Using an average adult weight of 60 kg and applying a safety factor of 10, the MRSD was calculated to be 87.6 mg. Considering safety, potential clinical benefits, and the drug formulation specifications of HZ-A-018 (25 mg and 75 mg), the initial dose for the first-in-human phase I study was set at 75 mg.

In the dose-escalation stage, cohorts of three participants were initially assigned to each dosage group, with safety evaluations conducted after four weeks of HZ-A-018 administration. Enrollment at each dose level was temporarily suspended until a comprehensive safety assessment of all current participants (n = 3 to 6) was completed by a safety review committee at the conclusion of cycle 1. This committee included members of the site investigative team and the medical monitor representing HealZen. Escalation to subsequent dose levels was contingent upon the frequency of DLT observed during the initial cycle. If none of the three participants exhibited a DLT at a given dose level, escalation to a higher level was authorized. When a single DLT emerged in three subjects, enrollment of another trio of patients occurred at that identical dosage. A return to the prior dosage became necessary upon observation of DLT in at least two subjects. Treatment-related non-hematological adverse events at grade 3 or above were covered by DLT, with the exception of any-grade alopecia, as well as emesis, diarrhea, or nausea at grade ≥3 that subsided in under 72 h via suitable intervention; grade 4 thrombocytopenia or grade 3 with significant bleeding from a clinical standpoint; grade 4 neutropenia lasting at least 7 days; febrile neutropenia; plus grade 4 anemia. In cycle 1, the highest dosage where dose-limiting toxicity affected fewer than one-third of subjects constituted the maximum tolerated dose. Dose modifications were prohibited throughout the DLT monitoring phase (cycle 1, lasting 28 days). Beyond this interval, adjustments such as temporary interruption, dose reduction, or treatment termination were allowable for addressing AEs.

During the dose-expansion phase, patients with R/R B-cell malignancies reveived HZ-A-018 300 mg once daily. Throughout each segment of the trial, participants were administered one oral dose of HZ-A-018 on the initial day of cycle 1 to facilitate 48-h pharmacokinetic profiling after a single application. Starting on day 3 of the first cycle, daily dosing was maintained following a 26-day schedule (from day 3 to day 28) until discontinuation triggers emerged—such as toxicity, disease progression, voluntary withdrawal, or investigator-determined cessation.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Council for Harmonization (ICH) Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines, and applicable regulatory requirements. The study protocol and informed consent forms were reviewed and approved by the independent ethics committee at the First Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (the lead center; Approval No. 2019-EC-153). Each participating center additionally obtained approval from its local institutional review board prior to patient enrollment (Table S1). Written informed consent was obtained from every participant prior to enrollment.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoints encompassed the evaluation of the safety profile and the establishment of DLT, and MTD. The additional study objectives comprised evaluation of pharmacokinetic behavior, investigation of pharmacodynamic responses, and assessment of initial antitumor efficacy. Tumor assessments were performed at baseline and every 8 weeks. Efficacy was evaluated according to the criteria established by the International Workshop on WM 2012, the International Workshop on CLL/SLL 2018, and the Lugano 2014 guidelines for other non-Hodgkin’s lymphomas.36,37,38 Preliminary anti-tumor efficacy was assessed based on ORR, PFS, and DOR.

Sample size determination

In the dose-escalation segment, the number of participants depended on both observed toxicities and predefined dosage tiers. For the expansion phase, sample size estimation assumed an ORR of 37.5%. Enrollment of 16 subjects would yield a 95% confidence interval (CI) for ORR ranging from 15.2% to 64.6%, a range considered adequate to indicate antitumor activity of HZ-A-018.

Method details

Safety assessment

Subjects underwent physical examination, assessment of vital signs, hematology, chemistry, and coagulation, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group assessment, and 12-lead electrocardiogram monthly and at the end of treatment. AE severity was evaluated in accordance with NCI CTCAE v5.0.The safety set (SS) comprised all subjects administered a minimum of one HZ-A-018 dose. The DLT-evaluable population encompassed participants in part I who either exhibited a DLT or received at least 75% of the planned study drug during the first cycle. AEs that emerged during treatment and TRAEs were documented. AESIs included major bleeding (defined as serious, grade ≥3, or any-grade CNS hemorrhage), cytopenias, infections, hypertension, diarrhea, atrial arrhythmias, tumor lysis syndrome, and second primary malignancies.

Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic assessments

Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic assessments were conducted in the first cycle. For pharmacokinetic assessment, blood specimens were obtained prior to HZ-A-018 administration and at intervals of 20 min, 40 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 5 h, 8 h, 10 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h after the initial dose. Under repeated dosing conditions (from day 3 onward), sampling occurred before dosing and at 20 min, 40 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 5 h, 8 h, 10 h, 12 h, and 24 h on day 9 (steady-state). Trough concentrations were assessed right before administration on days 7, 8, 14, 21, and 28. A validated HPLC-MS/MS assay enabled quantification of HZ-A-018 concentrations within plasma, establishing a lower limit of quantification at 0.5 ng/mL. Derivation of pharmacokinetic parameters occurred through noncompartmental evaluation conducted via Phoenix WinNonlin software.

Serial blood samples were collected to evaluate BTK occupancy in PBMCs. Pre-dose samples were obtained on days 1, 2, 3, 14, 21, and 28 to characterize trough occupancy. Post-dose samples were drawn at 4 h on days 1 and 9 to capture peak effects. BTK occupancy was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay.Free BTK proteins in the cell lysates were captured by a biotinylated-HZ-A-018 probe coupled to plates coated with Streptavidin.Total BTK protein levels were measured in a parallel assay using plates coated with with capture antibody and detected by anti-BTK antibody (Abcam).

Mutational analysis

Before initiating treatment, peripheral blood specimens amounting to 10 mL were obtained in tubes that contained EDTA. Following mild agitation, these specimens reached the central laboratory on the identical day to undergo examination. Isolation of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) took place at the central laboratory. Utilization of a targeted NGS-oriented 59-gene panel (Shanghai Rightongene Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) facilitated the identification of mutation patterns in every cfDNA specimen, employing an Illumina NovaSeq 5000 for sequencing (2 × 150-paired-end reads). Splice sites along with coding exons comprised the targeted areas in each gene test.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For clinical data, SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC) was utilized to conduct all statistical evaluations. Results for pre-specified cohorts are summarized as counts with percentages and continuous data were summarized as medians with ranges. The EAS comprised all subjects from the safety population who received at least one valid on-treatment tumor assessment. Using the Clopper–Pearson approach, ORR and its 95% CI were estimated. Time-to-event endpoint analyses, presented as curves with quartiles, employed the Kaplan–Meier technique.

Blood drug concentrations were transformed by natural logarithm, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess whether a steady state was reached. Dose proportionality was assessed utilizing a graphical analysis of dose-adjusted AUC and Cmax, as well as the power model technique. Skewed PK parameters were log-transformed, and PK parameters were compared between groups using Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA.
