Consequences of weight cycling in patients with obesity and the potential impact of high protein diet: a health economic assessment from a US societal perspective
Authors: Bjoern Schwander, Kirk W Kerr, Dominique Williams, Suela Sulo, W Scott Butsch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesObesity is a leading risk factor for global morbidity and mortality, associated with significant healthcare costs that exceed US$260 billion annually in the USA. Weight cycling, the repeated pattern of intentional weight loss followed by unintentional regain, can exacerbate obesity-related health complications. This study aimed to assess the health economic consequences of weight cycling in patients with obesity defined by a body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m², comparing ‘weight cyclers’ with ‘non-cyclers’, and evaluating the impact of a high-protein oral nutritional supplement (HP-ONS) as a maintenance strategy following weight loss via glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs).DesignLifetime state transition modelling study with monthly cycles to simulate obesity-associated disease progression.SettingUS healthcare system; societal perspective.ParticipantsSimulated cohort of adult patients with obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m²), stratified by weight cycling status.InterventionsWeight loss via GLP-1RAs with or without HP-ONS for weight maintenance.Primary and secondary outcome measuresKey outcomes included costs per obesity-related event avoided, life years (LYs) gained and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained, calculated from a US societal perspective. Transition probabilities for disease states were derived from meta-analyses and adjusted for weight cycling and other relevant risks. Costs and health utilities were based on published US studies with future costs discounted at 3% per year. Uncertainty was investigated by deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses.ResultsNon-cyclers experienced 0.090 fewer obesity-associated events, gained 0.602 LYs and achieved 0.518 QALYs compared with cyclers, resulting in total cost savings of approximately US$4592 per patient. In the second scenario, the combination of GLP-1RA treatment and HP-ONS for weight maintenance yielded effective health outcomes with a cost-effectiveness ratio of US$24 276 per QALY gained, well within accepted cost-effectiveness thresholds in the USA, ranging from US$100 000 to US$150 000 per QALY gained.ConclusionWeight cycling significantly impacts health and economic outcomes for patients with obesity, underscoring the need for effective weight management programmes, including the use of HP-ONS focused on sustained weight maintenance after weight loss to curtail associated risks and costs.

Introduction

Obesity is a highly prevalent chronic disease characterised by excessive fat accumulation or distribution that presents a risk to health and requires lifelong care, which has significant consequences for individuals and society at large.1 It is now recognised as a chronic condition influenced by various factors, including genetic, environmental and behavioural elements, rather than the result of poor dietary choices or limited self-control.1 Obesity is a leading risk factor for global morbidity and mortality, contributing to numerous chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes (T2D), cardiovascular disease (CVD) and certain types of cancer.2 In the USA alone, the obesity epidemic incurs substantial healthcare costs, estimated to exceed US$260 billion annually, driven by obesity-related health complications.3

Given the high burden that obesity places on patients, society and healthcare systems, there is an urgent need for effective treatments to help mitigate its effects. While weight loss might seem like a straightforward solution to combat obesity and associated health issues, achieving and maintaining lasting weight reduction proves to be a considerable challenge for many individuals. After weight loss, it is common for people to experience weight recurrence, entering weight cycling—the intentional act of losing weight followed by unintentional weight gain. This phenomenon is well documented in numerous studies and is associated with systemic and chronic inflammation, negatively impacting overall health outcomes.4

Meta-analyses indicate that weight cycling is prevalent among individuals with obesity and is linked to an increased risk of various obesity-associated diseases, including myocardial infarction (MI), stroke and premature cardiovascular mortality.57 However, despite the significant health implications of weight cycling, its health economic consequences remain largely underexplored.

This study aims to address this research gap by developing a state transition model (STM) to estimate the health economic implications of weight cycling among patients with obesity from a US societal perspective. Consequently, this manuscript conducts a health economic comparison between individuals who are weight cyclers (after initial weight loss) and those who maintain or continuously lose weight, referred to as ‘non-cyclers’. Additionally, the model explores the potential benefits of a high-protein diet as a strategy for weight maintenance following initial weight loss achieved through a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA). This comparison contrasts the outcomes of GLP-1RA treatment alone with those that combine GLP-1RAs with high-protein oral nutritional supplements (HP-ONS) for sustained weight maintenance.

Methods

The health economic assessment model was developed in Microsoft Excel to quantify the health outcomes and economic effects of weight cycling and evaluate the cost-effectiveness of a high-protein diet for weight maintenance to mitigate weight recurrence and weight cycling risks.

State transitions

A STM, also known as a Markov model, was created to simulate the progression of obesity-associated diseases over time by defining distinct health states with transitions occurring at fixed cycles. This model is especially valuable for chronic diseases like obesity because it allows for calculations of cumulative costs and health benefits. Using a lifetime horizon (with monthly cycles), the model captures the long-term impacts of obesity-related diseases on patient survival and healthcare costs.8 The health states in this STM include disease-free, T2D, past and acute cardiovascular events (MI and stroke), kidney cancer and death. It should be noted that after transitioning from kidney cancer to a cardiovascular event, cancer-related transitions are no longer explicitly modelled. This simplification reflects the predominance of cardiovascular risk in this population and was chosen to maintain tractability, although it may lead to under-representation of long-term cancer effects.

These diseases were selected because they are influenced by weight cycling57 and are recommended by an expert consensus on obesity modelling.9 The STM structure showing health states differentiated by diabetes status and the transitions possible between them during each simulation cycle is illustrated in figure 1.

Transition probabilities

Transition probabilities between health states were calculated monthly, adjusting base disease risks for factors such as age, body mass index (BMI), weight-cycling status and T2D status of the patient cohort. Base risks were derived from US national disease statistics, converting annual case numbers to monthly probabilities (online supplemental appendix table 1-1). These were refined using relative risks (RRs); BMI-related RRs from meta-analyses were applied for each unit increase in BMI above 24.9 kg/m² (online supplemental appendix table 1-2). For patients with diabetes, T2D-related RRs adjusted the already modified base risks (online supplemental appendix table 1-3), capturing increased susceptibility due to high BMI and diabetes. Additionally, weight-cycling RRs from meta-analyses were used for those experiencing weight cycling, reflecting compounded health risks (online supplemental appendix table 1-4). The model recalculated transition probabilities monthly, incorporating patient characteristics, BMI development and obesity-associated disease risks.

Event-specific mortality rates were integrated for both acute and chronic conditions. Short-term (30-day) mortality rates were applied to acute events like stroke and MI, while longer-term mortality rates were used for chronic conditions, based on US national statistics and cohort studies (online supplemental appendix table 1-5). Background mortality rates were derived from US life tables and converted to monthly probabilities, providing a consistent baseline across all health states (online supplemental appendix table 1-6). To mitigate potential double-counting of cause-specific mortality (eg, CVD and cancer), the BMI-related RR on all-cause mortality was conservatively adjusted using a 60% reduction, based on the share of CVD and cancer deaths already explicitly modelled (online supplemental appendix table 1-2).

Model comparisons, populations and intervention effect

Scenario 1: non-cyclers versus cyclers

In the first scenario, the model examined the health economic consequences of weight cycling by comparing non-cyclers (patients not experiencing weight cycling) with cyclers (patients experiencing weight cycling). This comparison facilitated the quantification of the specific impacts of weight cycling. The population of interest was the US population with obesity, defined as having a BMI of ≥30 kg/m², informed by data from the Look AHEAD (Action for Health in Diabetes) Movement and Memory (M&M) study10 and the CARDIA (Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults) study.11 The Look AHEAD M&M study was used to quantify BMI development over time for both cyclers and non-cyclers, while the CARDIA data adjusted the model for a population with a lower proportion of T2D (6% in CARDIA versus all patients having T2D in Look AHEAD). Patient characteristics, intervention effects and weight development over time are detailed in the online supplemental appendix tables 3-1–3-4 and figure 3-1.

Scenario 2: GLP-1 and HP-ONS for maintenance versus GLP-1 natural maintenance

In the second scenario, the model compares HP-ONS as part of a high-protein diet for weight maintenance against natural weight maintenance following weight loss accomplished using a low-calorie diet and a GLP-1RA. Natural weight maintenance data post-GLP-1RA and low-calorie diet were derived from the original step 1 trial extension study.12 Meta-analysis evidence supports the effectiveness of high-protein diets for maintaining weight after initial loss,13 while the efficacy of HP-ONS was informed by the DIOGENES (Diet, Obesity and Genes) trial,14 which reported a 34.5% reduction in weight gain simulated over 12 months. The trial did not report the impact of this decreased weight gain on weight cycling, leading to reliance on clinical expert input. Based on data from the Look AHEAD M&M,10 CARDIA11 and DIOGENES14 studies, a clinical expert suggested that on average a 25% reduction of weight cycling might be achieved, over a 12-month horizon. This weight cycling reduction is a central assumption for scenario 2 and was therefore investigated in the uncertainty analysis in Section ‘Uncertainty and quality control’.

Detailed patient characteristics, intervention effects and weight development over time for this scenario are available in the online supplemental appendix tables 4-1–4-4 and figure 4-1.

Costs, health effects and cost-effectiveness

The STM adopted both healthcare payer and societal perspectives to capture direct medical costs and broader economic impacts, including productivity losses. Cost data were obtained from literature searches and adjusted to 2024 values using US inflation factors. Direct costs encompassed both acute and chronic expenses for conditions like stroke, MI and T2D (online supplemental appendix tables 2-1 and 2-2), while indirect costs related to productivity losses were inflated using GDP growth factors (online supplemental appendix tables 2-3 and 2-4). Future costs and effects were discounted at an annual rate of 3%, consistent with economic evaluation practices. Health utilities were informed by US-specific studies and converted from annual to monthly estimates to align with the model’s cycle length, accurately reflecting health-related quality of life impacts (online supplemental appendix tables 2–5).

The primary simulated health outcomes were obesity-related events avoided, life years (LYs) gained and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained. Calculations of cost-effectiveness per event avoided, LY gained and QALY gained were also performed. Cumulative values for LYs, QALYs and costs were integrated over time using the trapezoidal rule across monthly time steps. Given the fine temporal resolution, no further correction (eg, half-cycle or Simpson’s rule) was applied.

Although the USA does not have an official cost-effectiveness threshold, a commonly referenced range is US$100 000–150 000 per QALY gained,15 underscoring the emphasis on reporting and interpreting cost per QALY results.

Uncertainty and quality control

To assess result uncertainty, both deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) were performed. Deterministic analyses used point estimates to derive initial results, with one-way sensitivity analyses depicted as tornado diagrams to identify key influencing factors. PSA employed Monte Carlo simulations, performing 10 000 iterations while drawing variable values from their distributions to capture a range of outcomes. These results are presented as isocontour scatterplots and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves. Distributions and extreme values for all model variables are detailed in the appendix (online supplemental appendix section 3 including online supplemental tables 3-1–3-5 and figure 3-1 for scenario 1; online supplemental appendix section 4 including online supplemental tables 4-1–4-5 and figure 4-1 for scenario 2). The central assumption of scenario 2 (influence of HP-ONS on weight cycling) was investigated in one-way and PSA.

Rigorous quality control measures were implemented to ensure accuracy and reliability, including reviewing formulas and data inputs, validating results, conducting extreme value testing and thoroughly documenting the model’s design and functionality (online supplemental appendix section 5.1). To validate internal consistency, we performed additional model checks (online supplemental appendix section 5-2–5-4 including online supplemental tables 5-1–5-4 and figures 5-1 and 5-2). These included reproduction of cohort survival curves aligned with US life tables, confirmation of expected RRs for weight cycling effects (eg, CVD, T2D and kidney cancer) and sensitivity checks confirming directional plausibility of key drivers identified in tornado diagrams (eg, retirement age, baseline BMI).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Deterministic modelling results

As presented in table 1, the comparison between cyclers and non-cyclers (scenario 1) reveals that non-cyclers avoid more obesity-associated events, with an incremental difference of 0.0896 events avoided per patient over lifetime. Furthermore, non-cyclers experience greater LYs (0.6018) and QALYs (0.5181) compared with cyclers. From a financial perspective, non-cycling is cost-saving, with total cost savings of −US$4592 driven by lower direct costs of −US$1005 and indirect costs of −US$3588. The cost-effectiveness analysis highlights the substantial health economic advantage of non-cyclers, as evidenced by negative cost-effectiveness ratios, representing that non-cyclers are more effective and simultaneously less costly than cyclers.

In scenario 2, the effectiveness and costs of GLP-1RA and HP-ONS for maintenance are compared with those of GLP-1RA with natural maintenance over a lifetime horizon. The results show incremental per-patient gains of 0.0015 events avoided, 0.0102 LYs gained and 0.0152 QALYs gained for GLP-1RA and HP-ONS over GLP-1RA alone. While the addition of HP-ONS for weight maintenance results in slightly better health outcomes, it also incurs an additional intervention cost of HP-ONS of US$1153, leading to a total cost increase of US$369. The positive incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) for GLP-1RA and HP-ONS (US$246 000 per event avoided, US$36 176 per LY gained and US$24 276 per QALY gained) reflect that adding HP-ONS for weight maintenance is more costly but also more effective. Considering that the US$24 276 per QALY gained by the GLP-1RA and HP-ONS intervention is far below the US cost-effectiveness threshold, also known as the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of US$100 000–150 000 per QALY gained, makes the GLP-1RA and HP-ONS intervention a cost-effective option.

Deterministic one-way analysis

In scenario 1, comparing cyclers versus non-cyclers, the variables with the greatest influence were the mean baseline age and the mean retirement age. This is in line with the influence of indirect cost savings on the cost-effectiveness results (−US$3588 indirect cost savings presented in table 1) and is because productivity losses were only assumed until the mean retirement age of 64 years is reached. Therefore, younger patients or a higher retirement age have a positive impact on the cost-effectiveness results and vice versa. This highlights that intervening at a young age maximises the value of the intervention. All other variables had a lower influence on the ICER per QALY gained, and in all cases, the ICER per QALY gained stayed negative, indicating that non-cyclers were less costly and more effective.

In scenario 2, comparing GLP-1RA and HP-ONS for weight maintenance versus GLP-1RA natural maintenance, the variables with the greatest influence were the mean baseline age (for comparable reasons as in scenario 1) and intervention effect (effect of HP-ONS) on the weight maintenance, which reflect the central assumption in scenario 2. As this effect varied widely between 0% (no effect of HP-ONS and weight cycling) and 50% relative reduction of weight cycling (expert opinion 25%), the ICER per QALY gained shows a significant variation. Even if assuming no effect of HP-ONS on weight cycling, the ICER per QALY gained would be US$51 785 and thus far below the official threshold of US$100 000 up to US$150 000 per QALY gained.

Discussion

This health economic evaluation provides valuable insights into the implications of weight cycling among patients with obesity. Our findings indicate that non-cyclers experience a significant reduction in obesity-associated events, resulting in an increase of 0.602 LYs and 0.518 QALYs compared with weight cyclers. This translates to total cost savings of approximately US$4592 per patient. Additionally, in scenario 2, the incorporation of HP-ONS for weight maintenance following GLP-1RA induces weight loss achieved yielding incremental gains of 0.0015 obesity-associated events avoided, 0.0102 LYs gained and 0.0152 QALYs gained compared with natural weight maintenance. While the addition of HP-ONS incurs an incremental cost of US$369, it demonstrates a cost-effectiveness ratio of US$24 276 per QALY gained, well within commonly accepted thresholds for economic viability.

To our knowledge, this study represents the first comprehensive health economic evaluation of weight cycling, further reinforcing that preventing weight cycling is a vital health goal. Our results underline the importance of implementing effective interventions that promote long-term weight maintenance, not only to improve health outcomes but also to decrease healthcare spending. The model also highlights the importance of intervention at a young age to maximise the health gains for the patient. This observation is supported by the sensitivity analyses, where baseline age emerged as a major cost-effectiveness driver, with ICER differences exceeding US$80 000 depending on age at intervention onset.

The model assumes the use of a high-protein diet with HP-ONS for 1 year following GLP-1RA treatment. However, maintaining a high protein diet beyond 1 year may improve outcomes more or for a longer period of time.

Weight cycling, with characteristic periods of weight loss (muscle loss) and weight recurrence (adiposity gain), not only impacts health outcomes and expenditures but may also raise the risk of sarcopenia and related complications.16 17 Reduction in fat mass and muscle mass is expected during intentional weight loss across all treatment modalities. The emergence and increased utilisation of the highly effective GLP-1RAs for weight management and glycaemic control have heightened awareness of the proportion of muscle loss during weight loss.18 Weight recurrence that occurs after the disruption or discontinuation of these medications may also negatively affect body composition. Moreover, in patients with obesity, factors like ageing, chronic conditions, physical activity and diet increase the risk of suffering from low muscle mass or sarcopenia.16 Promoting adequate nutrition during weight loss may support weight maintenance as well as mitigate the risk of muscle loss or sarcopenia.18 But little is known about the dietary quality or nutritional status of patients using GLP-1RAs.19 Data from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and simulation models suggest that during caloric reduction, patients with obesity may encounter gaps in nutrient intake.20 Although additional research is needed, the results of this study and other investigations confirm that the benefits of HP-ONS are many and varied during weight management. Moreover, efforts to improve the quantification of real-world weight cycling risks and longitudinal nutritional intake will be critical to strengthening the evidence base for such models.

This study has limitations, which are common among economic modelling studies. There is limited available literature detailing long-term weight development for both cyclers and non-cyclers, with the Look AHEAD M&M study10 being one of the few identified sources. We adjusted these findings using data from the CARDIA study11 to better represent outcomes in a population with a lower proportion of people with diabetes. The link between weight maintenance and the relative reduction of weight cycling remains poorly quantified, necessitating reliance on expert opinion to estimate the impact of HP-ONS. As this is a central assumption related to the second scenario, we excluded any effect of HP-ONS in sensitivity analyses. Even when excluding this effect entirely, the cost per QALY gained is well below the typical cost-effectiveness thresholds.

Additionally, the model assumes that transitions into a cardiovascular event override the risk of subsequent cancer events. While this simplification reflects the clinical predominance of CVD risks in this population and supports model tractability, it may slightly underestimate long-term cancer risks in patients who first experience CVD. A further methodological limitation concerns the handling of transition probabilities. The model uses standard exponential transition probabilities (1−exp(−rt)) independently across health states, rather than embedding competing risks in a generator matrix. While this approach may introduce bias in the absolute estimates of individual state transitions, these biases are likely to cancel out when comparing incremental outcomes between scenarios. This modelling choice has been clarified in the Methods Section and is acknowledged as a potential source of bias. Similarly, while background mortality was implemented based on US life tables, potential overlap with disease-specific mortality (especially from CVD and cancer) was addressed by conservatively adjusting BMI-related all-cause mortality risk by 60%. This step aimed to reduce the risk of double counting. While the model implements BMI- and cycling-related risks using published RRs, internal validation revealed a slight downward deviation of simulated RRs over time relative to theoretical expectations. These differences, however, were small in magnitude (mean deviation for BMI 35∼2.8%) and consistent with the expected limitations of monthly-cycle STMs. The direction of deviation was conservative and unlikely to bias incremental outcomes in favour of the intervention. Additionally, the implementation of background mortality was independently validated using age-specific survival rates from US life tables. The model accurately reproduced life expectancy from age 60 within 0.4 years and showed deviations below 0.3% across all ages. These findings confirm that no structural over- or underestimation of mortality affects LYs or QALYs in baseline or comparator arms.

To further evaluate model behaviour, extensive structural validation was conducted, including logic and plausibility checks for mortality, BMI risk implementation, cost components and tornado sensitivity directions. Across both modelled scenarios, key input parameters exhibited predictable and interpretable effects on ICERs, supporting the face validity of the model.

Our model focused specifically on simulating obesity-associated diseases for which there is a clear association with increased risk due to weight cycling. Adding further obesity-associated diseases to the model could enhance the robustness of the findings and improve the overall cost-effectiveness outcomes if sufficient evidence is available to support their inclusion.

Collectively, our findings strongly advocate for the prevention of weight cycling as a highly effective measure that enhances health outcomes for individuals living with obesity and significantly reduces healthcare costs. A high-protein diet using HP-ONS is a cost-effective intervention to improve weight maintenance and mitigate weight recurrence. Future research investigating the long-term effects of weight maintenance strategies, in particular the impact of HP-ONS, can inform future health economic evaluations.
