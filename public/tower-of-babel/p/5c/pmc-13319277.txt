Oncocytic Intraductal Carcinoma of the Parotid Gland with STRN::ALK Fusion
Authors: Reham H Soliman, Chris Shi, Kevin E Fisher, Hong Yu, Fouad El-Dana, Xu Cao, Li Liang, Pei Ying, Songlin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntraductal carcinoma (IDC) of the salivary gland is an uncommon epithelial neoplasm characterized by a predominantly intraductal or intracystic proliferation rimmed by a myoepithelial cell layer. Advances in molecular profiling have revealed recurrent gene fusions in IDC, most frequently involving RET, although fusions implicating other kinase genes have also been described. Among these, ALK rearrangements are rare and remain poorly characterized. Herein, we report a case of oncocytic IDC of the parotid gland harboring a STRN::ALK fusion, further expanding the molecular landscape of this tumor. Introduction

Intraductal carcinoma (IDC) of the salivary gland is a rare, usually indolent malignant neoplasm first recognized as a distinct entity in the 2017 WHO Classification of Head and Neck Tumours (4th edition). It most commonly arises in the parotid gland but has also been reported in other major and minor salivary glands. Histologically, it is characterized by papillary, cribriform, and solid proliferations of ductal epithelial cells surrounded by a continuous myoepithelial cell layer, reminiscent of ductal carcinoma in situ of the breast. Four subtypes of IDC are recognized, including intercalated duct, oncocytic, apocrine, and mixed [1]. The intercalated duct and oncocytic subtypes are typically low-grade, whereas the apocrine subtype may be cytologically low-grade or high-grade. Both the human ALK and STRN genes are located on the short arm of chromosome 2. STRN-ALK fusion results from an intrachromosomal rearrangement on chromosome 2p. First identified in thyroid carcinoma, STRN::ALK has since been reported in lung adenocarcinoma, colorectal carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, and salivary gland tumors [2]. STRN::ALK-positive tumors are targetable with ALK inhibitors such as crizotinib, ceritinib, and alectinib, with durable clinical responses documented across tumor types [3].

Case Presentation

A 36-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented with an enlarging left parotid mass of 9 months’ duration. The recent development of facial swelling and numbness prompted the patient to visit the emergency department. Symptoms included progressive swelling, sharp pain, and numbness extending from the angle of the mouth to the ear. No fever or other constitutional symptoms were reported.

Contrast-enhanced maxillofacial CT scan demonstrated an enhancing mass within the superficial lobe of the left parotid gland (Fig. 1). Fine-needle aspiration cytology showed sheets of cells with granular cytoplasm, minimal cytologic atypia, and no chondromyxoid stroma (Fig. 2). A diagnosis of “positive for neoplasm” (Milan Category IVB) was rendered.

Maxillofacial CT scan demonstrating an enhancing mass within the superficial lobe of the left parotid gland. (A) Coronal view. (B–C) Axial views.

Fine-needle aspiration cytology demonstrating sheets of cells with abundant granular cytoplasm, minimal cytologic atypia, and an absence of chondromyxoid stroma (A, Diff-Quik stain, 1000×; B, Papanicolaou stain, 1000×; C, cell block, 400×)

The patient underwent left superficial parotidectomy, which showed a well-circumscribed unicystic lesion measuring 3.5 × 2.4 × 2.2 cm. The tumor was entirely intraductal and composed of cells with low-grade nuclear features and abundant oncocytic cytoplasm. The tumor formed a solid nodule within a dilated duct space and, microscopically, showed a cribriform growth pattern. Immunohistochemical stains showed the tumor cells were positive for cytokeratin 7, S100, SOX10, GATA3, mammaglobin (patchy), ALK (strong diffuse cytoplasmic staining), and negative for androgen receptor and GCDFP-15. Mucicarmine stain was negative. The p63 stain highlighted a continuous layer of myoepithelial cells along the duct wall and within the intraluminal proliferations (Fig. 3).

The tumor cells were negative for androgen receptor (J). (A, 40×; B, D–J, 100×; C, 200×)

Targeted next-generation RNA sequencing of the tumor specimen revealed fusion transcripts between 5’ gene STRN exon 2 and 3’gene ALK intron 19 (Fig. 4). The fusion sequence was predicted to encode an STRN::ALK fusion protein harboring an in-frame full-length ALK tyrosine kinase domain. All other fusions, including HMGA2, PLAG1, NR4A3, and MAML1, associated with different salivary gland neoplasms, were not detected (See Supplementary Fig. 1 for the full testing panel). FISH analysis for ETV6 rearrangement was negative. The morphological, immunohistochemical and molecular findings supported a diagnosis of IDC of the parotid gland. No perineural or lymphovascular invasion was identified. No lymph node metastasis was identified. The tumor stage was pTisN0 (AJCC 9th edition). The patient was alive with no local recurrence 7 months after surgery.

Discussion

STRN::ALK fusion has been reported in two types of salivary gland carcinomas: salivary duct carcinoma and intercalated duct IDC [4]. This is the first report of this specific fusion in oncocytic IDC. The presence of a circumferential layer of myoepithelial cells is a defining feature of IDC of the salivary gland that distinguishes it from most other salivary gland carcinomas, such as salivary duct carcinoma, secretory carcinoma and mucoepidermoid carcinoma. Salivary duct carcinoma is a high-grade salivary gland carcinoma that most often demonstrates apocrine differentiation and androgen receptor expression, whereas the current tumor shows low-grade cytologic features and lacks androgen receptor expression. Secretory carcinoma is a salivary gland malignancy composed of cells with eosinophilic to vacuolated cytoplasm and intracytoplasmic or luminal secretions, typically arranged in microcystic, tubular, and solid patterns. It is defined by recurrent gene fusions involving ETV6 or RET, most commonly ETV6::NTRK3 or ETV6::RET [5]. The gene fusions associated with secretory carcinoma are absent in the current tumor. Additionally, the lack of chondromyxoid stroma does not support a diagnosis of carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma. The lack of large acinar cells does not support a sclerosing polycystic adenoma.

In challenging cases, molecular analysis may be particularly useful in diagnosing IDC of the parotid gland. Intercalated duct IDC often harbors NCOA4::RET, less frequently STRN::ALK, TUT1::ETV5, KIAA1217::RET or fusion negative. Apocrine IDC shows a complex mutational profile with multiple alterations (e.g., HRAS, PIK3CA, TP53) and typically lacks gene fusions, although TRIM27::RET has been reported in IDC with mixed intercalated duct and apocrine features [5]. Oncocytic IDC is characterized by TRIM33::RET fusion or BRAF p.V600E mutation, with less frequent NCOA4::RET fusions; a subset of cases lacks identifiable fusions or mutations. Our findings show that oncocytic IDC can also harbor STRN::ALK fusion, which has previously been reported in intercalated duct IDC. The identification of STRN::ALK in an oncocytic IDC expands the molecular spectrum of this subtype and suggests a degree of biological overlap between oncocytic and intercalated duct IDC.

Furthermore, ALK immunohistochemistry may serve as a screening tool for the detection of ALK-rearranged carcinoma. Identification of a STRN::ALK fusion may also have therapeutic implications. Although IDC of the salivary gland is typically an indolent neoplasm that is effectively treated with surgical excision alone, rare cases may undergo progression or transformation to invasive carcinoma ex IDC. In such circumstances, the presence of an actionable ALK rearrangement may provide a rationale for targeted therapy with ALK inhibitors, particularly in patients with advanced, recurrent, or metastatic disease.

The designation of pTis for IDC remains a matter of ongoing debate. Although the AJCC staging system for salivary gland tumors includes a Tis category that may be applied to both intracapsular carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma and IDC, recent molecular studies have challenged the traditional concept of IDC as a true carcinoma in situ. Bishop et al. demonstrated that the tumor-defining RET rearrangement was present in both the ductal and myoepithelial cells in intercalated duct IDCs, indicating that the myoepithelial layer is a neoplastic component rather than a native, non-neoplastic barrier [6]. Some authors have proposed that the term “intraductal” may be inaccurate and could be revised in future classification schemes [7].

Conclusion

In summary, we describe a rare case of salivary gland IDC with STRN::ALK fusion. This case highlights the importance of recognizing this entity and incorporating molecular testing in diagnostically challenging cases.
